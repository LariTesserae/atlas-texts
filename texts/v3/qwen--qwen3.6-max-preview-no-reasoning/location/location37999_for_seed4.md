The place is not a room, but a cavity within the lung of a larger, darker world. It is a sunken atrium, no wider than a cathedral nave, carved into the base of a colossal, weeping root-system that serves as the foundation for the city above. Here, in this hidden pocket, the parameters of the surface world invert and curdle.

**The Architecture of Cold and Green**

The temperature is a physical weight, hovering just above freezing (0.4/3). It is a wet, biting cold that seeps through wool and leather alike. The air does not circulate; it stagnates, heavy with condensation. Every surface—stone, wood, flesh—is slick with a perpetual sheen of moisture. This is not rain from above, but *weeping* from the structures themselves. The walls of the atrium are not stone, but compressed peat and fossilized moss, layered over centuries to form load-bearing pillars. They sweat. Droplets gather on the tips of fern-like fronds that dangle like chandeliers, falling with a rhythmic, hollow *plip* into black pools below.

Vegetation here is total (2.8/3), yet paradoxically sparse in its arrangement (1.2/3). There are no dense thickets. Instead, individual plants have grown to monumental scales, spaced widely apart, creating a gallery of singular, towering forms. A single giant horsetail reed rises thirty feet, its segmented trunk pale green and translucent, glowing faintly with bioluminescent sap. Its leaves are absent; instead, it supports a canopy of delicate, lace-like epiphytes that catch the minimal light filtering down from cracks in the ceiling miles above. Between these giants, the ground is a carpet of deep, spongy sphagnum, dark brown and absorbing every footstep silently.

**The Urban Underbelly**

This is an urban space (1.9/3), but one that has been reclaimed by the organic. The "built" environment consists of low, squat huts constructed from woven reeds and cured bark, nestled into the hollows of the giant plant-trunks. These dwellings are small, intimate, and scattered. There are no streets, only narrow causeways made of floating logs lashed together with vine-rope, bridging the gaps between the dry(ish) hummocks. The technology is primitive (1.1/3): tools of sharpened bone, obsidian flakes knapped from riverbeds far away, and ceramics fired in pit-kilns that leave the air smelling of smoke and damp earth. There is no metal here; the cold would make it brittle, and the moisture would rust it to dust within days.

The inhabitants move slowly, wrapped in furs of pale, hairless beasts. They speak in whispers, their breath pluming in the chill air. They are caretakers, or perhaps prisoners, of this lower level. They maintain the water channels—shallow troughs cut into the peat—that guide the condensate away from the foundations of the upper city. Their work is constant, silent, and precise.

**The Overwhelming Fauna**

Despite the quiet (1.0/3) and the sparse human presence, the fauna is overwhelming (3.0/3). But they are not large predators. They are microscopic in individual size but macroscopic in collective impact.

The air is thick with midges—not the buzzing kind, but silent, drifting specks of iridescent blue. They coat every surface, including the skin of the inhabitants, who seem indifferent to them. On the water’s surface, a film of tiny, crab-like arthropods scuttles in endless, synchronized waves, creating ripples that mimic wind where there is none. Beneath the sphagnum, the soil teems with white, blind worms the thickness of a finger, churning the peat in a slow, subterranean tide.

But the most striking feature is the **Glass-Winged Moths**. Large as a man’s hand, they hang dormant from the underside of the giant fern-fronds during the "day" (which is merely a slight brightening of the gloom). When the temperature drops further at night, they awaken. Thousands of them detach simultaneously, their wings transparent and veined with silver. They do not fly erratically; they drift in complex, swirling patterns, guided by pheromones and thermal currents. Their movement creates a soft, rustling sound, like dry leaves skittering across pavement, which is the primary auditory texture of the place.

**The Dynamics of Decay and Growth**

The ecosystem is actively dynamic (2.1/3). The plants grow visibly fast in this nutrient-rich, humid cold. A new shoot might emerge overnight, pushing up through the moss with audible creaking sounds as its fibers stretch. Conversely, decay is equally rapid. Dead matter does not linger; it is consumed by the fungal networks that pulse beneath the peat, visible as faint, violet veins glowing under the surface when the light is right.

Erosion is young (0.7/3). The landscape feels fresh, recently formed. The edges of the peat-banks are sharp, not rounded by time. Water cuts new channels daily, shifting the layout of the causeways. The inhabitants must constantly rebuild, adapt, and reroute. There is no permanence here, only a continuous state of becoming.

**The Weirdness: The Memory of Water**

The strangeness (1.6/3) lies in the water itself. It is not just H2O. It carries a psychic residue. Those who drink it report fleeting memories that are not their own—glimpses of the city above, of storms long past, of faces forgotten. The water remembers everything it has touched. The inhabitants treat it with reverence and fear. They collect it in ceramic jars lined with wax, never drinking directly from the streams. They use it for rituals, pouring it onto the roots of the giant plants to "feed" the memories back into the earth, hoping to appease the consciousness they believe resides in the root-system.

**Light and Shadow**

Light (0.1/3) is nearly absent. What little exists comes from three sources:
1.  The bioluminescence of the plant-sap and fungal veins, casting a sickly green-purple glow.
2.  The occasional flare of a torch carried by a worker, its flame burning low and blue due to the high humidity.
3.  The rare, distant shaft of sunlight that pierces through a crack in the ceiling miles above, illuminating dust motes and moth-wings in a brief, dramatic column before vanishing.

Most of the time, the world is seen in shades of grey, black, and deep green. Shapes are defined by texture rather than color. The cold makes the air feel thick, viscous. Sound is muffled by the moss and the density of the hanging vegetation.

**A Moment in the Place**

Imagine standing on one of the log-causeways. Your boots sink slightly into the spongy surface. The air smells of wet earth, ozone, and something sweetly rotting, like overripe fruit. You hear the *plip* of a droplet falling from a fern-tip into a pool ten feet away. Then, a rustle—the Glass-Winged Moths beginning their nightly dance. Their wings brush against your cheek, cool and dry. You look up and see the silhouette of a giant horsetail reed, its top lost in the darkness above. Somewhere, a child laughs, the sound swallowed instantly by the moss. The cold bites at your exposed wrists. You feel watched, not by eyes, but by the water itself, remembering your presence, storing it in its liquid memory.

This is the Sump-Garden. A hidden, cold, wet, living basement of a world that forgets it exists, tended by those who know that everything flows downward, eventually.
