The air here is not merely warm; it is a heavy, wet blanket woven from the breath of a million lungs. You stand at an elevation that should be thin and cold, yet the heat presses against your skin with the insistence of a fever. It is high, yes—the curvature of the world dips away beneath your feet, revealing a horizon that stretches into a hazy, violet twilight—but the atmosphere is dense enough to swallow sound before it can escape, only to amplify it within the confines of this constructed valley.

This place is entirely made. There is no bedrock, no soil, no raw stone. Every surface you touch is engineered, layered, and repurposed, yet time has worn the sharp edges off the construction until it feels organic. The "ground" is a vast, undulating floor of interlocking tiles—terracotta, slate, polished basalt, and glazed ceramic—laid in patterns so complex they mimic the fractal growth of moss. These tiles are weathered, cracked by centuries of slow, tectonic shifting, their grout lines filled not with mortar, but with rich, black humus that has accumulated over eons. This is the erosion parameter: established, settled, in balance. The artificiality has been accepted by the earth’s memory, or perhaps the earth itself was built from the start.

Vegetation erupts from every seam. Because the substrate is entirely constructed, the roots do not dig deep; they spread wide, tangling across the surface like nervous systems exposed to the air. Ferns uncurl from the cracks in the plaza stones, their fronds brushing against your knees. Vines of thick, woody ivy cascade down the walls of towering structures that rise around you—structures that look less like buildings and more like geological formations carved by human hands. Arches of white marble, now stained green with lichen, bridge gaps between spires of red brick. It is lush, overwhelmingly so. The greenery is not sparse decoration; it is the dominant texture, a verdant fur covering the skeleton of the city.

But it is the darkness that defines the space. The light level is low, a perpetual dusk. High above, the canopy of giant, broad-leaved trees—grown atop the highest towers—interlocks to form a roof that blocks out the sky. Only slivers of pale, diffuse light filter through, catching on the dew that constantly condenses on the leaves. This moisture is the water parameter: damp, intermittent, clinging. It does not rain heavily, but the air is saturated. Droplets fall from the upper branches with a rhythmic *plip*, landing on the broad waxy leaves below, then rolling off into the hollows of the architecture. Pools of still, dark water collect in the depressions of the tiled floors, reflecting the dim glow of bioluminescent fungi that cling to the underside of the arches.

And then, there is the noise.

It is deafening, but not in the way of machinery or industry. It is a biological roar. The fauna parameter is overwhelming. This place is alive with creatures that have claimed the constructed environment as their own. Birds—not just dozens, but thousands—nest in the crevices of the stonework. Their calls are a continuous, shrieking chorus that bounces off the hard surfaces of the towers, creating a cacophony of echoes that never quite dies. Insects hum in the humid air, a vibration you feel in your teeth. Small mammals scuttle through the undergrowth of ferns that cover the rooftops. The density of life is suffocatingly high. Every square inch of the built environment hosts a colony, a nest, a burrow, or a perch.

You walk forward, your boots sinking slightly into the layer of leaf litter that covers the paved streets. The scale of the place is open—it extends beyond your immediate vision, a regional sprawl of interconnected terraces and valleys—but the density makes it feel intimate, claustrophobic. You are surrounded by presences. A monkey-like creature swings from a vine draped over a stone balustrade, chattering loudly. Below, in the shadow of a collapsed aqueduct, a pack of small, sleek predators stalks through the tall grass that has overtaken the dry riverbed. The water here is not a flowing stream, but a series of stagnant, algae-choked ponds formed in the broken channels of the old infrastructure. They smell of wet earth and decay, a scent that mixes with the sweet perfume of night-blooming flowers that open only in this permanent gloom.

The rules of this world are simple: nature has won, but it wears the clothes of civilization. The tech level is zero. There are no lights, no wires, no engines. The only technology remaining is the passive kind—the shape of a dome that catches rainwater, the angle of a wall that deflects wind, the curve of a gutter that directs flow. Everything else is biology. The warmth comes from the mass of living bodies and the composting matter beneath the tiles. The sound is the collective voice of the ecosystem.

As you move deeper into the valley, the structures change. They become more ornate, more intricate. Carved faces of forgotten gods watch from the cornices, their eyes hollowed out by nesting swallows. The vegetation becomes thicker, forming tunnels of green that you must push through. The air grows warmer, heavier with the scent of musk and pollen. The deafening roar of the birds shifts pitch, becoming a lower thrum as larger creatures stir in the shadows.

Here, in the heart of the constructed wilderness, you realize that the "built" aspect is not just the material, but the intent. This place was designed to hold life, to contain it, to frame it. And now, life has spilled over the frames, filling every corner, every crack, every void. It is a garden gone feral, a city reclaimed by the wild, but because it was built with such care, such durability, it has not crumbled. It has simply become something else. A sanctuary of noise and shadow, where the line between the architect’s hand and the gardener’s seed has vanished entirely.

You stop near a large, circular plaza. In the center stands a fountain, long dry, its basin filled with mud and reeds. Around it, hundreds of animals drink from the shallow pools. Deer, strange and antlered, lift their heads as you approach, their eyes wide and unafraid. The sound here is slightly muted by the thick carpet of moss that covers the plaza floor, but the ambient roar of the surrounding jungle-city continues, a constant reminder that you are never alone. The light is so dim now that you can barely see the far end of the plaza, where the silhouette of a massive tree rises above the rooftops, its branches disappearing into the dark ceiling of the canopy.

This is a place of slow dynamics. Nothing happens quickly. The vines grow inch by inch over decades. The stones settle millimeter by millimeter over centuries. The animals live and die in cycles that feel eternal. But in that slowness, there is a intensity. The density of life means that every moment is crowded, every breath shared. You are part of the ecosystem now, another presence in the volume, another note in the deafening song.

The dampness clings to your clothes. The warmth seeps into your bones. The darkness wraps around you, not as a threat, but as a embrace. And the sound—the endless, overwhelming, beautiful noise of life thriving in a cage of stone and tile—fills your ears until you can hear nothing else. This is the landscape: a high-altitude, warm, damp, dark, loud, lush, built, ancient, teeming world. It is normal, in its own weirdly perfect way. It is exactly what it is supposed to be.
