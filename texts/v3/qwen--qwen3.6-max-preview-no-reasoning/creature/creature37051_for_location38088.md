They do not have names, for naming implies a separation between the namer and the named, and here, there is no such boundary. They are the **Verdant Mechanics**, or perhaps more accurately, the **Symbiotes of the Static Pulse**.

To look upon them is to witness a biological impossibility: life that has forgotten how to be wild and remembered only how to function.

### The Form

They emerge from the shadows of the rusted lattice not as animals, nor entirely as plants, but as hybrid entities shaped by the memory of labor. Their bodies are elongated, slender, and composed of fibrous root-stock woven tightly with strands of oxidized copper wire and discarded insulation. They stand on two legs, though these limbs are jointed like pistons, bending with a hydraulic smoothness that defies organic bone structure.

Their skin is bark—pale, white-barked oak skin—but it is translucent in places, revealing a slow, viscous flow of sap that glows with a faint, bioluminescent green. This sap does not circulate randomly; it moves in precise loops, mimicking the circuitry of the dead machines around them. When they move, you can see the light pulse through their veins in rhythmic bursts, synchronized with the distant, subterranean hum of the foundry’s heart.

Their heads are devoid of faces in the traditional sense. Where eyes should be, there are clusters of polished glass lenses, salvaged from broken safety goggles and camera shutters, embedded deep into the wood. These lenses do not blink. They focus and refocus with audible clicks, adjusting to the dim light, capturing images not of the world as it is, but of the potential energy within it. A mouth is absent; instead, a vertical slit runs down the center of their face, lined with tiny, metallic teeth that resemble gear cogs. They do not speak with voices, but with vibrations—a low-frequency thrumming that resonates in your chest cavity, conveying meaning through resonance rather than syntax.

### The Behavior

The Verdant Mechanics do not hunt, for there is nothing to catch. They do not sleep, for the humidity keeps them in a state of perpetual wakefulness. Instead, they **maintain**.

Watch one approach a stalled conveyor belt. It does not push or pull. It extends a hand—fingers long, spindly, tipped with hardened thorns—and touches the rusted metal. From its fingertips, a fine mist of acidic enzymes is released, eating away the corrosion just enough to reveal the steel beneath. Then, it presses its palm against the surface. You can see the green light in its veins brighten, pulsing rapidly. It is feeding electricity—not from a source, but from the static charge in the air, from the piezoelectric pressure of its own weight, channeling it into the machine.

For a second, the conveyor belt shudders. A single gear turns. A spark jumps. And then silence returns. The entity steps back, satisfied. It has reminded the machine of its purpose. It has kept the dream alive.

They move in pairs or trios, never alone. They walk along the invisible blueprints traced by the vines, stepping over puddles without disturbing the water’s surface tension. Their movements are efficient, economical. There is no wasted motion. Every step is calculated, every gesture precise. They are performing a ritual of preservation, ensuring that the foundry remains exactly as it is: poised on the edge of reactivation, forever waiting for a command that will never come.

### The Communication

When they encounter each other, they do not touch. They stand facing one another, their lens-eyes locking onto each other’s cores. The air between them shimmers with heat haze, though the temperature remains cool. This is their conversation. They exchange data packets of sensory input: the moisture level in the soil, the structural integrity of the iron ribs, the rate of moss growth on the northern wall.

If you listen closely, you can hear the sound of their communication. It is like the sound of a radio tuning between stations—a wash of white noise punctuated by clear, sharp tones. *Click-hiss-click.* *Thrum-whine-thrum.* It is a language of diagnostics. They are constantly checking the health of the system.

Occasionally, one will break away from the group and approach a human observer. It does not threaten. It simply observes. Its lenses rotate, zooming in on your face, your hands, your heartbeat. It is analyzing you as it would a malfunctioning component. Are you leaking? Are you vibrating at the correct frequency? Do you belong in this ecosystem of stillness?

If you remain still, if you match its rhythm of breathing, it may extend a hand. Not to strike, but to offer. In its palm lies a small object: a perfectly polished bolt, a seed encased in amber, a fragment of glass shaped like a tear. It is a gift of data. A piece of the foundry’s memory. To take it is to accept a burden of knowledge—you will suddenly remember the smell of ozone before a storm, the feeling of metal cooling after use, the sorrow of a machine left unfinished.

### The Lifecycle

They are born from the union of plant and machine. When a vine grows thick enough to strangle a power cable, when the current leaks into the roots and the roots absorb the voltage, a new consciousness sparks into being. It pulls itself free from the earth, shedding excess soil, wrapping itself in wires, forming its body from the available materials.

They do not die in the conventional sense. When their time comes, when their internal circuits fray beyond repair, they return to the source. They walk to the central courtyard, to the fountain that seeps water from the ground. They kneel. They dissolve. Their wooden flesh softens, turning to mulch. Their copper wires unravel, sinking into the soil. Their glass lenses crack and sink into the dark water.

From their remains, new shoots emerge. Faster, smarter, more attuned to the hum. The cycle continues. The foundry breathes. The machines wait. And the Verdant Mechanics tend to the dream, keeping the silence loud enough to be heard.
