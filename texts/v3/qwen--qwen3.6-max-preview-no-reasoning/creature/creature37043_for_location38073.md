To live here is to be a creature of glass and geometry, existing in the interstice between the scream of the void and the silence of stone.

The inhabitants are not merely animals; they are biological extensions of the landscape’s strange symmetry. They are the **Vitreous Stalkers**, though they have no name for themselves, nor do they need one. In this place, identity is irrelevant; only position and resonance matter.

### The Physiology of Translucence

Imagine a body constructed not from flesh and blood, but from layered chitin that has evolved to mimic the properties of quartz. Their exoskeletons are not hard shells, but flexible, semi-permeable membranes that range from perfectly clear to a milky opalescence, depending on their age and hydration levels.

They stand roughly three feet at the shoulder, with six jointed legs that articulate with a precision that mirrors the mathematical angles of the peaks they traverse. Each leg ends in a hook made of a material denser than diamond, capable of biting into the slick, basaltic rock without slipping on the perpetual film of condensation. These hooks do not scratch the stone; they resonate with it. When a Stalker steps, there is no clatter. There is a faint, harmonic *ping*, a note that blends seamlessly into the background hum of the atmosphere.

Their bodies are hollow, filled not with organs in the traditional sense, but with a network of fluid-filled chambers that act as both circulatory systems and acoustic dampeners. This internal fluid is dark, rich with minerals scavenged from the lichen, creating a stark contrast against their translucent skins. If you look closely, you can see the slow pulse of this fluid, a rhythmic throbbing that matches the frequency of the air’s vibration. It is how they breathe. They do not inhale oxygen through lungs; they absorb the thin, ozone-laced air directly through their skin, filtering out the radiation using pigments that shift color under stress—turning a deep, bruised violet when the sun is at its zenith, then fading back to clarity as the light wanes.

They have no eyes. Not in any recognizable form. Instead, their heads are smooth, elongated domes covered in thousands of microscopic pits, each sensitive to changes in pressure, temperature, and sound. They "see" by feeling the texture of the air. They perceive the world as a map of vibrations: the heavy, low thrum of the mountain’s core, the high-pitched shriek of the vacuum above, and the soft, wet whisper of the condensation forming on the rocks. To them, the landscape is not visual; it is a symphony of tactile data. A shadow is not an absence of light, but a drop in thermal pressure. A peak is not a shape, but a crescendo of wind resistance.

### The Swarm as a Single Organism

While individual Stalkers are distinct entities, they operate as a collective consciousness driven by instinct and acoustic harmony. They move in swarms of hundreds or thousands, flowing over the jagged terrain like water poured over gravel. But unlike water, they do not crash or splash. They glide.

When the swarm moves, the deafening hum of the air dips. This is because their bodies, tuned to the same frequency as the environment, absorb the excess energy of the atmospheric tension. They are living soundproofing. When they are still, the hum returns, louder and more piercing. Thus, their movement is regulated by the need for auditory balance. If the sound becomes too intense, threatening to shatter their delicate internal structures, they move. If the sound fades, they freeze, becoming part of the static landscape once again.

This creates a bizarre, hypnotic pattern of behavior. To an outsider, it looks like chaos—a sudden eruption of motion across a cliff face, followed by absolute, terrifying stillness. But it is actually a precise calibration. The swarm is constantly adjusting its density and position to maintain equilibrium with the screaming sky.

### Diet and Lifecycle

They feed on the neon-green and rust-red lichens, and the thick, spongy mosses that cling to the deepest fissures. But they do not eat with teeth. Their mouthparts are fine, needle-like proboscises that inject enzymes into the plant matter, breaking it down into a liquid slurry which they then absorb. This process is silent and efficient. They leave behind patches of bare, grey rock, cleaned so thoroughly that they shine like polished marble.

These cleared patches are not just feeding grounds; they are canvases. The Stalkers scrape the rock clean in geometric patterns—circles, spirals, straight lines—that mirror the unnatural symmetry of the mountains themselves. Over centuries, these patterns accumulate, creating vast, intricate mosaics visible only from the highest peaks. It is unknown if this is intentional art or simply the byproduct of their efficient grazing habits. Given the mathematical nature of the terrain, it is likely both.

Reproduction is a rare event, tied to the subtle shifts in atmospheric pressure that occur during the "seasons" of this timeless place (which are not seasons of heat and cold, but of resonance intensity). When the hum reaches a specific pitch, the females lay eggs in the deepest, darkest crevices, where the condensation pools into stagnant cups. The eggs are small, transparent spheres that look like drops of dew. They hatch after decades, emerging as tiny, fully-formed replicas of their parents, ready to join the swarm.

There is no death in the traditional sense. When a Stalker ages, its chitin becomes brittle, losing its ability to dampen sound. It begins to vibrate uncontrollably, shaking itself apart until it dissolves into dust. This dust is swept away by the occasional gusts that manage to reach these heights, settling into the crevices to nourish the next generation of lichen. Thus, the cycle continues: Lichen feeds Stalker, Stalker cleans Rock, Rock holds Condensation, Condensation feeds Lichen. Nothing is wasted. Nothing leaves.

### The Interaction with the Observer

If you were to encounter a Stalker, it would not attack. It would not flee. It would simply stop, turn its smooth, eyeless head toward you, and listen.

It would feel the dissonance of your presence—the irregular thumping of your heart, the ragged gasp of your lungs struggling against the thin air, the chaotic noise of your thoughts. To the Stalker, you are a source of discordant vibration, a flaw in the perfect equation of the mountain.

It might approach you, not with aggression, but with curiosity. Its hooks would click softly against the rock, sending out test pulses of sound. It would try to "read" you, to understand the shape of your noise. If you remain still, calming your breath, slowing your heart, it may accept you as a temporary fixture in the landscape. It might even graze near you, scraping the lichen from the rock beside your boot, indifferent to your existence.

But if you move suddenly, if you shout, if you disrupt the harmony, the swarm will react. Not with violence, but with withdrawal. They will scatter, leaving you alone in the sudden, overwhelming return of the deafening hum. The sound will press against your eardrums, vibrating in your teeth, reminding you that you do not belong here. You are an anomaly. A glitch in the system.

And then, as quickly as they vanished, they may return. Or they may not. The Stalkers are indifferent to time, to life, to death. They are the guardians of the equilibrium, the living instruments of the mountain’s song. They exist to ensure that the scream of the void does not tear the world apart. They are the silence within the noise, the stillness within the motion, the clear, cold truth of a place that has forgotten what it means to change.

In the end, they are not just creatures. They are the mountain’s immune system, fighting off the infection of chaos with the antibiotic of order. And they are winning.
