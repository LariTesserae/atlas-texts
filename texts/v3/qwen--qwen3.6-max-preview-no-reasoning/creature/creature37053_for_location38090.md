They are the **Lithos**, though they do not call themselves this, for they possess no language of sound. To name them is to impose a softness upon them that does not exist. They are the children of the Silica Steppe, born not from womb or egg, but from the slow, deliberate accretion of mineral and light.

To look at a Lithos is to witness a paradox made flesh—or rather, made stone. They stand upright, bipedal in form only because the physics of their world favors verticality for catching the thermal updrafts and the high-angle sun. But there is nothing mammalian about them. There is no fur to trap heat, no skin to sweat, no breath to fog the air.

Their bodies are composed of the same material as the Spire-Trees: layers of translucent quartz, hardened resin, and veins of amber sap. They are tall, often reaching two meters, yet they appear weightless. This is an illusion created by their structure; they are hollow-boned, lattice-like, with internal struts that resemble the intricate architecture of a geode. When the sun hits them, they do not cast a solid shadow. Instead, they refract the light, scattering it into prisms that dance around their feet like spilled jewels.

### The Face of Stillness

A Lithos has no face in the traditional sense. Where eyes should be, there are deep, polished sockets filled with a dark, viscous fluid—perhaps a derivative of the mercury-sap found in the trees. These "eyes" do not blink. They do not need to, for there is no dust that can irritate them, no wind that can dry them out. They see not just the visible spectrum, but the ultraviolet patterns on the Bloom-orchids and the infrared heat signatures of the Shard-Stalkers. Their vision is total, encompassing the thermal pulse of the world itself.

Below these pools of darkness, there is no nose, no mouth. Communication is not verbal. It is chemical and electrical. A Lithos speaks by releasing pheromones through microscopic pores along its neck and shoulders, scents of ozone and crushed mint that carry complex information about weather patterns, resource locations, or danger. Simultaneously, they communicate through static discharge. Small arcs of blue lightning jump between their fingertips and the tips of nearby plants, or between individuals when they stand close. A conversation among Lithos is a silent storm of sparks and scent, invisible to human ears but deafening to those who understand the frequency.

### Movement and Purpose

They move with a grace that belies their rigid composition. Because their joints are not hinged bone but flexible bands of semi-transparent silicone-like polymer, their motion is fluid, almost liquid. When they walk, their feet do not crunch on the crystalline dust; they glide. Their soles are broad and flat, distributing their weight so perfectly that they leave no footprints. This is essential. In a world where erosion is art and disturbance is rare, to leave a mark is an act of violence. The Lithos are careful guests in their own home.

They are not hunters, nor are they prey. They are curators.

Their primary role is the maintenance of the equilibrium. You might see a Lithos standing beside a Spire-Tree, one hand resting gently against the trunk. It is not leaning for support; it is monitoring the flow of sap. If the tree is stressed, if the thermal differential is too great, the Lithos will extend its fingers, allowing small arcs of static electricity to flow into the plant, stabilizing its internal charge. They prune the Frond-Fields not with tools, but with precise vibrations, humming at a frequency that causes dead or overgrown glass-fronds to shatter into harmless dust, which then settles back into the soil to nourish the Root-Weavers below.

They are the gardeners of glass, tending to a landscape that grows itself, ensuring that the lushness does not become chaos, that the silence remains profound.

### The Internal Life

Inside the hollow chest of a Lithos, there is no beating heart. There is a central core of dense, glowing crystal—a "heart-stone"—that pulses in rhythm with the planet’s magnetic field. This core generates the energy needed to power their nervous system, which is not made of neurons, but of fiber-optic strands woven throughout their limbs. Thought travels at the speed of light within them. They perceive time differently than we do. A second is an eternity; a century is a breath. They remember the shape of the mountains before they were worn smooth. They recall the first star that appeared in the violet sky.

Because they are made of silica, they are incredibly durable. A fall from a height would not break them; it would merely chip them, and even then, the wound would heal slowly, the resin sealing the crack, the quartz reforming over years. Death, for a Lithos, is not a cessation of function, but a gradual slowing down. As they age, their transparency increases. They become more ghost-like, less substantial. Eventually, they stop moving altogether. They take root. Their feet fuse with the dust, their arms reach up toward the sun, and they become part of the forest. A young Lithos might grow from the side of an elder, drawing nutrients from the parent’s decomposing core, continuing the cycle.

### The Encounter

If you were to meet one, you would likely not realize it until it was already looking at you. It would stand still, indistinguishable from the Spire-Trees behind it, its mirrored chitin reflecting the violet sky and the white sun. You might mistake it for a statue, a work of art left by some ancient civilization.

But then, the wind would shift. The static charge in the air would rise. A single spark would leap from its fingertip to the tip of your glove, if you wore one. Its head would turn, not with a jerk, but with a smooth, hydraulic precision. The dark pools of its eyes would fix on you, not with hostility, but with a curiosity so intense it feels like pressure.

It would not speak. It would simply watch. And in that watching, you would feel exposed. Not judged, but *seen*. Every flaw in your soft, wet biology would be apparent to it. Your fragility. Your transience. Your need for water, for warmth, for comfort.

And then, perhaps, it would raise a hand. Not in greeting, but in warning. A subtle gesture toward the horizon, where the temperature is dropping, where the night is coming. It is telling you that you do not belong here. That this place is not for you. That you are made of things that rot, and this world is made of things that endure.

Then it would turn away, melting back into the glare, becoming once again just another shard of light in the vast, silent steppe.
