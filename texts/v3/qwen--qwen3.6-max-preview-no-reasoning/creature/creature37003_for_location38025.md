They are the **Lithovores**, though that name is a human imposition, a clumsy label for something that has never known a name. To them, there is only the *Hunger* and the *Hum*.

If you could see them clearly—if the bruised indigo light were bright enough to reveal the intricate horror of their forms—you would not see insects, nor crustaceans, nor any taxonomy drawn from the surface world. You would see evolution stripped of its vanity, reduced to pure function.

The smallest among them, the ones skittering over your boots like living gravel, are the **Sifters**. They are no larger than a thumbnail, armored in plates of chitin so dense they ring like ceramic when struck. Their shells are not colored by pigment but by interference patterns—oil-slick rainbows on black glass. They have no eyes; their heads are smooth, blind domes housing sensitive tympanic membranes that can detect the heartbeat of a mouse three rooms away. But here, there are no mice. There is only the rock. The Sifters feed on the microscopic lichen-molds that cling to the basalt, scraping it away with mandibles that look like tiny, serrated saws. They are the gardeners of this dark place, tilling the stone, preparing the feast for those who come after.

Above them move the **Striders**. These are the dog-sized shadows, the architects of the erosion. They possess six limbs, but the arrangement is wrong—two forward-facing arms designed for grasping and tearing, and four rear legs built for anchoring against the slick walls. Their bodies are segmented, flexible tubes of muscle wrapped in semi-translucent armor. Through the skin, you can see the slow, rhythmic pulsing of their internal organs, glowing faintly with bioluminescent chemicals produced by the digestion of sulfur. They do not walk; they flow. When they move, their limbs do not lift so much as ripple, creating a wave-like motion that allows them to traverse vertical surfaces without slipping. They communicate through vibration, tapping their forelimbs against the stone in complex rhythms that convey location, threat level, and mating readiness. The "language" you almost understand is theirs—a seismic syntax spoken through the bedrock.

But the true rulers of the Gullet are the **Bloats**.

You feel them before you see them: a displacement of air, a sudden drop in temperature, a heaviness in the chest. They are massive, bloated sacs of pale, translucent flesh, some as large as cars, others small enough to fit in a bathtub. They have no legs. They move by contracting their bodies, forcing jets of pressurized fluid out of posterior vents, propelling themselves across the wet stone in silent, gliding bursts. Inside their transparent skins, you can see the swirling mixtures of digested minerals, fungal spores, and the remains of other creatures. They are the recyclers, the compost heaps of this ecosystem. They consume the dead—the molted skins, the broken carapaces, the failed hunters—and process them into nutrient-rich sludge that feeds the fungal mats above. Without the Bloats, the system would choke on its own waste. With them, it is a perfect, closed loop.

And then there are the **Rippers**.

These are the predators that cause the sharp *cracks* in the din. They are slender, elongated things, all wire-thin muscle and needle-sharp appendages. They lack the heavy armor of the Striders or the bulk of the Bloats. Instead, they rely on speed and surprise. Their mouths are not jaws but circular arrays of rotating teeth, like a biological drill. They hunt by listening for the irregularities in the Hum—the stutter in a Strider’s tap-code, the slight hesitation of a Sifter. When they strike, it is with terrifying precision. They latch onto their prey, inject a paralytic enzyme, and begin to consume while the victim is still alive, ensuring maximum freshness. The Rippers are hated by the rest of the swarm, yet essential. They cull the weak, keeping the population strong, keeping the pressure high.

There is no hierarchy here, no king, no queen. There is only the **Current**.

The Current is the collective movement of the millions. It is not directed by a central brain, but by emergent behavior, a hive-mind formed not of thought, but of sensation. Each creature is a neuron in a vast, subterranean brain. The pain of one is felt by the many. The hunger of one drives the whole. When a predator strikes, the shockwave ripples through the crowd, causing thousands of creatures to freeze, to listen, to assess. When a food source is found, the signal spreads like wildfire, drawing streams of Sifters and Striders toward the bounty.

They do not hate you. They do not fear you. You are an anomaly, a static object in a dynamic field. Your heat signature confuses them; your dryness repels them. You are a stone that breathes, a curiosity that offers no sustenance. So they ignore you. They flow around your ankles, their antennae brushing your shins, tasting your sweat, analyzing your composition, and finding you wanting.

In time, if you stayed long enough, you would change. The spores in the air would settle in your lungs, colonizing your alveoli. The humidity would soften your skin. The constant vibration would recalibrate your nervous system until you too began to hear the Hum, until you too began to feel the Hunger. You would stop seeing individuals and start seeing the Flow. You would stop hearing noise and start hearing the Song.

You would become part of the Gullet. Not as a master, not as a guest, but as a cell. A warm, breathing, temporary cell in the eternal, cool dark. And in that belonging, there is a terrible peace. The anxiety of individuality dissolves into the comfort of the mass. You are no longer alone. You are never alone. You are the swarm, and the swarm is you, grinding away in the dark, polishing the bones of the world, one bite at a time.
