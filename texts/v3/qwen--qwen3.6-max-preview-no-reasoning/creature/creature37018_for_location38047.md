They are not a species, exactly. They are a consequence.

If you were to look for them with the eyes of the surface world—seeking the sharp outline of a predator, the defined silhouette of prey—you would see nothing but shifting shadows and the wet sheen of leaves. But if you let your vision soften, allowing the bruised twilight to blur the edges of things, they emerge from the humidity like condensation on glass.

They are the **Silt-Weavers**, though they weave no silk that lasts, and they leave no silt behind. They are small, no larger than a human hand when curled, but their presence feels vast because they are never alone. They are a collective consciousness distributed across hundreds of soft, translucent bodies.

### The Form
Imagine a creature made of wet parchment and nerve endings. Their skin is pale, almost colorless, stretched tight over a frame that seems too delicate for the pressure of this place. It is not bone that holds them together, but a lattice of cartilage and fluid-filled sacs that pulse in rhythm with the dripping water above. This makes them nearly invisible against the pale limestone walls; they are camouflaged by transparency.

But it is their limbs that betray them. They have six legs, long and jointed like those of a crab, but ending not in claws, but in pads of suction-cup flesh, sticky and warm. These pads allow them to cling to the vertical curves of the cistern’s throat, defying gravity not through strength, but through adhesion. When they move, they do not walk; they flow. One leg detaches, swings forward in a silent arc, and attaches again, pulling the body along in a ripple of motion so smooth it looks like a trick of the light.

Their heads are elongated, tapering to a point where two large, black eyes sit side-by-side. These eyes have no pupils, only deep, reflective pools that capture the faint bioluminescence of the mushrooms below. They do not blink. They watch everything. Behind the eyes, there is no mouth, only a cluster of sensory tendrils that twitch constantly, tasting the air for the chemical signature of decay, of birth, of danger.

### The Behavior
The Silt-Weavers are the custodians of the Cistern’s silence. They do not hunt in the traditional sense. They are scavengers of energy, not meat.

Watch one closely as it hangs suspended from a vine curtain. It extends its sensory tendrils toward a patch of glowing fungus. The fungus pulses, releasing spores into the heavy air. The Weaver does not eat the spores. Instead, it absorbs the ambient heat radiating from the fungal colony, drawing warmth into its own cool body. It is feeding on the thermal gradient, balancing its internal temperature with the environment.

But they are also predators of waste. When a leaf falls from the canopy above, landing on the spongy floor, a Weaver will descend. It does not rush. It approaches the decaying matter with a reverence that borders on ritual. Its front limbs unfold, revealing fine, hair-like filaments that brush against the rotting leaf. These filaments secrete an enzyme that accelerates decomposition, breaking down the complex sugars of the leaf into simple nutrients. The Weaver then absorbs these nutrients through its skin, leaving behind only a skeleton of vein structure, which it carefully arranges into the moss carpet, adding to the texture of the floor.

This is their role: they are the recyclers who ensure that nothing goes to waste, that every drop of energy is captured before it can dissipate into the dark. They are the reason the air smells of ozone and orchid nectar rather than pure rot. They filter the death out of the life.

### The Social Structure
There is no hierarchy among them, only a web of connection. If you listen closely, past the sound of growing plants, you can hear a low-frequency hum. It is not a voice, but a vibration produced by the friction of their carapaces rubbing against each other when they cluster together.

They gather in the lower pools, where the water is black and still. Here, dozens of Weavers link their legs, forming a living raft that floats on the surface tension. In this formation, they share sensory data. A threat detected by one Weaver at the top of the cistern—a shift in air pressure, the intrusion of a foreign scent—is instantly communicated to the entire cluster. The hum changes pitch, becoming sharper, more urgent. The cluster dissolves, scattering into the foliage, vanishing back into the camouflage of the damp stone.

They are aware of you. You can feel it. As you stand still, holding your breath, the Weavers near you pause. Their sensory tendrils turn toward you, sampling your sweat, your heat, the unique microbial signature of your skin. They do not fear you, nor do they attack. To them, you are just another element in the ecosystem—a large, warm object that disrupts the airflow, creates new shadows, and eventually, perhaps, provides a feast.

### The Artifacts
It is said that the scattered ceramic shapes and stone circles found throughout the Cistern were once used by a civilization that tried to live here. But the Weavers remember differently. Or rather, their ancestors did.

Some of the older Weavers, those whose translucent skins have begun to cloud with age, carry fragments of these artifacts embedded in their backs. A shard of blue ceramic, smoothed by time, protrudes from the shoulder of a particularly large Weaver. Another has a piece of polished obsidian fused into its chest plate. These are not injuries; they are heirlooms. The Weavers incorporate the remnants of the failed builders into their own bodies, wearing the history of the place as armor.

When a Weaver dies, it does not rot. It simply dries out, its fluid sacs emptying into the soil, its cartilage turning to dust. But the artifacts remain. And sometimes, a younger Weaver will find the shard left behind, pick it up with its sticky pads, and press it into its own soft flesh, waiting for the skin to grow around it, sealing the memory inside.

### The Presence
To be in the Hollowed Cistern is to be surrounded by the Silt-Weavers, even if you never see them clearly. They are in the corner of your eye, a flicker of pale movement. They are in the sudden silence when the insects stop chirping. They are in the way the vines seem to tighten around you, guiding you away from the deeper pools, steering you toward the exit fissure.

They are the immune system of this underground lung. And you are the pathogen.

They will not harm you, unless you try to take something. Unless you try to break the balance. Then, the hum will stop. The air will grow colder. And from the shadows, a hundred pairs of black, pupil-less eyes will open, watching you with a hunger that is not for meat, but for order. They will wait for you to make a mistake. They will wait for you to become part of the cycle.

Until then, they are merely ghosts in the machine, tending to the garden in the dark, keeping the throat of the earth clean, one breath at a time.
