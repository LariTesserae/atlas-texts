They are not named, for names imply distinction, and here, distinction is a friction that has been smoothed away by eons of damp stillness. They are the **Lithosymbionts**, though they do not call themselves anything at all. To speak would be to vibrate the air, and vibration is the enemy of their existence.

They emerge from the architecture as naturally as condensation beads on quartz. You might mistake them for statues at first—pale, rounded forms seated in the alcoves of moss-coral towers or leaning against walls of resin-encased root-fiber. Their skin is the color of the ceiling above: a milky, translucent white, thick and waxy like the fronds of the ferns that unfurl beside them. It is hairless, save for fine, downy filaments along their spines and forearms, designed to trap the thin layer of warm air generated by their own slow metabolism and the fermentation pits beneath the floorboards.

They are short, compact, and dense. Gravity in this place feels heavier because the light offers no depth cues; everything is equally present, equally pressing. The Lithosymbionts have evolved to withstand this pressure. Their bones are broad and flat, their joints wide and cushioned with cartilage that never stiffens, only compresses. They move with a fluidity that defies their stockiness, sliding rather than stepping, their bare feet gripping the slick peat-pavement with toes that have widened into subtle, suction-like pads.

They do not breathe heavily. The air is so saturated with moisture that respiration is almost passive, a gentle exchange of gases through pores in their chest and back. If you were to stand close to one, you would hear nothing—not the rush of blood, not the click of joints, not the whisper of fabric (for they wear little more than woven spider-silk loincloths and cloaks of felted lichen). You would feel only the slight warmth radiating from them, a biological counterpoint to the ambient cold.

Their eyes are large, dark, and devoid of whites. They have no pupils, for there is no sun to constrict them, nor shadows to expand them. Instead, their irises are vast, black pools that reflect the diffuse, directionless luminescence of the quartz roof. They see not by contrast, but by texture. In a world without shadows, depth is perceived through the minute variations in opacity and surface tension. A Lithosymbiont can tell the age of a stone by the way the light refracts through its microscopic cracks; they can sense the mood of a neighbor by the subtle shift in the tension of their shoulders, the angle of their head.

Communication is a silent dance of intention. A hand raised palm-up might mean *acceptance* or *sharing*; a finger tracing a circle in the air might indicate *completion* or *cycle*. They write, too, but not with ink. They arrange small, polished stones, shards of calcite, and dried fungi in intricate patterns on windowsills or ledges. These arrangements are temporary messages, left for others to find and interpret before the next rain of condensation washes them away or new growth covers them. A cluster of blue berries might mean *danger* or *change*; a single white stone might mean *peace* or *presence*.

They are caretakers of the stasis. Their technology is primitive, yes, but their understanding of balance is profound. They tend the fermentation pits with ritualistic care, ensuring the heat remains constant, neither too hot nor too cold. They harvest the glowing algae for lamps, carefully pruning the colonies so they do not overgrow and choke the light sources. They mend broken walls with mycelial mortar, guiding the fungi with gentle touches, encouraging them to bind the fragments back together. They do not build anew; they maintain what is. To build would be to introduce chaos, to disrupt the equilibrium.

Their lives are long, measured not in years but in cycles of growth and decay. A Lithosymbiont might sit in the same alcove for decades, watching a vine grow inch by inch, observing the slow petrification of a fallen leaf. They do not age in the traditional sense; they simply become more integrated with their surroundings. Their skin may take on the hue of the moss they rest upon; their movements may slow until they are indistinguishable from the sway of a fern in the draft. When they die, it is not an event but a transition. They do not rot. They are absorbed. Their bodies, rich in minerals and organic matter, are quickly colonized by the same fungi that bind the city’s walls. They become part of the structure, their bones turning to stone, their flesh to soil, their memory to the pattern of stones left on a ledge.

There is no fear here. No anger. No joy, in the explosive, chaotic sense. There is only a deep, resonant contentment, a harmony with the cold, the damp, the light. The Lithosymbionts are the embodiment of the geode turned inside out: precious, hidden, and perfect in their isolation. They are the silence made flesh, the stillness given form. And if you were to enter their city, if you were to stumble through the narrow fissure and find yourself in that bright, shadowless nave, you would not be greeted. You would be observed. Watched with those dark, unblinking eyes. And then, slowly, deliberately, one of them might rise, extend a pale hand, and offer you a single, glowing berry—a gesture of welcome, or perhaps, a warning to remain still.
