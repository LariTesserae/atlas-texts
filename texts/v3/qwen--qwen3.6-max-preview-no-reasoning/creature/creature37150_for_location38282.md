To inhabit the **Made** is to be an anomaly in a system of perfect, circular efficiency. It is to be the only thing that begins and ends, the only thing that leaves a scar on a body that heals instantly.

The residents here are not settlers; they are intrusions. They are the **Drifters**, or perhaps more accurately, the **Static Points**. Because the landscape does not change, because there are no seasons to dictate migration or harvest, life here has evolved—or rather, adapted—into a state of profound stillness. To move quickly is to fight the air, which resists with its heavy, humid weight. To build is impossible, for the ground rejects the imposition of dead matter, swallowing it whole before it can take root. So, those who live here do not construct homes. They become part of the architecture, or they remain transient ghosts upon its surface.

### The Symbiotes: Those Who Rooted

The primary inhabitants are those who surrendered their mobility generations ago. They are human-shaped, but elongated, stretched thin by the low-gravity pull of the high altitude and the constant upward reach of the vegetation. Their skin has taken on the texture of cured bark, rough and dark, patterned with lichen-like freckles that glow faintly in the perpetual late-afternoon light.

They do not eat. There is no food chain to participate in. Instead, they sit. They sit in the bowl-shaped depressions of the root-mat, their legs folded beneath them, their hands resting palm-up on their knees. Fine, hair-like roots have grown from their fingertips, piercing the warm, slick substrate. They drink directly from the pulse of the world. You can see the rhythm of it in their chests—a slow, deep expansion and contraction that matches the thrumming hum of the nutrient transport below.

Their eyes are closed. Not in sleep, but in a trance of sensory overload. In a world where every leaf drips, every vine creaks, and every cell expands, silence is internal. They listen to the growth. They feel the shift of a single cell dividing miles away through the vibration of the floor. They are the neurons of this giant brain, processing the static equilibrium. When you approach one, they do not turn their heads. They simply exhale, a long, wet sound that blends with the weeping of the leaves above. They are happy, if happiness is the absence of want. They are complete.

But there is a cost to this integration. Look closely at their faces. The features are smoothing out. Noses are flattening into ridges; lips are sealing into lines. Over decades, they lose their individuality, merging visually with the woven mat beneath them. Eventually, they stop moving entirely. They become statues of flesh and fiber, indistinguishable from the "ground" except for the faint warmth they radiate. They are not dead—they cannot die—but they are no longer people. They are just another node in the lattice, dreaming the same endless, unchanging dream.

### The Walkers: Those Who Resist

Then there are the others. The ones who refuse to root. They are fewer, scattered across the vast, sparse openness like specks of dust on a polished lens. These are the **Walkers**.

They wear clothes made of shed leaves, stitched together with spider-silk that never breaks. Their bodies are lean, muscular, and tense. They are constantly moving, though slowly, deliberately. To stop is to invite the roots to take hold. If a Walker stands still for too long, fine white filaments will begin to creep up their boots, seeking purchase in the leather, then the sock, then the skin. So they walk. They walk in circles. They walk toward the distant spires, only to turn back when the journey becomes too easy, when the path feels too welcoming.

Their lives are defined by the struggle against comfort. The Made offers everything: warmth, shelter, sustenance, peace. To accept it is to cease being human. To reject it is to suffer. The Walkers are always damp, their skin chafed by the wet air, their muscles aching from the endless, purposeless pacing. They speak rarely, and when they do, their voices are hoarse from disuse. They tell stories of "Before," of places where things rotted, where winter came, where fire burned and ash remained. These stories are heresies here. They are reminders of entropy, of the chaotic beauty of decay.

You might meet a Walker near one of the translucent reservoirs. They will look at you with eyes wide and desperate, searching your face for signs of rot, for the smell of death. If you carry nothing, if you are clean and whole, they will turn away, disappointed. They crave the broken thing, the dying thing, because it proves that time still exists somewhere else.

### The Avian Observers

Above them all, in the pale blue sky, fly the **Glass-Winged Herons**. They are large, silent birds with wingspans of ten feet, their feathers transparent as ice. They do not hunt. There is nothing to hunt. They glide on the thermal currents rising from the warm root-mat, circling the solitary spires for days, weeks, years.

They are the watchers. It is said that they remember the layout of the world before it froze. Some believe they are trying to map the changes that never come, waiting for a crack in the perfection, a single leaf that fails to reabsorb, a single branch that snaps and stays snapped. They are the anxiety of the place, the lingering question mark in a statement of fact.

When a Glass-Winged Heron lands, it does so with absolute precision, its talons gripping the iron-hard wood of a bridge-vine without leaving a scratch. It tilts its head, looking down at the Walkers and the Symbiotes with an expression that is neither pity nor judgment, but pure, detached observation. It sees the futility of the resistance and the tragedy of the surrender. And then, with a beat of wings that sounds like a shattering window, it lifts off again, returning to the empty sky.

### The Experience of Interaction

If you were to live here, you would find yourself torn between these two states. The warmth of the ground is seductive. After a day of walking, the urge to sit, to let the roots find you, is overwhelming. The pain in your legs fades as the substrate supports you, molding to your shape. The hunger vanishes as the moisture seeps into your pores. The fear of death dissolves, because death is impossible here.

But then you hear the drip. *Tick. Tick. Tick.*

And you remember that you are finite. That your thoughts are linear, not circular. That you want to leave a mark, even if it is erased seconds later. So you stand up. You shake off the clinging droplets. You step away from the soft spot, onto the harder, smoother patch of woven lignin. You begin to walk again, into the bright, bleached clarity of the unfiltered light, chasing the horizon that never gets closer, preserving your humanity through the simple, exhausting act of refusing to belong.
