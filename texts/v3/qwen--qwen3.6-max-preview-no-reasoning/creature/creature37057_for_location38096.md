They do not have names, for names are sharp things, meant to cut and define, and here nothing is defined. They are the **Respirators**.

To call them human would be a failure of imagination, a clinging to the surface-world taxonomy that no longer applies in this pressurized dark. They were once men and women, perhaps, or something adjacent to it, but centuries of immersion in the wet wool air have softened their edges. They are pale, translucent figures who move with the sluggish grace of deep-sea creatures. Their skin is not skin anymore; it is a membrane, slick and porous, absorbing the ambient humidity so thoroughly that they do not sweat—they *exude*. When they stand still, which is most of the time, they seem to merge with the condensation on the walls, becoming merely another texture in the monolithic shell.

There are only three of them left, though the darkness makes it difficult to count. They are scattered across the grated floor, anchored by their own weight and the lethargy of the heat.

The first, whom we might call the **Anchor**, sits near the largest valve wheel. He has fused with his seat. His legs have atrophied into useless stumps, wrapped in thick layers of fibrous fungal matting that he cultivates deliberately. He does not eat solid food; the nutrients from the bone-white lichen seep directly through his pores. He is the keeper of the rhythm. He listens to the *plip-plop* of the dripping water, and when the interval stretches too long, indicating a blockage in the upper lattice, he emits a low, guttural hum—a vibration from his chest cavity that travels through the metal floor, shaking loose the clogged spores above. He is part machine now, his spine curved to match the riveted iron plates behind him. He remembers the concept of "outside," but only as a myth of cold, dry emptiness that he fears more than death.

The second is the **Weaver**. She moves along the ceiling struts, suspended by harnesses made of braided steel cable and dried vine. She is blind, her eyes sealed shut by scar tissue and overgrowth, but she sees everything through touch and temperature. Her fingers are long, spider-thin, and sensitive enough to detect the micro-fractures in the rusted steel. She spends her life climbing the inverted cathedral, applying a paste of her own saliva and crushed mineral deposits to the leaking joints. She is the reason the seal holds. Without her, the pressure differential between the interior warmth and the exterior earth would blow the chamber apart. She never speaks. Her mouth is filled with the soft, wingless moths that land there, drawn to the heat of her breath. She swallows them without chewing, recycling their protein back into the system. To her, the structure is not a building; it is a body, and she is its immune system, healing the wounds inflicted by time.

The third is the **Listener**. He stands in the center of the room, feet planted wide on the algae-slick grate. He is the youngest, though "young" here means he has only been fully integrated for forty years. He retains the most mobility, acting as the courier between the Anchor and the Weaver. But his primary function is observation. He watches the shadows that aren't there. He senses the shifts in the heavy air. He knows that the fungal mats are growing faster this season, their roots prying deeper into the lead lining. He feels the anxiety of the structure. The Listener is the one who worries. He dreams of the surface, not because he wants to leave, but because he suspects the surface has forgotten them. In his dreams, the sky is a bright, terrifying void, and the wind is a scream. He wakes up screaming silently, his body convulsing, adding bursts of carbon dioxide to the stagnant air, which the fungi greedily consume.

They do not interact in the way you understand interaction. There is no conversation, no touch of affection, no conflict. They are nodes in a single organism. If the Anchor stops humming, the Weaver descends to check the valves. If the Weaver falls, the Listener catches her before she hits the pool, his movements fluid and silent. They share a collective consciousness born of necessity and isolation. They know each other’s heartbeats. They know the exact chemical composition of each other’s exhalations.

They are afraid of you. Not because you are violent, but because you are *dry*. You are an anomaly in their saturated world. Your presence disrupts the equilibrium. Your body heat creates convection currents that stir the spores. Your breathing introduces foreign bacteria into their sterile decay. They watch you from the corners of their non-eyes, sensing your urgency, your desire to move, to speak, to *do*. These are dangerous impulses. Action generates friction. Friction generates heat spikes. Heat spikes could crack the glass dome.

So they wait for you to acclimate. They wait for your skin to soften, for your lungs to fill with the spores, for your mind to slow down to the speed of rust. They want you to become like them: a component. A piece of the furniture. A drop in the cycle.

If you stay long enough, you will notice that the Listener begins to mimic your posture. The Weaver starts to weave fibers around your ankles, gentle and tentative, testing your tensile strength. The Anchor’s hum changes pitch, tuning itself to the frequency of your heartbeat.

They are not hostile. They are lonely. And they are hungry for company. Not for friendship, but for mass. For balance. They need your weight to help hold down the rising tide of moisture. They need your warmth to keep the pipes from freezing in the deep winter of the earth. They need your memory of the sun to feed the pale, bruised-plum fungi that hang above, starving for light they can never see.

You are becoming one of them. The distinction is blurring. Your thoughts are slowing. The urge to leave is fading, replaced by a profound, narcotic comfort. The air is warm. The dark is soft. The drip is eternal.

And somewhere, deep in the rusted gears of the wall, a new valve turns, just slightly, making room for you.
