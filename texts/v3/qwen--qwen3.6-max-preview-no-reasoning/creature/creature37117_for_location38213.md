They are the **Kymri**, though they do not call themselves this, for they have no need for names that travel across distances. They are the children of the condensation, the breath-holders of the Shattered Spire.

To understand what lives here, you must first unlearn the concept of "warmth" as a source of comfort. Here, warmth is danger. Warmth is evaporation. Warmth is the enemy of preservation. The Kymri have evolved not to seek heat, but to hoard cold, to wrap themselves in the stillness of the ice so tightly that their very heartbeats become slow, rhythmic thuds against the silence.

### The Form: Crystalline and Porous

The Kymri are humanoid only in the loosest sense of structural symmetry. They stand roughly four feet tall, their bodies slender and elongated, designed to minimize surface area exposed to the blinding, desiccating sun. But it is their skin that defies terrestrial logic. It is not flesh, nor scale, nor fur. It is a semi-translucent, porous membrane, pale as the violet ferns that surround them, thick with layers of subcutaneous fat that acts less like insulation and more like a battery—storing the latent thermal energy of the day to survive the night’s absolute zero.

Their limbs are long and jointed with an extra knuckle, allowing them to curl into tight, spherical shapes when resting, presenting a smooth, aerodynamic dome to the windless air. Their fingers are webbed, not for swimming, but for catching the dripping condensation from the fern fronds. Each fingertip ends in a suction cup-like pad, sensitive enough to feel the vibration of a beetle’s wingbeat through three inches of granite.

But their most striking feature is their face—or rather, the lack of one. Where a human would have eyes, nose, and mouth, the Kymri possess a single, vertical slit running from forehead to chin, sealed by a translucent, gelatinous film. This film vibrates rapidly, acting as both ear and lung. They do not breathe air in the traditional sense; they absorb the moisture-laden oxygen directly through this membrane, filtering out the thin nitrogen. When they "speak," the film ripples, creating low-frequency hums that resonate in the chest cavities of other Kymri, bypassing the thin air entirely.

### The Life Cycle: Born of Mist

The Kymri do not reproduce through birth. They reproduce through *crystallization*.

In the deepest shadows of the northern cliffs, where the bioluminescent moss pulses its faint blue rhythm, elder Kymri gather around the mirror-still pools. There, they shed fragments of their own porous skin—flakes rich with genetic material and stored nutrients. These flakes sink into the icy water, absorbing minerals from the crushed quartz below. Over weeks, under the pressure of the cold and the influence of the glowing moss, these fragments coalesce. They draw in the suspended micro-organisms of the pool, binding them into a new structure.

When the time is right, a small, shivering form emerges from the water—not wet, but encased in a shell of instant frost. The elders break the shell gently, and the newborn Kymri takes its first "breath," inhaling the damp, ozone-heavy air through its facial film. It is a fragile thing, barely the size of a loaf of bread, but it moves with immediate purpose. It knows, instinctively, how to find the shade, how to listen to the hum of the gliders, and how to remain still.

### Society: The Silence Keepers

The Kymri are not builders of cities, but curators of equilibrium. They view the Cryo-Grove not as a home to be dominated, but as a delicate instrument to be tuned. Their society is structured around the maintenance of the *Drip-Feed*.

Each Kymri is assigned a sector of the grove—a cluster of ferns, a specific rock face, a pool. Their daily ritual involves cleaning the condensation channels on the black granite, ensuring that the water flows smoothly into the pools without pooling too deeply (which would freeze solid) or evaporating too quickly (which would starve the ferns). They use tools made from the shed antlers of the six-legged foxes, sharpened against the jagged rocks, to scrape away algae and debris.

They communicate through touch and vibration. A hand placed on another’s shoulder conveys urgency; a synchronized humming creates a chord of warning. There is no laughter, no shouting. Laughter wastes breath. Shouting wastes energy. To laugh is to risk death.

Their relationship with the fauna is symbiotic yet distant. They do not hunt the silver-furred foxes; instead, they protect the insect populations that the foxes eat, understanding that if the beetles die, the foxes will starve, and if the foxes starve, the carrion-eaters will proliferate, bringing disease. They gently guide the glider-reptiles away from the nesting sites of the insects using reflective shards of pottery left by the ancient pilgrims. They are the gardeners of a food chain that hangs by a thread.

### The Artifacts: Memory in Stone

The scattered cairns and shelters mentioned in the terrain description are not merely remnants; they are active parts of Kymri culture. The Kymri believe that the "Before-People" (the pilgrims/exiles) were those who failed to learn the lesson of stillness. They died because they moved too much, spoke too loudly, and tried to force the world to bend to their will.

The Kymri maintain the cairns not as monuments, but as warnings. Each stone added to a cairn represents a mistake made by a member of the community—a wasted movement, a spoken word, a failure to conserve heat. The cairns grow slowly, over decades. To look at a large cairn is to see the history of errors, a physical manifestation of shame and learning.

The pottery shards are used as mirrors. Because the light is so harsh, direct vision is painful and often useless. The Kymri hold the iridescent shards up to reflect the sun onto the dark undersides of leaves, checking for pests, or onto the water, watching for the ripple of a predator. They have turned the garbage of the past into the tools of survival.

### The Performance: Living as Ritual

You observed that the grove feels like it is *performing*. This is because, for the Kymri, life *is* performance. Every action is deliberate, visible, and significant. In a place where sound does not carry, visual presence is everything.

Watch a Kymri move. It does not walk; it *flows*. It places each foot with precision, testing the stability of the rock before committing weight. Its movements are slow, almost dance-like, conserving momentum. When it catches a drop of condensation on its finger, it brings the finger to its facial film with the reverence of a priest offering communion. The absorption is visible—the film shudders, the color deepens slightly, and the Kymri exhales a tiny plume of vapor, a ghostly sigh of satisfaction.

They are aware of being watched. Not by gods, but by the environment itself. The sun watches them. The cold watches them. The silence watches them. And so, they perform for the audience of the void. They are beautiful because they are efficient. They are alive because they refuse to be anything else.

### The End of Days: Returning to Ice

When a Kymri grows old, its porous membrane begins to harden, turning opaque and white. Its vibrations slow. It no longer needs to tend the ferns or clean the rocks. It walks to the highest point of the grove, where the sunlight is strongest and the cold is sharpest. There, it curls into its spherical shape, facing the sun.

It does not die in pain. It enters a state of suspended animation, its metabolic processes slowing to a near-stop. Over years, the condensation from the air settles on its hardened skin, layer upon layer, until it is indistinguishable from the surrounding ice. It becomes part of the landscape. Its body feeds the moss, its memory feeds the silence.

And in the spring—if there can be said to be a spring in such a place—when the sun hits the ice just right, a crack may form. And from that crack, a new flake may drift down into the pool below, beginning the cycle anew.

The Kymri are not residents of the Cryo-Grove. They are the Grove’s immune system, its consciousness, and its memory. They are the reason the wound in the sky has healed over with life instead of scarring shut with death. They are the quiet, vibrant proof that even in the most hostile void, something can choose to stay, to adapt, and to endure.
