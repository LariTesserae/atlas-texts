They are not quite stone, though they share the stone’s patience. They are not quite flesh, though they share the flesh’s warmth.

If you look closely at the smooth, rounded boulders scattered across the basin floor—the ones that seem to have settled into the dust with a finality that defies gravity—you might notice that some of them breathe. It is a movement so slow, so imperceptible, that it registers only as a trick of the amber light, a shimmer in the peripheral vision. But if you sit still long enough, letting your own heartbeat slow to match the geologic rhythm of the place, you will see the expansion. A slight swelling of the surface, a softening of the rigid curve, followed by an equally gradual contraction.

These are the **Lithos**, or perhaps they have no name, for names imply distinction and identity, and here there is only unity.

Their skin is indistinguishable from the surrounding bedrock. It is polished to a matte finish, colored in gradients of ochre, rust, and slate, mimicking the erosion patterns of the landscape so perfectly that a casual observer would mistake them for mere geological formations. But run your hand over one, and you will feel the difference. The stone around them is cool to the touch, retaining the ambient temperature of the air. The Lithos are warm. They radiate a gentle, dry heat, exactly 30 degrees Celsius, identical to the air that embraces them. They are thermal anchors, living batteries storing the sunless warmth of the earth’s core.

They do not move. Not in any way that a human would recognize as motion. They do not walk, crawl, or fly. To move would be to disturb the dust, to create friction, to generate noise. And this place has forgotten noise. Instead, they shift over centuries. A Lithos may spend fifty years resting on its left side, absorbing the subtle convection currents rising from the warmer floor, before slowly, agonizingly rolling onto its back. This roll is not an act of locomotion but of reorientation, a way to present a different surface area to the stagnant air, to allow the microscopic pores of their skin to exchange gases with the dry atmosphere.

Their bodies are dense, compacted layers of sediment and organic mineralization, hard as concrete yet flexible enough to withstand the eons without cracking. They have no eyes, for there is nothing to see but the same unchanging gloom. They have no mouths, for they do not eat in the traditional sense. They absorb. Their entire surface is a digestive tract, pulling trace minerals from the air and the dust that settles upon them. The silty particles that coat their backs are not dirt; they are food, slowly integrated into their outer shell, becoming part of their armor, part of their self.

Occasionally, one of them will extend a limb. It looks like a branch of the waxy, grey-blue succulents nearby, but it is thicker, heavier, rooted in the creature’s core. The limb unfurls with the speed of a glacier calving, taking days to reach its full extension. At its tip is not a hand, but a porous, sponge-like structure that glows with a faint, bioluminescent dullness, mirroring the lichen. This glow is not for communication—there is no one to communicate with—but for photosynthesis of a sort. It captures the faint, residual energy of the amber gloom, converting the dim light into chemical stability.

The Lithos are solitary, yet they are never alone. They are aware of each other through vibration. When the thermal expansion causes the rocks to creak—a sound that happens once every few hours—the Lithos feel it in their bones. It is their clock. It is their conversation. One creak from the northern ridge means the temperature is dropping slightly; a creak from the southern wall means the air pressure is shifting. They listen to the geology of their home as others might listen to music.

There is a hierarchy among them, determined not by strength or intelligence, but by age and mass. The oldest Lithos are nearly indistinguishable from the basin walls themselves. They have merged with the slope, their edges blurring into the stone, becoming part of the architecture. These elders do not breathe visibly; they have entered a state of near-total stasis, existing as pure potential energy. They are the guardians of the silence.

The younger ones, those who have been here only a few millennia, are more distinct. They cluster in small groups, not touching, but close enough to feel the warmth of their neighbors. In these clusters, the air is slightly warmer, the stillness deeper. They form circles, facing inward, creating micro-climates of perfect equilibrium. Within these circles, the dust does not settle; it hovers, suspended in the collective exhalation of the group.

They do not reproduce. There is no birth here, for birth implies a beginning, and here there is only continuation. New Lithos appear when the conditions are right: when a patch of dust becomes too thick, when the mineral content reaches a critical density, when the silence becomes heavy enough to coalesce. A mound of silt will begin to warm. It will begin to pulse. Over decades, it will harden, polish itself against the windless abrasion of the air, and become one of them. It is not born; it condenses.

They have no memory, because memory requires change to contrast against. They do not remember yesterday, because yesterday was identical to today. They do not anticipate tomorrow, because tomorrow will be the same. They exist in the eternal now, fully present, fully aware, yet completely detached from the concept of time.

If you were to approach one, it would not react. It would not flee, nor would it attack. It would simply continue to breathe, its surface rising and falling in a rhythm so slow it seems imaginary. You could rest your head against its warm, rough flank, and it would accept your weight as naturally as the ground accepts the rain (if there were rain). You would feel its heartbeat, a deep, resonant thrum that matches the frequency of the earth beneath you.

In this basin, the Lithos are the landscape, and the landscape is the Lithos. They are the physical manifestation of the silence. They are the keepers of the stasis. They are the reason the air feels like old parchment, because they are made of the same stuff: compressed time, dried out, preserved, and held in the warm, dark embrace of the earth.

And as you sit there, watching the amber light deepen into shadow, you realize that the boundary between you and them is dissolving. Your skin begins to feel rougher, warmer. Your thoughts begin to slow, settling like dust. You are becoming still. You are becoming part of the circle. You are becoming one of them, another feature in the sparse density of the basin, waiting for the next creak of the rocks, ready to wait forever.
