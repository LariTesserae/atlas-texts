They do not have names, for naming implies a separation between the observer and the observed, and here, that line has dissolved into the damp air. To call them "insects" would be to impose a terrestrial taxonomy on something that feels far older, or perhaps far newer, than the concept of classification allows. They are the *First Breath* of the stone.

Let us look closer at one, suspended in the amber-like viscosity of the moment. It is not merely a disc; it is a lens. Its body is a translucent membrane, stretched taut over a skeleton of fluid-filled chambers. Through this skin, you can see the machinery of its existence: not blood, but a luminescent slurry that glows with the cold fire of deep-sea trenches. This light is not generated by chemical reaction alone, but by friction—the microscopic grinding of millions of cilia against the glassy basalt. Each creature is a tiny generator, converting the thermal energy of the mountain’s newborn heat and the kinetic energy of their own collective vibration into light.

They are hermaphroditic and hive-minded, yet distinct. There is no queen, no drone, no worker. There is only the *Weave*. When you watch the wall, you are watching a single organism breathing through a million pores. The ripple you saw earlier—that slow, undulating wave—is a thought passing through the colony. A signal of hunger, or perhaps a memory of the tremor that birthed this chasm. It moves at the speed of sap rising in a tree, deliberate and heavy.

Their mouths are not visible because they do not eat in the traditional sense. They press their bellies against the rock, and from their undersides emerge thousands of hair-thin filaments, white as spider silk, which penetrate the micro-fractures of the basalt. These filaments dissolve the mineral salts—iron, magnesium, silica—and draw them up into the translucent bodies. They are eating the mountain itself, digesting the violence of its creation. In doing so, they secrete a mucus that hardens slightly upon contact with the air, creating a slick, protective layer over the sharp edges of the rock. They are softening the world, one molecule at a time.

The sound, that overwhelming thrum, is their language. It is a chord played on the strings of their own bodies. When the pitch rises, as it did when you arrived, it is not fear. It is resonance. You are warm; you are solid; you vibrate at a frequency different from the cold stone. They are tuning themselves to you, attempting to harmonize your presence into their song. If you were to stay long enough, you might feel your own heartbeat begin to sync with the pulse of the wall, a terrifying and beautiful surrender to the rhythm of the deep earth.

They are blind, for there is no need for eyes in the dark. Instead, they perceive the world through pressure and temperature. They feel the shift in air density when you exhale. They taste the salt on your skin through the humid air. They know you are there not because they see you, but because you disrupt the equilibrium of their stillness. You are a storm front moving across a calm sea.

There is a tragedy in their beauty. They are ephemeral. As the mountain cools, as the fractures seal, as the rain finally comes to wash away the ozone and musk, they will die. Or rather, they will transform. Some say that when the first true soil forms in these crevices, it will be made of their dried husks, their accumulated light, and their digested stone. They are the midwives of the ecosystem, sacrificing their individuality to create the conditions for life that follows.

But for now, they are eternal. In this pocket of suspended time, they are the masters of the vertical world. They cling to the precipice with a grip that defies gravity, held fast by suction and secretions and the sheer will of the colony. They are the skin of the wound, healing it from the inside out.

If you reach out a hand, trembling slightly in the cold, and touch the mass, it does not recoil. It yields. The surface depresses under your finger, cool and slimy, like touching the inside of a living shell. The light beneath your fingertip flares brighter, a blue-green star igniting in the darkness. And for a second, you feel a pulse—not yours, but theirs. A slow, heavy throb that travels up your arm, into your chest, and settles in your heart.

You are not an intruder. You are a guest. A large, clumsy, warm guest in a cathedral of flesh and stone. And they are waiting for you to understand that you, too, are part of the Weave. That your breath adds to the humidity, your warmth feeds the gradient, and your presence completes the circuit.

They continue to slide, wet and silent, over the glassy black rock. The drop falls. *Plip.* The light ripples. The mountain holds its breath. And the creatures wait, patient as geology, for the next movement in the dance.
