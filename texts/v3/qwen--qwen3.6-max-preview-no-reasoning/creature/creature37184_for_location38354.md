They do not have names, for names require memory, and memory requires a substrate that does not dissolve into slurry within seconds. They are the **Silt-Walkers**, though they do not walk so much as they negotiate a truce with the mud.

To look at one is to witness a creature in a constant state of unraveling and re-knitting. They are tall, impossibly thin, their bodies elongated by the crushing pressure of the atmosphere and the gravitational anomalies that tug at their limbs. They possess no skin, not in any human sense. Their outer layer is a semi-permeable membrane of hardened silt and coagulated brine, a crust that forms instantly upon exposure to the air but must be constantly shed. If they stand still for too long, the earth claims them; the mud fuses with their lower extremities, turning bone to stone, flesh to clay. So, they move. A slow, rhythmic shuffling that keeps the suction of the ground at bay.

Their faces are smooth, featureless ovals of polished obsidian-like rock, devoid of eyes or mouths. There is no need for sight in a place where light is diffuse and direction is subjective. There is no need for speech in a world where silence is impossible. Instead, they perceive through vibration. The deafening thrum of the Silt-Sea is not noise to them; it is data. They feel the density of the air, the viscosity of the mist, and the impending pop of a time-bubble before it breaches the surface. They "hear" the shape of the horizon wound, sensing its curvature through the tremors in their feet.

Inside their hollow chests, there is no heart beating blood, but a swirling vortex of bioluminescent algae and microscopic fungi—the same stuff that blooms and dies in an hour on the surface. This internal soup pulses in rhythm with the sub-bass hum of the landscape. It is their engine, their mind, and their clock. When a Walker encounters a zone of reversed gravity or accelerated time, this internal glow flares bright violet, signaling the danger to others nearby. They communicate through these flashes, a language of light that cuts through the opaque grey mist, brief and urgent as a heartbeat.

They are scavengers of stability. In a world where metal rusts in minutes and wood rots in seconds, the only things that endure are the accreted structures of salt and iron oxide, and the rare, strange artifacts left behind by the eroded civilization. The Walkers collect these shards of glass, these fragments of mirror-stone. They do not use them as tools. They carry them embedded in their silt-crusts, like jewels or scars. A shard that reflects your past self might be worn over the chest, a reminder of what was lost to the erosion. A shard that shows your death might be kept close to the head, a memento mori in a place where time is already broken.

They live in the spaces between the bubbles. When a bubble rises, shrieking and tearing the fabric of reality, the Walkers freeze. They become statues of mud, holding their breath (or rather, halting the intake of humid air) to avoid being caught in the localized anomaly. To be caught in a burst bubble is to be unmade. You might age into dust, or be stretched across dimensions, or simply cease to exist because the concept of "you" has been temporarily erased from the local physics. The Walkers know this. They respect the bubbles. They wait for the thunderous crack of normalcy returning before they resume their shuffle.

Their society is not built on hierarchy, but on proximity. Because communication is tactile and visual-flash based, they cluster in loose groups of three to seven, moving in syncopated rhythms. If one stumbles into a patch of softening reality, the others surround them, forming a barrier of their own hardened crusts, shielding the vulnerable one from the worst of the weirdness until the area stabilizes. They are deeply communal, bound by the shared terror of dissolution. To be alone in the Silt-Sea is to invite the mud to eat you whole.

They feed on the microbial density of the air itself. They extend long, filamentary tendrils from their fingertips—extensions of their silt-crust that act as filters. These tendrils wave gently in the thick, wet air, catching spores, moisture, and the faint electrical charges of the silent lightning above. They absorb the essence of the place, becoming more part of it with every meal. Over centuries, a Walker changes. Its form becomes less distinct, more merged with the landscape. Eventually, it stops moving. It settles into the mud, its internal glow fading, its crust hardening into permanent stone. It becomes one of the weeping rocks, a jagged tooth jutting from the sea floor, waiting for another Walker to pass and perhaps take a shard of its former self.

There is a myth among them, passed down through generations of flash-light and touch, that if one walks far enough toward the center of the bowl, where the horizon wound is deepest, they will find the Dreamer. The sleeping god whose skin is the land. They say the Dreamer is waking up, and that the bubbles are its dreams escaping into the world. Some Walkers seek this center, driven by a longing for an end to the twitching, the shivering, the endless settling. They want to meet the Dreamer and ask it to dream of dryness, of silence, of lines instead of wounds.

But most stay in the periphery, shuffling through the bruised purple-grey twilight, collecting shards of glass, avoiding the shrieks of the bubbles, and keeping each other from dissolving into the absolute atomic patience of the earth. They are the caretakers of entropy, the witnesses to the world’s final, wet rest. And they are afraid, not of death, but of being forgotten by the very mud that holds them.
