To live in Orem is to be part of the mortar.

The inhabitants, known simply as the **Oremi**, do not conquer their environment; they are calcified by it. They are a people of heavy bone and dense muscle, shaped by the high gravity and the verticality of their world. To see an Oremi is to see a creature built for endurance rather than speed. Their frames are compact, broad-shouldered, with short, powerful limbs that anchor them against the pull of the earth. They move with a deliberate, fluid slowness, conserving energy in the thick air, their steps landing with a soft, rhythmic thud that contributes to the city’s eternal hum.

Their skin is pale, almost translucent, having evolved under layers of glass, smog, and shadow where direct sunlight is a rare luxury. It is mapped with fine lines of soot and grease that no amount of washing can fully remove, giving them the appearance of being made from the same polished concrete and rusted iron as their homes. When they sweat, it is not merely water but a saline mixture that smells faintly of ozone, mirroring the condensation that weeps from the walls around them.

But it is their eyes that tell the true story of Orem. The Oremi have developed large, dark irises, capable of dilating widely to capture the dim, filtered light that penetrates the lower strata. In the deeper levels, where gas lamps sputter and bioluminescent cisterns provide the only glow, their eyes shine with a reflective tapetum, allowing them to navigate the labyrinthine tunnels with ease. In the upper levels, where the sun breaks through in golden shafts, they squint, wearing wide-brimmed hats woven from recycled polymer fibers or strips of old insulation, shielding their sensitive vision.

### The Culture of Weight and Sound

Life in Orem is governed by the physics of density. Because sound carries so clearly and secrets are impossible to keep in the echoing plazas, the Oremi have developed a culture of radical transparency and communal intimacy. There is no concept of private space in the traditional sense. Walls are thin, windows are open, and conversations flow from one balcony to another like water.

This has created a society that is deeply empathetic yet fiercely protective of its collective harmony. Disputes are not fought in silence; they are aired publicly, resolved through mediation by elders who sit on the granite benches, listening to the echo of arguments until a consensus resonates through the crowd. To shout is considered vulgar; to whisper is suspicious. The Oremi speak in measured tones, their voices blending into the background drone, ensuring that the "heartbeat" of the city remains steady. If the hum stops, panic ensues. Therefore, even in grief or anger, the Oremi maintain a certain volume, a certain presence, to reassure their neighbors that life continues.

Their language is tonal and rich in consonants, designed to cut through the ambient noise of grinding gears and dripping water. They have dozens of words for different types of dampness, for the varying textures of rust, and for the specific sounds of mechanical failure. A "creak" is not just a creak; it is a *k’tik* (metal fatigue), a *sh’loom* (loose hinge), or a *gr’nath* (structural settling). To name the sound is to diagnose the problem, and to diagnose is to heal.

### The Economy of Scarcity and Abundance

In a closed-loop ecosystem where nothing is lost, value is defined by utility and history. Currency exists, but it is secondary to barter and favor. The most valuable items are those that function: a well-oiled gear, a sharp tool, a sealed container that holds water without leaking.

The Oremi are master recyclers. Nothing is thrown away. A broken pipe becomes a railing; a shattered window becomes a mosaic tile; a worn-out boot becomes leather strips for binding. This creates a aesthetic of patchwork elegance. Their clothing is a tapestry of mended fabrics—wool, canvas, synthetic blends—dyed in muted earth tones of gray, brown, rust, and moss-green. Each garment tells a story of repair, of survival. To wear new, unblemished cloth is seen as wasteful, almost arrogant. The most respected elders wear robes that are centuries old, layered with patches from different eras, each stitch a testament to their longevity.

Food is derived from the fungal blooms and algae that coat the north-facing walls, cultivated in vast, humid greenhouses powered by geothermal heat. The diet is rich in proteins and minerals, supplemented by insects—roasted beetles, moth larvae, and midge cakes—which are considered delicacies. Meat comes from the sleek, hairless rats that thrive in the pipes, raised in controlled farms deep within the city’s core. The taste of Oremi cuisine is earthy, savory, and slightly metallic, seasoned with salts harvested from evaporated condensation.

### The Social Strata: Vertical Hierarchy

Society in Orem is stratified vertically, but not in the way one might expect. The wealthy do not live at the top; they live in the **Middle Strata**, where the temperature is most stable, the air is cleanest, and the light is sufficient but not harsh. Here, the buildings are most intact, the markets most vibrant, and the community most cohesive.

The **Upper Levels** are inhabited by the **Sky-Watchers** and the engineers. These are the scholars, the astronomers, and the maintenance crews who keep the ventilation systems running. They endure the wind and the occasional rain, living in structures that are more exposed to the elements. They are revered for their knowledge but pitied for their isolation. They spend their lives looking up, studying the clouds, the stars, and the slow erosion of the skyline. They are the dreamers of Orem, those who remember—or imagine—a world beyond the plateau.

The **Lower Depths** are home to the **Deep-Dwellers**. These are the miners, the waste processors, and the fungal farmers. They live in near-total darkness, illuminated only by the blue-green glow of bioluminescent bacteria. Their bodies are adapted to the pressure and the cold, their skin paler, their eyes larger. They are the backbone of Orem, processing the waste of the millions above and converting it into fuel and fertilizer. They are rarely seen by those in the Middle Strata, but their presence is felt in the warmth rising from the grates and the hum of the pumps. They are stoic, silent, and deeply spiritual, believing that the heart of the city beats in the dark.

Movement between these strata is difficult. The elevators are few and reserved for essential goods. Most travel is via the **Stair-Ladders**, massive spiraling structures of iron and stone that wind through the city like arteries. Climbing them is a rite of passage. Young Oremi prove their strength and endurance by ascending to the Upper Levels or descending to the Depths. It is a journey that takes days, during which they encounter travelers from all walks of life, sharing stories and songs in the rest stations carved into the rock.

### The Spirituality of Endurance

The Oremi do not worship gods in the traditional sense. They revere the **City-Mother**, the entity that is both their home and their sustainer. They believe that the city is alive, a conscious organism that breathes, sweats, and dreams. The hum of the machinery is her voice; the condensation is her tears; the warmth is her blood.

Rituals are centered on maintenance and care. Before repairing a leak, a plumber will place a hand on the pipe and whisper thanks. Before eating, a family will offer a crumb to the mold growing on the wall, acknowledging the cycle of decay and rebirth. Death is not seen as an end but as a return to the structure. The dead are cremated, and their ashes are mixed into the concrete used to repair cracks in the foundation. To die in Orem is to become part of the bedrock, to hold up the generations that follow.

This belief fosters a profound sense of continuity. An Oremi does not think in terms of individual lifespan but in terms of structural integrity. A building that stands for ten thousand years is holy. A person who contributes to that stability is blessed. Anxiety about the future is low because the past is always present, visible in the layers of stone and steel beneath their feet.

### The Daily Rhythm

A day in Orem begins not with the sun, but with the shift in the hum. As the industrial fans adjust to the morning temperature drop, the pitch of the city changes. The Oremi wake in their cramped, warm apartments, the air thick with the scent of wet stone and brewing tea. They dress in their patched clothes, check their tools, and step out onto the walkways.

The streets are already busy. Pedestrians move in streams, flowing around obstacles with practiced grace. Vendors set up their stalls, displaying gears, fabrics, dried fungi, and carved stones. Children chase moths, their laughter ringing out, bright and clear. Elders sit on benches, watching, listening, judging the health of the city by the quality of the sound.

Work is manual and tactile. Hands turn cranks, tighten bolts, weave threads, and harvest fungi. There is no digital distraction, no screen to gaze into. Attention is focused on the immediate, the tangible. Conversations are long and meandering, drifting from topic to topic like the mist. News travels fast, carried by the echoes of voices and the gossip of the market.

As evening approaches, the light fades, replaced by the soft glow of gas lamps and bioluminescent cisterns. The hum deepens, becoming slower, heavier. Families gather around tables, sharing meals of roasted rat and fungal stew. Stories are told, songs are sung, and the city listens.

And then, sleep. Not a deep, unconscious oblivion, but a light, alert rest. The Oremi sleep with one ear open, listening for any change in the rhythm. For in Orem, silence is death. And as long as the hum continues, they know they are safe. They know they are home.

### The Outsider’s Perspective

To an outsider, Orem would seem hellish. The weight, the humidity, the noise, the lack of green—it would be overwhelming. But to the Oremi, it is paradise. It is a place where everything has its purpose, where nothing is wasted, where every individual is connected to every other through the shared vibration of the city. It is a place of immense comfort, of predictable rhythms, of enduring love.

They do not look up at the sky with longing. They look down at the stone beneath their feet with reverence. For the stone is their ancestor, their provider, their god. And in the endless, echoing hum of Orem, they find their peace.
