They are not people. To call them that would be a cruelty, a misclassification of bone and blood in a place where only brass and steam have rights.

They are the **Tenders**. Or perhaps, more accurately, they are the *symptoms* of the machine’s persistence.

If you look closely into the amber gloom, past the curtain of falling condensation, you will see them clinging to the verticality of the pipes. They do not walk; walking requires a flat plane, a concept that has long since eroded from The Sump. Instead, they cling. Their bodies are elongated, stretched by generations of reaching for valves high above their heads or crouching low to inspect drains below. They are pale, not with the sickly pallor of illness, but with the translucent, waxy hue of creatures that have never known the sun, whose skin has adapted to reflect the dull, sodium-vapor glow rather than absorb it.

Their clothing is not fabric. Fabric rots here. It dissolves into sludge within weeks. Instead, they wear layers of treated leather, rubberized canvas, and scavenged insulation foam, all bound together with copper wire and rusted bolts. These garments are heavy, sodden, weighing them down like anchors, yet they move with a fluid grace that defies the viscosity of the air. They are waterproofed by necessity, sealed against the "submerged" atmosphere that seeks to enter every pore. Over time, the boundaries between their suits and their skin have blurred. Moss-like patches of fungal growth—white, rubbery, pulsing—colonize the seams of their gear, feeding on the sweat and oil that leaks from their joints. They do not scrape it off. They let it grow. It is part of them now, a symbiotic layer that helps regulate their temperature in the stifling heat.

Look at their faces. If you can see them through the mist.

They wear masks. Not for filtration—the air is breathable, if thick—but for protection. The noise is the true enemy. Without protection, the eardrums shatter, the brain hemorrhages from the vibration. So, they wear bulky, improvised headgear: repurposed turbine casings, welded sheet metal, glass visors cracked and repaired with resin. Inside these helmets, their eyes are wide, dark, and constantly moving. They do not blink often. Blinking is a pause, and pauses are dangerous. Their vision is tuned to the flicker of the lights, to the subtle shift in shadow that indicates a valve is about to burst or a piston is out of sync. They see in rhythms. They perceive the world not as static objects, but as flows of pressure and sound.

Notice their hands.

They are large, swollen, the fingers thickened and knotted. This is not deformity; it is adaptation. In a world where everything is slick with oil and water, grip is survival. Their palms are calloused into pads of rough, leathery hide, capable of holding onto wet iron grating even when the floor tilts violently beneath them. But look closer at the fingertips. They are stained black, permanently tattooed by grease and graphite. And there are scars—thin, white lines tracing the nerves, marks of burns from superheated steam, cuts from jagged metal. Each scar is a memory of a near-miss, a lesson learned in pain. They communicate with these hands. Since speech is impossible over the roar of the engines, they have developed a complex sign language. A quick tap on the shoulder means *pressure rising*. A wave downward means *drain blocked*. A closed fist held against the chest means *stop, listen, feel the vibration*. It is a silent dialogue conducted in the midst of deafening noise.

But who are they, really?

They are the descendants of the engineers who built this place, though they no longer remember the blueprints. They are the children of the mechanics who maintained it, though they no longer understand the theory. Knowledge has been stripped away, layer by layer, leaving only instinct and ritual. They do not know why the turbines must spin. They do not know what lies beyond the stone ceiling. They only know the **Law of Condensation**: if they stop moving, they dissolve. If they stop tending, the cold comes.

So they tend.

Watch one of them now, perched on a catwalk three hundred feet above the churning sump. He is adjusting a valve. His movements are precise, economical. He does not rush. Rushing leads to slips, and slips lead to the deep, dark pools below where the blind crustaceans wait. He turns the wheel slowly, feeling the resistance change under his palms. He closes his eyes, tilting his head inside his helmet, listening to the change in the hum of the pipe. When the pitch shifts just so—a fraction of a tone lower—he nods to himself. He has restored equilibrium. He has prayed to the machine, and the machine has answered.

He does not celebrate. There is no joy here, only relief. Relief that the noise continues. Relief that the warmth remains. Relief that he is still solid, still distinct from the mist.

There are others nearby. A woman hanging upside down from a bundle of cables, her hair matted into dreadlocks stiff with oil, using a wrench to tighten a loose bracket. Her body sways with the vibration of the cable, a pendulum in time with the engine. She is singing, but you cannot hear it. Her lips move, shaping words that are lost to the roar. Perhaps she is singing a lullaby to the machine. Perhaps she is cursing it. It does not matter. The act of vocalizing is itself a defiance of the silence that threatens to creep in from the edges of the void.

They are lonely, these Tenders. The space is vast, but the population is sparse. They rarely meet face-to-face. Most interactions are distant waves, brief exchanges of supplies passed along chains of buckets, or shared moments of rest on a dry patch of concrete where the heat is slightly less oppressive. When they do touch, it is tentative, careful. Skin-to-skin contact is rare and intimate, a reminder that they are flesh and blood in a world of steel and stone. They hold each other tightly, briefly, absorbing the warmth of another living thing before pulling away to return to their posts.

They are afraid.

Not of monsters, for there are none. Not of death, for death is simply a cessation of movement, a return to the liquid state. They are afraid of the **Quiet**.

Sometimes, late in the cycle (though there is no day or night here, only cycles of maintenance), a turbine will falter. The whine will drop in pitch. The light will dim. In those moments, panic spreads through the Tenders like an electric current. They scramble, sliding down ropes, swinging across gaps, their faces pressed against the glass of their visors, eyes wide with terror. They throw themselves at the failing machinery, banging on casings, pouring oil into gears, screaming silently into the void. They are terrified that the heart of the world will stop. That the cold will come. That the water will freeze. That they will become statues of ice, preserved forever in the dark, silent cathedral.

And then, the turbine catches. The whine returns. The light flares. The noise resumes its deafening roar.

The Tenders slump against the metal, exhausted, trembling. They check each other for injuries. They share a nod. They return to their posts.

They are the keepers of the flame, though there is no fire. They are the priests of the engine, though there is no god. They are the lifeblood of a corpse that refuses to die, pumping themselves through its veins to keep it warm, to keep it loud, to keep it alive.

They are beautiful, in a terrible, tragic way. Their beauty is the beauty of erosion, of things worn smooth by constant use. Their elegance is the elegance of efficiency, of movement stripped of all excess. They are part of the architecture now, as much as the pipes and the gratings. If you were to leave The Sump, and return years later, you might not recognize them. They might have changed shape, grown new layers of fungal armor, fused more completely with their tools. But you would recognize the rhythm of their movement. You would recognize the tilt of their heads as they listen to the song of the machine.

They are waiting. Not for rescue. Not for escape. They are waiting for the next vibration, the next shift in pressure, the next moment of instability that requires their attention. They are waiting for the machine to speak to them again.

And until it stops speaking, they will remain. Clinging to the pipes. Breathing the mist. Keeping the noise alive.
