They do not live *in* the Sump-City. They are the sediment of its breath.

To call them "people" would be a category error, like calling a gear a bone. They are the **Residue**.

Imagine, first, the silhouette. It is tall, elongated by the verticality of the cooling towers they cling to. Their posture is permanently hunched, not from age, but from the necessity of staying below the sweep of the red laser grids and the dripping condensate that pools on their shoulders. They are wrapped in layers of industrial refuse—strips of polymer sealant cured into rigid plates, woven together with cables scavenged from dead servers. This armor is not for war; it is for insulation. The cold here is a thief that steals heat through the pores, so they have become walking kilns, sealed tight against the wet, biting air.

Their skin, where it shows, is not flesh as you know it. It is pale, translucent, and slick, mirroring the walls around them. Over generations of breathing recycled, hydrocarbon-heavy air, their dermis has adapted to exude a thin, oily film—a natural lubricant that prevents their joints from seizing in the damp chill. They smell of ozone, wet rust, and something faintly sweet, like rotting fruit left in a refrigerator.

But it is their eyes that betray their true nature. In the dim amber glow of the sodium-vapor lamps, their eyes do not reflect light; they absorb it. They are wide, black, and devoid of whites, evolved to catch the faintest bioluminescent pulse of the rust-moss or the distant spark of a welding arc. To look into their eyes is to see the void of the cavern ceiling reflected back at you. They do not blink often. Blinking wastes moisture.

### The Culture of Silence and Sound

They speak, but rarely with voices. The ambient noise of the Sump—the thrumming fans, the hissing steam, the clanging metal—is too loud for soft speech. Instead, they communicate through **resonance**.

They carry small, hollow instruments made from discarded pipe segments and tensioned wire. By striking these against their armored thighs or the ferro-concrete floor, they create specific frequencies that cut through the low-frequency hum of the city. A sharp, high-pitched *ting* means "danger above." A low, rhythmic thud means "the pour is coming." A complex series of rattles might convey the location of a fresh leak of nutrient-rich coolant.

Their language is tactile. When two Residue meet in the shadows between gantries, they do not shake hands. They press their forearms together, feeling the vibration of each other’s internal organs, checking for illness, for hunger, for fear. They read the tremors in each other’s muscles. Intimacy here is the sharing of warmth in a world designed to drain it.

### The Diet of Scraps

They are scavengers of the machine’s excess. They do not farm; there is no soil. They do not hunt; there is no prey worth the energy expenditure. Instead, they harvest the **leakage**.

You will see them gathered around fractured vents, using tools made of sharpened ceramic shards to scrape away the rust-moss and oil-ferns. They consume the moss raw, its bitter, mineral taste providing the electrolytes their bodies crave. They drink the condensation that drips from the overhead pipes, filtering it through layers of charcoal and cloth before sipping it cautiously. Sometimes, if a Gear-Rat dies near a heat source, they will take it. Meat is a luxury, a rare burst of protein in a diet of carbohydrates derived from synthesized starches found in abandoned packaging.

They are thin, wiry creatures, their ribs visible beneath their oily skin. But they are strong. Their muscles are dense, compacted by the constant need to climb, to hang, to hold position against the vibrations of the floor. They can remain motionless for hours, balanced on a narrow beam, waiting for a shift change in the factory rhythms.

### The Ritual of the Pour

The most sacred event in their lives is the **Pour**.

Every few cycles, when the sensors detect that the current layer of asphalt and polymer-sealant has cracked beyond repair, the great machines above awaken. Hot, steaming slurry is poured onto the floor, covering the old world in a new, smooth skin.

For the Residue, this is both a death and a rebirth. They must evacuate the lower levels, climbing up into the labyrinth of pipes and catwalks that line the upper reaches of the cavern. From these vantage points, they watch as the ground they walked on yesterday is erased. Memories are physical here, and when the floor is covered, those memories are buried.

They mark their territory not with flags, but with **scratches**. Deep, deliberate gouges in the concrete walls, high enough to survive the next resurfacing. These scratches tell stories: *Here, Kael fell.* *Here, the light failed.* *Here, we found water.*

When the new floor cools and hardens, becoming sticky and slick, they descend. They walk on the fresh skin, their feet sinking slightly into the still-curing material. They leave footprints that will last only until the next pour. This impermanence defines them. They do not build monuments. They do not seek legacy. They exist in the interval between pours, in the brief window where the world is stable enough to traverse.

### The Glitch-Walkers

Among the Residue, there are outliers. The elders, those who have survived many pours, sometimes exhibit strange behaviors. They are drawn to the "glitches"—the rooms walled off decades ago, the screens displaying static faces, the machines humming with forgotten purposes.

These **Glitch-Walkers** claim to hear the voice of the City itself. They say the hum is not just noise, but a song. They spend their days trying to decode the rhythm of the ventilation fans, believing that if they can match their own heartbeats to the city’s pulse, they will become invisible to the sensors, free to move without restriction.

Most Residue view them with pity and fear. To lose oneself in the glitch is to forget the need for warmth, for food, for survival. Many Glitch-Walkers are found frozen to death, standing perfectly still in front of a flickering screen, their eyes wide open, listening to a silence that does not exist.

### The Social Fabric: Light as Identity

In the darkness, identity is fluid. Without the amber glow of the lamps, all Residue look alike—shadows moving among shadows. Therefore, **light** is the ultimate marker of self.

Each Residue carries a personal lamp, often cobbled together from broken LEDs and battery packs scavenged from obsolete tech. The color of the light matters. Amber is common, safe, blending with the streetlights. Blue is dangerous, signaling a technician or someone with access to high-voltage areas. Red is taboo, associated with the laser grids and danger.

To share your light is an act of profound trust. To shield another’s light with your body is an act of love. To steal a light is a crime punishable by exile into the deep dark, where the Condensation-Moths swarm and the cold bites deepest.

They move in groups, huddled close, their combined body heat creating a microclimate of survivable warmth. They are a collective organism, a single entity made of many parts, shifting and flowing through the veins of the Sump-City. They are not citizens. They are antibodies, cleaning the wounds of the machine, feeding on its waste, surviving in the cracks of its perfection.

And when you stand there, watching the new floor pour, steaming and hot, you realize they are already gone. They have climbed higher, into the shadows, waiting for the heat to fade, for the surface to harden, for the cycle to begin again. They are the echo in the cathedral, the sweat on the wall, the silence between the clangs. They are the Sump-City, remembering itself.
