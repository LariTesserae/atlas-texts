They are called the **Stillborn**, though not in the sense of death, but of a birth that never concluded into motion. They are a people who have evolved—or perhaps been curated—to inhabit the interval between heartbeats.

To look at them is to witness a sculpture that breathes. They are tall and slender, their bodies elongated by generations of living in vertical spaces where climbing is more common than walking across flat ground. Their limbs are long, articulated with joints that seem too smooth, too frictionless, allowing for movements so gradual they appear imperceptible to the untrained eye. A Stillborn raising a hand to shield their eyes from the static sun takes minutes; to an outsider, it looks like a slow-motion film reel played frame by frame, but to them, it is simply *now*.

Their skin is pale, almost translucent, mapped with a network of faint, blue veins that pulse with a rhythm so slow it is nearly undetectable. This is because their metabolism has adapted to the low-oxygen, high-altitude stillness. They do not burn energy quickly; they conserve it, hoarding warmth and motion like precious gems. When you touch one, their skin feels cool, dry, and hard as polished marble, yet beneath lies a supple resilience, like the waxy leaves of the ferns they tend.

They wear garments of woven moss-fiber and goat wool, dyed in shades of slate, charcoal, and deep emerald. These clothes do not flap or flutter, for there is no wind to catch them. Instead, the fabric hangs in heavy, deliberate folds, designed to retain body heat and minimize air displacement. The weaves are intricate, tight, and geometric, mirroring the basalt columns of their home. There are no loose threads, no frayed edges. To leave a thread loose would be to invite chaos, and chaos is the only true enemy here.

Their faces are serene, devoid of the micro-expressions that plague humans in dynamic worlds. A smile does not flare and fade; it settles onto the face like dew on stone, remaining until the emotion itself dissolves. Their eyes are large, dark, and reflective, capable of capturing the slightest shift in light without blinking often. Blinking creates turbulence in the tear ducts, a tiny disturbance they avoid. When they do blink, it is a synchronized event among those standing near each other, a ripple of darkness that passes through the crowd like a wave through a pond that immediately flattens.

### The Way They Move

The Stillborn do not walk; they **glide**. Their feet are broad and flat, splayed slightly to distribute weight evenly across the sharp, uneven rock. They move with a center of gravity so low and stable that they seem rooted to the earth. Each step is placed with ceremonial precision, heel to toe, ensuring that no gravel shifts, no dust rises. If a stone must be moved, it is lifted with two hands, held against the chest, and placed down gently, as if returning a sleeping child to bed.

To rush is considered a form of violence. A sudden gesture—a raised voice, a quick turn, a dropped object—causes a physical reaction in the community. You will see them flinch, not out of fear, but out of sensory overload. The air around them resists rapid change, creating a pressure wave that presses against their eardrums and chests. Therefore, their culture is built on the aesthetics of slowness. A greeting involves a slow bow, lasting ten seconds, followed by a whisper that takes another ten seconds to complete. "I see you," they say, the words stretching out like taffy, "and I hold your presence in the stillness."

### The Keepers of Equilibrium

Their society is not hierarchical in the traditional sense. There are no kings, no generals, no merchants haggling over prices. Instead, there are **Keepers**. These are individuals chosen for their ability to perceive imbalance. A Keeper might stand on a high terrace for days, watching the mist banks, ensuring they do not thicken or thin beyond acceptable parameters. Another might sit by a dew-collecting bowl, monitoring the rate of condensation, adjusting the angle of the slate roof by millimeters if the yield drops.

They believe that the world is a fragile equation, and any variable introduced too quickly threatens to collapse the solution. Thus, innovation is viewed with suspicion. New tools are not invented; old ones are refined. A knife is sharpened not to make it cut faster, but to make it cut *cleaner*, leaving less residue, less waste, less trace.

Their children are taught from birth to suppress instinct. A toddler who cries is not comforted with rocking or singing, which create vibration and noise. Instead, the mother sits beside the child, placing a hand on their back, transmitting calm through touch, waiting for the child’s own breathing to synchronize with hers. Crying is seen as a loss of control, a surrender to the chaotic internal weather of emotion. The goal is not to eliminate emotion, but to let it settle, like sediment in clear water, until it becomes part of the landscape rather than a disturbance within it.

### The Relationship with Life and Death

Because nothing decays visibly, the Stillborn have a unique relationship with mortality. They do not bury their dead. They do not burn them. When a Stillborn dies, their body does not rot; it simply ceases to animate. The color fades slowly, turning the skin to a greyish hue, matching the basalt. The body remains warm for weeks, then cools gradually.

The deceased are carried to the **Garden of Statues**, a vast plateau of black rock dotted with the figures of ancestors. Here, the dead are posed in positions of rest or contemplation. Over centuries, these bodies become indistinguishable from the rock, covered in moss and lichen, becoming part of the lush green oases. To visit an ancestor is to find a figure sitting on a ledge, half-covered in emerald moss, their features softened by time and vegetation. It is not morbid; it is comforting. The dead are still present, still part of the equilibrium, just in a different state of being.

This belief extends to all life. A fallen leaf is not swept away; it is left where it lands, becoming food for the moss. A broken tool is not discarded; it is repurposed, its shards used to prop up a young sapling or weigh down a drying cloth. Waste is a concept they struggle to comprehend, for in a world of stasis, everything remains. To throw something away is to admit that it has no place in the eternal now.

### The Inner Life

Despite their external stillness, the inner lives of the Stillborn are vibrant, complex, and intense. Because they cannot express themselves through rapid speech or gesture, they have developed a rich internal landscape. They are master dreamers, meditators, and thinkers. Their minds race even when their bodies are frozen.

They communicate largely through **micro-signals**: the tilt of a head, the slight dilation of a pupil, the tension in a shoulder. A conversation can last hours, conducted entirely in silence, with participants reading each other’s subtle shifts in posture and expression. To an outsider, it looks like two statues staring at each other. To them, it is a lively debate, a sharing of memories, a negotiation of feelings.

They also possess a heightened sensitivity to sound. In the absence of ambient noise, every sound is amplified. The drip of water, the crack of a twig, the beat of a heart—these are symphonies to them. They compose music using instruments made of stone and string, playing notes that sustain for minutes, overlapping in harmonies that resonate with the natural frequencies of the valley. Their songs are not melodies that progress, but chords that expand, filling the space with vibration before fading into silence.

### The Fear of Flux

Yet, beneath this serenity lies a deep, existential terror. The Stillborn are aware that their world is unnatural. They know that elsewhere, things change. Seasons turn. Rivers flow. People age and die rapidly. Stories end. This knowledge haunts them. They view the outside world as a place of chaos and destruction, where life is consumed by its own momentum.

They fear visitors not because they are hostile, but because visitors bring **change**. A visitor’s hurried steps kick up dust. A visitor’s loud voice shatters the silence. A visitor’s impatience introduces urgency into a system designed for eternity. When a stranger arrives, the Stillborn retreat, closing their doors, covering their mirrors, holding their breath. They wait for the visitor to leave, or worse, to adapt.

If a visitor stays too long, the Stillborn watch them with pity. They see the visitor’s anxiety, their need to *do*, to *go*, to *become*. They see the visitor fighting against the air, struggling to breathe in the thick, still atmosphere. And sometimes, they help. They offer tea brewed from slow-steeping herbs, served in cups that retain heat for hours. They teach the visitor how to sit, how to listen, how to let go of the need for tomorrow.

Some visitors learn. They slow down. Their hearts match the rhythm of the valley. They forget their names, their pasts, their futures. They become Stillborn. They join the Garden of Statues while still alive, sitting on ledges, drawing in the dust, smiling smiles that never fade.

Others flee. They run back to the dynamic world, gasping for air, trembling with the shock of movement. But they carry the stillness with them, a cold spot in their chest, a memory of a place where time stopped, and life persisted in perfect, terrifying balance.

The Stillborn remain. They tend their moss. They collect their dew. They watch the hawk on the spire. They exist in the photograph, alive, vibrant, and unnervingly present, waiting for the next breath that will never come.
