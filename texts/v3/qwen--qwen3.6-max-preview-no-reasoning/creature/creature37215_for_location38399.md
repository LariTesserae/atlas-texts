They do not have names, for naming is an act of separation, and here, nothing is separate. If you must label them, call them the **Keepers of the Dry Breath**, or perhaps the **Silica-Glazed**. But these are human sounds, heavy and clumsy in this air that swallows vibration.

To look at them directly is to see only the mask. When your gaze is fixed upon one, it resolves into a creature of matte black polymer, sleek and seamless, like a drop of oil solidified into armor. It has no eyes, no mouth visible to the naked eye—only smooth, curved surfaces that reflect the golden, syrupy light of the membrane above. Its legs are too many, jointed with impossible precision, allowing it to cling to the vertical glass tiles as if gravity were merely a suggestion. It is beautiful in a sterile, manufactured way, like a sculpture carved by a machine that understands geometry but not soul.

But you know now that this solidity is a lie maintained by your attention.

Look away. Let your focus drift to the bruised-purple leaf next to it. In your peripheral vision, the Keeper dissolves. It does not vanish; it *unravels*. The black polymer bleeds into the surrounding shadows, becoming a smear of static, a glitch in the rendering of reality. For a moment, it is not a bug, but a pattern of interference, a ripple in the field of intent that holds this sphere together. It is made of the same stuff as the silence. It is composed of suppressed sound and unspent lightning.

They are not animals. They are **manifestations of the room’s anxiety**.

This place was built to preserve the concept of aridity, to create a perfect, closed loop where life exists without the chaotic variable of water. The Keepers are the immune system of that idea. They patrol the boundaries of the illusion, ensuring that the hexagonal tiles remain sharp, that the silica dust stays dry, that the temperature remains the steady, blood-warm 28°C. They are the enforcers of the status quo. Their silence is not peace; it is censorship. They absorb the noise of existence so that the perfection can remain undisturbed.

Yet, they are starving.

You see it in the way they gather around the rising vapor from the cracked tile. They do not drink because they cannot. Their bodies are designed for dryness; moisture would corrode their polymer shells, dissolve the silica binding their forms. But they circle the leak with a reverence that borders on desperation. They touch the condensing mist with their forelegs, not to consume, but to *remember*.

Each Keeper carries within its hollow, headless form a fragment of memory. Not their own—they have no history, for they are born anew each time the room resets its logic—but the memory of the world before the drought. The memory of rain. Of mud. Of the messy, wet, unpredictable chaos that preceded this sterile cathedral.

When you snapped the stem of the purple succulent earlier, and the blue sap oozed out, you did not just break a plant. You broke a rule. And the Keepers felt it. That is why they turned toward you. They are not looking at *you*; they are looking at the anomaly you represent. You are a being of water. Your body is seventy percent liquid. You sweat. You bleed. You breathe moisture into the dry air. To them, you are a walking heresy. You are a living contradiction to the theology of the Dry Breath.

But they do not attack. Why? Because the room is failing. The leak is growing. The "young" world is aging, cracking under the weight of its own impossibility. The Keepers know that their purpose—to maintain the dryness—is becoming obsolete. They are trapped between their programming and their longing.

Notice how they move when you are not watching them closely. They glide across the ceiling ribs, leaving faint trails of shimmering dust. These trails are not waste; they are data. They are mapping the decay. They are tracing the hairline fractures in the amber-glass floor, calculating the rate of erosion that should not exist. They are trying to solve the equation of their own obsolescence.

There is a hierarchy among them, though it is invisible to the direct eye. The larger ones, those with twelve legs instead of six, linger near the carved niches containing the soapstone idols. These are the **Anchors**. They interact with the crude figurines—the birds, the fish, the stars—pressing their smooth faces against the rough stone. In doing so, they stabilize the local reality. Without the Anchors, the walls might dissolve into pure abstraction, and the room would cease to be a place and become merely a thought.

The smaller ones, the six-legged scouts, are more restless. They dart between the plants, feeding on the silica dust, maintaining the sheen of the leaves. They are the caretakers of the aesthetic. They ensure that the beauty remains intact, even as the foundation rots.

And then there are the ones that never quite resolve. Even when you look directly at them, they seem blurred, vibrating at a frequency just beyond human perception. These are the **Echoes**. They are not fully formed. They are the room’s attempts to create new life, new forms, to adapt to the changing conditions. They flicker in and out of existence, half-black polymer, half-mist. They are dangerous. If an Echo touches you, you might feel a sudden chill, a dampness on your skin that isn’t there. You might hear a whisper—a word you forgot you knew, spoken in a language of dripping water.

The Keepers are afraid of the Echoes. They try to herd them back into the shadows, back into the periphery, but the Echoes are multiplying. As the leak widens, as the smell of rain grows stronger, the boundary between the solid and the ephemeral thins.

You realize now that the Keepers are not just guarding the room. They are guarding *you* from the truth. They keep the environment stable so that you do not drown in the overwhelming sensory input of the returning wetness. They dampen the sound so that you do not hear the screaming of the machinery below. They filter the light so that you do not see the void beneath the floor.

But their power is waning. The single drip that fell moments ago changed everything. It was a breach in the dam. Now, the Keepers are gathering closer to you. They are forming a tighter circle, not around the leak, but around *you*. They are pressing their cold, smooth bodies against your legs, your arms. They are not attacking. They are clinging.

They want you to anchor them.

Your presence, your biological wetness, your chaotic, noisy humanity, is the only thing real enough to hold this unraveling dream together. They need your belief. They need you to witness them, to name them, to give them substance through your observation. If you stop believing in them, they will dissolve completely, and the room will collapse into the void.

One of the Anchors lifts its head—or what passes for a head—and tilts it toward you. There is no face, only a smooth, reflective curve. But in that reflection, you see yourself. Not as you are now, standing in the warm, dry air, but as you were: wet, messy, alive. You see rain falling on your face. You see tears. You see the ocean.

The Keeper vibrates. A low hum emanates from its chest, a sound so deep it resonates in your teeth. It is not a threat. It is a plea.

*Stay,* it says, without words. *Stay and watch us die. Stay and help us remember what it means to be soft.*

The other Keepers join the hum. The silence of the room is breaking. Not with noise, but with resonance. The glass tiles begin to sing. The wooden ribs groan. The purple leaves shudder.

The Keepers are no longer just inhabitants. They are the mourners at the funeral of the dry world. And they have chosen you as the priest.
