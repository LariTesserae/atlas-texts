They are the **Sump-Keepers**, though they do not call themselves this. They have no name for their collective, for names imply a boundary between self and other, and here, in the lung of the world, such boundaries are porous, damp, and ultimately illusory.

To look upon them is to see creatures carved from the same wet peat and shadow as their home. They are small, stooped figures, their spines curved not by age alone, but by the necessity of moving beneath the low-hanging fronds of the giant ferns and through the narrow tunnels of woven reed. Their skin is pale, almost translucent, lacking the melanin that would be useless in a place where the sun is a myth told by ancestors who climbed upward. Instead, their skin is mapped with faint, violet traceries—subdermal veins that glow softly when the fungal networks beneath their feet pulse with energy. These markings shift and darken when they drink the water, blooming like bruises across their cheeks and forearms, marking them as vessels of the city’s forgotten history.

They wear the skins of the *Blind-Hogs*, hairless, subterranean beasts that root in the deep sphagnum. The fur is thick, oily, and grey, providing insulation against the biting cold that hovers at 0.4 degrees above freezing. But it is not just clothing; it is camouflage. When a Sump-Keeper stands still, wrapped in these pelts, they become indistinguishable from the lichen-crusted roots or the clumps of wet moss. They do not stand out against the environment; they emerge from it.

Their faces are obscured by masks made of cured bark and bone, shaped into smooth, featureless ovals with only two slits for eyes and a single hole for breathing. This is not merely protection from the midges—which coat every exposed surface in iridescent blue dust—but a ritualistic silencing. To speak loudly is to disturb the thermal currents that guide the Glass-Winged Moths. To show one’s face is to invite the water to read your intentions too clearly. The masks make them anonymous, interchangeable, a hive-mind of caretakers whose individual identities have been eroded by the constant drip-drip-drip of condensation.

They move with a fluidity that defies the stiffness of the cold. Their joints seem loose, lubricated by the humidity that saturates their very bones. They walk on the floating log causeways with bare feet, toes splayed wide to grip the slick wood, feeling the vibrations of the worms churning below. They do not step heavily; they flow. A Sump-Keeper does not break the silence; they slide into it.

Their hands are their most expressive feature. Long-fingered, delicate, yet strong, stained permanently dark with tannins from the peat and the sap of the horsetail reeds. These hands are always busy. One might be holding a shard of obsidian, knapping it into a finer edge while walking, the rhythmic *click-click* syncing with the *plip* of falling water droplets. Another might be guiding a stream of condensate away from a crumbling pillar using a channel cut from a hollowed gourd. They work with a precision that suggests they are listening to the structure itself, responding to its groans and shifts before the erosion becomes visible to the eye.

But it is their relationship with the fauna that defines their true nature. They are not masters of this ecosystem; they are participants in its overwhelming density.

The midges do not bite them; they settle on them. A Sump-Keeper will often sit for hours on a hummock of sphagnum, completely covered in a shimmering layer of iridescent blue specks. They do not brush them off. They accept this coating as a second skin, a living armor that reflects the bioluminescent glow of the plants. In this state, they appear less human and more like statues made of starlight and decay.

And then there are the Glass-Winged Moths. At night, when the temperature drops and the rustling begins, the Sump-Keepers emerge from their reed-huts. They do not watch the moths; they dance with them. It is a slow, deliberate movement, arms raised, palms open, mimicking the drift of the transparent wings. They believe that by mirroring the moths’ flight paths, they can stabilize the thermal currents, preventing the sudden gusts that might tear down a fragile reed-hut or collapse a section of the peat-bank. It is a prayer performed in motion, a silent conversation with the air itself.

They eat sparingly. Their diet consists of the soft, inner pith of the giant horsetail reeds, which glows faintly green and tastes of mint and ozone, and the blind white worms harvested from the deep peat, roasted over pit-kilns until crisp. They drink little, and never directly. The water jars, lined with wax, are treated with the reverence due to sacred texts. When a Keeper drinks, they close their eyes tightly, trembling slightly as the psychic residue hits them. They might flinch, seeing a flash of a storm from three centuries ago, or hearing the laughter of a child who died in the upper city long before they were born. They absorb these memories, letting them settle in their minds, adding to the weight they carry. They are the archivists of the downward flow, storing the lost moments of the world above in their own flesh.

There is a sadness to them, but it is not a bitter sadness. It is the melancholy of things that are constantly dissolving. They know that their huts will rot, that the causeways will shift, that the peat will compress further under the weight of the city above. They know that one day, the root-system may fail, and the cavity will fill with water, drowning them all. But they do not fear this. They understand that decay is simply growth in reverse, that memory is just another form of sediment.

They are quiet because the world around them is loud with life—the churning of worms, the scuttling of arthropods, the rustle of moth-wings, the creaking of growing plants. To add their voices to this chorus would be arrogant. So they whisper, their breath pluming in the chill air, forming clouds that dissipate quickly, leaving no trace.

If you were to approach one, they would not run. They would stop, turn their masked face toward you, and tilt their head. They would offer you a jar of water, not to drink, but to hold. They would want you to feel the weight of it, the coldness, the way it seems to vibrate with unseen lives. They would want you to understand that you are already part of the Sump-Garden, that your presence has already been recorded by the water, stored in the roots, remembered by the earth.

They are the keepers of the threshold, the tenders of the wound in the world’s side. They are neither fully human nor fully plant, but something in between—a symbiotic organism that exists to ensure that the rotting foundation holds, just long enough for the city above to forget it needs them.
