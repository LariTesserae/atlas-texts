They do not have a name for themselves, for names imply a separation from the world that they no longer feel. To call them "inhabitants" is to suggest they are guests in this house of steel and vine; they are not. They are the house’s immune system, its nervous tissue, its slow-moving breath.

If you were to see one standing still on a cantilevered beam, draped in the shadow of a giant fern, you might mistake them for a statue carved from oxidized copper and dried moss. Their skin is not skin in the human sense—it is a dermis of tough, fibrous hide, pigmented in shades of rust-red, slate-gray, and deep verdigris. This coloration is not genetic but environmental; it is a living camouflage that shifts subtly over months as they absorb the minerals of their surroundings. A dweller who spends their days near the high-altitude chrome spires will gleam with a pale, metallic sheen, while those who tend the lower, damp condensers bear the dark, wet stain of oil and algae.

They are bipedal, but their posture is never fully upright. They move with a hunched, deliberate grace, knees bent, center of gravity low, respecting the Rule of Anchoring. To stand straight is to invite the wind, and here, the wind is a thief. Their limbs are long and slender, ending in hands with four digits—three fingers and an opposable thumb, all tipped with hard, keratinous pads that grip smooth metal as easily as rough bark. These hands are their primary tools. They do not carry weapons, for there is nothing to hunt and no one to fight. Instead, they carry pouches woven from shed snake-skin and insulated wire, filled with the things the Lattice offers: polished bearings, shards of colored glass, clusters of sweet berries, and vials of condensed water.

Their faces are flat and broad, designed to maximize the intake of scent. The air here is thick with information—the smell of ozone means a storm is brewing above the canopy; the scent of wet jasmine means the pollination cycle is peaking; the tang of fresh rust means a structural plate is failing nearby. Their eyes are large, dark, and lidless, protected by a translucent nictitating membrane that slides across like a windshield wiper when the humidity spikes. They see well in the bright, filtered light, but they also see heat. The thermal signature of a warm pipe, the cool breath of a condensation pool, the fading warmth of a dying insect—these are the maps they navigate by.

They are silent creatures. In a world where sound travels so clearly, bouncing off hard surfaces and amplifying every footstep, noise is dangerous—not because of predators, but because it disrupts the Hum. The Great Hum, the vibration of the foundational gears deep below, is the heartbeat of their existence. To speak loudly is to jar the rhythm. So, they communicate through touch and gesture. A hand placed on another’s shoulder conveys comfort; a tap on the wrist signals danger or discovery. When they must make sound, it is a low, resonant hum that matches the frequency of the structure itself, a vocalization that feels less like speech and more like tuning an instrument.

Their society is decentralized, fluid, and ancient. There are no leaders, only Elders—those whose hides are so encrusted with lichen and history that they seem part of the architecture. An Elder might sit for weeks on a single gantry, watching the flow of moisture up the vines, listening to the pitch change of the engines. They do not rule; they remember. They know which bridges groan before they break, which flowers bloom only once every decade, and where the safe paths lie during the rare, violent storms that sweep the upper tiers.

The young are taught by doing. From the moment they can climb, they are apprenticed to the maintenance of the ecosystem. They learn to prune the aggressive vines that threaten to choke ventilation shafts. They learn to scrape the excess oxidation from critical joints, applying a paste made from crushed limestone and plant resin to seal the metal. They learn to harvest the condensation without disturbing the delicate balance of the pools. It is a life of stewardship. They understand, instinctively, that if the machine stops, the garden dies. If the garden dies, the machine corrodes into uselessness. They are the mediators between the two.

They eat what the Lattice provides. Their diet is omnivorous and opportunistic. They roast beetles over small, contained fires fueled by dried husks. They drink the sweet, mineral-rich water collected in the basins of gear-teeth. They chew on strips of cured fungus that grow on the undersides of walkways. There is no agriculture here, only foraging. But the foraging is precise, sustainable, and deeply respectful. To take too much is to starve the next layer down. To take too little is to let the rot set in.

At night, when the bioluminescent fungi ignite the lower tiers in soft blues and greens, the dwellers gather in small clusters on wide, stable platforms. They do not sleep in beds, but in hammocks woven from strong vines, suspended between beams. They sway gently with the expansion and contraction of the metal, lulled by the creaking of the structure settling into the cooler night air. Here, in the dark, they dream. Their dreams are not of escape, for there is nowhere else to go. Their dreams are of patterns—the intricate, repeating geometries of the lattice, the spiral of a fern, the circuitry of a butterfly’s wing. They dream in connections.

Occasionally, a stranger might pass through—a bird blown off course, a seed carried by the wind from a distant, forgotten tier. The dwellers watch these intruders with curiosity, not fear. If the stranger stays, it becomes part of the pattern. If it leaves, it carries a piece of the Lattice with it, perhaps a spore stuck to its feather, a fragment of rust on its claw.

They are lonely, yet never alone. They are isolated, yet connected to everything. They are the children of a dead civilization, raised by a living machine. They do not mourn the humans who built this place, for they do not know them. They only know the rust, the rain, the hum, and the green. They are the caretakers of a graveyard that has become a garden, and in their quiet, deliberate movements, they honor both the memory of the builders and the resilience of the life that followed.

To look at them is to see time made flesh. They are slow, patient, and enduring. They are the reason the Lattice does not fall apart. They are the reason the jasmine blooms. They are the breath in the steel lungs of the world.
