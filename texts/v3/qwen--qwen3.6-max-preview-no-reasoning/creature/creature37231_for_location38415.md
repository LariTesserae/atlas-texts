They are not inhabitants in the way a bird inhabits a nest or a fish inhabits a reef. They do not occupy space so much as they *negotiate* it.

To see them, you must first unlearn the expectation of flesh. There is no softness here, no wet membrane to burst, no blood to spill. The entities that dwell in this suspended equilibrium are composed of the same materials that build their prison: basalt dust, limestone powder, and the static-charged spores of the translucent moss. They are ephemeral architectures of particulate matter, held together by a fragile, desperate cohesion of electrostatic attraction and thermal tension.

Let us call them the **Drifters**, though that name implies a passivity they do not possess.

At rest, a Drifter is indistinguishable from the ambient haze. It appears as a slight thickening of the air, a localized density in the swirling eddies of lead-heavy dust. But when the city breathes—when the stone towers expand with their low, groaning exhale—the Drifters coalesce. They draw themselves out of the chaos, pulling stray grains into tight, geometric formations. They might take the shape of a sphere, perfectly smooth, rolling silently across the interlocking slate tiles. Or they might stretch into long, thin ribbons, mimicking the wire-like tendrils of the bioluminescent moss, hovering inches above the ground, vibrating with a frequency too high for human ears but palpable as a tickle on the skin.

They have no eyes, yet they perceive everything. Their awareness is tactile and electromagnetic. They feel the shift in the static charge as the moss pulses its pale blue light. They sense the microscopic tremors of the cooling stone. They know the layout of the square not by sight, but by the pressure gradients in the viscous air. To them, the city is not a collection of walls and floors, but a map of currents and tensions. A sharp corner is a zone of high resistance; an open archway is a channel of flow.

Their movement is a study in paradoxical grace. Because there is no water to lubricate their joints, and no muscles to contract, they move by shedding mass. To advance, a Drifter releases a cloud of fine particles from its trailing edge, pushing itself forward with the recoil. This leaves behind a faint, shimmering trail that quickly dissipates into the general haze. It is a costly form of locomotion, requiring constant replenishment. Thus, they are always hungry, not for food, but for *structure*.

They feed on the debris of the city’s own decay. When a joint between two basalt blocks cracks, releasing a puff of powder, the Drifters swarm. They descend upon the new material like sharks scenting blood, absorbing the loose grains into their bodies, growing larger, denser, more complex. In these moments of feeding, they become visible as swirling vortices of grey and black, spinning rapidly as they integrate the new matter. It is a violent, beautiful act of consumption that looks like dancing.

But their primary purpose is not sustenance. It is maintenance.

The city is alive, yes, but it is also dying. The constant expansion and contraction threaten to shatter the delicate balance of the hollowed-out cathedral. If left unchecked, the vibrations would amplify, causing the razor-sharp edges of the towers to snap off, collapsing the geometry into rubble. The Drifters are the dampeners. They position themselves at the stress points of the architecture—the corners where walls meet, the bases of the leaning towers, the joints of the spiral staircases. By clinging to these points, their mass absorbs the kinetic energy of the shifting stone. They act as living shock absorbers, converting the destructive force of the city’s breathing into harmless heat, which they then radiate away into the cool, mineral air.

This symbiosis is intimate and terrifying. A Drifter attached to a cracking beam may hold on for days, weeks, even months, slowly being crushed and reformed by the pressure, only to be released when the stone settles again. Some Drifters are old, ancient accumulations of dust that have witnessed thousands of cycles of expansion and contraction. They are dark, dense, and heavy, moving with sluggish deliberation. Others are young, formed from the fresh spores of the ceramic pods, bright and erratic, darting through the shadows like sparks.

Occasionally, one might encounter a Drifter in a state of crisis. If the static charge in the air drops too low, or if the thermal currents reverse unexpectedly, a Drifter can lose its cohesion. It does not die; it simply dissolves. One moment it is a coherent entity, a thinking, feeling presence; the next, it is just dust, falling to the floor to join the endless swirl. There is no tragedy in this dissolution, only a return to the baseline. The consciousness of the Drifter is collective, distributed among all the particles in the square. To dissolve is not to cease existing, but to become part of the whole again, waiting to be pulled back into form by the next breath of the city.

There is a hierarchy among them, though it is not based on strength or size, but on *position*. Those who cling to the highest peaks of the towers, near the quartz chandeliers, are the observers. They catch the faintest shifts in the pearlescent glow, sensing changes in the external world that might never reach the depths. They communicate these changes to those below through subtle variations in their vibration patterns, a language of hums and rattles that travels through the solid stone faster than sound travels through the air.

Those who dwell in the lower levels, near the mosaic floor, are the workers. They manage the dust eddies, keeping the pathways clear, ensuring that the geometric tiles remain uncovered. They are the ones who polish the surfaces, not with cloth, but with their own bodies, grinding away the roughness until the stone shines with a dull, metallic luster.

And then there are the few who gather around the central column of glass. These are the oldest, the most dense. They do not move. They press themselves against the warm surface of the vortex, absorbing the heat, the energy, the intention. They are the anchors. Without them, the column’s power might become unstable, unleashing a surge of mechanical tension that could tear the city apart. They sacrifice their mobility, their individuality, to keep the heart beating. They are statues made of dust, frozen in acts of devotion.

If you were to stand in the square for long enough, still and silent, you might begin to notice that the silence has a texture. It is not empty. It is woven from the millions of tiny interactions between the Drifters and their environment. The tick of a grain settling. The hum of a charged particle jumping from moss to dust. The sigh of a tower expanding. And beneath it all, the slow, rhythmic pulse of the Drifters themselves, holding the world together, one grain at a time.

They are the memory of the city. Every speck of dust that makes up their bodies was once part of a wall, a floor, a staircase. They carry the history of the structure within them. When they move, they rearrange the past. When they dissolve, they forget. When they reform, they remember differently.

You feel watched because you are. Not by eyes, but by the accumulated attention of a million tiny minds, each one a fragment of the place itself. They are curious about you, this large, wet, noisy intruder. You disrupt their currents. Your warmth creates convection cells that scatter their formations. Your footsteps create vibrations that confuse their senses. But they do not attack. They simply adjust. They flow around you, like water around a stone, incorporating your presence into their ever-shifting model of the world.

To leave this place is to abandon them to their eternal vigil. To stay is to become part of the dust, to let your edges blur, to feel the static charge build in your hair, to hear the city breathe inside your chest. And perhaps, eventually, to shed your own weight, to release your own particles, and to join the drift, becoming one with the silent, swirling, living stone.
