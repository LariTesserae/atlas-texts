They are not the builders, for the builders are long dust, their names dissolved into the lime of the walls. Nor are they merely the inhabitants, for one does not simply live in a throat; one is swallowed by it, digested by its humidity, and remade in its image.

They are the **Keepers of the Equilibrium**.

At first glance, from the shadows where the light fails to penetrate the heavy sodden cloth above, they appear to be part of the architecture itself. They move with a fluidity that mimics the runoff on the walls. They are tall, attenuated things, their limbs elongated by generations of reaching for high ledges and crouching low to avoid the central drain’s suction. Their skin is not pale, but translucently grey, mapped with blue veins that mirror the vein-like channels carved into the earthen mortar. When they stand still, which they often do for hours at a time, they are indistinguishable from the water-logged poles of dark wood that hold up the ceiling. It is only when they breathe—a slow, rattling intake of the thick, spore-laden air—that you realize they are alive.

They wear no armor, for metal rusts instantly here, turning to orange sludge within days. Instead, they are draped in layers of cured hide and felted wool, treated with oils distilled from the fat of the sleek, hairless rats that scurry along the ledges. These garments are heavy, saturated with the ambient moisture, yet they repel the worst of the chill. The clothes are not stitched with thread, which rots, but bound with sinew and knotted with intricate, watertight ties that resemble the knots used by sailors, though these people have never seen an ocean. Their hoods are deep, casting their faces into permanent shadow, protecting eyes that have grown large and black, adapted to see the subtle shifts in the bioluminescent moss and the glint of condensation on spiderwebs.

They do not speak. To speak is to waste breath, and breath is precious in a place where the air is already half-water. More importantly, sound travels too well here. A whisper becomes a shout; a shout becomes a thunderclap that might disturb the delicate tension of the hanging drapes or startle the chorus of thumbnail frogs. So, they communicate through the language of the house.

A tap of a fingernail against a wet stone: *The eastern cistern is full.*
A rhythmic scraping of bone against bone: *The western beam is swelling; check the tension.*
A soft, wet slap of palm against thigh: *Danger. Or perhaps, reverence.*

Their hands are their most striking feature. Long-fingered and webbed slightly between the digits—not by evolution, but by callus and scar tissue formed from constant exposure to the acidic runoff—they are instruments of precision. They carry tools made of antler and polished river glass, smooth as eggs. With these, they adjust the flow of the water. They are plumbers of a sacred order. If a drip falls too fast, they place a shard of glass beneath it to divert the stream. If a channel in the wall begins to dry out, threatening the structural integrity of the compacted earth, they apply a paste of clay and fungal spores to seal it, guiding the moisture back into the system.

They treat the water not as a resource, but as a deity, or perhaps as a patient. They watch the "weeping" with the intensity of lovers. They know the taste of every drop. The sweet runoff from the east is reserved for drinking, collected in porous clay jars that sweat in sympathy with the walls. The brackish trickle from the west is used for washing the floors, keeping the spiral mosaic slick and clean. The stagnant pools in the corners, where the translucent moss grows thickest, are left undisturbed, for they believe the fungi there digest the bad dreams of those who sleep in the damp.

There is a hierarchy among them, though it is silent and invisible. The eldest, whose skin has turned nearly the color of the blackened wood, sit near the central bone-grate. They listen to the hum of the drain. They can tell, by the pitch of the *plink-plink-hiss*, whether the storm outside is breaking or building. They are the barometers. The younger ones, more agile, climb the vertical poles like spiders, adjusting the hanging cloths, ensuring the lattice remains taut. They move in unison, a dance of maintenance. When one moves, another mirrors the motion on the opposite side of the nave, maintaining a balance so perfect that the air currents generated by their movement seem to cancel each other out, leaving the atmosphere undisturbed.

They eat sparingly. Their diet consists of the small, blind fish that sometimes wash in through the external filtration systems, caught in nets woven from vine fibers. They also consume the mushrooms that grow in the darkest crevices, and occasionally, the rats—though this is done rarely, and with ritualistic solemnity, for the rats are the cleaners of the floor, and to kill one is to disrupt the sanitation of the sanctuary.

But what truly defines them is their relationship with time. They do not measure it in hours or days. They measure it in saturation levels. They know the season by the viscosity of the air. They know the year by the depth of the stain on the northern wall. They are trapped in a perpetual present, suspended in the twilight of the constructed world. They have forgotten the sun, if they ever knew it. They have forgotten the wind. They know only the weight of water and the strength of wood.

Sometimes, an observer might catch one of them staring at the ceiling, at the heavy, bruised lung of cloth that blocks the sky. In those moments, their large, black eyes reflect a longing so profound it feels like a physical ache. But then a drip falls, landing on their shoulder, and they blink, shaking off the melancholy. They reach for their antler tool. They adjust a knot. They return to the work.

For they understand the terrible truth of their existence: if they stop maintaining the equilibrium, the house will collapse. The wood will rot. The earth will crumble. The water will flood the spiral stones, drowning the moss, silencing the frogs, and washing away the bones of the grate. They are the antibodies of this sick, beautiful body. They keep it alive by feeding it, cleaning it, and loving it, even as it slowly consumes them.

And so they remain, silent figures in oiled leather, moving through the green gloom, their hearts beating in sync with the dripping water, forever holding the throat open, forever swallowing the rain.
