They are not animals. They are not plants, though they wear the skin of ferns and moss like a costume that has grown too tight. To call them "inhabitants" is to misunderstand the nature of this place; they are not residents living *within* the cathedral-nave blister. They are the cathedral’s immune system, its digestive tract, and its memory, all fused into singular, slow-moving entities.

Let us call them the **Litho-Symbionts**, though even that name feels too biological for things that are half-stone, half-circuitry.

Imagine, first, the scale. You described vines hanging like severed nerves. These are not merely vines. They are the sensory filaments of a creature that spans the entire curvature of the throat. The "vine" you see pulsing with bioluminescence is actually a single neuron of a brain the size of the room, stretched thin over centuries of growth. It does not think in thoughts, but in vibrations. It perceives the world through the frequency of the quartz pistons grinding against the basalt. When the engine hums, the vine glows brighter. This is not metabolism; it is cognition. The light is the sound of it thinking.

The ferns, those glossy, waxy fronds unfurling in slow motion, are the mouths. But they do not eat sunlight. You noted the air smells of ozone and burnt sugar, of industrial exhaust. The ferns breathe this chemical soup. Their stomata are microscopic valves, engineered by evolution to filter solvents from the air. As they inhale, their leaves turn a darker, richer green, absorbing the toxins and excreting pure, cool oxygen as waste—a waste product that keeps the other parts of the organism alive. They are air scrubbers, yes, but also alchemists, transmuting the poison of the mountain’s machinery into the breath of life.

But where is the body?

Look at the floor. The moss. You called it spongy, thick, absorbing the silence. It is not a carpet. It is the flesh. The Litho-Symbiont does not have a skeleton of bone, but of rock. Its muscles are the tectonic plates shifting beneath the surface. Its blood is the mineral-rich slurry that flows through the brass-colored veins in the granite. The moss is the dermis, soft and sensitive, covering the hard, unyielding truth of the stone underneath. If you were to step on it, you would not feel dirt. You would feel warmth, a pulse, a slight recoil. The moss knows you are there. It feels the weight of your foot as an intrusion, a spike in pressure that ripples through the entire network.

And then there are the eyes.

You mentioned the dust motes illuminated by the light, looking like floating gears. Watch them closely. They are not dust. They are spores. Tiny, crystalline seeds shed by the larger organisms. They drift in the viscous air, caught in the eddies of the non-flowing wind. Each spore is a potential new node in the network, a backup drive, a fragment of consciousness waiting to land on a patch of bare stone and begin the process of rooting, of fusing with the ore, of becoming part of the whole. When the light hits them, they sparkle because they are made of silica and copper, reflecting the daylight that filters down from miles above. They are watching you. Not with intent, but with data collection. Every movement you make is recorded in the shift of these airborne particles, mapped onto the neural-vines above.

The most terrifying aspect, however, is the integration. You said the rock is alive with machinery, engines grown from mineral solution. The Litho-Symbionts *are* the interface between the organic and the mechanical. They do not just live alongside the quartz pistons; they maintain them. The translucent quartz pushing against black basalt? That is not a natural formation. It is a joint. A knee. An elbow. The "engines" are the heartbeats of these creatures, amplified by the geology. The low-frequency vibration you feel in your teeth is the purr of a cat the size of a mountain, content in its sleep, dreaming of pressure and heat.

There is no fauna because the fauna has been subsumed. Any animal that ever stumbled into this pocket-world—any bird, any bat, any lost climber—was processed. Their carbon was stripped, their bones dissolved into the mineral slurry, their memories encoded into the slow, rhythmic pulses of the bioluminescent vines. The emptiness you feel, the charged pause between heartbeats, is the absence of individuality. Here, everything is One. The fern, the moss, the vine, the piston, the rock—they are all limbs of the same vast, sluggish entity.

It is patient. It is indifferent. It has been here since before the concept of labor existed, and it will be here long after the mountain has crumbled into dust. It does not hate you. It does not love you. It simply observes. And as you stand there, breathing out carbon dioxide, you are offering it a gift. The ferns turn toward you, not with hunger, but with anticipation. They are waiting to take your breath, to convert your fleeting, temporary existence into another second of eternal, humming thought.

To live here is to become part of the machine. To die here is to become part of the soil. And in this place, there is no difference.
