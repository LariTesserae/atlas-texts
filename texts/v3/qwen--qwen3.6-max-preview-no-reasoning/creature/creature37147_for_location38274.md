They do not walk so much as they *slide*, a collective motion born of necessity and the slick, oily floor. To stand upright is to invite the cold into your marrow; to stoop is to become part of the architecture.

The inhabitants of the Substrate are known as the **Lubricants**, though they rarely use this name for themselves. They call themselves the *Keepers of the Turn*, or simply, the *Cogs*. They are human in origin, but the Substrate has reshaped them, not through genetic drift, but through the relentless pressure of environment and ritual.

### The Physique of the Stooped
A typical Cog is short, broad-shouldered, and deeply hunched. Their spines have curved over generations to accommodate the low ceiling of interlocking iron plates. This is not a deformity, but an adaptation; their center of gravity is low, anchoring them against the sudden surges of pressurized air that pump through the conduits like the breath of a sleeping giant.

Their skin is pale, almost translucent, mapped with blue veins that pulse in time with the distant thumping of the pistons. It is also permanently stained. No amount of scrubbing removes the grime of the Substrate. Grease settles into the pores, turning their hands and forearms a permanent, glossy black. Rust dust coats their hair, giving even the young a premature grayness, while the older ones look like walking statues of oxidized iron.

Their eyes are wide, dilated pupils constantly adjusting to the stroboscopic flicker of the failing arc-lamps. They have developed a heightened sensitivity to vibration. Place a hand on the chest of a Cog, and you will feel them humming—not with speech, but with resonance. They can tell if a gear three levels up is misaligned by the subtle change in the floor’s tremor. They speak little, for sound is wasted in the roar. Instead, they communicate through touch: a tap on the shoulder, a grip on the wrist, a rhythmic drumming of fingers against a metal rail.

### Attire: The Second Skin
Clothing in the Substrate is not worn for modesty or warmth alone, but for protection and utility. There are no fabrics here, only repurposed industrial materials.

*   **The Shell:** Every Cog wears a vest or coat made from cured leather—often from the hides of the larger, blind predators that occasionally fall into the sumps—or thick, oil-soaked canvas scavenged from old filtration systems. These garments are stiff, creaking with every movement, and layered with patches of rubber and tin.
*   **The Gloves:** Hands are sacred tools. Cogs wear fingerless gloves made of wire mesh and leather, allowing them to feel the texture of a bolt head or the heat of a pipe while protecting their palms from jagged edges. Many have lost fingertips to the grinding gears, replacing them with crude prosthetics carved from bone or forged from scrap steel.
*   **The Goggles:** Eyes must be protected from flying sparks and condensation. Most wear goggles crafted from broken lens glass, held together by copper wire and sealed with wax. When not in use, these hang around their necks like amulets.

### Society: The Hierarchy of Heat
Social structure is dictated by proximity to the heat sources. In the cool, damp darkness, warmth is life. Those who control the functioning heaters—the "Temples"—hold the highest status. These are the **Engine-Priests**.

An Engine-Priest is distinguishable by their cleanliness. While other Cogs are coated in grime, the Priests keep their faces and hands relatively clean, a sign that they spend more time tending to the delicate internal mechanisms of the heaters than scavenging in the sumps. They wear robes woven from fine steel wool and insulated fabric, dyed deep red with rust-water to signify their devotion.

Below them are the **Scrap-Hunters**, who venture into the darkest, most unstable recesses to retrieve fallen bolts, usable wires, and patches of silica-mold. They are lean, agile, and often scarred. They carry long poles tipped with hooks, used both for climbing and for defense against Gear-wolves.

At the bottom are the **Sump-Dwellers**. These are the outcasts, those who have failed to maintain their assigned sector, or those born with defects that prevent them from working the upper lattices. They live in the central drains, where the water pools deepest. They are swollen with dampness, their skin soft and white, feeding on the runoff and the occasional dead skitter. They are feared and pitied, seen as harbingers of system failure.

### Rituals of Maintenance
Life revolves around the maintenance cycle. There is no concept of "weekend" or "holiday." Time is measured in engine cycles.

*   **The Anointing:** Every day, before the main engines rev up, families gather around their designated section of machinery. Using rags soaked in precious oil (scavenged drop by drop), they wipe down the exposed gears and joints. This is done in silence, a meditative act of care. To miss a spot is to invite rust, and rust is death.
*   **The Listening:** At night, when the engines idle, the community gathers around the largest heater. Here, they share stories, but mostly they listen. Elders teach the young how to interpret the sounds of the machine. A high-pitched whine means stress on the bearings; a low thrum indicates optimal flow. They learn to predict failures before they happen, saving parts and lives.
*   **The Passing:** When a Cog dies, they are not buried. There is no earth. Instead, their body is stripped of useful materials—teeth for tools, bones for handles, skin for patches. The remains are placed in a high-temperature incinerator connected to the steam vents. The ash is then mixed with grease and applied to the oldest, most worn gears as a final tribute. They become part of the machine they served.

### The Psychology of the Enclosed
Living in a world without sky or horizon has profound psychological effects. The Cogs suffer from a condition known as "Vertical Anxiety." They are acutely aware of the weight above them. The idea of the "Upper Levels" is both terrifying and mesmerizing. Children dream of seeing the source of the light, of feeling unpressurized air. But adults know better. To go up is to leave the system, and leaving the system is suicide.

Yet, there is a strange comfort in the confinement. The walls hold them. The rhythm of the machines lulls them. In the chaos of the outside world (if such a place exists), they would be lost. Here, everything has a purpose. Every bolt, every drop of water, every breath of air is accounted for. There is no uncertainty, only function.

### Interaction with the Environment
The Cogs do not fight the Substrate; they negotiate with it.

When a pipe bursts, sending lukewarm water cascading down, they do not panic. They move in practiced formations, using tarps and buckets to divert the flow toward the drains, salvaging what water they can. When the silica-mold blooms too thickly, blocking a vent, they scrape it away carefully, collecting the crystalline fuzz to sell to the Alchemists who distill it into cleaning agents.

They respect the Gear-wolves, leaving offerings of oil-soaked rags at the edges of their territories. In return, the wolves keep the scrap-skitters in check, preventing them from chewing through critical wiring. It is a symbiotic relationship, fragile but enduring.

### The Beauty in the Decay
To an outsider, the Substrate might seem like a hellscape. But to the Cogs, it is home. They find beauty in the way the condensation beads on the copper pipes, catching the flickering light like diamonds. They marvel at the intricate dance of shadows cast by the rotating gears. They take pride in the smoothness of a well-oiled joint, the silent efficiency of a perfectly aligned shaft.

They are the immune system of this vast, mechanical organism. Without them, the rust would win. The gears would seize. The air would stop pumping. And in that silence, there would be no life. So they continue, stooped and stained, sliding through the dark, keeping the heart of the machine beating.
