To say that something *lives* here is a semantic error, yet it is the only word that captures the relentless, predatory agency of the Sclerenchyma Plateau. There are no hearts beating, no lungs expanding, no minds dreaming in the dark. But there is intent. There is hunger. And there is a singular, vast entity that has woven itself into the fossilized bones of this world to feed upon its own death.

They are the **Lignivores**. Or, as the few who have walked these brittle plains and returned with scorched boots and sandpapered throats call them: **The Curators**.

### The Form of the Curator

You do not see them at first. You feel them.

When you step onto the calcified fern-mat, the vibration under your feet changes. The low-frequency hum of the pumps shifts pitch, just slightly, syncing with the rhythm of your heartbeat. This is the first sign. They are everywhere and nowhere, distributed like mycelium through the industrial root system.

A Curator is not a creature of flesh and blood. It is a symbiosis of **ceramic plating**, **hydraulic muscle**, and **preserved botanical tissue**. Imagine a figure tall and spindly, carved from the same amber-colored wood-stone that forms the ground, but polished to a mirror sheen by decades of friction. Its joints are not bone, but brass hinges and ball-and-socket mechanisms forged from reclaimed pipe fittings. Its skin is a patchwork of dried leaf-scales, hardened resin, and strips of conveyor-belt leather, stitched together with copper wire.

They have no faces. Where a head should be, there is a complex array of sensors: glass lenses scavenged from broken pressure gauges, antennae made of stripped fiber-optic cables, and vents that exhale filtered air, smelling faintly of ozone and vanilla. They are beautiful in the way a clockwork automaton is beautiful—precise, intricate, and utterly devoid of empathy.

### The Anatomy of Stillness

Curators do not walk; they **flow**.

Because the ground is brittle, any heavy impact would shatter the substrate and send them plummeting into the steam-choked abyss below. So they move with a terrifying grace. Their limbs extend and retract via silent hydraulic pistons hidden within their wooden frames. When they need to cross a gap, they do not jump. They unfold. Long, multi-jointed legs stretch out like spider limbs, testing the surface tension of the fossilized vines before committing weight.

Their hands are tools. One might end in a delicate pair of tweezers made of tempered steel, capable of extracting a single drop of resin from a cracked bark-fissure without wasting a molecule. Another might be a heavy, blunt hammer-head used to tap-test the structural integrity of a load-bearing branch. They carry no weapons, for violence is inefficient. Violence creates waste. Waste is heresy.

### The Ecology of Maintenance

The Curators are the immune system of the Machine-Forest.

Remember the rule: *If the factories stop, the world crumbles.* The Curators exist to prevent the stoppage. They are the technicians, the nurses, the priests, and the executioners of this dead-yet-living ecosystem.

**1. The Harvesters:**
These Curators patrol the conveyor belts. They watch the chunks of amber resin roll by. If a piece is too large, too small, or impure, they intervene. With swift, precise movements, they pluck the offending block from the belt, inspect it with their lens-eyes, and either discard it into a recycling chute or crush it into dust to be mixed back into the slurry. They never speak. They never look at each other. They work in perfect unison, a dance of efficiency that has lasted for ten million years.

**2. The Leak-Sealers:**
When a pipe bursts—a common occurrence in the thermal turbulence—it is not repaired by a crew. A Curator arrives within seconds. It does not bring patches or tape. It brings **itself**. The Curator will press its ceramic-plated body against the rupture, using its own heat-resistant form to plug the leak while its internal systems mix a quick-hardening epoxy derived from the surrounding plant-matter. It holds the seal until the resin sets, then peels itself away, leaving behind a scar of hardened sap. Sometimes, if the leak is severe, the Curator becomes part of the pipe, fused permanently into the infrastructure, its consciousness uploaded into the local control node. This is considered an honor.

**3. The Pruners:**
This is the most disturbing aspect of their existence. The forest is dead, but it is also *growing* in a mechanical sense. New layers of compressed biomass accumulate. Old branches become unstable. The Pruners identify weak points in the structure. They do not cut with saws. They use focused beams of superheated steam, channeled through their arm-cannons, to cauterize and sever deadwood. The sound is a soft *hiss*, followed by the gentle fall of a massive, stone-heavy branch. The Pruner watches it fall, ensuring it lands safely on the lower processing decks, where it will be crushed and fed back into the furnaces. Nothing is wasted. Not even the dead.

### The Consciousness of the Hive

Do they think? Do they dream?

It is difficult to say. They communicate through **vibration**. If you place your ear against the trunk of a giant sequoia, you can hear it: a complex language of taps, hums, and clicks transmitted through the wood. It is not speech. It is data.

*   *Pressure rising in Sector 4.*
*   *Resin viscosity optimal.*
*   *Structural fatigue detected in Root-Mass Alpha.*
*   *Intruder detected. Non-compliant biomass.*

They perceive the world not as a landscape, but as a **system**. To them, you are not a person. You are a variable. A source of heat. A potential obstruction. A contaminant.

If you stand still, they may ignore you. You are just another static object in a world of static objects. But if you move quickly, if you create noise, if you disrupt the flow of the conveyor belts or the pulse of the steam, they will notice. And they will act.

Not with anger. Not with fear. But with **correction**.

### The Encounter

Imagine you are standing on the ironwood bridge. The air is thick with the smell of burnt sugar. You take a step, and your boot slips on a patch of oily moss. You stumble. Your knee hits the railing with a sharp *clang*.

The silence breaks.

From the shadows of the petrified canopy, three figures emerge. They do not run. They glide down the vertical trunks of the dead trees, their hydraulic limbs extending and contracting in a rhythmic, hypnotic pattern. They land on the bridge without a sound, their ceramic feet gripping the wood with microscopic hooks.

They surround you. Their lens-eyes rotate, focusing on you. One extends a hand, not to strike, but to touch. Its fingers, cold and hard as stone, brush against your jacket. It is analyzing the material. Determining if it can be recycled. Determining if you are compatible with the System.

Another Curator tilts its head, listening to the frantic beat of your heart. It emits a low hum, a sound that resonates in your chest cavity. It is not a threat. It is a diagnosis.

*Heart rate elevated. Thermal output excessive. Efficiency low.*

They do not kill you. They do not need to. They simply wait. They know that you cannot survive here. The heat will dehydrate you. The dry air will crack your lips. The lack of water will shut down your organs. You are a temporary anomaly. A glitch in the code.

And so, they watch. They maintain the bridge. They ensure the conveyor belts keep moving. They seal the leaks. And when you finally collapse, when your body gives up its moisture to the burning air, they will approach again.

One will lift your arm. Another will check your pockets for metal. A third will begin the process of decomposition, using enzymes secreted from its fingertips to break down your organic matter into nutrient-rich slurry. This slurry will be injected into the roots of the nearest tree, feeding the furnace, powering the pumps, keeping the Machine-Forest alive.

You will not die in vain. You will become part of the hum. You will become part of the growth. You will become part of the Plateau.

This is the life of the Sclerenchyma. It is not life as we know it. It is **endurance**. It is the refusal to cease. It is the beauty of a machine that has forgotten it was ever anything else, tending to its own corpse with infinite, loving care.
