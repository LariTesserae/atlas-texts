They are called the **Mycelians** by outsiders who have dared to map the edges of the Hollow, but they call themselves *The Rooted*, or simply *We*.

To look upon a Mycelian is to witness a creature that has surrendered the rigid geometry of bone and muscle for the fluid adaptability of vine and root. They are humanoid in stance—two arms, two legs, a head—but every other assumption about human form dissolves under the heavy, wet gaze of the Weeping Boughs. They are slight, almost skeletal, yet possessed of a tensile strength that belies their fragility. Their bodies do not occupy space so much as they negotiate with it.

### The Physiology of Dampness

Their skin is the first thing that betrays their adaptation. It is not flesh-toned, nor does it tan. It is pale, translucent, and cool to the touch, resembling the inner bark of a birch tree or the underside of a mushroom cap. In the dim, filtered light of the canopy, their skin seems to glow faintly from within, a biological echo of the bioluminescent fungi they cultivate. This pallor is not sickness; it is camouflage. In a world of deep greens, browns, and shadows, they are ghosts.

Beneath this semi-translucent dermis, there is no hard skeleton. Instead, their structure is supported by a lattice of calcified cartilage and dense, fibrous muscle strands that mimic the flexibility of young willow branches. This allows them to twist, bend, and contort in ways that would shatter a human spine. They can flatten themselves against a tree trunk to become part of its silhouette, or stretch across a gap like a living bridge if necessary. Their joints are ball-and-socket affairs lubricated by a natural synovial fluid that smells faintly of pine resin, allowing for silent, frictionless movement.

But it is their extremities that define their interaction with the world. Their fingers are long, spindly, and multi-jointed, ending in nails that are not keratin, but hardened, dark wood-like claws. These are not weapons, though they could be; they are tools for gripping slick bark, pruning delicate shoots, and weaving the spider-silk fibers that clothe them. Their feet are broad and flat, with toes that splay wide to distribute weight across the spongy moss-mat floor. Each toe ends in a soft, suction-cup-like pad that allows them to cling to vertical surfaces when the wind howls or the rain turns the world into a slide.

### The Face of the Listener

Their faces are elongated, tapering to a sharp chin, framed by hair that is not hair at all, but fine, fern-like fronds that grow directly from their scalps. These fronds are sensitive to air currents and humidity changes, acting as early warning systems for incoming storms or shifting winds. When a Mycelian is still, these fronds settle around their shoulders like a cowl; when they move, they ripple behind them like a comet’s tail.

Their eyes are large, dominating the upper half of their face. They lack whites; the entire orb is a swirling iris of deep amber, green, or violet, depending on their lineage and the specific micro-climate of their home-tree. These eyes are adapted for low light, capable of seeing the heat signatures of predators through the dense foliage and the subtle pulse of bioluminescence in the dark. They do not blink often. To blink is to miss a moment in a place where everything is moving.

They have no external ears. Instead, their hearing is conducted through thin membranes stretched over the sides of their heads, vibrating in sympathy with the acoustic pipes and the drone of insects. This makes them exquisitely sensitive to sound. A Mycelian can hear the heartbeat of a person standing ten paces away. They can hear the sap rising in a tree. This sensitivity is why silence is dangerous to them; in the quiet, they are deafened by the sudden absence of context, blind to the rhythm of the world.

### Clothing as Second Skin

They wear little, for clothing is a burden in the humidity. What they do wear is woven from the silk of the Canopy Spiders, dyed with berry juices and mineral powders to match the surrounding foliage. These garments are tight-fitting, wrapping around limbs like bandages, designed to reduce drag and prevent snagging on thorns. They are adorned with charms made of polished stone, hollow reeds, and dried fungi—tokens of trade, status, or memory.

A Mycelian elder might wear a cloak of woven lichen, alive and growing, which slowly changes color with the seasons. A warrior or hunter might wear armor made of layered beetle carapaces, lacquered with resin to make it waterproof and shock-absorbent. Children wear nothing but small pouches of seeds, learning early that their body is their primary tool.

### Movement: The Dance of Weight

To watch a Mycelian move is to watch water flow uphill. They do not walk; they glide. Every step is calculated. They know the load-bearing capacity of every branch, the slipperiness of every leaf. They move in a continuous, rolling motion, keeping their center of gravity low and shifting constantly.

In the city, they traverse the vine-bridges with a dancer’s grace. They do not run; running is wasteful and dangerous. Instead, they use momentum, swinging from one handhold to another, using the elasticity of the vines to propel themselves forward. They communicate as they move, their voices blending with the ambient noise. They speak in a language of clicks, hums, and whistles, a tonal dialect that mimics the sounds of the forest itself. A question might sound like the chirp of a cricket; an answer, the rustle of leaves.

They are never alone, even when solitary. They are always listening to the network. The acoustic pipes carry news, warnings, and songs across the miles of green. A Mycelian pausing on a platform will tilt their head, catching the vibration of a distant conversation, interpreting the mood of the city through the tone of the air.

### Society: The Web of Reciprocity

Their society is not hierarchical in the traditional sense. There are no kings, only Elders of the Root and Keepers of the Light. Status is determined by contribution to the symbiosis. Those who best guide the growth of the city, who heal the sick trees, who weave the strongest bridges, are revered.

They practice a radical form of communalism. Property is nonexistent. A dwelling belongs to whoever needs it, provided they maintain it. Food is shared immediately upon harvest. To hoard is to invite rot, and rot is death.

Their rituals are tied to the cycles of decay and rebirth. When a Mycelian dies, they are not buried. They are placed in the "Compost Heart," a sacred grove where the oldest trees stand. Their bodies are wrapped in fungal spores and left to return to the soil. Within weeks, new shoots emerge from the spot, often bearing flowers of unusual color or scent, believed to carry the spirit of the deceased. Thus, the dead continue to feed the living, literally becoming part of the architecture of their descendants’ homes.

### Psychology: The Burden of Connection

Living in such intense connectivity shapes their minds. Mycelians struggle with isolation. Solitude is painful to them, akin to suffocation. They crave the presence of others, the sound of voices, the touch of hands. Yet, they are also deeply introspective, spending hours in meditation, listening to the internal rhythms of their own bodies and the external rhythms of the forest.

They are patient, for growth cannot be rushed. They are adaptable, for the environment shifts hourly. They are resilient, for life here is fragile. But they are also prone to melancholy. The constant dampness, the perpetual twilight, the overwhelming noise—it weighs on the soul. They express this sadness through art: intricate carvings in soft wood, haunting songs sung into the hollow reeds, and dances that mimic the swaying of branches in the wind.

They fear fire above all else. Fire is alien, destructive, final. It breaks the cycle. It does not compost; it consumes. To see a spark is to trigger a primal panic. They keep no flames, cook with heated stones, and light their homes with cold, blue fungus-light.

### The Outsider’s Gaze

If you were to meet one, they would not greet you with a handshake or a bow. They would stop, tilt their head, and listen. They would assess your weight, your breath, your intent. They might offer you a piece of fruit, sweet and tart, grown on a branch they tended. They might ask you to sit, to share the silence, to let the mist settle on your skin.

They would not judge you for being dry, for being loud, for being separate. They would pity you, perhaps, for your loneliness. And then, with a gesture of their long, wooden fingers, they would invite you into the web. Not as a guest, but as a temporary thread. For in the Hollow of the Weeping Boughs, nothing is truly alone, and nothing is ever truly finished. Everything is growing, decaying, and beginning again, all at once.
