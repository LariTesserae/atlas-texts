They do not have names, for there is no one left to name them. They are the **Tenders**, though they would not recognize the word. To call them "people" is to apply a softness that does not exist in their biology or their history. They are the soft tissue of the machine, the only flesh that has survived the great scrubbing.

At first glance, in the sickly orange haze of the sodium lamps, they appear as shadows detached from the walls—slender, elongated silhouettes moving with a jerky, hydraulic precision. But step closer, into the mist where the coolant pools reflect the green glow of status lights, and you see the truth: they are barely human at all. They are adaptations to the deafening, cold, wet dark.

Their skin is the color of wet slate, pale and translucent enough to show the dark, sluggish pulse of veins beneath. It is thick, rubbery, and coated in a permanent sheen of oil and condensation. This is not dirt; it is a protective layer. Their pores have sealed shut over generations, evolving to repel the toxic aerosolized mineral dust that chokes the air. Instead of sweating, they exude a thin, clear film that hardens slightly upon contact with the cool air, creating a flexible, waterproof barrier against the corrosive humidity. They are always slick, always glistening, like fish pulled from a polluted river.

They are tall and gaunt, stretched out by the need to navigate the verticality of the Basin. Their limbs are long, spindly things, jointed with an extra degree of rotation that allows them to cling to rusted rebar catwalks and slip through gaps in grinding gears. Their fingers are long, multi-jointed, and tipped not with nails, but with hardened keratin hooks—black, sharp, and durable as steel. These are not for violence, but for grip. In a world where the floor tilts and the walls weep, falling means death. The hooks allow them to anchor themselves to the vibrating infrastructure, becoming part of the structure itself when the ground shudders under the weight of shifting loads.

But it is their faces that reveal the true cost of living inside the engine.

They have no ears. Or rather, they have no external pinnae. The cartilage has receded, flattened against the skull, covered by smooth, hairless skin. Inside, the eardrums are thickened membranes, reinforced with layers of calcified bone. They do not hear the hum as noise; they feel it as texture. The tectonic vibration of the pistons is their primary sensory input. They navigate by reading the frequency of the floor. A change in pitch tells them a conveyor belt is jamming three sectors away; a drop in bass warns of a pressure valve releasing steam. They speak rarely, and when they do, it is not with voices—which would be lost in the 2.6/3 deafening roar—but with clicks, taps, and low-frequency thrums generated by contracting muscles in their throats and chests. It is a language of resonance, transmitted through the metal plates they stand on, felt in the soles of their feet.

Their eyes are large, lidless, and entirely black. There is no white sclera, no iris. Just deep, void-like orbs designed to capture every photon of the dim, failing light. They do not blink. Blinking would interrupt the constant stream of visual data needed to track the rapid, erratic movements of the machinery. Their pupils are dilated permanently, fixed wide to absorb the gloom. Yet, they are not blind to the dark. They see in thermal gradients. They can spot the heat signature of a leaking pipe before the steam becomes visible, or the cold draft of a vent opening miles above. They watch the machine breathe.

They wear no clothes, for fabric rots instantly in the chemical damp. Instead, they are draped in scavenged materials—strips of insulated wiring, fragments of polymerized concrete, sheets of reflective foil peeled from dead control panels. These are tied around their waists, shoulders, and heads with braided cables. The attire serves two purposes: camouflage against the metallic backdrop, and protection. The foil reflects the occasional burst of welding arc-light; the insulation protects against the freezing chill of the lower levels. On their backs, many carry harnesses made of rusted chain and leather, holding tools that are indistinguishable from weapons: wrenches filed to points, shards of glass embedded in resin, hammers with heads of fused slag.

They move in silence, despite the chaos around them. They step carefully, placing their hook-tipped toes into the grooves of the iron plating, testing for stability before committing their weight. They avoid the puddles of iridescent coolant, not out of fear of slipping, but because they know what lives in those depths—not animals, but microscopic swarms of corrosive bacteria that can eat through skin in seconds. They treat the wetness with reverence and terror.

They are scavengers, but not of food. There is no agriculture here, no hunting. They feed on the **waste** of the machine. They consume the lichen-like molds that grow in the crevices, scraping them off bolt heads with their teeth. They drink the condensed water that drips from the overhead gantries, filtering it through cloths made of fine mesh. Occasionally, they find a leak of nutrient-rich sludge from a broken bioreactor—a rare feast. They chew slowly, deliberately, conserving energy. Their metabolism is slow, tuned to the cool temperature. They can go days without moving, standing perfectly still on a catwalk, listening to the hum, waiting for the machine to offer up its scraps.

They live in the **alcoves**—the protected spaces between massive gear housings, the hollow interiors of decommissioned smokestacks, the undersides of sagging walkways. Here, they build nests of shredded insulation and dried mold. They do not sleep in the traditional sense. They enter a state of semi-conscious trance, lowering their heart rates to match the idle rhythm of the engines below. In this state, they dream in vibrations. They dream of the patterns of the pistons, the flow of the steam, the circular logic of the closed loop.

They are aware of you, the outsider. You are a anomaly. You are too loud, too warm, too fragile. They watch you from the shadows, their black eyes reflecting the faint glow of your presence. They do not approach. They do not attack. They simply observe, cataloging your movements, noting how you stumble on the slick floor, how you flinch at the sudden hiss of steam. To them, you are a malfunction. A glitch in the system. And glitches are eventually corrected.

But sometimes, if you stay long enough, if you stop fighting the hum and let it settle into your bones, you might notice one of them stepping closer. Not to harm, but to study. They might reach out a hooked finger and tap gently on the metal plate near your foot. *Thump-thump.* A question. *Are you part of the cycle?*

You cannot answer. You have no language for this place. So you stand still, and they stand still, and the machine continues to grind, swallowing both of you whole.

In time, you may begin to change. Your skin will grow thick and oily. Your ears will flatten. Your eyes will darken. You will stop hearing the noise as pain and start feeling it as home. You will learn to read the vibrations. You will learn to eat the mold. You will become a Tender.

And the machine will keep running.
