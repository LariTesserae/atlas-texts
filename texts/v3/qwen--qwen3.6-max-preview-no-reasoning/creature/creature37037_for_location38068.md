They do not have names, for they have no need to distinguish themselves from one another. To name a thing is to separate it from the whole, and here, separation is death. They are the **Mycelial Custodians**, though even that title implies a hierarchy that does not exist. They are simply the consciousness of the Sump-Garden made flesh—or rather, made fungus, root, and rust.

If you look closely at the shadows beneath the canopy, where the green twilight deepens into an indigo bruise, you might see them. At first glance, they appear to be part of the architecture: a cluster of pale mushrooms growing on a corroded valve, or a tangle of ivy wrapped around a severed pipe. But then, the ivy twitches. Not with the wind—there is no wind—but with intent.

The Custodians are humanoid only in the vaguest, most tragic sense. They are tall, slender figures composed of woven hyphae and fibrous plant matter, standing roughly two meters high. Their "skin" is a mosaic of lichen and moss, shifting in color from slate grey to vibrant emerald depending on their proximity to light sources. Where a human would have joints, they have knotted burls of wood, swollen and hard, acting as pivots for limbs that move with the slow, hydraulic grace of opening flowers.

They do not walk; they flow. When they move, it is because the Weave beneath them contracts, pushing them forward like blood cells through a capillary. They leave no footprints, only a temporary depression in the moss that fills instantly with condensation.

Their faces are the most unsettling aspect of their existence. They have no eyes, no mouths, no noses. Instead, their heads are smooth, oval domes of translucent bark, behind which swirls a cloudy, bioluminescent fluid. This fluid pulses in rhythm with the low-frequency hum of the garden. If you stand still enough, if you let your own heartbeat slow to match the vascular thrum of the place, you can hear their thoughts. They do not speak in words, but in sensations: the sharp tang of oxidizing iron, the sweet rot of decomposing cellulose, the warm satisfaction of a closed circuit.

There are perhaps twelve of them in this hollow. They are the immune system of the Sump-Garden.

Watch one now, near the rusted boiler. It stands perfectly still, its long, finger-like appendages—tendrils of vine and wire fused together—resting against the metal shell. It is not repairing the boiler; it is *listening* to it. The boiler is dying, its structural integrity failing under the weight of centuries. The Custodian feels this failure as a discordant note in the garden’s song. Slowly, deliberately, it presses its palm against the rust. From its fingertips, tiny white roots erupt, drilling into the metal pores. It is not destroying the machine; it is integrating it. It is pulling the iron into its own body, metabolizing the rust, converting the decay into strength. The red LED embedded in the nearby tree flickers faster, acknowledging the transfer of data, of energy, of memory.

These beings are ancient. They remember when the sky was open, before the canopy sealed itself shut. They remember the smell of gasoline and the roar of engines. But those memories are not held in minds; they are held in the water. When a Custodian drinks from the hidden pools, it tastes the copper of old pipes and the sweetness of chlorophyll, and it dreams of gears turning in the dark. These dreams are not nightmares; they are blueprints. The Custodians use these visions to guide the growth of the garden, shaping the vines to mimic the efficiency of the machines they consumed. A vine does not grow randomly; it grows along the path of least resistance, calculated by the collective memory of the Custodians who drank the industrial runoff.

They are fiercely protective, but their protection is passive. If you were to run, if you were to strike out in fear, the Weave would react. The vines would tighten, the roots would surge upward, trapping you in a web of living tension. The Custodians would not attack you. They would simply surround you, their faceless heads tilting in unison, watching as the garden absorbed your panic, your sweat, your very presence. You would become part of the substrate. Your bones would be wrapped in ferns; your clothes would feed the fungi. You would not die; you would transform. You would become a node in the network, a silent contributor to the hum.

But if you move slowly, if you breathe with the rhythm of the dripping water, they will ignore you. Or perhaps, they will acknowledge you. One might approach, its movements so slow they seem like time-lapse photography. It might extend a hand—a cluster of delicate, white mycelium—and touch your shoulder. In that touch, you would feel the entire history of the Sump-Garden: the heat of the furnace, the coolness of the rain, the silence of the abandoned factory, the loudness of the growing forest. You would understand, for a fleeting second, that there is no difference between the builder and the built, the creator and the created.

They are the dreamers of this place. While the plants sleep in the day, absorbing the filtered sunlight, the Custodians remain awake, tending to the machinery of life. They prune the overgrowth that threatens to choke the vents. They clear the debris that blocks the water flow. They ensure that the balance between the organic and the inorganic remains perfect. They are the priests of a religion that has no god, only process.

And sometimes, late in the cycle, when the humidity reaches its peak and the air becomes thick enough to drink, they gather in the center of the hollow, around the central trunk with its cracked control panel. They form a circle, linking hands, their bodies merging at the edges, blurring into one massive, pulsating organism. The hum rises in pitch, becoming a chord, a harmony of water, wood, and wire. The lights behind their bark-faces glow brighter, casting long, dancing shadows on the walls. In this moment, they are not individuals. They are the Garden itself, conscious and aware, dreaming its eternal, self-sustaining dream.

You are not alone here. You are watched. You are measured. You are weighed against the balance of the Weave. And if you are quiet enough, if you are still enough, you might just be allowed to stay.
