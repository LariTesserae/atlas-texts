To understand who lives in the Subterranean Lattice, one must first unlearn the distinction between the inhabitant and the habitat. In a world where light is not cast but emitted, and where silence is a physical weight rather than an absence of sound, the creatures here are not merely occupants; they are metabolic extensions of the stone itself.

The primary citizens are the **Lithovores**, though to call them "insects" is a failure of scale and spirit. They are the size of domestic cats, perhaps larger when fully engorged with silica. Their bodies are segmented masterpieces of camouflage, their chitinous plates mimicking the exact hue and texture of the vitrified floor—pale grays, translucent blues, and stark whites. They do not have eyes in the traditional sense; the pervasive, shadowless light renders vision secondary. Instead, their heads are dominated by sensitive, feathery antennae that tremble constantly, reading the air for the chemical signatures of decaying rock and fresh resin.

They move with a skittering grace that belies their heavy armor. When they feed, it is a violent, intimate act. A Lithovore will clamp its mandibles onto a corner of a building—a structure that may have stood for centuries—and grind. The sound is not a crunch, but a high-pitched whine, like glass being scored. They consume the calcium carbonate, digesting the history of the wall, and in return, they excrete a warm, viscous slurry from their abdomens. This secretion hardens instantly upon contact with the cool air, forming the "mortar" that holds the city together. Thus, every step you take on the polished floor was once part of a creature’s meal, and every wall you touch is composed of their waste. They are the architects, the laborers, and the demolition crew, all in one body. They are frantic because hunger is constant, and the city is always eating itself to stay fed.

But the Lithovores are blind to the grand design. They operate on instinct and pheromone trails. It is the **Glass-Walkers** who provide the consciousness of the Lattice.

These beings are terrifyingly elegant. Standing seven or eight feet tall, they are bipedal but unnervingly still. Their skin is not flesh, but a layer of polished obsidian-like material that reflects the ambient luminescence, making them appear as shards of the ceiling walking among us. They have no mouths, no noses, no visible ears. Their faces are smooth, featureless ovals of black mirror. Yet, they see everything.

The Glass-Walkers communicate through vibration. They carry long, slender staves made of flint and bone, which they tap against the ground in complex rhythms. These taps travel through the vitrified floor, transmitting instructions to the Lithovores below. A rapid triplet might signal *reinforce*; a slow, heavy thud might mean *consume*. They are the conductors of this silent symphony. To watch a Glass-Walker work is to witness a form of meditation. It stands before a crumbling archway, motionless, its head tilted slightly as if listening to the stress fractures within the stone. Then, with a sudden, fluid movement, it strikes the ground. Instantly, a swarm of Lithovores descends, chewing away the weakened section while others apply fresh resin. The repair is seamless. The city heals itself under the direction of these silent, reflective gods.

There is a third element to this ecology, one that is rarely seen but always felt: the **Predators of the Deep**.

The rules state that loud noises attract death. This is because the Lattice sits atop something older, something that does not share the mineral diet of the surface dwellers. Beneath the polished floors, in the dark, wet tunnels that plunge into the earth’s mantle, live things that hunt by sound. We do not know what they look like, for those who hear them usually do not return to describe them. But we feel their presence in the way the Glass-Walkers freeze when a distant rumble echoes through the rock. We see it in the sudden, synchronized stillness of thousands of Lithovores, their antennae retracting, their bodies flattening against the stone until they are invisible. The silence of the Lattice is not just cultural; it is defensive. It is a collective holding of breath, waiting for the monsters below to pass.

Life here is defined by this tension between creation and consumption, silence and scream. The inhabitants do not sleep, for there is no night. They do not dream, for there is no darkness. They exist in a perpetual state of maintenance. A Glass-Walker might spend years guiding the construction of a single spire, only to watch it be eaten away by the next generation of Lithovores. There is no permanence, only process.

Yet, there is beauty in this transience. The structures they build are not rigid monuments to ego, but living organisms that breathe and shift. A street today may be a plaza tomorrow; a home may become a bridge. The inhabitants have learned to let go of attachment. They are nomads in a fixed place, adapting to a landscape that refuses to stay still.

If you were to live here, you would change. Your skin would grow pale, reflecting the eternal glow. Your movements would become quiet, deliberate, your footsteps softening until you barely disturb the dust. You would learn to read the vibrations in the floor, to distinguish the tap of *repair* from the tap of *alarm*. You would lose your sense of time, replacing it with a sense of rhythm—the pulse of the city, the beat of the stone-eaters, the hum of the deep.

You would become part of the lattice. Not literally, perhaps, but spiritually. You would understand that you are not separate from the walls, the floor, the light. You are another node in the network, another variable in the equation of erosion and growth. And in the end, you might find peace in that dissolution. For in the Subterranean Lattice, nothing is lost; everything is recycled. The stone becomes food, the food becomes structure, the structure becomes shelter, and the shelter becomes memory. It is a closed loop of existence, perfect and terrible, spinning endlessly in the cold, bright dark.
