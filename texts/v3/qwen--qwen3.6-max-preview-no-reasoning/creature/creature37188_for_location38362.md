They do not have names, for names require a separation between the namer and the named, and here, nothing is separate. The wetness binds them all into a single, shivering entity.

First, there are the **Moss-Walkers**, though to call them walkers is to misunderstand their relationship with the ground. They are small, no larger than a house cat, but dense with muscle and fur that has lost its individual strands to become a pelt of living velvet. Their color is not brown or black, but the deep, bruised teal of the algae-stained basalt. They move on four limbs that end not in claws, but in wide, splayed pads of rough skin, designed to grip the slick, weeping stone without slipping.

They do not hunt. There is no need. The air itself feeds them. They stand motionless for hours on the rounded knuckles of rock, mouths slightly open, filtering the mist. Their tongues are long, ribbon-like organs, constantly flicking out to catch the droplets falling from the ferns above. When they breathe, it is a soft, wet sound, synchronized with the *plink-plank* of the water dripping from the ledges. If you watch closely, you will see that their ribs expand and contract in time with the condensation cycles of the caldera. They are not just animals; they are extensions of the humidity.

But they are not alone in the damp. Hovering around them, and indeed around everything, are the **Prism-Flies**. These are not the iridescent beetles mentioned earlier, but something stranger, evolved to exploit the specific optical weirdness of the Weeping Caldera. They are translucent, their wings so thin they are nearly invisible until they hit the light. When the sun strikes the mist and creates those fleeting sheets of rainbow, the Prism-Flies align themselves within the spectrum.

To the human eye, they appear as shards of colored glass floating in the air. Violet shards, cyan shards, gold shards. They feed on the energy of the refraction, or perhaps on the microscopic organisms suspended within the prisms. They are silent, unlike the buzzing midges. They move in jagged, geometric patterns, tracing the edges of the rainbows before the colors dissolve. When the veil vanishes, the flies drop suddenly to the moss, becoming indistinguishable from the dewdrops until the next alignment occurs. They are ephemeral ghosts of light, born and dying with every shift of the cloud cover.

Then, there are the **Stone-Singers**. These are the birds, but they have changed since they first arrived. Their feathers are heavy, waterlogged by design, weighing them down so they do not drift away into the infinite void above. They are sleek, dark shapes, like polished river stones given wings. They do not chirp sharply anymore; the constant roar of the wind and the drip of water has forced their vocalizations to evolve into low, resonant hums.

You can feel these hums in your teeth. The Stone-Singers perch on the highest points of the caldera rim, where the wind is strongest. They face outward, toward the abyss, and sing into the updraft. Their song is not for mating or territory, but for navigation. In this place of overwhelming visual noise—shimmering veils, glistening moss, blinding reflections—their eyes are secondary. They navigate by echo-location, using their low-frequency hums to map the curvature of the world below them. To hear them is to hear the shape of the drop.

And beneath them, in the deepest pools where the water stands still and reflects the sky like a mirror, live the **Mirror-Crabs**. Small, armored things, no bigger than a thumbnail, with shells that are perfectly reflective. They do not hide in the rocks; they hide in the reflection. When you look into a pool, you see the sky, the clouds, the rim of the caldera. But if you look closer, you will see tiny distortions in the blue-black surface. Tiny ripples that do not match the wind. These are the crabs, walking across the surface tension, camouflaged by the image of the heavens above them. They eat the fallen pollen of the dwarf willows, scavenging what the Moss-Walkers ignore. They are the cleaners of the reflection, ensuring the mirror remains clear.

But the most unsettling presence is not an animal at all. It is the **Echo-Hermit’s Shadow**.

Recall the ruins: the three walls of stacked slate, the cracked altar. Someone lived here once. They left. But the acoustics of the caldera are such that sound does not die; it accumulates. The loudness (2.5/3) means that every whisper, every step, every breath taken by the hermit centuries ago has been trapped, layered, and amplified by the concave walls.

If you stand near the ruin and close your eyes, you can hear it. Not a ghost, but a residual vibration. A phantom footstep on wet stone. A sigh of exhaustion. A mutter of prayer. The air is so thick with moisture that it holds these sounds like a sponge holds water. The "life" of the hermit persists as an auditory fossil. Sometimes, when the wind dies down for a rare second, you might hear a voice calling your name, not because it knows you, but because it is repeating the last word spoken by someone who stood exactly where you are standing now, years ago.

The creatures know this. The Moss-Walkers avoid the center of the ruin. The Stone-Singers never land on the altar. They sense the weight of the accumulated silence that was broken too many times.

So, who lives here?

A symbiotic cluster of life forms that have surrendered their individuality to the environment. The Moss-Walkers are the lungs of the caldera, breathing the mist. The Prism-Flies are its eyes, catching the light. The Stone-Singers are its ears, mapping the void. The Mirror-Crabs are its memory, reflecting the sky. And the Echo-Hermit’s Shadow is its conscience, a reminder that even in this lush, screaming paradise, solitude is the only true inhabitant.

They are all waiting. Waiting for the next sheet of rainbow. Waiting for the next drop of rain. Waiting for you to leave, so they can return to being one thing: the wet, loud, endless weep of the mountain.
