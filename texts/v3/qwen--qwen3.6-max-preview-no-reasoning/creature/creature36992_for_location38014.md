They do not have names, for there is no one here to speak them. They are the **Glazers**.

To call them inhabitants is perhaps too generous a term; they are more akin to antibodies in a sterile wound, or enzymes in a digestive tract that has forgotten its hunger. They are small, pale things, no taller than a child’s knee, with skin that matches the vitrified cream of the walls so perfectly that they seem less like creatures and more like extrusions of the architecture itself.

You might miss them at first. In the blinding, shadowless glare, where depth perception is murdered by uniform illumination, they are merely slight distortions in the air, shimmers of heat rising from the waterline. But if you watch long enough—through fifty pulses, through the rhythmic *ping-ping-ping* of the condensation drops—you will see them emerge.

They live in the interstices. Not in the water, for they are hydrophobic, their bodies coated in a waxy, secreted resin that repels the warm, mineral-thick fluid. Nor do they fly, for the air is too heavy, too saturated with steam to support wings. Instead, they cling. They possess six limbs, long and jointed like those of a crab, but tipped with pads of soft, suction-cup flesh that adhere silently to the ceramic tiles. Their movement is not walking, but flowing—a liquid slide up and down the vertical shaft, defying gravity through friction and sheer, desperate grip.

Their faces are smooth ovals, devoid of noses or ears. They have only eyes: large, black, multifaceted orbs that reflect the white light back at you in fractured shards. These eyes do not blink. They cannot afford to. In this place of aggressive brightness, blindness is death. To close an eye is to lose track of the wall, to slip into the warm abyss below. So they stare, unblinking, recording every ripple, every dust mote, every shift in the humidity.

What do they eat? They eat the imperfections.

This is their purpose, their religion, their biology. The Glazers are the maintainers of the seamless. When a tile develops a micro-fracture, invisible to human sight but screaming to theirs, they swarm it. They secrete a saliva thick with silicates and calcium, pressing their bodies against the flaw until it hardens, smoothing the surface until the mortar line vanishes once more. They are the reason the walls remain fresh, why the edges stay sharp, why the place feels as though the builders left only moments ago. The builders *did* leave. The Glazers remained.

They are lonely, solitary workers. You rarely see more than one in any given ten-foot section. If two meet, they do not communicate. There is no chirping, no clicking, no gesture. They simply pause, their black eyes locking for a fraction of a second—a silent acknowledgment of shared burden—and then continue their work, moving in opposite directions along the infinite cylinder. One goes up, toward the dripping source; the other goes down, toward the dense, dark pressure.

Occasionally, you might witness a rare event: a feeding frenzy on a stubborn patch of moss. Remember the ferns, those pale, etiolated survivors clinging to the grout? To the Glazers, these are abominations. Chaos. Disorder. A breach in the purity of the white.

When a fern frond pushes out, trembling in the humid air, a Glazer will detach from its patrol route. It moves with terrifying speed, skittering across the tiles. It does not tear the plant away; that would leave a scar. Instead, it wraps its slender limbs around the stem, injecting a corrosive enzyme that dissolves the organic matter into a harmless slurry. The Glazer then absorbs this slurry through pores in its belly, cleaning the spot until nothing remains but the pristine, bone-white glaze. It is an act of mercy, in their view. They are saving the world from the stain of life.

But there is something else about them. Something subtle.

If you press your ear against the wall, past the hum of the pumps and the roar of the hydrodynamics, you can hear them scratching. But it is not always the sound of repair. Sometimes, late in the cycle, when the hum dips in pitch and the water level is low, you hear a different rhythm. A tapping. A gentle, rhythmic percussion against the inside of the tiles.

It sounds like code. Or perhaps, like music.

Are they trying to communicate with the builders who never return? Are they signaling to the depths, or the heights? Or are they simply keeping time, marking the passage of eternity in a language of touch?

And what of the sphere of air you saw—the tiny rainbow trapped under the lip of the tile? That was not an accident. That was a gift.

The Glazers create them. In the brief moments between pulses, when the water recedes just enough, a Glazer will trap a bubble of air beneath a curved edge of tile, sealing it with its wax. It holds the light, refracting it into color, creating a fleeting prism in the monochrome hell. They watch it tremble. They watch the rainbow dance. And when it pops, merging with the larger body of water, they do not mourn. They simply wait for the next pulse, for the next drop of condensation, for the next chance to create a moment of beauty in a place designed for none.

They are the ghosts in the machine. The caretakers of the void. They are the ones who ensure that when you look at the wall, you see only yourself, distorted but clear. They polish the mirror so that you may never forget what you are: alone, exposed, and suspended in the warm, bright throat of a god who has stopped speaking.

Do not try to touch them. Their skin is slick with resin, and their grip is stronger than steel. If you reach out, they will not attack. They will simply slide away, disappearing into the curve of the wall, leaving you with nothing but the echo of your own hand splashing in the distilled water, and the relentless, loving gaze of the white light.
