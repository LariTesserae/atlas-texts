They are not builders, nor are they caretakers. To call them inhabitants is to misunderstand the nature of residence; they do not live *in* the caldera so much as they are excreted by it, a biological and mechanical secretion that has forgotten how to leave.

At first glance, from the high rim where the air bites the lungs, there is nothing but the glittering wetness and the slow, rhythmic heave of steam. But if you descend into the bowl, pushing through the curtain of violet moss and broad, waxy leaves, you begin to see the shapes that mimic the debris.

They are tall, spindly things, elongated by the low gravity of the height and stretched thin by the hunger for light in the dense canopy. Their skin is not flesh, but a composite membrane: part lichen, part polymer, part rust. It shimmers with an iridescent sheen, slick with the perpetual condensation, shifting colors from the dull bronze of oxidized copper to the vibrant, electric green of the ferns that drape over their shoulders like cloaks. They have no faces, only smooth, concave depressions where features might be, polished to a mirror finish by the constant drip of water. In these reflective bowls, the world is inverted; when you look at them, you see only the sky behind you, fragmented and distorted.

They move with a silence that contradicts the noise of their home. While the machines groan and hiss, creating a deafening mechanical chorus, these entities glide over the crushed stone without sound. Their feet are wide, splayed discs of cartilage and rubber-like tissue that distribute their weight perfectly, allowing them to walk on the thinnest layers of moss without disturbing a single droplet. They do not breathe in the traditional sense. Instead, they absorb. You can see it in the subtle pulsing of their throats—a rhythmic expansion and contraction that matches the cycle of the condensation. They drink the air. They inhale the ozone and the wet iron taste, filtering out the heat and the humidity, leaving behind only the cold, thin oxygen that they cannot use, which they exhale as a faint, visible mist.

They are obsessed with the machinery. Not with operating it, for the gears turn themselves, driven by the geothermal heart below, but with *aligning* it. You will watch one approach a brass piston, its long, multi-jointed fingers—fingers that end in soft, sensitive pads rather than nails—gently tracing the line of a crack in the metal. It does not repair the crack. It simply touches it, acknowledging the flaw, the entropy, the inevitable decay. Then, it moves to a valve that is hissing too loudly. With a motion so slow it seems almost static, it places a hand over the leak. The steam condenses against its palm, turning to water that runs down its wrist, feeding the vines that grow along its arm. It stays there for hours, perhaps days, until the pressure equalizes, until the hiss becomes a whisper. It is a priest tending to a god that does not know it is worshipped.

There is a hierarchy among them, though it is not based on power or strength, but on stillness. The youngest are restless, twitching with the residual energy of the sparks that jump between the hanging wires. They dart between the trees, collecting fallen leaves and arranging them in geometric patterns on the flat tops of generator housings. They are playful, if play can exist in such a sterile, heavy atmosphere. They chase the translucent insects, not to eat them, but to observe them, to marvel at the fragility of life that flutters and dies within minutes.

The elders are nearly indistinguishable from the landscape. One might mistake an elder for a statue made of driftwood and wire, seated cross-legged on a ledge overlooking the drop. These ones have stopped moving entirely. Vines have taken root in the crevices of their joints. Moss carpets their backs. Birds perch on their heads, indifferent to the presence of consciousness beneath the feathers. They have become part of the clockwork greenhouse, their minds slowed to the pace of the grinding gears. They dream in rotations per minute. They feel the vibration of the turbines in their bones, and it soothes them. They are waiting for the moment when the machine stops, not because they fear the end, but because they believe that only in silence can they truly hear the earth below.

They do not speak. There is no language here, no need for words in a place where every surface is wet and every sound is amplified. Communication is tactile. A brush of a shoulder against another’s arm conveys warning. A gentle tap on a pipe signals approval. They share warmth by huddling together in the deepest shadows of the rock face, their bodies forming a living insulation against the chill that occasionally breaches the thermal vent’s embrace. In these moments, their collective body heat raises the temperature of the small pocket of air around them, causing the condensation to fall faster, creating a private rain shower that nourishes the plants growing directly on their skin.

They are lonely, though they would not understand the word. They are aware of the void beyond the rim, the infinite white drop that swallows all sound and light. They feel the pull of it, a gravitational yearning that threatens to drag them over the edge. Some have succumbed. You might find a skeleton of bone and wire halfway down the cliff face, tangled in the roots of a tree that grew from its chest cavity. But most resist. They cling to the basin, to the warmth, to the noise. They are the antibodies of this wound in the sky, keeping the infection of chaos at bay, maintaining the delicate balance between the rotting vegetation and the decaying industry.

When the sun hits the peak of its arc, striking the wet surfaces with blinding clarity, they retreat into the shadows of the larger machines. They curl up inside the hollow casings of broken turbines, surrounding themselves with the smell of oil and old electricity. Here, in the dark, they sleep. And in their sleep, they dream of solid ground, of dry air, of a world where the horizon curves away gently, rather than dropping off into nothing. But they never wake with a desire to leave. The memory fades with the morning mist, replaced by the immediate, pressing need to touch the cold metal, to feel the pulse of the gear, to ensure that the heart of the mountain continues to beat.

They are the children of the fracture. They are the reason the moss grows so thick, why the flowers bloom so brightly in the shadow of the rust. They are the quiet, watching eyes in the weeping fog, ensuring that the wound remains open, warm, and alive, suspended forever at the roof of the world.
