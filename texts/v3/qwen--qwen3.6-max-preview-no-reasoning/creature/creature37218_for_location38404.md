They are the **Vitrea**.

To call them "people" is to impose a shape upon something that has long since learned to flow around the edges of definition. They are humanoid only in the way a shadow is humanoid—a suggestion of form, elongated and attenuated by the low gravity and the verticality of their home. But where a shadow is an absence of light, the Vitrea are an accumulation of it, faint and internal, echoing the bruised purple glow of the cryo-lichen that clothes their world.

### The Physiology of Glass and Mist

Their skin is not flesh in any terrestrial sense. It is semi-translucent, pale as moonstone, stretched tight over bones that are less calcium and more silica-infused cartilage. This is the legacy of the "Glass-Eaters," though they do not eat glass in the literal, crunching sense. They consume the microscopic silicate dust that coats every surface of the Hollow, filtering it through gill-like slits along their ribs. In return, their bodies secrete a resinous sweat that hardens into the very ropes and bindings that hold their city together. They are architects who build with their own essence.

Because the air is heavy and cold, their metabolism is glacial. A Vitrean does not rush. To move quickly is to burn energy they cannot spare; it is to disrupt the thermal equilibrium of the damp air. Their limbs are long, multi-jointed, and ending in fingers that possess six digits each—three opposing pairs capable of manipulating the finest fern-fiber threads or the coarsest basalt shards with equal delicacy.

Their faces are devoid of noses, for breathing is done through the porous skin of their necks and chests. Instead, they have wide, dark eyes that occupy nearly half their face. These eyes lack whites; they are pools of obsidian liquid, reflecting the bioluminescence of the moss and the dim grey sky. They see heat and movement rather than color, perceiving the world as a tapestry of thermal currents and kinetic shifts. When a Vitrean looks at you, you feel seen not as a person, but as a disturbance in the humidity.

### The Language of Knots and Silence

The Vitrea do not speak. Sound is dangerous here. A shout can trigger an avalanche of loose scree; a loud laugh might attract the attention of the larger, predatory gliders that nest in the upper fog banks. Instead, they communicate through a complex system of **Knot-Speech** and **Placement-Rituals**.

Every interaction is a physical act. To greet another, a Vitrean might tie a specific loop in the sleeve of their companion’s cloak—a knot that signifies *peace*, *kinship*, or *warning*, depending on the tension of the fiber. To ask a question, they place a small, smooth stone on a flat ledge, oriented toward the north. The answer comes when another places a second stone beside it, angled differently.

This silence extends to their emotional lives. They do not display anger or joy through facial expression. Instead, their internal bioluminescence flares. A surge of bright violet indicates urgency or fear; a deep, pulsing indigo suggests contemplation or sorrow. To walk among them is to be surrounded by a silent symphony of shifting lights, a language of color that pulses in rhythm with the mountain’s own circadian cycles.

### The Role: Stewards of the Threshold

The Vitrea are not masters of the Hollow; they are its immune system. They understand that the ecosystem is precarious, balanced on the knife-edge of tectonic youth and biological saturation. If the moss grows too thick, it smothers the vents that release geothermal warmth, freezing the settlement. If the insects swarm too densely, they strip the ferns bare, causing erosion that undermines the cliff-side dwellings.

Thus, the Vitrea spend their days in meticulous maintenance. They prune the glowing lichens with tools made of sharpened bone, guiding their growth away from structural supports. They harvest the silk of the giant spiders, not to kill them, but to encourage them to weave stronger webs between the bridges, reinforcing the infrastructure. They collect the condensation from the leaves, channeling it into cisterns carved into the rock, ensuring that the water tables remain stable.

They live in constant negotiation with the fauna. The amphibious creatures in the pools are treated with reverence; the Vitrea believe these beings remember the time before the mountains rose, when this place was ocean floor. To harm one is to invite a curse of stillness, a stagnation that would suffocate the entire hollow. The gliding mammals are tolerated, even fed scraps of dried fungus, because they keep the insect populations in check.

### The Life Cycle: Dissolution and Rebirth

A Vitrean’s life is measured not in years, but in cycles of mist. They are born from the union of two individuals who merge their resinous secretions into a single, hardened cocoon attached to a high, sheltered crevice. For months, the cocoon absorbs moisture and nutrients from the air. When it hatches, the newborn emerges fully formed, already knowing the knot-language, already attuned to the pulse of the mountain.

Old age for a Vitrean is not a decline, but a transformation. As they near the end of their lifespan, their translucency increases. They become lighter, more ethereal. Eventually, they stop moving entirely. They choose a spot on the cliff face, often near a patch of particularly vibrant cryo-lichen, and simply stand there. Over weeks, their body softens, dissolving into a nutrient-rich slurry that feeds the moss. Their bones, infused with silica, remain behind, becoming part of the rock itself. To die is to become the foundation. It is the ultimate act of stewardship: giving oneself back to the wound in the world so that it may heal.

### The Outsider’s Experience

If you were to encounter a Vitrean, you would first notice the smell: ozone, wet stone, and crushed mint. You would hear nothing, yet feel a pressure in your ears, the subtle shift in air density as they approach. They would not look at your face, but at your hands, assessing your potential for destruction or creation.

They might offer you a gift: a small, perfectly knotted bundle of fern fiber. Inside, perhaps, is a piece of polished basalt, warm to the touch. This is not hospitality in the human sense. It is a test. Can you accept the weight without breaking? Can you hold the silence without filling it with noise?

If you fail, if you stumble or shout or break a frond, the Vitrea will not attack. They will simply withdraw. The lights in their skin will dim. The moss will seem to close around you. The gliders will circle overhead, watching. You will be left alone in the damp, chilling dark, realizing that you are no longer a guest, but a contaminant. And the Hollow will begin to digest you, slowly, silently, turning you into soil for the next generation of glass-eaters.
