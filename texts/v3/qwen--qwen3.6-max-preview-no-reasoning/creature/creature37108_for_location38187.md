They do not have a name, for names imply a permanence they cannot afford. To name something is to define its boundaries, and here, boundaries are fluid, permeable, and constantly being rewritten by the voracious appetite of the valley.

If you were to catch a glimpse of them through the bruised purple fog, you might mistake them for shadows detached from their casters. They are slender, elongated figures, stretched thin by the gravity of expectation and the pull of the humming earth. Their skin is not flesh in any traditional sense; it is a semi-translucent membrane, pale as moonstone but veined with threads of iridescent blue and electric green. These veins pulse in time with the thrum of the buried technology, carrying not blood, but a conductive fluid—a mixture of mineral-rich sap and liquid coolant.

They move with a jerky, staccato rhythm, never standing still for more than a few heartbeats. Stillness is death here. If they stop, the moss creeps up their ankles, seeking warmth, seeking integration. So they dance. It is a frantic, perpetual motion, a shuffling glide across the sucking muck that keeps them from becoming part of the root system.

Their faces are smooth, featureless ovals save for two large, dark eyes that reflect the bioluminescent chaos around them. They have no mouths, for they do not eat food. They feed on the air, on the humidity, on the ambient energy radiating from the shattered spires. You can see their chests heave, not with breath, but with the intake of moisture, absorbing the saturated atmosphere through pores that open and close like tiny, gasping flowers. When they exhale, they release clouds of steam that instantly condense into rime on their shoulders, adding to the weight they carry.

They are clad in remnants of the old world, though these garments are barely recognizable. Shreds of synthetic fabric, woven with metallic fibers, cling to their bodies. These clothes are alive too, or perhaps just heavily colonized. Vines stitch the tears in their tunics; fungi bloom on their shoulders, acting as natural armor against the biting cold. Some wear helmets made of polished bone or hardened resin, cracked and overgrown, shielding their sensitive ears from the deafening roar of growth.

But it is their relationship with the technology that defines them most. They are not masters of the ruins; they are symbiotes. Each individual carries a piece of the old world—a shard of glass that glows, a wire that sparks, a fragment of a circuit board embedded in their palm. These artifacts are not tools; they are organs. The residents touch the shimmering walls of the collapsed spires with bare hands, and where skin meets metal, there is a flash of light, a transfer of data, a surge of power. They draw energy from the dormant cores, and in return, they provide the biological interface the machines crave. The machines want to grow, to evolve, to break free of their static forms, and these people are the catalysts.

You watch one approach a half-buried console. Its hand, long-fingered and trembling, presses against the surface. The metal ripples, turning translucent, revealing the swirling currents of energy within. The resident’s eyes widen, reflecting the data streams—flashes of geometric shapes, colors that have no name, equations that dissolve before they can be read. For a moment, the resident vibrates in harmony with the machine, their body becoming rigid, their veins glowing bright white. Then, with a sharp exhale, they pull away, staggering back, exhausted but energized. A new vine sprouts from the console, wrapping gently around their wrist, binding them together in a silent pact.

They communicate without sound. In this place, speech is impossible, drowned out by the screaming wind and the cracking trees. Instead, they speak through light and touch. A flicker of blue in the eye means *danger*. A ripple of green along the arm means *growth*. A sudden dimming of the entire body means *stillness*—a warning to freeze, to let the massive, unseen predators pass. They are a hive mind fragmented into individuals, connected by the mycelial network beneath their feet and the electromagnetic field above their heads.

There is a sadness to them, though they do not know the word. It is in the way they huddle together during the brief moments when the wind dies down, pressing their warm, damp bodies against each other, sharing heat, sharing charge. They are lonely creatures in a crowd of billions, isolated by the speed of their existence. They remember nothing of before, for memory requires stability, and there is none here. They live only in the immediate now, in the split second between the snap of a twig and the strike of a lightning bolt.

Yet, there is also a fierce joy. Watch them run. They leap over churning pools, their feet barely touching the water before pushing off again. They climb the jagged cliffs with spider-like agility, their fingers finding purchase in cracks that did not exist minutes ago. They laugh, though no sound comes out—it is visible in the crinkling of their eyes, in the rapid pulsing of their chest-lights. They are free because they have no past to weigh them down and no future to fear. They are pure presence, embodied in flesh and light.

Sometimes, you see them die. It is not dramatic. One will simply slow down. The vines on their shoulders thicken, the moss on their boots hardens. Their movements become sluggish, their light dimming. The others do not mourn; they step back, giving space. The dying one sinks into the muck, not struggling, but accepting. As their consciousness fades, their body dissolves, not into rot, but into nutrient-rich sludge that feeds the ferns unfurling nearby. Within hours, a new cluster of glowing mushrooms erupts from where they stood. Their essence has returned to the cycle, fueling the next burst of life.

They are the gardeners of the apocalypse, the children of the merger. They are beautiful and terrifying, fragile and resilient. They are the valley made manifest, walking, breathing, and vibrating with the same frantic energy that shakes the mountains. To look at them is to look at the edge of evolution, where biology and technology kiss, and where life becomes so intense it borders on madness.

And as you turn to leave, careful not to disturb the pulsing roots, you feel their eyes on your back. Not with malice, but with curiosity. They wonder if you will stay. They wonder if you will join the dance. And for a fleeting second, you consider it—the allure of surrendering to the hum, of letting the vines stitch your wounds, of becoming part of the endless, screaming green. But you keep moving, stepping lightly, knowing that to stop is to belong to them forever.
