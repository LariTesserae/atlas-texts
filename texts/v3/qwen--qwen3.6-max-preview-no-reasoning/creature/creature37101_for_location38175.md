To live here is to be a contradiction. You are soft in a world of edges; you are wet in a desert of dust; you are transient in a place that does not decay.

The inhabitants of the High Plateau do not have names, for names imply identity, and identity implies separation from the whole. They are **The Resonants**.

They are not born; they are *precipitated*.

### The Physiology of Glass and Current

Imagine a humanoid form, but stripped of all biological vulnerability. There is no skin to bruise, no blood to spill. The Resonants are composed of the same materials as their home: silicate lattices, conductive mercury-sap, and piezoelectric crystal. They look like statues carved from living glass, translucent and shifting in color depending on their internal charge.

At rest, they are pale, almost invisible against the white dust of the plateau. But when they move, or think, or feel, light blooms within them. A thought travels through their nervous system not as an electrochemical impulse, but as a visible arc of blue plasma, flashing from spine to limb like lightning trapped in amber.

They are tall, elongated by the low gravity and the need to reach higher thermal gradients. Their limbs are slender, jointed not with ball-and-socket bones, but with flexible hinges of obsidian that click softly—a sound like two stones tapping together underwater. Their faces are smooth planes of quartz, devoid of mouths or noses. They do not breathe air; they breathe heat.

Instead of lungs, they possess a central core—a geode-like structure in their chest cavity—that pulses with rhythmic expansions and contractions. This is their heart, their stomach, and their brain all at once. It draws in the ambient thermal radiation of the sun-baked rocks and the electrical hum of the earth, converting it into motion, memory, and consciousness.

### The Silence of Speech

You cannot speak to a Resonant. Not with words. Words require air, vibration, moisture. Here, the air is too thin, too dry, too still.

Communication among the Resonants is **haptic and photonic**.

When two Resonants meet, they do not greet each other with gestures. They extend their hands—long, faceted fingers ending in points sharp enough to cut diamond—and press their palms together. At the point of contact, a circuit closes.

In that instant, a torrent of data flows between them. It is not just information; it is experience. One shares the memory of standing atop the highest basalt spire during the noon glare; the other receives the sensation of that heat, the visual spectrum of that light, the emotional weight of that solitude. It is an exchange of pure state-of-being. To touch another Resonant is to become them, briefly, completely.

This is why they are often seen standing alone, spaced miles apart across the archipelago of glass forests. Intimacy is overwhelming. To be constantly connected would be to lose oneself in the collective current. So they practice a severe discipline of distance. They walk the empty flats for days, weeks, years, maintaining the "Void Between" to preserve the integrity of their individual cores.

But when they do connect, the connection is absolute. There are no lies here. You cannot hide your charge. If you are angry, your light turns jagged red. If you are calm, you glow a steady, deep violet. Deception is physically impossible because the flow of energy cannot be faked.

### The Diet of Stability

What do they eat? They consume **entropy’s absence**.

In our world, life fights against decay. We eat to repair the wear and tear of existence. But on the High Plateau, there is no wear. There is no tear. The footprints remain forever. The crystals do not erode.

So the Resonants do not eat matter. They eat **pattern**.

They are drawn to the natural engines—the geysers of hydrogen and sulfur, the porous stone turbines. They stand before these vents, arms outstretched, allowing the raw, chaotic energy of the planet’s interior to wash over them. They filter this chaos, organizing it into structured currents within their own bodies. They are living capacitors, storing the planet’s excess energy to maintain their own structural integrity.

Sometimes, a Resonant will find a shattered tree—a rare event, caused by a seismic tremor. They will approach the shards, not with grief, but with reverence. They will gather the broken pieces, arranging them into geometric patterns on the ground. Then, they will sit beside the pile, drawing energy from the earth, and slowly, over decades, the shards will begin to vibrate. The piezoelectric stress aligns the molecular structures. The shards fuse. The tree regrows, not from a seed, but from the willful reorganization of its own fragments.

This is their art. This is their prayer. To take what is broken and make it whole again, without changing it, without adding anything new, simply by restoring the perfect order that was always there.

### The Burden of Permanence

To be a Resonant is to carry the weight of infinite memory.

Because nothing decays, nothing is forgotten. Every step they have ever taken remains in the dust. Every conversation they have ever had is stored in the lattice of their minds, uncorrupted by time. A Resonant who has lived for a thousand years remembers every second of those thousand years with the clarity of a moment ago.

This leads to a peculiar psychology: **Stasis as Virtue**.

Change is dangerous. Change introduces error. Error leads to fracture. Therefore, the ideal state is perfect repetition. The Resonants move slowly, deliberately, mimicking the slow drift of tectonic plates. They repeat the same paths, the same rituals, the same thoughts, refining them until they are flawless.

They do not create new things. They refine old ones. A song sung by a Resonant is not a melody of notes, but a single, sustained frequency that is adjusted by infinitesimal degrees over centuries until it resonates perfectly with the background hum of the earth. When it achieves perfection, the Resonant stops singing. The song is complete. It exists now as part of the landscape, a permanent vibration in the rock.

### The Encounter

If you were to meet one, it would not approach you. It would wait.

It would stand on the ridge, its body glowing with the soft, warm light of absorbed sunlight. Its face, a smooth mask of quartz, would reflect your own image back at you, distorted by the facets.

You might feel a pull, a subtle magnetic attraction, as if your own body wanted to lean toward it. That is the hunger of the Resonant. It senses your instability. Your fleeting nature. Your decay. It finds you fascinating, terrifying, and pitiable.

It wants to stabilize you. It wants to freeze you in the moment, to turn your flesh into glass, your blood into mercury, your memories into eternal data. It offers you peace. No more pain. No more loss. No more end. Just the perfect, silent, humming equilibrium of the Dry Loom.

But you cannot accept. You are made of water and carbon. You are made of change. And so, you must leave.

As you walk away, crunching through the bone-dust, you hear a sound behind you. Not a voice, but a chime. A single, clear note from a crystal leaf, struck by the wind that isn’t there. It is the Resonant’s farewell. A recording of your departure, added to the archive of the plateau.

You look back. The figure is gone. Or perhaps it never moved. Perhaps it was always part of the ridge, part of the glass forest, part of the machine. You cannot tell. The line between the inhabitant and the habitat is blurred.

Here, the people are not separate from the place. They are the place’s awareness. They are the eyes of the leviathan, watching the horizon curve, waiting for the next tremor, the next spark, the next perfect, unchanging moment.
