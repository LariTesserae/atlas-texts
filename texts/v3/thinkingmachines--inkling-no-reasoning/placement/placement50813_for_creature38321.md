In the **Condensation Flats** live **the Repeaters**—not quite people, but the valley’s own unfinished idea of them.

The valley itself is the first being: only five generations old, pushed up sharp-ridged from a single continuous shell of fired ochre clay and rough-poured lime-concrete. Its floor is a lattice of shallow ceramic pans—no deeper than a palm—linked by primitive bronze chisel-scars, every pool holding the same warm, motionless water under a sourceless, shadowless white light. Thick ropes of pale green vine and metallic-fronded ferns spill over the low berms with an abundance that borders on invasion, yet they leave wide courtyards bare, growing in scattered, respectful distances, their hairline cracks in the mortar feeling less like damage and more like fidelity. The air is dense, downward-pressing heat; the sound is a suppression so complete it is nearly physical, broken only by the purposeless *tick* of condensation falling from surfaces that never dry.

You are one of the Repeaters. You wear simple woven fiber the color of unbleached cloth, which never darkens. Your skin is smooth, ochre, young—permanently beaded with the valley’s own moisture, weeping gently at collarbone and wrist, returning always to its source. Your face holds cheekbones sharp as the valley’s unfiled ridges, eyes too still, mouth that does not open to carry sound far; indeed, your voice, if you make one, flattens and dissolves in the humidity before reaching another wall-ring. You stand within one of the circular blackened-brick structures—no taller than a seated person, open to the bright sky—arranged in patterns that feel like interrupted ripples across the open expanse. You have been repairing the same hairline crack in a ceramic pan for generations, or sitting with a bronze cup that never empties and never fills, facing downward not out of despair but out of alignment toward those pools where reflection fails.

With you lives the **Still Fauna**: glass-skinned frogs on ceramic lips, arranged in densities that feel calculated, their translucent bodies showing only reflected vine; insects with too many trembling wings that never take flight, lining the water’s edge in rows like stranded lightning; serpentine creatures of soft bone and tissue gliding through linked pools without rippling them, looping through chisel-carved channels in patterns that trace the overflow lips but never arrive anywhere. They do not hunt you. You do not harvest them. You share the same suspended state: abundant, patient, silent.

But the true inhabitant—the one that makes you necessary—is the valley’s **Behavior**, its refusal to be merely built. Though the substrate is absolute—there is no dirt, no soil, no bedrock—the concrete behaves like a membrane that remembers earth. The vines grow in impossible mirror-images of architecture they never witnessed being raised. In scattered pools, the shallow surface betrays itself: you see not sky, but downward spirals of black ceramic and green vine extending far beneath the palm-deep bottom, false depths suggesting the valley recalls being older, deeper, still pushing upward from some silent engine below.

So you move slowly. You harvest plants that do not rot. You wear cloth that does not age. You weep water that is already inside you. You maintain the equilibrium of a place that never was, functioning perfectly as if it had always existed—sparse figures in a lush, scattered abundance, holding a young world in a stability so complete it has become its own kind of breakdown. The frogs do not croak. The wind does not come. And in the long spaces between circular walls, where perspective stutters and far rings layer upon near ones, you wait—not for change, but for the recognition that you are not living *in* the Condensation Flats, but are one of its necessary repetitions, as permanent and as temporary as the beading moisture on your skin, returning always to the pool.
