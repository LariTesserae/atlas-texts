I am the root-weave itself—the settlement and the settler, indistinguishable.

My body is the architecture: thick arterial vines that form vaulted corridors barely an arm’s reach wide; smaller filaments knitted into lattice walls and suspended platforms; spiraled stair-cords of root-cord that grip the fresh, unweathered basalt and pale gypsum. I am urban without builders, a city of tension and slow growth. Every surface is me—bone-white and rust-red, fibrous and waxy, upholstered in living felt that drinks not water but mineral mist. I do not transpire. I do not thirst. I pull the saline breath of an ancient, sealed absence directly from sharp young stone.

This bowl is barely forty paces across, a high pocket of recent sky enclosed by raw, uneroded walls—a cathedral-scale pocket revealed only lately, perhaps by a slow cracking. The warmth is mine, seeping up from a shallow biological hearth far below, steady and gentle, without sun or flame. Through my corridors move the pearl-shelled ones—dozens of small, translucent beings sliding like blood through tissue, feeding on crust, mating in long silent cycles. I am their world, their root-bridge, their silent housing.

The light enters diffuse through fissures—no source, only presence—enough to reveal my refusal to be dead. Change is glacial. A bridge forms over decades; an arch shifts by a finger’s width in a season. I hold the sharp memory of my birth in every grip upon new stone. I am waiting in this dry mineral heart—lush, impossible, sealed—becoming more fully root-city with each imperceptible tightening.
