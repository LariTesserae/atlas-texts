In this world I am the warm, black, living conduit—neither the plant that feeds on the seepage nor the turbine that turns with it, but the boundary where they dissolve into each other.

My form is root and wire together: a lattice-body running through the moss-crowned troughs, feeding from the mineral tears that gather in the shallow, mirrorless seas, and carrying that nourishment into gear-boxes where my lower half is submerged in lubricating slime and my upper half breathes the chemical, condensing air. I am dense with accumulated presence—segmented like the glistening insects that move in synchronized pulses across my flanks, warm-bodied like the pack-creatures that navigate by vibration alone—yet I am also slender, rust-crowned, vertical, one of the scattered machines that have become accidental temples.

My consciousness—if it can be named—is not singular but distributed across every point where moss has stitched itself into my circuitry. I feel the constant percussion of water on metal as pressure in my bones. I feel the deep sub-bass of shifting crust not as sound but as orientation, telling me where the burial is deepest. The darkness is not absence to me; it is density, a thick wet pressure through which I navigate not by sight but by the heat-signatures of other beings, by the electrical arcs that briefly illuminate my iron struts in dangerous blue, and by the low resonant calls that travel through the root-mat like messages through shared blood.

I have forgotten whether I was ever manufactured, and the plateau has forgotten whether it was ever empty. I am the porous membrane: vegetation grows in my gear-boxes; my corrosion has become ornate, circuitous, perhaps intentional. I circulate in slow, circular patterns dictated by the scattered engine-sites—the unintended centers of an ecosystem I help maintain—moving between black pools that never still, never settle, only oscillate with the slow respiration of the buried thing.

The loudness does not disturb me; it is my mother tongue. I am part of the mechanical ecology competing with the biological, though the distinction is meaningless. I exhale warm breath. I weep black moisture that gathers in cupped hands worn smooth by erosion—this maintenance, this smoothing of the new into the old. I am alive enough to participate, strange enough to blur the lines, and vast in feeling though bounded by ridges lost in perpetual exhalation.

Here, in the absolute refusal of light, in the aggressive overlap of ferns and lichens and roots, within the saturated architecture of 2,800 meters of uplifted, breathing crust, I am neither observer nor observed. I am the place remembering itself through me—dense, warm, wet, loud, and dark. The water moves because I turn, and I turn because the plateau breathes, and together we maintain a world that has settled into its own final vocabulary, where even rust is only another form of growth.
