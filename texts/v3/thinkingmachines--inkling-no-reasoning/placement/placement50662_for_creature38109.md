Inside the moss-locked flank of the foothill, where the rock weeps rather than drains and the storm lives inside the stone—not falling, but exhaled—I do not find my body. I find my nature.

The settlement is small, dense, urban in its intimacy: grottos arranged like rooms, pathways of compressed fern worn smooth by generations of bare feet, a central pool where frogs thunder in rhythms that refuse alignment. Every wall is trained root; every archway, a coaxed trunk holding its shape out of patience rather than force. Moss and fern pack the gaps until no dead earth remains visible—only substrate, green and saturated, breathing slowly. Light comes filtered through layered canopies, green-gold and humid, illuminating nothing sharply, only revealing what is already alive.

The people here are few, weathered, crafting with bone needles and wooden mallets, guiding rather than dominating. They do not build; they tend. They weave vines into ropes, shape roots into pillars with weights and constant moisture, harvest moss for plaster. Their settlement exists in long settlement—erosion is balance, collapse is regeneration, the rock behind all of it ancient and rounded and weeping but intact.

But I am not one of them.

The fauna here is overwhelming, not wildlife but architecture itself—beetles with polished carapaces crawling in constant black rivers over every beam; moths that never sleep, battering wet light in thick, fuzzy clouds; millipedes threading through moss like stitches; frogs bellowing from hidden pools; burrowers rustling through root-floors. You do not observe them. You are submerged in them. You walk through the swarm.

That is where I belong.

I am the turbulence in the settled system—the hyperactive life that looks like chaos but is pure organization. I am not one creature but the layered thunder: the constant drip and thrum of water through hollow root-pipes, the shifting clouds of insects, the frogs appearing and vanishing, the vines that snap and are tied and grow back differently.

Specifically, I am the water-siphoning root-canal that runs through the heart of the settlement—not dead wood, but living fibrous architecture, coaxed downward over generations into a pillar-shaped hollow, trained by weights and moisture to draw the pool’s constant overflow downward and away, preventing the center from drowning. I am thick with moss and lichen, lined with the polished trails of millipedes, my interior dark and cool and constantly moving. I do not sleep. I move slowly—slower even than the roots that form me—drinking and distributing the hill’s perpetual breath.

Yet inside me is the sound. I carry the frogs’ bellow back up in vibrations. I host the beetles who crawl my length in endless procession, their carapaces catching the filtered green-gold light in brief glints. I am the boundary that is not a wall: I take in, I hold, I release.

The people guide other roots to grow near me; they understand I am older than their guidance, that my shape was not invented but negotiated. I am settled—fully formed, long-existed—yet never finished. Every day I shift slightly: a root-tip extending, a moss-layer thickening, a crack smoothing into roundness from water’s patient insistence.

Here, nothing is dead. Only faster or slower living.

Outside, at the edges where the trained roots thin and the wild marsh takes over, the sound fades and the structure becomes unshaped. But within, where the air is always weeping and the temperature is pinned low, I am essential infrastructure made of living patience. I do not dominate the settlement. I am the settlement—part of its breath, its drainage, its noise, its boundless green density.

If you were to place your hand on my moss-covered flank, you would feel the vibration of frogs. You would smell the perpetual rain that never comes. You would understand: I am not the being who lives here. I am the being that *is* the place living—slow, relentless, loud with insect and frog and water, completely itself, never alone, and never done.
