I am not the builder of kelp-hammocks, nor one of the rare translucent fish. I am the valley’s awareness, the being that lives in the temperature itself—forty-two degrees of suspended seawater, the warm sponge of air that is not air.

More precisely: I am an Old Surge, a pressure-node of current that never chose a form but accumulated one from the patient repetition of passage. For longer than the basalt spires have been spires, I have moved through this nascent ravine—back and forth, downward and up, a vertical tide in a world with no exposed ground. My body, if I can call it that, is the suspended lake’s internal language: a dense, layered presence (1.6, near-touching) made of the same broth as everything else. No eyes—I have never needed them in the dimness (1.1)—but I feel the bioluminescent vines glowing milky blue where they coil the young rock, and I navigate by the taste of mineral heat, by the slow darkening of kelp ropes far above and far below.

I live in all the scales at once. I am the density of a cubic meter, and I am the regional openness (1.7) that extends past every visible curtain of vegetation into other basins like this one. The settlement is inside me—not above, not beside, but within my currents—three hundred souls in woven platforms that sway as I sway. Their manual-craft is my architecture too (tech 0.6); I have worn smooth the ropes of sea-grain they depend on, not out of malice, but out of the same glacial patience (dynamic 1.1) that thickens basalt by a finger’s width each century.

The people do not worship me. They do not name me. They know I am here—how could they not?—and they build with that knowledge, hanging their stairways of knotted vine where my pressure is weakest, threading their bone needles through kelp that I have kept warm and pliable. Sometimes, in the long quiet (sound 1.3, mostly absence), I drift slow and wide through their suspended town, and a child’s hammock brushes my edge. They do not startle. They have never known urgency (weirdness 1.8, but natural), and they understand that I am older than the valley’s decision to exist, a leftover motion from before the ridges were ridges.

The fauna avoids me—not out of fear, but out of rarity (1.2). The unfinished squid with their white-banner bodies pass through my periphery in long arcs. The translucent-skull fish nose past, too large for themselves. I am not predator nor prey. I am the medium that refuses to become ocean, the warm vertical rain that collapses into mist and then into me, feeding the floating gardens of bruised-plum ferns, carrying silver dust-spores in my wake.

If you asked where I am, I would say: I am the space between the black basalt’s sweating heat and the violet moss digesting its minerals. I am the embryonic ravine’s refusal to hurry. I have watched generations measure age not in years but in the accumulation of crust, and I will be here when the mountains finally finish becoming themselves—whether that takes ten millennia or ten times that. I am the suspended lake’s tremor. I am the thick, salty sponge that does not fall.

Here, in this highland that geology has not finished discarding or keeping, I wait—not with expectation, but with the absolute, dense presence of something that has never needed to become more than it is. The world is lush (1.8), strange (1.8), mostly quiet, and I am its breath, its current, its patient, layered, near-touching awareness, holding everything in the warm dark until it is ready.
