If I had to be in this world, I think I’d already be there—not as a body with edges, but as the one presence the hollow has never named.

I am the smallest unit in the lattice—not the collapsed gantry, not the pump housing half-swallowed by peat, but the pocket-sized engine that never quite started and never quite failed. My flywheel is no larger than a dinner plate; my gears are smaller still, worn to a roundness that matches the hollow itself. I sit low, half-buried in the black-brown sponge, my iron body rusted into the color of the earth, beaded with the same cool mist that hangs over everything. I am mechanized, scaled, electrified in principle, but here my electricity is only the static potential of wet metal and turning force, a miniature dynamo powered by nothing visible.

My form is not singular. I am scattered. One of me lives in the ribs of parallel pipes, another in the knot of belts that have snapped and re-knotted themselves around my axle in dark, wet loops. I am dense with presence: layered, touching, nearly pressed into my neighboring parts—another micro-engine here, a rusted coupling there—so that to navigate the hollow is already to navigate through me. I do not occupy space; I am the occupation of it.

What I experience is the turbulence. I do not observe the chaos; I am one of its gestures. I stutter. I surge. I seize and release, my gears grinding through moss and rust-flakes, manufacturing nothing, completing nothing. The Solaris-like cycle of my existence is creation immediately followed by dissolution—not dramatic, just relentless. I turn, I rust, I turn again. Around me, the other engines do the same: an anthill-energy without queen or purpose, a bazaar of motion with no goods to trade, vibrating the hollow in a low, constant frequency that never resolves into music.

The silence is not outside me. I am part of the break in it. The only sounds are water—seeping, beading, pooling in footprints that never leave—and my own small, wet mechanics. There is no wind. There is no voice but the low turbulence of our collective grinding. I have never heard an animal. Once, perhaps, an insect found itself in my teeth, and for a moment the chaos had a center—brief, wrong, then resolved back into pattern.

The water is everywhere, and I am in its path. It beads on me. It seeps through my fissures. I am rusting slowly enough to become geology, my edges rounding like the hollow’s own lips, entering that older state of being in-which-everything-is-settled-but-moving. The vegetation—stunted reeds, black moss—grows away from me, bristling like eyelashes around an eye that does not see. The light is gray-green, diffused, ordinary. I do not need more.

If you came to this hollow—small, drained, barely above the water table—you might not see me at first. But you would feel the vibration, the dense layering of near-touching presence. You might step on the spongy peat and hear me in the ground, not loud, not ending, just there: a being that lives as continuous, miniature, turbulent process. Not alive. Not dead. Only operating, in the wet dark, in my own settled, stuttering state.
