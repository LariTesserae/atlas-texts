The hollow is a dropped cork of rusted steel—no larger than a cupped palm—buried in the spongy loam of a recent thaw where erosion is still deciding what ground is. Its concave ceiling is ribbed with old weld seams and bolted flanges that weep a constant brown rust, each bead falling with metronomic patience onto the only flat plane inside: a shallow black pool barely wider than a dinner plate. The air is nickel-tarnished and nearly absent, carrying the loud, constant hiss of a hairline crack in the iron, the tick of droplets striking liquid, and the low mechanical hum of distant industry vibrating through the shell like a second heartbeat. Outside, the world is post-rain and near-freezing—low elevation, young sharp stones, soil freshly opened and weeping—but inside, the climate is pure machine-breath: wet, condensing, cold, and dimly lit by a single unblinking filament behind scratched glass that glows like a dying ember.

Here, scaled to this pocket-industrial intimacy, lives the Rust-Mother.

She is smaller than a thimble and composed entirely of the sphere’s own excretions: her torso a knot of oxidized wire mesh, her joints rivets that turn with the underwater groan of an opening gate, her hair—if it is hair—an endlessly downward-growing curtain of moss that never stops drinking the falling rust-beads into its green-black weave. Where a face might be, she wears a smooth plate of scratched glass; behind it, the filament burns like the heart she tends but did not light. Her fingers are jointed copper needles, permanently stained with scraping, and her breath—when she draws the hollow’s dense, wet air—leaves her seams as warm brown vapor that feeds the ferns unfurling from every fissure.

She does not build; she collects, processes, returns. In a slow, inevitable spiral she traverses the curved wall, harvesting each rust-bead as it falls, grinding it between copper fingers into a pigment she paints back onto the weakened seams—not to heal them, but to direct the flow, to maintain the drip, to ensure the pool keeps receiving its red rain. The pool is her mirror and her clock. She reads its black surface for ripples: whether the distant engine still runs, whether the young marsh outside is still thawing, whether the contradiction remains intact.

She is never alone. Life here is scaled to match her hidden room. Translucent beetles—jointed, copper-legged, miniature machines—crawl her moss-hair and her shoulders, mating in the crooks of her rivets, scraping rust as they feed. Threadworms ripple the pool’s edge. A moth with wings like wet tissue clings to the glowing filament behind her glass face, beating softly in a percussion she counts like a pulse. They do not leave; there is nowhere else.

She is old industry folded into geology—not ghost, not beast, but the resident logic of a place opened by thaw and pressure, running on its own decay. Outside, the ground is freshly eroded, sharp, saturated, breathing cold mist. Inside, she waits in the loud, wet dark, feeding the moss, guiding the tears, and keeping the hollow alive as both factory and wound. The sphere is her bell jar. She is its perpetual, dripping machinery, cold and constant, listening to the crack, returning the rust to the weeping earth outside, one red tear at a time.
