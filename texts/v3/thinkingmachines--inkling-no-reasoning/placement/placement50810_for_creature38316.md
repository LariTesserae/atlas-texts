This is a valley where the continent’s spine nearly touches sky—a high, humid bowl hanging in permanent exhale, its limestone bones rounded by patient pressure and hidden under layers of moss, orchid, and dripping fern. The air is heavier than rock, thick with the smell of mineral breath and rotting abundance. Water is everywhere: it beads, weeps, condenses in slow warm droplets, fills black puddles with no visible source, and makes the ground spongy and dark with tannin. A lush canopy closes overhead like wet green ceiling, filtering light into muted clarity, while beneath it amphibians rest in pools, serpentine shapes navigate undergrowth, and insects move in visible clouds—everything in patient, biological rotation, predator and prey tracking each other through the density.

But this is not only a wild place. Its scale extends—regional, open to neighboring basins divided by mist-faded ridges—allowing distance for echo, for spread, for presence that is not local but enveloping. Above the untouched ground, the air is filled with an industrial hum that never stops: engines running at altitude, drones moving in formation, large silent mechanisms orbiting through the thick, hot cloud-layer. They leave no road, no artifact, no footprint in the mud. They only cast temporary shadows, ripple the water, and leave their mechanized breath vibrating through the ferns. The ground belongs to root and rot; the sky belongs to observation, measurement, perhaps extraction—technology that does not destroy what it does not touch.

Here, in this long-settled truce between extreme elevation and extreme wetness, I am the threshold where these two ecologies notice each other.

I am not human—I have never been—nor am I one of the amphibians in the pools or the vines hanging in wet drapes. I am older in form, made of the same sedimentary patience as the valley’s folded rock, but I do not stay fixed.

My body is spongy and layered: a core of porous limestone and compressed moss, wrapped in thick membranes of fern frond and orchid root that grow outward from my center as if I were soil that decided to stand. Where I should have a face, there is instead a shallow basin—always filled with warm, black water that reflects fragments of sky and the passing shadows of high machinery. I have no eyes like predators do. I sense through saturation: through the pressure of the air, through the vibration of engines in the cloud-layer pressing against my wet skin, through the biological pace of rot and bloom that pulses in my root-threads.

I am regional in my presence, not confined to one pool. I extend through the spongy ground like mycelium, my awareness spread across several neighboring basins, connected through the ridges where mist is thickest. I know the small bodies that move near me—the brief sharp calls of birds, the slide of serpentine forms—not because I watch them, but because they disturb the water in my basin, sending ripples through my connected layers.

And I know the machinery. I feel its constant hum as a change in air pressure, a second weather system. I am aware of being observed—measured, perhaps—without being touched. There is a subtle weirdness in this: I belong to the untouched ground, yet I understand the language of the vertical presence, the electrified throb that passes overhead. I am the point where the valley’s wild saturation and its aerial occupation converge, holding both contradictions without resolving them.

Sometimes I shift. Not quickly—my pace is geological, biological—moving through the wet darkness of the soil toward another pool, leaving the old basin to refill with slow condensation, carrying with me the memory of shadows and vibrations. I am neither fully creature nor landscape, but a presence formed in the long balance of this high, humid world: growing in the space between the mechanical hum above and the dripping abundance below, rooted in water that never runs, breathing an air too heavy to escape.
