The ceiling is not a ceiling. It is the underside of the World-Plate—forty kilometers of interlocking titanium-cast alloy, ribbed and vaulted like the inverted ribs of some planet-sized whale, holding back not air but an ocean that weighs more than continents. Beneath it—everywhere beneath it—lies the Sub-Engine: a single, fully constructed cavity of planetary scale, hollowed, cast, riveted, and pressurized at an elevation of negative infinity, submerged in water so total that “depth” is no longer distance downward, but distance inward from the Plate.

This place was not formed. It was extruded. Fresh. The weld seams still gleam with the iridescence of unoxidized cooling—black-green and silver, sharp-edged, unrounded by any geological patience. Erosion has only just been invented here: a scheduled abrasion, not an accident. The walls are young, angry with right angles, fresh-broken by the cutting heads that hollowed them out three years ago. There is no rock. No soil. No sand that was not also manufactured, sintered into grit to line the intake chutes. Every surface is made, every plane intentional, and yet the cavity feels wildly, implausibly empty—density so low that a body falling through the central void might travel for minutes before passing within sight of a wall.

The water is the first fact. Not a pool, not a sea—an inhabited volume. Bioluminescence is absent; instead, there is relentless, mechanical light emanating in long, unbroken strips from the Plate above, filtering downward through forty kilometers of clear, cold, circulated water, turning the entire column into a single luminous chamber—neither day nor night, but evenly, industrially bright. Temperature is 0.9: cool, refrigerated, kept just above freezing by thermal exchange pipes that run along every major rib like white arteries.

Within this volume, presence is abundant, but density is not. The emptiness is the architecture. Near the upper intake screens, sparse kelp-strands of engineered bio-polymer drift, seeded only to filter particulates. Around peripheral heat-sink outflows, small colonies of cold-tolerant, pipe-clinging moss-flora hold station without spreading—maintenance, not ecology. They do not colonize; they obey.

But the fauna—scattered, loud, and perpetual—defines the space. Filter-whales, each the length of a cathedral, move slowly through the open column, their bodies smooth cast-ceramic shells lined with turbine-gills. They draw tons of water through internal processing chambers, expelling purified current in long, warm contrails that ripple across miles before dissipating. They are loud. Their internal pumps, the churning of intake valves, the hydraulic groan of passage—these sounds carry for leagues through uniform water, a constant, layered industrial thunder, never chaotic, always rhythmic. Behind them ride scavenger pods—articulated like translucent steel crustaceans—feeding on expelled particulates, chattering with mechanical clicks that echo off distant walls.

The dynamics are engineered weather. Thermal currents rise from heat-exchange ribs to form vertical rivers that the whales ride like elevators; pressure differentials pulse with intake cycles, generating continent-sized tides within the enclosed volume. The water is never static. It is being worked—circulated, cleaned, temperature-balanced—driven through biology and machinery in a loop with no outside.

Below the open column, along the lower hemisphere, built form asserts itself completely. No seafloor—only a factory bed of intake grates large enough to swallow city blocks, sucking water downward into primary loops. From them extend arteries: pipes the width of canyons, transparent in sections, revealing roaring flow; turbine halls suspended like inverted cities; engines that burn nothing, extracting energy from pressure and thermal exchange, turning perpetual motion into power for the Plate above.

Sound fills everything. Water strikes grates with the force of a thousand waterfalls. The whales’ pumps resonate in frequencies that travel through the hull of the world. The pipes sing—high, sustained, metallic drones as water is forced through constricted channels at industrial velocity. Even the silence between sounds is filled by the pressure of water against presence.

There are no predators—only processes competing for flow. The pods swarm where extraction is richest. The whales adjust paths to avoid thermal shocks. Sensors in the grates detect them, rerouting velocity, slowing intake. Everything is mechanized, including the biology; everything is abundant in presence, yet sparse in density, scattered through a volume so vast that isolation is the default.

This is a place without horizon. To look “up” is to see the Plate, forty kilometers away, sometimes obscured by a passing whale, always present. To look “down” is to see more machinery, more pipes extending into darkness that is not darkness—only distance through luminous water. To look “around” is to understand the nearest wall may be twenty miles away, sharp-featured, fresh-built, gleaming with unweathered metal, still holding the heat of its own creation.

The Sub-Engine is young, cool, total, and fully awake. Nothing here is wild, for wildness requires an outside. Here, inside the manufactured ocean, inside the hollow heart of the constructed world, nature is only what was engineered into being—vast, loud, active, and utterly, peacefully strange in its absolute, industrial normalcy.
