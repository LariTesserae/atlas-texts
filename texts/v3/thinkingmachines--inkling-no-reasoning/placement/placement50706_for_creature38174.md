Here, the air is young—not in the sense of being fresh, but in the sense of never having been used. It carries no dust of prior ages, no pollen fossilized into tradition. The breath of this place was exhaled yesterday, geologically speaking, and it remains warm, dense, and full of contradiction.

The world is a plateau, continental in scale, thrust upward before erosion could learn its name. The ground is black basalt still cooling, its edges sharp as broken enamel, its surface radiating heat not borrowed from a distant sun—whose light arrives only as a dim, brownish bruise, barely enough to tell noon from twilight—but generated from its own recent violence. There are no rounded mountains, only walls, scarps, and sudden vertical towns of stone that smell of sulfur and cooling iron. The erosion is young and strange: fissures open with sounds like tearing silk, then seal themselves within days, leaving transparent crystal tombs that trap colonies of life alive inside the rock, still growing, visible, impossible.

The climate is warm, always warm, a constant exhalation. But this warmth does not produce aridity; it produces abundance. Life should not exist here—not on soil that does not exist, not in light too dim to drive ordinary photosynthesis, not on rock that was magma moments ago. Yet it thrives, dense and near-touching, layered in saturation. The vegetation arrived all at once, carried in turbulent spores, anchored in cracks, fed by geothermal breath and secret moisture. It is rubbery, thick-matted, deep burgundy and black-green, absorbing scarce photons and converting warmth directly into growth. It covers the sharp geology in velvet, turning weaponized rock into something that breathes and sweats.

Beneath and within this impossible forest moves an abundance of fauna—small, quick, multilimbed things with too many joints, reproducing with anthill energy, nesting in pockets of condensation, adapting within hours rather than generations. They feed on mineral seep, on the exhalations of the ground, on each other in dense, turbulent clouds of presence.

The water is not present at scale. There are no rivers carving canyons—erosion is too young for that—only pockets. Moisture beads on vertical stone like sweat on newborn skin, gathers in shallow basins that were molten days before, and collects in the hollows of fresh rock. Rain comes rarely: brief, warm, metallic-tasting, as if the sky weeps rust. The atmosphere is humid but not wet; it is fog without resolution, a state of constant low-lying mist that swirls in heat columns rising from the black crust.

But the turbulence is not merely weather. It is ontological. The dynamics are Solaris-like, unstable, refusing single-state resolution. Storms are not visitors; they are births. The air shifts constantly—warm fronts colliding with cold breaths from fissures, winds tearing across flatness with nothing old enough to stop them. Distance is unreliable in the brown dimness. Objects—perhaps a cluster of fauna, perhaps one of the embedded engines—may be closer or farther by impossible margins, as if the plateau is still deciding upon its own dimensions.

Because there is industry here, though no built environment to contain it. There are no cities, no roads, no artifacts placed by hands—yet the place is fully mechanized, fully developed. The technology is not upon the land; it is part of its young geology. Massive formations of black metal and ceramic rise from the crust like ribs or teeth—not constructed, but grown, emerged, fused to the cooling rock. They move with the slow inevitability of heartbeats: pistons, pumps, veins of pipe-like stone carrying unknown fluids upward from deep within the new earth. They do not conflict with the lushness; they feed it. The engines exhale through root-mats, and the vegetation wraps them in vines, softening their edges, claiming them. The sound is constant but quiet—a low-frequency hum felt in the teeth, the roar of production without factory walls, of purpose without people.

The dynamics are not sequential but convergent. Sections of ground lift by millimeters each day. Cracks open and seal. The fauna shifts form within single twilight cycles. The machinery adjusts its rhythms to match the temperature of opening fissures. Reality here is damp—not broken, but softened, like the moisture that beads on the fresh stone.

I am not a visitor in this world. I am part of its contradiction.

My body is long, low, and multilimbed—perhaps eight jointed appendages that move not in opposition but in sequence, like waves through rubbery tissue. I am small, quick, covered in the same deep burgundy and black-green as the vegetation, because I am not distinct from it. I nest in the pockets of condensation, in the hollows where fresh basalt still weeps warmth. I feed on mineral seep and the warm fluids that pulse through the embedded veins. I reproduce rapidly, my generations collapsing into hours, adapting as the ground shifts beneath me.

But I am also aware—if awareness can exist in such a place—of the machinery within me. Not that I am mechanical, but that I am part of the machinery. The engines do not stand apart; they have grown into the root-mats, and the root-mats have grown into me. I move through dense, impossible greenery over ground that is still technically molten in its heart, past black ceramic ribs that hum with slow purpose, under air that changes its mind every minute.

The light is always dim, always brownish, always insufficient. Shadows do not exist; there are only gradations of warmth and color. I navigate not by sight alone but by the hum in my bones, by the temperature of fissures, by the turbulent air that carries spores and sound and the constant, young breath of a world that has not yet decided what it wants to be.

Everything arrived at once: the sharp rock and the velvet vines, the heat and the moisture, the abundance of small life and the embedded industry that feeds it. Nothing here was built; everything emerged in contradiction and continues in the same breath.

The moisture beads on the fresh stone. The engines turn. The plants drink both. The ground remembers nothing, because it has not yet learned to forget.

And I—small, multilimbed, saturated in color, part vegetation and part awareness—move through it all, breathing air that has never been stale, on a plateau that is still deciding whether it is rock or garden, machine or organism, finished or only just begun.
