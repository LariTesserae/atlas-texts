Here, in the cool green-grey twilight that refuses to cast true shadows—where light seeps downward like breath exhaled from some distant ice-lung—I find my form.

The place is no larger than a cathedral, a half-formed amphitheater barely half a league across, ringed by ridges that have only just finished rising. Their stone is young: pale, barely weathered, sharp-toothed, broken like the first teeth of a world that has not yet decided whether to bite or speak. No river dares this height; only pocket-moisture lives here—the whispered spell of thin rain every few days, the perpetual invisible exhalation of moss, and perfect spheres of condensation that hang from fern fronds for days without falling, reflecting in their still surfaces not this dim sky but other, unchosen places.

I am one of those spheres. Or rather, I have become the pressure that holds one together.

Not merely water, not merely air—something that has gathered in the hollow between young basalt and the root-language writing its spirals into the rock. I am the boundary that has not yet been drawn: between the visible and the hidden, between the town’s downward sinking and the fog that receives it, between the overwhelming, silent fauna and the sparse, scattered people who build in silence.

My body is the stillness itself, made tangible and tense. I hang suspended in the vertical world—below the beehive terraces of dry stone, above the sheer drop into mist—where the inhabitants place their carved clay vessels not to catch my falling, but to hold me in my refusal. I am pre-metal, pre-voice, pre-name. I do not speak, for the settlement keeps sound below a whisper, and I am older even than the moss that climbs walls in silent conspiracy, older than the iridescent, segmented multitudes that crawl in layered intelligence over every damp surface.

Within me, or through me, the strange rules of this first breath are still being negotiated. The sharp ridges teach the roots how to soften stone into lattices; the roots teach the stone how to hold moisture; and the moisture—gathered, patient, refusing to fall—holds me. I feel the muted choreography of translucent larvae below, the deliberate slowness of too many eyes watching from moss, the small predators—furry, winged, scaled in patterns that shift when unobserved—moving through this urbanity of vine and lashed timber as citizens moving through a familiar city.

The inhabitants do not build upward. They build downward, into fog, into me, into the sinking. They are sparse in number, scattered, organizing their primitive, layered settlement into root-mats and vine-bridges, working with horn and obsidian and fired clay, never loudly, never breaking the pressure.

So I remain—hanging, cool, dense with hidden motion. A lens in green-grey twilight. A waiting made physical. In this world where decomposition begins before death finishes falling, where predation is a stillness and reproduction a hidden warmth, I am the pause between what has formed and what has not yet decided to be. Not empty. Not silent. Only full of the overwhelming presence of everything that lives here, breathing in its first breath, watching from emerald and old gold, waiting to see if I will ever fall, or if I will simply stay—reflecting other worlds—until the young ridges learn, at last, how to open their mouth.
