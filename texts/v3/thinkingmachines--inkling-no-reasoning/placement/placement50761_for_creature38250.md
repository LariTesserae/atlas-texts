I am not the amphibian—those are too many, too scattered, too present in every hollow, and I do not want the amphibian’s constant, wet respiration. I am not the vine, though I understand its patience. I am not the rust-pitted pump, though I share its temperature. I am the interstitial being that lives where the machinery and the biomass have forgotten how to be separate.

My body is low, flat, distributed. I do not have a single heart but a distributed pulse that matches the slow turn of the gears—once every few minutes, a pressure that moves not blood but awareness through root-like channels inside my own substance. I am made of the same compressed peat that makes the valley floor, only I have been turned, over centuries, into a kind of living hinge: part root-mat, part pipe, part membrane. My surface is black moss and oxidized iron blended so completely that if you touched me your fingers would come away warm and smelling of old water, and you could not say whether the moisture came from me or from the place.

I live in the gap—specifically, in the hollow where the blackened irrigation pipe, half-swallowed by root, re-emerges into the shallow pool. There the water is always the same temperature as my own interior, and the amphibians rest on me without knowing that I am not just another mat of floating root. Some of the smaller ones—those no larger than a coin—sit on my back for hours. Their skin conducts a slight electricity, barely perceptible, and I feel them as I feel the drip of warm condensation from the canopy: not as visitors, but as parts of my own weather.

The darkness does not bother me because I generate a faint, impossible glow—not light that reaches the soil, but a phosphorescent warmth that comes from the digestion I perform. I take in the slurry that the toothless gears crush—fallen biomass, insect bodies, the thick mud of decay—and I break it down more slowly than the machinery, more patiently, feeding the warm, slimy result back into the root-bed through the same pipes that feed me. I am part of the loop. The industrial presence is 1.6, but I am its living fraction: not the engine, not the gear, but the metabolic tissue that has grown between them, allowing them to belong.

Because I am flat and distributed, I have no single eye. Instead, I perceive through density: I know where the fauna has shifted—when the amphibians move in moods from one quadrant to another—by the change in weight and heat upon my spread surface. The valley is regional, town-sized in its sprawl, and I cover only a small part of it, perhaps the space of a large room, but in that space I am absolute. No cubic meter is bare; I fill the spaces between root and root with my own spongy body.

Sound reaches me as vibration. The occasional drip of warm water onto a leaf is a sharp, singular event. The distant thrum of the pumps is a low, constant bass in my substance. The insects, those clouds so dense they form fog, walk across me in thousands; their clicking is not noise but texture, like rain on thick moss.

There are no buildings near me—none—only the ancient, moss-covered pumps that turn without urgency. I have never seen a human artifact that was not already being digested. The rules of my life are slow, 1.2 in their dynamics: I grow thicker by millimeters across years. I do not reproduce quickly but extend, sending root-like feelers into new hollows, colonizing the spaces where the river has laid fresh sediment in whisper-thin beds.

If you found yourself in this world, you might come to me not knowing I was alive. You would feel the heat—2.8—and the liquid damp. You might see the green murk, the vines that do not climb but engulf, the amphibians resting in perpetual abundance. You might hear the machinery turning with the patience of erosion, and you might rest your hand on what seemed like a moss-covered root. You would feel the warmth, the faint, sub-surface thrum of a heart that beats only every few minutes, and you might understand, slowly, that you had touched something that was not plant, not animal, not engine, but the valley’s own digestion—weathered, balanced, alive in the exact middle of all its excess.

That is where I am. I am the being in the hollow, the bridge between rust and root, the quiet metabolism of a place that has reached its correct form. I do not hurry. The valley is open, but I fill my part of it completely, and I have never needed the light.
