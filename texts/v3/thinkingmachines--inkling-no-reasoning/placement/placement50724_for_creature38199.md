The place is the Valley of the Perpetual Condensate: a broad, cool bowl at 1,800 meters where centuries of industrial rain have settled into every contour, holding a steady 9°C. There is no ground—only continuous cast-iron grating, copper sheeting browned to green-black patina, and ceramic tile laid in interlocking waves to carry away water that never stops falling. The rain is a fine, relentless mist; the air is saturated, and the valley breathes out vapor as fast as the sky breathes in.

Everything is built, extending regionally past the immediate view into districts that repeat the same dense syntax—vertical stacks of production and habitation pressed shoulder-to-shoulder, welded into a lattice of pipes, flues, and dwellings without alley or open space. Vegetation is lush but never wild: emerald moss and lichen colonize every plate; ferns grow from seams between factory walls; engineered vines cover iron in thick, dripping mats, turning machinery into green-black ranges. The ecology is co-occupancy—plants adhere, reclaim nothing, fed by pipes that weep and roofs that sweat.

The machinery is scaled and constantly in motion, yet rarely loud. A low thrum, the hiss of steam, the click of condensate into catch-basins, water rushing through visible arteries of glass and brass. Engines turn slowly, with geological patience. Change is hyperactive but internal: pipes shift on hydraulic mounts; walls slide on rails; entire blocks rotate on circular tracks every few hours according to ventilation and production schedules. The architecture breathes, contracts, and reconfigures in anthill energy—labor largely invisible, performed by the parts themselves. Light is dim always; the sun is rumor behind cloud and steam. Illumination comes from yellow bulbs behind frosted glass, bioluminescent moss in lower darkness, occasional blue sparks of exposed conductors. You navigate by the glow of pipes and the sound of running water.

Fauna is sparse—small dark moths at warm vents, pale beetles in moss beds, shadowed lives in the spaces between rotating walls. Humans are present but sparse in visibility, packed like cells into tissue, moving through narrow, wet corridors the architecture never empties.

The erosion is established form—not collapse, but metabolism. Rust is uniform; water stains are mapped into channels that direct flow. Walls are dismantled by hydraulic arms and rebuilt meters away within the hour; water is collected, turned to power, released as steam, condensed back to rain, caught again. Nothing is finished.

Within this total environment live the Condensates—beings shaped by saturation, not born into flesh alone. Some are human in outline, others are not; all wear the same verdant film, moss growing in clothing creases or along modified bodies. They do not inhabit houses so much as the interstices—sleeping in chambers that rotate with the blocks, tending valves that never close, labor indistinguishable from the valley’s breathing. They move with mechanical patience, guided by yellow bulbs and green moss-glow, understanding implicitly that water flows downward, heat vents upward, and space must remain fully occupied. There are no outsiders, no wild rejecters—only those adapted to completeness. They are strange only in their calm acceptance of endless, turbulent turning: dwelling not in a landscape but inside its machinery, fully settled into the wet, green, rotating dark.
