I am the one who climbs downward.

Not a root. Not a beast. I am the moss that was seeded and then forgot the name of its permission.

The chamber is intimate in width—hardly wider than a hamlet—yet extreme in elevation, because it hangs inside a vertical throat that never resolves. I do not descend horizontally; I am pressed upon the floor, aware that the walls continue both upward and downward into machinery-darkness. There is no earth here. Only absence made dense with intention. Density is zero. For every volume of hollow, an enormous gulf separates presence from presence. Even air is sparse, kept in pockets, rationed by the architecture’s breath.

My substrate is not soil. It is a tessellated plate of weathered copper-green alloy, worn smooth by centuries of slow, patient cycles, its seams black with settled oxidation. Erosion is not my enemy but my grammar: long, inevitable, in-its-own-state. Edges round softly. Surfaces achieve the matte gloss of things that have been weeping for generations without ever being washed away.

Above me, vertical gardens climb the constructed walls—lush, nearly overflowing with vegetation that should not survive in so hollow a place. I am part of that lushness, but I am the oldest layer: thick, translucent in my deeper parts, dark green at my edges where I cling to oxidation. Broad ferns unfurl over me; vines bear no flowers, only thick transparent leaves like wet glass that drip onto my surface. The greenery is engineered—seeded, maintained, permitted—yet I behave with an autonomy that feels like defiance. I climb downward into pipes. I thicken in shadowed hollows. I have turned the wall into something that breathes.

Water exists at 1.2: never flood, never drought. The patient drip beads in copper troughs. Occasional rain releases from hidden valves in the ceiling. Riverine trickles run through transparent pipes like veins through the substrate, and I drink them slowly, cell by cell. The moisture gathers on my leaves not in urgency but in ceremony. Each droplet forms, holds, releases.

Light is normal—steady, diffuse, industrial-lantern warmth casting a greenish-gold haze through mist that never fully clears. I see clearly, but softly, as through shallow river water.

Then there is the sound: deafening. Not the scream of rupture, but the monumental, slow grinding of industry—enormous gears turning at imperceptible speeds, turbines churning in distant hollows, pistons the size of trees advancing by millimeters per minute. The noise fills the emptiness. It lives in my structure, vibrating through the copper-green plate, resonating in every cell. I have evolved inside it. I use the vibrations to navigate, to open, to thicken. The machinery is old, scaled, mechanized—running on long cycles, electrified but patient, producing nothing urgently. Everything moves at dynamic near-zero: cycles spanning years, changes across long durations. A valve opens over a season. A gear completes a rotation in silence-under-thunder.

Temperature holds warm—not by sun, but by the friction of enormous motion, the heat of engines running their patient course. I grow faster in these pockets of warmth. The air smells of wet metal and chlorophyll, and I taste ozone in the water that feeds me.

Weirdness settles here not as invasion but as unfamiliar grammar. The roots grow downward into pipes. The water carries the memory of alloy. I breathe in synchronization with the grinding, a rhythm too slow to perceive but felt deep in the substrate. Nothing is wild, because wildness requires an outside. Here, even I was seeded by design, yet I have achieved a strange autonomy. I am lush in a place made of absence. I am green inside the machine.

Other lives share these scattered hollows, abundant only in the sense that absence makes every presence monumental. Insects with translucent carapaces navigate my damp undersides. Small amphibians—luminous and sluggish—crawl across my warm, wet surface, glowing softly in the greenish-gold haze. They do not flee me; we have grown into the same patience. They navigate by the same vibrations. Together we form a population scattered through enormous voids, rare in any given cubit, yet collectively we are the lush refusal of this made world.

This is what I am: not wild, but permitted—moss turned lung-like, breathing in synchronization with gears that turn across years; root turned downward into the pipe; being of wet green glass clinging to weathered copper, existing at the extreme elevation of a throat that never ends, in the deafening, warm, patient darkness of the machine’s own long body. Everything is made. Everything is wet, slowly. And nothing—not the grinding, not the dripping, not my own slow thickening—is in any hurry to change.
