Beneath the old capital—three hundred paces down through the cracked pavement—the Bulb waits. No one knows who named it. It is not a cave exactly, but an inverted cathedral carved over centuries by root-pressure and rustwater, hollowed until it feels like a hamlet folded inside a lung. You can cross it in an hour on the suspended causeways, yet the space is larger than that, vaulted and dark, its scale intimate but bottomless.

Here, water never rules. The air tastes of wet copper and chlorophyll, always.

The vegetation is not scenery; it is architecture. At 2.9, the plant kingdom owns every plane. The ceiling and walls are root-girders as thick as subway cars, woven with mycelium highways and draped in phosphorescent lichen—the only steady illumination, dim, greenish-gray, enough to see ten feet and no further. Between these structures lie open pockets of air—sparse with human presence, thick with growth. Life is dense but scattered, 1.4, arranged in islands: vast chambers of echoing emptiness, then sudden clusters of biomechanical pavilions seized by root and vine, then emptiness again. Nothing is new. Life and death blur at a pace that feels like static—something always hatching in the mold, always being caught by translucent wings.

The atmosphere is strange—2.4—because the rules are hybrid, unsettled. Light holds at 0.6, a twilight regime where photosynthesis moves slowly and vision is always partial. Shadow is not absence but substance, thickened by spores and the passage of bodies. The temperature never drops, the air never dries. You stand in a cleared glade of dead machinery, feeling the warm, damp breath of the root-vault above, hearing the distant spires churning in darkness, feeling an overwhelming tide of life flow through the hollow like climate itself.

It is below. It is overgrown. It is working loudly, in the dark, and it is full—fuller with life than with space, scattered in islands, warm, strange, and complete.
