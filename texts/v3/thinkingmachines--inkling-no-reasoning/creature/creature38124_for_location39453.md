Here live the ones who do not need sky—who came not to settle but to wait for settlement to finish deciding on them.

They are scattered, these inhabitants, matching the chamber’s own reluctant density: not citizens, but cohabitants of machinery and moisture. First, the **Insects of the Slow Thunder**—moths with dusty brown wings the span of palms, beating in patient clouds around every orange bulb; armored beetles clicking like falling stones across the half-settled floor; swarms of smaller things—midges, gnats—forming breathable curtains in the humid pockets near the forges. They are overwhelming in number, utterly normal, filling the air with a sound like constant, soft rainfall made of bodies. They do not flee the heat. The heat is the architecture of their lives.

Then, the **Small Warm Ones**—lizards with bodies adapted to crushing temperatures, scaling the rusted pipes with feet that do not slip on condensate; rats of hardened stock, their fur dulled to the color of wet stone, moving through the broad dark gulfs between structures with the confidence of true natives. They navigate not by sight—the light is too dim, too shadow-heavy—but by the gradients of warmth, threading closer to the glowing grates when the deep machinery exhales, retreating to the weeping walls when a valve releases its long, hissing breath.

There are also the **Pipe-Gardeners**, barely visible: colonies of grey lichen clinging to iron in stubborn patches, and one or two stunted vines that have found the condensate lines, wrapping them in green so dark it is almost black, less vegetation than rust that has learned to grow.

But the true residents—the ones who will outlast any map—are the **Engines Themselves**, the black iron lungs in scattered alcoves. They are not alive in the biological sense, yet they breathe: pumping, contracting, exhaling steam through valves that open and close on geologic schedules. Around them, the sparse urban bones—scaffolding, tracks, grates—cling like preliminary sketches. The machinery is scaled down, intimate, separated from its kin by broad empties, yet it persists with mechanical patience.

All of them—the insects swarming, the lizards sunning on warm metal, the rats nesting in hollows beneath the unfinished floor—live within rhythms not of day or season, but of long-duration change. Condensation collects; it falls. The pumps labor, then rest. The temperature holds at its crushing 2.9. The floor cracks slowly, revealing older dark spaces, and from those spaces come other crawlers, smaller ones, seeking the same warmth.

This is not a population that thrives despite the chamber. It thrives *as* the chamber—ordinary, overwhelming, embedded in the unfinished stone, filling the spaces that urban intent forgot to claim. They are the first citizens of a place that has not yet earned a name. When the forges eventually cool and the pipes fall silent, the moths will still beat their wings against the dead bulbs, the lizards will still seek the last stored warmth in the iron, and the water will continue its weeping, mineral and patient, over them all. They do not live here. They *are* here—scattered, low-density, enduring—while the settlement around them remains forever in the act of becoming.
