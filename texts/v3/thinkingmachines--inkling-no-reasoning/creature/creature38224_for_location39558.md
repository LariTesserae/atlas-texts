Inside lives the root-weave itself—no citizens, only architecture that is also inhabitant.

Thick arterial vines form the vaulted corridors and suspended platforms; smaller filaments knit the lattice walls and spiraled stair-cords that grip the sharp young basalt. There is no builder separate from the building—each root is both wall and dweller, tightening its hold on fresh gypsum and pale basalt in cycles so slow that a finger’s shift takes a season.

Through these root-corridors move the only other residents: dozens of small, translucent beings shelled in pearl, feeding on mineral crust, mating in long silent years. They are blood-in-tissue here—quiet, abundant, sliding through the biome without voice, leaving only the occasional click of shell against vine.

The air itself feels inhabited—not by breath, but by warm, steady presence seeping up from a shallow biological hearth below. Every ledge wears a carpet of rust-red and bone-white moss-analogs, fibrous and waxy, drinking mineral mist rather than water. Nothing green exists; everything is upholstered in living felt, thick with vine and leaf that do not transpire.

This is a sealed, urban ecology of absolute drought. Light enters diffuse through ceiling fissures—no source, only presence—revealing a settlement-scale grove that refuses to be dead. There is no technology, no fire, no metal, no tool—only the patient, pre-tool logic of growth arranging itself into rooms and bridges.

The place lives at the pace of its own construction: glacial, mineral-bound, young. The stone remembers its recent birth; the roots remember nothing older. Together they wait—warm, silent, lush without thirst—in this forty-pace cathedral of dry breath and pearl-shelled motion.
