Here is the place that lives in those low, settled flats.

What lives there is not any one creature but an entire climate of persistence—an immense, hidden metropolis of segmented crawlers and translucent larvae and hard-shelled multitudes that have turned the absence of people into the presence of something older and more patient. They live in the dark humidor of hollow pipes, beneath the floor grates, across the surfaces of sluggish canals where warm water moves slowly enough to hold footprints of no one. They do not invade; they inherit. They have been inheriting for so long that the rusted conveyors and lowered smokestacks have forgotten they were ever meant for other hands, and now serve only as architecture—rounded by years of corrosive breath, smoothed into familiar shapes by the same moisture that feeds the life beneath.

The settlement itself lives there, too—not dead, but in a long sleep of its own making. Its machinery breathes. Generators turn over in their sleep with a low, constant vibration. Cooling metal clanks in distant yards, and condensate drips with the regularity of a clock that measures nothing but its own endurance. There are no sharp edges left; erosion and time have worn everything into the appearance of inevitability, as if the town grew out of the black basalt and stagnant water table rather than having been placed upon it. The buildings are arranged with the logic of decades, patient and dense with infrastructure—layered pipes, storage silos, rail spurs stilled—yet the spaces between them are vast, open, extending in every direction across cracked obsidian paving and shallow pools that mirror a dim, cloud-filtered sky.

Above all this, the emptiness lives. It is a physical presence, a hollow volume that fills every cubic meter not occupied by rust or chitin. The air holds it—thick, humid, carrying the mineral scent of hot wet stone, poor with light, gray-brown and deceptive in distance. No trees soften it. No grasses split the paving. No vines. The surface belongs entirely to mineral and metal and the continuous, slow weeping of the earth.

But do not mistake the emptiness for death. The ground moves subtly with hidden weight. In chambers below, in canals barely above the water table, across every warm dark surface, the multitudes feed and lay and migrate in cycles that keep the place in constant, quiet motion. Their presence is overwhelming—not seen in bodies, but inferred in the faint organic click of chitin against rust, in the slow tide of movement just beneath awareness, in the way the air itself seems to hold a background hum of activity that never quite resolves into a single sound. They are exactly the ordinary life of warm, wet, mineral-rich places, except here there is nothing to limit them—no predators, no seasons, no end.

What lives there, then, is a balance without observers: the machinery in its subdued sleep, the architecture rounded by its own long exhalation, the hidden populations in their endless ordinary cycles, and the vast, open, horizontal absence that accommodates them all. It is a place that has become more itself the longer it waits—quiet, active, profoundly empty of anything except what belongs there, extending past every view in a flat, warm, wet, rust-colored infinity.
