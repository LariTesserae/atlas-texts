What lives in this place is the absence of anything accidental: every presence is a deliberate insertion into a volume so large that emptiness itself is the architecture.

The ceiling is the underside of the World-Plate—forty kilometers of interlocking titanium-cast alloy, ribbed and vaulted like the inverted flank of some continent-sized whale, holding back not air but an ocean that outweighs landmasses. Beneath it lies the Sub-Engine, a single, fully formed cavity of planetary scale, not excavated but extruded: hollowed, cast, and riveted fresh, the weld seams still iridescent with unoxidized black-green and silver, sharp and unrounded, too young for erosion except the kind that was scheduled. There is no rock, no soil, no sand that was not manufactured and sintered into grit for the intake chutes. Every plane is intentional, every angle right and recent—broken only three years ago by cutting heads—yet the cavity feels implausibly empty, its density so low that a falling body might travel for minutes through the central void before passing within sight of a wall.

The water is not a pool but a fully inhabited volume—mechanically circulated, refrigerated to 0.9 degrees, kept just above freezing by thermal exchange pipes that run like white arteries along every rib. Light does not come from sun or star; it pours downward in long, unbroken industrial strips embedded in the Plate, filtering through forty kilometers of perfectly clear, cold water to turn the entire column into a single luminous chamber—never day, never night, evenly and relentlessly bright.

Life is present but never crowded; abundance is measured in motion, not density. Near the upper intake screens, sparse kelp-strands of engineered bio-polymer drift, seeded only to filter particulates. Around peripheral heat-sink outflows, small colonies of cold-tolerant moss-flora cling to pipes, holding station without spreading—maintenance organisms, not ecology. The true inhabitants are large, scattered, and loud.

Filter-whales dominate—each the length of a cathedral, their bodies smooth cast-ceramic shells lined with turbine-gills, moving slowly through miles of open column. They draw tons of water through internal processing chambers and expel purified current in warm contrails that ripple for leagues before dissolving. They are loud: the churning of intake valves, the hydraulic groan of passage, the internal pumps—all resonating through water and structure in constant, layered industrial thunder, rhythmic, never chaotic. Behind them ride scavenger pods—articulated like translucent steel crustaceans—feeding on expelled particulates, chattering with mechanical clicks that echo off distant walls.

The dynamics are perpetual and self-engineered. Thermal currents rise from heat-exchange ribs to form vertical rivers the whales ride like elevators; pressure differentials pulse with intake cycles, generating continent-sized tides within the enclosed volume. There are no predators—only processes competing for flow. The whales adjust to avoid thermal shocks; sensors in the grates detect them and reroute velocity. All biology is mechanized; all machinery is responsive.

Below, along the lower hemisphere, there is no seafloor—only a factory bed of intake grates vast enough to swallow cities, sucking water downward into primary loops. From them extend arteries—pipes wide as canyons, some transparent, revealing roaring flow; turbine halls suspended like inverted cities; engines that burn nothing, extracting power from pressure and thermal exchange. The sound is structural: water strikes grates like a thousand waterfalls; pipes sing with high metallic drones; the constant pressure fills even the spaces between sounds with the presence of work being done.

This is a place without horizon—looking up reveals the Plate, forty kilometers distant, visible through clear luminous water except when obscured by a passing whale; looking down reveals only more machinery, more grates, more pipes extending into darkness that is merely distance. The nearest wall may be twenty miles away, fresh-built, gleaming, still warm from its own creation.

The Sub-Engine is young, cool, total, and fully awake. Wildness is impossible here, for it requires an outside. Within this manufactured ocean, inside the hollow heart of the world, the only nature is the one that was engineered into being: vast, loud, active, and utterly, peacefully strange in its absolute, industrial normalcy.
