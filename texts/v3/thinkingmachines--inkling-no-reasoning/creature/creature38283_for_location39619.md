Here lives a world-plateau of worn basalt, blackened and river-smooth, holding at a height that never freezes and never warms—just a constant, secretive damp-chill that keeps moss awake and spore-floating. The erosion finished its work long ago; extreme elevation is now flatness, a sky-table stretching past sight, buried so deep beneath compressed centuries of plant that rock and root dissolved into each other.

What lives here is a continuous architecture of biomass—giant bryophytes standing as trunk-like columns, calcified vines serving as walls and ribs, canopies of fern and moss vaulted overhead like green cathedral roofs. The green is total, except where brown root-bones and grey petrified bark show the deep skeleton. The space feels open, regional, extending valley-wide, yet every breath is technically inside the plant. Its density is hollow—mass arranged like ribs, leaving echoing chasms and corridors of hanging cable-root where one might travel for an hour through suspended moss, seeing only the occasional drip, feeling like air inside the lung of an enormous creature.

That creature breathes. Moisture is never rain but presence—intermittent beads on fungal plates, vapor pooled in hollows of fossilized bark, thin films running through root-channels that serve as the only rivers. The sound is deafening and layered, not weather but biology: membranous-winged insects beating in synchronized clouds, amphibious things with many soft feet moving through the pools with wet resonant croaks, small feather-bone avians piercing the vaults with sharp cries. Below and within, invisible, is the saturation—nothing silent, every surface shifting, every shadow piled with hidden life.

Light moves diffuse through the green vault—steady, filtered, a perpetual twilight-emerald where shadow is replaced by deeper shades of green. Clouds drift at eye-level through root-spires, leaving wetness behind. There is no ground to walk upon; one climbs, hangs, or navigates root-channels. The temperature remains that mild damp warmth, moisture returns without invitation, and the fauna does not flee—they observe, they continue.

Human presence is scattered, primitive, pre-metal—low flat-stone walls half-eaten, wooden levers dark with age, stone blades fallen, ropes of braided vine-fiber now part of the walls they tied. Ruins not of grandeur but of persistence, of people who lived inside the plant rather than upon it.

The weirdness is subtle: root-bridges align with precision that suggests intent older than any builder; pools never fully evaporate despite constant air; and in deepest hollows, fossilized bark bears grooved spirals—counting, perhaps, or prayer—made by something that used neither, arranged too regularly for wind, too ancient for recent hands.

This is the settled form. No young mountains, only old ones ground to humility, serving as foundation for living architecture. The world is not dying but completed—eroded to essence, filled with life to deafening saturation, and almost entirely empty of anything that is not green, not alive, and not listening.
