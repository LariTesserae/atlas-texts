Here live the ones who never arrived, and never needed to—the continuous inhabitants of a settlement that spends its long absence like breath.

They are the root-cities beneath, seen only in translucent floors: older coherences, earlier attempts at being a town, layered like sediment under the present walk. They do not move so much as remember, and in remembering they shift—corridors that were straight become gently curved by morning; a stair of compressed fiber remembers a different purpose and offers its tread to the wrong foot. They live in repetition, in the mechanical rehearsal of growth that is also decay, in the endless circulation of cool water pumped through hollow pipe-bone. Their bodies are the vines that hang like isolated fruits between the districts, the corky bark that forms walls without doors, the spongy plates that serve as both ceiling and lung.

The loud ones live everywhere and nowhere at once. They are the thunder in root and resin—the slow boom of translucence being drawn through miles of hollow infrastructure, the clack of root-wheels crawling through living streets, the pressure-engine that hums not with fire but with the insistence of being maintained. They are industry given plant-form, wooden gears that prevent the long sleep. They do not work *upon* the place; they are the place working, manufacturing its own continuation.

Rare are the small ones—flickers of translucence in ventilation pores—who seem almost like visitors, or like symptoms of the architecture’s strange memory. They crawl through pores that should be too narrow, leaving no mark but a brief wrongness in the air, a shadow that feels wet.

What lives here, finally, is the damp itself: the perpetual exhalation of pore and stoma, beading into pearls along root-corridors, falling in scattered rhythm onto hollow floors that have become drums. It is the climate that never warms, suspended in its coolness, carrying the sound of internal rivers from quadrant to quadrant. The light is flat, clear, casting sharp shadows through sparse canopies, yet color arrives with wrong feeling—a bright vine that sounds like a bell, a darkness that tastes of old rain.

This is a place inhabited by its own end-state: an ecosystem that has reached coherence and now merely circulates itself, growing downward like inverted trees, connected by aerial causeways of woven stem that sway not with wind—there is none—but with the internal pressure of being alive for too long. There are no people. There are no animals. Only the slow, loud machinery of plant-civilization, the intermittent drip of water falling through layered ages, and the quiet, enormous presence of something that has built itself into being and now waits, not for arrival, but for the permission to finish remembering.
