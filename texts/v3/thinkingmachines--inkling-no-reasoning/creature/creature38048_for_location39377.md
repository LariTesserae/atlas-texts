Here lives the Sump—though “lives” is almost too quick a word for what occupies this sixty-meter sphere, because nothing in it hurries, and nothing in it is born of accident. It is a dome of green-black alloy and ceramic ribbing, newborn, still venting the heat of its own breaking-through into a world that offers no sky, only a darker, wetter gradient climbing away into infinite warm saturation. You are high inside it—suspended in a vertical shaft of liquid that has no bottom and no top—while being entirely, breath-deep, submerged. The contradiction holds: enclosed, yet open to an atmospheric column that refuses to end.

Inside, space is not left empty to happen; it is built to its edges. Every cubic hand is accounted for: grooved plates, mesh grilles, thick conduits in near-perfect grids that pack the volume wall to wall, root to root. There is no rock, no soil, no pocket of accidental air—only manufactured surface, only the warm, soft, heavy liquid that moves through them all. The water is neither flood nor ocean floor here; it is substrate and bloodstream together, running transparent through pipes in constant, patient circulation, filling the interstices, carrying warmth rather than cold.

The plants are relentless. Dark emerald and bioluminescent teal, they do not root in earth—there is none—but in engineered hydroponic collars that line every pillar, every wall. Vines as thick as wrists descend in curtains between pump housings; broad, waxy leaves cluster so densely they form false ceilings, filtering the light that issues from recessed industrial lamps behind thick glass: a normal, yellow-white glow, unmagical, illuminating a scene made strange only by its saturation. Nothing freezes, nothing boils; electric traces in the walls maintain the slow current, and small pumps hum beneath the threshold of hearing, a submerged murmur that never quite stops.

There are no animals. The parameter is absolute zero—not absence as lack, but as a silence made physical, so the chamber belongs entirely to vegetation and mechanism, to water and geometry. Erosion has barely touched it; the seams are sharp, the corners unblunted, the alloy still fresh from emergence—perhaps three thermal cycles old, assembled rather than grown into being, a sealed seed of production suspended in the high, warm rain.

What lives here, then, is the system itself: not frantic, not sleeping, but cycling on durations that outpace urgency. Valves turn across months; stems expand with near-inaudible pressure against metal ribs; thermal regulators click, softly, occasionally. The sound is minimal—water passing through ceramic throats, the distant thrum of circulation, the patient growth of green against black-green ribs. The rules are those of containment and flow. The outside, if there is one, is only more water, more height, more of the same quiet. Inside, everything is filled, everything is made, everything waits at the pace of machinery that has no need of sleep, in a round, sharp-edged cathedral of slow industry, held deep in an endless, warm high place with nothing to share it but itself.
