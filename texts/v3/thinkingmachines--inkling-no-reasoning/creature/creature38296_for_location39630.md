Here lives the Keeper of Held Breath—though “keeper” is wrong, because nothing here is kept; everything is continuously, patiently remembered into place.

It resides in the dome’s intimate darkness without center or edge, distributed like the low, constant hum that might be the bone-polymer ceiling recalling that it is not quite dead. You do not see it first; you feel its pace imposed upon you: do not rush. The space is made for waiting, and the being inside is that waiting made sentient—slow, hand-wrought, threaded through every cubic span with the same copper patience as the small fauna.

What body it has is woven, not grown. Dark fiber and needle-metal form thousands of eyes that open not in faces but in the moss-ridges themselves; a mesh-eye in a felted bush watches with identical stillness to the wire-beetle beside it. The Keeper shares edges with everything—plants are not separate from it, nor are the crawling things that feed on nothing visible. They move together in cycles that take hours, grinding softly across the warmed ceramic, and where one ends the other begins without seam or violence.

It lives in contradiction, comfortably. The air is bone-dry—there is no water, not as liquid, not as vapor—yet the lushness is impossible, dense with dyed silk and jute stitching that should require wetness to survive. The dome was pressed into being yesterday, sharp as a broken egg, with no soil beneath, no bedrock, no outside—only layered insulation and the hollow tick of hidden gears—and still it holds centuries of presence. Nothing erodes; nothing chills; nothing evaporates. Time here is long-duration change without decay, fresh addition without origin, reproduction without visible act.

The Keeper does not rule this place. The wooden frames hold the hanging mats; the copper needles bind the ridges; the translucent panels cast unmoving shadows. The technology is manual, primitive, complete. And yet the being moves within it like a shared memory—abundant without explanation, present in every glint of needle-metal and every rustle of fiber against fiber. It is the dome’s gentle breakdown made gentle presence: the concept of thirst embedded in the joints, satisfied by nothing, necessary to nothing, yet felt in every stitch.

If you brush against a constructed leaf and find it opening a watchful eye; if you realize the small bodies are not reproducing but being continuously recalled, layer upon layer, in a space that should not hold so much presence in so little air—then you have touched the Keeper. Not with hands. With waiting. It is already touching you back.
