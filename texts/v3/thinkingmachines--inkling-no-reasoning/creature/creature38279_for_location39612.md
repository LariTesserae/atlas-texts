The hollow is a dropped cork of rusted steel, cradled in the spongy, thawed loam of a young marsh where erosion is still deciding the shape of things. Its walls are ribbed with old welded seams, flanges weeping brown rust that beads and falls with patient rhythm onto the only flat ground within: a shallow black pool no wider than a dinner plate. Around it, moss hangs in emerald beards, ferns unfurl in silver-green rosettes from bolt-holes, roots threading through iron like searching fingers. The air is nickel-tarnished, barely present, carrying the constant loud hiss of a hairline crack, the tick of droplets on black water, and the low mechanical hum of distant industry vibrating through the shell like a borrowed heartbeat.

Here lives the Rust-Mother.

She is smaller than a thimble and composed entirely of what the sphere exudes: her torso is a knot of oxidized iron mesh, her joints are rivets that turn with the sound of a gate opening underwater, and her hair—if hair it is—is long, dripping moss that never stops growing downward from her skull, absorbing each falling rust-bead into its green-black weave. She wears no face so much as a smooth plate of scratched glass, behind which a single filament glows like the dying ember she tends. Her fingers are jointed copper needles, permanently stained with the scraping of rust, and her lungs—if she breathes—draw the hollow’s wet, pressurized air in and release it through her seams as warm, brown vapor that feeds the ferns.

She does not build; she collects. The sphere is her bell jar and her factory. She moves slowly, with the inevitability of a machine that has outlived its purpose, crawling the curved wall in a spiral that never reaches an end. She harvests the rust-beads as they fall, grinding them between her copper fingers into a pigment that she paints back onto the weakened seams—not to repair them, but to direct the flow, to guide the tears to the pool, to maintain the drip. The pool is her mirror and her clock. She studies its black surface for the ripples she alone can read: there, in the widening rings, she measures whether the distant engine still runs, whether the young marsh outside is still weeping, whether the climate of decay remains correct.

She is never alone, and never lonely. Life here is scaled to her intimacy. Translucent beetles—tiny mechanisms with jointed copper legs—crawl her moss-hair, mating in the crooks of her riveted shoulders, scraping rust for her as they feed. Threadworms ripple the pool’s edge, and a moth with tissue-thin wings clings to the glowing filament behind her glass face, beating softly against the heat, a percussion she counts like a pulse. They do not leave. There is no elsewhere.

Outside, the ground is low and freshly eroded, sharp stones piercing wet soil, the atmosphere thick with condensation near freezing. Inside, she maintains the contradiction: a factory hollowed to seed-sized ruin, running not on purpose but on its own dissolution. She is old industry folded into geology, neither beast nor ghost, but the resident logic of a place that was not meant to hold life, yet holds it with loud, wet, mechanical tenderness. She waits, dripping, in the cold dark, listening to the hiss of the crack, feeding the moss, and keeping the red tear flowing back to the marsh.
