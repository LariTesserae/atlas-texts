Here lives the Unmoving Season—though “lives” is already a misstep, for nothing within the Pocket is quite born, nor can it finish being dead.

The residents are not arrivals but residues: the moss itself, settled into upholstery so thick it has forgotten it was ever a plant; the shelf fungi, laminated like scales or old coin, layered over iron railings beaten into shape by vanished hands; the vines threading empty window-frames with the patience of rope-makers who have lost all urgency. Beneath them, small bodies—scaled, shelled, furred with the softness of perpetual winter—drift through green darkness at speeds that deny the word “movement.” They are embedded, not traveling. Their paths have been worn not by repeated journeys but by the single, eternal passage of being in exactly one place.

The true inhabitant is absence held in form. The architecture is intimate and collapsed—hand-laid stone turned to sugar by centuries of damp, timber blackened and petrified, simple iron nails blooming into orange flowers of rust. This is a low elevation, a forgotten rib of a city that no longer remembers its streets, compressed into a single hollow chamber where corridors have become burrows and courtyards have become caves. The scale is that of a breath held: small enough to feel enclosed, vast enough that the green density never reveals its edges.

Above, a ragged aperture admits flat white light—constant, shadowless, without sun or hour. It illuminates nothing and changes nothing. The temperature does not move; the air is near freezing, thick with the dampness of stone weeping slowly, of condensation gathering in broken basins, of moisture that never fully becomes rain and never quite dries. There is no wind to disturb the ferns rooting in stairwells that go nowhere.

Everything here exists in suspended negotiation. The moss does not advance; it has already arrived. The fauna do not breed or diminish; they persist as texture, as softness moving with such geological slowness that they seem part of the masonry. The drip of water onto stone is not a new event but the same event, repeated with such fidelity it becomes architecture rather than sound. This is erosion made permanent, a balance held so long it has become geology.

You are not outside it, observing. You have stepped through absence—through a curtain of ivy—into a pocket within a pocket, a micro-world sealed inside the collapsed ribs of something once urban and now utterly private. The air tastes of mineral cold and chlorophyll. You understand, without being told, that presence is tolerated only in stillness, for stillness is the only law here. The Pocket does not live; it remains—lush, green, and perfectly unfinished, held in the hollow between two demolished bones that have forgotten they were ever part of a greater body.
