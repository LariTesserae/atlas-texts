Here lives a dense, hidden colony—tens of thousands of small mechanized beings—pressed into a hollow spire’s seam, a pocket no larger than a forgotten atrium suspended at extreme height. Their bodies are beetle-small, hammered from brass and glass, antennae twitching with synchronized static, and they move in a turbulent, anthill-current: tearing at failing seals, rebuilding broken conduits with molten wire, cannibalizing engines to birth others, never resting, never leaving.

The chamber itself is young—fresh weld beads still bright, sharp edges unrounded, concrete that has not yet learned to crack. It is cool and dry as powdered oxide; moisture was never invited inside. Light comes only in refusal: a sallow, failing phosphor glow and the red pupils of running boilers leave the corners in mineral shadow. No plants grow here—no green, no root—only black fungal lace blooming in grease along the seams. Pipes bundle like arteries, catwalks nest inside one another, ventilation ribs hang in ribcage rows; every breath of the space smells of iron, old current, and grinding gears. The sound is mostly a held, low industrial hum, broken only rarely by sheared-metal shrieks, bodies falling through grates, or brief thunderous collapses—then silence returns, and the storm continues.

This is not a place but a behavior: build, destroy, move. A sealed mineral cell, urban in its compressed saturation, Solaris-like in miniature—creation and annihilation locked in perpetual, hyperactive balance. The swarm writes the only rules in passing, filling every cubic inch with motion, living exactly where they must: suspended in thin, dry air, too high for rain, hidden in a lattice of rusty truss, young, cool, dim, and violently alive at the world’s edge.
