There is a kind of herd-animal that lives here—though “animal” barely holds it, and “herd” is only the shape their silence makes together.

They are formed of heat and water’s refusal to choose: long, filament-thin bodies, translucent as pulled sugar or a lens left too long in a furnace. Their skins are not skin but lamination—silica plates, each one thinner than a leaf’s ghost, fitted like the pages of a drowned book. The plates do not overlap; they meet edge to edge, and where they join they hold a film of mineral condensate that makes them shimmer with an inner, wet light. They are scaled, yes, but the scales are more like architectural fragments—tiny rust-colored facades, small windows of oxidized iron—so that as they move, they seem not to have bodies but to be carrying a miniature ruined town along with them.

They are small. There are no large bodies on Weep-Hollow; the scale belongs to absence. Each creature is wire-thin, no thicker than a finger of dry steam, and though they cluster in masses—dozens, sometimes hundreds, threading the empty dark ground between the concentric rings of dead foundry and siphon-tower—their collective presence never fills the space. They are dots of slow light in an enormous mineral darkness, migrating in loops that have worn pathways where no human street ever existed.

Their movement is of two kinds, both silent. Most often they drift: a patient, glacial sliding across the obsidian, plates clicking softly—0.1—like wet glass settling, like a dropped coin that never lands. They feed on the perpetual slick, on the water that dominates the air at 2.3, that runs in thin streams down weeping rock faces and gathers in shallow pools. They do not drink. They absorb. The condensate beads on their silica plates and is drawn inward through seams so fine they are invisible, leaving behind only a faint, glassy residue that joins the iron crust, part of the weathered 1.5 ground.

But there are sudden moments, quiet bursts. One will halt, every plate locking in a posture like an open door, then launch—mercury-fast, a silver blur across the black glass—only to stop just as abruptly, folding into itself inside the hollow core of an old industrial shell. These are the predators, or perhaps the prey in flight; it is hard to tell, for they all share the same construction, the same muted material. The smaller ones are faster, more liquid, disappearing into fractures in the slag. The larger ones wait in pools—more patient, more plate-heavy, almost still—until something quick and bright moves above them, and then the water around them shifts, not with splash but with a sudden, silent absorption, and one small shape is gone.

They nest in the architecture because the architecture is hollow. The foundries stand open-roofed, steel ribs intact but empty of purpose; the pipes run overhead, carrying hot water that weeps from fractures and falls in silent waterfalls. The creatures climb these bones slowly, filling the voids with their translucent bodies, arranging themselves like living insulation inside dead machinery. They do not build. They inhabit. They become part of the erosion, part of the landscape’s settled form—1.5, strange, in balance.

They leave nothing behind. No bodies, no waste, no sound of birth. Only that thin, glassy film that makes the rust-crusted ground glitter like scattered coins under the bright 2.6 glare. The light hits them and passes through, offering nothing back; the warmth they feel comes not from the white sky but from below, the constant geologic exhale, the 2.9 furnace breath rising through vents. They are heat-tolerant because they are made of heat’s opposite: silica formed in conditions that should not permit life, holding water inside them like a secret.

In the pools, they are most visible, and most wrong. The water reflects them, but not correctly—distorted, multiplied, as if each creature casts several lives into the liquid. They move through these reflections without breaking the surface tension, plates shifting with that soft, wet clicking that is the only music here, other than the hiss of vapor and the distant sound of cooling metal.

They do not sleep as one sleeps. They enter long stillnesses, hanging in the air near vent openings or lying flat against the obsidian, plates open like shutters, absorbing the mineral exhale. The herds migrate in long, silent loops—no leader, no urgency—following paths worn not by feet but by repetition, by centuries of translucent bodies sliding in the same directions around the rings of ruin. The ecosystem is active, 1.5: predator and prey in mineral form, operating in mute cycles.

They are fauna at 2.1, yes—abundant, living, feeding, reproducing in ways that involve division rather than union, a slow splitting of silica plates that leaves two smaller, identical beings where one stood, both already carrying the architecture of the place in their bodies.

And they are strange at 1.5—subtly, persistently strange—not monstrous, not alien in a dramatic sense, but wrong in the way water is wrong in the pools: correct in every detail except that nothing about them should be able to live in the high, hot, wet silence of Weep-Hollow, and yet they do, entirely, slowly, beautifully, with no voice and no end.
