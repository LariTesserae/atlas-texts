The bowl is not climate in any usual sense—around 1,600 steps above where trees should still be, there should be wind and thin air, but instead there is only a cupped, sweating warmth that holds like a palm’s memory of holding. It lives alone, one place only, cathedral-small, no vaster than a churchyard turned outward and left to forget walls.

Who lives? Not people. Not beasts, not birds with voices. The primary presence is absence made patient. The warmth lives here—retained, mineral, coppery-green, clinging to every surface. The moss lives in thick solitudes, enormous with space between them: emerald reeds in single, unhurried postures; dark cushions of moss spread downward rather than reaching; flat lichens holding shallow cups of water like offerings no one takes. Ferns unfold with an agonizing slowness that suggests they are not growing but remembering how.

The granite lives longest—black, weeping, eroded not toward ruin but toward a polished, settled patience. Rounded shoulders; water-smoothed hollows; surfaces in long-own states. They have learned to weep in threads that never fall but cling, tremble, and sink back into themselves. The water lives too—perpetual, shallow, never leaving, never storm-arriving, always near the surface, running slowly in the dark seams between root-fingers. A mirror of wet earth at the center that reflects not sky (there is no visible sky change, only flat, ordinary light) but the green-dark undersides of the very plants that drink it. The dynamics are patient to the point of immobility: a drop completes its long descent with an almost imperceptible click; warm vapor exhales softly from rock; time is measured in moss creeping across stone, in the widening of a single crack by a finger’s width across centuries.

Even the tools live, though surrendered. At the eastern wall, three stones arranged—not stacked with purpose but balanced with intention older than metal—wedged with smooth rods of blackwood. No blade has split them; only time, only root-pressure, only the patient becoming-geology of craft. They do not hold weight. They hold memory, perhaps, or the idea of holding. Primitive fabrication, hand-worked and already returning to soil.

The density is sparse, intimate: a single feature, a room with no ceiling. You can see every presence at once—the clump, the thread, the moss—nothing hidden, nothing competing. The sound is near-absence: water’s occasional completion, warmth’s soft exhale, a quiet so present it becomes its own inhabitant.

What lives here is the strange harmony of wrongness corrected. Warmth that should not hold at this height holds. Water that should seek lower ground pools and circles back. Nothing moves with urgency, because urgency has never arrived. The place is not observed—there are no eyes to observe with—but held, enclosed in granite and moss and perpetual shallow water, always in the slow act of being exactly, immovably here. Change will come, yes, but not within a life. The wood will become soil within millennia; the moss will claim it; the weeping will continue, warm, mineral, unblinking—never leaving, never leaving.
