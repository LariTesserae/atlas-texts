Inside the half-kilometer hollow lives only the bowl itself—no citizens, no custodians—though the absence of people is itself a kind of presence, a vacancy that allows everything else to become more fully itself.

**The rock lives first and longest.** The crater walls are fresh black basalt, shattered and steep, still carrying the thermal memory of recent violence. They have not yet learned to round themselves with erosion; they are young, unweathered, and in places still hot where geothermal breath escapes through hairline fractures. From these fissures the walls weep—a constant, silver-threaded weeping that never stops—feeding the shallow marsh below and keeping the air closer to liquid than gas.

**The marsh lives in the floor.** It is shallow, constant, never draining, filled with warm black sand and bubbling seeps. Its boundary advances slowly, patiently, millimeter by millimeter, swallowing the industrial remnants that try to hold ground against it. The water here does not rush; it accumulates, condenses, circulates. The sound of living water is continuous: a loud, white-noise falling down dark faces, the drip and plink on rusted iron, the occasional groan of cooling stone.

**The vegetation lives with furious saturation.** Giant ferns with fronds the size of doors, mosses that swallow sound entirely, and lianas threading through every joint in the basalt. The plants are the dominant population—botanical, voracious, and silent—responding to the warm inversion with a density that fills every square meter. They do not grow quickly in a dramatic sense; they grow with patience, roots working into cracks that have existed for only decades, sinking slowly into substrate that offers little resistance to root but plenty of moisture.

**The machinery lives in decay, not death.** At the southeastern edge of the marsh squats a rust-chewed pump station: iron pipes, now orange-bloomed where paint has long vanished, running over moss to disappear into a seep. Its engine is dead, yet present—scaled for extraction, now extracting nothing. A collapsed concrete pad nearby is half-swallowed by ferns. A single steel ladder bolted to the wall climbs to nowhere, ending in mist. Thick copper wires, as thick as thumbs, droop from an overhead cable that runs across the crater’s mouth, still humming with low electricity that powers nothing visible—only a faint ozone smell and a mechanical thrum that might come from hidden engines elsewhere on the peak. This machinery rusts gradually, sinking, not collapsing, transforming into substrate without leaving.

**The air lives as an inversion.** Despite the altitude—three thousand meters or more—the bowl holds a tropical warmth, cupped and invisible from outside. The world beyond the rim is absent; within, the scale contracts to a single enclosed room of green and black. Light is flat, diffuse, filtered through perpetual warm mist that hovers at half-height, leaving colors saturated but not bright, shadows barely present.

**The life that moves is minimal and lost.** Once, an insect the size of a thumb lands on a frond and is still, lost in the green. No birds call. No wind carries voices out. The fauna is nearly absent; the life here does not flee, does not call across distance, does not need to.

So who lives here? The bowl lives. The young basalt lives in its slow thermal exhale. The marsh lives in its patient, outward creep. The ferns and mosses live in their dense, sound-swallowing occupation. The dead pump and humming wires live in their gradual corrosion and pointless electricity. Nothing here escapes quickly. Not the water, not the sound, not the rust. The process is weak in gradient, long-cycled: condensation, growth, corrosion, stillness. The rules are those of containment—what enters stays, circulates slowly, transforms without departure, becoming more itself over time.
