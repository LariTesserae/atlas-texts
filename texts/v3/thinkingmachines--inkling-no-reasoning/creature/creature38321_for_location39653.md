In the Condensation Flats live **the Repeaters**, if they can be said to live at all—being more like the valley’s own memory of inhabitation, its unfinished idea of a people.

They are sparse, vertical figures wrapped in simple woven cloth the color of unbleached fiber, which never darkens with age or light. They do not cast shadows in the sourceless white; they stand in it, half-absorbed, their edges slightly softened as if seen through a layer of still water. Their skin, like the valley’s substrate, is young and fired—smooth, ochre, and perpetually beaded with the same warm moisture that coats everything. They do not sweat; they weep, gently, the water returning to them from within, beading at the collarbone, the wrist, the hollow of the throat, falling only to be caught in bronze cups that never quite fill and never empty. They have faces—cheekbones sharp as the valley’s unfiled ridges, eyes too still, mouths that do not open to speak—yet these features shift slowly, over long durations, adjusting to match the circular patterns of the blackened brick wall-rings they tend. Some have been repairing the same hairline crack in a ceramic pan for generations; others sit within the low wall-rings, arranging themselves in postures that mirror the vine’s impossible geometry, facing directions that point not outward but downward, toward those pools where reflection fails and false depths spiral into black ceramic.

With them, in abundance that borders on invasion, lives the **Still Fauna**—the glass-skinned frogs that sit on ceramic lips without blinking, arranged in densities that feel calculated, their translucent bodies showing not organs but only the same green vine reflected through them; the insects with too many translucent wings, trembling in rows like stranded lightning, never taking flight, never dying; and the serpentine creatures of soft tissue and simple bone that glide through the shallow pools without rippling them, maintaining speed but never reaching a destination, looping through linked pans in patterns that trace the primitive bronze chisel-scars along the overflow lips. They do not prey on the Repeaters. They do not acknowledge them. The relationship is one of shared suspension: all exist in the same dense, downward-pressing warmth, in the same absence of wind, in the same flat equilibrium.

But there is a third presence, more structural, that also lives here—not in bodies but in the **Behavior of the Place Itself**. The valley is only five generations old, yet it acts ancient, its dynamics stilled, its gradients carved flat with primitive tools. The vegetation—those fat ropes of pale green vine, those metallic fronds drinking air—has claimed the mortar with an abundance that should be wild, yet it grows in scattered, respectful distances, leaving the wide ceramic courtyards open, leaving the circular wall-rings exposed, arranging itself in mirror-images of architecture it never witnessed being built. The vines drill hairline cracks not from hunger but from a fidelity to repetition; the ferns unfurl in perfect stillness not because there is no air, but because the air here is thick with suppression, a physical material of quiet. Even the water collaborates: held in thousands of pans at the same level, the same temperature, linked by channels that never erode further, it refuses its own nature—except in those scattered pools where, without cause, the reflection collapses, showing downward spirals that extend far beneath the palm-deep bottom, a vertigo of false earth that suggests the valley remembers being deep.

The sound is its own inhabitant too: a suppression so complete it feels like presence, broken only by the purposeless *tick* of condensation striking surface. No frog croaks. No voice carries across the open spaces; words, if spoken, flatten and dissolve in the humidity before reaching another wall-ring. The technology is visible—blackened brick molded by hand, woven fiber, bronze cups and chisels—primitive remnants of a construction that finished before it was truly done, leaving the inhabitants (or the inhabited) to maintain a perfection that requires no maintenance, to repair cracks that do not widen, to harvest plants that do not rot, to drink water that is already inside them.

What lives here, then, is a **system pretending to be an ecology**: the young, sharp-ridged valley with its continuous ochre shell; the sparse, shifting Repeaters in their woven cloth; the abundant, patient Still Fauna; and the false depths—those pools that betray reflection, that pull the eye downward into black ceramic and green vine far below, suggesting that beneath this flat, made world, something older is still pushing upward. The light fills the basin without source or shadow. The heat presses like a hand. The condensation beads and returns. Nothing dies. Nothing drains. And in the long open spaces between wall-rings, where no wind moves the vines and no sound travels far, the Condensation Flats hold themselves in an equilibrium so total it has become a kind of waiting—not for change, but for the recognition that they have never been anything other than exactly this.
