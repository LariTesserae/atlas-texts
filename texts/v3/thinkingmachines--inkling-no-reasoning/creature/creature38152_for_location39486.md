Here lives the Low Flat itself—nothing with breath or voice, only the enduring machinery of a world that finished building itself long ago and never stopped running. It is inhabited by absence given form: solitary towers stand in shallow pools like pins left in a drained tray, each wrapped in its own pocket of accumulated dark, separated by yards of wet space where nothing clusters and nothing grows.

Its true life is hidden, below the weeping concrete and black iron slate—turbines churning water that refuses to flow, pistons moving through viscous black oil with the rhythm of buried hearts, pumps cycling in a rhythm with no external purpose. The built surfaces are porous, not to soil—there is none—but to a constant cool exhalation that weeps upward through hairline fractures. Dark moss clings to northern-facing seams; black reeds—engineered or not—poke through grates in stunted isolation, separated by open distance.

The place lives in motion without destination: cold blue arcs skitter along dead transmission lines, smelter mouths glow dull orange, pressure releases in rhythmic sighs, and water condenses only to be drawn back into hidden intakes. No fauna reacts—there is nothing to witness—so the roar propagates outward through open space, a wall of vibration that seems to come from within bone rather than air.

What lives here is the end-state made active: erosion not breaking but smoothing every edge into rounded, worn nubs; darkness not falling but thickening slowly between structures; industry scaled beyond need, electrified in dead lines that still spark, speaking only to itself in the perpetual, deafening drone of a constructed valley that has become its own only inhabitant.
