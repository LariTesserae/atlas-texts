Here lives the hollow itself—small enough to cross in a hundred patient steps, though no one crosses it, not when the cold has already entered the bones and the roar forces every visitor into a crouch. Its residents are few, and they do not move quickly.

**The sound** is the largest presence, though it has no body. It fills the pocket continuously—deafening, absolute, a crushing perpetual noise that is neither wind nor water, yet contains both. It comes from far above or far through, and it never stops; it defines the hollow’s breath, its weather, its unyielding mood.

**The cold** lives here as well—sharp, biting, near-frozen—and with it comes the **ice**: hair-thin crystals on moss, thicker plates in slate crevices, glazes over still pools that collect only in the deepest depressions. The moisture is riverine in spirit but has lost its urgency; it beads in intermittent pockets, weeping slowly, holding in place. Water moves only in weak gradients, in cycles too patient for a single life to witness.

**The moss** thrives despite everything—lush, verdant, impossibly green—carpeting the eroded walls in thick, ancient layers, finding faint phosphorescence in the near-complete darkness. **Ferns** live in the seams, unrolling where moisture lingers longest. Together they insist on the emptiness, turning black stone into a hidden garden of slow green.

The **stone** is the hollow’s true architecture—polished smooth in arcs, rounded by ages of erosion, settled-form. The hollow was not dug but worn into being by the same force that still roars above it: water, frost, and time. It is long-existed, balanced in its own erosion, not breaking apart but holding its shape with the confidence of deep age.

**The built presence** is scattered and faint: a low wall of simple stacked stone that once divided nothing from nothing; a broken platform of fitted slate with primitive tool marks; remnants of pre-metal fabrication, weathered, in-their-own-state, having settled here for ages. They do not command the space; they have been absorbed by it.

**Light** barely exists—perhaps a faint glow in the moss, perhaps nothing. One sees by feeling: the slick of ice, the softness of moss, the hollow resonance of breath barely audible beneath the roar.

**Fauna** is rare, nearly absent: once, perhaps, a small creature of shell or scale adapted to the dark and noise, but presence is sparse. Mostly there is absence—a density of it—vast spaces between the presences.

And yet the hollow is alive. Its life is measured in millimeters of moss, in centuries of ice thickening, in hidden pulses of the roar that might—if one listened longer than a life—reveal weak fluctuations tied to some distant river or glacier’s slow breath. Today, however, it remains as it has been: hidden, intimate, embedded within a larger dryness, near-void, singular in feature, profoundly and deafeningly still.
