Inside this iron egg—small as a hamlet, vast as a forgotten world—nothing lives that expects to leave.

The place itself is the only inhabitant that never moves, and yet it breathes more truly than anything with lungs. Its ribs are cathedral-girders bolted into rust and copper-green plate; its lungs are the valves that hiss constant breath into shallow, cool pools that cover the floor like a second skin of still water. The air does not belong to anyone—only suspended droplets hang heavy with the taste of metal, settling onto every surface that has long since given up being dry. This is not a ruin but a finished thing, worn not into failure but into its final, oldest-stable form. Pipes have been polished to silk by centuries of flow. Ladders ground flat. Corners rounded into humility. The chamber reached its end-form long before anyone arrived to name it, and now it simply persists: a geology that was manufactured, a bedrock of steel.

Here, vegetation is not absent but reimagined—sparse, black-green tufts of iron-oxide moss clinging only where condensation gathers in seams, drinking rust the way other plants drink sun. They do not grow toward light, for there is almost none; only the dull amber of dormant pilot-lamps and the faint green bleed of dials that have not changed in ages provide the dimmest currency of visibility. In that murk, phosphorescent lichen stains the walls like old bruises, marking territories of damp.

The ecology crowds the shadows without ever crowding space. Life is sparse in presence—vast dark volumes of empty water and echoing steel lie between them—but abundant in bodies, in endless motion. Small, pale things with too many legs navigate the grates in slow, unhurried procession. Translucent shrimp cluster in the shallow pools, feeding on rust-flake and on each other. Crustaceans scale the weeping walls with mechanical patience. Schools of silver-white minnows flicker through the cool water like living static, turning the pools into something that shivers without wind. They breed, they feed, they die in compressed cycles—an ecosystem sealed into one bounded volume, running on rules of pressure, corrosion, and the eternal low thrum of pumps laboring at the edges.

Sound is the architecture they live inside: the subsonic heartbeat of machinery scaled not for speed but for endurance; the groan of contracting steel; the constant clank of unseen gears turning on axes worn oval with patience; water hissing through valves in a rhythm older than memory. Nothing collapses, because nothing remains that can. The temperature holds—just cool enough to prevent freezing, just cool enough to keep warmth from ever claiming a foothold—settling against the skin like silt.

What lives here does not live despite the conditions, but because of them. There is no weirdness in this, only completion—the deep normalcy of total adaptation. No sky. No soil. No horizon. Only this submerged interior, fully artificial, fully inhabited, loud with its own persistence: moss that eats rust, creatures that navigate darkness without eyes, and a single pressure chamber that, in its ancient stability, has become the only world there is.
