Because the fauna is overwhelming at 2.6, yet the density is near-void. This is the central contradiction, and here the weirdness at 2.9 takes form.

The animals—if they are animals—are industrial-scale organisms, each one enormous and solitary, separated by vast tracts of emptiness. Some are locomotive, traversing miles between feeding stations; others are sessile, anchored in thermal vents, growing over centuries like coral made of piston and wire. They are not plants—there is no vegetation at 0.3—nor are they purely mineral. They exist in a state of reality breakdown: they metabolize heat, reproduce through division of machinery, and bleed black lubricant that evaporates instantly into the hot air. Their presence is overwhelming not in number, but in magnitude and wrongness. You can travel for hours across the empty Shelf and encounter one, sleeping or working, its body half-submerged in the glass plain, generating its own localized storms of static and sound.

The illumination makes the place more real and less real at once: you can see every rivet in the fauna’s flanks, every vent in the machinery, but the scale is wrong, the ecology is impossible, and the air smells of electricity.

This is a world without rules of life or death, only of heat and motion. The plants never came—0.3 left the surface bare of green, leaving only mineral fungal mats in the cooler cracks, dark and slow, simpler than life. The water never arrived—0.2 means all chemistry is sealed, circulating inside bodies and machines, never falling, never pooling. In this empty, open, hot high plain, reality holds together just barely, stitched by heat and sound, inhabited by overwhelming, scattered things that are not quite alive, made of metal and wrongness, moving through a desert that breathes in low, loud, industrial rhythms.
