The valley is low—barely a shallow breath in the flatlands, elevation held in check—cool and damp, though never truly wet. Moisture lives in the margins: it clings to the undersides of broad leaf-casings, beads on iron pipes that have long since softened with oxide, collects in the hollow throats of old gear-housings, and falls in occasional gray rain that never lasts long enough to fill the gutters. Everywhere there is the faint smell of rain that was, and rain that might come back.

This was a settlement once—industrial, mechanized, organized into blocks and avenues, layered with steel and brick and the promise of scaled production. Now it is plant-as-substrate. The buildings did not fall; they were inhabited downward, grown into, digested into architecture made of living mass. The streets remain, but they are corridors of sparse, high-ceilinged canopy—walls not of masonry but of thick, cool-barked trunks that stand with space between them, scattered like columns in a cathedral of something older than prayer. The roof, where it remains, is a woven lattice of rope-vines and broad, waxy leaves that filter the normal light into a steady, green-tinged dimness, neither bright nor dark, constant and readable.

The scale is intimate—hamlet-sized, bounded, a single feature-area you can cross in minutes if you know the paths. The density is sparse, balanced with emptiness. Much of the ground is open platform, moss-covered and soft, suspended over root-chambers that descend like quiet vaults. Where machinery remains—rusted gears half-swallowed by fibrous growth, a conveyor belt now serving as a trellis, electrified rails that hum with a low, strange charge—it does not dominate. It participates. Engines sit idle but not dead; they turn occasionally, powered not by fuel but by the pressure of slow-growing stems, the thermal exchange of decomposing matter, the quiet industrial pact renegotiated between metal and root.

The ecosystem is total—pure plant, all-living-mass—yet it is not lush in the way jungles are lush. It is sparse vegetation filling the space like breath filling lungs: present everywhere, but leaving room. The plants are substrate and inhabitant both; there is no soil visible, only compressed root-mat and living fiber. Fauna is rare—an occasional silvered beetle crawling across a rusted piston, a bird that does not stay, a small mammal that lives in the hollow of a fallen smokestack. The quiet is deep. You hear the intermittent drip of condensation falling from vine to steel, the soft creak of growing wood adjusting itself against iron, and the distant, low thrum of the old machinery being gently, persistently turned by the biomass it now serves.

This place is established, settled-form, in-its-own-state. Erosion happened long ago, and then stopped—left in balance, weathered but not collapsing. The dynamic is active: growth is constant but slow, a biological pace interwoven with mechanical rhythm. The water shifts, the plants extend, the gears turn a quarter-revolution, then hold. The weirdness is not spectacle but integration—industrial infrastructure become digestive tract, electricity running through root-nodes, a town that did not die but was metabolized.

It is cool here. Always cool. The air holds moisture without being heavy. Nothing is hidden exactly, but everything is embedded—structure within structure, machine within tree, settlement within ecosystem. You stand in the open corridor, light falling flat through the sparse high canopy, and understand that this is an urban form still functioning: not for people, but for the process that replaced them, quiet, damp, and completely, actively alive.
