You enter at the bottom of a bowl that was once a city. Not ruins—ruins imply collapse and absence. This is an occupation. The buildings are still standing, but they have become stems. Brick and concrete have been infiltrated by something that roots in mortar; walls are now thick, warm, damp cylinders of living matter, hollowed into tubes and chambers by centuries of slow digestion. The elevation is low—only a few meters above an old riverbed that no longer flows but still exhales moisture, so the air hangs heavy with condensation, occasional rain that seems to come from nowhere, droplets catching on surfaces that behave like both leaf and stone. Warmth holds steady, a body-heat climate inside the hollow growth.

Inside is the density of emptiness. You expected a jungle, but there is no canopy crushing together, no vines closing over your head. Instead, the vegetation is total but arranged as architecture—plant-as-substrate, pure-plant ecosystem—forming cathedral-scale hollows with enormous negative spaces between them. One chamber might be forty meters across, its floor a soft, spongy mat of moss-bacteria and root-hair that releases mist with every step, its ceiling far above lost in shadow-plant, supported not by beams but by the tensile strength of braided fibrous tissue. Then nothing. Vast hollow corridors. The air is nearly void; presence is scarce per volume, despite the fact that every wall lives. The built environment persists—street grids visible as ridges of hardened sap, old windows now round openings lined with membrane—but it has been translated into biological infrastructure, layered over itself in a state long since settled. This is not chaos. The erosion is established, in its own balance, weathered into something that no longer remembers being human architecture and now simply is: a town-scale organism arranged like a lung.

This is small—0.9—cathedral-scale, grove-scale, bounded. You can walk its edges in an hour. A single-feature area with clear boundaries: to the west, the old riverbed where moisture pools; to the east, a wall of hardened growth that stops abruptly, as if the city-plant decided its limits and obeyed them. Within, the ecology rules: no tools, no fire, only the negotiation between your body and the damp warmth of total vegetation.

You realize the deafening roar is not hostile. It is the place being alive. The emptiness is not absence—it is the necessary hollow for sound to travel, for heat to circulate, for the abundant hidden fauna to move unseen. You stand in a room that was once a bank, now a warm, damp throat. The plants do not notice you. They have built this, eroded it, settled it, and filled it with sound beyond bearing, leaving the spaces between vast and nearly empty, active and strange, entirely themselves.
