The valley called Korr-Vhel had been open to the sky for perhaps six hundred years—new, by the patience of stone—so its walls still carried the raw, unsoftened edges of recent emergence. It was low country, barely rising above the marshes that ringed it, and the ground was always wet: not pooled in obvious lakes, but weeping, the water near-surface, condensing in the cold air, hanging in the breath of every moss-covered lip and freshly split crag. Temperature sat near stillness; a cold that did not bite so much as soak in, saturating the dense vegetation that had already claimed every fissure. Ferns thick as bedsprings, black-leafed vines, spore-wool so lush it seemed to grow upon itself in layered, pressing abundance—green and violet and wet-black, a jungle arrested in constant, dripping saturation.

But this was no dead garden. The place was open, extending well past sight, a regional bowl of many features—sinkholes and ridgelines, half-collapsed causeways, scattered remains. Here and there, single buildings or partial artifacts jutted through the foliage: one smooth pillar of impossible geometry, a partial arch of metal that did not rust but hummed, faint marks of engineering that current physics could not fully explain. The technology was not modern; it was post-modern, embedded at nanoscale or cosmic, making the weeping rocks themselves sometimes glow with wrong colors, or shift imperceptibly, or hold moisture in defiance of gravity. Strange, but not strange—there was a weirdness score of near zero here, because everything obeyed local rules with perfect, almost bureaucratic consistency. Nothing was uncanny; everything was exactly as it should be for Korr-Vhel.

The darkness was almost absolute—light near 0.4—though “darkness” was a misnomer in air this dense. Light came not from sun but from bioluminescence and from the artifacts: a violet hum from the pillar, a silver weep from the arch, the green pulse of deep marsh-moss. In this gloom, fauna was abundant, layered, near-touching—winged things with too many segments, glistening amphibians the size of hands that moved in constant streams across the weeping ground, insects in such density that the air itself seemed to have texture, a second atmosphere made of wings.

And the sound. Deafening—2.8, relentless. Not a single noise but the aggregate roar of the turbulent system: the constant creation and destruction of the young, eroded land, the marsh bubbling with thermal upset, the structures humming and tearing at the air, and the fauna—millions of voices, clicks, hums, thunders—merged into a din that never permitted silence. The place was fully turbulent, 3.0—Solaris-like in its hyperactive exchange, matter and life and technology all in unstable negotiation. Rocks formed in one week and split the next; vines grew feet in an hour; the built remains were slowly being digested by the lushness, then rebuilt atom by atom by the embedded tech. Chaos, yes, but a chaos of energy, anthill-bazaar density, everything present at once, pressing.

What emerged was a valley that was normal—utterly, impressively normal within its own logic. Cold. Wet. Lush to the point of choking. Dark and loud and unstable, but never wrong. You walked through it knowing the rules held, even as the ground dissolved and reformed beneath your boots, and the deafening roar of its becoming filled your chest.
