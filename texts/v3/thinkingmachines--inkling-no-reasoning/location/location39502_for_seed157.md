The hollow is small—no larger than a chapel—set high in cool air where the world thins but refuses to clear. You do not arrive there; you come upon it, because every surface is packed with presence, every interstice filled, every breath already shared. The water is near everywhere: not falling, not flowing, but weeping from the rock faces in a steady, unhurried seep that beads the dark walls and pools in shallow, black mirrors across the floor. The temperature never wavers—cool enough to thicken the air, to make your exhalation linger as a visible ghost—while outside, far below and distant, whatever storms might exist stay away. Here, equilibrium is absolute. The stones are weathered into rounded, settled forms, long in their own state, eroded by an age of stillness rather than violence. There are no buildings, no paths, no artifacts of hand or metal—only the raw architecture of moss and root and dripping limestone arranged with a saturation that admits no absence.

It is dark. The light never reaches the hollow’s heart; it lives at the threshold of sight, a blue-grey memory that allows the eye to distinguish shapes only slowly, as if the darkness itself is dense enough to carry form. And in that near-dark, the vegetation does not grow toward the sky but packs horizontally, inward—luminous with moisture, lush in a way that feels more like upholstery than forest, every frond pressed shoulder-to-shoulder with its neighbor.

But the stillness belongs to the fauna.

They are overwhelming—not in motion, for nothing moves, not ever, but in occupancy. The hollow is their body, and they are its cells. Insects cluster so thickly upon the damp ceiling that the rock is hidden beneath wings; they do not crawl, do not fly, do not twitch, but hang in suspended architecture, a living vault. Along the ledges, small creatures—eyeless, cool-skinned—rest with bellies flat to the weeping stone, breathing water instead of air, neither sleeping nor waking, simply present in perfect equilibrium. The roots of the lush plants are wound through with worms and translucent larvae, bodies packed into bodies, and in the shallow pools, amphibians float with eyes open and mouths slightly parted, the water so crowded with them that the surface is a living hide, unbroken, unruffled.

There is no sound, or almost none—only the almost-quiet of constant, minute presence: the faint, collective hum of respiration synchronized, the occasional bead of water falling into a pool that never ripples because the bodies are already there to receive it. The air smells of cold earth and living density.

This is the rule of the place: nothing changes. The erosion is settled, finished, leaving forms that will not round further. The fauna never departs, never breeds in frenzy, never dies and releases its space—death, if it occurs, is replaced in the same breath, the hollow maintaining its perfect saturation. There are no seasons here, no winds to carry seeds away, no predators, no prey. The moisture never dries; the cool never warms; the dark never lifts. Even the primitive—this absence of tool or construct—is preserved with fidelity, untouched not by neglect but by an older logic that has never invited a hand to alter it.

It is weird only in its completeness. Outside, the high elevation might promise distance and clarity, but inside, you understand that this is a room made of being—small, enclosed, eternally itself. You stand at the edge (if there is one; the boundary is felt more than seen) and realize that every presence is accounted for, every drop of water already touched, every cubic inch of air thickened with life that asks nothing, seeks nothing, and will remain, packed and breathing and utterly still, long after your own breath in the cool dark has faded.
