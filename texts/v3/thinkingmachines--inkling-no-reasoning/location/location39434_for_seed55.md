Beneath a black sky that is only absence, not firmament, lies the Submerged Foundry—an expanse of still, cold water so old and so shallow that it has become a single, continuous lens over the world. There is no horizon. The elevation is low everywhere: the terrain never climbs above the knees of a tall creature, never falls into true abyss. What was once land is now a vast, flat floor at the bottom of a global tide, smoothed by eons of near-motion into a plane as even and patient as sleep.

But it is not earth.

Every grain is made. The substrate is not sand or soil but crushed glass-ceramic—billions upon billions of hexagonal tiles, each the width of a thumb, pressed together across continental distances. They are dark: obsidian, slate, wet basalt black, fused so long ago that the lines between them have eroded into hairline seams filled with the same cold water that drowns them. The surface is a mosaic with no image, only repetition, worn to a dull, rounded softness. Here and there, where the hand of some forgotten builder pressed deeper, the tiles form low ridges—no higher than a kneeling stone—creating the only topography in this drowned flat.

Above them stands the architecture of absence. Pillars of cast basalt, primitive and unadorned, rise from the tiled floor in groves so dense they overlap like dead trees in a forest without air. They are hollow cylinders, hand-poured in molds long since lost, their surfaces rough with the fingerprints of ancient labor. Some lean; none have fallen, held in their leaning by the same impossible stasis, the same settled-into-final-form endurance. Between them stretch walkways—grids of planks, woven metal, or bone-white coral-like growth that is not coral—forming layers of path and platform at every height from ankle-deep to shoulder-high, a lattice of built space suspended in the dark water.

The vegetation is lush, but it is not wild. There is no soil, no seedbed, no root in dirt. Everything green grows only where a ledge, a pillar-cap, or a submerged stair offers a purchase. Thick, cold-water kelp hangs in curtains from the lower platforms, dark green to black, moving with a slowness that is barely movement at all—currents so weak they take hours to sway a single frond. Ferns with fronds like wet velvet spread over the tiled ridges, their roots gripping ceramic seams. Moss blankets the vertical surfaces, not fuzzy but slick and dense, a continuous green-black skin. Vines of some aquatic ivy, thick as ropes, wrap the pillar groves, linking them in living nets. The plants are not hungry; they exist in a state of deep, cold saturation, fed by minerals dissolved in the ancient water, growing only millimeters per year, filling every available ledge until the built world is nearly hidden beneath them—not reclaiming it, only clothing it.

The water itself is the true volume. It fills the spaces between the pillars, the hollows of the tiles, the air that should exist but does not. It is not clear; it is black-green, thick with microscopic presence—dense with suspended life that is not animal, not fish, not anything that swims with purpose. Only slow, drifting cells, fungal threads, and the unseen chemistry of age. The temperature is cold, not freezing—the chill of deep ocean, of stone kept in shadow—biting in its persistence. There are no waves. The surface, far above, is a skin of black ice or open black sky, but down here the water is still, held in place by the sheer weight of its own antiquity.

Light is nearly absent. What little exists comes from phosphorescence—faint blue-green glows embedded in the lowest tiles, in the moss, in the water itself—casting a light so weak it reveals texture rather than form. The pillars become silhouettes. The vegetation looks like dark velvet in deeper darkness. One sees only what is near; distance is swallowed.

Sound is mostly quiet. The only noise is the slow, occasional drip of water from a higher lattice to a lower, a sound spaced by minutes, each drop falling with a soft, hollow note into the stillness. There is no wind underwater, no thunder, no call of creature. No animals live here—none that move with intent, none that breathe or hunt. The fauna is absent, replaced entirely by the slow flora and the dense, invisible plankton of this drowned machine-world.

The rules are the rules of settlement. Nothing here was born of chaos; everything was placed, poured, woven, or grown into place, and then left. The erosion is not destructive but cumulative: every sharp edge of the primitive construction has been rounded by centuries of cold water, not into ruin, but into a final shape that will not change again. The scale is vast—one could walk for days and still be within the pillar groves, still among the same tiled plane, the same layered green, the same black water—large enough that the climate here is its own: a perpetual, cold, wet stillness.

It is a place that has finished becoming. The built world and the lush growth have reached an equilibrium of total coverage and total stillness. There is nothing to do here, no event to await, no predator or prey. Only the slow, dense, dark saturation of water, plant, and made stone—held together in the deep, patient quiet of a world that built itself, drowned itself, and then settled into its final form.
