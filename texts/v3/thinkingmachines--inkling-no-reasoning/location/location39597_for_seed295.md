The valley is an instrument, not a landscape—every wall, every arch, every moss-stuffed crevice shaped by hands centuries dead, leaving behind only the geometry they trusted more than earth. Elevation is absolute: not hills but stacked retaining walls of black limestone, rising in tiered amphitheaters that hold the air like a held breath. The warmth is old—stored in the thermal mass of stone, radiated back in a constant, body-level damp heat that never spikes and never drops, the climate of a sealed jar.

Water is intermittent, disciplined. It arrives not as weather but as exhalation: sweat beading on cold marble undersides, thin films sliding along channels carved for runoff that never quite runs, pocketing in the bowl of every broken cistern. There are no rivers visible, only the sound of them—muffled, moving in darkness, somewhere behind the masonry.

But the masonry is not dead. Vegetation has colonized it with the patience of glaciers. Lush, yes—ferns with fronds like wet velvet, moss that drinks the damp and swells into green insulation between every joint, vines that root in mortar cracks and hang in thick, slow-draping curtains from arch to arch. The growth is dense to the point of near-solid: walking through the lower tiers means pushing through layers of cool, dripping plant matter that has made the built world its substrate. There is no bare stone visible for long; the architecture wears its green like a second, living skin.

Above that skin, darkness. The valley’s openness extends—past the immediate, into a regional bowl of structures and terraces—but the light is almost gone. A dimness close to 0.4, just enough to distinguish form from shadow, cast perhaps by faint bioluminescence in deep moss or by distant, dying warmth. Everything is made, all the way to the bedrock-equivalent: no natural substrate remains, only engineered surfaces now softened by centuries of root and rot. The tech is absent—zero tools, zero mechanisms—only the biological and the built, intertwined.

And the fauna. Overwhelming. The air and every surface move with life that requires no technology to sustain it, only the constant warmth and moisture and darkness. Insects in numbers that blur—winged bodies coating the undersides of arches, beetle-like things the size of thumbs crawling in deliberate, patient lines across fern fronds. Moist-skinned amphibians in the thousands, throating out a continuous, wet, rhythmic noise from every pool and moss bank. Slow-worms in the crevices, slow-moving but present in such volume that the walls seem to crawl when watched. The sounds merge into deafening: not sharp, not abrupt, but a saturated roar of biological presence—chirp layered on croak layered on the rustle of countless bodies, the constant percussion of water dripping onto occupied ground.

Erosion has settled into its own balance. The structures are weathered, yes—corners rounded, steps worn smooth, some arches collapsed into themselves—but the decay is long-established, in its own state. The valley is not falling apart in crisis; it is in equilibrium, the built environment having achieved the same permanence as an old forest floor. Change is glacial: roots thicken over decades, pools shift by millimeters per year, the fauna ebbs and flows in slow cycles tied to the intermittent wetness.

This is a place of total construction and absolute biology, open and high and dark and dense, where nothing is wild in the sense of untouched, and nothing is artificial in the sense of dead. The sound never stops. The warmth never leaves. The green never thins. And in the near-darkness, the overwhelming presence of living things moves at the speed of stone—slow, patient, and utterly, deafeningly alive.
