The hollow is small—no larger than a forgotten chapel, a single room of earth and air—yet it sits very high, where the air is thin and warm, pressed against a plateau so old it has forgotten how to be a mountain. Here erosion has finished its work; there is nothing left to wear away. The rock is polished to its final form, rounded into a single concave bowl of dark basalt, its slope so gentle and complete it resembles the inside of a skull. This is the end of life for geology: settled, near-flattened, primordial, stable into eternity.

Inside this bowl, darkness is absolute—0.0, a black thick enough to drink. There is no sky visible, no star, no reflection, only the void of a space that has kept out every photon for millennia. And yet the place is not dead. Vegetation thrives here in lush, impossible abundance: pale, rootless mosses and translucent ferns that do not photosynthesize but drink the weeping wetness that beads constantly from the ancient, sweating rock. The temperature is warm—2.1—held steady by deep geothermal breath rising through fractures older than time. Water is everywhere near the surface; it condenses from the air in slow, patient droplets that fall with soft, loud percussion, each one resonating through the hollow like a bell struck in a cathedral. The sound is loud not because of chaos, but because emptiness amplifies even the gentlest event.

There is almost nothing here—density near zero. Vastness exists within the smallness. The space between things is enormous. You might wait an hour to see one presence cross the hollow. The fauna is abundant but distributed across an immense emptiness: translucent, slow-moving creatures—something like millipedes of glass, or floating larvae—drifting through the humid dark on currents that take centuries to complete a circle. They are not frightened; they are patient, moving with the weak gradients of a world that changes only across long durations.

Built marks are scattered and faint. At the edge of the bowl, a half-circle of slender pillars remains—technology beyond modern comprehension, structures that do not obey current physics, maintaining a subtle field that holds the hollow’s air and warmth and weeps. They are nanoscale in effect but cosmic in origin, perhaps maintaining the very boundary of the place. They do not shine. They hum at a frequency below sound, contributing to the loud, resonant quiet.

The rules of this world are slowness and saturation. Time is not linear inside the hollow; it pools like the warm water that collects in the lowest point, forming a shallow, black mirror that reflects nothing. The ecology feeds on condensation and ancient heat, on the minerals bleeding from the worn rock. The built remains do not guard; they observe. The fauna does not prey; it waits.

To stand inside is to feel the scale shift—this is a hamlet-sized room, a grove enclosed, yet the emptiness within makes it feel like a universe with borders. The weirdness is slight, subtle: the sense that every drop of water falling has done so before, that the moss remembers every footstep before it happened, that the darkness is not an absence but a presence—full, warm, and very, very old. Change comes, eventually, across epochs. The pillars will shift a millimeter. The pool will deepen. Until then, the hollow breathes, loud and wet and dark, settled into its final form.
