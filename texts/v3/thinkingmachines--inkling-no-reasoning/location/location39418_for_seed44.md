The settlement is called the Low Weave, and it occupies a single depression no larger than a cathedral nave—low, bounded, and flat—where the ground never fully dries. The temperature holds steady and warm, a humid breath that never breaks, and everywhere the water is close: not as pools or rivers, but as weeping. The old masonry—brick, iron, and ceramic—exudes moisture in slow beads; pipes lined with moss-drip run along every wall, releasing calculated seepage onto the surfaces below; the air itself condenses against cool metal and falls in a patient, continuous rain that never reaches storm. The scale is intimate and enclosed—this is one room of a larger idea, a hamlet-scale factory, a grove enclosed not by trees but by architecture that has become substrate.

Every surface is vegetated. Not gardens planted in rows, but a total living mass—moss, liverwort, creeping vine, and dense fern—that has grown into the brick, through the gears, over the pipes, using the settlement as soil. The plants form the walls now. They are the insulation, the filter, the ceiling. In the warm, wet dark, they have built a second settlement atop and within the first: corridors of green that narrow and widen, rooms whose boundaries are root-mat rather than stone. The ecology here is pure plant-as-architecture; the fauna are almost absent—only the occasional moisture-beetle, a few blind slugs in the root-fiber—leaving the place to the slow, loud business of growth and machine.

For this place has always been urban, always industrial. The erosion is established, settled-form, weathered into balance: the rust is stable, the rot is complete, and the machinery—vast for the space—operates on patient cycles. Engines turn slowly, powered by steam drawn from the constant weeping, driving pumps that circulate water through living channels. Factories are layered constructions, half-submerged in the green, producing not goods for shipping but conditions for maintenance: the scale is that of a single feature-area, a contained district where mechanized process and vegetation have reached equilibrium. The sound is loud and constant—the low thrum of rotors, the hiss of valves, the heavy drip of condensate on metal, the rustle of leaves against iron—yet nothing moves quickly. Change is gradual, measured across long durations, weak gradients of wear and growth.

Light is ordinary, flat, filtering through translucent canopies of fern and moss that hang like green glass, casting a damp, yellow-green shade over everything. The density is near-saturating: every volume is filled with the presence of root, leaf, pipe, and gear, touching, interwoven. There are rules here, though they are unwritten: the water must keep weeping; the plants must keep filling; the machines must keep turning slowly, not to conquer the green, but to serve it. The built world and the living mass are not in conflict. They are one body, old, balanced, and entirely itself—a small, loud, warm, wet cathedral of industry and pure plant, where nothing is strange because everything belongs.
