The high ground is not earth. At this elevation—level with the throat of an old sky—the substrate is a plate of fused, blackened lignin, a continent-scale floor woven by roots that have never known soil or rain. There is no water here: not in veins, not in pores, not in vapor. The dryness is absolute, a mineral seal, a void where chemistry runs on heat and pressure alone.

Above that floor, vegetation is total. Not fields. Not forest. Architecture made of living mass. Trunks as thick as temples twist upward into the dark, branching into vaults, ribs, and domes. Leaves do not fall; they are the ceiling, layered so densely that they form a continuous, breathing roof far overhead. Every space is filled. Every corridor is choked with stems. The green is not gentle—it is saturated, packed, a total occupation of air and shadow. You do not walk through plants; you move inside them, through arteries and chambers that the growth has decided to form.

The heat is nearly lethal, three degrees below the point where plant-flesh chars. The air is not air but a thick, warm respiration of cell-wall and resin, sealed away from any wind that remembers water. This heat has been here since the first root struck the plate, and it has cooked the world into its final form. Erosion is ancient not in the sense of wear, but in the sense of settlement—this is the end-state, the primordial architecture that has outlived all its ancestors. Nothing crumbles. Everything has arrived at its ultimate shape and stopped, except that it has not stopped.

Beneath the vault, in the near-dark—light barely scraping 0.2, a sub-glow of ember and phosphor—the industrial presence is indistinguishable from the growth itself. Pipes of dark metal run through hollow trunks like veins in a greater body. Engines throb inside bulbous root-knots, not separate but grafted, producing motion and pressure for a purpose that no longer references an outside world. This is not machinery placed upon nature; it is machinery that has become the plant’s bone, its metabolism mechanized, scaled and running in sealed loops without fuel that can be named. Factories exist here, but they are chambers of compression and separation, refining the very resin that sustains the vault.

There is no fauna. No insects hum in these corridors. No birds navigate the dark canopy. Only the plants and their metal hearts, and the absolute absence of animal intention.

The scale opens past the immediate. One does not stand in a grove; one stands in a valley-sized region of woven biomass, extending into a darkness where other domes, other root-cathedrals, are implied but never fully seen. The density is relentless—no void, no sky visible, no ground visible except where the black floor shows through tears in the root-weave.

And yet, despite all this mass, the sound is mostly quiet. The engines do not roar; they exhale, a low, pervasive sub-bass like a sleeping beast. The vegetation does not rustle; in its ancient, heat-cured state, it creaks slowly, with the sound of old wood expanding in a fire that never ends. The turbulence is not loud. It is internal, hyperactive, a constant churning of growth and decay and mechanical processing happening at once. The world is in a storm-state of becoming, but the storm is silent, contained within walls of leaf and pipe. Roots tear through industrial plating and are reabsorbed by machinery that produces more roots. The chaos is absolute—turbulent, dynamic, anarchic in its change—but it happens without witness, in darkness, under heat, without water.

This is a sealed ecology at the end of its becoming: total, built, vegetative, hot, dry, ancient, dark, and quietly, endlessly in motion.
