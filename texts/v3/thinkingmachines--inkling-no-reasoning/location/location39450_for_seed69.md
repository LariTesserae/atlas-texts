The plateau does not begin so much as accumulate. At 2,800 meters across a crustal uplift that has been weeping since the last continent forgot to sink, the air is its own weather system—dense, warm, and permanently saturated. Water is not an event here; it is the architecture. Every basalt shelf and moss-crowned crag exudes moisture in long, patient threads. The stone weeps black, mineral-rich tears that gather in troughs older than the first engine, forming shallow, mirrorless seas that never settle, never still, only oscillate in the dark with the slow respiration of something buried.

The scale is continental in feeling, though bounded by ridges lost in perpetual exhalation. Vast does not mean empty. The vegetation—lush, sprawling, aggressively alive—fills every vertical and horizontal plane with overlapping densities of ferns taller than a human, lichens that glow with faint, failed bioluminescence, and root-systems that have stitched the eroded soil into a living mat. This is an established landscape, eroded into its own final vocabulary. The gullies are smooth, not sharp—weathered into throats and palms and cupped hands that catch the constant condensation. Nothing is new. Even the storms, which roll across the plateau with industrial regularity, have worn their paths into the rock.

Light registers at zero, not because of absence, but because of refusal. The darkness is absolute, thick, wet—an optical density matched only by the saturation of life. Visibility is a matter of pressure and sound, of heat-haze and the trembling of air against vibrating metal.

For this place is also industrial, though the industry is scattered like seeds thrown across a graveyard. There are machines here—tall, slender, rust-crowned—standing at calculated intervals across the moss and the seepage. Factories reduced to single buildings, partial remains of scaled production now overtaken. Engines still turn, powered by the weeping itself: hydro-mechanical turbines half-submerged in black pools, gears lubricated by mineral slime, smokestacks that do not smoke but rather exhale warm, chemical breath into the dark. Electricity exists in localized arcs—faint, blue, dangerous—jumping between wet cables and iron struts, illuminating for seconds at a time the contours of machinery that has become architectural, then geological, then mythic.

The sound is loud, constant, layered—a mechanical ecology competing with the biological. Water strikes metal in rhythmic percussion. Turbines groan with the weight of centuries. Deep below the root-mat, shifting crustal plates or perhaps older, stranger mechanisms emit sub-bass frequencies that travel through the feet, through the bones of the built remnants, through the bodies of the fauna that have learned to navigate not by sight but by vibration.

The fauna is abundant—dense in number, near-touching in the tight spaces between stalks and pipes. There are insects with too many segments, their bodies glistening with collected moisture, moving in synchronized pulses that mirror the rotation of the nearest turbine. Larger things, warm-bodied, move through the undergrowth in packs—creatures evolved to darkness and density, their calls low and resonant, communicating through the same frequencies the machines use. Predator and prey here share the same rulebook: adapt to the loudness, use the darkness, stay close to the water that feeds both the plants and the engines.

Weirdness permeates without spectacle. The boundary between the living and the mechanized is porous. Moss grows inside gear-boxes. The weeping rocks have developed, over eons, patterns of corrosion that resemble circuitry—whether by coincidence or by some deeper logic of the place is impossible to tell. The air itself behaves strangely: heavy with warmth yet always condensing, as if the atmosphere is in a state of perpetual exhalation after some vast, hidden combustion.

Everything is active. The erosion continues—not destructively, but as maintenance, smoothing the new into the old. The vegetation spreads with almost audible urgency. The animals feed, hide, migrate in circular patterns dictated by the scattered engine-sites, which have become unintended centers of an ecosystem they were never built to serve. The machines run—not for anyone, not for production in any human sense, but because the water moves, and the water moves because the plateau breathes.

This is a world that has forgotten it was ever worked, and a factory that has forgotten it was ever manufacturing. What remains is dense, warm, wet, loud, and dark—vast enough to generate its own storms, strange enough to confuse growth with rust, and alive enough that even the eroded stone seems, in its constant weeping, to be participating.
