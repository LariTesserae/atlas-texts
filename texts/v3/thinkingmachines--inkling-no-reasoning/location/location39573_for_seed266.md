Consider the world called **Aeru**—not a planet with a sky, but a single continental-scale shelf of fused basalt and vitrified clay suspended in a warm, dry void. Its elevation is extreme relative to every other structure in the region: Aeru is the only high ground for thousands of kilometers of emptiness, a solitary plateau rising out of a salt-dark expanse with no horizon line but its own abrupt, windless edges. There is no water. There has never been rain. The chemistry of the place runs on heat, mineral dust, and slow electrical discharge.

The air is warm—about 1.8 on whatever scale measures comfort—dry enough that breath feels like inhaling warm ash, and thin with altitude. There is no sky in the conventional sense; above the shelf, the void is neither black nor blue but a deep, featureless brown-gray, and the light that falls on Aeru is diffuse, sourced not from a star but from a distant, steady thermal glow that keeps the world in perpetual, flat afternoon. Normal light, if one is accustomed to absence.

The terrain is old. Erosion here is not the violence of rivers—there are none—but the patient, settled-form weathering of a place that has reached its own equilibrium. Wind, carrying microscopic silica, has carved the basalt into long, low ridges that run like ribs across the plateau. The surface is a mosaic of black glass, rust-red iron oxide, and pale, chalky dust. In the low places between ridges, the dust collects in drifts as fine as flour, still and unbroken for centuries. Everything is in balance with its own stillness.

The scale is continental. One could walk for months across Aeru and never reach its other edge. The land does not feel large in the human sense; it feels *structural*, like being inside a single, enormous room with no ceiling.

Within this immensity, presence is sparse—1.4 of saturation—scattered like islands of intent in an ocean of mineral silence. The built environment occupies narrow, linear bands along the ridge tops and in a central cluster that might be called a city, though it behaves more like a machine arranged for sleeping. This is **Thrum**, the settlement: an urban lattice of low, circular towers and long, covered arcades made not of stone but of compressed dust-brick and dark ceramic. The towers are close together—urban density—but they are small, and between them run wide, empty plazas where nothing grows and nothing moves quickly.

Thrum is industrial. Its presence is defined by engines that never stop, though they move slowly—1.0 on the dynamism—turning over on decadal cycles with the patience of geology. Deep below the ceramic streets, massive piston-arrays and thermal mills draw heat from the plateau's own warm bedrock, turning that energy into electricity that runs in thick, insulated cables along the ridge-spines. Factories do not roar; they *pulse*, a low, subsonic thrum (the city's namesake) that passes through the feet and the chest rather than the ears. The soundscape of Aeru is mostly quiet—1.2—broken only by the distant, slow breathing of these mechanisms and, occasionally, the dry crackle of thermal vents.

There are no plants. The vegetation parameter is 0.2—functionally absent. In place of flora, the ridges host thin, brittle mats of crystallized sulfur that grow like slow coral, and in the darkest crevices between towers, fungal analogues spread in flat, translucent films that feed on heat and electricity rather than light. These are not recognized as life by the old categories, but they are present—simple, mineral-adjacent, patient.

The fauna is abundant—1.9—though abundance here does not mean crowding. The creatures of Aeru are large, sparse in distribution, and strange. They are not warm-blooded in any familiar way. The dominant forms are long, low bodies of articulated chitin and ceramic-like shell, ranging from the size of carts to the size of buildings, that crawl through the dust-drifts on hundreds of thin legs. They move slowly—matching the world's 1.0 dynamic—sometimes remaining motionless for years, feeding on the sulfur mats and the slow-decaying mineral waste of Thrum's industry. Their eyes, if they are eyes, are flat plates that reflect the thermal glow. There are smaller ones too: swarms of dry, wingless insects that navigate by sensing electrical fields, and flightless birds with hollow bones filled with warm air, nesting in the factory exhaust flues.

The ecology is circular and slow. The creatures produce waste that mineralizes into new dust. The dust feeds the fungal films. The films feed the sulfur mats. The mats feed the creatures. Thrum's engines extract heat, and the extraction draws new mineral flows upward from deep within the plateau, shifting the ground imperceptibly over centuries. The world is dynamic only on long durations—gradual cycles, patient processes, weak gradients.

The rules of Aeru are rules of patience and conservation. There is no water to waste, so every chemical process is closed-loop. The industrial tech—2.3—is not rapid but sustained: mechanized production of ceramic parts, electrical storage in dense mineral batteries, the slow refining of rock into building material. The settlement is urban—1.8—not because people crowd together out of love for proximity, but because heat is shared and resources must be kept within walking distance across a terrain that kills the isolated.

Weirdness—2.2—is present in the details that do not resolve. Some of the large creatures carry embedded pieces of old machinery in their shells, fused there over generations. The electrical cables running along the ridges emit a faint, visible shimmer in the warm air, and following them far enough away from Thrum, one finds them continuing into the empty dust-plain, serving nothing, carrying power to dead ends. In certain low basins, the erosion has revealed perfect geometric hollows in the rock—too regular for nature, too old for the current settlement—filled with a fine, humming black sand that moves in slow spirals without wind.

This is a world without sky, without plant, without rush. Warm, high, dry, vast, and scattered. The sound is mostly quiet. The change is slow. The presence of the built and the living is sparse but absolute—industrial, abundant in creature, strange in its harmony, settled into a form that will outlast anyone who tries to hurry it.
