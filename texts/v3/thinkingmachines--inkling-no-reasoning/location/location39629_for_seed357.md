The valley is only generations old. Steam vents through fractures in black basalt that have not yet learned roundness—sharp, fresh, glass-edged—releasing hot mineral mist into air already saturated. This is a high shelf, open and extending for miles, recently uplifted by forces that left no time for soil to form, no time for weather to soften anything. Every surface is made, but nothing was manufactured; everything was grown into being, engineered by root and tendril rather than hand.

The vegetation is total. There is no bare rock visible, no earth exposed. Instead, the ground is a thick, continuous substrate of interwoven living carpet—moss that behaves like concrete, vines that braid into load-bearing cabling, trunks fused into arching causeways over hollows where water pools inches below the surface. The plant mass is architecture: walls, floors, bridges, ceilings, all alive. They grow not in clusters but in singular, monumental structures separated by long, empty expanses of humid air—dense in substance but sparse in presence, like scattered cathedrals in a fogbound valley. Between them, mist hangs in vast, open chambers of negative space.

Water is everywhere, though rarely in open bodies. The atmosphere is wet—condensing on every leaf-surface, beading on waxy fronds, weeping in constant thin rivulets from the upper canopies. Post-rain conditions are perpetual; the valley never dries because the heat (steady, thick, tropical in its insistence) drives evaporation that immediately returns as warm precipitation caught in the plant mesh. Small pools of warm black water sit in the hollows of fused root-systems, reflecting diffuse gray-green light that filters down through layers of translucent foliage.

There are no animals. No insects buzz, no birds call from the spires of green. The ecology is purely botanical—pure plant-as-substrate, a kingdom without citizens. And yet it is loud. The sound is constant, turbulent, biological and thunderous: the high-pressure hiss of steam escaping young fractures; the creak and pop of rapid woody growth, trunks thickening audibly in the heat; the rush and snap of water being pulled through root-veins like blood through arteries; the wet collapse of overgrown sections dying and falling back into the substrate to be immediately consumed and rebuilt elsewhere. The valley creates and destroys itself in continuous hyperactive turnover—structures reaching toward the mist and then sagging, falling, being overgrown within hours. It behaves like an anthill without ants, like a mind without animals, all energy directed into growth and collapse.

The built environment is complete. Every feature—pillars, arches, the very ground—is plant constructed into form, grown with the precision of architecture but the chaos of storm-state. No tools exist, no technology, no marks of design except the design of biology itself operating at massive scale. The place is strange, young, sharp, and endlessly wet: a high valley of total green architecture suspended in hot mist, loud with its own turbulent becoming.
