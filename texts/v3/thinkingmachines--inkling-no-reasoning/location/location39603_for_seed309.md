The hollow is called the Bulb, though no one remembers who named it. It lies beneath the surface plate of the old capital—three hundred paces below the cracked pavement—an inverted cathedral carved not by miners but by centuries of root-pressure and dripping rustwater. Scale is intimate, almost hamlet-sized: one can cross it in an hour if they stay on the suspended causeways, yet the space feels larger because of its height, its vaulted darkness, and the sheer volume of living matter packed into every vertical plane.

This is a place where water is present but never triumphant. The atmosphere is strange—2.4—because the rules here are hybrid and unsettled. Light is dim, 0.6, a twilight regime where photosynthesis happens in slow motion and vision is always partial. Shadow is not absence but a substance, thickened by spores and the bodies of passing insects. The place feels small—as human spaces go—yet infinite in its repetition of arch, root, and machine. You can stand in a cleared glade of dead machinery, feeling the warm damp breath of the root-vault above, and hear the factory-spires churning in the dark, while an overwhelming tide of life flows through you like weather.

It is below the world. It is overgrown. It is working, loudly, in the dark.
