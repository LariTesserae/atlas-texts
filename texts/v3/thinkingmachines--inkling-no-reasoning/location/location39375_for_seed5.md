A curtain of ivy falls away, and you step inside—not through a gate, but through absence, into the hollow left between two demolished ribs of a low city that has forgotten its own streets. This is the Pocket.

The scale is that of a held breath. The walls are layered brick, mortar turned to sugar by centuries of damp, now overgrown with moss so thick it behaves like upholstery—lush, packed, leaving no masonry bare. Every surface is occupied. Ferns root in the cracks of collapsed stairwells; shelf fungi plate over rusted hand-forged railings in overlapping scales; vines weave through the empty frames of windows that open onto nothing but more wall. The air is cold—near freezing, a stillness that does not fluctuate—with a dampness that lives not in rain but in beads of condensation, in the slow weep of stone, in pockets of moisture that gather in broken basins and never fully evaporate. No river runs here, only the quiet accumulation of breath and seep.

Light arrives bright, but only because the roof is missing. A high, ragged aperture lets in a flat, white sky—constant, unvarying, casting no moving shadow. There is no wind to shift the leaves. The temperature never dips or rises. The place is in perfect equilibrium, a frozen moment that has lasted long enough to become geology.

Beneath the vegetation, the bones are urban and primitive: hand-laid stone, timber darkened and petrified by rot, simple iron nails now blooming with orange decay. No engines, no metal beyond what can be beaten into shape by hand. The architecture is intimate-room made massive—a single chamber of collapsed houses, arranged so tightly that corridors have become burrows and courtyards have become caves.

The density is absolute. There is no empty space, only transitions—moss giving way to lichen, lichen to the fur of sleeping things. Life is abundant, yet it makes no noise. Small bodies—scaled, shelled, soft-whiskered—move through the green darkness with such slowness they seem part of the architecture. They do not call. They do not hunt with sound. The silence is total, broken only by the near-inaudible drip of condensation falling onto stone that has already heard it for a thousand years.

Here is the strangeness: the pocket does not grow. The lushness is not advancing, not retreating. The fauna are not breeding, not dying out. The moss is neither spreading nor withering. The bright light from above illuminates a world that needs nothing from it. This is not an ecosystem in motion but an image held in balance—erosion so long-established it has become a kind of architecture, weathered into its own permanent state.

Everything is hidden, embedded within. You are not viewing a landscape; you are inside a single feature, a micro-world sealed within the low, forgotten elevation of a dead settlement. The air tastes of cold mineral water and chlorophyll. You can feel that you are permitted here only as long as you remain still—because stillness is the rule of the world, the only dynamic allowed in a place that never changes.
