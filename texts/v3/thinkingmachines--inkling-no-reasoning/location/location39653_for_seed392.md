**The Condensation Flats**

The land is flat and young: a valley barely five generations old since the earth finished pushing it up, its ridges sharp as unfiled teeth, its floor a lattice of shallow ceramic pans no deeper than a palm, every one filled with warm, motionless water. There is no dirt here, no soil, no bedrock. The substrate is a single continuous shell—fired ochre clay and rough-poured lime-concrete—broken only by weeping seams where groundwater, pushed upward by some silent engine, beads along vertical walls and hangs in heavy droplets that never fall. The air is thick with it; condensation coats every surface in a sheen that never evaporates, a perpetual post-rain haze without the rain ever having come. It is hot, always the same heat, a dense low-country warmth that presses downward like a hand.

This is a made valley—built, not grown—yet the vegetation has claimed it with an abundance that borders on invasion. Thick ropes of pale green vine, fat-leaved and translucent, spill over the low ceramic berms and pool in the water, their roots drilling hairline cracks in the mortar through sheer persistence. Ferns with metallic fronds cluster where the weeping is strongest, drinking from the air itself, unfurling in perfect stillness. Nothing dies. Nothing browns. The growth is lush but scattered, leaving wide spaces between clumps—empty ceramic courtyards where steam shimmers upward and nothing moves. The plants do not sway. There is no wind.

The water, too, is arranged. Thousands of shallow pools, linked by narrow overflow channels carved with primitive bronze chisels—early-tool scars visible in the clay lips—hold the same level, the same temperature, day after unchanging day. No erosion wears the edges further; the concrete is new, young, its sharp features holding. Nothing drains away. The valley exists in a frozen equilibrium, its dynamics stilled, its gradients flat.

Light comes without a visible sun, a bright, sourceless white that fills the basin from edge to edge, casting no shadows that shift, illuminating the sparse architecture: circular wall-rings of blackened brick no taller than a seated person, open to the sky, arranged like interrupted ripples across the open expanse. They are dwellings or observation points, separated by long stretches of emptiness. Between them, small fauna exists in strange density—glass-skinned frogs that sit on ceramic lips, insects with too many translucent wings that line the water’s edge in trembling rows, and serpentine creatures of simple bone and soft tissue that glide through the pools without disturbing them. They do not hunt. They do not flee. They maintain position, abundant and patient, in a silence so deep it feels like a physical material, broken only by the occasional drop of condensation striking water with a soft, purposeless *tick*.

But the reality here is not entirely obedient. The built environment is absolute—there is no ground that is not made—yet it behaves like a membrane that remembers being earth. At certain distances, the repetition of pool and wall creates a visual stutter; looking across the open valley, one sees the far rings not smaller, but compressed, layered on top of near ones, as if perspective itself has been carved flat. The vines, though scattered, grow in patterns that mirror the circular architecture with impossible precision, even in pools far from any wall, as if they were mapped before being planted.

More unsettling is the water’s refusal to be still. Though its level never changes, its surface does not always hold. In scattered pools—no pattern, no cause—reflection fails. The water shows not the bright sky above, but depths that do not exist, downward spirals of black ceramic and green vine that extend far beneath the shallow pan, a vertigo of false bottom. Those who drink from these pools—there are figures, sparse, in simple woven cloth, using bronze cups—do not thirst. The water is already inside them, weeping gently from their pores, returning always to its source.

The sound remains low, a quiet that is not absence but suppression. The frogs make no croak. The wind does not arrive. Voices, when they come, are flat and do not carry far across the open space, dissolving into the humidity before reaching another wall. The people move slowly, repairing a crack that will not widen, harvesting plants that do not rot, filling cups that do not empty. The technology is primitive—hand-molded bricks, woven fiber, simple metal. The world is young, fully constructed, lush with invasive life, bathed in unwavering bright heat, and held in a stability so complete it has become its own kind of breakdown: a place that never was, functioning perfectly as if it always had been.

Here, the condensation never dries. The pools never spill. The valley breathes without lungs, and waits.
