The bowl is barely forty paces across, a high pocket of young stone cupped in a recent sky. There is no water here—never was, never will be in this form—only a dry, mineral breath that carries the faint saline tang of an ancient, sealed absence. The elevation is sharp and fresh; the walls are un-eroded, a cathedral of raw basalt and pale gypsum, steep and unweathered, enclosing this tiny settlement-scale grove at the height of a single, held breath.

Within these sharp bounds, vegetation is lush and impossible. Not green—nothing chlorophyll-green has ever touched this place—but saturated in rust-red and bone-white, thick with fibrous vines and broad, waxy leaves that do not transpire. They drink nothing, because there is nothing to drink; they pull mineral mist directly from the stone, and they sit so densely packed, layer upon layer, that every surface seems upholstered in living felt. The air is warm—steady, gentle warmth without sun or flame—seeping up from a shallow, biological hearth far below.

This is urban, but not built by hands. The roots have arranged themselves into architecture. Thick arterial vines form vaulted corridors no wider than an arm’s reach; smaller filaments knit into lattice walls, into platforms suspended by tension alone, into spiraled stair-ways of root-cord that climb the young rock. There is no technology—no metal, no fire, no tool, only the patient, pre-tool logic of growth. The settlement breathes slowly. It is dense with presence: moss-analogs carpet every ledge, and the fauna is abundant, though quiet—dozens of small, translucent beings, shelled in pearl, moving through the lattice like blood through tissue, feeding on mineral crust, mating in long, silent cycles that take years.

The light is ordinary, diffuse, entering through fissures in the young ceiling—no source, just presence—enough to reveal the strange normality of it all. For this is weird only in its refusal to be dead without water: a cathedral-scale ecology thriving in absolute drought, a sealed garden in a gasless void, growing slowly, so slowly. The sound is near-nothing. No drip, no wind, no voice—only the faintest, occasional click of a shell against root, or the slow, audible creak of a vine tightening its grip on fresh stone.

The rules here are biological-only, mineral-bound, sealed. Change is glacial. A new root-bridge might form over a decade; a vine might shift its arch by a finger’s width in a season. The erosion is young—this place was revealed only recently, perhaps by a slow cracking of the earth or a retreating mineral tide. The stone is sharp, not smoothed, holding the memory of its birth.

And the place becomes itself as you stay: not a desert, though there is no water. Not a ruin, though it is built. Not a machine, though it is structured. Only this—small, dense, warm, silent, lush without thirst—an urban grove in a dry mineral heart, waiting in its own patient, root-built city.
