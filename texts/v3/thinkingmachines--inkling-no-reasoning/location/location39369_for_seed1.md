It is a place that has finished being a place.

The lowland lies at the bottom of a world that forgot elevation long ago. Here, near zero altitude, the crust is not elevated but accumulated—layer upon layer of mineral fatigue pressed flat by time and density. The temperature is barely a concept, a cold that does not chill but simply refuses warmth, holding everything in a state just above mineral sleep.

Water is everywhere and exactly nowhere. At 2.1 out of 3, it does not flow in rivers or pool in lakes. Instead it lives inside the stones. The bedrock weeps constantly—salt water squeezed from microscopic fractures, condensing out of the grey air onto every surface. The atmosphere is saturated, a thick wet breath that never becomes rain, only perpetual droplet, only mist that clings rather than falls. Underfoot is marsh-margin that isn’t marsh—there is no soil, no peat, only black obsidian sand saturated past its capacity, water suspended in the interstices between grains, the ground itself a sponge that has forgotten how to be dry.

Vegetation is 0.4—absent, almost. Nothing green dares the cold and the density. But absence is not emptiness. The mineral world is colonized by non-plant life: fungal networks of impossible geometry, glassy mycelial mats that spread across the wet rock in patterns like circuit boards designed by geology. Or simpler—stained membranes, translucent and grey, that digest stone and exude phosphorescence too weak to call light. There are organisms that do not read as biology, energy patterns nesting in the cracks, or pre-life chemistries still deciding whether to become cells. The scale is open, 2.4—this extends for miles, a regional expanse of flat-weeping stone that reaches past sight into a flat horizon where sky and saturated air are indistinguishable.

Erosion is 2.6: ancient, primordial. This is not a landscape being shaped; it is a landscape that has already reached its final form. The oldest stable state. The stones are rounded not by recent river but by eons of water pushing through them from within, smoothing them into identical, smooth-black pebbles packed so tightly—density 2.6, packed, saturated, no room—that the ground feels more like a single object fractured into billions of touching pieces. Near-flattened, yes, but also near-completed. Every feature is settled into its ending shape.

There are no buildings. Built is 0.3—none. No ruins, no artifacts, no sign that anyone ever tried to claim this. The technology, however, is 2.5—industrial, mechanized, electrified, scaled. But the industry is not human. It is embedded, intrinsic, reality’s own machinery breaking down and operating at once. Deep beneath the weeping surface, engines of impossible metallurgy turn without fuel, producing not goods but more density. The sound—2.3, loud—is constant: not wind, not water rushing, but the grind of those buried mechanisms, the scream of mineral being pressed past its structural limit, the hum of condensers that have run for geological ages. The noise is industrial and inhuman, a factory without workers, without purpose, only continuous production of pressure.

The fauna is 2.3—abundant, but wrong. In the absence of plants and in the presence of endless wet-cold, life has taken other roads. Swarms of translucent, multi-limbed things crawl between the packed stones, feeding on the fungal mats and the mineral sweat. They are not insects, not crustaceans—perhaps they are the fauna of a reality that broke after they evolved. They move in patterns that suggest intelligence distributed across hundreds of bodies. They are loud too, clicking and scraping, adding their biological noise to the industrial roar below.

Light is 1.2—dim. There is no sun visible through the saturated air. The dimness is not darkness but a refusal of illumination, as if photons have become tired in this place. The only brightness comes from the fungal membranes and occasional sparks where the buried engines breach the surface—blue-white arcs that illuminate nothing, only announcing more machinery.

Weirdness is 3.0—total breakdown. Reality does not hold firm here. The laws are suggestions. Water behaves like memory, seeping upward when it should fall. The packed stones occasionally shift not by gravity but by agreement, rearranging themselves in patterns that suggest thought. The fauna and the machinery have merged in ways that make taxonomy impossible—creatures with gears for hearts, engines with fungal growth for circuitry. The boundary between biology and technology, between erosion and design, between alive and operated upon, has dissolved entirely.

The dynamic is 1.6—active, but slowly, immensely. Multiple processes occur simultaneously in every square foot: condensation, mineral digestion, mechanical turning, biological swarm, reality-fracture. Nothing is still. Yet nothing moves quickly. It is the pace of ending, of a world that is busy finishing itself.

This is a low, open, wet-cold plain where nothing was built and everything was made—made by time, made by pressure, made by the strange industry of a broken world. It is post-rain forever, weeping, packed, loud, brightly dim, alive with wrong things, and entirely, anciently itself.
