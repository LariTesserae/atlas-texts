Somewhere in the vertical scale of the world, far above the compressed floor and far below the broken sky, there is a plateau of water that does not fall.

The air here is not air. It is a warm, dense liquid—thick with fine mineral breath, slow as syrup, carrying the taste of ancient copper and the soft rot of things that have never seen drought. At 2.4 elevation, the pressure should crush, but the physics here have settled into a different agreement. The water does not weigh downward; it holds. It pools in vast sub-aquatic continents, suspended in endless twilight, moving only in the long, warm pulses of its own slow heart.

This is a place of nearly total erasure—erosion 2.7—where every sharpness has been dissolved by eons of patient abrasion. There are no mountains, only the faintest ghosts of them: ridges worn into spiraling curves, valleys filled not with stone but with layered sediment of deep color—ochre, bruised violet, black-green. The world has reached its oldest-stable-state, its final form, a near-flattened realm of endless shallow basins that touch edge to edge like the scales of some immense, sleeping creature.

The vegetation is lush—1.8—though "lush" implies light that does not exist here. In this dimness—1.4—the plants are not green but bioluminescent in the slow register of deep things. Vast forests of frond and root hang downward and float upward with equal intent. They are not anchored to soil, for soil is long dissolved; they cling to each other in dense, interwoven cathedrals of fiber and translucent membrane. The scale is vast—2.6—continental weather moving in liquid spirals, warm fronts of nutrient-rich current that roll across hundreds of miles like weather fronts made of silk. The density is high—2.4—every cubic yard thick with suspended life: microscopic blooms, drifting spores, root-networks that have never touched ground, and the slow, silent negotiation of roots that recognize each other by chemical memory.

The fauna is rare—0.6. Where there is movement, it is slow, tentative. Translucent things drift like thoughts—creatures without eyes, or with eyes that have never needed to open, filtering the warm darkness for the chemical signatures of other lives. They are scattered, solitary, ancient in their own briefness. There is no predation here, only the slow merging and separating of bodies that have forgotten the purpose of speed.

Sound is nearly absent—0.5—replaced by a silence that has weight. The only noise is the occasional, faraway collapse of a root-mass too heavy for its own patience, sending slow ripples through the warm liquid like a bell struck in a cathedral of wool.

The weirdness—2.8—permeates not as spectacle but as normalization. Here, reality has broken down and then reassembled itself according to rules that do not include gravity as we understand it, do not include death as finality, do not include the separation of self from environment. The boundary between plant and water is theoretical. Roots drink and are drunk. The water remembers every living thing that has dissolved into it, and in its warmth—2.3—those memories circulate, not as ghosts but as chemistry. Time moves differently. A single generation of the floating frond-forests takes centuries; the erosion takes millennia; the place itself feels like the end of life not in the sense of death, but in the sense of having achieved a state so complete, so settled, that change is only the slow rearrangement of what is already perfect.

Built structures—0.8—are scattered, faint, and not truly built. They are the calcified remains of things that may have once been architecture, or may have always been the exoskeletons of organisms that grew too large and then collapsed into geometric patterns. There are no tools, no technology—0.5—only the biological. These remains stand as single, isolated pillars or half-circles of smooth, bone-white material that do not erode further because they have already reached the final stage of erosion: they are part of the landscape’s settled grammar. No one built them; no one lives in them. They are artifacts of a process that produces form without intent, like stalactites in a cave that has forgotten the surface.

The world is active—2.4—not with violence, but with relentless, gentle motion. Currents circulate nutrients in loops that take years. Warm pockets of decomposing vegetation release bubbles of ancient gas that rise slowly through the dense liquid and never break the surface, because there is no surface—only higher layers of equal patience. The ecology moves at the pace of geology married to biology: nothing is still, but nothing hurries. It is a post-flood world without the concept of dry land, an ocean made vertical, an atmosphere made aqueous.

This is a place where you could drift forever and never fall, never starve, never be alone in the chemical sense—because the dense, warm, silent water is full of everything that has ever lived here, dissolved and present and waiting. It is vast enough for its own weather, old enough to have forgotten youngness, warm enough to feel like being held, and strange enough that to enter it is to understand, slowly, that the division between observer and observed was only a temporary arrangement, now gently corrected by the patient water.
