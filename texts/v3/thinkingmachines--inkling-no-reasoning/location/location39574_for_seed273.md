The sky is not open. At this elevation—where the air is thin, bright, and relentless—there is only the underside of the Canopy, a single, continent-spanning plate of polished obsidian-glass and rust-bloomed iron, suspended over the valley like a lid that was once a sky. Light does not fall from above. It comes sideways, refracted through the translucent seams of the substrate, and it comes up, reflected from the polished black basalt floor of the Basin, a warm, scattered luminosity that fills the valley without shadow, bright enough to bleach the colors of growth into saturated amber and emerald and bone.

This is a place of absolute construction. There is no earth, only engineered substrate: layer upon layer of sintered stone, ceramic lattice, and compressed slag that form the floor, the walls, and the mountains of this enclosed world. The walls do not slope naturally; they stair-step in slow, eroded terraces of black basalt and bronze-green oxide, each step the remnant of a long-banished geological process now locked in its own weathered equilibrium. The erosion is established, patient—nothing collapses suddenly, everything settles over centuries into roundness, into the polished sheen of surfaces that have been touched by time and moisture since long before memory.

The scale is open, extending past the immediate: the Basin stretches for miles in every visible direction, a town-sized valley of multi-feature density, where structures and growths and mechanisms interleave without boundary, near-touching, layered, high-saturation. The air is warm, holding heat in the thermal mass of the substrate, damp with the constant, intermittent breath of moisture—condensation beading on ceramic surfaces, rain that gathers in channels and falls in brief, localized showers from the seams of the Canopy, riverine pockets that move in sluggish, engineered channels through districts of overgrowth, never flooding, always present, always returning.

There is vegetation everywhere—lush, abundant, and entirely made. Roots do not seek soil; they seek nutrient channels, wrapping around ceramic pipes and threading through the porous stone in dense, verdant mats. Moss-glow covers the lower walls in emerald velvet. Thick, waxy leaves—some broad as shields, others needle-thin—crowd the vertical surfaces, growing in gardens that hang from the stepped terraces like green waterfalls. Vines with pale, translucent stems climb the flanks of old machinery, not destroying but embracing, turning rust into habitat. The ecology here is dense, thick with presence: every volume is occupied, every surface shared.

The machinery is industrial, but it is old—engines that turn with the patience of geology. Massive pistons move in slow, grinding cycles, their sound loud but constant, a bass thunder of gears and the hiss of releasing steam that fills the valley without urgency. There is electricity, but it is subdued, running through exposed conduits that glow with a warm, amber light, powering pumps that circulate the riverine moisture, powering vents that exhale the damp, warm breath of the place. Nothing here is rapid. Change is gradual, cycles measured in decades. A gear turns; a garden advances an inch per year. The plants grow slowly into their mechanical partners, and the machinery rusts slowly into its green symbiosis.

Above all, there is the fauna—abundant, strange only in their perfect belonging. They are made, like everything else, but they move with an autonomy that feels like life: small, articulated creatures of brass and ceramic and woven fiber, with many jointed legs and gentle, probing mandibles, crawling through the vegetation in dense, layered colonies. They tend the growth—pollinating the artificial flowers, cleaning the channels, carrying moisture in their hollow bodies from one district to another. They are not alone. Larger forms, like slow-moving factory-beasts with ribbed carapaces of polished steel and eyes of soft green phosphor, wander the open spaces between structures, grazing on the thick moss, leaving behind polished trails in the substrate.

The atmosphere is one of settled form—long-existed, weathered, in balance with itself. This is not a place of conflict or sudden revelation. It is a place that has learned its own rules: the light is bright and eternal, the sound is loud and continuous, the temperature is warm, the water is always present but never overwhelming. The built environment is total, yet nothing dominates; everything is made, yet everything has been given time to become itself. The valley breathes slowly, beneath its broken-glass sky, a dense and lush and patient world, where the industrial and the verdant have had centuries to agree on what they are.
