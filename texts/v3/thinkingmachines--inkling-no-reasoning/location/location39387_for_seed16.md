The hollow keeps its heat.

It is not large. You could pace its width in thirty strides, yet it feels cavernous because almost nothing fills the air between its walls—only dry mineral warmth, the low thunder of unseen engines, and the scent of iron and resin. They call it a pocket: a sealed crater in a dead flatland of baked salt and black basalt, sunk barely ten meters below the surrounding floor, its ceiling lost somewhere in smoke. Scale is 0.5—intimate, hidden, embedded—yet within that containment there is an urban density of purpose, layers of hand-built settlement stacked like the rings of a tree that never grew.

No water exists here. Not in pipes, not in cells, not in the breath of the air. The dryness is absolute, a zero-value world where chemistry runs on oils, on molten salts, on the vapor of crushed minerals. Temperature hovers at 2.8—oppressive, constant, the kind of heat that does not fluctuate with day or shadow because there is little of either. Light is 0.6—dim, bruised-red, seeping from fissures in the basalt rim and from smoldering grates at the floor’s center, casting long shadows that do not move with any sun.

And yet, vegetation is lush—1.5—thriving in conditions that should be lethal. The life here does not drink. Thick, waxy vines with black-purple leaves spiral up the inner walls, their roots plugged into warm ceramic cisterns that circulate molten salt instead of sap. Broad fungal caps, broad as serving trays, cluster in the hollow’s eastern quadrant, glowing faintly with bioluminescence in the dimness, feeding on thermal seepage. Moss-like growths carpet the lower tiers in deep green and rust-orange, not photosynthetic but chemosynthetic, drawing energy from the mineral breath of the earth itself. They are abundant, dense with presence, but they share the space with almost nothing else; density of presence is 0.4—empty, hollow, a near-void where every plant stands isolated in its own heat-zone, separated by wide gulfs of bare, polished stone.

The built environment is urban—2.1—though primitive—1.0—constructed without electricity, without steel-beam skeletons, without the language of modern architecture. It is a settlement compressed into a pocket. Low walls of fired clay and stacked brick form nested districts: sleeping cells carved into the upper lip, tool-forges in the middle ring, and at the bottom a circular plaza of black stone where the heat is fiercest. Ropes of braided fiber hang between levels. Ladders are hewn from petrified wood. Simple metal—copper beaten to sheet, iron cast in sand—holds the structures together, riveted by hand, maintained with constant labor. The architecture is layered, settled, long-existing; erosion is 2.1—established-form—weathered into balance, not crumbling but worn smooth, the sharp edges of new construction long gone, everything rounded and settled by decades of thermal expansion and manual repair.

Sound is 1.5—loud, constant, active—2.4—moving. Not the chaos of wind, but the organized roar of human-scale industry. Heavy hand-cranked bellows wheeze like dying lungs, feeding oxygen-starved fires that burn resin and crushed coal. Hammers strike copper in rhythmic waves. From deep vertical shafts drilled into the hollow’s floor, there comes a steady, resonant thrum—low, bone-deep, like the heartbeat of the earth itself being amplified—where primitive turbines, turned by rising heat-pressure, generate motion without electricity. The air is loud with work, with circulation, with the active processes of a settlement that operates in continuous motion because stillness here means cooling, and cooling means death.

There are no animals—0.0—none whatsoever. No insects hum at the fungal caps. No birds cross the dim air. No rodents nest in the brick. Even the plants do not host them; the ecology is purely botanical and mineral, a closed loop of heat, root, and labor. Fauna is absent, leaving the space to the vegetation and the built, making the loudness of machinery and the silence of creaturely absence feel equally profound.

Weirdness is 0.6—just enough to unsettle. The vegetation moves too slowly, but with purpose, vines tightening overnight in response to temperature shifts. The fungal caps pulse with their own faint rhythm, not quite matching the distant thrum of the shafts. The air tastes of iron and absence. There is a sense, in the roundness of the architecture and the heat that never breaks, that this place was not built for outsiders, that its rules are internal and ancient—no water, only flow; no sun, only fire; no animals, only the labor that maintains the loop.

The people—if they are still people—wear thick woven robes soaked in oil, carrying simple tools, faces obscured by smoke and shadow. They maintain the vines not for beauty but for temperature regulation; they harvest the fungi for dye and medicine rendered in molten salt. They do not leave footprints in the usual sense, for the floor is polished smooth by generations of passage, worn into a surface like black glass.

This is the pocket: hidden, low, empty of life except where it clings in lush defiance, urban in its layered bones, primitive in its tools, loud with the work of staying alive, and weird only in the quiet acceptance of its own impossible balance—dry, hot, active, and permanently on the edge of its own contained heat.
