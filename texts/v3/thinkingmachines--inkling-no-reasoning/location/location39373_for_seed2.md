The ceiling is not a ceiling. It is the underside of the World-Plate, forty kilometers of interlocking titanium-cast alloy, ribbed and vaulted like the inverted ribs of some planet-sized whale, holding back not air but an ocean that weighs more than a continent. Beneath it—everywhere beneath it—is the Sub-Engine. A single, fully constructed cavity of planetary scale, hollowed, cast, riveted, and pressurized, lying at an elevation of negative infinity, submerged in water so total that “depth” is no longer a measurement of distance downward but of distance inward from the Plate.

This place was not formed. It was extruded. Fresh. The weld seams still gleam with the iridescence of unoxidized cooling, black-green and silver, sharp-edged, unrounded by any geological patience. Erosion has only just been invented here: a scheduled abrasion, not an accident. The walls are young, angry with right angles, fresh-broken by the cutting heads that hollowed them out three years ago. There is no rock. No soil. No sand that was not also manufactured, sintered into grit to line the intake chutes. Every surface is made, every plane intentional, and yet the cavity feels wildly, implausibly empty—density so low that a body falling through the central void might travel for minutes before passing within sight of a wall.

The water is the first fact. Not a pool, not a sea—an inhabited volume. Bioluminescence is absent; instead, there is normal, relentless light, emanating in long, unbroken strips from the Plate above, filtering downward through forty kilometers of clear, cold, mechanically circulated water, turning the entire column into a single luminous chamber, neither day nor night, but evenly, industrially bright. Temperature is 0.9: cool, refrigerated, kept just above freezing by thermal exchange pipes that run along every major rib like white arteries.

Within this volume, vegetation is an afterthought—scarcely present. Sparse kelp-strands of engineered bio-polymer drift near the upper intake screens, seeded to filter particulates. A few colonies of cold-tolerant, pipe-clinging moss-flora cluster around heat-sink outflows at the periphery. They are maintenance, not ecology. They do not spread; they hold station.

But the fauna—abundant, active, loud—is everywhere and nowhere. The Sub-Engine holds no plankton clouds, no swarms dense enough to blur light. The emptiness is the point. The space between presences is vast, hollow, a near-void broken only by motion. Here, the constructed water hosts its engineered inhabitants: filter-whales, each the length of a cathedral, their bodies smooth cast-ceramic shells lined with turbine-gills, moving slowly through the open column, drawing tons of water through internal processing chambers, expelling purified current in long, warm contrails that ripple across miles before dissipating. They are loud. Their internal pumps, the churning of intake valves, the hydraulic groan of their passage—these sounds carry for leagues through the uniform water, a constant, layered industrial thunder, never chaotic, always rhythmic. Smaller creatures—scavenger pods, articulated like steel crustaceans with translucent shells—ride in their wake, feeding on expelled particulates, chattering with mechanical clicks that echo off the distant walls.

The dynamic is perpetual. Weather of its own scale: thermal currents rise from the heat-exchange ribs, creating vertical rivers that the filter-whales ride like elevators; pressure differentials pulse with the intake cycles, generating slow, continent-sized tides within the enclosed volume. The water is not static. It is being worked, always—circulated, cleaned, temperature-balanced, driven through the biology and the machinery in a loop with no outside.

Below the open column, along the lower hemisphere of the cavity, the built environment asserts itself completely. This is not a seafloor but a factory bed—miles of intake grates, each large enough to swallow a city block, sucking water downward into the Sub-Engine’s primary processing loops. From these grates extend the arteries: pipes the width of canyons, transparent in sections, revealing the roaring flow within; turbine halls suspended in the water like inverted cities; engines that do not need air to burn, that extract energy from pressure differentials and thermal exchange, turning the perpetual motion of the column into electricity for the Plate above.

Sound fills everything. The loudness is structural. Water strikes the intake grates with the force of a thousand waterfalls. The filter-whales’ pumps resonate in low frequencies that travel through the hull of the world. The pipes sing—high, sustained, metallic drones as water is forced through constricted channels at industrial velocity. Even the silence between sounds is filled by the pressure of water against the ear, the constant, active presence of the environment working.

There are no predators in the old sense, only processes competing for flow. The scavenger pods swarm where extraction is richest. The filter-whales adjust their migration paths to avoid thermal shocks near the exchange ribs. The machinery adjusts to them—sensors in the grates detect their passage, slowing intake velocity, rerouting flow. Everything is mechanized, including the biology; everything is abundant in presence, yet sparse in density, scattered through a volume so vast that isolation is the default condition.

This is a place without horizon. To look “up” is to see the Plate, a grid of light forty kilometers away, sometimes obscured by a passing whale, sometimes clear, always present. To look “down” is to see more machinery, more grates, more pipes extending into darkness that is not darkness—only distance through luminous water. To look “around” is to recognize that the nearest wall might be twenty miles away, sharp-featured, fresh-built, gleaming with unweathered metal, still holding the heat of its own creation.

The Sub-Engine is young, cool, total, and fully awake. Nothing here is wild, because wildness requires an outside. Here, inside the manufactured ocean, inside the hollow heart of the constructed world, the only nature is the one that was engineered into being—vast, loud, active, and utterly, peacefully strange in its absolute, industrial normalcy.
