The dome was pressed into being yesterday, geologically speaking—sharp as a broken egg, low and round, no larger than a merchant’s courtyard. Its ceiling is a single, unseamed sheet of yellowed bone-polymer, ribbed with shallow channels that never carried rain. The floor is tessellated ceramic warmed from within; there is no soil, no bedrock beneath it, only layered insulation and the hollow tick of slow-moving gears. Every surface is built, total, sealed. Water never existed here—not as liquid, not as vapor—only the concept of thirst embedded in the joints.

But the interior is lush. Not with plants—there are no roots, no need for them—but with fabrications of vegetation: woven jute and dyed silk stitches emerging in dense, overlapping thickets. They hang from the ceiling in curtain-mats, cluster in the corners in felted bushes, and carpet the floor in low, plush ridges of moss-colored fiber that never decay. The air smells of dry wool, warm resin, and the faint copper of simple hand-wrought wire. The temperature holds steady at a drowsy warmth; nothing evaporates, nothing chills. Light comes through translucent panels in the dome—steady, mouth-of-cave normal—casting soft, unmoving shadows across the dense green weave.

Everything is small in scale, intimate, near-touching. The walls lean close, the thickets layer upon each other, and there is barely room to move without brushing against some constructed leaf. The fauna is abundant within this tight volume: thousands of small, slow creatures—perhaps they are beetles woven from dark fiber and copper thread, or they are real—moving through the woven canopy with patient, grinding steps. They do not fly; they crawl and settle. Their presence is near-total; every cubic hand-span of air seems to hold at least one small body, one glint of eye or needle-metal. They feed on nothing visible, moving in cycles that take hours to complete.

The technology is primitive, manual. Simple wooden frames hold the hanging mats. Copper needles and hand-twisted wire bind the moss-ridges together. There are no engines, only the occasional creak of a wooden dowel shifting under the weight of accumulated stillness. The dome itself was made—not grown, not eroded—pressed into its low, young shape without weather to wear it. Its edges are sharp, fresh, unrounded by time.

Sound is mostly absent. The quiet is deep, holding only the dry rustle of woven fiber against fiber, the patient scrape of small feet on ceramic, and a low, constant hum that might be the dome remembering it is alive.

But reality breaks down here, gently, without violence. The air is dry—there is no water, no concept of thirst satisfied—yet the woven vegetation is impossibly lush, dense with color and texture that should require wetness. The woven plants and the small fauna are not separate things; they share edges. A moss-ridge might open a mesh-eye and watch you with the same patience as the copper-threaded beetle resting beside it. The creatures do not reproduce in any visible way, yet they are abundant, layered, always present, as if the dome continuously remembers them into being. Time moves slowly—everything changes across long durations—yet there is no erosion, only fresh addition, fresh weave, fresh stitch. The dome feels like a single breath held for centuries.

No rules are spoken, but they are felt: do not rush. The space is made for waiting. The built world is complete; there is no outside, no natural substrate to return to. You are inside a small, warm, dry cathedral of false green, surrounded by patient, constructed life, in a place that should not be able to hold so much presence in so little air, in so little water, in so much stillness. And yet it does. Slowly. Entirely. Absolutely.
