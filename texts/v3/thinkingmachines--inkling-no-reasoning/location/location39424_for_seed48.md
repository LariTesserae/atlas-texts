The low flats extend in every direction, a settled town of weathered machinery and black basalt lying barely above the warm, stagnant water table. This is not a ruin but a long-established industrial settlement, its buildings arranged with the patient logic of decades—smokestacks lowered, conveyor belts stilled, foundry walls smoothed by years of corrosive steam into rounded, familiar shapes. Vegetation is absent in every form; no trees, no grasses, no vines split the paving, no moss softens the iron. The surface belongs entirely to mineral, metal, and the continuous, slow weeping of the earth.

Water defines the place without flooding it. The elevation is low—just enough slope to let heat shimmer in pools between warehouses—but water remains ever-present: it beads on vertical pipes, condenses on the undersides of bridges, and collects in shallow basins that mirror the dim sky. The air is thick, humid, carrying the mineral scent of hot wet stone. Light is poor, filtered through persistent low clouds and the haze of vented geothermal pressure, casting everything in a gray-brown dimness where shadows are soft and distances deceptive.

The buildings are dense with infrastructure—layered pipes, rail spurs, storage silos, generator housings—yet the density of presence is almost nothing. The volume is hollow. Vast open spaces lie between structures: empty yards of cracked obsidian paving, wide canals of sluggish warm water, air that holds no dust and no smoke. For every cubic meter of constructed world, there is an overwhelming absence of human or visible life. The settlement operates, or once did, at a scale meant for crowds, but now accommodates only silence.

That silence is broken only subtly. There is no wind through leaves—there are none—but a constant, quiet mechanical breath: the distant clank of cooling metal, the drip of condensate into open gutters, the low vibration of engines turning over in their sleep. The sound is mostly quiet, a background hum that emphasizes the emptiness.

What fills the void is not visible from any single vantage. The fauna is overwhelming—not in bodies seen, but in presence inferred. The warm, wet, mineral-rich environment supports multitudes of small, hard-shelled creatures, translucent larvae, and segmented crawlers that move through the dark spaces beneath floor grates, inside hollow pipes, across the warm water surfaces at the pace of a slow tide. They are not strange; they are exactly the ordinary life that colonizes post-industrial warmth and moisture—except here, without predators or plants, they have multiplied into an ecosystem of overwhelming numbers. They are active, always: feeding, laying, migrating through the infrastructure in cycles that keep the place in motion. The ground moves subtly with their weight in hidden chambers. The air carries the faint, organic click and scrape of chitin against rust.

Everything is in balance, settled into its own long state. Erosion has rounded the sharp edges of this urban landscape into a form that looks inevitable, as if the town grew out of the marsh rather than being placed upon it. The heat is constant, the water near the surface, the machinery present but subdued, the life overwhelming but hidden, the space open and extending past the immediate view in all directions—a regional flatness of built and biological process, quiet, active, profoundly empty of anything but what belongs there.
