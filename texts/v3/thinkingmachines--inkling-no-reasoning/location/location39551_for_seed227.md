The hollow is not large. You could cross its floor in a hundred patient steps, but you do not, because the noise keeps you crouched, and the cold has already entered your bones. It is a pocket in low ground—elevation barely registering—buried within a larger dryness. The air is near-empty of anything that moves. A density of absence. Vast space between presences. The ceiling is low, unseen in darkness so thick it has weight, and from somewhere far above or far through comes the sound: deafening, absolute, a perpetual crushing roar that is neither wind nor water, though both live inside it.

The water is not abundant. It exists in pockets—intermittent moisture, occasional weeping, condensation that beads on every surface and refuses to fall. The cold—sharp, biting, near-frozen—holds it in place. Ice traces every contour: hair-thin crystals on moss, thicker plates in the crevices of slate, a glaze over the still pools that collect only in the deepest depressions. The moisture is riverine in origin, perhaps, but here it has lost its urgency. It moves slowly, if at all. Slow cycles, patient processes, weak gradients.

Yet vegetation refuses the emptiness. Moss thrives in the damp darkness—lush, verdant, impossibly green against the black stone—carpeting the eroded walls with thick, ancient layers. Ferns unroll in the seams where water has lingered longest. They grow in the cracks of structures half-lost to time: scattered remains of primitive build, simple stone stacked by hand, a low wall that once divided nothing from nothing, a broken platform of fitted slate, an artifact of simple fabrication long since abandoned. Pre-metal. Early-tool marks. The built presence is faint, scattered like seeds, and in this hollow, it has been settled for ages—weathered, in-its-own-state, balanced within the erosion.

The erosion itself is the true architecture. Long-existed. The hollow is not new; it was worn into being by the same deafening force that still fills it with sound—water and frost and time working at a pace too gradual to witness in one life. The stone is polished smooth in arcs, rounded, settled-form. The hollow knows itself. It is not breaking apart; it is holding.

The darkness is nearly complete. Light is barely present—perhaps a faint phosphorescence in the moss, or nothing at all. You see by feeling: the slick of ice, the softness of moss, the hollow resonance of your own breath, barely audible beneath the roar.

Fauna is rare. Once, perhaps, something small moved here—a creature of shell or scale, adapted to the cold and the dark and the noise—but presence is slow and sparse. Mostly there is the hollow itself, and the sound that defines it, and the vegetation that insists on living despite the emptiness, despite the cold, despite the crushing, timeless noise.

Everything here moves on long durations. Ice thickens across centuries. Moss advances by millimeters. The sound does not vary; it is constant, eternal-seeming, though perhaps—if you listened long enough, longer than a life—you might notice its gradients weaken and strengthen with some hidden pulse, a river far away, a glacier’s slow breath. But not today. Today the hollow is as it has been: hidden, intimate, embedded within the larger world, near-void, singular in feature, and profoundly, deafeningly alive in its stillness.
