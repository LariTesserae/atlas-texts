The spine of the world runs near flat now—erosion having won its long argument with stone—so the extreme elevation is not a knife but a plateau, a high table of blackened basalt worn smooth as river-glass, stretching valley-width across the sky. At this height, warmth is a faint courtesy; the air holds near-constant at a mild, damp chill, never freezing, never inviting, just enough to keep the moss awake. The moisture is not rainfall but presence—intermittent, secretive: beads of condensation clinging to the undersides of fungal plates, pockets of vapor pooling in the hollows of petrified bark, thin films running through root-channels that serve as the only rivers here.

Everything that is not air is alive, or was alive, or is being remade into life. The substrate is plant: not soil layered upon rock, but rock buried beneath centuries of compressed vegetation until the distinction dissolved. The terrain is a continuous architecture of root and fiber—giant bryophytes that have achieved trunk-hood, their bark woven tight as thatch; creeping vines that have calcified into load-bearing walls; canopies of fern and moss that form vaulted ceilings over open spaces. Green reaches totality. Brown and grey are only the deep root-bones, ancient and hard. The space is open—regional, extending past sight—yet every cubic breath of air is technically inside the plant. The density, however, is hollow; the biomass is arranged like the ribs of some impossible cathedral, leaving vast, echoing chasms between them. One can travel for an hour through corridors of hanging root-cable and see nothing but suspended moss, the occasional drip, and the feeling of being inside the lung of a world-sized creature.

That creature breathes. The fauna is overwhelming, though rarely visible all at once—hidden inside the architecture, piled in the root-walls, swarming in the gaps. The sound is deafening: a constant, layered thunder that is not weather but biology. Insects with membranous wings beat in synchronized clouds, their drone filling the warm pockets of air like the hum of old machinery. Below them, in the hollows, amphibious things with many soft feet move through the condensation pools, calling with wet, resonant croaks. Small avian shapes—little more than feather and bone—dart through the plant-vaults, their cries piercing and constant. The overwhelming presence is not menace but saturation: there is no silence in which an animal is absent. Every surface crawls, every shadow shifts.

The elevation presses against the sky. Clouds move at eye-level, drifting through the root-spires, leaving wetness behind. Light is normal—steady, diffuse, filtered through the green vault—casting a perpetual twilight-green glow that makes shadow seem almost absent, replaced instead by deeper shades of emerald.

Erosion has done its worst and best; this is the end-state, the settled form. There are no young mountains here, only old ones ground to humility, serving as foundations for the living architecture. The world has found its final posture and is slowly digesting itself into it.

Against this, human presence is scattered—almost an afterthought in a biosphere that needs none. There are ruins not of grandeur but of persistence: low walls of stacked flat-stone, perhaps shelters or boundaries, now half-eaten by moss. Simple tools remain where they fell—wooden levers dark with age, a blade of worked stone, ropes braided from vine-fiber that have become part of the wall they once tied. These are primitive things, hand-made, pre-metal, left behind by people who lived inside the plant rather than upon it.

The weirdness is subtle but definite: the root-bridges align with precision that suggests intent yet predates any builder; the pools of moisture never fully evaporate despite constant air movement; and occasionally, in the deepest hollows, one finds plant-matter arranged into spirals too regular to be wind, too ancient to be recent—grooved patterns in fossilized bark that resemble counting, or prayer, made by something that used neither.

The rules of this place are biological: you do not walk on the ground, for there is none. You climb, or you hang, or you navigate the root-channels. The air is thick with spores and the noise of life. The temperature remains warm-damp, the moisture comes not when it should but always eventually, and the fauna does not flee. They observe. They continue.

Here, the world is not dying but completed—eroded to its essence, filled with life to saturation, deafening with the sound of its own persistence, and almost entirely empty of anything that is not green, not alive, and not listening.
