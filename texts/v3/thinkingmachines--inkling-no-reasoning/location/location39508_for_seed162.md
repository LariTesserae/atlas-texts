The air here is young.

Not long ago—measured in geologic breaths—this place was a single flat breath of magma. Now it is a plateau the size of a continent, still warm from its own birth, its edges sharp as broken enamel, its face high enough to scrape the undersides of weather. You stand at elevation, but the height is not ancient dignity; it is recent violence, a crust that thrust upward before it could learn erosion. There are no rounded mountains, only walls, scarps, and sudden vertical towns of stone that still smell of sulfur and cooling metal.

The temperature is warm, always warm, not from a distant sun—whose light reaches this place as a dim, brownish glow, barely enough to distinguish noon from twilight—but from the ground itself. The plateau breathes heat. Condensation lives everywhere: not rivers, not seas, but pockets. Moisture gathers in the hollows of fresh rock, beads on the vertical faces like sweat on new skin, collects in shallow stone basins that were molten days ago. The water scale is small, intermittent, secretive—dampness as atmosphere, not landscape. Rain comes rarely, and when it does, it is brief, warm, and metallic-tasting, as if the sky is weeping rust.

And it is lush.

That is the first contradiction that refuses to resolve. The vegetation should not exist here, not on ground this fresh, not in light this dim. Yet it thrives—dense, near-touching, layered in saturation—because the plateau is not dead rock but a living contradiction. Life did not wait for soil. It grew downward from spores carried in the turbulent air, anchored in the cracks of cooling basalt, fed by the constant exhalation of the ground. The plants are thick-matted, rubbery, deep burgundy and black-green, absorbing what little photons reach them and converting warmth into growth. They cover the sharp edges in velvet, turning weaponized geology into something that breathes. The fauna is abundant—small, quick, multilimbed things that move in the undergrowth, feeding on the warm mineral seep, nesting in the pockets of condensation, reproducing with anthill-energy, a dense, turbulent buzz of presence per cubic hand.

The scale demands its own weather. Storms do not visit this plateau; they are born here. The atmosphere is turbulent, hyperactive, a bazaar of changing states. Heat rises from the black crust in enormous columns. Moisture is pulled from the pocket-lakes and turned to low, swirling fog that never quite becomes rain. Winds tear across the flatness with nothing old enough to stop them, carrying spores, carrying the faint sounds of the living ground.

But there are sounds, and they are not ecological.

The sound scale is mostly quiet—an eerie, suppressed hush, as if the world is holding its breath—but within that quiet, there is machinery. Industrial presence, scaled and mechanized, though there are no buildings, no artifacts, no structures left untouched by absence. The technology is not placed; it is embedded. Engines rise from the crust itself, not made but grown or emerged—massive, piston-like formations of black metal and ceramic, moving with the slow inevitability of heartbeats. They do not sit upon the land; they are part of its young geology, pumping something—heat, fluid, or idea—from deep within the new earth upward through veins of pipe-like rock. There is electrification in the air, not lightning but a constant, low-frequency hum felt in the teeth, the sound of production without purpose, of factory without factory walls.

This is where reality breaks down.

The dynamics are not merely turbulent; they are unstable. The plateau is in a state of constant creation and destruction, Solaris-like in its refusal to be one thing. Sections of the crust lift by millimeters each day. New fissures open with a sound like tearing silk, exhaling warm mist. Old ones seal themselves with fresh crystal, trapping colonies of vegetation in transparent tombs that continue to grow, visible and alive, inside the stone. The fauna adapts not over generations but within hours—shifting color, multiplying, merging with the mechanized veins that pulse through the root-mats. The machinery does not conflict with the lushness; it feeds it. The engines pump nutrients—if nutrients they are—through the living carpet, and the carpet, in turn, wraps the engines in vines, softening their edges, claiming them.

There are no rules that hold for long.

The built environment remains at zero—no cities, no roads, no made things—yet the place is fully developed, fully industrial, fully inhabited by processes. The weirdness is high enough that causality is damp, like the water: present, but not flowing where expected. Distance is unreliable in the turbulent air. Objects seen in the dim light—perhaps one of the moving engines, perhaps a cluster of fauna—may be closer or farther by impossible margins. The young erosion means the land has no memory; there are no rivers carving valleys, only sudden drops, fresh chasms that open for days and close in nights.

You walk—if walking is possible—through dense layers. The ground is sharp, but cushioned by the impossible vegetation. The air is warm, damp, and mostly silent except for the mechanical breathing of the embedded industry and the quiet, turbulent rustle of abundant life. Light is dim, brown, diffuse; shadows do not exist, only gradations of warmth and color. The place is vast, continental, a world-scale brain of recent formation, and it is not finished. It will not finish. It is high, young, lush, mechanized, abundant, turbulent, strange, and quietly, constantly, remaking itself.

The moisture beads on the fresh stone. The engines turn. The plants drink both. Nothing here was built; everything arrived at once, in contradiction, and continues in the same breath.
