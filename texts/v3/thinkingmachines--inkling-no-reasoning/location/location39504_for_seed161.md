At the summit—where air is not a given but a held breath—the world collapses into a single room without a ceiling. The scale is intimate: a cathedral of root and stone no larger than a hamlet, pressed against the extreme edge of elevation at 3. Here is no horizon, only walls of dark moss that rise like the flanks of an impossible green city, dense with presence, layered and near-touching. Water is absent in every form—no dew, no running vein, no salt, no vapor. The vegetation thrives on absence, feeding on mineral fog and the warm, still air that pools like a closed palm.

It has always been this old. Erosion is not a force here but a finished state; the rock has worn into its final, primordial face, smooth as bone polished by ages, flattened and settled into deep-age stability. Nothing shifts quickly. Change moves on a patient clock, gradual cycles measured in centuries of root-growth and the slow migration of spores.

The settlement is urban in its logic, though built of living tissue and primitive craft. Towers of woven lichen stand in ordered rows, connected by bridges of braided vine and hand-laid stone, a vertical town suspended at the world’s top. The technology is simple—rope twisted by hand, shelves of pressed earth, tools of carved shell and soft copper—enough to organize, not enough to dominate. Every surface is occupied. The density is absolute: life upon life, moss over moss, insects threading through it all.

Sound is loud, relentless, but never chaotic. The roar of a thousand small bodies—wings, legs, mandibles—fills the dimness with a constant, rumbling hum. Light comes only faintly, filtered through the green architecture, leaving everything in warm shadow.

And the fauna—abundant beyond counting—owns the space. They crawl, burrow, feed, and sleep in the layered walls, part of the ecology, part of the structure. There are no predators and no prey, only the slow, loud, patient congress of beings in a warm, dry, high place that reached its end-state long ago and decided, quietly, to stay.
