The Hollow Under the Crust spreads across a continental ceiling only thirty meters beneath the surface crust—a low world, inverted and sealed, its every surface facing downward like the inside of a skull. There is no sky here, only a distant, dull membrane of calcified rock that holds back the dry void above. No rain falls. No rivers run. Water is a concept preserved in chemistry alone: moisture exists only as trace vapor locked in the spongy tissue of living things, never pooling, never running, never reaching a dredge or pool. This is aridity made architecture, a desert of absence where the air is thick but bone-dry.

The place is embryonic—erosion has not begun, for there is nothing to wear away yet. The rock faces are fresh fractures, raw geology still warm from the pressure that birthed them hours or years ago. The floor is barely formed, rising in gradual swells rather than mountains, keeping the elevation uniformly, suffocatingly low. There is no horizon. The space extends for thousands of kilometers in every direction, a continent of ceiling, large enough to hold its own stillness, large enough that air moves in slow, continent-scale breaths that take days to cross the expanse.

Yet it is not empty.

The density is absolute. Every cubic meter is spoken for. The ceiling and walls and floor are layered in an urban lattice of biological construction—countless tubes of calcified chitin, fibrous mats, and spongy towers woven so tightly they form continuous streets, districts, and vaulted neighborhoods. There are no tools, no metal, no light other than the faint, steady bioluminescence seeping from the organisms themselves. The settlement is alive, self-built, a city without builders, growing upward and downward in interlocking neighborhoods that have no spaces between them. The structures are soft and warm, pulsing gently with metabolic heat.

Within this packed architecture, life is everywhere and always in motion. The fauna is abundant—small, translucent, soft-bodied creatures that crawl through the chitinous tunnels in constant drift, grazing on the thick mats, laying eggs in the fibrous walls, being consumed by others in a cycle that never pauses. There are no predators with voices; the hunt is silent, a matter of chemical sensing and slow envelopment. The air is filled with the quiet sounds of living: a faint, continuous rustle of bodies passing through narrow passages, the almost-inaudible crack of chitin expanding, the sigh of spongy tissue contracting as it absorbs what little warmth remains. Silence dominates, but it is a populated silence, dense with presence.

The vegetation is lush, but it is not green in any familiar way. The plants—if they are plants—are thick, waxy, and deep crimson, growing in vertical forests that hang downward from the ceiling like inverted jungles, their roots reaching into the porous rock above, drawing minerals directly from the fresh fracture surfaces. They form canopies that interlock, leaving no open air. The temperature holds steady at a warm, womb-like equilibrium, never hot, never cool, maintained by the collective respiration of trillions of organisms in this enclosed, waterless ecology.

This is a world just coming into being. The geology is unfinished, still deciding its own shape. The settlement is not old; it is immediate, a response written into existence by the first pressure, the first warmth, the first chemical breath. It is active without urgency—biological pace multiplied by continental scale, a slow-motion explosion of occupation that fills every gap as quickly as the raw rock can provide it.

There is no outside. The crust above is sealed. The void beyond is dry. Within, the Hollow breathes, builds, eats, and grows in perfect, silent saturation, a city of flesh and fiber spreading across the underside of a newborn world.
