The ground remembers being a riverbed. Only months—perhaps years—have passed since the waters pulled back, leaving this low hollow, barely above the old course, sharp with fresh geology. Gravel ridges still bear the knife-edges of recent erosion, cutting lines through moss that has rushed in to claim them with unnatural speed. It is cool here, always cool, the air holding a riverine dampness even without rain: beads of condensation gather on black iron railings, on the glass of factory windows, on the broad, flat leaves of ferns that have colonized every crack between cobblestones. The moisture is intermittent, pocketed—dripping from one eave, absent ten paces away—so the lushness is patchy, dense thickets of green separated by bare, young stone.

The scale is intimate, a single neighborhood of perhaps four curving streets, bounded by the crumbling lip of the old embankment on one side and a still-breathing canal lock on the other. Within these limits, the built world asserts itself with industrial confidence—brick warehouses with iron truss roofs, a single square smokestack that exhales slow, gray rhythm, and cobbled lanes where rusted tracks disappear under carpets of creeping vine. The settlement feels recently layered, as though machinery and masonry arrived together and have not yet negotiated their borders. Electric lines hang in loose loops between buildings, not humming but resting, patient.

The vegetation is abundant but scattered in clumps—spatial, respectful of distance. Ferns rise waist-high between abandoned rail cars. Moss softens every vertical surface not actively scoured by wind. A kind of broad-leafed weed—almost ornamental—grows in the gutters, its roots finding the fresh mortar of young walls. There is plenty of life, but it holds back, leaving long corridors of empty air, of open cobble, of sky visible between structures. The noise is constant but not chaotic: the low, resonant churning of a water wheel that never stops, the clank of a distant gate in slow repetition, the steam-release hiss from the factory every several minutes like a breathing animal. Beneath it, if you stand still, the drip of condensation and the rustle of something large but hidden in the ferns.

Fauna is everywhere, though rarely seen. Pigeons cluster on the iron beams, but also stranger, slower birds—gray, heavy-bodied, watching—roost in the derelict loading bays. Insects move through the moss carpets in thick, dark streams when the moisture peaks. Small mammals have tunneled into the fresh erosion banks, leaving neat holes that seem too large for the bodies that made them. The ecology is young, experimental, finding its rules in real time.

The weirdness is subtle, a slight wrongness in the geometry. The buildings lean not with age but with an early, untested precision—corners too sharp, windows placed at intervals that feel calculated for a purpose no one remembers. The moss grows in perfect spirals on certain walls and not on others, as if selecting. The sound echoes differently here; voices carry farther across the empty spaces, returning with a half-second delay, as though the hollow itself is still learning its acoustics. Nothing moves quickly. The water in the canal shifts almost imperceptibly, carrying leaf-drifters in long, patient arcs. Even the industrial mechanisms operate at a restrained pace—the wheel turns, the gate lowers, the pipes warm—cycles measured in hours, not seconds.

This is a place of patient occupation: human industry and fresh, eager ecology locked in a slow negotiation, separated by generous spaces of air and shadow, loud with mechanical breath, cool and intermittently wet, small enough to walk across in minutes but strange enough to feel like it extends inward without boundary.
