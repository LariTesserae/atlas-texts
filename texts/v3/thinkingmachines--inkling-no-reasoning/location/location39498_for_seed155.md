Above the fog line of an older world lies the Great Pith Town, a cool, high settlement spread across a valley-sized basin of ancient erosion—everything worn flat not by water but by the slow gnawing of root and time. Here, vegetation is not scenery; it is the substrate. The ground, the walls, the roofs, and the sky-hung highways are all continuous living architecture: a dense, sparse-in-between weave of corky vines, spongy bark-plates, and fibrous moss-cables that fill every structural need with plant material. Because every surface breathes, the air is thick with damp—not rain-soaked, but perpetually exhaling cool condensation from pore and stoma; moisture beads along root-corridors in intermittent pearls, dripping in a slow, scattered rhythm onto the hollow floors below.

The scale is open—regional, extending past immediate sight—but the density is low, airy, almost hollow. Buildings do not cluster; they hang like isolated fruits, or root downward like inverted trees, connected only by thin aerial causeways of woven stem. The urban plan is legible—streets of compressed fiber, districts of spore-colored districts, a logic of settlement—but it is all grown rather than placed. This is an industrial town in an unearthly register: great pulsing mills of lignin and resin, mechanized harvesters that crawl on root-wheels through the living streets, engines that thrum not with combustion but with pressure, pumping translucent coolants through the vascular infrastructure. The tech is not metal-over-nature; it is wood and vine given gears, a factory-civilization that manufactures growth itself.

And it is loud. Not in chaos, but in the deep, rhythmic thunder of biological industry: the slow boom of resin being drawn through miles of hollow pipe, the clack of root-tread machinery, the constant rush of internal rivers moving moisture from one quadrant to another. The sound is low-frequency, architectural, carrying across the open spaces between structures like thunder in a canyon.

But reality here has long since settled into a strange final form. The erosion is not of rock—there is almost none—but of coherence. Boundaries blur. The plant-architecture behaves like memory; corridors shift subtly between mornings. In places, the ground becomes translucent, showing older, earlier layers of root-cities beneath the present ones, and in these thin spots the world reveals its layered age: primordially settled, an ecosystem that has reached its end-state and now merely circulates itself. Light is ordinary—clear, cool, somewhat flat—casting sharp shadows through the sparse canopy, yet colors sometimes arrive with wrong emotional weights: a shadow that feels wet, a bright vine that sounds like a bell. The weirdness is not violent but sedimentary: the town has become so old that its living parts have begun rehearsing events that have not happened, or remembering events that never did.

Fauna is nearly absent—rare flickers of small, translucent things that crawl through the ventilation pores of the buildings—so the only movement is the town’s own: vines creeping by inches, engines cycling, the damp air slowly turning. The climate never warms; it remains cool, stable, suspended. Dynamic processes continue—growth, harvest, circulation, decay into compost that becomes new wall—but they move at the pace of deep time, hurried only by the relentless, loud machinery that prevents the settlement from completing its long sleep. This is a place that has built itself into a plant-city, an industrial ecology without people, without animals, without sky—only the cool breath of root, the distant clank of wooden gears, and the intermittent drip of water falling through a world that has forgotten whether it is alive or merely being maintained.
