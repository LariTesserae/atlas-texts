The settlement is not on a hill but inside one—burrowed into the cool, weeping flank of a moss-locked foothill that never fully dries. The air is heavy, near-constant condensation beading on every surface, temperature pinned low enough that breath ghosts in brief white puffs. Water is near everywhere: it weeps in thin sheets from the rock ceiling, pools in shallow plastered basins carved into the floor, and fills the air with a soft, perpetual hiss of droplets falling onto broad-leafed canopies below. The place feels post-rain and always about to be rain again, though here the storm is internal, exhaled from living walls rather than fallen from sky.

Every wall, beam, and bridge is plant—not dead timber but living architecture. Thick, fibrous trunks have been coaxed into archways; vines braided by generations of hands form load-bearing ropes; roots have been trained downward into pillars by weights and constant moisture. Moss, lichen, and climbing ferns pack the gaps until there is no bare earth visible, only a saturated green substrate that serves simultaneously as soil, wall, and street. The vegetation is total, all-living-mass: rooms are hollows inside root-masses, staircases are spirals of compressed fern, and light filters through layered canopies in shifting green patterns. The settlement is small—cathedral-scale at best, a hamlet of grottos and chambers arranged in a single dense cluster—yet inside it feels boundless because the plants have filled every cubic handspan with presence.

The sound is loud: water, constantly, in rivulets and drips and the deeper thrum of it moving through hollow root-pipes; but louder still is the fauna. Insects are overwhelming—swarms of beetles with dark, polished carapaces crawling over every beam in constant motion; moths that do not sleep but batter themselves against wet lantern-glow in thick, fuzzy clouds; millipedes threading through the moss like living stitches; and throat-deep frogs that bellow from hidden pools in rhythms that never quite align, creating a turbulent, layered thunder. Small burrowers rustle through the root-floors. The fauna is not wildlife at a distance; it is substrate, architecture, constant co-inhabitant. You do not walk past them; you walk through them, your feet shifting through living strata.

The people here—few, weathered—use primitive craft: bone needles, wooden mallets, woven-cord tools, simple iron hooks hammered flat. They have no metal to reshape the world, only hands and patience, guiding roots into new arches, harvesting moss for plaster, weaving baskets from the same vines that hold up their roofs. The settlement is urban in its density—layered construction, organized pathways, a town built with intent—yet every piece of infrastructure is cooperative growth. Roads are compressed fern-beds worn smooth by bare feet; walls are trained hedges; the central square is a shallow pool surrounded by terraced moss, where water collects and frogs chorus loudest.

Everything here is in its own settled state, long-existed. The erosion is not ruin but balance—water has found its channels and wears them smooth without breaking them; roots have settled into the shapes they were guided toward and now maintain themselves; the rock behind it all is ancient, rounded, weeping but whole. The turbulence is not destruction but hyperactive life: constant small collapse and regeneration, vines snapping and being tied, pools filling and draining through root-siphons, insects swarming in shifting black clouds, frogs appearing and submerging, the whole system in anthill-energy—organized chaos, creation and consumption in the same instant.

Light is normal—filtered, green-gold, coming through gaps in the canopy or through translucent membranes stretched across high openings. There is no sun-burn here, only luminous humidity. The scale remains intimate. You could cross the whole settlement in minutes, but every meter is so densely saturated—water, plant, insect, sound, cool breath—that it takes hours. Boundaries exist: the settlement ends where the trained roots thin and the wild marsh-edge takes over, where the weeping rock becomes unclimbable and the frogs’ song fades into simpler rain. Within those edges, however, it is overwhelming, loud, cool, and entirely alive—no dead matter, only different speeds of living.
