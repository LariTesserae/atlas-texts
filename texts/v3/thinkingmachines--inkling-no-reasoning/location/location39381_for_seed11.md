Above the tree line but far below the sky, there is a valley that erosion forgot to finish.

The elevation is extreme—2.6 on the scale of things—yet the altitude is not sharp. These are old mountains, worn to their final argument with gravity, rounded to the softness of bone. The air up here is thin and warm (1.6), carrying moisture not as weather but as a chronic condition: condensation pearls on every moss blade at dawn, intermittent rain falls in vertical needles that vanish into cracks before touching ground, and riverine pockets—small, black, unmoving—lie hidden in the root-bowls of collapsed ridges. Water is present at 1.2, never dominant, always arriving just late enough to keep things from desiccating entirely.

The vegetation is lush (2.3) in a way that feels excessive, almost apologetic. Ferns larger than carriages unfold from fissures in the limestone. Moss the color of deep copper covers every vertical face, making the rock appear upholstered, overgrown, embarrassed by its own hardness. Trees grow horizontally out of sheer faces, their trunks twisted into ropes, roots dangling in the air like the frayed ends of old banners. The ecology here has reached a primordial settlement—erosion at 2.8 means this landscape is near-flattened not into monotony, but into an end-state of deep age. There are no young stones. Every pebble is rounded. Every slope has agreed upon its final angle.

But the openness is what first unmans you.

The scale extends (2.4) past the immediate into a regional emptiness that behaves like a bowl. You can see three valleys over, and every one is identical: green-black, hollow, softly thunderous. The density is 0.5—almost nothing per volume. The air is mostly absence. You stand in vast spaces between presences; there are no crowds, no herds, no cities visible on the horizon. Only scattered structures (0.6): a single lean-to made of black slate and vine, built into a crevice; a circle of hand-set stones where primitive tools (1.1) lie scattered—bone scrapers, stones lashed with hair, a broken wooden cup with moss growing inside it. The technology is pre-metal, hand-craft, manual-craft. Someone was here, or comes rarely, leaving only faint marks.

The fauna is rare (1.3): a bird that circles without crying, insects that move in single file across moss, the occasional flash of something scaled slipping between roots in the riverine darkness. But they do not dominate. Nothing dominates.

Except the sound.

The sound is 2.7—deafening—not constant, but absolute when it arrives. This is the turbulence (2.9). The valley is turbulent in a Solaris-like state of hyperactive creation and destruction. The mountains do not fall with drama; they settle with a low, continuous groan that fills your chest cavity. Underground rivers shift course suddenly, and when they do, the whole hollow sounds like a cathedral being torn slowly in half. The wind moves in chaotic, turbulent cells—updrafts that smash into downdrafts, creating a roar that is never the same twice, an anthill-energy of air and stone. The deafening quality is not noise for noise’s sake; it is the sound of the landscape still arguing with itself, ancient but not dead, in constant turbulent negotiation with its own final form.

The weirdness is 1.5—present, but not theatrical. It lives in the strangeness of the moss, which glows faintly at the roots and smells of iron. It lives in the way the scattered artifacts never rust, never rot, but slowly become moss-covered themselves, as if the valley is digesting them back into the primitive. It lives in the normal light (1.6), which is flat and warm and reveals everything without shadow, making the extreme height and lush overgrowth feel like they exist in a hall without walls.

The rules here are simple and severe: nothing is young. Nothing is still. The water comes intermittently, the vegetation takes everything that stands still, the erosion has settled into a primordial end-state that still refuses full death, and the emptiness is so complete that your own presence feels like an intrusion of density into a near-void. The structures remain scattered, partial, occasional—evidence that even primitive life barely anchors here.

You do not visit this valley. You survive it, briefly, in one of the pockets where condensation collects on flat stone, and you drink, and then you move on before the sound shifts and the turbulence makes its next decision about where you belong.
