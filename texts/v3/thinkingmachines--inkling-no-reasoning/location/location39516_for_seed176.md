The bowl is no more than half a kilometer across, cupped in the sharp, unweathered lip of a crater that has not yet learned to erode. Its walls are fresh black basalt, shattered and steep, recently exhaled from some young vent, the edges still hot to the touch in places where geothermal breath escapes through hairline fractures. Everywhere the rock weeps: water condenses in the warm air and runs in silver threads down the dark faces, pooling in a shallow marsh that fills the floor. The temperature hangs thick and warm, almost tropical, an inversion held by the walls, and the vegetation responds with a furious, saturated density—giant ferns with fronds the size of doors, mosses that swallow sound, and lianas threading through every joint in the stone. The air is closer to liquid than gas.

This is a high place, three thousand meters or more, but the altitude expresses itself not in barrenness but in pressure and isolation. The world outside the rim is invisible. Within, the scale is intimate, a single enclosed room of green and black, dense with presence—every square meter layered in root, frond, condensation, and machinery.

The machinery is scattered but undeniable. A rust-chewed pump station squats at the southeastern marsh edge, its iron pipes—paint long gone, replaced by orange bloom—running over the moss to where they vanish into a seep. The engine is dead now, but its presence is industrial, mechanized, scaled for extraction though only one structure remains intact, with others reduced to a collapsed concrete pad half-swallowed by ferns, and a single steel ladder bolted to the wall that climbs to nowhere, ending in mist. Copper wires, thick as thumbs, droop from an overhead cable that runs across the crater’s mouth, still humming with low electricity that powers nothing visible, only a faint ozone smell near the pump.

Water is everywhere, near the surface, audible. The marsh is shallow but constant, fed by the weeping walls and by warm seeps bubbling up through black sand. The sound is loud and continuous: not thunder, but the constant white-noise of falling water, the industrial thrum of distant, hidden engines—perhaps elsewhere on the peak—the drip and plink of condensation on metal, and the occasional groan of cooling stone. The fauna is nearly absent. Once, an insect the size of a thumb lands on a frond and is still, lost in the green. No birds call. The life here is botanical, voracious, and silent.

The process is slow. The rock is young, sharp, not rounded. The plants grow with patience, their roots slowly working into cracks that have existed for perhaps only decades. The machinery rusts gradually, not collapsing but sinking, millimeter by millimeter, into the wet substrate. The marsh slowly claims the floor, its boundary creeping outward at a rate measured in years. Change is patient, weak in gradient, a long cycle of condensation, growth, corrosion, and stillness.

The light is normal—flat, diffuse, filtered through the perpetual warm mist that hovers at half-height—illuminating everything evenly, without shadow drama, leaving the colors saturated but not bright. The weirdness is slight, present in the inversion of expectation: an industrial scar inside a newborn world, electricity humming through dead pipes, a warm wet cathedral at the roof of the world where nothing should grow, yet everything does. The rules here are those of containment: what enters the bowl—rain, heat, wire, root—stays, circulates slowly, transforms without leaving. Nothing here escapes quickly. Not the water, not the sound, not the rust.
