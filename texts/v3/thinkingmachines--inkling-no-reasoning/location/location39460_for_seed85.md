The cavity is small—cathedral-scale, if a cathedral had been hollowed out of pressure rather than stone. You do not enter it; you are admitted, through a membrane of still, black water that hangs at an elevation high enough that the air—though there is no air—feels like absence itself. Here, water is not a surface but the only wall, a submerged chamber where every plane is liquid and every vertical is a column of suspended, unmoving rain that never reaches the ground.

The ground, when you find it, is young. Bare, mineral, recently emerged. There are no plants—no fungi, no moss, no hidden crawl—only smooth geomantic substrates, black basalt pooled in perfect curves, glass that formed not by heat but by the sudden refusal of physics to continue in the old direction. The features are sharp, fresh, untouched by time’s grinding; nothing has been worn round because nothing here has ever moved.

Across this sparse, cool interior—temperature held at a constant, numb coolness—lie the remnants of settlement-scale construction. Not ruins exactly, but an urban skeleton rendered in post-modern technology: lattices that hold themselves together without tension, corridors that organize emptiness, layered infrastructure that hums in frequencies beneath hearing. They are arranged with the density of a town that has been emptied of its inhabitants long before anyone arrived. Much space lies between these structures, wide basins of dark water, so the presence is scattered, low-saturated, balanced with absence.

The darkness is near-total—light scaled to 0.4—yet visibility comes from other sources: faint bioluminescence embedded in the mineral walls, not living, more like stored decision; and the ghost-gleam of the tech itself, a soft, cyan breath exhaled by machines that no longer need to run in sequence. They maintain equilibrium. This is the rule of the world: dynamic at 0.2, never changing. Time is invariant; there are no gradients, no weather, no erosion beyond that first moment of birth. The water does not flow. The rain does not fall.

Sound, in this stillness, is mostly quiet—0.8—reduced to the occasional, distant tonal chime of pressure balancing itself, or the near-silent click of a nanoscale repair occurring in the architecture. Rare fauna—scattered, almost theoretical—drift in the high, liquid darkness: translucent shapes that may be organisms or may be instruments that have forgotten their purpose, too few to form an ecology, too strange to be counted.

The weirdness is constant but not violent. Space curves gently wrong; distances are organized like city blocks but travel like ocean depths. The technology operates beyond modern comprehension—engineering that does not borrow from physics so much as negotiate with it, maintaining this pocket of high-altitude, submerged stasis against the collapse that should have followed.

It is a place that holds contradiction without resolving it: urban but empty, submerged but not wet in the expected way, alive with machinery but dead to change. A small, high, cool chamber of liquid darkness, young and sharp and permanently still, where a post-human settlement floats in perpetual, silent equilibrium, and nothing will ever grow, fall, or age.
