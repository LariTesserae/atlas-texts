The plateau breathes in cool, bright absence.

No water arrives here—never has in any form living things call drink. No rivers, no clouds that carry weight, no dew that beads and falls. The dryness is not lack but substrate: the air holds only mineral haze, ionized and sharp, and the chemistry that moves through the world does so without solvent. What lives, lives dry, or lives on what dry things exude.

Yet vegetation is total—not as tree or blade, but as the ground itself made living. A continent-spanning mat, high and flat, covers every visible slope and hollow. The plants are not rooted so much as fused; they form the architecture of the earth, a thick woven hide of fibrous, translucent tissue stretched across the plateau’s bone. Where erosion has worked—long and patient, 2.2—there are valleys worn smooth, canyons where the living mat hangs in torn, weathered curtains, and ridges that have settled into their own eternal shape. The terrain is not built; it is grown, worn, and balanced, untouched by hand or artifact.

Above it, the scale defies closing the eye. From one rim of the plateau, the other is lost in the curve, and the living carpet continues without boundary. But for all this total green, density is sparse—much space breathes between presences. The vegetation gathers in continent-thick islands separated by channels of bare rock, by salt-crusted fissures, by stretches of exposed mineral plain that gleam white under the relentless, bright light. In those gaps, the emptiness is as present as the growth; the world is a scattered archipelago of life in an ocean of high, cool air.

That air is loud.

Sound here is 2.8—deafening—not in the way thunder strikes and fades, but in constant, layered roar. The root-structures, thick as buildings, carry vibrations upward from deep fractures where industrial mechanisms turn. Engines. Not artifacts placed upon the land—there is nothing built, no factory wall, no tower—but machinery embedded in the geology itself, scaled and mechanized, producing at an industrial rhythm that has no factory face. Turbines spin in dark chasms beneath the plant-mass; gears mesh in the hollow spaces between root-bundles; electricity runs in dry arcs through mineral veins, powering processes that have no visible product. The sound is the world’s primary weather—mechanical, perpetual, chaotic—not storm of rain but storm of force.

And indeed, the world is turbulent, dynamic at 2.9, in constant creation and dissolution. The green substrate does not sit still. It pulses, shifts, tears and knits in response to the vibration below. Sections of the living crust collapse into caverns, then regenerate elsewhere with hyperactive speed, feeding on ionized light and dry mineral. Fauna—abundant at 1.6—move through this chaos: winged things without names, translucent and sparse as the vegetation itself, navigating the gaps, feeding on the exudates of the plant-mass, falling into the churning gaps and being replaced in endless cycle. There is death and immediate replacement; the ecology is a bazaar of exchange without water, a hyperactive anthill of dry chemistry.

The temperature holds cool—1.3—never warming, never freezing. A chill persists in the bright, hard illumination, which strikes without softness across the vastness. Shadows are sharp but brief; the light dominates, bleaching color from the fibrous plants until they read as silver-green, bone-white, translucent.

Weirdness is present but settled—1.3—unusual not because it shatters expectation suddenly, but because it has long since replaced expectation. The absence of water is not noticed; the presence of industrial heart without built form is not questioned; the sound is not heard as noise but as the voice of the place. The world is in its own state, weathered in its own logic, dynamic without being unknown to itself.

Here, high in the dry, cool, deafening brightness, life fills every available surface but leaves the spaces between untouched, and machinery drives the rhythm of the green, and everything moves—turbulent, bright, vast, and utterly without thirst.
