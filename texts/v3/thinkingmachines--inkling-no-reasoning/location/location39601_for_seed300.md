The hollow is small—no cathedral, no valley, just the cupped depression of a single drained watercourse, low enough that the cool air pools and never quite dries. Elevation is nothing here; you are barely above the water table. And water is everywhere: not rivers, but presence—seeps through every fissure, beads on rusted iron in the air, pools in footprints that do not evaporate, hangs in a cool, weeping mist that makes the world feel recently rained upon and permanently unready. The ground is peat-dark, spongy, black-brown, saturated to the point that walking sounds like pressing into a soaked sponge.

Erosion is the only ancient authority present. The hollow has been carved into its own settled form—not dramatic canyons, but smoothly worn, long-rounded lips of earth, channels that have forgotten how to be sharp, rocks smoothed to pebble-fatigue, surfaces that feel weathered into balance. It is in-its-own-state, old in the specific manner of machinery that has been rusting slowly enough to become geology.

Above and within, vegetation is sparse—stunted reeds, black moss in the cracks, a few tufts of sedge that grow not toward light but away from the machinery, bristling like eyelashes around a mechanical eye. This is not wilderness. The density belongs to the built and the industrial, layered tightly into every available space. Scattered through the hollow are the remains and workings of a single, dense complex: not grand architecture, but small structures, partial remains, occasional artifacts—a collapsed gantry here, a half-buried pump housing there, a lattice of pipes no thicker than a wrist running in parallel lines like ribs.

The technology is mechanized, scaled, electrified—but miniature, intimate. Small engines sit in clusters, not one factory but dozens of micro-engines, their flywheels barely larger than dinner plates, connected by belts and gears in a lattice so dense that to move through the hollow is to navigate between touching, layered presences. They are not silent.

The silence is absolute except for the water and the machinery. The air holds the quiet of near-silence—no wind, no animal, no human voice—broken only by the low, constant turbulence of operation. The dynamic is chaotic, hyperactive, storm-state: these small engines do not run steadily but in stutters, surges, and reversals; belts snap and re-knot themselves in wet loops; gears grind and release; the entire hollow vibrates with anthill-energy, a bazaar of motion without vendor or buyer. Parts turn, seize, release, turn again—Solaris-like in its creation and destruction, manufacturing nothing visible, consuming and rearranging the rust and moss in continuous, turbulent cycles.

Light is normal—gray-green, diffused through the constant low mist, neither bright nor shadowed, just present, enough to see the rust and the water beads.

Fauna is rare; perhaps a single insect caught in a gear, or nothing. The hollow is occupied only by water, rust, and motion.

Weirdness is present but not flamboyant—just the wrongness of constant mechanized turbulence in a place too small, too wet, and too old to need it. The rules here seem settled: water must enter, iron must rust, gears must turn, and nothing must complete itself. The hollow is an area with boundaries—defined by its own worn lips, dense with near-touching industrial fragments, scattered with artifacts that are also processes, cool and wet and quietly, relentlessly turbulent in its small, silent scale.
