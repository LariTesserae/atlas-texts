# The People of the Condensory

The inhabitants of The Condensory are slender, almost translucent beings whose lineage is impossible to trace. They call themselves the Condensori, though they rarely need to name themselves at all—outside visitors are so uncommon that self-identification becomes unnecessary among those who know nothing else.

Their skin has a faintly bluish undertone, thin enough that in certain lights, one can trace the branching patterns of veins like intricate map-work beneath the surface. They are not tall—few exceed five and a half feet—but their proportions give them an elongated appearance: narrow shoulders, tapering fingers that possess an extra joint, and slightly oversized eyes adapted for the perpetual twilight. These eyes are their most striking feature—dark pupils surrounded by irises the color of mountain mist, with a subtle reflective quality that makes them gleam in moments of darkness.

The Condensori move with deliberate, fluid economy. Their gaits are precise, bodies instinctively avoiding collisions in narrow passageways without conscious thought. A lifetime navigating these confined spaces has bred a population that never wastes motion—they pivot rather than turn, slide rather than step, and can flatten their bodies against walls to let others pass with a practiced grace that appears choreographed.

Their speech is soft, often just above a whisper. The acoustics of The Condensory—with its countless surfaces to absorb and reflect sound—make louder communication unnecessary. Their language contains an unusual abundance of liquid consonants and elongated vowels that mimic the constant music of dripping water. When multiple Condensori speak together, their conversations blend with the ambient sounds of the settlement until it becomes difficult to distinguish human voices from the environment's own utterances.

Each Condensori bears intricate markings across their skin—not tattoos, but natural pigmentation that develops from adolescence onward. These patterns, resembling frost crystals or river tributaries, are unique to each individual but share familial similarities. The elders possess the most elaborate markings, some covering their entire bodies in whorls and delicate branches that seem to shift slightly when caught in peripheral vision.

The Condensori's relationship with their unusual home transcends mere habitation. They function as both caretakers and components of The Condensory's living system. Their daily routines involve constant maintenance: adjusting collection funnels, tending to the moss gardens that filter their water, calibrating the tiny machines. But there is something symbiotic in these activities—they appear less like work and more like mutual grooming between settlement and inhabitant.

Their diet consists primarily of carefully cultivated fungi that grow in special chambers deep within the settlement, supplemented by nutrient-rich mosses and occasional transparent fish from the small pools where excess water collects. They consume very little solid food; instead, they prefer to drink various distillations and infusions made from their harvested water, often enhanced with mineral extracts from the living stone. These concoctions—some medicinal, some purely for pleasure—are served in tiny copper cups that must be drained in a single, continuous sip according to tradition.

Children are rare and precious among the Condensori. They develop slowly, remaining infantile for nearly a decade before a sudden acceleration into adolescence. During their early years, they are kept in special nursery chambers where the mist is thickest, absorbing minerals directly through their skin. These children often display an unnerving stillness, observing their surroundings with ancient eyes.

The most curious aspect of the Condensori is their collective memory—or rather, the gaps within it. No individual recalls a time before The Condensory, yet they possess intricate knowledge of maintenance techniques that suggest centuries of accumulated wisdom. When pressed about their origins, they offer contradictory accounts: some believe they emerged from the stone itself; others suggest they were once ordinary mountain dwellers who gradually adapted; still others speak of "arriving from elsewhere" with no specificity as to where or when.

The eldest Condensori, whose age is impossible to determine with certainty, serve as "Resonators"—keepers of whatever fragmented history exists. These elders spend much of their time in meditative communion with the mountain, pressing their hands or foreheads against raw stone surfaces for hours. After these sessions, they sometimes share cryptic insights about necessary changes to the settlement's configuration or warn of upcoming shifts in the mountain's growth patterns.

At irregular intervals, the entire community participates in a ceremony they call "The Reconciliation," during which they gather in the rare open spaces and synchronize their breathing with the mountain's deep rumbling. During these events, the condensation intensifies, the luminous flowers bloom out of season, and the Condensori enter a trancelike state where they move in perfect unison, reconfiguring aspects of their home with unconscious precision. When they emerge from this collective state, none can fully articulate what occurred, yet The Condensory has invariably changed in subtle but significant ways.

The Condensori possess no concept of leaving their home. The few who have ventured beyond the perpetual mist returned changed—their skin losing its translucence, their movements becoming awkward and ungainly. Within days, these explorers developed respiratory difficulties and an intense physical longing for the settlement's unique atmosphere. The Condensori believe they have evolved to exist solely within this peculiar ecosystem—or perhaps, as some quietly suggest, they were designed for it.

Most unsettling is how the Condensori occasionally refer to themselves not as the mountain's inhabitants but as its thoughts—temporary concentrations of consciousness that, like the mist they harvest, might someday disperse and reform as something else entirely.
