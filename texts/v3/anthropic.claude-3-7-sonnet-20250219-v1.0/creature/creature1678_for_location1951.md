# The People of Mist Haven

The inhabitants of this plateau settlement call themselves the Aural People, though others in distant lowlands know them as the Mistkeepers. They are slightly shorter than their valley-dwelling cousins, with compact, sturdy frames built for the thin air and cool temperatures of their high home. Their skin has a faint bluish undertone that seems to shift with the mist, sometimes appearing almost luminescent in the early morning fog.

The most striking feature of the Aural People is their ears—not dramatically larger than other humans', but architecturally different, with complex inner ridges and a remarkable ability to rotate slightly toward sounds without moving their heads. These adaptations developed over centuries to distinguish the countless bird calls that fill their world. Children are born with the basic structure, but their ear cartilage continues to form and specialize throughout adolescence, shaped partly by which bird languages they focus on learning.

Their eyes, too, show adaptation—irises of unusual colors ranging from pale silver to deep indigo, with pupils that adjust rapidly to the shifting mists. Many have developed a nictitating membrane that protects against the intense high-altitude sunlight when the mists clear. Their movements are deliberate and efficient, conserving energy in the thin air, yet they can burst into surprising speed when necessary.

The Aural People organize themselves not by family lineage but by "chorus affiliations"—groups who specialize in communicating with and caring for different bird species. A person typically belongs to two or three choruses throughout their life, sometimes changing affiliations as new bird species arrive or as their own aptitudes develop. Each chorus has its elder—the one who can interpret the most nuanced communications from their avian partners—but leadership flows between choruses depending on seasonal needs and bird migrations.

Their language reflects their environment—a tonal speech with clicking consonants and trilled vowels that can carry clearly through mist. Most conversations incorporate bird-like calls that convey emotional context, and important community discussions often involve "speaking in full voice," a practice where human speech and bird-mimicry merge into something neither fully human nor avian. Children begin learning these calls before they can walk, carried on their parents' backs, facing outward to observe the birds.

The Aural People practice no agriculture beyond their small herb and root vegetable gardens. Instead, they are sophisticated water harvesters. Their diet centers around specialized algae they cultivate in the countless pools, supplemented by nutrient-dense lichens, wild mountain grains collected by trained birds, and protein from eggs (taken according to strict protocols) and fish raised in the deeper pools. During certain festivals, they practice what they call "mist-drinking"—catching and consuming the nutrient-rich condensation that forms on specially designed structures during heavy fog periods.

They mark time not by sun or moon but by bird migrations. Their year contains seven seasons, each named for the dominant avian behavior: Nesting, Fledging, First Flight, High Soaring, Gathering, Departing, and Waiting. The Waiting season—when many migratory species have left—is considered the most spiritually significant, a time of contemplation when the plateau feels emptier and the people turn inward.

The mysterious geological activity beneath them has shaped their spiritual beliefs. They understand themselves as living on the back of a "world still being born." Their creation stories tell of emerging from the stone itself, awakened by bird song. They practice a form of divination based on interpreting the patterns of new springs as they emerge, believing these reveal the plateau's intentions.

Their most sacred ritual involves what they call "the listening." At specific times, determined by subtle geological vibrations that only their most sensitive elders can detect, the entire community enters special chambers carved into the living stone. There they press their ears against the rock for hours, occasionally tapping responses with specialized stone hammers. They believe they are communicating with the consciousness of the plateau itself, learning where new springs will emerge and where they should build next.

Each person bears two names—a "sound name" that mimics a bird call, used in daily life, and a "stone name" received during adolescence after their first deep listening experience. Stone names are rarely spoken aloud except during ceremonies, and each is believed to connect the individual directly to a specific part of the living plateau.

Despite their isolation, the Aural People maintain communication with similar settlements on distant plateaus through a sophisticated relay system using their avian partners. Specific birds are trained from hatching to carry small message capsules, and their handlers can direct them to particular destinations using specialized call sequences. This network connects the constellation of plateau settlements into a loose confederation that shares knowledge about geological changes and bird migration patterns.

What appears to outsiders as primitive technology masks a profound understanding of sound. The Aural People have developed acoustic tools that can detect minute changes in bird calls (indicating approaching weather or predators), amplify the subtle sounds of water moving through stone (revealing new spring formation), and even—it is rumored—communicate across vast distances using the reflective properties of the mist itself.

As the plateau continues its slow emergence, so too do the Aural People evolve. Each generation develops slightly more specialized hearing, more efficient respiration for the thin air, and deeper intuitive connection to their avian partners. They see themselves not as masters of their environment but as part of its ongoing creation—a species being born alongside their still-forming homeland, learning to hear the voice of a world coming into being.
