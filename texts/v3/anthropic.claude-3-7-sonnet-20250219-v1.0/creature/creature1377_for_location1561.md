# The People of Sahl Nahar

The Nahari are a people sculpted by stone and water, just as their city has been. Shorter than lowlanders but remarkably strong, their bodies have adapted over generations to the thin air and vertical terrain. Their skin holds a pale, almost bluish undertone that strangers sometimes mistake for cold, though it's actually an adaptation that helps them absorb what little warmth the sun provides at this altitude.

Most striking are their eyes—large, deep-set, and ranging from pale gray to an unusual shade of aquamarine that seems to capture the glow of their sacred waters. These eyes see perfectly in the dim light of narrow passageways and can detect the subtlest movements across great distances, a skill developed from generations of watching for weather changes across the mountain faces.

The Nahari speak in a distinctive cadence, their language flowing like their waterways—sometimes rushing and bubbling with enthusiasm, sometimes measured and deliberate. They've developed a complex system of whistles that carry between the tiers, allowing messages to travel up and down the city faster than a person could climb. Children learn these whistles before they can properly speak, their small fingers held to their lips by patient elders.

Hair is a point of pride and practicality among the Nahari. Both men and women grow it long and thick, typically in shades of deep brown to black, though about one in twenty is born with hair as white as the mountain frost. This hair is never cut before adulthood but is instead elaborately braided and coiled atop the head, creating natural insulation against the cold. The most respected elders often have braids so long they must be wound multiple times around the crown.

Their hands tell stories of their lives—strong, nimble fingers perpetually stained blue-gray from the mineral-rich waters. Even the wealthiest Nahari bear these marks; they're considered signs of connection to the city's lifeblood rather than indications of status. From childhood, they develop calluses in distinctive patterns from gripping the smooth stone handholds carved into stairways and walls.

Society is organized around water stewardship. The most honored profession is that of the "channel-keepers," who maintain the intricate flow systems, checking daily for blockages or degradation. These individuals can read the waters like others read books, detecting changes in mineral content or flow rate that might indicate problems within the mountain's hidden springs.

Nahari families typically cluster around particular pools, with three or four generations sharing interconnected homes. Children are raised communally, taught from their first steps to respect the precipitous edges and flowing waters that define their world. By age five, most can navigate the entire tier where they live without adult supervision; by ten, they know every staircase, shortcut, and acoustic anomaly in the city.

The elders hold particular reverence. Those who have witnessed eight decades or more of water flowing through the channels are believed to have absorbed the mountain's wisdom. These venerable figures often sit beside the largest pools, their fingers trailing in the sacred waters as they settle disputes or offer counsel. Their words are treated as extensions of the mountain's own voice.

Spirituality permeates daily life without formality. The Nahari practice no religion with temples or priests, yet they observe countless small rituals honoring the waters. Before taking their first drink each morning, they touch three fingers to their foreheads and then to the surface of the water, creating ripples that are said to carry dreams back to the mountain's heart. They believe the glowing salamanders are fragments of ancestral spirits, returned to guide and protect the living.

Artisans work primarily in stone and wool, creating objects of surprising delicacy. Stone carvers produce intricate relief sculptures depicting the flow patterns of water, while weavers incorporate patterns representing the city's tiers into thick, warm textiles. Music emerges from the environment itself—percussion instruments made from hollow stones, flutes carved from the bones of mountain eagles, and the haunting sounds produced by skilled musicians running wetted fingers along the rims of specially crafted ceramic vessels.

The Nahari mark time differently than other cultures. Rather than counting days, they observe the subtle changes in mineral content that occur seasonally in their waters. "Three flows from now" might mean when the water shifts from winter's heavy mineral load to the clearer springs of what lowlanders would call summer. Their histories are recorded in pattern-songs that describe the city not by events but by how the water has changed its course through generations.

Despite their isolation, the Nahari are not unwelcoming. They greet visitors with cautious hospitality, offering a ceremonial cup of their clearest spring water as both refreshment and test—how one drinks from this cup reveals much about character to their observant eyes. Those who gulp greedily or fail to acknowledge the gift properly might find themselves guided back down the mountain before nightfall. Those who sip respectfully, acknowledging the water's sacred nature, might be invited to sit beside a pool as the day's last light fades and the channels begin their mysterious, luminous dance through the darkness.

In their deepest beliefs, the Nahari hold that they do not merely inhabit Sahl Nahar—they are Sahl Nahar. Like the water flowing through stone, their lives are temporary channels for something ancient and enduring. When they die, their bodies are returned to the lowest pools, where mineral-rich waters gradually transform flesh to stone, incorporating them back into the mountain that has always been their true home.
