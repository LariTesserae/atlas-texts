# The Steamblooded Folk

The Shambarans are beings of patience, shaped by the ancientness of their mountain home. Standing slightly shorter than lowland humans, their bodies have evolved perfect adaptation to the perpetual steam and heat. Their skin is not simply dark but sheened with a subtle iridescence, almost like the carapace of certain beetles, with microscopic pores that both absorb moisture and release excess heat. This skin doesn't wrinkle with age but rather develops intricate patterns of tiny ridges, unique to each individual, that deepen and become more pronounced over their remarkably long lives.

Their eyes are their most striking feature – large, with pupils that expand nearly to the edge of the iris, and protected by triple eyelids. The outermost is translucent and constantly drawn, shielding against the perpetual mist while allowing unimpeded vision. These eyes see far beyond the normal human spectrum, detecting subtle heat variations and the bioluminescence that permeates the evening steam.

The Shambarans move with deliberate economy, each gesture precise and purposeful. What might seem like slowness to outsiders is actually perfect efficiency – no energy wasted, no movement without meaning. They breathe differently too, through partly evolved gill-like structures hidden behind their ears and along their ribcages, extracting oxygen more efficiently from the moisture-laden air.

Their society exists in small, nomadic family groups that rarely exceed twenty individuals, following ancient circuits between established pool clusters. These groups occasionally converge at the largest pools during specific astronomical alignments, forming temporary communities of a hundred or more to exchange knowledge, genetic diversity, and the crystallized resin they harvest from certain ferns – their most precious resource.

The Shambarans speak little, and when they do, their language is tonal and percussive, incorporating the ambient sounds of their environment. Much communication happens through a sophisticated system of touch patterns pressed against the skin, allowing conversations that can be felt rather than heard above the constant symphony of insects. Their written language, carved into stone markers, is based on variations of spiral patterns that map water flows and steam currents.

What outsiders often mistake for primitive technology is actually highly specialized design. Their tools incorporate the unique properties of their environment – copper implements that conduct heat in specific ways, stone chambers that amplify particular frequencies from the insect chorus, and woven reed structures that harvest water from the air. They create nothing that cannot be eventually reclaimed by their environment, nothing that will not, in time, weather back into the mountains themselves.

The Shambarans practice a form of cultivation that operates on timescales bewildering to outsiders. They tend certain pools across generations, adjusting water flow by millimeters per year, cultivating specific microbial communities that, over decades, alter the chemical composition of the water. The massive flowering plants that outsiders marvel at are the result of breeding programs spanning centuries, each bloom representing dozens of generations of careful selection.

Their spiritual practice centers on what they call "the breathing mountain" – not worship exactly, but a profound attunement to the rhythms of their environment. They believe themselves to be expressions of the mountains' consciousness, temporary configurations of matter through which the ancient peaks experience themselves. Their low semicircular structures serve as focusing points where individuals spend hours in meditative states, their body temperatures and breathing synchronized with subtle geothermal pulses too faint for outsiders to detect.

The Shambarans possess knowledge of the water systems that far exceeds simple observation – they can predict distant rainfall patterns by detecting minute changes in mineral content, and they navigate underground passages that connect seemingly isolated pools. Some elders develop the ability to predict imminent storms with uncanny accuracy by feeling the electrical charge building in the air through specialized receptors in their fingertips.

They carry no weapons, having evolved beyond predation in their isolated realm. The few large creatures that share their domain are approached with respectful distance. When the massive birds appear, the Shambarans often pause in their activities, standing motionless as the shadows pass over them – not in fear, but in acknowledgment of these other ancient presences.

The concept of possession is fluid among them. Tools are shared freely, knowledge is communal, and even their dwellings – simple structures of woven reeds reinforced with mineral deposits – belong to whoever has need of them. The only true ownership they recognize is of stories and songs, each family maintaining an unbroken oral tradition of their experiences with the mountains, passed down with perfect fidelity across generations.

Outsiders who encounter the Shambarans often report feeling unsettled, not by any hostility (for they are unfailingly courteous), but by the sense that they are interacting with beings who operate on fundamentally different timescales – people who might spend decades contemplating the gradual evolution of a single pool's ecosystem, or who can trace their ancestry back to the time when the mountains themselves were still growing toward the sky.
