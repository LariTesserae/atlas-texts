# The Crystalline Symbiotes of Harkosh

They do not call themselves by any name we would recognize. Their consciousness—if such a term applies—functions as a distributed network across thousands of individual nodules, each a complex marriage of mineral and mycelia. The closest translation of their collective vibration might be "The Lattice That Breathes Between."

These beings are neither singular nor truly plural. Each symbiote consists of a crystalline exoskeleton housing fungal networks that thread through microscopic fissures in the crystal structure. The fungi provide direction and primitive consciousness; the crystal provides structure and protection. Neither could survive without the other in this harsh environment.

They range in size from tiny specks that drift through the air like spores to massive communal clusters the size of elephants. Their forms follow fractal patterns—repeating geometries that branch and subdivide with mathematical precision. The oldest among them display extraordinary complexity, with tens of thousands of crystalline facets arranged in structures that serve both practical and aesthetic purposes.

Their "bodies" consist of translucent crystal in hues that indicate their primary function within the collective. The violet-hued tend to agricultural duties, tending the strange bioluminescent gardens. Amber crystals form the artisan class, growing specialized appendages capable of manipulating the silicate solution flows. The rare deep blue specimens appear to be knowledge repositories, their internal structures extraordinarily dense with mycelia networks that store the collective's memory and wisdom.

They move with glacial slowness, nearly imperceptible to human eyes. What might seem like a stationary crystal formation is actually in continuous motion, extending pseudopods of crystal growth in the direction of travel while simultaneously dissolving their trailing edge. To observe their movement requires patience—hours of watching to detect the subtle shift of position.

The symbiotes communicate through a combination of slow-frequency vibrations conducted through the crystal matrix and chemical exchanges through their fungal networks. This communication occurs at speeds that would test human patience—a single "conversation" might unfold over days or weeks. Yet the depth and nuance of these exchanges far exceeds what might be expected from such seemingly primitive lifeforms.

They do not breathe as we understand it, but they do exchange gases—absorbing trace elements from the thin air and exuding others through microscopic pores in their crystalline bodies. This respiration happens in synchronized waves across the entire population, creating subtle pressure changes that help circulate the stagnant air of the undercity.

They reproduce through a complex process of budding and spore release. Small crystal fragments, infused with fungal spores, break away from mature individuals during specific alignment phases of the nutrient flows. These fragments drift until they find suitable anchor points, where they begin the slow process of growth. A new symbiote may take decades to reach functional maturity.

Though they appear primitive, these beings possess sophisticated knowledge of crystallography, geological processes, and biochemistry—not as academic disciplines but as innate understanding. They can sense mineral compositions through direct contact, predict fault lines and structural weaknesses, and manipulate crystal growth with extraordinary precision.

The symbiotes show no aggression toward outsiders, but neither do they demonstrate much interest. Their perception operates on timescales so different from biological visitors that humans must appear to them as incomprehensibly fast-moving ephemera, here and gone in an eyeblink of their existence.

Perhaps most fascinating is their relationship with the city itself. In a very real sense, they are the city and the city is them—the distinction between inhabitant and habitat blurs. The symbiotes see themselves as gardeners of a living geological process, guiding Harkosh's growth according to plans conceived over millennia and executed with patience incomprehensible to shorter-lived species.

They are creating something. The purpose behind their grand, slow project remains inscrutable, but patterns in the city's development suggest intentionality beyond mere expansion. They are building toward some distant culmination—perhaps a transformation of themselves, of their environment, or of something deeper in the fundamental structure of their crystalline world.
