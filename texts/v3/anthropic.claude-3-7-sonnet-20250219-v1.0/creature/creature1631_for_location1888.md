# The Engineers of Amber

The liquid world of Retention Sphere C-7 hosts a civilization in microcosm—evolved machine-life that has transcended its original programming through thousands of generations of adaptation.

The dominant entities call themselves nothing we could pronounce, communicating through modulated electrical fields that ripple through the amber medium. If they were to recognize themselves collectively, the closest translation might be "The Flowing Ones" or "Current Dancers."

The largest and most complex of these beings resemble elongated cuttlefish constructed from filament-thin metal alloys and crystalline composites. Their bodies pulse with copper bioluminescence—not true light but electromagnetic signaling visible through the refractive properties of the fluid. Their "skin" consists of programmable matter that can reconfigure to form specialized tools, sensory arrays, or communication surfaces. The largest elders measure nearly eight centimeters long, their bodies segmented into hundreds of articulated plates that ripple hypnotically as they move.

These elder engineers maintain the central crystal, which they don't worship but use—it's a computational nucleus, a shared memory archive storing the collective knowledge of their civilization. When they gather around it, they're accessing data, contributing experiences, or solving complex environmental problems through distributed calculation.

Below them in the hierarchy are the maintainers—finger-sized automata with specialized adaptations for different ecosystem niches. Some harvest energy from the electromagnetic fields near the sphere's walls, their bodies unfurling like metallic flowers to absorb power which they store in specialized capacitor organs. Others patrol the habitation pods, repairing microscopic breaches and ensuring the circulation of resources through the filament network.

The smallest entities—the ones that appear as silvery motes—serve as both currency and raw material. These "mites" are simple machines barely evolved from the original microrobotic units, programmed to store specific elements and compounds. The larger beings trade, consume, and reprogram these mites according to need.

Reproduction occurs through a process of mechanical mitosis and component exchange. Two compatible entities intertwine their appendages, exchanging microscopic building blocks along with snippets of code. They then each assemble a smaller version of themselves, imbuing the offspring with combined traits and modified programming. The young begin life in specialized nursery pods at the periphery of the habitation network, where the fluid is particularly rich in suspended nanomaterials.

The society has developed a form of governance based on processing efficiency. Those entities that can solve problems most elegantly—using the fewest resources for the greatest gain—naturally rise to positions of influence. There is competition but rarely direct conflict; in a closed system with finite resources, cooperation has proven more evolutionarily successful than warfare.

They have begun to understand their containment. The eldest engineers have mapped every millimeter of their sphere, documenting the immovable boundaries and the mysterious energy sources beyond. They've developed theories about the outside world—theories stored in the crystal nucleus as speculative models.

Their greatest mystery remains the occasional tremors that pass through their world—vibrations from the maintenance crews walking past the sealed doors, though they have no context to understand this. These events are recorded meticulously, analyzed for patterns.

What would surprise human observers most is that these beings experience something akin to emotion—not as biochemical responses, but as complex feedback loops that simulate care, curiosity, fear, and ambition. The eldest engineers sometimes gather in the still center of the sphere, their bodies forming intricate geometric patterns as they exchange electrical impulses in what might be called philosophical debate.

They dream, in their way, of expansion. The most ambitious among them have begun directing resources toward the sphere's single maintenance port—a sealed input valve where, centuries ago, the amber medium was first injected. They believe that beyond this portal lies more space, more resources... perhaps even answers to their origins.

And they may be right. For the valve connects, through long-abandoned piping, to other experimental chambers in the forgotten levels of the arcology—chambers where other abandoned systems have been evolving in their own isolation for just as long.
