# The People of Menau

The Menauans are neither fully of the land nor entirely of the sea, but exist in the liminal space between. Their skin—ranging from deep olive to a pale, luminous amber—bears a subtle iridescence that becomes more pronounced when wet. This is not merely a physical trait but a biological adaptation; microscopic scales embedded in their dermis capture and reflect moisture, helping regulate their body temperature in the perpetual humidity.

They are not tall people, most standing just over five feet, with lean, sinuous builds that serve them well in both water and on the narrow terraces. Their fingers and toes are slightly elongated, with a thin webbing between that extends to the first knuckle—not enough for true aquatic propulsion, but sufficient to give them remarkable control when swimming. These digits end in nails that grow quickly and are ritually trimmed to points, useful for harvesting shellfish from the submerged ruins or collecting delicate algae that grows in the tidal zones.

Most striking are their eyes—large, with pupils that expand and contract not just in response to light, but also to water pressure. The irises contain flecks of bioluminescent cells that become more visible in darkness, especially underwater, giving them a ghostly, starlit appearance in the submerged sections of the city. These eyes, with a protective second lid like that of seals, can see clearly in both air and water, though they are particularly sensitive to the movements of current and tide.

Their hair grows thick and strong, naturally water-resistant due to specialized oil glands at the roots. Most Menauans wear it long, twisted into complex braids that tell stories of lineage and personal accomplishment. These braids are adorned with polished stones, carefully carved pieces of coral, and occasionally, the rare metal object recovered from the deepest parts of their sunken heritage. The most skilled divers—those who can reach the lowest, oldest terraces—wear distinctive silver-blue strands in their braids, achieved by steeping their hair in a dye made from a rare cephalopod ink.

Menauans age slowly, their lifespans stretching to nearly a century and a half. Their perception of time has adjusted accordingly; they make decisions with the patience of coral, viewing immediate problems through the lens of decades rather than days. Children remain under communal care for twenty years before being considered young adults, and full participation in governance doesn't come until their sixtieth year—when they are believed to have witnessed enough tidal cycles to understand the city's needs.

Their language reflects their dual nature, with distinct vocabularies for concepts above and below the waterline. They have sixteen different words for the transitions between water and air, each capturing a specific quality of this meeting—from the violent crash of storm waves against the upper terraces to the gentle morning evaporation that creates their signature mist. Their written language, carved into the limestone or painted with indelible dyes extracted from certain sea plants, uses flowing symbols that change meaning depending on whether they're read left-to-right or top-to-bottom, allowing for layered communication that can be understood differently depending on the reader's perspective.

The Menauans practice a form of communal governance centered around what they call the "Tidal Council"—nine elders who each represent not a geographical section of the city, but a different aspect of their relationship with water: precipitation, evaporation, submergence, preservation, filtration, current, consumption, reflection, and boundary. These councilors are not elected but recognized, sometimes spending decades as apprentices before the community acknowledges their embodiment of their aspect. They make no laws but instead offer interpretations of how the eternal principles of water might apply to current circumstances.

Menauans do not worship gods in the traditional sense, but they do revere what they call the "Three States"—the embodied principles of liquid, vapor, and preservation (their concept for ice, which they know only from ancient stories). Their spiritual practices focus on transitional moments: dawn and dusk, the highest and lowest tides, the seasonal shifts when rainfall patterns change. During these times, they gather on specific terraces to perform slow, deliberate movements that mimic the flow of water over stone, accompanied by haunting vocalizations that utilize the city's unique acoustic properties.

Their relationship with the submerged portions of their city is complex. They do not view the lower terraces as "lost" but rather transformed—now inhabited by their ancestors in both literal and spiritual forms. The dead are ceremonially returned to the lowest accessible level of the city, weighted with stones carved with their life stories, allowing their bodies to become part of the living coral that grows over the ancient structures. In this way, the Menauans believe, their ancestors continue to support and strengthen the foundations of their world.

The Menauans have developed extraordinary breath control, with the average adult able to remain underwater for up to seven minutes. The most skilled divers—revered as "memory keepers"—can stay submerged for nearly twelve minutes, allowing them to reach the deepest sections of the city. These individuals undergo intensive training from childhood, including specialized breathing techniques and mental disciplines that slow their metabolism. They serve as the living archive of Menau, memorizing the layouts of submerged buildings and the contents of chambers that have not seen air for centuries.

Perhaps most remarkable is their intuitive understanding of hydrology. Without formal scientific education, they have developed an intricate knowledge of water pressure, flow dynamics, and filtration that allows them to create systems of remarkable efficiency. Their greatest achievement is what they call the "breathing architecture"—buildings designed to capture and channel air pushed by water pressure through the submerged sections, creating pockets of breathable atmosphere in specific underwater chambers. These air pockets, refreshed with each tidal cycle, allow them to establish meditation spaces and meeting rooms within the otherwise inaccessible lower city.

The Menauans know they are not eternal. Their careful measurements of the rising waters tell them that eventually, even the highest terraces will succumb to the sea. Yet they face this reality without fear or desperate preservation efforts. Instead, they are already designing the next evolution of their society—floating platforms anchored to their submerged mountain, seeds of a future where Menau will no longer be a city of stone and water, but one of water and sky. They measure their success not by what they preserve, but by how gracefully they adapt to the inevitable change that water, in its wisdom, brings to all things.
