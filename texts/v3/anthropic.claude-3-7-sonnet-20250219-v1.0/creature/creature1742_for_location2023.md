# The Cultivators of the Stalk Reserve

The Stalk Reserve belongs primarily to the Hollow Folk—though they would never claim ownership of it, considering themselves merely its most privileged stewards. 

They stand roughly human height but with proportions subtly adjusted to this vertical world—longer limbs, narrower shoulders, fingers that extend into extra joints perfect for climbing and weaving. Their skin exhibits a faint chlorophyll tint that deepens with age, marked with patterns resembling the veined translucence of the giant stalks surrounding them. The oldest among them appear almost plantlike themselves, their hair growing in tendril-like formations that respond to light and moisture.

The Hollow Folk live within the largest bamboo-like structures, coaxing the plants to form chambers through techniques passed down across generations. They don't cut or harm the living towers; rather, they sing to them—literally. Their language contains tonal patterns that resonate with the plants' acoustic sensitivities, encouraging growth in specific directions. A family home might comprise five interconnected chambers within a single massive stalk, with window-like translucent sections precisely oriented to capture morning light.

Their society functions as a collection of acoustic communities. Those living within earshot of each other form tight-knit groups called "resonance families," not always connected by blood. Children learn from an early age to contribute to the Reserve's symphony, playing instruments carved from fallen plant material that produce tones specifically calibrated to nurture certain species. Their music isn't just art—it's cultivation, communication, and ecological maintenance all at once.

The Hollow Folk aren't alone. Throughout the Reserve live the Spore Tenders—smaller beings reaching only to a human's knee, with oversized eyes that can perceive spore patterns invisible to others. They build their homes in the bell-canopied plants, creating suspended villages connected by bridges of living vines. The Spore Tenders collect, catalog, and redistribute spores, maintaining the Reserve's diversity through deliberate pollination routes. They ride the "birds that move like fish," directing these flocks to carry specific spores to regions requiring genetic refreshment.

Between these two peoples exists not hierarchy but symbiosis. The Hollow Folk provide protection and stability; the Spore Tenders ensure renewal and adaptation. Neither could maintain the Reserve alone.

Less visible but equally essential are the Moisture Dancers—amphibious collectives that appear as individuals but function with hive-mind coordination. Each finger-sized Dancer contains specialized organs that can extract, purify, or redistribute water throughout the Reserve's ecosystem. During drought, they gather in the thousands to pull moisture from the air; during floods, they channel excess into underground reservoirs. They communicate through ripples sent across water surfaces, creating messages that can travel kilometers in minutes.

The architecture scattered throughout the Reserve represents collaboration between all three peoples—acoustic chambers designed by the Hollow Folk, living materials cultivated by Spore Tenders, water systems maintained by Moisture Dancers. These structures serve as council halls, seasonal celebration spaces, and repositories of communal knowledge.

None of these inhabitants consider themselves separate from the Reserve. Their bodies return to the living root mat when they die, their nutrients feeding the very plants that sheltered them. The Hollow Folk have a saying: "We are breathing the exhalations of our ancestors."

Perhaps most mysterious are the Beckoners—rarely glimpsed beings who inhabit the perpetually inaccessible regions of the Reserve. Theories about them vary: some believe they're the Reserve's original creators; others suggest they're evolved forms of the Reserve itself gaining sentience. They appear as elongated figures composed partly of light, partly of plant matter, visible mainly during bioluminescent waves. The Beckoners seem responsible for the Reserve's gradual expansion, appearing at the boundaries during certain astronomical alignments to sing the surrounding land into transformation.

All inhabitants share certain adaptive traits: exceptional hearing capable of distinguishing hundreds of tonal variations; respiratory systems that filter and even benefit from the Reserve's spore-laden air; and most importantly, patience—the ability to think in the timescale of plants rather than animals. The fastest among them move deliberately, the youngest learn stillness before walking, and even their most urgent communications unfold at a pace outsiders might find maddening.

They record history not in writing but in carefully cultivated plant arrangements, living libraries where the growth patterns of certain species encode information legible to those trained in biomemory arts. Their oldest records suggest the Reserve began as an experiment by ancestors who foresaw environmental collapse and developed this resilient ecosystem as response—though whether those ancestors were human, posthuman, or something else entirely remains unclear.

For all their differences, every being in the Reserve participates in the same cycle: listening, cultivating, transforming. Their highest celebration comes during full synchronization, when every plant in the Reserve briefly resonates at complementary frequencies, creating a harmony so profound it temporarily alters consciousness, allowing communion with something that might be the collective awareness of the Reserve itself.
