# Inhabitants of the Avian Citadel

They are not birds as we understand birds. They merely wear the shapes of flight as one might wear a cloak—convenient forms for beings that exist partially outside conventional dimensions.

The Architects are the largest, with wingspans that seem to vary between twelve and twenty feet depending on who observes them and when. Their feathers are geometric impossibilities—hexagonal plates that sometimes overlap, sometimes phase through each other, colored in ultraviolet and infrared that human eyes translate imperfectly into shimmering oil-slick iridescence. They never land. They have never landed. Their existence is perpetual suspension, and the oldest of them have been aloft since before the plateau itself was thrust upward from whatever world lies below. They communicate through the precise angling of their primary feathers, creating mathematical patterns that ripple through the air like equations given physical form.

The Weavers are small, no larger than sparrows, but they move in perfect unison—thousands acting as a single organism, creating living braids of motion through the air. Their bodies emit trails of crystalline dust that hardens instantly into the strange materials that form the city's foundations. They have no eyes, only smooth, reflective domes where eyes should be, mirroring everything but revealing nothing of their inner nature. When they work, they vibrate at frequencies that would shatter ordinary matter.

The Sentinels perch at precise intervals throughout the citadel, their bodies so motionless they might be mistaken for sculptures. Each stands exactly alike—three feet tall, with feathers of metallic rigidity arranged in patterns resembling circuit boards or ancient script. Their heads rotate in complete circles with mechanical precision, though no sound accompanies the movement. They watch everything, remember everything, yet communicate nothing. Some appear to have been in the same position for so long that crystal formations have begun to grow around their talons, merging them with the city itself.

The Chorus exists as much in sound as in physical form. Their bodies are translucent, organs and hollow bones visible through skin that seems more like thinly stretched glass than flesh. They are the source of the pure notes that occasionally ring through the citadel, though their beaks never open. When they gather, which is rare, the air between them becomes visible—strands of connection like filaments of light forming complex geometric patterns. After such gatherings, new structures invariably appear in the city, as if their harmonies have condensed into physical form.

The Wanderers are perhaps the most disturbing to human sensibilities. Unlike the others, they change form constantly, shifting between different avian shapes with liquid ease. A hawk becomes a hummingbird becomes a creature with too many wings and not enough eyes before settling briefly as something resembling an owl. They move erratically through the city, their paths seemingly random but eventually tracing perfect fractal patterns across vast distances. They are messengers, though what messages they carry and to whom remains unclear.

Below these recognizable castes exist countless others—birds that flicker in and out of visibility, birds that exist partially embedded in the crystal spires, birds whose bodies seem to bend around corners that aren't there. Some appear to be composed entirely of light given temporary substance, while others have a density and presence that makes the surrounding reality seem insubstantial by comparison.

None of these beings hatched from eggs. None will die as creatures do. They are not a species but a presence, a manifestation of something vast that has chosen to express itself through these myriad forms. Some scholars who have observed the citadel from a safe distance speculate that the birds are not inhabitants at all but cells in a larger organism—that the entire city, perhaps the entire plateau, is a single entity slowly building itself into some final configuration.

As for the massive egg at the center—it is not an egg at all, though that is how human perception interprets it. It is something older than eggs, older than birds, older perhaps than the concept of birth itself. It is a seed, a theorem, a question posed by the universe to itself. And the citadel is not protecting it—it is containing it. For whatever gestates within is not something being nurtured toward birth but something being held at bay, its emergence delayed until some cosmic condition is met.

The birds know this. It is why they build with such purpose and precision. It is why they never cease their labors. It is why they exist at all.

For when the dome finally fails—and it will, eventually, as all constructions must—what emerges will not be a beginning but an ending. And the birds, in all their impossible variety, are simply the universe's immune response to what waits inside.
