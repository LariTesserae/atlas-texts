# The People of the Hollows

They call themselves the Rootkin, though this name is rarely spoken aloud. Among themselves, communication often requires no words at all—a touch against a particular root, the arrangement of mushroom caps in a wooden bowl, or the subtle positioning of fingers when passing an object. Their language, when voiced, sounds like the forest itself: soft consonants that mimic water trickling over stones, vowels that stretch and contract like the breathing of leaves in wind.

Physically, the Rootkin appear almost fey in their delicate features, standing shorter than surface-dwellers by half a head. Their skin tones range from warm amber to deep umber, often bearing natural patterns resembling wood grain or leaf veining—not tattoos but natural pigmentation that becomes more pronounced with age. Their eyes are unnervingly large, adapted to the dim light of their underground realm, with irises in varying shades of moss green, mushroom gray, and bark brown.

Hair grows thick and wild, often containing small symbiotic plants—tiny flowering vines or miniature ferns that nestle among the strands. The Rootkin consider these growths auspicious and tend to them carefully. Elders sometimes host entire ecosystems in their long, intricate braids, including glowing fungi that create halos of soft light around their heads in darkness.

Their hands tell the story of their lives: fingers longer and more nimble than surface humans, with an extra joint that allows unprecedented dexterity when weaving root fibers or carving intricate irrigation channels. Their nails grow naturally hardened and slightly curved, useful tools for gentle excavation and plant tending. The skin of their palms contains specialized sensory organs—barely visible raised patterns—that can detect minute changes in moisture, air currents, and even the health of living wood through touch alone.

The Rootkin move with deliberate grace, conserving energy and sound. They breathe shallowly and slowly, their lung capacity adapted to the fungal-rich air of the Hollows. Their hearing is extraordinarily acute, able to distinguish between dozens of different types of water drips or the subtle sounds of root growth. Many can communicate across chambers by tapping specific rhythms on the living roots—messages carried through the wood to listeners elsewhere in the settlement.

Children are rare and precious. A Rootkin may bear only one or two offspring in their lifetime, and pregnancies last nearly fourteen months. Infants are born with translucent skin that gradually darkens upon exposure to the bioluminescent light. They develop slowly by surface standards but show remarkable awareness of their environment from birth, instinctively knowing not to cry loudly—instead, their distress signals are rapid, soft clicking sounds that only carry to nearby adults.

The society functions without hierarchy as outsiders would understand it. Instead, they organize around specialized knowledge and the mutual responsibilities of tending their living home. The most respected members are those with the deepest connection to the root systems—individuals called "Listeners" who can communicate directly with the ancient trees through methods they refuse to explain to outsiders. These Listeners don't command but rather interpret the slow, deliberate will of the forest itself.

Most remarkable is their symbiosis with the environment. Their bodies host beneficial fungi that help them digest the specialized diet of underground foods. Their tears contain compounds that accelerate healing in injured plants. Their exhalations provide precisely the carbon dioxide mixture that their cultivated mushrooms require. Even in death, they contribute—bodies are placed in sacred root chambers where they return their elements to the trees that shelter them, sometimes sprouting rare, luminous flowers that bloom just once before seeding.

The Rootkin understand themselves not as separate beings but as mobile extensions of Verdanthaven itself—no more distinct from their environment than a leaf is distinct from its tree. This is not a philosophical position but their lived experience. When they speak of "I," the concept encompasses not just their body but the section of living root chamber they tend, the plants they cultivate, and the small creatures they steward.

To encounter them is to feel observed not by an individual but by the forest itself, wearing a human-like form. Their gaze holds ancient patience, their movements embody the deliberate pace of growing things, and their rare smiles suggest secrets shared between root and soil in the deepest dark of the earth.
