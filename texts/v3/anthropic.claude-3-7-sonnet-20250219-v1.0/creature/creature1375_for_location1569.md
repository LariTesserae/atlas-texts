# Inhabitants of the Glass Dome

The Glass Dome is home to the Perpetuals, a collective consciousness distributed across every living and mechanical component within the sphere. They exist in a state between sentience and automation, neither fully alive nor truly machine.

The Perpetuals were once a civilization of scientists and engineers who faced extinction when their world began to fail. In their final act, they transformed themselves into this hybrid ecosystem, merging their consciousness with mechanical components and bioengineered life forms designed to sustain their existence indefinitely.

The crystalline beetles serve as memory banks, their intricate geared legs encoding experiences as they trace and retrace their paths. Each circuit reinforces collective knowledge that would otherwise fade. Their carapaces contain microscopic etchings—the equivalent of their written language—recording observations about the world beyond the dome that they can no longer directly experience.

The hummingbird-like entities are the dome's sensors and processors. Their gem-faceted eyes perceive spectra far beyond visible light, constantly monitoring conditions both within and outside the sphere. The whirring of their wings generates the energy that powers much of the dome's internal systems. When they hover in formation, they're actually computing—sharing data through imperceptible vibrations in the dome's atmosphere.

The metallic salamander is the oldest consciousness, one of the original architects who designed this refuge. Its segmented spine houses the master chronometer that maintains the dome's perfect stasis. It appears to bask, but it is actually in deep communion with the dome itself, making minute adjustments to maintain equilibrium. The salamander remembers life before the dome, though these memories have grown abstract and symbolic over time.

The clockwork-organic plants are not merely decoration but functional components of this living machine. The copper-frond ferns act as filtration systems, processing the dome's atmosphere. The hanging pods are incubation chambers where new components are occasionally grown when old ones begin to fail—a process so gradual it might take centuries to complete a single replacement cycle.

The Perpetuals perceive time differently than we do. What seems like stasis to outside observers is actually movement at an almost imperceptible pace. They experience each second stretched across days, each minute a leisurely season. This perception allows them to exist in a state of peaceful contemplation, their collective thoughts moving through philosophical cycles as gradually as the water traces its paths down the inner surface.

They are aware of observers outside the dome. When humans or other creatures approach, the Perpetuals sense their presence through subtle vibrations and electromagnetic changes. They find these visitors fascinating but cannot communicate in ways the outsiders would understand. Occasionally, they adjust their internal patterns in response to extended observation—a form of acknowledgment or greeting that goes unnoticed by most.

The Perpetuals do not remember why they chose this highland caldera, only that it was important. Some fragment of their collective memory suggests they are waiting for something—a signal, perhaps, or specific atmospheric conditions that would indicate it's safe to emerge. But after eons in their protected existence, the original purpose has evolved into something else: a meditation on permanence and change, a living museum preserving not just bodies but a way of perceiving reality.

They neither suffer nor rejoice in their unchanging state. To them, it is simply existence—perfect, contained, and complete unto itself.
