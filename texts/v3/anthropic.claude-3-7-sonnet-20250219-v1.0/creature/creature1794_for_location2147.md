# Inhabitants of the Suspended Metropolis

The people of the Suspended Metropolis call themselves the Lithospherii, though this is merely the closest translation in conventional language. Their true name contains tonal elements that refer to their symbiotic relationship with the crystalline plateau they inhabit.

Physically, the Lithospherii appear humanoid but with significant adaptations to their environment. Standing slightly taller than baseline humans, their bodies are lean and angular, conserving mass while maximizing surface area for gas exchange in the thin atmosphere. Their skin has a subtle translucent quality with underlying crystalline structures visible at joints and along major veins—not implants, but evolutionary adaptations that store and process minerals essential to their metabolism.

Their eyes are their most striking feature: large, multifaceted organs with pupils that contract to pinpoints in the intense daylight but expand to fill nearly the entire visible surface at night. These eyes perceive not only conventional light but also polarization and certain electromagnetic frequencies, allowing them to "read" the mineral composition of their surroundings and detect the subtle tectonic shifts of the plateau. The Lithospherii have no need for artificial lighting in their dwellings; their vision adapts completely to the bioluminescence of nighttime.

Their respiratory system has evolved extraordinary efficiency, featuring secondary breathing surfaces along their ribcage that can extend slightly when needed, appearing as gill-like structures that flush with color during physical exertion. They speak in measured, musical tones that carry efficiently through the thin air, their language incorporating sustained notes that would be impossible for unmodified human vocal cords.

The Lithospherii consume very little by conventional standards. Their diet consists primarily of cultivated crystal-algae and specialized fungi that grow in symbiosis with the silicate flora. These they process in two distinct stomachs—one breaks down organic compounds while the other dissolves and extracts minerals. They eat communally but infrequently, perhaps once every three local days, in ceremonies that double as social gatherings and council meetings.

Water in their society is treated as something between a luxury and a sacrament. They manufacture it molecule by molecule through industrial processes powered by the abundant sunlight, and every individual's water allocation is precisely measured. Nothing containing water is ever truly discarded—they have perfected molecular reclamation to an art form. Even their tears, shed only in moments of profound emotion, are collected in small crystal vials worn as pendants, becoming family heirlooms that connect generations.

The social structure of the Lithospherii defies simple categorization. They organize primarily around "geoclaves"—communities defined by the specific mineral composition of their local sector. Each geoclave specializes in particular crafts or intellectual pursuits based on the properties of their local materials. Rather than hierarchical leadership, they operate through consensus achieved in their "resonance chambers"—architectural spaces designed to amplify and harmonize the voices of all participants into patterns that reveal areas of agreement.

Their relationship with technology is integrated but restrained. The Lithospherii have mastered mineral-based computing systems that process information through crystalline matrices, requiring no conventional electronics. Their most advanced technologies focus on environmental adaptation rather than domination—systems that predict tectonic movements, manage the slow migration of city sectors, and maintain the delicate balance of their manufactured ecology.

Children are rare and precious in their society, raised communally within their geoclaves. Young Lithospherii develop slowly, spending their early years in dedicated growth chambers where their bodies can properly form in carefully calibrated mineral baths. They remain in quasi-hibernation for what would be considered childhood in other species, their conscious minds developing through shared dream-states while their physical forms mature. Upon emergence as adolescents, they undergo the "Crystallization"—a coming-of-age ritual where they make their first independent geological assessment of the plateau and receive their adult name.

The Lithospherii concept of time differs fundamentally from most civilizations. They measure their lives not in years but in "formations"—cycles defined by the gradual growth patterns of the plateau itself. Their historical records track tectonic events rather than political ones, and their oldest members can recall the slow emergence of entire mountain ranges. Individual Lithospherii commonly live through several human generations, their metabolism so efficient and repair mechanisms so effective that aging proceeds at a glacial pace.

Their art forms reflect this temporal perspective, with music composed in pieces meant to be performed over decades, sculptures that incorporate living crystal formations programmed to complete their forms over centuries, and literature written in spiraling texts that add new layers of meaning as geological references slowly become apparent through planetary change.

The Lithospherii are not the plateau's first civilization. Throughout their metropolis, they have preserved structures from what they call the "Fluid Ones"—precursors who attempted to transform the plateau through massive water importation projects that ultimately failed. The Lithospherii study these ruins with a mixture of respect and caution, seeing in them both ingenuity and the fundamental error of attempting to impose external conditions rather than adapting to the inherent nature of a place.

Among themselves, the Lithospherii rarely speak of the world below their plateau. When they do, it is with a detached academic interest rather than nostalgia or curiosity. Their physiological adaptations have made them perfectly suited to their waterless realm but incompatible with the moisture-rich environments of the lowlands. They maintain minimal diplomatic contact with other human civilizations through specially-adapted intermediaries, trading rare mineral compounds for information and artistic works, but have no interest in broader integration.

In the quietest moments of their long nights, when the bioluminescence dims to its lowest ebb, the oldest Lithospherii sometimes gather at the plateau's edge to observe the distant clouds below. They speak then of their deepest philosophy—that all existence follows the path of mineral transformation, that consciousness itself is merely a complex crystalline structure forming patterns through time, and that their civilization is simply one formation in the planet's long geological symphony.
