# The People of Ven Pahzi

The Pahziin move like the mist itself—deliberate, fluid, and nearly silent. Their bodies are tall and slender with elongated limbs that allow them to stretch across the terraces without disturbing the water's perfect stillness. Their skin has developed a distinctive iridescent quality that shifts between pale alabaster and a subtle blue-gray, an adaptation to the relentless light and perpetual moisture. This skin naturally exudes a thin protective oil that refracts the harsh sunlight, giving them a slight shimmer visible only when they move through the steam.

The most striking feature of the Pahziin are their eyes—large, with pupils that contract to mere pinpoints in the bright day but expand to fill nearly the entire iris during the dimmer evenings. The irises themselves are uniformly silver-white, evolved to filter the intense reflective glare from the pools. These eyes rarely blink, having developed a transparent second membrane that continuously moistens the surface without interrupting vision. When they do blink—a deliberate action rather than a reflex—it carries meaning: acknowledgment, agreement, or sometimes warning.

The Pahziin communicate primarily through a language of subtle gestures. Their long, six-fingered hands trace precise patterns in the air, each movement conveying complex ideas without disturbing the sacred silence of the terraces. When they must speak aloud, their voices emerge as soft whispers that seem to merge with the ambient hiss of evaporation. Their vocal language consists of extended vowel sounds punctuated by gentle aspirations, almost impossible for outsiders to distinguish from the natural sounds of Ven Pahzi itself.

Children are born rarely to the Pahziin, perhaps once every seven years across the entire settlement. These births are considered momentous occasions, and the children are raised communally, educated in the meticulous arts of water management and plant cultivation. Young Pahziin are distinguished by a faint luminescence to their skin that fades as they mature, believed to be a vestige of their ancestral connection to the stars from which local mythology claims they descended.

The elders of Ven Pahzi are known as the Stillkeepers. Distinguished by intricate patterns of mineral deposits that have bonded with their skin after decades of immersion in the pools, they spend most of their time partially submerged in specially designated terraces. There, they practice the discipline of perfect stillness—sometimes maintaining their positions for days without perceptible movement. The mineral patterns on their bodies are believed to record the history of the waters and the subtle changes in the terrace ecosystem.

Every Pahziin wears a personal water gauge—a small transparent vessel hung around the neck containing a precise amount of terrace water. The level in this vessel responds to subtle changes in atmospheric pressure, allowing them to anticipate shifts in the evaporation cycle hours before they occur. These gauges are crafted at birth and remain with the individual throughout their lifetime, gradually accumulating microscopic mineral patterns that are as unique as fingerprints.

Their diet consists primarily of the protein-rich seeds harvested from the bulbous water plants, supplemented by specialized algae cultivated in designated pools. They consume food only once daily, in a communal ritual that takes place as the light shifts to evening gold. This meal is eaten in complete silence, with each participant focused on the subtle flavors and the awareness of how these nutrients connect them to the terrace ecosystem.

The Pahziin practice no burial of their dead. When a member of the community reaches the end of their life—typically spanning up to two centuries—they enter a special meditation terrace where they submerge themselves completely. Their bodies dissolve over several weeks, returning their elements to the water system that sustained them. The mineral content of their bones becomes part of the ever-thickening borders of the terraces, literally building upon the foundations laid by their ancestors.

Visitors to Ven Pahzi are rare but accommodated with formal hospitality. The Pahziin provide specially designed breathing apparatus to help outsiders adjust to the mineral-rich steam, and protective eyewear to shield against the intense reflective light. These visits are strictly limited to the peripheral terraces and certain designated areas of the settlement. No outsider has ever been permitted to see the central pool—a perfectly circular terrace at the exact center of the complex that remains shrouded in particularly dense steam at all times.

The Pahziin measure time not by days or years but by cycles of mineral accretion in the terraces. Their historical records, kept on specially processed paper made from water plant fibers, document thousands of these cycles—suggesting that the settlement has existed far longer than any other human habitation in the region. These records are maintained by the Archivists, who apply mineral-based inks using brushes made from their own hair, the only writing implements considered pure enough for the task.

Though they appear to be engaged in endless routine—the maintenance of the terraces, the harvesting of plants, the operation of the water-powered machinery—the Pahziin understand their work as a form of continuous meditation. Each action is performed with complete presence, every movement an extension of their fundamental philosophy: that perfect balance is achieved not through stasis, but through the harmonious management of constant change.
