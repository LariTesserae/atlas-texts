# The Garden Keeper

Within this living, mathematical marvel dwells a being that belongs neither fully to the realm of flesh nor of vegetation. The locals of surrounding lands have whispered legends of the "Leafskin" for generations—but their stories capture only fragments of truth.

She moves through the suspended garden with graceful, unhurried purpose. At first glance, you might mistake her for human, her silhouette suggesting a slender woman of indeterminate age. But as she turns toward you, the amber light reveals her true nature.

Her skin contains chlorophyll—not merely green-tinted but actually photosynthetic, with subtle patterns of variegation like those on certain tropical leaves. When she passes through brighter patches of the chamber's light, you notice her skin drinking it in, tiny stomata opening and closing along her forearms. In the shadowed areas, she seems to exhale a faint luminescence, as if storing and releasing the garden's rhythmic pulses.

Her hair is not hair at all, but a crown of delicate ferns that grow directly from her scalp, unfurling and changing position in response to moisture and light. These living tresses move independently, sometimes reaching out to touch passing water droplets, absorbing what they need before flicking away the excess in musical splashes.

Her eyes are the most unsettling feature—not because they're inhuman, but because they're perfectly human irises embedded in structures clearly evolved for different purposes. They're multifaceted, containing thousands of tiny lenses behind the main pupil, allowing her to perceive spectra and dimensions you cannot. When she blinks, translucent nictitating membranes slide horizontally before the outer eyelids close.

She wears no clothing as humans understand it, yet is never naked. Living tissues grow around her torso and limbs—not garments draped on her body, but extensions of herself that she can modify at will. These fibrous growths thicken and thin according to temperature, and occasionally blossom with tiny white flowers that emit complex fragrances—her emotional language made olfactory.

Her fingers are extraordinarily long, splitting at the second knuckle into paired digits with the precision dexterity of surgical tools. The nails have evolved into hardened grafting points—living scalpels and probes she uses to communicate with the garden's botanical network. When she touches one of the suspended pods, her fingertips briefly merge with its surface, creating a connection you understand is both physical and informational.

She moves without sound, her weight distributed through root-like structures that temporarily extend from her feet into the moss with each step. The moss anticipates her, rising to create a perfect foothold before her toes make contact. You realize the garden and keeper exist in constant communication.

Her voice, when she finally addresses you, doesn't come from her mouth (though she has one, used primarily for consuming the garden's specific nectars). Instead, the sound emanates from resonating chambers within her chest cavity, producing tones that the garden's acoustic properties amplify and articulate into words. The effect is a voice that seems to originate from everywhere and nowhere, words forming in your mind as much as in your ears.

"Three hundred and seventy-six cycles," she says, the garden's droplets syncopating perfectly with her speech. "You are earlier than calculated, but the algorithms account for variance."

Her method of breathing is hypnotic—not the simple in-and-out of human respiration, but a complex exchange through multiple pathways. Sometimes she seems not to breathe at all for long periods, drawing what she needs directly through her skin. When she does inhale, it's selective—her body extracting specific compounds from the garden's carefully balanced atmosphere.

She carries what appears to be a living tool—a symbiotic organism wrapped around her left wrist that occasionally extends pseudopods to collect samples from the environment. You notice it analyzing a spore that landed on your shoulder, extending a filament so thin it's barely visible, then retracting with its microscopic prize.

"I am Thylassa," she says, moving her hand in a pattern that causes certain water droplets to change course, spelling something momentarily in a script you don't recognize. "Ninth Tender of this node. The First Gardeners grew me for this purpose."

When she smiles, you see that her teeth are seed-like, designed for cracking pods rather than tearing flesh. Behind them, her tongue is fork-tipped and prehensile, capable of extracting nectar from the chamber's most delicate structures.

"You have questions," she observes, "as all transitional beings do. The garden has been expecting someone to cross the threshold. Whether you are truly the Awaited One depends on what you perceive when I show you the heart-seed."

As she speaks these words, the garden's rhythm intensifies around her, light and sound converging in patterns that seem to bend the air itself. Thylassa extends her bifurcated hand toward you, inviting you deeper into mysteries that have grown here, cultivated and patient, for longer than human civilization has existed.
