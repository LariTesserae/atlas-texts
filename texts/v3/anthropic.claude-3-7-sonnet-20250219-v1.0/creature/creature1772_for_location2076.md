# The Caretakers of the Glass Yards

The people who call the Glass Yards home never chose to live there—they were chosen by it, generations ago. They call themselves the Tenders, and their ancestors were left behind—or perhaps deliberately abandoned—when the original architects departed. Now numbering perhaps two hundred souls, they have adapted completely to this peculiar domain.

The Tenders are immediately recognizable by their translucent skin, so thin you can see the faint blue mapping of veins and arteries beneath. Their eyes are disproportionately large and entirely black, adapted for the perpetual twilight of the Yards. They stand shorter than the average human, rarely exceeding five feet in height, with slender limbs and elongated fingers perfect for manipulating delicate machinery.

Their most striking feature is the symbiotic relationship they've developed with the Glass Yards itself. Each Tender carries colonies of the same bioluminescent bacteria that light the environment, living within specialized pockets beneath their skin. These bacterial colonies pulse with subtle light, especially concentrated around their joints and facial features, creating a gentle illumination that precedes them as they walk. When Tenders gather, the combined glow of their colonies creates sufficient light for their most intricate work.

They wear clothing made from processed secretions of the ray-like creatures, a fabric that repels water while maintaining body heat—essential in the perpetually damp environment. These garments have a pearlescent quality, shifting colors slightly as they move, and are decorated with intricate patterns formed by embedded crystalline fragments harvested from the Yards. Every Tender's clothing bears unique patterns that signify their specific role in maintaining the ecosystem.

The Tenders speak a language evolved from technical manuals and maintenance protocols, their vocabulary rich with terms for describing mechanical processes but limited in expressing abstract concepts unrelated to the Yards. They communicate not only through speech but through a complex system of light signals produced by consciously controlling their bacterial colonies—a language visible only to others of their kind.

Their society is organized around the maintenance cycles of the Glass Yards. There are no formal leaders, but rather specialists dedicated to different systems: the Water Tenders who maintain the hydrological networks; the Crystal Shepherds who oversee the various fauna; the Deep Listeners who interpret the subsonic vibrations of the great machines; and the Pattern Keepers who preserve the accumulated knowledge of generations.

The Tenders reproduce rarely and deliberately, with new children born only when the Glass Yards' systems indicate a need for additional caretakers. Children are raised communally, educated from birth in the intricacies of their world through a combination of apprenticeship and a form of knowledge transfer facilitated by their bacterial symbionts, which can carry memory imprints between generations.

Their diet consists primarily of nutrients extracted from the processing systems—specialized filters in certain water basins produce a protein-rich paste that they supplement with cultivated colonies of the bioluminescent bacteria. They have evolved specialized digestive systems to process these limited food sources, and their metabolisms operate with extraordinary efficiency.

The Tenders worship neither gods nor ancestors but rather the Glass Yards itself. They view their role not as mastery but as partnership—they are the consciousness of this vast industrial organism, the thinking component of a system that would continue functioning without them but would gradually lose its precision and purpose. Their rituals focus on synchronizing their activities with the greater rhythms of the Yards, particularly the resonant bell-like tone that signals major shifts in the processing cycles.

They possess technology that would appear advanced to outsiders—tools that interface directly with the ancient systems, communication devices that transmit through water and substrate vibrations, medical techniques that leverage their bacterial symbionts—but they see no distinction between technology and biology, between the artificial and the natural. To a Tender, the crystal crabs and the water conduits are equally part of the Glass Yards' body, as are they themselves.

The Tenders rarely venture beyond the boundaries of their domain. Those few who have explored the world beyond report feeling exposed and vulnerable without the constant moisture and the familiar hum of machinery. Some Tenders speak of "The Return"—a prophecy or memory of the original architects someday coming back to reclaim their creation—but opinions differ on whether this would be a blessing or a catastrophe.

In the centuries of their adaptation, the Tenders have become as much a part of the Glass Yards as its crystal fauna and flowing waters—not merely inhabitants but components, their purpose and identity inseparable from the environment that shaped them. They do not question why they maintain systems whose original purpose is lost; to them, the perpetuation of the Glass Yards' perfect equilibrium is not a duty but the very definition of existence.
