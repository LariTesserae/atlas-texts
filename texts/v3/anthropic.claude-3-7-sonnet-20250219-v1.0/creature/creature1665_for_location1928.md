# The Marshfolk

The people of the marshworks are neither fully part of the industrial operation nor entirely separate from it. They call themselves Seepers, and the name suits them—they seem to emerge gradually from the landscape rather than arrive with any suddenness.

Most obvious are the harvester crews, their bodies adapted to the perpetual damp and chill. Their skin has developed a waxy sheen that repels moisture, faintly iridescent in certain angles of light. These aren't workers who arrived from elsewhere; they're third, fourth, fifth generation marsh-born. Their fingers are slightly webbed—not dramatically so, but enough to be noticeable when they remove their thick work gloves. Their eyes have adapted too, with enlarged pupils that gather the diffuse light, giving them excellent vision in the perpetual half-gloom.

The Seepers have developed their own methods of communication across the vast marshlands. Their voices carry a distinctive harmonic undertone that travels remarkably well through the mist, a skill developed over generations. The sound resembles a melodic hum that rises and falls with their speech. When necessary, they can produce calls that carry for nearly a mile across the open marsh, bouncing off the water's surface in ways outsiders cannot replicate.

They live in clustered family settlements built on the few natural elevations, supplemented by structures raised on pilings of preserved marsh pine. These homes are curved rather than angular, with rounded roofs that shed the constant drizzle. Walls are constructed from a composite material—part harvested peat, part industrial byproduct, sealed with resins extracted from the waxy-leaved shrubs. The homes smell earthy and slightly medicinal, a scent outsiders find strange but not unpleasant.

Their social structure revolves around extended family units called "pools," each responsible for a section of the marshworks. Leadership passes not necessarily to the eldest but to whoever demonstrates the greatest sensitivity to the marsh's rhythms—the ability to predict weather shifts, locate the richest peat deposits, or sense when machinery needs adjustment before it actually fails. This sensitivity is considered a gift, sometimes manifesting in children as young as five, who are then apprenticed to the current pool-leader.

The Seepers maintain an uneasy relationship with the corporate owners of the processing plants. They understand their role as custodians of the marsh extends beyond simple resource extraction. Through generations of observation, they've developed sustainable harvesting patterns that allow sections of marsh to recover completely before being worked again. The corporations value their expertise but are perpetually frustrated by their refusal to increase production beyond what the marsh can bear.

Their diet is specialized to the environment—various marsh grasses yield starchy tubers when properly prepared; a species of small fish that inhabits the deeper channels provides protein; fermented cranberries produce both preservatives and a potent liquor that helps keep the cold at bay. They supplement these staples with cultivated fungi grown in the warm, humid spaces beneath their elevated homes.

The Seepers' religious practices blend animism with industrial reverence. They leave small offerings at the bases of the oldest processing structures—not to the machines themselves, but to what they call the "patient spirits" who they believe reside where human industry and natural forces have coexisted long enough. Their ceremonies mark not calendar dates but marsh cycles: the spring thaw, the summer flowering of certain crucial plants, the autumn deepening of the pools.

Few outsiders choose to live among them, but those who do undergo a lengthy acclimation. The locals call it "steeping," as the newcomer must quite literally allow their body to adjust to the constant moisture and unique bacterial environment of the marsh. Many develop rashes or respiratory issues that eventually force them to leave. Those who stay past the first year often develop the beginnings of the physical adaptations seen in native Seepers—the first signs being a subtle change in their skin's texture and a darkening of their eye color.

Children born in the marsh grow up with an innate sense of direction that confounds outsiders. Even in the most disorienting fog, when all landmarks disappear, young Seepers can navigate unerringly. They claim to feel the subtle currents of water flowing beneath the surface, the invisible pathways that connect the seemingly identical pools and channels.

The most respected members of their society are the "depth readers"—individuals who can assess the quality and depth of peat deposits by sound alone. They walk the marsh with specialized wooden staffs, tapping against the ground and listening to the resonance. The best among them can determine not just the depth of usable peat but its age, composition, and even the ancient vegetation that formed it, information crucial for both harvesting operations and the marsh's long-term health.

When Seepers die, they aren't buried or cremated but returned to the marsh through a process they call "settling." The body is prepared with preservative herbs, wrapped in biodegradable fiber mats, and placed in specially designated pools. Over years, they become part of the peat itself—a literal return to the landscape that shaped them, continuing the cycle that has sustained their people for generations.
