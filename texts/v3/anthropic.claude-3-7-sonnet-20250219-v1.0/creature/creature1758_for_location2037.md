# The Denizens of the Dockside District

In the Dockside District, humanity and other forms of life have adapted to exist in harmony with the perpetual dampness and rhythmic tidal systems. The inhabitants here are neither fully of the land nor completely of the water, but something in between—amphibious in habit if not in biology.

The most numerous residents are the Tidewalkers, families who have lived here for generations. Their skin has taken on a peculiar translucence, almost opalescent in quality, that seems to capture and subtly reflect the district's muted light. They move with an economy of motion that outsiders find unsettling—no wasted gestures, no unnecessary conversation. Their clothing is practical: waxed canvas, treated leather, and rubberized fabrics in colors that mirror their environment—slate grays, algae greens, and the occasional rust red of old iron.

Tidewalkers rarely speak above a murmur, having developed a form of communication that incorporates the ambient sounds of their environment. A conversation might pause precisely when a particular lock clanks open, the mechanical sound serving as punctuation or emphasis. Children learn early to distinguish between dozens of varieties of water sounds and what each portends. Their language includes words for water states that have no equivalent in the common tongue.

Most distinctive is their relationship with time, which is measured not by clocks but by the engineered tides. "Three ebbs from now" or "at the second flow after tomorrow's first" are typical ways of making appointments. Their homes reflect this tidal consciousness—furniture on pulleys that can be raised during high water, sleeping quarters always on upper levels, kitchens equipped with specialized stoves that function regardless of water level.

The Mechanists form a separate social class, responsible for maintaining the complex systems that regulate the district's waterways. They are recognized by their distinctive brass-framed goggles and tool harnesses. Unlike the Tidewalkers, they move with deliberate haste, always attending to some calibration or repair. Their fingers are stained with lubricating oils and technical dyes used to mark different mechanical systems. Many sport elaborate tattoos that mimic the layout of the district's pneumatic tubes or lock systems—living maps that they constantly update as changes are made to the infrastructure.

The Mechanists live primarily in converted water towers and control stations, spaces filled with gauges, valves, and technical diagrams scrawled directly onto walls. They form a quasi-religious order, passing down knowledge through apprenticeship rather than written manuals. They speak of the district's systems with reverence, attributing to them a consciousness that others find superstitious but that seems entirely rational to those who have spent decades observing the almost organic behavior of the engineered waterways.

Less numerous but equally integral to the district are the Salvagers, who emerge at low tide to collect whatever valuables have been deposited in the mudflats. They wear specially designed mud-shoes that prevent them from sinking into the silt and carry long-handled tools for retrieving items without bending down. Their clothing is layered and always partially wet, giving them a shaggy appearance. Many wear face coverings against the occasional pockets of gas that bubble up from disturbed mud.

The Salvagers operate in family units, each with rights to specific sections of the exposed canal beds. These territories are unmarked to outsiders but are respected through complex arrangements dating back generations. What they recover—coins, tools, occasionally more valuable artifacts—is sorted, cleaned, and sold through a network of small shops tucked into the district's less trafficked corners. Despite the apparent haphazardness of their work, the Salvagers maintain meticulous records of their findings, creating a material history of the district that academic institutions sometimes consult.

The canals themselves host the Water Nomads, who live entirely on narrow houseboats that navigate the changing waterways with practiced precision. Their vessels are marvels of compact design—every surface serves multiple functions, and nothing is wasted. The boats themselves are shallow-drafted with retractable keels that can be adjusted as water levels change. Many are powered by small steam engines that run on compressed fuel bricks, leaving minimal smoke.

The Water Nomads serve as the district's connective tissue, carrying messages, trading goods, and transporting people between the more isolated sections. They know every shortcut, every low bridge, every dangerous current. Their children learn to pilot almost before they can walk, developing an intuitive understanding of hydrodynamics that formal education could never provide. They mark the changing of seasons not by temperature but by subtle shifts in the district's engineered tidal patterns that outsiders would never notice.

Above these human inhabitants lives a parallel society of semi-domesticated creatures. The most visible are the messenger bats—smaller than their wild cousins and bred over generations for intelligence and homing instinct. They carry tiny scrolled messages in metal tubes attached to their legs, navigating between specific buildings with unerring accuracy. The Tidewalkers maintain special rooftop structures for these bats, feeding them and caring for them in exchange for their services.

Less beloved but equally ubiquitous are the canal rats, which have evolved into distinct colonies, each specialized for different niches within the district. The warehouse rats are larger, with powerful jaws for gnawing through containers. The bridge rats are smaller and faster, adept at stealing from pedestrians. The canal-edge rats have partially webbed feet and waterproof fur, allowing them to swim short distances. Despite periodic extermination efforts, the rats persist, having developed a complex relationship with the district that is somewhere between pest and unofficial custodian.

Perhaps most mysterious are the rarely-seen Canal Speakers—individuals who have developed the ability to predict the district's moods and movements. Neither Tidewalkers nor Mechanists, they often live alone in small rooms directly above the water, where they spend hours in apparent communion with the canal system. Some believe they can influence the engineered tides through ritual or persuasion, though the Mechanists dismiss such notions. Regardless, their predictions about flooding patterns, structural failures, or even good fishing spots are treated with respect by most district dwellers.

Together, these inhabitants have formed a society as intricate and interdependent as the lock systems that regulate their watery home—never entirely visible from any single vantage point, but functioning with a complexity and resilience that allows them to thrive in conditions others would find uninhabitable.
