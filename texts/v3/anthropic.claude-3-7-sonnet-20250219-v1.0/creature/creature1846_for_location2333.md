# The Caretakers of The Hollows

The Hollows hosts a rare symbiotic community of beings that exist somewhere between the classifications of human, amphibian, and fungal organism. They call themselves the Meruthi in their own language—a word best translated as "those who listen to water."

The adult Meruthi stand slightly shorter than humans, with slender frames and skin that varies from pale green to deep amber depending on their age and lineage. Their skin is not quite amphibian but possesses similar qualities—slightly permeable, always glistening with a thin layer of protective mucus that carries chemical information between individuals. This dermal layer allows them to absorb nutrients directly from their environment, supplementing their limited diet.

Their faces blend human and amphibian characteristics—large, expressive eyes with horizontal pupils that can adapt to the dim light, wide mouths that stretch in genuine smiles, and small gill-like structures behind their ears that filter air rather than water. They have no hair, but patches of fine, colorful filaments grow from their shoulders and the crown of their heads—these are actually specialized fungal symbionts that help regulate their body chemistry and allow them to sense changes in barometric pressure.

The Meruthi move with deliberate grace, each step carefully placed between the pools. Their four-fingered hands feature elongated digits with subtle webbing and extraordinarily sensitive fingertips that can detect minute changes in water chemistry. Their feet are broad and slightly adhesive, allowing them to navigate the slippery terrain with confidence.

Their social structure is non-hierarchical but highly specialized. Each Meruthi tends to specific pools within The Hollows based on their natural affinities. Some care for the breeding pools of rare amphibians, others cultivate the medicinal fungi that grow in specific water conditions, while others maintain the delicate balance of the deeper pools where their most sacred rituals occur. Knowledge is passed through an apprentice system, with each Meruthi eventually mastering multiple specializations throughout their long lives.

They communicate through a combination of verbal language, body posture, chemical signals, and patterns tapped onto water surfaces that create meaningful ripples. Their spoken language is melodic and fluid, incorporating sounds beyond human vocal range—clicks, trills, and sustained tones that interact with the natural acoustics of The Hollows. When multiple Meruthi speak together, their voices create harmonic overtones that seem to resonate with the calls of the frogs and the dripping of water.

The Meruthi reproduction cycle is intimately tied to the seasonal rhythms of The Hollows. They are sequential hermaphrodites, transitioning between reproductive roles based on environmental cues and community needs. Their offspring begin life as fully aquatic tadpole-like forms in the deepest, most protected pools, tended by the entire community. As they mature, they gradually develop their amphibious traits and join the younger Meruthi in simpler tasks before finding their own specializations.

Their dwellings are minimalist—small grottos carved into the basin walls, expanded from natural formations. These spaces are adorned not with possessions but with living arrangements of bioluminescent fungi and carefully cultivated moss patterns. Many Meruthi prefer to rest semi-submerged in specific pools rather than in dry shelters, entering a meditative state as they absorb nutrients.

The Meruthi's relationship with human visitors is complex. They neither seek contact nor avoid it entirely. They understand that their continued existence depends partly on maintaining their secrecy, yet they recognize the value of limited cultural exchange. They will observe human visitors for days before deciding whether to reveal themselves. Those humans who approach The Hollows with respect and genuine curiosity might eventually glimpse a Meruthi tending to a distant pool or hear their songs echoing after nightfall.

The stone markers that visitors mistake for trail signs are actually components of a sophisticated calendar system maintained by the Meruthi, tracking celestial cycles and marking important boundaries in their territory. They periodically rearrange these markers to reflect changes in The Hollows' delicate ecosystem.

The Meruthi possess knowledge of plant compounds that induce temporary perceptual shifts—substances they use in their own rituals but occasionally share with trusted human visitors. These experiences don't merely alter consciousness but specifically attune one to the water-based networks of communication that permeate The Hollows, allowing a fleeting glimpse into the Meruthi understanding of interconnectedness.

Perhaps most remarkable is the Meruthi concept of self, which extends beyond their bodies to include their specific pools and the organisms within them. They do not perceive themselves as separate from their environment but as specialized expressions of The Hollows itself—stewards and embodiments of its consciousness. In their understanding, The Hollows is not merely a place but a living entity of which they are integral components, like organs in a body.
