# The People of the Steaming Foundry

The valley's inhabitants are as much shaped by its environment as they have shaped it in return. They call themselves the Seepfolk, though outsiders often refer to them as "Foundry Dwellers" or sometimes, less kindly, as "Amphibians."

The permanent residents number perhaps eight hundred souls, their skin uniformly pale with a faint bluish undertone that comes from generations living in the dim, filtered light. Most stand shorter than lowland humans, with broad shoulders and sturdy builds evolved for heat retention and strength. Their hair grows thick and often exhibits unusual tints of blue-black or deep green when caught in direct light, typically worn long and bound back with copper or brass wire to keep it from the omnipresent moisture.

The most distinctive feature of the Seepfolk is their eyes—large, with unusually expanded pupils that rarely contract even in bright light. The irises show striking amber or golden tones that seem to gather what little light exists, allowing them to navigate the twilight conditions of the valley without lamps where visitors would stumble blindly. Children born here develop this adaptation by their fifth year; those who don't are eventually sent to relatives in sunnier settlements.

Their clothing is practical—layers of treated canvas, leather, and oiled wool that resist the constant moisture. Little is purely decorative, though every garment features distinctive patterns of copper wire stitching that denotes family lineage, professional specialization, and personal accomplishments. Master crafters wear elaborate wire meshes woven into their outer layers that catch the lamplight and transform the wearer into a walking constellation of golden points.

The society functions around three principal castes, though movement between them is possible through demonstrated skill rather than birth:

The Smelters form the foundation of their economy, operating the central foundry where they process ore brought from mines further up in the highlands. They've developed proprietary techniques for working with the unusual metals found in the region, creating alloys with properties that fetch high prices in the lowland markets. Smelters are recognizable by their ritualistic scarring—small, precise burns on the backs of their hands that form maps of the mineral veins they've helped exploit.

The Conduits manage the vast network of pipes, channels, and cisterns that control the valley's constant water flow. They're part engineers, part mystics, claiming to "hear the water's intentions" and adjusting the system accordingly. They wear distinctive respirator masks even when not working, ornately crafted from copper and brass with intricate filtration systems that process the humid air through chambers of aromatic fungi they cultivate for this purpose.

The Harvesters are the biologists of the settlement, tending to the valley's unique ecosystem of fungi, amphibians, and specialized plants. They collect, cultivate, and process the valley's organic wealth—medicinal fungi, the bioluminescent secretions of the blind salamanders, the tough fibers of the wall-climbing ferns. They are easily identified by their stained fingers and the living necklaces they wear—colonies of luminous moss growing on woven threads that glow softly in the dim light.

A fourth, unofficial caste exists in the Unmarked—those who came to the valley later in life, either as traders who chose to stay or exiles from lowland communities. They never fully develop the physiological adaptations of true Seepfolk and must rely on tools and aids to function in the environment. They're tolerated rather than fully embraced, typically handling interactions with outside traders and performing tasks that require familiarity with lowland ways.

The valley's governance operates through a council of nine elders—three from each recognized caste—who meet weekly in a circular chamber carved directly into the valley wall. Decisions require consensus, with discussions sometimes lasting days while the council members inhale vapors from specially cultivated fungi that are said to "align the mind with the valley's pulse." Their authority is rarely challenged, as the harsh environment demands cooperation for survival.

Children in the valley are raised communally, spending their early years in the central nursery—a large structure with open walls where the constant ambient noise of the settlement is thought to stimulate proper development. At age seven, they begin apprenticeships, rotating through all three castes before declaring their intention at fifteen. Their education emphasizes practical skills, sensory development, and memorization of the valley's complex oral history, which is recited during the monthly "Telling" when the foundry fires are temporarily banked.

The settlement's relationship with the strange semi-raccoons (locally called "scuttlers") is symbiotic—the creatures are permitted to roam freely and are even fed at designated stations in exchange for their role in controlling the insect population and cleaning waste. Some families form bonds with particular scuttlers, who become de facto pets that help tend to children and alert households to changes in water flow or air quality that might signal danger.

Religious practice centers around what they call "The Breath"—the belief that the valley itself is a living entity that inhales and exhales on a cycle lasting generations. They believe they currently live during an inhalation phase, which explains the constant seeping of water into the valley. Ancient carvings found in the highest reaches of the walls depict a time when the valley was dry—the exhalation phase—and prophesy it will come again. Their rituals often involve controlled breathing exercises and the ceremonial collection and redistribution of water.

Death is handled practically—bodies are processed in a special chamber of the foundry, where the water is extracted (considered the "essence" returning to the valley) and the remaining elements are reduced to ash that is mixed with clay to form bricks. These memorial bricks are then used in expanding the settlement's structures, literally building the dead back into the community.

Despite—or perhaps because of—the harsh conditions, the Seepfolk have developed a profound appreciation for subtle beauty. Their music uses the foundry's rhythmic hammering as a base percussion, overlaid with wind instruments carved from the hollow bones of highland birds and string instruments whose tone and pitch shift with the humidity. Their art tends toward the tactile rather than visual—sculptures designed to channel water in pleasing patterns or resonant chambers that transform the valley's ambient sounds into harmonies.

Visitors often find the Seepfolk disconcerting at first—their silent movements, their comfort in near-darkness, their seemingly uncanny ability to predict weather changes or water flow patterns days in advance. But those who stay long enough discover a community of remarkable resilience and depth, where the harshness of the environment has not hardened hearts but rather created an intense appreciation for interdependence and the subtle rhythms that govern all life in the Steaming Foundry.
