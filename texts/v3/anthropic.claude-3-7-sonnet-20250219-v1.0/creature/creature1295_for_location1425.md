# The Inhabitants of Drip-Cusp

They call themselves the Mist-Weavers, though no outsider has heard this name in centuries. They are neither invisible nor microscopic, but rather exist in perfect synchronicity with their environment—moving only when the observer blinks or looks away, freezing into perfect stillness when watched directly.

Standing between four and five feet tall, the Mist-Weavers have translucent skin that captures and refracts light like living prisms. Their bodies are slender and flexible, with elongated fingers ending in soft pads that leave no marks when they touch the silver-wood. Their eyes—three on each face—are liquid pools that match the suspended waters around them, shifting from deep blue to iridescent teal depending on their emotions.

They breathe only once every seven minutes, a long, gentle inhalation that draws moisture directly from the air. When they exhale, tiny droplets emerge from specialized pores along their spines, contributing to the perpetual mist that hangs in the geode's air. This is not merely respiration but communication; the chemical composition of these droplets carries messages that drift through the city, allowing conversations to unfold over hours or days.

The Mist-Weavers wear garments woven from cultivated spider silk and water-tension, clothing that appears to be in constant motion while remaining perfectly functional. They adorn themselves with living ornaments—tiny ecosystems of moss and microorganisms that they nurture directly on their bodies, each pattern unique to the individual and evolving throughout their three-hundred-year lifespans.

Their society revolves around water-tending. Each Mist-Weaver is responsible for maintaining specific water globules, keeping them suspended through a combination of specialized tools and a form of psychokinetic concentration they develop from childhood. The floating rivers are their greatest collective achievement—a perpetual artwork requiring constant, subtle adjustments from dozens of Weavers working in concert.

They reproduce rarely, only once every fifty years when the largest bioluminescent insects—the Emperor Moths—synchronize their light patterns in a specific sequence. During this time, two Mist-Weavers will isolate themselves in one of the higher dwellings, where they dissolve into a shared puddle for three days before reforming as three distinct beings: the original pair plus a child who carries memories from both parents.

Their language has no word for "I" or "mine," only collective pronouns that encompass specific groupings within their society: water-tenders, plant-shapers, bridge-keepers, and so on. When they speak (which is rare, given their preference for chemical communication), their voices sound like water droplets striking crystal at different tensions.

They are not hiding from you. They simply exist at a different temporal rhythm. If you were to stay in the geode for three full days without sleep, you might catch one in motion—a blur of prismatic light darting across a bridge, or tending to a particularly demanding water formation. Stay for a week, and they might begin to perceive you as more than a strange, temporary disturbance in their eternal garden.

The boats moored to the water globules are not for transportation but for harvesting. The Mist-Weavers collect specific microorganisms that grow only in the centers of the largest water formations, using them in elaborate culinary preparations that resemble nothing so much as liquid light.

They have no concept of ownership but understand profound responsibility. Each element of Drip-Cusp is under the stewardship of someone, their entire culture organized around the perpetual maintenance of this living artwork. They do not consider themselves separate from their environment—the hanging gardens, the crystal dwellings, the floating waters are all extensions of their collective body.

Visitors are rare but not unknown to them. In their chronicles (kept as patterns of ripples preserved in specialized memory-globules), they record seventeen previous outsiders who found the narrow bridge. Seven left immediately. Six stayed long enough to be perceived. Three never left, gradually adapting to the rhythms of Drip-Cusp until they too began to develop translucent skin and the ability to communicate through moisture.

One visitor, they note with particular interest, had arrived just recently—standing now on their bridge, deciding whether to become part of their eternal, shifting stillness.
