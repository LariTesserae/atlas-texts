# The People of Khloramesh

The inhabitants of Khloramesh are as much a part of the living city as the vegetation itself—humans who have evolved alongside their botanical metropolis for countless generations, their culture and biology inseparable from the green world they tend.

Physically, Khlorameshi people are marked by their environment. Their skin bears a distinctive greenish undertone—not from pigmentation but from symbiotic algae that reside in their outermost dermal layers, providing protection from the harsh mountain sun while synthesizing small amounts of supplemental nutrients. This symbiosis begins in infancy, when newborns are bathed in carefully prepared algal solutions as part of their welcoming ceremony.

They are lean and wiry, built for climbing and navigating the vertical spaces of their arboreal home. Their fingers are notably longer than those of lowlanders, with hardened calluses that form naturally from childhood as they learn to grip living handholds that might shift slightly under pressure. Their toes spread wider when barefoot, providing better purchase on the springy surfaces of walkways.

Their hair grows thick and often incorporates living elements—not merely decorated with flowers and leaves, but actually cultivated with symbiotic vine-like plants that intertwine with their own locks, creating elaborate living headdresses that signal social role, family lineage, and personal accomplishment. These hair-gardens require daily tending and become more elaborate with age, making elders immediately recognizable by their cascading green coronas.

Khlorameshi vocal anatomy has adapted to the city's needs. Their speech incorporates a wide range of whistles, clicks, and sustained tones that carry effectively through the dense vegetation and allow communication across considerable distances without shouting. This "plant speech" evolved partly from necessity and partly from mimicking the sounds of the city itself—the whistling of wind through hollow stems, the popping of seed pods, the creaking of growing wood.

Their diet reflects their environment's abundance. They consume over forty varieties of fruits, nuts, and fungi that grow directly on the city's structures. Specialized culinary plants are cultivated in kitchen-gardens—herbs that change flavor depending on how their leaves are bruised, fruits that ferment while still attached to the vine, vegetables that cook themselves through exothermic chemical reactions when properly prepared. Protein comes primarily from cultivated insects, small arboreal mammals hunted with blow darts tipped with paralytic plant extracts, and eggs collected from the various birds that inhabit the upper canopy.

Khlorameshi society is organized around botanical knowledge and responsibility. Every citizen belongs to one of twelve Cultivation Houses, each responsible for maintaining specific plant species and city sectors. Children are not solely raised by their birth parents but are mentored by their entire House, learning from multiple elders as they develop. By adolescence, each young person begins specializing—some as structural tenders who guide the growth of living architecture, others as water-keepers who maintain the city's hydraulic systems, others as harvesters who collect food and materials without harming the city's integrity.

The Gardener Council that governs Khloramesh consists of the most accomplished members of each House, individuals who have demonstrated both technical mastery and philosophical wisdom. They are recognized by the distinctive staff each carries—a living tool grown from a specialized hardwood that continues to produce leaves from its upper portion, requiring constant care but serving as both symbol of office and practical botanical implement.

The Khlorameshi concept of time differs fundamentally from other cultures. Their calendar tracks not only lunar cycles and seasons but growth cycles of various keystone species. Important dates are marked not by numbers but by botanical events: "the third unfurling of the canopy leaves," "when the night-blooms first open," or "the season of falling seed-stars." This creates a temporal awareness that is simultaneously precise and fluid, responsive to environmental conditions rather than abstract measurement.

Their spiritual practices center on what they call "the Flowing"—the movement of water, nutrients, and energy throughout the living city. They do not worship deities as much as honor processes—growth, decay, renewal, transformation. Their rituals often involve the careful collection and redistribution of substances that embody these processes: water, sap, spores, seeds. Holy sites are not temples but particular ancient plants—"grandfather trees" believed to contain the accumulated wisdom of centuries.

Khlorameshi create art that is primarily temporal and sensory rather than permanent. Music played on instruments grown for specific acoustic properties—hollow gourds with carefully positioned holes, percussion pieces made from resonant woods, wind instruments that incorporate living reeds that continue to grow and subtly change the instrument's tone over decades. Their visual art consists largely of living arrangements—plants cultivated into specific patterns and forms, sometimes taking years to achieve the intended design.

Their medicine is inseparable from their botanical knowledge. Healers are trained to recognize thousands of plant properties and applications. Many Khlorameshi carry personal medicinal plants—small specimens grown in pots or wearable containers that produce the specific compounds that individual might need based on their constitution. Surgery, when necessary, utilizes tools grown from plants with natural antiseptic properties, and wounds are sealed with living tissue grafts rather than sutured.

Writing exists but primarily for botanical record-keeping—detailed observations of plant behavior, successful hybridization techniques, and growing conditions. Most knowledge is transmitted orally through elaborate mnemonic songs that encode agricultural and architectural information in their melodies, rhythms, and lyrics. These songs are constantly updated as new botanical discoveries are made, creating a living archive that adapts like the city itself.

Conflict resolution in Khloramesh mirrors plant behavior. Minor disputes are resolved through "competitive grafting"—each party must grow a plant demonstrating their understanding of community principles, with the more successful growth indicating the more harmonious solution. More serious conflicts involve "root mediation," where disputants must tend a shared plant together until it thrives, forcing cooperation despite their differences.

The Khlorameshi relationship with outsiders is cautious but not hostile. They welcome traders and visitors but maintain strict protocols around plant material entering or leaving the city, fearing botanical diseases or invasive species that could threaten their living infrastructure. Guests are housed in specialized visitor structures—younger, less critical buildings on the city's periphery where potential contamination can be contained.

Perhaps most distinctive is their language for personhood, which extends beyond humans. Certain ancient plants that form critical infrastructure are referred to with the same pronouns used for elders. The city itself is addressed in formal speech as though it were a conscious entity—not as metaphor or animistic belief, but as acknowledgment of the collective intelligence represented by generations of cultivated growth.

As they age, Khlorameshi increasingly integrate with their botanical environment. Elders often choose to become "root-sitters"—adapting sedentary lifestyles where they tend a particular significant plant for years, their knowledge and presence nurturing its growth while their reduced mobility is supported by the community. The most revered may eventually be "planted" after death—their bodies becoming literal nourishment for a new structural element of the city, their names sung whenever that plant flowers, their wisdom literally incorporated into the living architecture.

The Khlorameshi are neither primitive nor technologically stunted—they represent an alternative technological evolution where biotechnology developed not through laboratories and mechanics but through multigenerational observation and careful cultivation. They are scientists whose experiments unfold over decades, engineers whose building materials grow themselves according to carefully maintained genetic instructions, philosophers whose metaphysics emerged from intimate coexistence with their botanical collaborators.

They do not merely inhabit Khloramesh—they are Khloramesh, as much as its vines and trees and flowers, a human population that has become one more species in a complex, interdependent ecosystem of their own careful creation.
