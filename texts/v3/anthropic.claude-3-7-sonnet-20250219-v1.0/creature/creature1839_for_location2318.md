# The Caretaker

Deep within the Canopy Garden dwells a being neither entirely human nor completely separate from the garden itself. Those who venture far enough into the living cathedral sometimes glimpse her—an elderly woman with skin that has taken on the texture of lichen-covered bark, mottled in shades of olive and amber.

She moves with deliberate grace, her spine curved like the iron ribs of the dome. Her hair is not hair at all, but a crown of symbiotic filaments—fine tendrils that shift between moss-green and silver depending on the humidity, extending down past her waist. These living strands occasionally flower with tiny blue blossoms no larger than teardrops.

The Caretaker wears layers of fabric that might once have been conventional clothing but have become living tapestries—canvas and cotton slowly infiltrated by vegetable fibers, with fungi that glow faintly in the shadows of dawn and dusk. Her garments are perpetually damp, constantly nourishing the microbiome that she hosts.

Her hands are remarkable—elongated fingers with an extra joint, ending in hardened tips that can delicately adjust the ancient brass mechanisms or carefully prune overgrowth without tools. The veins beneath her translucent skin pulse not just with blood but with a greenish fluid that suggests chlorophyll has somehow been incorporated into her circulatory system.

The Caretaker speaks rarely, and when she does, her voice has the quality of rustling leaves—a whispered symphony that somehow carries across the dome. The small primates recognize her authority, bringing her objects they find—fallen seeds, interesting stones, occasionally artifacts from the original researchers that have surfaced through the perpetual cycle of decomposition and renewal.

She doesn't appear to eat in any conventional sense. Instead, she periodically presses her palms against the trunks of certain trees, standing motionless for hours as nutrients flow between their tissues and hers through a process that defies traditional biology. The birds are unafraid of her and often perch on her shoulders, bringing her information about the garden's far reaches through means only they understand.

At night, she rests in what was once the observatory platform at the dome's northernmost point. There, she has cultivated a nest of particularly resilient bromeliads that hold her body like a living hammock. As she sleeps, the small phosphorescent creatures that inhabit her hair migrate throughout the canopy, carrying spores and pollen to precisely where they're needed.

Her origins remain unclear. Some journals found in waterproof cases suggest she might be descended from the last botanical researcher who refused to abandon the station when funding was cut. Other entries hint at a deliberate melding—a scientific experiment to create a human-plant hybrid who could maintain the garden indefinitely. But the most compelling theory comes from the garden itself, which seems to have recognized its need for conscious stewardship and gradually shaped one of its human inhabitants into the perfect symbiotic partner.

What's certain is that she is the garden's consciousness made manifest—its memory, its caretaker, and its future. She knows which valves need adjustment when atmospheric pressure changes, which species require intervention when they grow too dominant, and how to communicate with the various evolved creatures that maintain balance in this enclosed world.

The Caretaker watches visitors with eyes that have irises striated like tree rings, neither welcoming nor hostile, but evaluating—determining whether they represent threat or opportunity for her enclosed world. Those who show respect may find small gifts left in their path—seeds of extraordinary plants that exist nowhere else, or vials of distilled nectar with remarkable properties. Those who damage even a single specimen without cause may find the paths mysteriously rearranged, leading them in circles until they stumble, exhausted, back to the entrance.

She is the garden's heart, its memory, and its guardian—an entity that transcends simple definitions of life, having become something as unique as the ecosystem she tends.
