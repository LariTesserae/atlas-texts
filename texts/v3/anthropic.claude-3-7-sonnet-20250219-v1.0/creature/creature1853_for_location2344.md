# The Clayborn

They call themselves the Shapers, though outsiders know them as the Clayborn. Their skin has taken on the ruddy hue of the earth they work, not as mere staining but as fundamental transformation. Up close, you can see the fine cracks across their faces—not wrinkles, but the natural crazing of clay that has been fired and cooled countless times. When they smile, which is rare and precious, these lines deepen into delicate patterns like the finest pottery.

The children are born soft-skinned, almost translucent, but within days their bodies begin to absorb the elements of their environment. By their fifth year, a child's skin has hardened enough to withstand the heat that would blister an outsider. Their tears leave tracks that must be smoothed away quickly, for the Clayborn believe that hardened tear-channels invite sorrow to make permanent residence.

They grow tall and lean, their muscles corded like twisted rope beneath skin burnished to a dull sheen. There is no obesity here—the constant labor and rationed water make that impossible—but neither is there emaciation. Their bodies find efficiency in form, wasting nothing, storing precisely what is needed. Their hands are their most distinctive feature: broad palms with elongated fingers that seem to possess an extra joint, allowing them to manipulate clay with uncanny precision. These hands never fully close into fists; they remain partially open, as though perpetually ready to receive the earth.

The elders are most transformed. The oldest among them have skin that has hardened to a porcelain-like quality, with fine networks of cracks that gleam faintly in the kiln-light. They move slowly, deliberately, their joints sometimes emitting the faint click of ceramic pieces touching. When they die—always at their own choosing, when they feel the fire within them cooling—their bodies are placed in the hottest kilns. They do not burn as flesh burns; rather, they vitrify, transforming into glossy figurines that families place in wall-niches as ancestors who watch over the continuing work.

Speech among the Clayborn is low and economical, partially because of the din, but mainly from cultural preference. They believe words, like water, should not be wasted. Their language is heavy with consonants that can be felt vibrating in the chest, with few vowels that might evaporate in the heat. They communicate as much through touch and shared rhythm as through words—a mallet's pattern can convey complex messages about the day's work across vast distances.

They sleep briefly but deeply, usually just after midnight when the kilns burn lowest. Their bodies cool during these hours, the clay of their skin contracting slightly. They do not dream as others do; instead, they experience what they call "settling," when the day's work integrates into their being. They rise before dawn already knowing what must be done, their bodies having solved problems during the cooling.

Their diet consists mainly of dense seed-cakes carried in from the world beyond, supplemented by the sparse succulents they cultivate. They eat little but require gallons of special clay-rich water imported by traders who have learned to profit from their peculiar needs. This water they drink in small, measured sips throughout the day, allowing it to integrate slowly with their transforming bodies.

They reproduce rarely but with intention. Conception occurs only during the hottest months, when the kilns burn day and night and the very air trembles with heat. Pregnancy lasts exactly eleven months, during which the mother works shorter hours but never stops completely. The community believes that the rhythm of labor shapes the child within, teaching it the song of creation before birth. Delivery happens communally, with the women gathering to form a circle of hands that pass the newborn from person to person, each adding their touch, their blessing, their welcome to the perpetual work.

They have no formal government but are ruled by the work itself—by the necessity of the eternal building. Status comes from skill, from understanding the clay most deeply, from producing bricks that hold their form most perfectly or structures that balance beauty with function. The most respected are those who can "hear the earth speaking," who know without testing when clay is ready, when a kiln is at perfect temperature, when a wall will stand for generations.

They have one ritual that outsiders never witness. Once each year, when the air is thickest and the kilns burn hottest, the entire community gathers. Each person brings a small ball of the finest clay, which they have been saving and preparing all year. Together, in perfect silence, they build a single object—a massive urn or vessel that contains something outsiders can only speculate about. This creation is fired in a special kiln built just for this purpose, then buried deep beneath the slum's center, adding to a collection that has accumulated for centuries. What these vessels contain, what purpose they serve, the Clayborn never say. But sometimes, in the deepest night, the ground beneath the slum trembles slightly, as though something stirs there, something still taking shape, still becoming.
