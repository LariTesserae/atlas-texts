If you watch one of the cherry-red valve housings long enough—waiting out the twelve-minute cycle of the bronze wipers three or four times—you may notice a patch of the dark sulfide rind on the adjacent brick wall detach itself, fold inward like a wet glove, and pull smoothly through an unglazed transom. 

At first, this looks like another of the parchment-colored fish, only larger and darker. Look closer, where the ultraviolet bruise of a mercury-arc rectifier catches the turn of an alley, and the shape resolves into limbs, a broad, flattened ribcage, and a heavy belt hung with loops of braided copper wire and wedges of tallow.

There are perhaps nine or ten of them in the quarry at any given decade, though never more than two are seen in the same shaft of brick and iron at once. They are what became of the maintenance crew, or what was grown from the idea of a maintenance crew after the quarry flooded and the salt thickened to oil. Whether their grandfathers walked these catwalks in wool coats before the water rose, or whether they were lowered through a borehole as altered infants when the ice sealed over, is no longer legible in their anatomy. The brine has finished negotiating with them, too.

They are roughly the length of a tall man, though nobody stands upright here except when hooking a heel under a floor-joist to work both hands inside an overhead junction box. Their skin has the dense, poreless grain of cured hide pickled in alum—slate-gray across the shoulders and back, fading to a translucent, tallowy white over the belly where a thick, insulating apron of firm blubber smooths away the angles of hip and collarbone. To keep the sub-freezing brine from stealing their core heat, their extremities have simplified and thickened; fingers are slightly webbed at the base, stubby and calloused with gray horn at the tips like the pads of a terrapin, yet remarkably deft when teasing a hairline strand of tin dendrite out of a fouled magnetic latch. 

They do not breathe the water, and they cannot breathe the air above the ice—its dryness and sharp cold would sear their throats in minutes. Instead, they live off the upside-down ponds.

Half the tenements hold pockets of gas pinned beneath stairwell landings, inverted cast-iron vats, and cellar vaults. Most of these mirrors are lethal mixtures of hydrogen fizzed off leaky insulation and bitter carbon dioxide, and the wardens leave those alone—or rather, they farm them. Using broad wooden paddles shaped like bakers' peels, a warden will stand upside-down on a ceiling—feet braced against the joists, hairless head hanging down into the dark parlor—and gently herd bubbles of stray oxygen bled from a dedicated pair of low-voltage electrolytic plates into a single vaulted bedroom on the third floor. There, trapped beneath the plastered lath, hangs a clear, trembling lens of breathable dampness six inches deep and ten feet across, smelling faintly of ozone, old tallow, and wet slate. 

When a warden needs wind, they glide up through a doorway, tilt their chin back until mouth and valvular nostrils break upward through the mercury-bright skin of the ceiling-pond, and drink a slow, silent lungful. The surface ripples outward in heavy, oily rings against the wallpaper-stripped plaster, and then the warden pushes gently off the ceiling and sinks back into the dark, good for another forty minutes of slow labor.

Because warm blood is an extravagance in water two degrees below freezing, they move with the exact cadence of the machinery around them: unhurried, coasting on momentum, conserving every calorie. A single push of a broad, splayed foot against a vitrified windowsill sends a warden drifting diagonally down a three-foot alley, past two tiers of iron catwalks and a pulsing gutta-percha main, one hand trailing out to catch a lead-sheathed cable and pivot effortlessly through a sash frame without stirring a spoonful of graphite silt from the sill. Their blood runs cool and thick, rich with dark myoglobin and natural glycols that keep their extremities supple even when they descend below the milky halocline on the second story.

Down in that denser, freezing gloom—where the red heater-lamps smear into vertical pillars—their eyes take on a faint, dull gleam like tarnished pewter. The sclera has vanished behind a protective nictitating caul as clear and tough as watch-crystal, keeping the caustic salt from stinging the wide, moon-pale irises. Yet they rely very little on sight. Across the bridge of the flat nose, along the brow ridges, and down the backs of the forearms run faint, stippled seams of dark pores—ampullae sensitive to the slightest warp in an electrical field. 

To a warden floating stationary in an intersection of the alleys, the dark town is neither dark nor silent; it is an intricate, humming architecture of galvanic contours. With eyes shut, they feel the steady, buttery push of direct current rising up the bimetal thermocouples in the gutted stairwells. They feel the rhythmic, magnetic *swallow* of the sliding collars nudging the gutta-percha hoses along their copper rods. More importantly, they feel the sharp, sour prickle against their cheekbones when a wire harp grows over-plated in one corner and threatens to short against an iron balcony, or when a shoe-last isopod wedges itself beneath a wiper track and stalls the counterweight.

Much of their waking life is spent tending the margins where the town’s slow chemistry drifts out of balance. A warden will hang motionless beside a brick archway for twenty minutes, chest pressed lightly against the masonry near a submerged vacuum tube—stealing the fractional warmth just as the parchment-fish do, sometimes sharing the same warm housing without either creature acknowledging the other, two pale, flat bodies side by side in the bruised blue glow. Then, reaching to the heavy copper belt that serves as both tool-rack and ballast, the warden unclips a scraper carved from whalebone or hardened horn—never iron, which might throw a stray arc or confuse the local field—and reaches out to pare back a creeping shelf of calcareous scale before it can bind a valve stem.

Where the stoneware hoppers in the courtyards fill to the lip with shaved metallic snow, it is the wardens who hook greased leather slings to the overhead gantry blocks. Working in pairs without a word or gesture—each feeling the shift of the other’s muscular electricity in the conductive brine from ten feet away—they haul on the endless bronze chains. Slowly, silently, the heavy hopper rises out of the milky sump, crosses above the rooftops just beneath the jagged black keels of the ice ceiling, and tips its silver-lead frost down a brick chute into the deep thermal vents on the quarry floor, where the town’s obscure, closed-loop alchemy re-dissolves or sinters it down in the dark.

They do have a language, though it puts no vibration into the water. Sound travels too fast and too hollowly in dense brine to be private or precise. Instead, when two wardens pass in the narrow gap between tenements—barely wide enough to slip past chest-to-chest—one will lay two stubby fingers lightly against the bare forearm of the other and flex the muscles of their own wrist and palm without moving the joints. Tiny, deliberate micro-currents from the tensing muscle fibers jump across the salt-slick skin into the listener’s sensory pores: a quiet, prickling shorthand of *line-fray, third-tier north*, or *hopper jammed*, or simply the warm, steady pulse of greeting.

They sleep wedged into the lukewarm hollows behind the massive thermocouple banks in the gutted stairwells, three or four of them tucked together like smooth gray river-stones in a crevice, tethered lightly by wrist-loops of braided gutta-percha so the slow convection currents won't drift them down into the freezing sump while they dream. Above their sleeping heads, carefully corralled beneath the stone landing, hangs a wide ceiling-mirror of clean air. Every quarter-hour or so, without waking, one of the sleepers loosens slightly in their tether, floats upward twelve inches until their face breaks the silvery upside-down surface, draws three slow breaths that fog against the cold stone, and settles gently back down into the cluster as their lungs compress.

Nothing goes to waste among them. Their tallow—used to pack leaky cable glands and grease the bronze wiper tracks—is rendered without fire in warm water-jackets wrapped around the thickest busbars, rendered from the pale parchment-fish when one dies of old age and sinks toward the flagstones, or from their own dead. When a warden finally stills—heart slowing over days until their galvanic signature fades into the background hum of the masonry—the others strip off the weighted copper belt, carry the body down below the milky halocline, and lay it gently across the warm induction coils in the lowest pipe gallery. Within a month, the blind white isopods and the velvety sulfur films have cleaned the bones to the color of old ivory, and the wire-weed grows an inch taller on the copper collars that season, stiffened with borrowed iron.

Very rarely, one of the younger ones—smaller, sleeker, still curious about edges—will follow a warm copper exhaust riser all the way up past the fourth-story eaves, up among the dark, inverted mountains of the ice ceiling, to the twenty-inch throat of open water that breathes up and down with the distant wind. 

Floating vertically in that narrow chimney, hovering just beneath the shifting ribbon of slush, the warden will raise one horn-tipped hand toward the pale, freezing pinhole of the upper world. They never break the surface; even an inch below the slush, the fresh meltwater diluted from the ice burns against their hyper-saline skin like weak acid and carries no electrical sense at all—just a sudden, terrifying sensory blindness, dead and empty. After a minute of watching the dim gray daylight filter through the breathing slush, watching the tiny flakes of rime fall back into the well and dissolve, the warden tucks their chin, kicks once against the warm copper pipe, and glides back down into the dark, crowded streets where every brick has a pulse and the bronze wipers are just beginning their twelve-minute fall.
