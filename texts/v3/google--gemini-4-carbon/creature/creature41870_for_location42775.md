At first, if you stand very still on one of the upper galleries, you will mistake him for an accumulation of mineral paste and abandoned lineman’s gear left slumped against a switchboard. 

He is sitting on an overturned porcelain insulator, utterly motionless, allowing the forty-eight-degree mist to bead on his shoulders so he does not waste heat fighting the room’s equilibrium. Only when the twenty-minute hydraulic sigh passes through the walls does he move. As the brass vent exhales its cold vapor, a long, jointed arm uncoils from beneath his heavy, stiffened mantle. He reaches out with an iron-shod hook—not a hand, or rather, a hand wrapped in so much vulcanized rubber and calloused horn that its fingers no longer separate—and catches a single, saucer-sized brass snail by the rim of its shell, prying it from the glazed brick with a slow, steady leverage that makes no sound at all.

There is only the one, though whether he is very old, or merely the latest iteration of something that buds or moults in the dark of the lower sumps, is impossible to tell by looking. He is shaped broadly like a man who has spent three centuries hunching under low pipe-runs. His spine has curved into a permanent, load-bearing arch, placing his head forward and low, level with his chest, so that he looks naturally down into the water basins and sideways along the mortar lines rather than up toward the unreachable vault. 

What covers him is neither wholly skin nor wholly clothing, anymore. Generations ago, perhaps, his kind wore the heavy oilskin coats and gutta-percha waders of municipal maintenance crews, or perhaps they simply found them in the lockers of the dry-rotted changing rooms off the main nave and crawled inside them like hermit crabs. Now, the distinction is gone. His hide is a thick, rubbery, slate-grey integument, deeply creased at the joints like the bellows of an accordion, and over his back and shoulders it has been deliberately cultivated with the chamber’s secondary geology. He lets the calcium-heavy water drip onto him while he sleeps. Thin, overlapping plates of ivory flowstone and ochre rust-rind have grown into the grain of his back, forming an articulated carapace that smells of wet pennies and cold lime. Tucked into the mortar-like crevices of his shoulders are living gardens of the blue-green electrolytic sulfate, harvested from the frayed copper busbars; they fizz faintly when he passes near a live transformer, casting a dim, cold halo around his jawless face.

Not jawless—his jaw is heavy and broad—but his mouth is small, tucked beneath a fleshy, downward-pointing nasal hood that keeps the constant drizzle out of his lungs. His eyes are immense, set slightly toward the sides of his skull, completely lacking whites. They are spheres of milky, bruised violet, tuned entirely to the sputterless amber of the gas tubes and the ultraviolet leak of the mercury rectifiers. A second, translucent eyelid slides horizontally across them every few minutes, squeegeeing away the condensation with a wet, microscopic click. 

He does not walk the chamber so much as he measures it. His rhythms are locked entirely to the twenty-minute revolutions of the three great flywheels. 

Watch him descend from the gallery. He does not use the iron stairs, whose treads have grown treacherous with black silt and wire-sedge; instead, he lowers himself down a calcified gantry chain, hand over hand, his broad, splayed feet gripping the milky stone draperies with prehensile toes as pale and tough as tallow-root. When he reaches the flooded floor, he steps into the shallow basin without raising a single ripple. His feet are wide and flat, cushioned with thick pads of fat that insulate him from the cold iron grates below, sliding between the drifting, blind amphibians without disturbing a single filament of their pink gills. The foot-wide water-treaders barely shift on their hair-thin legs as his bulk parts the meniscus inches away; to them, he is simply another slow pier moving at the speed of rising water.

He is not an engineer—he remembers nothing of cities, or why pressure must be stepped down, or what lies four hundred feet overhead beyond the choked intake shafts—yet his entire existence is an obscure, custodial symbiosis with the room’s decaying physics. He feeds on what the machinery concentrates, and in feeding, he keeps the machinery from choking itself to death.

He pauses beside the central flywheel. Reaching beneath his heavy mantle, he draws out a tool: a two-foot length of flat copper busbar, worn down at one end to a dull chisel-edge by decades of patient honing against cast iron. Waiting until a specific, thickened ridge of lime on the wheel’s submerged rim rises out of the black water, he leans his weight against the copper bar and shaves a curling ribbon of soft, wet calcium from the iron. He does not scrape down to bare metal—bare metal rusts too fast—leaving instead a thin, protective glaze. The shaved curl of mineral-paste, rich with iron-oxidizing bacteria and tiny, encrusted crustacea, goes straight into his mouth. He grinds it slowly between flat, molar plates that sound, deep in his skull, exactly like the rasping of the snails on the wall.

If left unchecked, the secondary geology would eventually seize the flywheels, bridge the porcelain insulators with wet stone, and short out the thermal piles below, plunging the cathedral into freezing, starving dark. He knows this not as a thought, but as an appetite. 

He has a particular fondness for the electrical alcoves. When the cold of the floor-basins finally seeps through his fat-pads, slowing his blood to thin syrup, he climbs into the switchboard bays where the step-down transformers hum at fifty cycles. The spindle-crabs do not scatter when he approaches; they merely part in a slow,clicking wave of copper-wound shells, waving their feathery antennae against his shins. He settles his broad back directly against the warm cast-iron housing of a live coil, letting the vibration pass directly into his ribs, warming his core. 

Here, he performs his most delicate grooming. Lifting a heavy, three-fingered hand—the inner palm bare, pale, and extraordinarily sensitive—he reaches toward a frayed lead where blue-green sulfate coral has grown too close to a grounding plate, threatening to bleed the precious current into the limestone. With extreme slowness, he pinches off the branching tip of the live mineral. A sharp, violet arc snaps from the copper into his knuckle. 

He does not pull back. His grey, rubbery throat swells once, twice, like a bullfrog swallowing air, and the milky spheres of his eyes roll half-shut in sluggish, narcotic pleasure. His nervous system, starved of sun and warmth, drinks the stray amperage directly. For an hour afterward, sitting motionless in the amber gloom while the grey bat-things haul themselves silently up the chains beside him, you can see the faint, branching trace of his veins glowing dull blue beneath the translucent skin of his inner wrists.

He makes no voice. There is no one to speak to, and a voice would have sharp edges that do not belong here. Yet, when he is full of lime-paste and static, resting his chin on his knees in a recessed brick niche high above the nave, he exhales in time with the room. A low, resonant fifty-cycle hum vibrates in the hollow chambers of his nasal hood—indistinguishable from the buried transformers, blending seamlessly into the bruised light and the patient, weeping stone, until you could look right at the alcove and swear there was nothing there at all, only an old coat hung over a warm pipe, waiting out the centuries.
