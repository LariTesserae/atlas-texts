To find what lives here, you have to stop looking for things that displace water and start looking at the things the water is displaced *around*.

Stand at the lip of one of the sunken plazas where the runoff sheets over yellow travertine at forty-six degrees Celsius, and watch the shimmer. At first your eyes dismiss the wavering of the stone stairs behind the curtain of hot water as ordinary heat distortion, or a quirk in the current where a submerged ridge turns the flow. Stay still. Let the brutal, flat-line thunder of the city press against your skull until your inner ear gives up trying to balance against it and goes numb. 

The distortion on the third step does not flicker with the water. The water parts over it—thinning to a dry, glassy membrane—while the blur itself slips four inches sideways against the grain of the current, reaches the sharp black edge of a colonnade shadow, and stops dead.

Right at the boundary between sun-glare and shade, something has volume. It is about the size of a crouched hound, or a boulder that never finished precipitating, and it is visible only because light passing through it comes out slightly late and angled wrong, showing you the wet basalt wall three yards to the left instead of the mossy riser directly behind it. Steam rising off the flooded paving hits its underside and flattens outward like smoke striking a glass ceiling.

Then it lets go of the step, slides *downhill* through an arcade, and simultaneously emerges from an upper window twenty feet overhead—not climbing, not jumping, simply occupying a seam in the broken geometry where the lintel of the alley and the sill of the second-story room are the exact same piece of space. For half a second, before its halves reconcile, you see it in two places at once: its rear flank trailing out of the fern-choked tunnel below while its forequarters already rest on the sun-baked parapet above, warm gutter-water braiding cleanly around both ends as if it were a continuous invisible pipe bent through the air.

The city is teeming with them. Once your eyes learn the trick of the refraction—that greasy, slightly bruised bend in the afternoon light, like flaw-lines in thick industrial glass—you realize the empty avenues are crowded. 

They have no skin, no mouths, no eyes, and no discrete insides. Nothing with blood or cellular walls could survive here; anything biological that wasn't already locked into the mineralized truce of the plants long ago would poach in ten minutes and have its soft tissues shaken apart from the inside by the acoustic pressure. What lives in the metropolis is native not to the mountain, and not even originally to the stone, but to the *fault*—to the violent, permanent marriage of forty-five-degree water, knotted space, and standing-wave sound.

Think of them as knots of acoustic tension dressed in hot mineral film. 

Inside the deafening, unvarying roar that fills the basin, there are trillions of microscopic phase-cancellations where sound bouncing off a vaulted ceiling meets sound rising from a flooded drain and locks into a rigid three-dimensional lattice. Over ages that have nothing to do with the pinned sun overhead, the supersaturated water—heavy with dissolved calcium, silica, and iron—has caught in those tiny pockets of stillness, organizing itself not into solid rock, which would simply drop out and become part of the floor, but into coherent, gelid standing structures: blister-packs of liquid crystal and heavy, sluggish vapor held together by the sheer vice-grip of the surrounding noise. If the roar ever dipped for a tenth of a second, every single one of them would collapse instantly into a splash of warm rusty water and a handful of yellow dust. Because the roar never dips, they are harder than cartilage and older than the gorges below.

They come in habits rather than species, shaped by whichever part of the municipal loop they haunt.

In the wide, sun-blasted switchbacks where the water runs shallow and fast, there are flat,broad *skimmers*—sheets of refractive tension twenty feet across and no thicker than a thumbnail, sliding upstream and downstream at once along the satin-worn gutters. You only know one is passing under your boots when the ankle-deep current abruptly ceases to wet you, parting around an invisible, rubbery carpet that hums at a pitch slightly sharper than the city's general thunder, making the bones of your shins itch furiously until it slides past and drops through a drain that empties off a roof three blocks upslope.

Hanging between the mineralized root-beams of the covered arcades are heavier, slower things—*pendulums* or *drapes*—that look like heat-mirages shaped into clustered organ pipes or heavy, slack bellies of clear resin. They anchor themselves across topological splices where air pressure differs slightly between one side of an archway and the other, letting the invisible draft pass through their pleat-folds. Inside their warped transparency, suspended grains of black basalt sand and yellow sinter spin in furious, silent little vortices, arranged into concentric rings like iron filings around a magnet. Those spinning rings of grit are the closest thing they have to organs: gyroscopes of mineral memory that tip and precess whenever the water flow shifts by a fraction of a millimeter.

And that brings you to what they are *doing*, and why the stairs are softened at the lip without crumbling, and why not a single dead leaf clogs any of the ten thousand drains.

Watch one of the hound-sized refractions—the *masons*, you might as well call them—settled over a cluster of those pinhead-sized, shell-backed limpets in a shaded street gutter. 

The limpets are the only true flesh-and-shell animals left in the basin, reduced to blind, calcified buttons that haven't moved an inch in centuries, filtering dissolved trace salts out of the hot slime. Left alone, even at their geological pace, their shells would eventually multiply, crust over the narrow runnel, dam the trickle by half an inch, and spill water onto a dry flagstone where liverwort isn't supposed to grow.

The mason sits directly over the cluster. Inside its warped, glassy bulk, it adjusts the angle of its internal planes, catching the city-wide roar and focusing it downward like a burning-glass focuses sunlight—except instead of concentrating heat, it concentrates *silence*, or its opposite. A tight, needle-thin cone of altered vibration touches the edge of an overgrown limpet shell. There is no crack, no violence. At the exact point of contact, calcium carbonate simply loses its grip on itself and dissolves directly back into clear, warm bicarbonate solution, washing away down the groove. 

Ten yards away—which is also half a mile across the chasm on the middle granite horn—another portion of the *same* mason's body is draped over the lip of a weathered window sill where millennia of runoff would otherwise have cut a V-shaped notch deep enough to drain the room behind it. There, the creature bends the standing wave the other way. Dissolved travertine drops instantly out of the water passing through its belly, plating onto the worn stone in a microscopic, greasy sheen, rebuilding the dish of the sill by three microns to match the exact curve it held before.

The city was never abandoned by its builders, and the builders did not make it for themselves and vanish. The city *is* the shell, the circulatory tract, and the acoustic resonator of the things sliding through its alleys, and they grew it around themselves the way a reef grows toward the surf—except their surf is topological, a knot in the high air where heat, water, and afternoon got snagged on three granite peaks and could not get loose. Every vaulted ceiling shaped like the palate of a mouth is shaped that way to bounce a specific frequency back into the courtyard below so the inhabitants don't come unglued. Every nonsensical staircase that leads downward onto a high roof was precipitated step by slumped, rounded step to guide warm water back over a sun-ledge before it could cool by half a degree and starve the philodendrons whose transpiring leaves regulate the humidity of the rooms behind them.

Even the flawless plants are tended with terrifying, suffocating precision. Look closely at the tip of one of those wrist-thick creeping rhizomes arrested at the edge of a shadow. It isn't dormant. In forty-five-degree wet heat under bright afternoon sun, that root is trying with every tropical cell it possesses to surge forward across the yellow sinter. It cannot, because a thin, shivering collar of heavy air—one of the smaller, ring-shaped inhabitants, no bigger than a bracelet—is clamped permanently around its growing tip, scrubbing away every new cell division the instant it forms and turning the sugars straight back into warm vapor and dissolved carbon, holding the green jungle frozen at the exact volume the architecture requires.

If you stay in a square too long, they notice the discrepancy.

You are loud in the wrong way—not louder than the city, nothing could be, but *muffled*. Your soft meat, your lungs full of trapped air, your clothes soaking up sweat and condensates: you are a walking dead-spot in the standing wave, an ugly, ragged hole torn right through the acoustic lattice that holds them together. Worse, your boots track tiny films of slime onto dry patches, damming micro-currents, casting moving shadows across liverwort beds where no shadow has moved since the mountain rose.

They do not attack. They have no concept of an enemy, an intruder, or a meal. To them, you are a slump in the masonry—a sudden, inexplicable landslide of soft, wet rubble that has fallen into the middle of an avenue and is messing up the acoustics.

First, the heat-shimmer at the corners of the square thickens. You don't see anything approach down the streets; instead, the empty air under the surrounding arcades grows dense and lens-bright as dozens of *masons* and wide, Ribbon-like *skimmers* slide laterally across the city's folded shortcuts to converge on the disturbance. The light around you begins to bend wildly. Looking across the courtyard, you see the same crimson pitcher-plant repeated four times at slightly different heights through overlapping panes of invisible, pulsing crystal; looking down at your own hands, you see the paving stones six feet behind your heels instead of your wrists.

The air grows harder to breathe—not thinner, just *stiffer*, as though the forty-five-degree steam is turning to warm gelatin in your throat. That is their margins overlapping around you, testing your edges, trying to read your geometry through the roar.

Your teeth stop humming all at once. 

Right around your head and chest, a pocket of sudden, uncanny quiet opens up as three large shapes—massive, slow-moving *vault-keepers* the size of carriages that normally live high in the ribbed ceilings of the dark interiors—drop through a doorway behind you and phase-cancel the thunder across your body to see what happens to your structure when the sound changes. In that weird, cotton-thick hush, you can suddenly hear your own heartbeat hammering in your ears, ragged and wet, and the tiny *plink* of water dripping off your chin.

Around your ankles, the runoff splits cleanly away from your boots, leaving you standing in a dry, steaming depression on the flooded yellow stone. Something with the weight and yield of hot, invisible vulcanized rubber presses gently against your shins, your ribs, the small of your back—not shoving, just *conforming*, taking a cast of the flaw in the plaza. Tiny, high-pitched needles of vibration prickle across the backs of your hands and the bridge of your nose as they test whether you are limestone that needs dissolving back into the gutter, or basalt that needs plating over with yellow travertine until your awkward, shifting angles are smoothed into a proper balustrade that sheds water cleanly toward the east.

Take ten paces backward through the nearest fern-choked doorway—which drops you, stumbling and gasping back into the full, skull-crushing roar, onto a narrow bridged chasm half a mile away between two sheer granite horns—and they do not follow. The square you left is instantly balanced again. Below or above you, visible across the dizzying brightness of the abyss where the salt-flat clouds lie miles down, the afternoon light falls plain and unremarkable on the empty courtyard, the water slides over the steps in its unbroken satin sheet, and nothing moves at all except the faint, oily bend of the light settling back into the stone.
