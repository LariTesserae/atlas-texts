You register their presence through your boots long before you catch one against the stars.

In a country where loose stone is always shifting, the ear—or rather the ankle bone and the shin—soon learns the grammar of gravity. A thermal gust skitters surface mica in a light, chaotic patter; a cooling dike snaps once like a dry twig; a distant collapse arrives through the sole as a thick, rolling smudge of vibration that fades at the edges. Set against those noises, a traveller on the ridge produces something unmistakably clean: a measured, two-beat cadence, *tick-tick, pause, tick-tick*, never dragging a heel, never starting a slide, moving horizontally across a slope where everything else only ever moves down.

Look toward the origin of that tapping and at first there is only the spur of dark plagiogranite and the starfields crowded behind it. Even when one stands thirty yards away on an open bench, no white plume of breath betrays it in the freezing upper air. Anything with wet lungs at thirty thousand feet would trail banners of instant frost; when one of these exhales, all that rises from the vents behind its collarbones is a colorless twist of heat-shimmer, briefly warping the stars like air above a hot stove.

Only when it shifts weight does its geometry separate from the broken rock. It has the folded, acute-angled build of something designed around levers rather than bulk. Folded down at rest, it fits easily beneath the yard-high crown of a slate bivouac; unfolded to step across a widening fissure, it reaches well past the height of a tall man, though it carries almost none of a man’s thickness. Heavy muscle would suffocate in this starved sky. Instead, the limbs are long, flat-sided spars of light, honeycombed bone worked by dry, dark cables of mineralized tendon that cross high at the joints for mechanical purchase, moving with the quiet, precise tension of calipers.

There is no skin on them soft enough to bleed or sweat. Moisture here would boil and freeze in the same breath, and jagged volcanic glass would ribbon hide in an afternoon. Their exterior is a close-fitted tessellation of matte, charcoal-gray plates—keratin heavily loaded with silica and iron—laid overlapping like slate shingles on a steep roof. Where the plates rub at the shoulders, hips, and flanks, they do not wear smooth; they cleave along microscopic planes, shedding tiny, dry, translucent scales like flakes of dark mica when the wind hits them. Their feet are wide and deeply cleft into two opposing pry-toes shod in thick blocks of layered horn. That horn grows downward continuously, shaved away curl by curl on the razor talus so that their soles are always freshly squared. The hands are heavier still: three broad, spatulate fingers opposed by a stiff heel-spur, the nails dense and beveled like cold chisels, scarred white at the tips from levering stone.

Up close, the head is narrow and wedge-shaped, nearly neckless, set low between high, bellows-like ribs that expand and contract in slow, deep strokes—pulling enormous volumes of thin sky through valved slits behind the jaw merely to glean its trace oxygen. There are no external ears; starved air carries too little rumor to bother catching. Instead, the lower border of the jawbone and the broad pads of the knuckles and heels are thickened into dense acoustic sounding-blocks. When one stops on a crest, it drops into a crouch and lays the flat of its wrist or the side of its chin directly against bare bedrock, closing not its eyes—the eyes have no wet lids—but drawing a dry, electrostatic film across two wide, dark, cabochon lenses that catch the permanent copper smear on the western horizon like polished obsidian. Pressed to the stone, it listens five miles down and ten miles out, sorting the deep, rhythmic magma-throb from the brittle *ping* of a fault locking tight on the next massif over.

They do not touch the ochre and soot-violet crusts along the old breaks. To them, once the mineral mold has bloomed across a fracture, the rock has already gone stale—its reactive surfaces spent, its best heat bleeding off into the dark. They live ahead of the mold, working the raw edge of the mountain’s arrival.

Watch one patrol the pale, two-hand-wide band at the foot of an active scarp where fresh crust is still grinding upward out of the dark. It moves nose-down, testing hairline cracks with the back of a knuckle for warmth, searching out the shimmering, invisible seepages of hot nitrogen, hydrogen, and trace sulfur leaking from the deep shear. When it finds a lively vent, it settles directly over the crack and lowers its chest until a broad, hinged plastron between its ribs parts slightly, exposing pleated internal gills of the same dull soot-violet as the rock-crust—a captive, symbiotic cousin of the exterior mold, kept warm and sheltered inside a sealed thoracic cavity, drinking the hot mantle-gases straight from the seam and turning raw chemical disequilibrium into slow metabolic fuel without losing a molecule of internal moisture.

While it breathes the vent, it feeds on the break itself. Where tectonic compression has bowed an outcrop of fresh, unoxidized gabbro or iron-rich slate until the stone literally hums under the hand, the creature selects a dense prism of basalt carried wedged in the hollow behind its hip, sets the point against a cleavage line, and strikes it once with the heavy heel of its palm. The pent-up stress of the mountain does the rest. The rock splits with a muffled, ringing snap, exposing a foot-wide facet that has never touched air—warm, pale, bristling with unspent iron bonds and fractured silicate radicals. Moving quickly before the thin sky can tarnish the face, it scrapes the powdery white fault-gouge and razor-sharp splinters straight off the break with the beveled edge of its lower jaw, swallowing them dry. Deep in its midsection, a muscular gizzard lined with volcanic glass grinds the fresh stone to flour, stripping every reactive electron from the raw iron before passing what remains. Under the lee of the low slate windbreaks, you sometimes find the only true dust on the entire range: neat, thimble-sized cones of completely oxidized, talcum-fine red powder, blown flat by the next thermal gust.

Because fresh breaks cool and oxidize within weeks, no single shelf can keep one alive for long, and no two can work the same ridge without starving each other of vents. They live miles apart across the black, jagged voids, perpetually nomadic across an architecture that refuses to hold still beneath them.

That instability is the whole reason for the masonry. They are not monuments; they are instruments.

In a world where ten thousand tons of mountainside can detach and drop into an unlit gulf with no louder warning in the air than the sigh of poured flour, falling asleep on an unmonitored spur is suicide. Before a buttress shears away, however, it swells and leans—sometimes for days, sometimes for six minutes—as deep compression buckles its root. Inside every low herringbone dome, therefore, sits the balanced needle of resonant basalt. When an inhabitant crawls inside a bivouac to fold itself into torpor over a warm crack, it first checks the needle, shifting a counterweight flake the size of a fingernail until the beam hangs hair-fine above its notched stone stop. Then it sleeps with one horn-shod heel resting firmly against the upright’s base. If the mountain tilts by half a degree in the dark, the needle dips and strikes the stop. In the thin air the click is barely audible, a tiny dry tack—but through the stone upright and into the sleeper’s heel bone it arrives like a hammer blow against an anvil, bringing the occupant out through the loose slate doorway in two silent bounds well before the ledge drops out from under it.

The cairns on the high spires serve the wider watch. Against a sky crowded with needle-sharp stars that rise and set on clean, predictable tracks, a tall, tapered stack of flat slate acts as a plumb line and a foresight. Crouched by a windbreak half a mile away, an inhabitant lines up the tip of a distant cairn against a known star-pair at the edge of the western copper glow. If the cairn has risen half a thumb’s breadth against the constellation since last ten-day, a plume is pushing that block upward; fresh, pale rock will be squeezing out along its base by tomorrow. If the cairn leans and begins spilling single stones off its shoulder into the quiet, that spire’s fault has gone rotten and will soon take the whole saddle down with it.

When a path breaks—sheared six feet vertically in the night where a fault jumped—the next one to come trotting along the pale ribbon of flipped talus does not hesitate long at the step. It lays its jaw against both sides of the fresh offset, listening to see whether the seam is still lively or locked shut. If the upper shelf still rings sound and solid, it wedges three or four massive slabs into the corner to build a rough, rattling stair up the fresh scarp, taking time along the way to scrape and swallow the pale fault-meal smeared along the jump. If the stone beyond the break hums hollow and unstable, signaling an overhang waiting to go, it turns back—walking carefully backward for three hundred yards and flipping every flat, pale path-stone dark-side up again with the toe of its foot, erasing the trail into the surrounding black talus so the next traveller crossing under starlight will not follow a dead road off an edge.

They rarely meet face to face, except along great newly opened rift-seams where heat and fresh iron are briefly abundant enough to draw three or four out of the surrounding dark at once, crouching twenty yards apart along the shimmering fissure, clicking their jaws softly against the rock in truce while they feed, or wedging dry, stone-shelled egg-capsules deep into warm side-cracks where rising sulfur will incubate them unattended. Yet across the black miles they are in constant, spare conversation.

When a major buttress begins to groan or a mile-long seam rips open in an unpeopled basin, an observer on a high crest will pick up a heavy cobblestone of dense gabbro and strike an exposed rib of unbroken bedrock—not loose scree, which scatters sound, but the living spine of the ridge itself. *Clack. Clack-clack. Pause. Clack.* In the air ten yards away, it sounds like nothing more than two pebbles knocked together under a wool coat. Down through the continuous plagiogranite root of the mountain, however, the sharp compression wave races beneath the mile-deep gulf at three miles a second and rises cleanly up the opposite wall, where another solitary mason pauses mid-stride, drops one palm flat to the warm stone, reads the azimuth, distance, and nature of the break through its wrist bones, and taps twice in answer. If a chasm is broken by too many shattered faults to carry a clean vibration, one will climb to the highest wind-scoured notch on its spur, pry up a broad, mirror-flat sheet of book-mica, and tilt it sharply back and forth—catching the permanent copper smear on the western horizon and throwing a tiny, unmistakable copper wink five miles across the starlit dark.

Nothing preys on them except geometry and time. Even the safest chemistries accumulate slag at last. Over decades of grinding raw plagiogranite and breathing sulfur plumes, unpurged iron oxides and insoluble silicates slowly deposit inside their joints and thicken their plates. A young one newly levered out of a warm fissure is pale as unweathered slate and supple as spring-steel, bounding silently across unstable scree slopes without disturbing a shard; an old one is dark rust-black, thick-plated, clicking stiffly at knee and elbow, no longer fast enough to work the violent lower scarps where fresh rock jumps fastest.

Those older ones retreat upward to the broadest, oldest saddles near the thirty-thousand-foot crests, where the rock moves more slowly and the air is thinnest of all. There they spend their last seasons tending the master cairns and resetting the finest basalt balances after minor swarms, flipping fallen flakes flat along the high crossroads for faster feet than theirs. When the mineral buildup finally locks a hip or seals the bellows of the ribs too stiffly to draw the upper gale, an old one does not wander off the trail to fall. It crawls into one of the low crouching-domes on a quiet spur, pulls the loose slate lintel-stones inward behind it to keep the wind out, and folds itself tight into the dark.

There is no decay afterward, because there is no moisture and no bacteria to rot anything. For a month its residual warmth fades until it matches the kiln-heat rising from the bedrock beneath it. Over seasons of micro-tremors and constant low-frequency hum, the dry cables of the tendons part cleanly without odor, and the silica-heavy plates separate one by one along their natural cleavage planes, settling neatly among the rattling herringbone courses of the floor. Years later, when a younger traveller pulls apart a slumped, abandoned shelter on a dead ridge to salvage flat slate for a new windbreak higher up, it will occasionally turn over a dark, ringing shard with the slight, unmistakable dish of a breastplate or the beveled, white-scarred chisel of a thumb—test its edge with a knuckle, find it sound, and wedge it straight back into the new wall on edge, loose enough to rattle and hold when the mountain rises again tonight.
