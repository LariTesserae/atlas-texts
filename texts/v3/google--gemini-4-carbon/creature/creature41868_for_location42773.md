Watch one of the builders closely when it pauses at a street corner, and the greyhound-sized silhouette resolves into something less like an animal and more like an instrument for measuring invisible forces. 

There is no head, not in any cephalized sense, because there is no single direction in which the creature primarily travels. Its six stilt-legs radiate from a central, flattened ring-thorax—a hexagonal hub of pale, foliated horn that looks remarkably like the mica-schist of the deeper plateau, split into thin, translucent leaves. The legs themselves are double-jointed cantilevers of hollow, chalky quill, trussed internally with criss-crossing struts just like the city’s towers. Where a foot meets a delicate foam-girder, it does not grip with claws, which would shatter the brittle silicate; instead, the tarsus flares into a dry, velvety pad of millions of microscopic spatulae. By shifting a faint electrostatic charge into that pad, the builder clings to sheer undersides of arches or walks vertically down a spire through dry adhesion alone, releasing just as instantly to step across a four-foot void without breaking stride.

Because the basin steals all sound and the slate-grey sky offers no directional light to cast shadows, the builders are completely blind and deaf. Eyes would require wet corneas, or at least polished lenses that the constant traffic of sharp silicate grit and flint-dust would frost into uselessness within days. Ears would find nothing to gather in the acoustic dead-zone of the lattice. 

Instead, rising from the upper rim of the ring-thorax is a delicate, branching crown of dark filaments, fine as blown carbon and stiffly held. As the builder trots down a boulevard, this crown quivers incessantly, reading the steep, shifting topography of the city’s electric field and the precise laminar shear of the thermal drafts. To the builder, the silent metropolis is blazingly loud with voltage and pressure. A collapsing colonnade three blocks upwind registers instantly as a sharp sag in local capacitance and a sudden, turbulent eddy in the air-stream; a drift of sulfur-spores pooling in a cellar vault shines in its perception like a warm, negatively charged beacon.

When two of them meet in that sunken octagonal plaza, they do not touch—at least not at first. Their papery carapaces build up immense static potentials as they stride through the dry drafts, and an unmediated brush of limb against limb would arc sharply enough to scorch chitin. Stopping two yards apart across the cone of recycled ash, each raises two opposing stilt-legs and extends a braided, coppery-looking grounding filament from under its central ring, touching the tip first to the basalt-anchored floor tiles to bleed off excess charge, and then extending it into the air between them. 

For three or four seconds, the filaments weave just millimeters apart without making contact. Faint, violet-white brush discharges—too small to see from the cliff rim—bridge the gap in rapid, staccato bursts. They are exchanging civic telemetry. In the complete absence of memory-heavy brains, which require rich, wet metabolic budgets to run, the builders hold the blueprint of the city collectively in these brief, crackling handshakes and in the physical geometry of the streets themselves. One passes along the exact barometric drop it felt in the northeast quadrant; the other imparts the depleted mineral ratio in its own resin glands. No leader directs the urban planning. Like termites building a ventilation chimney, yet operating at ten times the speed, they respond to local flaws in the airflow. If a street allows the wind to move too fast, stripping away valuable spore-dust rather than dropping it, the sheer electrostatic friction on a passing builder’s crown compels its spinnerets to tighten, automatically extruding a curved baffle-wall right there until the draft slows to the precise, soothing velocity their nerves prefer.

Look closer at the feeding one, its fluted proboscis buried to the hilt in the neat white cone of powdered tenements and trapped spores. It isn't sucking air with lungs. Its ring-thorax expands and contracts subtly like a dry concertina, lined inside with pleated, parchment-thin electrostatic plates. As it draws the powder up the static-lined flutes of its proboscis, those internal plates alternate polarity, snapping the heavy silicate flour into one dry digestive sac—where volatile organic halides wait in sealed, waxy ampoules to dissolve the grit back into foaming architectural resin—while shunting the lighter, sulfur-yellow spores and organic dust into a separate chamber. There, the creature runs a slow, waterless combustion, stripping electrons directly from the sulfur and iron compounds in the dust, exhaling a faint, warm puff of sharp-smelling carbonyl gas from vents beneath its leg-joints. 

Yet the twenty-odd builders are only the largest, most visible moving parts of this dry ecology. If you crouch at the rim—careful of the glass-sharp rock—and watch the drifts of sorted dust in the shadowed lower vaults, or the rust-red fan-lichens clinging to the piers, smaller and stranger tenants emerge from the stillness.

The city has parasites, and it has janitors, and it is difficult to tell where one role ends and the other begins.

Along the leeward underbellies of the older causeways, where the white mineral foam has begun to oxidize from crisp chalk to a yellowed, crumbly meal, there are patches that seem to crawl. These are the **shingle-moths**, though they never truly fly; flying in the tightly gridded, static-laced lattice would immediately slam a winged thing into a razor-edged strut. Flattened like dry pumpkin seeds and covered in overlapping scales of dull, translucent selenite, they move in slow, jerky glides along the trusses, riding thin cushions of repelling charge. Their mouthparts are tiny rotary rasps of hardened iron-sulfide. Silently, industriously, they graze on the city itself, chewing through load-bearing cross-beams that have lost their fresh, sharp charge. When that five-story colonnade buckled and dissolved mid-fall, it wasn't oxidized by the air alone; three hundred shingle-moths had rasped its base struts paper-thin, riding the resulting silent avalanche down into the boulevards like surfers on a wave of white flour, vanishing into the crevices of the slate floor before a builder’s stilt-leg could spear them.

The builders will kill a shingle-moth if their sensory crowns catch the telltale buzz of its rasping static—impaling the flat creature with a swift, downward Piston-strike of a foreleg and Vacuuming the dry crumbs of its body straight up the proboscis—yet without the moths, the dead sectors of the city would linger too long, clogging the basin with stale, uncharged geometry and starving the central plazas of recycled building ash.

Then there is what lives inside the rust-red fan-lichens.

Those metallic-looking fronds snapping open and shut along the basalt margins are not plants in any true, sun-drinking sense; the permanent grey lid of the sky leaks far too little radiation into the deep slot of the pit to power photosynthesis. They are mechanical traps built by sessile colonies of microscopic, silicate-shelled polyps—dry coral, effectively—cemented into the conchoidal cracks of the volcanic glass. Each "frond" is a spring-loaded sheet of dead, keratinous alloy secreted by thousands of tiny zooids clustered at its base, coiled under immense tension as it dries in the parching air. 

As the city’s tall spires comb static out of the upper wind and funnel charged rivers of dust down the avenues, the metallic scales build up an opposing charge until a passing gust of heavy spore-powder triggers a hair-spring release at the hinge. *Snap*—though of course there is no *snap*, only a blur of rust-red motion—the fan claps shut against a neighboring frond, sandwiching the trapped dust directly over the mouths of the basal polyps, which slowly crank the metallic leaf back open over the next ten minutes using tiny ratchets of dry chitin-fiber. 

They fold flat when a builder approaches not out of fear of being eaten—their metallic scales are foul with bitter, astringent alum salts that would ruin the foaming chemistry of a builder’s resin—but because a passing builder carries enough ambient static in its six-legged frame to trip every spring-hinge in a ten-foot radius at once, wasting an hour of patient ratcheting on empty air.

And deeper still, down among the raw, un-weathered shards of black talus underneath the lowest hexagonal floor-tiles of the city—where the chalk-white foundation piers grip the native rock—lives the twenty-fifth builder, or what used to be one, or what will soon become three.

The builders do not mate, nor do they lay eggs that could desiccate in the flinty draft. Because their bodies are so sparse, so mineral, and so modular, wear takes them piece by piece. A stilt-leg splinters on a sharp basalt edge; an internal electrostatic plate fouls with insoluble heavy-metal slag from the volcanic grit and loses its capacity to hold a charge; spinnerets clog with prematurely cured stone. 

Down in a wide, unlit cellar beneath the central plaza—a vaulted room whose ceiling is a dense, sound-deadening sponge of cross-hatched struts—an aged builder walks slowly into a shallow pit of rich, sulfur-yellow spore-drift and locks all six of its legs into final, rigid tripods, anchoring its velvet pads permanently to the slate. Its sensory crown droops and brittle-fractures off. Over hours, its ring-thorax splits neatly along its six radial seams like a dry seed-pod popping in drought.

Two younger builders are already down there in the dimness, attending the husk. With delicate, surgical precision, they use their fluted proboscises and narrow fore-tarsi not to consume their predecessor, but to salvage and graft. The old builder’s still-viable organs—sealed waxy ampoules of solvent, bundles of braided copper-colored grounding nerve, dense calcified ganglia that carry the deep instinct for hexagonal angles—are lifted cleanly from the split mica-shell, dry and bloodless as clockwork parts. 

Beside the husk stand two smaller, half-formed radial frames, scarcely the size of hare-skeletons, spun earlier that day out of a specialized, flexible amber-colored foam and braced in temporary scaffolding of white silicate. Working in absolute, breathless silence, illuminated only by the occasional cold violet spark jumping from their tweezers-like tarsal tips as they splice electrostatic nerves together, the attendants slot the salvaged organs into the fresh frames, seal the seams with quick-flashing resin, and snip away the white scaffolding.

For a minute, the new, miniature builders stand inert on spindle-thin legs. Then a highspire forty yards overhead catches a heavy gust spilling off the plateau rim; a pale purple corona races noiselessly down the tower’s spine, straight through the plaza floor and into the grounding piers of the vault. The miniature crowns of carbon filaments snap upright, quivering as the voltage hits them. Their concertina thoraxes give a first, dry hitch, drawing in a shallow draught of yellow spore-dust. 

Without a pause for orientation, without a tremor of hesitation, the small new things step out of their nursery scaffolding, walk straight up the vertical wall of the vault on static-charged pads, emerge through a floor-grate into the shadowless twilight of the plaza, and immediately press tiny spinnerets to a chipped curbstone—extruding their first hair-thin threads of white, silent city into the dry wind.
