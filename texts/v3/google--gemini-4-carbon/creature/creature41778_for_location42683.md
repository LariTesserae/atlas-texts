Out in the middle of the tin-colored water, where the mist thickens until sky and slate merge into a single wet lung, a shape appears. At first it looks like one of the upright way-stones, except that it leans against the wind and moves. 

They walk with a peculiar, skating shuffle. You do not lift your feet in the basin. To lift a foot onto slick bacterial grease over polished granite is to fall, and to fall hard on unyielding stone a mile from your cairn with a cracked hip is to become meat for the ash-winged things before dusk deepens into night. So the people of the pan keep their soles flat, sliding forward from the hip in a low, wide-kneed glide, testing the slime before committing their weight.

Their boots are masterpieces of ugly necessity. The uppers are stitched from the tough, pebbled hide of the six-legged hounds, cured in urine and greased until black, drawn tight up to the knee with raw tendon. The soles are wide platforms of braided cartilage, layered thick, with shards of shattered quartz and rough iron slag pounded directly into the bottom fibers so they bite through the ochre slime like coarse files. Even in the deafening roar of the basin, if you put your ear down to the inch-deep water, you could feel the distinct *crunch-slide, crunch-slide* of a person approaching long before they broke through the fog.

They are built like stumps. Whether they began as ordinary men and women generations ago or were always an offshoot from some older, broader branch, the basin has planed them down just as it planed the mountains. They are short, barrel-chested, and immensely wide through the pelvis and shoulders. Long limbs lose heat; long bones snap when a shell-back clamps down on a careless wrist. Everything about them is gathered inward toward the furnace of the gut. Beneath skin the color of tallow and old pewter lies a dense, continuous rind of insulating fat, fed by a diet that is almost entirely protein and oil. 

From a distance, bundled in their slickers, they barely look mammalian. Over thick undergarments of softened gut-skin, they wear overlapping capes shingled with the dark, horn-like plates of juvenile grazers—hundreds of them, drilled through the apex and laced together with sinew so the wearer resembles a walking pine-cone of black armor. The plates shed the condensing mist and turn the snapping jaws of the six-legged predators if one grows bold in the fog. Every seam, every strap, and every inch of exposed skin on their broad, flat faces is smeared with a heavy paste of rendered fat mixed with pulverized slate dust. It cuts the windburn, stops the constant damp from rotting their pores, and gives them the dull, metallic gleam of wet lead. They smell profoundly, overwhelmingly, of rancid oil, iron-rich mud, and cold smoke.

Watch one at work on the margin of a grazing shoal. 

She carries her pry-bar across both forearms—four feet of dense coastal whale-rib tipped with a wedge of cold-hammered bog iron, bound so tightly with wet-shrunk leather that bone and metal have practically fused. She does not stalk the dinner-plate grazers; they are blind to her, and nothing could hear her over the *shhhk-shhhk* of ten million silica teeth and the bronze-anvil hammering of the flute-skulls on the whalebacks behind her. Still, she moves with slow, deliberate economy, conserving heat, watching the water-film for the V-shaped ripples that mean a pack of six-legged hounds is working the same shoal.

She picks a grazer near the edge—never the middle, where the footing is thickest with slime—and drops her center of gravity until her shingled cape trails in the grey sheet of water. She doesn't jab. If you touch a shell-back clumsily, its muscular foot locks onto the seamless granite with a vacuum seal that will snap a bone shaft before it yields. Instead, she sets the iron tip down a hand's-breadth from the creature’s rim, waits for the rhythmic, rasping lurch as it drags itself forward to take another bite of bacterial crust, and drives her whole thick torso downward onto the butt of the lever in that split-second before the suction resets.

*Pop.* You cannot hear it, even standing three feet away. You only see the grey water jump around her ankles as the seal breaks, and then the rapid, practiced flick of her wrists that leaves the creature upside down on the slate, its pale, rubbery foot curling helplessly in the cold air. Before she stands, she takes a short bone spike from her belt and drives it once through the nerve-knot behind its rasping plates, threads a loop of braided gut through the hole already worn in the edge of its old shell, and drops it onto a flat sled—nothing more than the single, giant shoulder-blade of some dead sea-beast, pulled behind her waist by a leather harness. When the sled holds twenty, dragging smoothly over the greased rock on a cushion of inch-deep water, she turns toward the drone of the wind-stones.

To understand what they are when the armor comes off, you have to crawl inside one of the cairn-huts.

You go in on your belly through a crooked, ten-foot stone tunnel sloped slightly upward so the runoff stays out and the warmth stays in. Halfway through, you push past two heavy curtains of greased hide. When the second curtain drops behind you, the most shocking thing in the world happens: the noise stops.

Not entirely. It never stops entirely. Through three feet of tightly wedged slate, chinked clay, and packed fat, the shriek of the basin becomes a deep, oceanic hum that lives inside your sternum, like sleeping inside the hull of an iron ship. After twelve hours out on the stone dish, the sudden drop in volume makes the ears ring violently.

Inside, there is barely room for four people to sit upright without their scalps brushing the corbeled stone roof, slick with soot. The floor is paved with flat cobbles raised above a sump-pit, carpeted five layers deep with cured hound-pelts, spongy dried bacterial mats peeled in thick sheets during rare dry winds, and hollow bird bones arranged beneath to break the chill of the bedrock. 

In the center sits the lamp—a naturally cupped boulder of basalt filled with clear, yellow oil rendered from the livers of the six-legged hounds and the fat pads of the flute-skulls. There is no wood, no peat, no dung that burns clean. For wicks, they use twisted ropes of shed cartilage soaked in grease and dusted with sulfur scraped from the yellow bacterial crusts. It burns with a low, blue-orange, sputtering flame that gives off intense heat for its size and smells sharply of matches and roasting meat. Over it, suspended from a tripod of coastal ribs wedged into the walls, hangs a flat, soapstone griddle where strips of dark shell-meat sizzle in their own liquor.

Here, stripped to the waist in the close, stifling warmth, you can finally see their faces.

Their eyes are narrow—not just folded at the corners against the wind, but protected by heavy, cartilaginous ridges at the brow and cheekbone, pale eyelashes clumped with grease, the irises almost universally a washed-out, mineral grey or very dark brown. Most striking are their ears, or what is left of them. A child born in the basin hears the world cleanly for perhaps five years. By ten, the relentless, amplified *clang-clang-clang* of the tubular callers and the glacier-roar of the shoals has thickened the eardrums into scar tissue. By thirty, nearly every adult in the basin is profoundly deaf to any pitch above a low rumble. Even in the muffled quiet of the cairn, they do not speak with their throats.

Or rather, they don't use words. They hum—deep, chest-rattling vibrations made with closed lips, shifting pitch slightly to show mood, pain, satisfaction, or warning. When a mother pulls a child against her greased ribs to warm its feet, she rumbles like a distant rockslide, so the child feels the comfort through its cheek against her breastbone. 

Real talk happens in the hands and on the skin. 

Watch three cairn-mates sitting knees-to-knees around the sputtering sulfur-lamp. While one uses a piece of coarse sandstone to put a fresh bevel on an iron pry-wedge, the other two converse. Their short, thick-knuckled fingers—scarred white from shell-pinches and stone-slips—move in a rapid, fluid shorthand, catching the dim orange light. When the lamp burns low, or when they are exhausted and lying tangled together under the heavy hides for warmth, the language moves onto the body itself: taps, drags, and firm squeezes along the forearm, collarbone, and shin. Every cairn-group has calluses on the insides of their wrists solely from decades of being spoken to in the dark.

Out on the pan, where hands are muffled in thick, thumbless mittens of doubled leather and the mist swallows sight at twenty paces, they speak through the stone and through their tools. Two hunters flanking a hound-pack will strike the butts of their bone levers sharply against the granite—*clack, clack-clack*—feeling the shockwave travel cleanly through the seamless rock and up through the quartz-studded soles of each other's boots. And around their necks hang flat whistles carved from the hollow, double-valved skulls of the blind callers themselves, ground down so that instead of a high metallic shriek, a hard blow produces a flat, sub-audible *thump* of air you catch in the pit of your stomach from fifty yards away.

They live sparse, solitary lives—two, three, rarely five people to a whaleback ridge, spaced half a mile apart because a grazing shoal moves unpredictably, and too many mouths on one hump of quartz means walking three miles into the grey soup just to fill a sled. Yet they are bound together by a quiet, fanatical maintenance of the pan.

Nobody owns a cairn forever, and nobody owns the weirs alone. If you follow a woman emptying a cartilage basket-trap at the point of a knee-high cobble wall—scooping double-handfuls of sluggish, blind white water-worms and rich mineral silt into a gut-sack—she will spend half an hour afterward resetting seven stones knocked crooked by a lunging predator, even if that wing of the weir feeds her neighbor's trap half a mile east. And before turning home, she will walk out into the thickening mist to the nearest bored slate slab. She will pull a twist of tough sinew-cord and a lump of abrasive quartz grit from her pouch, thread it through the fist-sized wind-holes, and saw back and forth for ten minutes, clearing out the fast-growing film of black slime and clustered tiny juveniles that constantly threaten to choke the stone's throat. 

To let a wind-stone go mute is the closest thing these people have to an unforgivable sin. Without that low, hollow *hooooooo* cutting underneath the clattering madness of the valley, a shift in the north wind turns the seamless basin into a trap without landmarks, gradients, or stars—just twelve miles of greased tin tilting toward the sea, where you skate in wide, slow circles until your fat burns away and the cold gets inside your armor.

Twice a year, though, they leave the basin's center and follow the water east, down the imperceptible tilt over three days of sliding travel, until the bronze shrieking behind them fades under a deeper, heavier thunder: the unseen sea crashing against the lip where the spoon of bedrock finally drops off into deep water. There, on beaches of black shingle littered with wrack, wash-up timber too precious and waterlogged to burn, and the carcasses of immense, blubbery marine beasts, the scattered cairn-families gather. Fifty or sixty of them at once, emerging from the mist like shining, armored beetles. 

For ten days on the coast, they gorge on salt kelp to stave off the rot that comes from eating nothing but grey meat and sulfur-slime. They trade bog-iron nodules dredged from western sump-pools for the long, curved ribs needed to frame drying racks and pry-bars. Young people from distant whalebacks—who have only ever known three faces besides their own—sit in circles on the wet gravel, strip off their heavy mittens, and talk with frantic, clumsy, eager hands on unfamiliar wrists, learning the slight dialects of touch from the far side of the pan before pairing off to build or inherit an empty cairn.

And when one of them dies back up in the basin—when an old man’s lungs finally fill with water from decades of breathing cold mist and sulfur-smoke, or a hunter takes a hound’s crushing jaw across the thigh and bleeds dark into the inch-deep sheet—there is no soil to bury them, and no fire hot enough to turn them to ash. 

They do not pile stones over their dead; stones are for the living, for weirs and roofs. Instead, the cairn-mates strip the body bare, wash the slate-grease from the pale, thick skin with clean water collected directly off the hide roof, and carry their dead out onto an empty stretch of shining slate between the shoals. They lay them flat on the seamless rock. With heavy chisels, they score a shallow groove in the stone around the body just deep enough to split the runoff sideways, keeping the corpse from sliding eastward in a heavy mist. 

Then they retreat to the whaleback ridge and leave the basin to do its work. First come the wide-winged scavengers dropping out of the bruised pewter clouds like wet ash; then the six-legged hounds trotting silently through the tin-colored film; and finally, days later, when only white bone remains gleaming in the dim dusk, a shifting shoal of dinner-plate grazers drifts slowly over the spot. Millions of rasping silica teeth grind across the ribs and skull—*shhhk-shhhk, shhhk-shhhk*—scraping away calcium and marrow until even the bones are planed flat and gone, turned into shell and sound, leaving the foundational stone once again completely smooth.
