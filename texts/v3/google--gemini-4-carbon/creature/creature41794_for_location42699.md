To understand what lives in the caldera, you have to stop looking at the loaf-sized masons, the twig-limbed cellar-tenders, and the turquoise mites as neighbors sharing a city, and begin to look at the grease they pass from mouth to mouth.

Nothing here drinks, because there is nothing to drink. Life runs on an economy of dense, non-volatile hydrocarbons—waxes, turpentines, liquid paraffins, and heavy lipids distilled by the copper vines from volcanic carbon dioxide, trace sulfur gases, and dry geothermal heat. Because lipids do not evaporate, the pit is a closed, lossless circuit of slow-burning fuel. And because these heavy oils carry complex, lingering chemical molecules far better than water ever could, the town’s plumbing is also its nervous system.

Take the masons first—the heavy, velvet-plated architects. If you lift one from the wall (which requires peeling its footpads sideways, like lifting heavy tape from glass, before the millions of dry micro-bristles let go), you find its underside is pale, soft, and pleated like the gills of a mushroom. It has no lungs and no blood vessels to speak of. Instead, its interior is a dense, spongy matrix of fat-bodies and grinding gizzards lined with black trachyte grit it swallowed as a larva. 

A mason does not eat to live in the frantic, furnace-stoking way of warm-blooded things. A single meal of slate-green leaf-wax sustains one for a season. Most of its energy goes into chemistry. Inside its gut, raw volcanic dust—sharp-edged pumice glass and iron-rich basalt—is tumbled with plant balsams and specialized, waxy enzymes secreted by its stomach lining. What emerges from its wide, trowel-shaped lower mandible is an anhydrous geopolymers: stone-paste that cross-links and vitrifies in ultraviolet light.

Why do they build? Not for shelter from rain that never falls, nor from predators that have never crossed the barren plateau outside. They build to manage heat and static.

The masons are exquisitely sensitive to temperature gradients of a tenth of a degree. If the pit were left empty, the black rock would bake to lethal temperatures at noon and plunge below freezing under the thin, clear night sky, cracking the thick oils in their bodies into solid tallow. Every ribbed vault, hexagonal chamber, and cantilevered plaza is a radiator and a heat-sink. When two masons spend ten minutes rotating around each other on a narrow viaduct, brushing their saffron antennae across one another's velvety plates, they are not merely greeting one another. Their matte shells hold localized static charges generated by the dry mountain air and the constant friction of moving mites; their shells are also coated in thin smears of ester-rich wax picked up from the walls they just touched. By tasting the static pattern and the oxidation rate of the wax on its neighbor’s back, a mason reads the thermal map of the entire sector—where an arcade is cooling too quickly, where a volcanic hairline vent has shifted its warm sulfur sigh two inches to the left. Neither creature decides to build an arch. Simply, after the encounter, one of them has a slightly altered chemical balance in its foregut, and when it next encounters a drafty gap where the static charge spikes, its mandibles automatically unclench and begin laying down a ribbon of frothy porcelain to baffle the air.

Then there are the cellar-keepers in the shaded undercrofts—the pale, twig-limbed things tending the spherical vats of golden grease.

Look closer in the amber half-light of the vaults, and you realize they are not a separate species from the masons at all. At the very bottom of the pit, where the sulfur vents are warmest and the oxygen slightly thinner, certain hexagonal nurseries are sealed with dark, basalt-heavy mortar that blocks all sunlight. Eggs laid in the upper, sunlit terraces hatch into wide, flat, UV-hardened masons. Eggs carried down into the warm, dark, high-sulfur vaults—and fed exclusively on refined, light-fraction turpentines rather than coarse leaf-wax—stretch out as they molt. Their shells never harden or grow velvet; they remain translucent, flexible, and amber-pale, their six limbs elongating into multi-jointed stilts tipped with delicate, spoon-like claws for skimming and ladling.

They are the city’s pharmacists and distillers. The copper vines and violet canopies pump out raw, crude lipids full of toxic alkaloids meant to deter chewing. The masons can stomach small amounts of this raw wax, using the toxins as resin-hardeners in their mortar, but the city as a whole cannot digest it raw. So the masons scrape crude balsam from cracked fronds, roll it into dusty pellets, and drop them down smooth, vertical chutes built into the masonry. 

Down in the undercrofts, the pale, spindly sisters catch the pellets and pack them into the leaf-leather vats—which are actually the cured, hollowed-out seed bladders of an older, deeper-rooted lithophyte. Into each vat, the keepers regurgitate a starter culture of dry, lipophilic yeasts and sulfur-eating bacteria that live nowhere else on the plateau. Over months, in the steady ninety-degree warmth of the lower vaults, this culture cracks the heavy, toxic plant waxes into digestible golden grease, sweet-smelling esters, and volatile solvents. 

The keepers never see the sky. Their eyes are reduced to tiny, heat-sensing pits that see the vats glowing softly in the infrared as the yeasts ferment the oil. They move with a slow, spider-like grace, uncapping a bladder that has grown too warm, stirring its thick contents with a long forelimb to release a silent shimmer of turpentine vapor, then capping it again with a disk of chewed wax. When a batch is ripe, they load small resin-glazed pots—actually the shed, cupped kneecaps of dead masons—and pass them hand-over-hand up the vertical shafts toward the light.

And what of the sandstone-beaded lizards draped like heavy ropes across the sunny vines, and the rivers of turquoise mites flowing over them? They look like opportunistic squatters in the arthropods’ town, vertebrates and arachnids taking advantage of the warmth. In truth, the town would choke to death inside a year without them.

In a world without rain, dust is lethal. Outside the rim, thin winds scour the plateau and drop a constant, invisible sifting of fine mineral talc and volcanic ash down into the windless bowl of the pit. Because the plants are sticky with wax and the walls are charged with static electricity, every surface wants to trap dust. Left alone, the slate-green canopies would soon be blinded under a gray crust, unable to drink the fierce mountain sunlight, and the molecular-adhesion footpads of the masons would clog and lose their grip, sending them tumbling silently into the chasms.

The turquoise mites are the city’s wipers and salt-miners. They do not eat the plants; they graze the boundary layer between the plant and the air. Their mouthparts are microscopic scrapers and electrostatic brushes. Flowing in millions up a sunlit tower, a river of mites strips every speck of windblown plateau-dust, every crystal of excess sulfur-salt sweated out by the lithophytes, and every flake of oxidized wax from the leaves and walls. As they march, they bind this sweepings-grit with a drop of dry oil from their own bodies and pass it backward through the swarm, rolling microscopic pellets together until, at the edges of the swarm, tiny blue-gray beads of clean, bound mineral dust drop onto the stepped plazas—ready-made aggregate for the masons to scoop up and chew into tomorrow’s mortar.

The lizards—broad, flat-ribbed skinks whose ancestors wandered across the plateau before the dust dried completely, and who have forgotten water so thoroughly their kidneys excrete only dry, chalky pearls of pure uric acid—are the thermal ballast and the heavy transport of this economy.

An invertebrate the size of a loaf of bread cools down quickly when the shadow of the rim hits it; a dense, oil-fat reptile three feet long holds noon’s heat well into the night. Watch what happens an hour after dusk, when the slate-green canopies have hinged shut to roof over the streets and the outside plateau drops below zero. The lizards do not stay up on the exposed copper vines. Slowly, with that faint chalk-on-slate *shhhk*, dozens of them slither down through wide, smooth-lipped portals built specifically to the width of their flattened ribs, descending into the mid-level nurseries and yeast-undercrofts.

The masons and the pale keepers do not flee them. As a warm lizard slides into a dark chamber and coils itself around a cluster of fermenting grease-vats or a brood-comb of soft eggs, the pale keepers gather around it, resting their twig-like limbs against its beaded flanks to soak up the banked solar heat radiating off its scales. In return, a keeper reaches out with a spoon-clawed foot and places a dollop of sweet, fermented golden grease directly onto the lizard’s snout. A thick, dark purple tongue—sticky with lipid-saliva rather than wet mucus—rolls out, takes the payment without a sound, and retracts. 

By midnight, when the stars above the jagged trachyte rim are hard, unblinking points in thin air, the pit is sealed tight as a waxed jar. Up near the surface, motionless masons plug the ventilation shafts with the exact width of their insulated shells. Beneath the closed leaf-roofs, coiled lizards bleed the afternoon’s hoarded sunlight slowly into the porcelain walls, keeping the yeast alive and the plant-oils liquid through the freeze.

Even death does not leave this circuit; it merely becomes furniture, then architecture, then history.

Because nothing rots, nothing smells of carrion. When an old lizard’s heart finally stops on a sunny copper vine, it does not bloat or draw flies. Over weeks, the turquoise mites strip every trace of surface oil and loose scale-dust from it. The pale cellar-keepers climb up at twilight—the only time they ever venture near the middle tiers—and carefully harvest the rich fat-pads from its tail to feed the deep vats. What remains cures in the dry, turpentine-scented heat into a hollow, translucent sculpture of amber tendon and sandstone leather, still gripping the vine. Eventually, a mason encounters the stiffened body while extending a parapet. Without hesitation, it lays a ribbon of pale gray pumice-paste across the dry ribs, anchoring a cross-beam to the spine, turning the reptile’s ribcage into a vaulted skylight for a niche below.

If you could slice the caldera cleanly in half from rim to floor, you would see that only the top six feet of this stepped metropolis are inhabited in the way an outsider understands habitation. Below the bustling sunlit plazas and the warm, grease-scented undercrofts lies eighty feet of solid, dry, honeycombed memory. 

Down in the oldest strata near the talus floor, sealed behind unbroken walls of porcelain, are the mummified husks of the pioneer weeds that first caught windblown spores in the warm volcanic cracks eighty years ago; the hollow, parchment-thin shells of the first dozen masons who crawled across miles of killing dust following a plume of warm sulfur; the petrified stems of dead vines still threading through rooms no creature has entered in half a century, now serving only as tension-rods holding the upper city against its own weight. 

Even down there, in total, breathless dark, the city is not quite inert. Hairline vents in the young volcanic rock still breathe warm static and trace gases upward through the buried carapaces and dry chambers, humming through a million hexagonal cells at a frequency far below hearing—warming the floors above, feeding the roots of the copper vines, and carrying the faint, cured scent of the grandparents up through the mortar so the masons on the highest parapets know, through the soles of their velvet feet, the exact shape of the foundation they are building upon.
