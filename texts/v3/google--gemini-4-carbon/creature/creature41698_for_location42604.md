At first, the ecology appears to have no top. It looks like a flat plateau of grazing and sifting—biofilm to slug, slug to wader—with nothing above the waders to trouble their stillness. 

If you sit on the warm curve of that condenser drum for six or seven hours, letting your own breathing slow to match the sixty-cycle hum in your tailbone, you begin to see the problem with that assumption. A cartilage-wader down in the flooded alley will suddenly tip forward. Nothing struck it. It simply reached the quiet statistical end of its tether—a valve in its narrow, two-chambered heart stiffened, or its blood grew too thick with precipitated minerals—and down it goes with a dull, leathery slap into the ankle-deep warm water. 

In a world packed this tightly with wet, eighty-five-degree flesh, a twenty-pound carcass ought to foul a basin in half a day. Yet the water around your boots is as clear as gin. There is no stench of rot anywhere in the hazy air, only the clean, mineral tang of hot copper and wet iron.

Watch the fallen wader for twenty minutes. The neighboring waders do not step back; they merely shift their splayed, cartilaginous toes an inch to accommodate the lump against their ankles. The ribbon-eels beneath it wriggle out from under the dead weight, unhurried. Then, from the bolted inspection hatch of a dry-well six yards away, something unspools.

At first you mistake it for a trickle of heavy bunker oil leaking from a blown gasket. It is flat, glossy dark-amber, perhaps eight inches wide and no thicker than a heavy wool blanket, hugging the diamond-plate so closely that the raised lozenges of the steel print right through its back as it moves. Behind the leading tongue comes more of it—ten feet, twenty feet, thirty—pouring silently down the gusset plate and sliding *under* the water without breaking the surface tension. It has no head, only a blunt, slightly paler margin lined with thousands of minute, brass-colored sensory cilia that tap-tap-tap against the rivets as it reads the thermal and chemical gradient of the deck.

These are the Sump-Sheets, though if you followed one back into the labyrinth of unlit maintenance plenums beneath the floor, you would realize the ribbon you are looking at is merely an exploratory pseudopod—a single finger of a colossal, reticulated animal wedged across half an acre of subterranean I-beams. Down in the dark between the decks, where the main return pipes radiate a hundred and ten degrees, the core body rests like a vast, pleated lung of translucent tallow, anchored around the structural columns. Up here in the bright glare, it sends out flat, muscular runners through weep-holes and cable-chases whenever the water carries the faint, sour amino signature of stopped blood.

The dark-amber sheet flows up and over the dead wader like warm caramel poured over a stone. It seals its margins tight against the surrounding iron, creating a gasketed digestion chamber right there on the floor. If you climb down and lay your bare hand on the slight mound it makes, it feels like oiled chamois leather stretched over slowly kneading knuckles. Beneath that seal, it floods the carcass with weak, sulfuric stomach-acids refined from the cooling-weir runoff, reducing hide, meat, and flexible cartilage to a clear broth in four hours. It drinks the slurry directly through its ventral pores, lifts its margins, and retracts backward into the inspection hatch, leaving behind nothing on the diamond-plate except a clean, chalk-white heap of the wader’s heaviest calcium leg-bones and the two halves of its keratin bill.

Even those do not last the week.

Look closely at the fist-sized rivets holding the terrace seams together, and you will notice that some of the rivets are slightly out of line. Touch one with the toe of your boot, and it pivots. 

It isn’t iron; it is a dense, matte-grey shell compounded of sintered calcium and chewed scale-rust, sculpted by blind evolutionary mimicry—or simply by the geometry of turbulent water flow—into the exact hemispherical dome of a structural bolt-head. Beneath that cap sits a Bone-Limpet, a fist of tough, pale-yellow muscle armed with a radula whose microscopic teeth are mineralized with magnetite. As soon as the Sump-Sheet withdraws, six or seven of these false rivets detach from the nearby bulkhead seams with tiny, wet *pops* of broken suction and glide imperceptibly over the wet steel toward the white pile. They clamp down over the clean femurs and bills. Over the next forty hours, you can hear them if you press your ear to a nearby stanchion: a faint, rhythmic *scritch-pause-scritch* conducted through the metal floor, like a tiny file working brass in another room, as they rasp the bones down to paste to build their own heavy domes and return the excess lime to the water.

Once you learn to spot the impostors, the young steel of the continent begins to look very different. Half the hardware isn't hardware.

Walk over to a bundle of thumb-thick copper instrument lines strapped along a catwalk railing. Run your fingers down them. Four of the tubes are hard, sweating metal, humming faintly with control-current. The fifth yields slightly, warm and rubbery as a gorged vein, and if you pinch it, a slow peristaltic ripple travels ten yards down its length and disappears around a ninety-degree elbow joint. 

These are Conduit-Worms—tubular, sessile filter-feeders that secrete a flexible, verdigris-tinted cuticular sheath cemented directly into the pipe-clamps alongside the real plumbing. At every flange-gap, where two sections of genuine brass pipe meet, the worm flares out a delicate, papery collar that mimics an asbestos gasket. In reality, that collar is a gill-net of feathery, rust-colored plumes held directly beneath the steady drip of an overhead condensation seam, catching airborne scale-mite husks, drifting bacteria, and microscopic droplets of aerosolized machine oil. They do not grow randomly across the catwalks; they grow strictly parallel to existing pipe-runs, guided by the magnetic fields of the adjacent electrical lines, keeping the architecture neat even as they upholster it in guts.

You begin to realize that the pristine youth of the steel is not an accident of time. The machine is not three years old. It might be thirty thousand years old, and the fauna is why the lathe-marks are still sharp.

Look at the faint, pale-orange blooms of oxidation along the weld seams. On an abandoned ship at sea, rust swells, flakes, traps salt water, and eats pit-craters straight through quarter-inch plate in a generation. Here, the moment an iron molecule oxidizes into ferric hydroxide, it becomes food. Tiny, flat-bodied creatures no bigger than lentil seeds—creatures shaped like miniature trilobites stamped out of translucent mica—spend their entire lives grazing exclusively along the weld-beads. They have specialized, scraper-lined mouths that plane away hydrated iron oxide down to the bare, bright steel without ever scratching the underlying crystalline metal, because bare steel is too hard for their chitinous teeth and rust is just soft enough. They excrete tiny, inert pellets of pure, reduced black magnetite sand, which wash down through the bronze floor-grates into the deep sumps below. Every loaf-sized gastropod on the steam jackets is polishing brass with weak oxalic mucus; every matted sheet of black biofilm is acting as an sacrificial anode, intercepting free oxygen before it can bite into the submerged grates. The ecosystem is a living, self-replicating maintenance crew that keeps the continent looking like it rolled off the milling machine last Tuesday.

Which raises the question of what happens when the ecosystem itself gets in the machine’s way.

Why haven't the Conduit-Worms choked the bronze floor-grates? Why haven't the millions of primer-red crawlers packed themselves so tightly around the heat-exchanger fins that the exchangers stop shedding heat, throwing the subterranean turbines out of thermal equilibrium? Why isn't every pressure gauge crusted blind with Bone-Limpets?

To see the answer, you have to follow that vertical column of mist you saw from the top of the condenser drum—the five-mile walk toward the dense knot of manifolds where the heat rises heavier into the bright, white sky.

As you get within half a mile of the manifold complex, the ambient temperature creeps up from eighty-five to ninety-two degrees. The sixty-cycle hum in your boots deepens, picking up a lower, slower sub-harmonic, like the purr of a transformer the size of a cathedral buried two decks down. The waders here are thicker on the ground, standing belly-deep in terraced weir-pools, their pale, oil-slicked necks craned slightly upward, letting the rising warm mist bathe their throat-pouches. Overhead, the gantries climb eighty feet high in a dense, cathedral-vault tangle of primary steam-headers and bus-bars, draped in heavy, rustling curtains of segmented crawlers.

Then, thirty yards ahead of you down the flooded corridor, the silent crowd of waders parts. 

They do not scatter or flap—they have nothing to flap with—they simply take two deliberate, long-legged steps sideways, pressing their warm flanks flat against the aqueduct walls to open a four-foot lane down the center of the iron street. Even the bread-loaf slugs clinging to the lower flanges contract their grey mantles, pulling themselves slightly flatter against the iron.

Around the corner of a galvanized scrubber tank comes something tall, narrow, and dry.

At fifty paces through the bright mist, you might swear a man in fouled foul-weather gear was walking down the catwalk—until the proportions resolve and your mind loses its grip on the silhouette. It stands nearly seven feet high, carried on four legs arranged not fore-and-aft like a hound’s, nor radially like a spider’s, but in two closely paired tandem sets, giving it an eerie, bipedal-looking uprightness as it steps through the ankle-deep water. Its limbs are long, extraordinarily thin levers of dense, mahogany-dark horn, jointed high up near a narrow, vertical thorax no wider than an anvil. It makes almost no splash; its feet end in narrow, cleft hooves shod in natural pads of vulcanized-feeling black callous that slot neatly *between* the raised diamonds of the deck plate.

It has no eyes—nothing here bothers with lenses when the white sky glares from every angle at once and half the world is dark plenum—Instead, its narrow, forward-leaning head-case is a smooth, swept-back cowl of matte-ivory bone, slotted along the cheeks like a welder’s louvre. Behind those slots sit wet, pleated rosettes of electro-receptive ampullae and exquisitely sensitive thermal pits. To this creature, the world is not a flat grey photograph of pipes; it is a blazing, three-dimensional topography of heat-gradients, sixty-cycle magnetic flux lines, and fluid-pressure vibrations.

Watch what it is doing. 

Tucked high under its narrow chest are three secondary arms—multi-jointed, asymmetrical, and weirdly delicate. One terminates in a heavy, blunt hook like a spanner wrench; the middle one ends in a flat, sharp-edged chisel-blade of dark keratin; the third splits into a bundle of long, dexterous, pale-skinned fingers coated in clear, tacky mucus, constantly trembling as they taste the air.

It stops beside a vertical standpipe where a colony of Bone-Limpets has crept out of bounds, cementing themselves across the brass stem of a gate-valve and locking the wheel-threads. The tall walker leans its ivory cowl within two inches of the valve, tilting its head left, then right, listening to the subtle choked whistle of restricted water flow inside the pipe and reading the cold-spot on the brass caused by the crust of shells. 

There is no malice in its motion, and no hurry. Its spanner-arm reaches out, hooks the rim of the largest limpet, and applies a smooth, tremendous levering torque. *Crack.* Suction breaks; the limpet comes free. Before it can drop into the puddle, the nimble finger-hand catches it, flips it upside down, and the chisel-arm slips neatly beneath the shell-skirt to sever the yellow foot-muscle in two clinical snips. It lifts the meat to a vertical slit beneath its cowl—swallowing it whole without chewing—and drops the empty, rivet-shaped shell down a nearby open sump-pipe where the gravel-wash will carry it into the deep grinders below. One by one, it clears the valve stem cleanly, wipes the brass threads once with its sticky fingers to lick off the sweet lime-paste residue, and then—most unsettling of all—places its spanner-claw against the rim of the iron valve-wheel and leans its weight against it until the wheel turns a quarter-inch, easing the internal thermal pinch until the pipe’s vibration rings true again in its cheek-pits.

They have no name, because no one has ever been here to name them, though if you watched them for a month you would begin thinking of them as the Gaugers.

They are sparse—perhaps one to every two square miles of surface labyrinth—solitary, long-lived, and slow-breeding. If you follow this one at a respectful twenty paces (if you get closer, it will pause, turn its blank, louvered ivory face squarely toward the messy, asynchronous eighty-beat-per-minute thump of your mammalian heart and the strange ninety-eight-degree flare of your skin, regard you for ten silent seconds as an unaccountable mobile hot-spot that isn't blocking a grate, and then go back to work), you will see the entire logic of its existence.

A Gauger does not hunt the open flats. The millions of waders and slugs sitting out on the neutral deck plates could graze beside its hooves for a century and never be touched. The Gauger eats *anomalies*. 

Its nervous system is tuned from birth to the baseline harmonic of balanced infrastructure. A sixty-cycle hum is silence to it; a fifty-eight-cycle lugging groan in an overburdened pump casing five yards away rings in its skull like a dinner bell. Uniform eighty-five-degree radiation is invisible; a ninety-nine-degree hot-spot on an air-starved radiator fin glows in its thermal pits like a ripe plum in dark foliage. When a curtain of segmented crawlers grows too thick over an intake screen, choking the draft, the altered whistle of the air draws a Gauger from half a mile off. It climbs the crisp galvanized ladders three rungs at a stride, hooks one long hind-hoof over a handrail for balance, and rakes fifty pounds of primer-red crawlers off the mesh in long, sweeping armfuls, gorging itself on the richest cluster while letting the rest rain down into the waiting bills of the waders forty feet below, until the airflow cools the fin back to equilibrium and the "scent" of the meal vanishes.

Near nightfall—not sky-nightfall, for the luminous white ceiling never dims by a single lumen, but the nightfall of your own exhausted eyes—you follow the Gauger down something you haven't dared enter yet: a broad, sloping ramp with safety chains on either side, descending through a rectangular cutout in the continental iron floor into the first maintenance plenum beneath.

Down here, the daylight cuts off cleanly twenty feet from the ramp’s lip, replaced by a humid, absolute velvet dark. If you click on a flashlight, the beam catches air thick with warm, drifting mist-motes and illuminates the underside of the world you’ve been walking on: a ten-foot-high ceiling of sweating I-beams, giant return-mains insulated in lagged jackets, and ceramic high-voltage bus-bars humming with that profound, bone-soothing sixty-cycle thrum.

Here, in the warm dark where the surface waders never venture, the Gaugers keep their nurseries, and here lives the rest of the world.

In the corners where massive steel columns meet the lower deck-plates, suspended between copper grounding-straps, hang translucent, amber-colored cocoons the size of punching bags, pulsing with a faint, internal blue-green bioluminescence—not to see by, since nothing down here has eyes, but simply a metabolic byproduct of copper-protein synthesis in the developing tissues of larval Gaugers. Around the bases of the columns, the central bodies of the great Sump-Sheets lie spread out across the warm iron like vast, breathing carpets of pale amber jelly, ten inches thick and forty feet across, gently digesting the steady drizzle of organic silt and magnetite sand that washes down through the overhead grates from the bright continent above. 

And coiled around the porcelain insulators of the thickest, hottest high-voltage conduits—cables carrying enough current to power a coastal city, radiating a dry, ozone-tinged warmth of a hundred and twenty degrees—sit creatures that never come up into the wet daylight at all. They look like flattened, legless lizards plated in overlapping scales of dull, tarnished silver, pressed belly-flat against the humming ceramic jackets. They do not eat biofilm, or meat, or rust. If you watch them in your flashlight beam, they never move a muscle, year in and year out, except for a slow, rhythmic flaring of pleated Copper-lined frills behind their jaws. Inside their broad, flat chests, loops of conductive, iron-heavy vascular tissue sit directly across the oscillating sixty-cycle magnetic field bleeding off the unshielded cables, inducing a tiny, continuous trickle of micro-amperage straight into their cellular chemistry, driving ATP synthesis directly off the grid the way a leaf drives it off the sun.

Switch off your flashlight, sit on the bottom step of the warm iron ramp, and listen in the dark.

Above your head, ten million drops of water *plink* into ankle-deep basins across a curve of unrusted steel five thousand miles wide. Around you in the velvet warmth, a Gauger moves off down an unlit corridor between two massive turbine-mounts—*tock, pause, tock* go its narrow hooves against the dry, lower diamond-plate—going to clear a weeping relief-valve half a mile away. Beneath your thighs, the steel hums, steady as anesthesia, neither speeding up nor winding down, holding an entire biospheric afternoon suspended on the flat mark of a clean brass gauge.
