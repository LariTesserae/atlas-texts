Wait by the iron-weighted basket until the eastward shift sustains that three-toned chord for twenty consecutive breaths. You will not hear footsteps—nothing could be heard over the metallic thrashing of the canopy and the saw-rasp of the climbers—but you will see the fine white chalk dust at the lip of a dry limestone pore suddenly puff outward, displaced by something rising from the calm, dead-air capillaries beneath the massif.

First comes a probing rod: five feet of glassy reed tipped with a hammered splint of native copper, poked cautiously out of the crevice into the whipping draft. A blue-white spark leaps from a passing frond-tip and grounds itself into the splint, running harmlessly down a braided wire drag-line into the rock. Only when the static load of the immediate air has been tasted does the harvester emerge.

They do not stand up. To stand upright on the matte-polished arena of the bowl is to become a sail and be dashed against the spires. Instead, they spill out of the stone fissure with the low, splayed geometry of crabs or hunting spiders, keeping their bellies an inch off the warm limestone. 

At first glance, they look like bundles of dry, oiled leather and chalk-dust given sudden, jerky animation. If you have human shapes in mind, their proportions feel warped by drought and gravity: their torsos are wide, flat, and heavily ribbed, encased in overlapping, flexible cuirasses woven from split silica-reeds, while their limbs are startlingly long, double-jointed in appearance, and roped with dry, sinewy muscle that carries almost no subcutaneous fat. Water is not a river in them; water is a hoarded grease, a dense, viscous sap locked tightly behind parchment skin the color of smoked tallow and tarnished brass.

Look at the face when one pauses behind the crescent windbreak to check the tension on a kinetic loom. There is no exposed wet tissue anywhere. Where soft, weeping eyes would be instantly cured to leather by the hot silicate gale, they have double-lidded apertures shielded by translucent plates of natural mica-like horn—an evolutionary nictitating shield grown thick and permanent, giving them a blind, insectoid stare that gleams copper in the perpetual dusk. Their nostrils are downward-facing, muscular slits lined with stiff, brassy bristles that flex rhythmically, combing the sulfur and grit out of every breath before it reaches their lungs. Their mouths are wide, lipless seams, kept tightly sealed. 

They have no hair at all—hair in this static furnace would stand on end, snarl with dust, and invite lightning to the skull. Instead, their scalps and shoulders are dusted with a paste of rendered glider-fat and crushed iron ore, rubbed into the skin until they gleam like cast bronze, turning their own hides into partial Faraday cages. Around their ankles and wrists hang trailing chains of cold-hammered copper links and heavy, slotted stones that drag across the floor with a dull clatter, grounding them continuously to the mountain so the static of the giant stalks doesn't stop their hearts when they reach up to harvest.

Now three more slide from the crevices. Watch how they speak to the one at the basket. 

Not a word is breathed into the roaring air. One harvester braces a knee against the windbreak, draws a flat wand of notched bone from their belt, and drags it sharply across the corrugated silica-plates sewn onto their own forearm. *Krr-chk-chk. Krrrr-chk.* The sound is pitched just an octave below the frantic rasping of the six-limbed canopy climbers, a dry, percussive code of friction and rhythm that cuts cleanly through the sheet-metal clatter of the leaves. Across the white stone gap, another answers by slapping a flat stone slab against the porous limestone floor—two heavy, bass *thumps* that travel through the rock straight into the palms and knees of the others. They converse through their bones and their armor, leaving their precious internal moisture locked behind sealed teeth.

The three-toned stone siren overhead suddenly drops a pitch, groaning into a deeper, hollow minor chord as the fumarole beneath the grove stutters—a brief, deceptive inhalation of the mountain before a massive thermal heave.

Instantly, the harvesters scatter into practiced, terrifying motion. 

Two of them scuttle out onto the exposed white floor between the monstrous, whipping reed-pillars. They do not run; they *tack* like ice-boats. Each carries two short obsidian-and-bone picks, driving the hooks into the microscopic pores and fractures of the matte limestone to haul themselves forward against a sudden, blistering crosswind that strips golden husks from above and hurls them sideways like knives. A stray arc of blue electricity snaps down from a low-hanging gas-bladder and strikes the elder harvester squarely between the shoulder blades; the spark races over their iron-greased skin, flares brightly along their wrist-chains, and vanishes into the chalk floor with a smell of scorched grease. The harvester doesn't even flinch—only shivers once, almost greedily, as if the shock were strong winter tea.

When the central vent finally fires—a hot, sulfurous belch that makes the colossal trunks bow outward and the canopy explode with noise—the younger of the two unclips a folded membrane of stitched, rust-colored glider hides tucked beneath their arms. They don't fly; flight is for creatures without heavy bones. They *vault*. Catching the booming updraft for just three seconds, anchored by a braided fiber tether held fast by their partner wedged into a root-cleft, the vaulter shoots twenty feet up into the thrashing lower fronds. 

With three savage, economical hacks of a chipped obsidian cleaver, they sever a swollen, translucent gas-bladder the size of a hogshead before the six-limbed chitinous climbers can swarm down the ribbon-leaf to defend their perch. As the updraft dies in its stuttering cycle, the partner hauls furiously on the tether, yanking the vaulter and the buoyant, bucking prize back down to the stone floor milliseconds before a violent downdraft slams a shower of brittle gliders against the opposite cliff.

Follow them back down when the baskets are full of fulgurite glass, severed bladders, and stunned, velvety kite-beasts pulled from the cleft-nets. 

They do not live *in* the amphitheater; the amphitheater is the threshing floor, the storm-pasture, the forge. Their true home lies fifty yards down through the narrow, twisting dry-pores of the chalk massif, where the roar of the grove fades into a constant, rhythmic *thrum* felt in the teeth, and the copper dusk gives way to warm, absolute black.

Down here in the windless pockets, heated to blood-temperature by the proximity of the fumarole's deep throat, the harvesters peel off their rattling silica armor and become strangely gentle, slow, and soft-limbed. In the total dark, the mica shields over their eyes slide sideways, revealing pale, dimly luminescent pupils adapted to read the faint static-glow they bring down on their tools and bundles.

Here is their whole economy of dust and spark:

In wide, hand-hollowed basins of stone, elders and children—small, leathery frogs of things who have not yet grown their adult horn-lids—sit cracking open the harvestedfulgurites. They don't use the fused glass for blades; obsidian is sharper. They grind the lightning-struck mineral glass with mortar and pestle and mix it with the heavy, pungent oils squeezed from the velvety kite-beasts and the sulfurous gel scraped from the interior walls of the gas-bladders. They eat this paste. Just like the towering stalks above them, their bodies have spent ten thousand generations forgetting rain; their guts are dense with symbiotic, sulfur-fixing bacteria stolen long ago from the grove's ecosystem, allowing them to pull metabolic moisture and chemical energy directly from rendered animal fats, mineral salts, and volatile canopy-gases. 

Nothing here is burned. Fire consumes oxygen and wastes heat in a mountain already warm, and smoke in deep caves is death. When they need light in the nurseries or workshops, a child sits beside a shallow bowl of black mineral dust and rhythmically strokes a sheet of dried glider-vellum with a paddle of amber-like plant resin, shedding steady, gentle cascades of cold purple static-sparks that illuminate the chalk walls in flickering, silent strobes.

In that stuttering violet dark, you can see what covers the walls of their sleeping chambers. Not paintings—pigment dries and flakes off this warm stone—but endless, intricate bas-reliefs carved with obsidian burins. Spirals within spirals, jagged branching trees of lightning, rhythmic rows of notches recording the great multi-year stutters of the fumarole. If you run your fingers over the carvings, as the blind, ancient ones tucked into the warmest back-crevices do all day long, you realize the walls are scored to match the exact rasp-frequencies of their bone-talk. Their history is tactile and acoustic; a grandmother drags a thumbnail down ten feet of carved limestone and plays back the story of a century-old wind-shift so everyone in the chamber can hear the rhythm of how their ancestors adjusted the stone sirens above to survive it.

High overhead, muffled by forty feet of porous white chalk, the three-toned chord groans again, shifting from the eastward call to a sharp, dissonant bleat that means a dust-cyclone is scouring the rim. 

In the violet strobe of the static-lamp, nobody speaks. Four harvesters near the tunnel mouth simply reach out, pull their silica-reed cuirasses back over their greased, bronze-gleaming ribs, check the drag-chains at their heels, and crawl back up the throat of the rock toward the thunder.
