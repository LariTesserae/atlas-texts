If you want to find the resident of the hollow, you must look where the two noises meet. 

Do not look at the weeping walls, and do not look at the calm, broader basins where the mineral rafts drift unimpeded like panes of frosted glass. Look instead along the three-foot span of bare basalt between the lowest A-frame pipe trestle and the cast-iron manifold, directly beneath the brass relief cock. Here, the broadband rush of the subterranean cleft collides with the hard, mechanical B-flat of the rotary pump. In the ankle-deep pan of scalding water below the pipe, this acoustic cross-fire sets up a permanent standing wave—a tight, invisible lattice of vibration that never shifts by half a millimeter.

Resting on the flat stone at the exact center of that lattice is what looks, at first glance, like a dropped bundle of surveyor’s wire or an unusually intricate pile of industrial slag. 

It is roughly the size of two cupped hands. It has no meat on it, no skin, no mucous membrane, and nothing that would register to a finger—if you were foolish enough to plunge your hand into the seventy-degree water—as soft. Its substance is built entirely from the thin, tarnished-tin mineral skins that precipitate across the pools, fused edge-to-edge with leached iron oxide from the bleeding expansion bolts and trace copper sulfates wept down from the brass relief valve. 

Watch the drifting mineral rafts on the neighboring shelf. Normally, they slide lazily with the millimeter creep of the spill until they ground themselves on a sharp flake of traprock, stacking up like broken slate. Near the manifold, however, their drift alters. As a fragile geometric shingle enters the standing wave of the pump’s B-flat hum, it begins to judder. Instead of piling haphazardly, it turns on its axis, drawn into the quiet acoustic nodes between the vibrations, and slots itself upright against the central lattice with a tiny, crystalline click you cannot hear over the roar.

The thing in the basin is growing, or rather, it is accruing, building an architecture of microscopic baffles, flutes, and razor-edged gills out of calcium, silica, and rust. 

Still, if that were all it did, it would only be an oddity of fluid dynamics—a freak crystal habit shaped by an electric motor. You have to watch it through three or four cycles of the relief cock to realize it is curating itself. 

Every six minutes and forty seconds, the spring-loaded brass valve overhead lets go. A cupful of superheated condensate, richer in dissolved pipe-scale and stripped ions than the surrounding seep, splashes down onto the basalt inches away. Ten seconds *before* the valve trips—when the internal pressure behind the manifold’s sweating glass gauge hasn't visibly budged, though the pitch of the steel pipe’s hum tightens by a fraction of a cent—the mineral lattice shifts. 

It does not use muscles. There are none to use. Instead, a localized chemical shudder runs through the iron-rich joints where its plates meet. By shifting electrons across its metallic seams—powered by the steep thermal gradient between the cooler basalt floor and the scalding surface film, and by the galvanic current running weakly through the wet stone between the zinc-galvanized pins and the cast iron—it alters the electrostatic charge of its outer leaves. Dozens of tiny, mica-thin vanes hinge outward like the pages of a wet book opening. 

When the scalding splash hits the water, the vanes catch the turbulent, mineral-heavy plume, funneling the fresh, unspent heat and dissolved metals straight into the hollow chambers at the structure's core. Seconds later, as the turbulence dies and the carbonic, acid-heavy water from the lowest corners washes back in, the vanes snap shut, sealing the interior against dissolution.

If a piece of a drifting raft arrives too thick, or warped at an angle that disrupts the B-flat resonance inside its chambers, the lattice rejects it. It concentrates a brief, sharp galvanic current at the point of contact, rapidly dissolving the offending seam in a tiny fizz of microscopic bubbles until the flawed plate breaks free and drifts away downstream, leaving the geometry clean.

This is the first and oldest piece of it, the dense, metallic-gray heart beneath the pipe, which has been tuning itself since the month after the engineers climbed out of the pit and left their wrench behind. Over time, though, it has begun to reach for the rest of the room.

Follow the wet stone away from the manifold toward the rusting orange wrench on its dry block, or toward the western cleft where the subterranean vapor roars. Running along the drowned, knife-sharp fractures of the traprock floor are hairline filaments that look like tarnished silver solder laid directly into the stone. They are hollow, brittle tubes no thicker than horsehair, precipitated out of iron silicate and copper. They grow forward at less than an inch a month, blindly feeling out the micro-gradients of the floor. 

Where a tube touches a pocket of water two degrees too cool, it dissolves back. Where it finds a hairline crack venting hot, sour gas straight from the bedrock, it thickens, flaring out into a tiny, fluted ear pressed tight against the stone, drinking both the rising sulfur compounds and the raw kinetic battering of the roar. One filament has reached the drowned beetle shell near the northern talus; it did not eat the insect, having no use for carbon chains of that sort, but it used the curved chitin dome as falsework, plating the beetle’s back in concentric, terraced rings of bronze-colored pyrite to anchor a turn in its line. Another branch has climbed two inches up the galvanized bolt of the nearest A-frame trestle, wrapping the zinc in a scaly cuff that bleeds a steady, tiny trickle of electrical potential straight back to the core beneath the drip.

It has no eyes to see the flat, colorless clouds passing twenty feet overhead, and if the sky went black for a thousand years, it would not register the difference. Its entire world is pressure, dissolved salt, electrical potential, and the two interlocking voices of the pit. 

In fact, it has begun to answer the pump. 

Inside the corrugated tin doghouse, the heavy rotary motor has been running unserviced for years. Its bearings are wearing infinitesimally oval; left to itself, a minute harmonic wobble would already have crept into its shaft, building month by month until the seals blew out. Yet the needle on the sweating brass gauge stays pinned at two o'clock without a tremor. Down on the stone floor, the encrusted lattice has crept up all four legs of the iron manifold and bridged the gap to the pump’s mounting plate. It has packed the microscopic gaps beneath the vibrating baseplate with dense, piezoelectric wedges of quartz-heavy scale, stiffening when the motor's pitch wavers, yielding by a micron when the thermal expansion of noon crowds the steel pipes. 

It does not know what an electric motor is, any more than coral knows what the moon is. It only knows that the B-flat is half the skeleton of the sky, and when the B-flat falters, the standing waves in the shallow pan lose their crispness and the drifting mineral rafts stop lining up at the door. So it braces the iron. It bleeds away stray harmonic shudders through its brittle silver-gray roots into the fractured traprock, holding the machinery true so the water keeps vibrating in the exact grid required to build itself.

If you climbed down the jagged ledges in thick-soled boots, shouting to make yourself heard, and kicked the cluster beneath the relief pipe, it would shatter instantly under your toe with the dull, unremarkable crunch of thin ice or dry cinders. There would be no blood, no panicked scurrying, no recoil. The water would cloud briefly with gray-red dust and clear within two minutes. 

By nightfall, before you were five miles across the grass plain, the broken shards still lying within the standing wave would be juddering softly against the fresh black basalt. By morning, pinned in place by the unrelenting B-flat hum and soldered by the six-minute drip from overhead, the vanes would be standing upright again, angled into the invisible current, catching the tin-colored skins as they drift past.
