Look closer at the moving floor, let your eyes adjust to the shadowless zinc glare and the deafening *shhhk-shhhk-thud* of the bowl, and the generalized swarm resolves into distinct trades, castes, and geometries of survival. Nothing in this dry pocket has the luxury of softness. Where water-born life uses hydraulic pressure and pliant muscle, the things in the sump are built like pocket watches, gun-locks, and rock-crushers. Their joints are ball-and-socket ceramic; their tendons are braided, dry-lubricated fibers of pure silica-chitin that creak when flexed. Inside them, there is no wet viscera—only tightly pleated electrostatic gills, grinding gizzards full of swallowed quartz, and packets of dense, waxy lipids kept from solidifying only by stolen heat.

At the very bottom of the heap, pressed directly against the soapy granite and the roaring cast-iron blocks, are the **Anvil-backs**. 

They are the dinner-plate-broad grazers, so flattened and bevel-edged that a predator cannot get a jaw under them once they clamp down. Their dorsal plates are three-quarters of an inch thick, composed of layered calcium carbonate and iron sulfide scavenged from the stone, matte slate-blue and scarred with a crosshatching of white scratches from thousands of smaller feet walking over them. Flip an Anvil-back over—a feat requiring a crowbar—and you find no legs worth mentioning, only stubby, high-torque ratchets and a broad, pale underside textured like a bastard file. They do not eat with mouths; they eat with their bellies. Pressing themselves against the vibrating housings of the donkey-pumps, they let the machine’s own percussive *thud-clank* oscillate their bodies thousands of times a minute, passively rasping away microscopic films of lithotrophic bacteria and oxidized iron from the stone and metal. When an Anvil-back has absorbed enough kinetic heat that its shell would blister a human palm, it unlocks its rim, lifts itself a quarter-inch on sixty tiny, ticking struts, and rides the outward current of the swarm toward the cool perimeter to digest its bellyful of rock-flour in peace.

Riding atop the Anvil-backs, and mounded two feet deep around the wobbling flywheel, are the **Brush-ticks** and **Ley-jars**. 

These are the creatures of the blue spark. Thumbnail-to-fist-sized, pill-shaped, and armored in dull, tarnished copper and bronze-colored chitin, they possess long, feathery antennae made of conductive, metal-plated bristles. Around the spinning flywheel, where the degraded copper brushes drag and spit regular snaps of ozone-smelling static, the Ley-jars shove and wedge themselves into a tight, crackling pyramid. When a blue arc jumps from the wheel, it doesn't kill them; it hits the raised, metallic bristle-tufts of the nearest creature and cascades down through the pile, jumping from shell to shell with a sound like tearing calico. 

Inside a Ley-jar’s segmented abdomen sits a biological capacitor—rolls of thin, dielectric horn separated by films of mineral salts. They drink the raw voltage directly, using the electrical potential to fix atmospheric nitrogen and drive the dry, chemical synthesis of their waxy internal fat. Fully charged, a Ley-jar actually hums, its bronze plates standing slightly apart with electrostatic repulsion, dust clinging to it in fuzzy halos. Predators know better than to bite a freshly charged Ley-jar straight off the mound; a careless snap of the jaws completes the circuit, blowing the hunter’s nerve-ganglia to ash in a sharp *pop* of violet light and the smell of scorched hair. Instead, the hunters wait in the middle distance, watching for the Ley-jars whose halos of clinging shell-dust have begun to droop—a sign their charge is spent.

Then there are the things timed to the iron itself: the **Half-Seconders**, or **Gasket-lice**.

Slender, flat as knives, with six sprawling, multi-jointed legs tipped in hardened hooks like sewing needles, they congregate around the active cylinders where the heavy pistons kick out, pause, and slam home. Evolution inside this forty-foot bowl has bred an absolute clock into their nervous systems. They do not watch the piston—their eyes are mere pinhole pits that register light and dark—they feel the preliminary *tick* of the valve-gear through their feet three-tenths of a second before the stroke. 

The piston slams inward. Before the iron has even settled, a fringe of gray-and-black Gasket-lice darts out from under the bolt-heads, flashing onto the exposed, polished steel shaft and the weeping lip of the cylinder seal. Their mouthparts are twin-bladed scrapers like miniature paint-strippers. In exactly four hundred milliseconds, they plane off ribbons of hot, black, sulfur-rich mineral grease—ancient petroleum jelly thick with Microscopic iron shavings—stuffing it into expandable throat-pouches. *Tick* goes the valve. Instantly, as if jerked back by invisible threads, every single scavenger snaps backward into the millimeter clearance behind the square-headed bolts just as eight hundred pounds of rusted iron crashes past their antennae. Those born with a slightly sluggish clock—even by a twentieth of a second—do not pass on their genes; they become a dry, yellow smear burnished onto the piston rod, immediately scraped up and eaten on the *next* stroke by their siblings.

Plowing through all of these, cutting wakes through the waist-deep drifts of pearl-flour and living shells, are the terrier-sized hunters—the **Shear-dogs**.

To see one move is to watch an industrial rock-crusher given spite and legs. Ten-legged, low-slung, and immensely broad across the thorax, a Shear-dog’s carapace is pale flint-gray, smooth and rounded on top so smaller bugs cascade off it like water off a submarine’s hull. Its entire front third is given over to a pair of asymmetric, compound mandibles worked by ratcheting plates in the chest. One jaw is a broad, hooked vice-anvil; the other is a narrow, chisel-pointed guillotine blade edged with biogenic magnetite. 

Because they have no liquid blood to circulate oxygen or shed heat, Shear-dogs breathe like bellows, their abdominal plates rhythmically telescoping in and out with a dry, wheezing *hiss-clack, hiss-clack*, pumping cool zinc-lit air through internal manifolds to keep themselves from cooking in their own violence. When a Shear-dog catches a heavy Anvil-back or a discharged Ley-jar, it pins the prey with its heavy forelegs, locks the vice-jaw over the rim, and cranks the chisel-blade shut. You can hear the *CRACK* across the basin, cutting right through the roar of the engines. The prey’s ceramic shell splits like a dropped porcelain plate. 

Out puffs that dry, sulfur-yellow cloud of lipid-granules and desiccated muscle-meal. The Shear-dog plunges its brush-like inner maxillae into the breach, shoveling the rich yellow powder down its gullet before the wind can take it. 

And instantly, the **Silt-mites** arrive. 

They are not a distinct wave; they are already there, making up thirty percent of what looks like inanimate pearlescent dust on the basin floor. Microscopic to pinhead-sized, translucent white, they lie dormant in the drifts until the specific triboelectric charge of a cracking shell wakes them. Within two seconds of a Shear-dog’s kill, the ground around the hunter boils upward like milk hitting a hot skillet. Billions of Silt-mites swarm over the cracked carcass—and over the hunter’s own jaws—stripping every stray molecule of wax, protein, and conductive salt, polishing the broken shard of shell until it is clean, chalky, and dead, ready to be ground under ten million feet into the pale flour that packs the crevices.

Yet the strangest inhabitants of the sump are not in the hot, frantic center at all. They are out among the dead iron, and down beneath the flour.

Look at the brass pressure gauge with the bent needle, packed full of pearl-colored eggs. Guarding its rim—and tucked inside the paper-thin, eroded curve of the dead hopper—are the **Brood-masons**. Many of the basin's species don't tend their own young; instead, the Brood-masons have turned egg-thievery and nursery-tending into a specialized niche, parasitic and protective at once. Spider-like, slow, with long, caliper-shaped palps, their shells are mottled to match corroded brass and pitted cast iron. They gather eggs dropped carelessly in the warm outward currents, carry them to the dead machines at the cool, wind-sheltered edges, and mortar them into place using a paste of ground chitin-flour mixed with a tiny, precious secretion of stolen machine-grease. They eat one egg in five to sustain themselves, meticulously rotating and brushing the remaining four with velvety tarsal pads to keep the suffocating dust from clogging the microscopic breathing-pores of the shells, until the hatchlings crack free—tiny, soft as fingernail parings for the first ten minutes of their lives—and skitter frantically inward toward the heat of the pumps before the cold air stills them forever.

And deeper still, never seen unless one of the great engines misfires and shifts on its rusted foundation bolts, are the **Sump-worms**—though they are not worms at all, but blind, pale, multi-segmented centipede-things as thick as a man’s wrist and four feet long, swimming slowly through the compacted drifts of pearlescent chitin-flour that fill the deepest fissures of the granite bowl. 

Down at the bottom of the dust-drifts, where the weight of millions of years of ground carapaces presses against the raw stone, and where the buried, sheared-off pipes still radiate a faint, steady geothermal warmth from deep within the ancient planet, these blind giants perform the final alchemy of the basin. They swallow the dead, stripped shell-dust by the pound, grinding it in gizzards lined with swallowed grit from the peneplain above, hosting dense gut-gardens of anaerobic, heat-loving bacteria that break the stubborn, mineralized chitin back down into raw ammonia gasses, sulfur compounds, and warm, dry pellets of nitrates. When these gasses vent upward through the flour-drifts in tiny, silent burps, the Anvil-backs and the stone-bacteria drink them in, closing the loop.

Stand at the lip of the forty-foot drop, with the ruler-flat gray talc of the dead continent behind you, and look down into the bowl. It is not chaos. It is a single, compound organism made of iron and horn. You can see its circulation written in color: dark, sluggish waves of slate-gray and cold copper pressing inward from the soap-smooth walls; piling up, brightening, snapping with blue fire and trembling with borrowed steam-power around the three surviving engines; and spilling outward again in warm, frantic, predatory rivers, shedding shells, cracking open in yellow puffs, laying pearls in broken brass, while the piston kicks, pauses, and slams back in, keeping time for the end of the world.
