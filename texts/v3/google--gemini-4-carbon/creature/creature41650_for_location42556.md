If you stand by the dry-stacked slate windbreak and stare out across a basin of cobblestone-carapaces—not scanning the horizon as an outsider would, waiting for motion, but letting your eyes unfocus to take in the immense, repeating pattern of the whole—you will eventually find a flaw in the geometry.

Fifty yards out, where three identical chalk-white grazers stand overlapped in a smear of colorless grease, the dust on their flat backs is disturbed. Not blown away, because nothing blows, and not brushed cleanly off, because nothing moves fast enough to sweep. Instead, there is a line of shallow, oval depressions stamped across the chitinous pavement. Each mark is a millimeter deep, clean-edged, spaced precisely twenty-eight inches apart, stepping directly from shell to shell, skirting the shimmering static of the spatial knots, leading away toward the distant copper gleam of a Silica-Tree.

Follow the tracks for three miles over the smooth swells of slate-blue granite, stepping where they stepped, and you will catch up to the thing that made them. You will almost certainly walk right past him first, mistaking him for a narrow, weathered cairn, or the lightning-split trunk of a dead tree wedged upright into the crush of parchment-beasts.

The builders did not leave the Spine. They did not die out, either—at least not in the wet, bacterial way things die in the lowlands far beneath the ochre haze. When the wind finally laid itself down and the air locked into its last, breathless equilibrium, the people who stacked the slate and lashed the meteoric hammers simply kept walking through the cooling world until their bodies made an accommodation with the altitude.

There are perhaps forty of them left across the entire forty-mile plateau, though counting them is difficult. Like the horned grazers, a single walker is occasionally smeared across a ridge in small, localized repetitions—not whole duplicates standing yards apart, because they still possess just enough momentum to tear themselves free of deep spatial ruts, but trailing faint, stubborn afterimages. When one moves, he leaves a dry silhouette of his shoulder or his heel hanging in the slate-colored light a few inches behind him, taking minutes or hours to evaporate like dry ice.

Up close, an inhabitant of the Spine is tall, narrow, and utterly desiccated, possessing the terrifying, lightweight solidity of seasoned ironwood. Whatever soft, wet ancestry brought them to the top of the world ages ago has been slowly sublimated away into the moistureless air. Their skin has cured on the bone into something resembling dark, oiled slate—matte, hairless, pulled drum-tight over corded tendons that no longer slide with grease, but ratchet silently against one another like dry hemp cables over wooden pulleys. Their chests do not rise and fall. Long ago, their lungs collapsed and calcified into dense, spongy lattices of trace-mineralized tissue, acting less like bellows and more like passive chemical traps, absorbing what little ambient oxygen touches them through narrow, permanently flared slits where a nose once softened a face.

They wear clothes made in an era when thread could still fray, though it hasn't frayed since. Swaddling their angular frames are heavy kilts and shoulder-wraps of woven copper-bark twine, stiffened with slate dust, layered over overlapping plates of pale, translucent beast-shell tied together with strips of gray hide. Over millennia of near-motionless contact, the distinction between wearer and garment has blurred. Where a rawhide strap crosses a collarbone, the dark, cured skin has grown up and over the leather in a slow, calloused lip, locking the armor permanently to the skeleton. Their feet are bare, broadened at the base into thick, flat pads of gray horn cracked like dry riverbed mud, hard enough to walk across sharp basalt without taking a scratch.

Looking into the face of one is like looking into a deep dry well. Beneath a heavy brow ridge caked with the pale ochre silt of a hundred centuries, the eyes have changed to survive a world without moisture or blinking. Tear ducts are sealed seams. The eyeballs themselves have hardened into smooth, amber-dark spheres coated in a permanent, glassy pellicle of keratin—natural goggles grown over lenses that take in the shadow-sharp midday light all at once. They do not dart from object to object. To turn the head wastes heat; to shift the eyes requires lubrication they do not have. Instead, their vision is wide, flat, and panoramic, soaking in the forty-mile expanse as a single, slowly developing photographic plate.

Compared to a visitor from below, they are statues. Compared to the Spine, they are blindingly, violently fast.

While a cobblestone-beast takes four years to blink an obsidian eye, one of the walkers can complete a full, deliberate stride in the span of six hours. If you camp beside one—sitting on the solid, unyielding flank of a kneeling carapace while the shadowless light refuses to dim—you cannot see him move. You see him standing with one gray-horned foot raised two inches above the shell of a buried grazer, his long fingers curled around a short, heavy pry-bar chipped from dark flint, his head tilted slightly toward a cluster of paper-thin origami creatures suspended at chest height. If you look away to examine a nearby petroglyph, walk a wide circle around the basin, and return three hours later, the raised foot is down. The flint bar is two inches closer to his hip. A faint, colorless smear hangs in the air behind his left elbow where his arm used to be, buzzing against your eardrums if you lean too close, feeling like cold static on the hairs of your wrist.

Listen—really listen, pressing your jaw against your own shoulder to block out the rushing roar of your own wet, frantic lowland blood—and you will realize they are the source of half the subterranean sounds on the plateau. Their joints no longer pop with fluid; they creak with the dry, microscopic *tick-tick-tick* of taut resinous fibers stretching over bone. Deep inside their ribcages, a dense, muscular knot of a heart still contracts once every forty minutes, pushing a thick, dark, tarry syrup—more heavy metal and lipid than water—through flat, ribbon-like vessels with a sound like two pieces of chalk being rubbed softly together.

What does a mind do when a single step takes half a day, and there is nothing to hunt, nothing to flee, and no weather to shelter from?

They are no longer builders; the shelters and windbreaks were abandoned when they realized the wind wasn't coming back, and that lying down in one place for too long was fatal. Stasis is a physical gravity on the Spine. Anything that stops moving entirely begins to sink into the plateau's frayed geometry. If a walker stood still for a decade, the stone and the crowded beasts would begin to forget his boundaries. A Silica-Tree might push a brittle copper root straight through his shin; worse, his spatial coordinates might slip, splitting him into two overlapping ghosts standing six inches apart, locked forever in a shimmering patch of lens-grease, unable to muster the unified leverage to pull himself back into one body.

So they walk, endlessly, in vast forty-mile circuits from abyssal rim to abyssal rim, serving as the glacial shepherds and untanglers of a stalled reality.

You can read their occupation in the things they carry and the small, meticulous alterations left in their wake. Tucked into the bark-twine girdles of most walkers are simple tools from the final days of manual craft: copper chisels worn down to rounded nubs, heavy bone wedges, loops of braided wire pulled from the cores of dead Silica-Trees. They do not eat the animals around them—their digestive tracts withered into dry cords ages ago, replaced by the same slow, radiological and mineral trickle-feeding that sustains the herds, supplemented occasionally by scraping a thumbnail of black, matte photosynthetic tissue from the paddle of a copper tree and tucking it behind a dry lip to dissolve over a month.

Instead, they tend the jam.

Where four tall parchment-beasts have drifted into the exact same square yard of granite, creating a blinding, colorless knot of static that makes the surrounding stones hum and lose their weight, a walker will stop. Over the course of three weeks, bracing his horn-padded feet against the cobblestone shells, he will slide a bone wedge directly into the shimmering seam between two intersecting ribcages. He knows how to touch the frayed places without getting caught in them. Millimeter by millimeter, applying the patient, relentless leverage of an expanding tree root, he prizes the overlapping coordinates apart—not hurting the beasts, who cannot feel anything faster than an earthquake, just nudging one reality three inches to the left until the grease-shimmer snaps out of existence and the two animals stand solid and separate once more, side by side.

Nobody knows if they do this out of some ancient, half-remembered custodial duty, or simply because the colorless static irritates their amber-glass eyes, the way a buzzing fly annoys a sleeper.

They are also the keepers of the broken cairns. If you follow a walker's shallow, twenty-eight-inch-spaced footprints backward for ten miles, you will find places where the visual echoes have been tidied. Where the top three stones of a trail-marker floated six inches off-center in the cool air, someone has stood for a season with both slate-dark hands pressed flat against the floating rock, leaning his dense, ironwood weight against the glitch until—with a soundless *pop* that leaves a faint smell of ozone in the dustless air—the stone surrendered its false coordinate and dropped back flush onto its base, its dark indigo shadow snapping obediently back into alignment with the rest of the pile.

Very rarely—perhaps once in a century of lowland time—two walkers’ paths will intersect on the crest of one of the sweeping, slate-blue whale-back ridges.

From a distance, watching them stand face-to-face amid the waist-deep sea of motionless, horned grazers, it looks like a monument left by giants: two narrow pillars of dark stone and copper twine, crusted with pale dust, framed against the flat, washed-out slate of the sky. They do not speak; there is no wet breath left to vibrate vocal cords, and the miles of surrounding chitin would swallow a voice before it crossed the twelve inches between their faces anyway.

Instead, over the span of half a year, they reach out and press their dry, horn-plated palms together, forehead leaning to touch forehead, letting the bone-to-bone contact carry the faint, piezoelectric *ticks* of their internal chemistry directly from one skull to the other. Through the slow ratchet of tendons and the forty-minute thud of tarry hearts, they trade whatever passes for news at the end of history: the shape of a deep spatial tear widening near the northern drop-off; the slow, four-inch lean of a copper tree in the third caldera; the exact count of pebble-cairns still holding their true shadows along the western rim.

And if you—warm, damp, blinking twice a second, burning through a lifetime of frantic breaths in an afternoon—stand right beside them while they commune, waving a hand in front of their glassy, dark-amber eyes, nothing happens at all. To their glacial retinas, you are less than a gust of nonexistent wind. You are a brief, invisible flicker of heat, too fast to cast a shadow on their minds, passing across the plateau and vanishing back down into the ochre haze before a single impulse has finished crawling the length of their dry, patient nerves.

Only years after you are gone, when they finally unlock their palms and step apart across the backs of the cobblestone herd, might one of them pause for a week—tilting his narrow, silt-caked head an eighth of an inch toward the dust basin below—puzzled by a chaotic, overlapping string of deep, frantic bootprints stamped into the ancient silt where, a blink ago, the mirror-flat dust was whole.
