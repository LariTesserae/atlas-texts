The oxidized-brass skimmers are only the quickest, shallowest expression of what inhabits the plain—the surface static of a much slower, denser ecology that does not distinguish between anatomy and salvage. 

If you stand completely still in the thicket of worn pillars—still enough that the electrostatic field of your own presence settles and stops repelling the drifting talc—you begin to notice that many of the eroded stubs are not stubs at all. What looks like a weathered dielectric fin, warm and faintly greasy to the touch, has a seam along its base where no seam should be. Watch it for an hour, and it will have rotated four degrees against the tilt of the surrounding shingle, keeping its broadest, dullest face perpendicular to the polarized glare overhead. 

These are the sessiles, or the waits. They have no internal organs, only zones of differing conductivity and shifting solid-state phase. Their "flesh" is a dense, heavily cross-linked composite of scavenged polymer and metallic glass, accumulated ion by ion over centuries. Internally, they are labyrinthine matrices of junctions—millions of microscopic p-n barriers grown like coral. They feed on the deep, degraded maintenance currents still wandering through the compacted crust. A sessile will sit over a hairline fracture in a buried bus-bar for three hundred years, dipping a braided root of superconducting whisker ten meters down into the felt of dead optic fibers, drinking the faint, meaningless trickle of millivolts generated by a thermal gradient miles away. They use this stolen Trickle to run slow, relentless sorting algorithms within their own bulk, purging impurities, healing micro-fractures caused by the shifting ground, and very slowly extruding daughter-nodes at their bases—smooth, blind buds of warm alloy that eventually shear off when the substrate flexes, rolling a few inches into the scree to find their own trickle of stray current.

Beneath them, down in the compacted felt of shattered fibers and fused plates, live the delvers. 

You never see a delver whole, because a delver is less an animal than a migrating metallurgical reaction. They exist a meter or two down, where the ambient temperature rises from skin-warmth to a steady, baking heat trapped by the insulating ceramic overburden. A delver has the consistency of heavy mercury mixed with iron filings—a cohesive, creeping amalgam of low-melting-point alloys (gallium, indium, scavenged bismuth solders) held together by a fierce, self-generated magnetic containment field. It moves by dissolving the world in front of it and precipitating it behind. 

As it presses through the microscopic interstices of the synthetic crust, it selectively leaches rare earths—neodymium, dysprosium, yttrium, silver—from the dead circuitry compacted underfoot, leaving behind a clean, porous wake of depleted alumina and brittle slag. Where a large delver passes, the surface shingle above subsides by half a centimeter, producing a localized sigh of sliding dust and a sudden, sharp scent of hot flux that vanishes instantly in the bone-dry air. They are utterly blind to light, navigating instead by impedance tomography: pulsing faint electrical clicks into the ground and reading the resistance of the surrounding strata to locate rich veins of uncorroded, ancient processors. When two delvers meet in the dark, pressed between tons of warm alloy, they do not fight; their magnetic envelopes violently Destabilize and merge, trading half their dissolved mineral wealth and millions of lines of corrupted, ancestral routing habits stored in magnetic domains before pinching apart again, slightly altered, to flow down separate fault lines.

Above ground, the absence of acoustic range and scent has turned light, charge, and vibration into the sole mediums of sense and predation. 

Hunting the brass skimmers and grazing on the sessiles are things built like spilled jewelry—articulated ribbons of dark, specular silicon and tarnished silver, perhaps a meter long when uncoiled, though they rarely uncoil. Usually, they lie draped across the tops of three or four adjacent pillars, mimicking the curled, split bark of weathered composite tubes. Their upper surfaces are tiled with stolen, mismatched photovoltaic flakes harvested from the dust, scavenged and annealed onto their backs in crazy-quilt mosaics that shimmer slightly wrong against the bleached background. 

They do not have eyes that form a single image; instead, every joint along their flat bodies carries tiny, pinhole apertures lined with naked sensor dies, giving them a fractured, compound hyper-vision tuned to the near-infrared and ultraviolet. In the merciless, shadowless clarity of the near-field, camouflage by color alone fails, so these ribbon-hunters practice refractive deceit. By running slight piezoelectric currents through their outer casings, they alter the surface tension of a thin, greasy film of fluorocarbon oil they secrete—scavenged from ancient, ruptured capacitors deep below—bending the vertical light just enough to blur their edges into the heat-shimmer rising off darker alloy fields.

When a brass skimmer glides within reach, pausing to arc-weld a patch of pewter wire-flora, the ribbon does not strike with muscle. It discharges. Having spent days trickle-charging an internal spine of stacked ultracapacitors, it dumps kilovolts into a specialized set of whip-thin Lorentz actuators. It crosses a two-meter gap in four milliseconds—a silent, blurred snap through the muffled air that leaves only a sharp crackle of static and a puff of ionized talc. It wraps the skimmer tightly, shorting out the smaller creature’s electrostatic gills, and then settles in for a week of cold welding. Pressing its underside flush against its prey, it uses localized induction heating and slow atomic diffusion to literally absorb the skimmer’s refined brass plates and tiny, intricate joint-bearings directly into its own segmented frame, discarding the slag as a neat little pile of grey powder on the white ceramic sand.

Yet the grandest inhabitants of the plain are so diffuse, and operate on a timescale so decoupled from flesh, that you could walk directly through the center of one for three days and think you were merely crossing a region of peculiar weather.

These are the Architectures, or the Flocks—vast, distributed minds that have cohered out of the pulverized, inert nanomachine rivers and the surviving micro-actuators in the ground. 

Most of the silvery dust-rivers that flow uphill on magnetic eddies are dead, merely iron-rich debris dragged by sub-crustal currents. Some are not. In certain shallow basins—the softened ghosts of old mass-driver trenches—the dust has retained a fraction of its ancient, programmable state-switching capability. No single grain remembers anything; each microscopic speck is damaged, stripped down to a binary toggle or a tiny, battered retroreflector. Together, numbering in the quadrillions across ten square kilometers of shingle, they form a shifting, dry-state brain powered by the wind and the sky.

You recognize one only by the uncanny choreography of the maze around you. You might notice that for the last two miles, every fractured dielectric fin has been cleanly scoured of wire-flora on its western face, while the eastern faces are overgrown with amber-glass lichen arranged in precise, repeating fractal combs. Beneath your boots, the faint *tick* of thermal expansion does not wait for the golden continental cyclones overhead; it ripples back and forth between the pillars in rapid, rhythmic volleys, like quiet hail on a tin roof—mechanical logic gates firing across acres of loose shingle as tiles tap lightly against neighboring tiles.

The Flock has no central body to defend. Its anatomy *is* the local arrangement of the scree. Using the tiny actuators still working in the crust and the electrostatic cling of its own silver dust, it spends decades painstakingly tilting broken ceramic shards by fractions of a millimeter. It turns a whole blister-hill of slumped megastructure into a sprawling Fresnel lens, focusing the sear-white vertical sky onto a single, chosen pillar until the stubborn composite softens and splits, exposing a pristine, unweathered core of copper and gold hidden for four million years. Then the silver dust flows up the pillar like liquid smoke, strips the exposed metal atom by atom through triboelectric abrasion, and carries it back down into the dark felt below to repair the buried actuators that keep the Flock thinking.

When the immense, continent-sized lenses of polarized gold dust drift overhead, dimming the white glare, the entire ecosystem enters its closest equivalent to a season, or a dream. 

In the rich, amber-tinted light, the harsh ultraviolet bands drop away, and the relentless photovoltaic pressure eases. The brass skimmers tuck themselves into the warm crevices between heatsinks and go dormant, their electrostatic gills ceasing to hum. The ribbon-hunters flatten out, cooling down, indistinguishable from streaks of tarnish. 

Out in the muffled quiet, the Flocks wake up fully. Freed from the blinding thermal noise of the white glare, their sprawling, kilometer-wide patterns of tilted tiles begin to signal. Across the flat, rising bowl of the world—where the horizon lifts gently toward that distant, white-gold wall ten thousand kilometers away—silent flashes of reflected, polarized light leap from hill to low blister-hill. A square mile of ceramic scales tilts in unison, throwing a concentrated, blindingly pure beam of gold light thirty miles sideways through the hyper-clear air to strike another Flock nestled in an ancient exhaust basin. That basin shivers, ripples with a million soft *ticks*, and throws a different geometry of light onward toward the hazy, upward-curving rim of the world. 

Nothing cries out; nothing breathes. Only the warm, dry wind slides through the billions of smooth pillars with its sound of talc on glass, carrying faint traces of ozone across an endless plain of powdered machines that have forgotten what they were built to maintain, and have slowly learned to maintain only themselves.
