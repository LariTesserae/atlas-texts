If you hold your gaze on a single dark street for half an hour—ignoring the milky tremor of the gutters and the hypnotic, unvarying crash of the vault—a piece of the masonry will eventually detach itself from a wall and climb the stairs.

At first it registers only as a shift in the wet charcoal gloom: a knob of river-chert or a crusted lump of flowstone changing its angle against a doorway. Then a pale, flat limb reaches out to find a purchase on a half-inch stair tread, and the proportions of the city suddenly snap into focus.

They have no name for themselves, because they have no voices, and no ears with which to miss them. Fully stretched—which they almost never are, preferring to keep their joints folded tight against the cold—one of them stands roughly the height of a man’s forearm from elbow to knuckle. Yet there is nothing delicate or insectile about them. They are built low, broad, and dense, with heavy-boned pelvics and bowed, thick-muscled legs suited to wedging into chimneys and hauling stone up vertical shafts. Their skin is naked, cool, and entirely devoid of pigment, showing the dull, translucent grey-white of tallow or wet alabaster, though you rarely see much of it bare. Like the architecture around them, they are encrusted. Because they move at a glacial, deliberate pace through the lime-heavy spray, minerals settle on them. Thin, flaking plates of grey travertine cling to the broad shelves of their shoulders, their spines, and the heavy ridges of their brows, stained here and there with pale green blooms of copper carbonate where they have brushed repeatedly against tarnished pins. When one crouches motionless on a parapet, knees drawn to its chest, water beading and rolling off its calcified back, it is indistinguishable from a gargoyle worn featureless by centuries of drip.

Their heads sit low between their shoulders without true necks, wide-jawed and flat-crowned so they can pass beneath low lintels without ducking. Where eyes might be, the skin stretches smooth and unbroken over shallow, fused sockets, save for two tiny, sunken pits beneath the brow-ridge that register nothing sharper than the vague presence or absence of the slime’s milky glow. Instead, their faces and forearms are crowded with sensory breadth of a different kind. Thick, fleshy, pale barbels—not whiskers of hair, which would rot or mat, but blunt, nerve-dense tendrils of skin—fringe their upper lips, their jaws, and the undersides of their wrists. When a mason-dweller moves along an aqueduct bridge, it walks on the knuckles of one hand and the flat sole of the opposite foot, trailing its wrist-barbels lightly along the slick stone parapet and dipping a lip-tendril into the rushing three-finger gutter.

Through those touches, it reads the entire pocket.

To live inside a roar that never ceases is to live in a medium as solid and informative as glass is to sight. To the dwellers, the deafening cataract-din of the vault is not noise; it is the baseline pressure of the world, and every structure in the city is a tuning fork. Pressed against a wall with its broad, bare chest or the sensitive pads of its heels, a dweller feels the distinct, thrumming signature of fifty different water-paths at once. A clean clay funnel high under the ceiling rings with a tight, crisp trill through the shale pinning; a cistern three stories down whose outlet grate has begun to choke with mineral rind sends a heavy, muddied thudding through the rib-bone corbels. If a hairline fracture in the dolostone vault widens by the thickness of a hair and doubles its drip-rate onto an upper roof, three dozen pale heads in dark rooms four stories below will tilt slightly toward the change in rhythm.

Watch the one on the stairs. It climbs with the slow, inexorable economy of a cold-blooded thing—or something very close to it, whose heart beats perhaps six times a minute to husband warmth and calories in an environment that offers almost none. Every movement is placed with total certainty before weight is committed; a fall from a sixth-story cantilever onto stone trenches below would shatter heavy, mineral-stiffened bones. Its fingers are short, thick as thumbs, four to a hand, tipped with flat, horn-grey nails worn square at the edges like chisels. Tucked into a fold of loose, leathery skin along its flank—a natural pouch spanning hip to rib—it carries a flake of black chert and a sliver of ancient, cured bone dark as bog-oak.

It reaches a congested intersection where three slate gutters cross above a tabletop plaza. Here, a lip of cold-hammered copper leaf, placed two hundred years ago to split a stream between two cisterns, has finally grown so heavy with blue-green crust and white flowstone that the water is spilling unevenly, starving one trough and overfilling the other. Even before the overflow could wear a new groove, the change in the stone’s hum drew the tender out of the dark.

The dweller wedges its wide hips between two thumb-thick colonnade pillars, bracing its broad, flat feet against the wet stone. It does not hurry. For ten minutes it simply rests both palms on the shaking slate, feeling the exact geometry of the blockage. Then it draws the chert flake from its flank-fold. With slow, grinding strokes—never striking, for a sharp blow might crack the brittle, calcified slate—it scrapes away the excess travertine rind from the copper lip. Pale, milky slurry washes away between its knuckles, briefly brightening the glowing slime downstream as the disturbed film flares. Using the flat of its heavy thumbnail and the bone pry, it bends the copper leaf back by a fraction of an inch.

Instantly, the pitch of the two cisterns below shifts back into their ancient, balanced fifth. The vibration under the dweller’s feet smooths out. It tucks the chert back into its skin-fold, lowers its chin, and licks the scraped mineral paste and slime from its fingers with a broad, rasping tongue.

That glowing film—and the sparse drift of life it supports—is their orchard and their herd. The city is not stacked six stories high out of vanity, nor out of crowding; there are scarcely forty of the pale builders alive in the entire pocket at any one time. The city is a farm for surface area. Left to itself, the vault would be a barren rock floor with water vanishing immediately into floor-cracks, supporting a few square yards of lithotrophic slime. By intercepting every drop at the ceiling and forcing it to travel hundreds of meandering, stepped, spiraling feet through open troughs, basins, and wide, shallow spillways before it finally drains away, the builders multiplied the wet, agitated stone surface a hundredfold.

In the broader courtyard wells and lower catch-basins, the hydraulic geometry slows the water just enough to settle into wide, trembling pans. Here the dwellers cultivate thicker mats of the luminescent film, seeding bare slates by smearing scrapings from rich gutters onto dry stone before diverting a trickle over them. Every few days—though days have no meaning here, time measured only by the slow growth of travertine and the sluggish turn of their own hunger—an individual will make a harvesting round, scraping half the width of a glowing trough with a curved rib-spatula and swallowing the cold, chalky, faintly metallic jelly, always leaving the upstream strip intact to regrow. When a blind amphipod washes into a curbside trough and is felt kick-twitching against a wrist-barbel, it is pinched neatly and eaten raw, shell and all, for its lime and scarce fat.

As for the great, pale salamanders that fill the narrow streets like sluggish barges: the dwellers treat them neither as prey nor as gods, though an outsider watching them meet might suspect either. When a dweller encounters one of the amphibians wedged across a sunken thoroughfare, its throat pulsing once every few minutes in the cold spray, the builder does not try to prod it aside. Instead, it lays one broad palm flat upon the salamander’s slick, cold skull—perhaps reading the health of the lower drains from the beast’s skin, perhaps merely acknowledging an older tenant—and then levers itself effortlessly up onto the nearest lintel, climbing over the roof-slates to drop back into the alley beyond. Only when one of the salamanders finally dies, once in an age, does the whole sparse population gather. Drawn by a subtle shift in the smell of the wet air or a series of rhythmic, booming foot-stamps struck against hollow cisterns, ten or twelve dwellers will converge in the dark trench to butcher the pale carcass with flint flakes. Nothing is wasted: the sparse meat is shared hand to mouth in absolute silence, the fat rubbed into tarnished copper joints, and the tiny, flexible ribs set into a dry, high niche under the vault ceiling to cure for decades in the mineral draft until a future bridge requires a corbel.

Inside the stacked houses—entered on all fours through knee-high arches that trap pockets of slightly warmer, dead air away from the spray—there is no furniture, no bedding, and no hearth. Fire has never existed here; a single smoky flame would choke the sealed vault in minutes. The interior rooms are smooth-walled stone chambers, dark as pitch, scarcely large enough for two or three dwellers to curl together like stones in a pouch. In these dry, inner recesses, where the outside roar drops to a deep, somatic hum through the floorboards, they spend eighty percent of their lives in a state halfway between sleep and torpor.

They press together back-to-chest and flank-to-flank, sharing what faint body heat their slow metabolisms generate, their wrist-barbels tangled loosely over one another’s wrists. That is their conversation. While they doze through long stretches of unchanging time, slight twitches of their tendrils, slow shifts of finger-pressure against a neighbor’s forearm, and the subtle flexing of toes against shared stone pass continuous, unhurried word of the pocket’s state: *the high east funnel thins; a harvestman has spun across the third-tier stair; two amphipods in the lower well; the arch by the bone-corbel thickens well.*

Occasionally, in the deepest, driest vaults at the base of the oldest towers, you would find a room whose doorway has been narrowing for centuries. When a dweller grows too old and stiff with internal calcification to climb the slick tiers—when its joints grind with lime and its skin-plates fuse across its spine—it crawls into one of these ground-level cells for the last time. Others bring it scrapings of slime and water in pinched clay cups until it ceases to reach for them. Nobody buries the body, for there is no soil to dig. Instead, a neighbor diverts a tiny, hair-thin copper spout from an adjacent gutter so that a steady, lime-rich mist breathes just inside the threshold. Over fifty or a hundred years, the doorway seals itself in milky flowstone, and the curled figure within becomes a solid, structural boss of travertine—another load-bearing pier holding up the five stories of wet black shale above.

Young are rarer than deaths seem to be, arriving perhaps once in several decades of surface time, single and large and pale, carried clinging to an adult’s calcified back with four stubby fists locked onto ridges of shoulder-stone until they are heavy enough to resist being knocked loose by a heavy roof-drop. From their first hour, their world is the roar, the cold, the chalky glow of the troughs, and the endless, patient truce between falling water and stacked rock. They are never taught how to build a new house; there has been no room for a new house in the pocket for a thousand years. They are taught only where the spare chert flakes lie cached under loose paving slabs, how to pry native copper from a deep floor-vein without snapping it, and how to feel through the soles of their feet when a gutter three roofs away needs a thumb’s breadth of clay pinched onto its rim.

Out in the street again, the tender by the colonnade finishes licking its fingers clean. For a long while it stays crouched on the tabletop plaza, water from an upper eave striking the stony shelf of its brow and shattering into cold mist that beads along its blind, tranquil face. Around it, the glowing milk-white threads of ten thousand gutters trace their crooked, tiered paths down into the floor grates, roaring with the force of a river inside a stone chest. Feeling no tremor out of place in all the six stories above or below, the dweller turns, drops onto all fours, slips backward through a hip-high arch into the pitch-black masonry, and pulls its limbs in tight against the cold, leaving the city once more looking entirely uninhabited.
