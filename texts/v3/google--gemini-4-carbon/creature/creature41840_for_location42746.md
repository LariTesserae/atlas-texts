No one lives on the ledges because there is nowhere to lie flat, and no human could sleep in that wet, chest-rattling roar. What lives here does not use the ledges at all. 

You have to look past the flashing undersides of the upward-streaming vines, down a yard or two below the lip of the lowest stone bridges, where the limestone stops catching the direct, blinding slam of the sun and curves inward to become the dark throat of the mountain. 

Pinned flat against the vertical wet bone of the shaft are things that look, at first, like slumped, waterlogged pelts—mats of pale, coarse hair grown green with algae, plastered to the rock by the sheer force of the rising gale. Then a raptor drops down the chimney, scattering the hovering swifts, and one of the mats shifts sideways with the sudden, multi-jointed snap of a crab, clearing a path.

The people who climb the outer spire to mend their sinew nets call them *the Rags*, when they speak of them at all, though the climbers mostly only see an arm—long, pale, double-elbowed in appearance because the wrist is nearly as long as the forearm—reaching up over a mossy rim to pluck a tangled swift from the bottom edge of a snare before the climber can haul the cord in. The climbers never contest the theft. They leave the vulture-bone whistle wedged in the cleft partly to mark the cache in sudden fog, and partly so the Rags always know exactly where the human territory stops.

A Rag is built for a world where gravity pulls down, wind pushes up, and nothing is horizontal. Stretched out across the curve of the inner flue, an adult is wider than a tall man is long, though its torso is no bigger than a soup kettle—a flat, flexible basket of broad ribs cushioned in dense, rubbery fat against the chill of the weeping stone. Everything has gone into the reach. Four limbs radiate from that central hub like the arms of a brittle-star, differences between arm and leg nearly erased by the chimney. Each ends in heavy,hooked spatulas of yellow horn—not sharp claws, which would chip on limestone, but curved wedges meant to slot into smooth, water-worn flutes and lock tight with the pull of a single tendon, allowing the creature to hang for days without spending a drop of muscle-heat.

When it moves along the curve of the ribs, it does not climb so much as haul itself from slot to slot in fluid, wheeling arcs, its body pressed two inches from the weeping rock, staying inside the thin boundary layer of slower air while the hurricane of the central shaft tears past its back. 

Its coat is double: underneath, a grease-thickened wool as dense as river-otter fur that keeps the skin dry even when water sheets directly over its spine; on top, long, hollow guard-hairs that trail upward in the draft just like the bladder-vines, streaked green and rust with symbiote mosses and rust-colored mineral stain. When a Rag locks its hooks into a wet groove under an overhang and lets its outer fringe stream up into the mist, a pika will run right across its knuckles to bite at an orchid root, never smelling beast beneath the wet-stone-and-fern reek of the pelt.

Close up, if one peers over the spruce-pole catwalk to watch a climber emptying the water baskets, the face is startlingly, uncomfortably consolidated. It sits low between the upper shoulders, tucked into a fleshy hood that shields it from falling grit and rising spray. The eyes are huge, set wide apart, and covered by heavy, translucent nictitating membranes that click shut sideways every few seconds like camera shutters to squeegee away the mist; behind those milky shields, the irises are the bright, startled gold of an owl’s. It has almost no external ears—the roar of the mountain would deafen anything with funnels to catch sound—only tight, muscular slits hidden in the fur behind the jaw. Instead, the skin across its flat throat and broad chest is bare, pale gray, and drumhead-tight, ribbed with sensitive ridges of cartilage. It *feels* the spire’s chord. It reads the pitch of the keyhole slots when the crosswind shifts outside; it feels the minuscule, high-frequency thrum of a hundred swifts banking at once, or the sudden dulling of the rock's resonance when a human boot steps onto an outer rib fifty feet below.

Its mouth is wide and lipless, opening vertically more than you expect, lined with interlocking pegs suited equally to crunching metallic beetles out of the air, grinding thumbnail-crabs shell and all, or shearing breast-meat off a raptor-dropped carcass. 

Because anything dropped in this place is lost—either plummeting two miles into the lightless gut of the peak if it is heavy, or blowing straight out the top of the cage into the freezing cobalt void if it is light—everything about the Rags’ lives is tethered, glued, or pouched. 

Across its belly, opening *downward* against the rock so the updraft cannot balloon it like a parachute, is a deep, muscular fold of skin sealed by a sphincter-like seam. Inside this warm, lanolin-slick pocket go the extras: three or four crushed pikas saved for lean days when ice chokes the lower vents and kills the bug-column; smooth, glittering gizzard-stones; molted swift primaries; and, for the first year of its life, a single rubbery-limbed infant no bigger than a human fist, clinging to a teat while its mother swings hand-over-hand beneath the thundering arches. 

Even their sleep is anchored. Lower down, just where the emerald cushion-mosses starve for light and give way to slick, black bacterial slime, small clans of five or six Rags gather in deep, scalloped vertical alcoves out of the main wind-blast. From glands at the base of their stubby, flattened tails, they extrude thick ribbons of amber mucus that hardens on contact with cold water into tough, horn-like straps. Over generations, these alcoves have become webbed with tough, translucent hammocks and safety-harnesses cemented directly into the limestone grooves. There they strap themselves in, overlapping like roof-shingles so the water sheets cleanly off the top elder and misses the younger ones below, hooking their horn-fingers into one another’s shoulder-fur, humming back at the mountain.

Their voice is the one sound in the cage that cuts cleanly through both the low organ-drone of the stone and the shrill, metallic chatter of the birds. They do not waste breath on air-borne cries that the chimney would rip from their throats and throw at the clouds. Instead, a Rag presses its broad, hard chin-plate flat against a dry vein of limestone and clicks its heavy throat-cartilage. The sound travels *through* the ribs—a sharp, resonant *tock-tock-trrrrrk*, like someone striking a heavy timber with a hardwood mallet, felt through the soles of the feet by every creature clinging to the crown.

Yet they are not strictly creatures of the rock. Every hour or so, when a particularly fat boil of warm valley air surges up the dark throat—bringing with it a swirling blizzard of stunned moths, winged ants, and loose fern-spores—one of the Rags will creep out onto an overhanging nose of stone where the updraft hits hardest. 

It waits until the sunlight slams through a gap, turning the rising spray into a column of fractured rainbows. Then it unhooks all four limbs at once and throws itself backward into the floorless ninety percent.

Spread-eagled, it reveals what stays folded and hidden while it creeps: thick, scalloped membranes of pale, violet-veined skin stretching from wrist to ankle, and from ankle to the broadened keel of its tail. It catches the vertical river of air with a wet, canvas-like *whump*. It cannot truly fly—put one outside the stone ribs in the thin, still cobalt sky and it would drop like an axe-head to the talus miles below—but inside the blast-furnace of the chimney, it doesn’t need to beat a wing. 

It hangs dead-center in the screaming void, fifteen feet below the sky, riding the column like a hawk on a thermal. By spilling air from one wrist-flap or curling its flat body, it corkscrews lazily upward through the fragmented rainbows and rising rain, mouth wide, funneling midges by the mouthful, reaching out with casual, hooked hands to snatch a hovering swift from behind before the bird even registers a shadow below it. Sometimes two or three of them hang in the shaft together, spinning slowly around each other in the mist, tussling in mid-air over a pale, heavy-taloned raptor they have mobbed and dragged out of its dive, tumbling thirty feet down into the dark throat in a Pinwheel of pale limbs and wet fur, only to flare their hides wide, catch the gale again, and shoot right back up into the blinding daylight at the top of the cage—spilling feathers toward the clouds before folding up at the last possible fraction of a second to slap, four-handed and silent, back onto the weeping bone of the rim.

They watch the climbers come and go across the slimy spruce poles. Sometimes, after the men have packed their dead birds and climbed back down into the white cloud-floor below, an old Rag will swing up out of the shaft into the leeward margin, hunching awkwardly where the lack of wind makes its long limbs heavy and clumsy. It will hook two fingers around the stone basin, dip its flat, owl-eyed face to drink the still, non-flying water the humans gathered, and sit for an hour beside the wedged vulture-bone whistle, pressing its throat-drum against the rock just an inch away—listening, with eyelids clicking steadily shut and open, to that single, thin, unwavering note that never changes when the weather shifts.
