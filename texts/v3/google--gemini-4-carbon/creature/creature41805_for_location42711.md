To live on the skin of a thing that refuses to scar, you must weigh almost nothing, or you must distribute your weight so cunningly that the floor never mistakes you for a wound. 

If you drop a stone onto the wet jade of an esplanade—assuming you carried one up from the world below, because you will not find a pebble here—the valley reacts. Within an hour, the clear sweat around the stone thickens into a bitter, astringent mucilage. Within three, a subtle, localized swelling of the epidermis tilts the intruder toward the nearest Pink Shaft, letting the sheet-flow slide it home into the roaring dark. The city-plant’s locked perfection is not passive; it is fiercely, chemically defended. Its sweat is laced with faint, volatile terpenes and growth-inhibitors that burn the root-hairs off stray windblown seeds and dissolve fungal spores before they can anchor. That is why there is no moss. Anything that tries to grip, pierce, or digest the tissue is quietly poisoned or sloughed away.

So the creatures that belong here—truly belong, over generations, inside that relentless, booming chord—are masters of the non-invasive touch. They are sparse, clean-lined, and strangely clean-bodied, living without burrows, nests, or territory, drifting across the monumental geometry like water-skaters on a fountain.

Begin at the level of your boots, in the warm, sixteenth-of-an-inch sheet of water sliding endlessly toward the drains.

At first glance, the bright film is completely sterile. Then a cloud passes the sun, the glare shifts, and you notice a faint interruption in the laminar flow: a V-shaped wake, six inches wide, holding entirely stationary against the current. Anchored there is something like a crustacean reimagined by a glassblower. It has no legs to scratch the wax; its entire underside is a broad, oval muscular foot, smooth as wet chamois, holding fast to the slick epidermis by sheer capillary suction. Its upper shell is a low, hydrofoil dome of clear, flexible cartilage, tinted the exact pale celery-green of the cambium beneath it. 

These *skimmers* do not graze on the valley—biting the floor would draw a rush of gummy, latex-like sap that cements mouthparts shut. Instead, they filter the sweat. The water wept by the terrace hydathodes is mostly pure, driven up to cool the sunlit skin and carry trace minerals, but no living membrane is entirely flawless. Microscopic blebs of lipid, stray amino acids, and traces of sucrose leak constantly into the runoff. The skimmers sit spaced twenty or thirty yards apart in loose, silent constellations across a flooded court, facing upstream into the millimeter-deep tide, combing the thin broth with feathery, iridescent gill-rakes. When one moves, it simply relaxes the rim of its suction foot and lets the water slide it fifty yards down-plaza toward the booming lip of a shaft, braking smoothly a body-length before the pink, ribbed drop-off.

Because there is no mud, no leaf-litter, and no shadow except the slow, crisp sundial-hands cast by the three-hundred-foot columns, predation happens in blazing, unobstructed daylight, and it happens in slow motion.

Watch that long-legged wader you glimpsed earlier—the crane-like walker half a mile out, taking twenty minutes to cross a court. Up close, it has nothing to do with birds or insects, though it borrows the engineering of both. Seven feet tall at the hip, its body is no larger than a greyhound’s ribcage, suspended high above the wet glare on four stilt-limbs of pale, mineralized bone sheathed in dry, ivory skin. Each foot ends not in claws, which would skate helplessly on the wax, nor in hooves, which would bruise the tissue and draw bitter sap, but in three wide, spatulate pads lined with microscopic, compliant ridges—like the toe-pads of a tree frog, scaled up to carry forty pounds across greased porcelain. 

Every step makes a soft, wet *shuck* that is instantly swallowed by the ambient roar of the valley.

The stilts have no ears—in a basin that sustains an eighty-five-decibel organ-chord day and night, conventional hearing is useless, a path to madness or ruptured membranes. Where ears would be, the narrow skull is smooth, polished bone. Instead, they read the floor through their toes and the air through long, tuning-fork filaments rising behind their eyes. They feel the deep, subterranean thrum of the vascular bundles, and they feel the minute skip in surface-tension when a glass-backed skimmer breaks suction fifty paces away.

When a stilt feeds, it is a study in delicacy. Sometimes, as you saw, it bends its long, counterweighted neck to press its split, velvety muzzle against the ninety-degree seam of a step-riser, sipping the warm, sweetish fluid straight from the weeping pores before the sheet-flow dilutes it. It never lingers at one riser for more than half a minute; stay too long, press too hard, and the pores constrict in local irritation, shutting off the tap. Other times, walking with infinite, unhurried poise, it slips the flat, horn-rimmed tip of its snout under the hydrofoil edge of a skimmer, breaks the seal with a practiced twist, and tosses the translucent disc whole down its gullet. 

Even their waste is shaped by the tyranny of the clean floor. A stilt never defecates on the open cambium—perhaps out of some deep co-evolutionary truce, or simply because fouling a terrace disrupts the clean laminar flow they rely on to hunt. Instead, a stilt will walk half a mile out of its way to stand at the lip of one of the roaring pink shafts, extending its hindquarters over the ribbed throat to drop a neat, dry, uric pellet straight into the booming abyss.

Which raises the question of the shafts themselves.

If you walk out across the warm, flooded jade—feeling the eerie, tireless pulse of the great xylem pumps beating up through the soles of your shoes—and peer down into one of those wide, circular mouths, the sound hits your chest like a physical wind. Thirty feet across, the shaft plunges down into the pale, damp interior of the valley’s under-structure. The walls are lined with tough, rose-colored epithelium, pleated like the inside of a bellows to handle the ceaseless cascade without tearing. Light reaches down perhaps a hundred feet, turning emerald and dim through the falling mist before the shaft curves into the dark resonance chambers below, where the water gathers to be re-drawn into the roots or vented out through unknown mountain fissures miles beneath.

You would think nothing could hold onto those slick, vertical throats under tons of falling water. Yet sixty feet down, arranged in a neat horizontal ring just where the shaft pinches slightly to form its acoustic choke—the exact spot that gives this particular drain its low, chest-rattling B-flat—cling the *reed-mouths*.

They look at first like growths of the shaft itself: fleshy, translucent-pink pennants, two feet long, streaming straight downward in the torrent. Only when one shifts laterally, undulating its muscular base across the ribbed wall like an abalone, do you realize it is an animal. They are blind, flattened filter-feeders, anchored by biochemical glue that mimics the plant’s own cell-to-cell adhesion, tricking the tissue into accepting them without sloughing. They catch everything the upper plazas wash away: drowned wind-blown insects carried over the high mountain rim from the ordinary world below, shed skin-flakes from the gliders, stilt-droppings, and the constant, dilute drift of plant sugars. 

Stranger still is what happens when too many reed-mouths crowd into a single shaft. Their hanging bodies damp the vibration of the walls and narrow the aperture; the shaft’s unchanging pitch bends flat by a quarter-tone. 

Up in the sunlit air, half a mile away under the curve of a parabolic arch, the flat-bodied gliders notice.

Those gliders—wide as dining tables, wafer-thin, coloured the translucent yellow-wood of shaded tissue—are not reptiles or mammals, though their damp, rubbery hides are warm to the touch. Their whole anatomy is built around two things: thermal theft and acoustic navigation. Having almost no body fat and very little muscle mass beyond the latches of their limb-girdles, they spend twenty hours a day pressed flat against the shady underside of the high arches or just beneath the circular parasols of the three-hundred-foot columns. There, the vascular pressure is immense, pushing water to the highest extremities of the living city, and the friction and cellular work keep the wood a steady, feverish ninety-eight degrees. The gliders plaster themselves over a main vein like living bandages, absorbing the metabolic heat of the monument so they do not have to burn precious calories making their own.

Because the valley has no landmarks in the usual sense—one smooth green court looks very much like the next, and clouds occasionally drift in over the high lip to turn the whole basin into a blinding white room—the gliders map their world by its chord. Each of the thousands of shafts sings a slightly different note depending on the depth and breadth of its chamber. To a creature born in the roar, that deafening wall of sound is a crystal-clear, three-dimensional topography of standing waves, phase-cancellations, and harmonic nodes. A glider launching into a thick mountain fog knows to the yard where every column stands by the acoustic shadow it casts against the eastern drains, and knows precisely which shaft has gone flat with too many reed-mouths.

You may see one peel itself off a column at noon—simply letting go, dropping forty feet in dead silence before its wide, taut membrane catches the rising warm vapor off the plazas. It circles down without a single beat, tilts vertically over a booming thirty-foot mouth, and folds itself like an umbrella to drop straight into the waterfall. Seconds later, tossed back upward on the violent updraft of displaced air and mist that boils out of the shaft’s center, it bursts back into the sunlight, shedding bright beads of water off its waxy wings, a fat pink reed-mouth clamped in its toothless, crushing jaws. One sharp pump of its whole body gives it ten feet of altitude; a wide thermal rising off an acre of sun-baked jade gives it two hundred more; and it sails lazily back to its column, slapping against the pale green timber with a wet *thwack* to digest in the roaring warmth.

Yet if you stay in the valley past midday, watching the blinding, jewel-like emptiness of the courts, you begin to suspect that all of these—skimmers, stilts, reed-mouths, gliders—are merely gulls on a harbor wall. They explain who eats the crumbs. They do not explain the harbor.

Why does a single organism grow ninety-degree terrace risers? Why throw parabolic arches across empty mountain sky, bearing no leaves, catching very little sun, serving no structural load? Nature grows hexagons in basalt and spirals in shells, yes, and big water-pumping structures exist in banyans and mangroves—but nothing grows a half-mile plaza sloped at an exact half-degree grade toward spaced acoustic drains unless something *taught* its meristems to want that shape, or is teaching them still.

Wait until dusk.

In the thin upland air, twilight is brief and cold. As the sun drops below the western rim of the valley, the photosynthesis across the miles of green pavement halts all at once. The valley does not go quiet—if anything, the roar seems louder in the violet gloom—but the *rhythm* changes. The sun-warmed skin cools rapidly under the clear stars; the thin wisps of vapor thicken for twenty minutes into knee-high fog before clearing. Deep below, the colossal vascular pumps step down a gear. The weeping at the terrace seams slows from a sheet to a sluggish trickle. The thousands of organ-pipe shafts drop in volume and drop together in pitch, sliding down a slow, mournful fifth until the whole basin hums like a banked furnace.

That is when the *grooms* come out of the columns.

Three hundred feet up, just under the wide, circular parasols where no stilt can reach and where the gliders sleep wedged in the warm crooks, the fluted grooves of the living timber conceal vertical slits—not wounds, but true anatomical stomata, five feet tall, rimmed with smooth, muscular lips of yellow-wood that stay sealed tight against the drying day. In the cold blue dark, those lips part with a long, hydraulic sigh.

What emerges does not look like a visitor to the valley. It looks like the valley’s own flesh given temporary leave to walk.

Imagine something the size of a man folded double, moving on six radial limbs of boneless, turgid hydrostat—the same principle that stiffens an octopus’s arm or an elephant’s trunk, except here the skin is pale, waxy, ivory-green, identical in texture to the plaza floor, though warmer and veined with faint, pulsing luminescences of deep amber where dense oxygen-carrying fluids move beneath the translucent hide. They have no heads, no eyes, and no mouths in the animal sense. At the central hub where the six limbs meet sits a dome of tight-packed sensory pits—thermal and chemical receptors—ringed by delicate, dexterous tendrils fine as surgical thread.

Six or eight of them slide down the sheer vertical shaft of a single column, moving rapidly, rippling their undersides in waves of grip-and-release over the wet flutes. Reaching the plaza, they fan out across the dark, cooling jade at a brisk, silent trot, looking like pale, six-pointed stars skating across black glass.

If you are still kneeling on the esplanade when one passes, do not move, and above all, do not bleed or sweat onto the floor. 

A groom’s entire existence is architectural. In truth, it is hard to say whether they are symbiotic animals that lost their autonomy ten million years ago, or detached, motile organs grown inside the brood-pockets of the great columns—white blood cells the size of wolves, budded off by the city-plant itself to do what rooted wood cannot. They have no digestive tracts; when they return to the column slits before dawn, they plug those fine central tendrils directly into the nutrient-rich phloem inside the stomata and drink the day’s stored sugars straight from the vein, before falling dormant in the dark warmth.

All night, under the cold mountain stars, they patrol the margins.

Watch one reach the crisp, ninety-degree edge of a terrace riser. It straddles the angle, pressing its central hub right over the seam, and runs at a steady walking pace for half a mile down the step. As it goes, its hair-fine tendrils probe every pinhead hydathode along the line. Where mineral scale has begun to crust a pore, a stiffened tendril reams it clean in a fraction of a second. Where a falling hailstone from a high summer storm bruised twenty cells of epidermis three hours earlier—a microscopic flaw that, left unchecked in an ordinary plant, might trigger irregular callus growth, a burl, a shoot, a stray leaf-bud that would ruin the line—the groom stops.

It hovers over the invisible blemish, pulsing faintly amber in the dark. From glands around its hub, it secretes precise, localized droplets of plant hormones: auxins, cytokinins, growth-suppressors, cellular signals dialed to the microgram. It is literally editing the valley’s growth instructions on the spot, telling this square inch of meristem *stop*, telling the neighboring millimeter *swell slightly to level the dent*, telling a latent bud *sleep forever*. 

This is why nothing advances, and nothing decays. The locked metabolic stalemate of the basin is not effortless; it is curated every single night by thousands of silent, tireless sculptors reading hormone gradients in the dark. Left alone—if some plague wiped out the grooms inside their high column pockets—within ten years this immaculate geometric dream would revert to madness. The ninety-degree steps would round over into bulbous tumors of green wood; wild, blind branches would erupt from the parabolic arches and tangle into sun-starved thickets; the drainage shafts would choke with unpruned pink flesh, their great organ-chord drowning in gurgling silence as the plazas flooded into stagnant, rotting marshes.

The arches, the columns, the wide empty intervals—they are the result of an ancient runaway feedback loop between host and tender. Ages ago, perhaps, an ancestor of the grooms cleared competing plants away from a water-weeping cliff tree, encouraging flat aprons of root-wood that shed more sugary water and warmed faster in the sun. Generation by generation, the tenders favored clean lines—surfaces easy to patrol, angles easy to verify by touch in the dark, resonant drains whose clear pitch proved from a mile away that the plumbing below was unobstructed. Aesthetics born out of sheer maintenance efficiency, magnified over geologic time until a mountain basin grew into a temple with no gods in it.

Once in a great while, down on the dark plaza, two grooms from different columns meet at the invisible boundary where their territories of vascular root-pressure overlap. They halt inches apart on the wet floor. Their fine tendrils reach out, interlace briefly in the starlight, exchanging chemical snapshots of the valley’s water-balance—*too much pressure in the northern risers; tell the eastern parasols to transpire harder tomorrow*—and then untangle without a sound, peeling away to finish their rounds.

And if they find *you* sitting there in the dark?

Two or three will pause ten paces off, their amber internal light brightening slightly as their thermal pits register your strange, hot, sharp-edged mammalian outline. Slowly, cautiously, one will glide close enough to brush the sole of your boot with three gossamer tendrils. Testing. Not for meat—they do not eat meat—but for threat. Checking whether you are putting down roots, whether you are secreting enzymes, whether you are dulling the ninety-degree crease of the nearest step or altering the acoustic pitch of the nearest drain.

Finding you inert, unrooted, just another warm, temporary lump like a sleeping glider, it will pull its tendrils back, step delicately around your shadow so as not to smear dirt from your cuffs onto the wax, and skate onward down the mile-long terrace—leaving you alone in the dark, warm, celery-scented air, until the sun clears the eastern rim, the great pumps roar back up to full pitch, and the fresh, bright water washes every trace of the night over the cliff into the clouds.
