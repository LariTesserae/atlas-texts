Before you see them in the fat-lamp’s orange puddle, you hear the clicking of their heels. 

They do not wear soft boots. Softness slips on wet rime, and softness mutes the world. Instead, they strap carved plates of dense, cured heartwood or grooved browser-jaw directly to their soles, fitted with twin ironwood pegs at the heel and toe. When they walk the forty-five-degree pitch of a trunk-highway, they drive those pegs into the grain with a sharp, percussive *snick-snick*. They are tapping the wood to hear how thick the ice is, how deep the sap runs beneath, and whether anything with hook-claws is galloping along the underside of the bough ten yards beneath their feet.

To live on a tightrope stretched over five miles of freezing nothing, a body has to change its mind about what matters. Height is a liability here; the wind shears off anything proud. The people of the upper lattice are built low, wide-hipped, and remarkably dense, with long, heavy-boned arms that hang nearly to their knees. Their collarbones are thick as sleigh-runners, anchoring chests swollen to barrels to process the thin, starved air. When a stratospheric gale comes screaming over a bark-ridge, they do not brace against it upright—they drop instantly to four points, hooking long, calloused fingers and spiked boots into the fissures of the wood, becoming just another low, streamlined burl until the gust passes.

In the rare, guttering light of a grease-bowl, their faces look almost carved from the same pale, winter-starved inner bark they braid into rope. Their skin is thick with a layer of subcutaneous fat and permanently stained in the creases with black resin, soot, and the rusty grease they smear over their cheeks and knuckles to stop frostbite. Their noses are broad and flat to warm the razor-cold breath before it hits their lungs, lined inside with stiff, dense bristles that catch freezing moisture before it can ice the throat. And their eyes—wide, startlingly dark, with irises so dilated they swallow the whites entirely—rarely blink. In the indigo gloom, when they turn their heads toward a grease-lamp, their eyes catch the light with a faint, wet silver-blue flash from the reflective layer behind the retina, like the eyes of deep-water fish or night-jars. 

When they step close to a lamp, they pull down thin visors of scraped, translucent horn or slotted bone, squinting fiercely against a brightness that you would barely call candlelight.

Watch one of them stop at the edge of a drop. Watch what they do before they trust a rope bridge. 

They take off a heavy mitten—stitched from three layers of browser-hide stuffed with shredded dry inner-bark—and press a bare palm flat against the naked, freezing timber. Look at that hand. The palm and the undersides of the thick, spatulate fingers are covered in pale, dry ridges, far deeper and coarser than ordinary fingerprints, almost like the pads of a gecko or the ribbed sole of a boot. Beneath that calloused skin lies a dense web of hypersensitive nerve endings. They close their eyes, tilt their head sideways so the wind doesn’t roar directly into the ear canal, and *listen* through their wrists and ankles. 

To them, the wood is transparent. Through the soles of their feet and the heels of their hands, the miles-deep lattice is a continuous three-dimensional map of vibration. They can feel the slow, hydraulic groan of a thermal plume rising two miles down—meaning the rime ice on the windward cliff is about to turn to treacherous slush in four minutes. They can feel the brittle, high-frequency chatter of a dead branch preparing to snap along its crystalline grain half a league away. They can feel the heavy, rhythmic *thump-scrape* of a browser herd gouging cambium around the curve of the trunk, and they can count how many hook-clawed predators are pacing them from below by the faint, syncopated prickle of claws striking the wood’s underside.

Because the wind steals voices and throws them miles down into the dark, these people rarely shout. Human speech at normal pitches gets torn apart by the howling aeolian harp of the root-voids. Instead, they speak in two registers at once. 

Close up, huddled inside their resin-mortared barnacle huts, their voices are fast, breathy, and full of sharp, percussive clicks of the tongue, palate, and teeth—sounds that cut cleanly through the constant, sub-audible bass drone of the flexing continent-tree without wasting precious lungfuls of thin air. They talk with their hands pressed against the ribs of their houses, or touching one another’s forearms and knees, letting chest-resonances carry half the grammar. 

Across distance, they do not use their throats at all. Every person wears a heavy striker of dense, fossil-hard knotwood slung at their hip. Every crossroads, camp-ridge, and tarn-edge has a sounding-fin: either a thin, resonant spur of natural splinter-wood shaved to a specific thickness, or a tensioned plank wedged into a bark cleft. A traveler approaching a county-sized limb-knot doesn't call out; they step up to a fin and strike a rapid, complex cadence. *Boom-tak-tak-boom.* The ironwood grain carries the shockwave for miles faster and cleaner than the thin air ever could. Three ridges away, inside a dark hut, a family sitting with their backs against the living wall feels the sentence arrive in their shoulder blades: *Two walkers, coming up from the wet side, dragging meat, wind rising behind us.*

Everything they own smells of turpentine, rancid fat, and crushed mint. 

Without ore or stone, they have become supreme engineers of grain, tension, and chemistry. Ironwood cannot be sawed easily, and chopping across the grain dulls bone in minutes, so they do not fight the tree—they read its stress lines. A carpenter here is half-acoustician, half-quarryman. To harvest timber or carve steps into a trunk-highway, they tap the wood with small bone hammers, listening for the tight, singing pitch of compressed grain. They drill a tiny starter-notch with a bow-spun flint-splinter (knapped not from rock, which none of them have ever seen, but from vitrified, amber-hardened ancient sap mined from deep scar-pockets in the boughs), pour boiling water from a gut-skin flask into the crack, and let the razor-cold air freeze it instantly. The expanding ice splits the crystalline wood with a gunshot crack, peeling off long, razor-edged laths as sharp as obsidian blades.

Sap is their metallurgy. Fresh from the cambium, the world-tree’s blood is thick, sweet, stinging with volatile oils and natural antifreeze. Gathered in wooden troughs, they boil it over small, fiercely guarded grease-lamps—suspended in cradles of wet heartwood packed with dripping frost-moss so the heat never touches the living floor beneath them. Boiled for an hour, the sap becomes a heavy syrup they drink hot with melted browser-tallow; it burns the throat like gin and keeps their fierce, furnace-hot metabolisms roaring through the freezing dark. Boiled for six hours with pulverized bone-ash and scraped bark-dust, it polymerizes into black pitch-iron: a substance tougher than horn, lighter than stone, and completely impervious to wet. They cast spear-points in wooden molds with it, glue hook-claws onto climbing-gauntlets, coat their glider-wing windbreaks until they ring like sheet-metal in the gale, and seal every seam of their huts until the interiors are airtight bubbles of dense, musky warmth.

Inside one of those barnacle-houses, the world shrinks to the size of a ribcage. 

You enter from below or from the extreme leeward tail through a double airlock of stiffened hide sleeves, crawling on your belly to keep the precious heat from spilling upward. Inside, there is no standing room. Five or six people—usually an extended knot of siblings, spouses, and cousins—sit wedged together in a tiered nest of cured glider-membranes and dry needle-mats. The heat of their own bodies, combined with one thumb-sized wick burning clear rendered oil in a hanging bone cup, raises the temperature to a thick, humid sixty degrees. It smells overpoweringly of lanolin-heavy hair, mint-tar, drying sinew, and hot broth. 

Here, stripped of their bulky, wind-shedding carapaces of overlapping bark-scales and padded hides, they look astonishingly limber. Their toes are nearly half as long as their fingers, prehensile enough to hold a bone awl steady while both hands twist inner-bark cordage. As they work in the near-total dark, they hum constantly—a low, multi-part, vibrating chord that matches or intentionally clashes with the deep structural groan of the bough they are glued to. It sounds like music, and it is, but it is also an anchor. If the pitch of the bough’s groan shifts by a quarter-tone against their humming, every voice stops dead in the same heartbeat. Hands slap flat against the walls. Someone reaches for the emergency harness-knives. 

Because nothing here is permanent, and the ground is alive.

That is the quiet terror and the central religion of their lives: the tree has not finished growing, and it does not care about them. A county-sized intersection that has been stable for three generations—a whole town of forty barnacle-huts clustered around a black tarn-lake, laced with ancestral bone-chime bridges and carved stairways—can change its mind in an afternoon. Deep in the abyss, a root-column shifts; a thermal vent migrates; two fused boughs under ten million tons of tension finally shear apart to reach an inch higher toward the indigo twilight. 

First comes the sap-bleed, bubbling up black and mint-sharp through the floorboards. Then the artillery booms begin directly beneath the tarn, shattering the surface ice into dancing white plates. Within an hour, a wooden valley splits open into five miles of howling dark.

So they keep nothing heavy that they cannot abandon, and they sleep clipped to the wall by braided gut-tethers, wearing their climbing-claws on their belts. 

Yet they are not merely survivors clinging to the bark; they use the abyss too. 

Go out to the splintered tip of a broken lateral bough when a major thermal plume begins to roar up from the buried core. When the rime frost starts hissing and sloughing off the wet side in sudden, violent cascades of slush, you will see three or four figures scrambling *out* toward the edge rather than retreating to shelter. These are the drop-hunters, the line-riggers, the crazy young ones whose ear-bones haven't yet thickened from decades of wind-roar. 

They wear slick, tight-laced suits of greased glider-membrane with broad triangular webs stitched from wrist to hip and ankle to ankle—not wide enough for true flight, which would break their arms in these gales, but wide enough to catch air like a falling maple seed. Each carries a coil of ultra-fine, resin-waxed sinew cord anchored to a heavy wooden winch spiked into the splinter-crag, and a heavy, barbed bone harpoon tipped with black pitch-iron.

They crouch on the flint-sharp precipice, four miles of freezing emptiness whistling beneath their spiked heels, clicking loudly with bone clackers held between their teeth—*tak! tak! tak!*—throwing sound out into the roaring upward river of warm, wet vapor, listening for the hollow, leathery echo of a skiff-sized glider riding the plume up from the deep dark. 

When the echo bounces back fat and close, they don't hesitate. They throw themselves straight off the wood into the pitch black. 

For ten, twenty, thirty seconds, they are creatures of pure void and howling steam, plummeting and banking on stiffened leather ribs, tethered to the living world by three hundred yards of humming sinew thread, hunting blind by the click of their teeth and the rush of displaced air against their greased cheeks. If they strike true, the winch-crew back on the splinter-crag throws their weight onto the wooden ratchets, hauling hunter and thrashing, winged leviathan alike back up out of the clouds of condensing frost before the hook-claws smell the turpentine-and-copper tang of blood on the updraft.

And when one of them finally dies—whether torn loose by a rogue downdraft, crushed by a snapping frost-bough, or simply worn thin by seventy years of burning fat in the thin air—they cannot bury them, for there is no dirt, and they dare not burn them, for fire belongs only in little moss-lined bowls. 

Instead, they carry the body to the dry, leeward cliff of an actively growing young shoot, high up where the bruised indigo twilight actually stains the bark. There, with bone chisels, they prize open a deep, clean pocket in the living cambium. They fold the dead knees-to-chin, pack their striker-mallet and climbing-pegs against their chest, and wedge them tight inside the wound. 

Within hours, the tree’s defensive sap floods the cavity, thick and clear and stinging of cold iron and mint. Within a season, the sap cures hard as amber, sealing them clear and unrotting behind a window of golden-black resin while new bark rolls slowly over the scar. Generations later, walkers tapping their way along that massive, sky-vaulting highway will strike their mallets against that stretch of trunk and hear a slightly denser, sweeter note in the timber—an ancestor swallowed whole into the architecture, lifted higher and higher into the freezing twilight as the world keeps growing.
