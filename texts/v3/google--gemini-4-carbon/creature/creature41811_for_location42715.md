At first the eye passes right over it, reading it as another pillowed slump of limestone along the eastern undercut, or as a fifth boulder settled near the bottom of the northern talus. In a place without directional light, nothing announces itself by its silhouette. Everything shares the same matte, buff-grey luminance, and whatever stays still belongs immediately to the floor.

Only if you measure the space against the sound of the western wall—counting the blunt *plink* of the seep, three seconds, four seconds, another—does a second rhythm eventually separate itself from the rock. Every twelfth or fifteenth drop, a low mass tucked inside the two-course stone rectangle expands slightly along its upper curve, holds that fullness across three more drops, and lets it go without a sound.

It lies on its side within the old foundation the way water lies in a dish, filling the corner where two dry-laid walls meet and cushioned by the drifted silt. Folded down like this, it is roughly the bulk of a large calf or a heavy, long-bodied man drawn up knees-to-chest, though its proportions belong to neither. Its skin has the dry, finely grained matte finish of unglazed stoneware, pale yellow-grey across the flanks and shoulders, darkening to the wet-putty color of marl in the deep creases behind the knees, under the heavy throat, and inside the crooks of the elbows. There is no hair anywhere on it. Where its hip, shoulder, and ankle bones take the weight of its rest against the bedrock, the hide has thickened over ages into broad, cushiony pads—not cracked or horn-hard, like a camel’s callus, but dense and yielding as damp chamois, silencing every shift of weight against the stone.

Its bones are heavy with lime. Centuries—or generations so slow and overlapping they amount to the same thing—of drinking from the crusted catch-pools have thickened its frame until the ribs are wide and flat nearly to touching, the pelvis broad as a mortar, the skull smooth and thick-walled with rounded brow ridges that merge seamlessly into the bridge of a flat, slit-nostriled snout. Moving quickly with such a skeleton would be costly and jarring, so it does not move quickly. When it decides to rise, the decision travels through it like warmth through a slab. First a broad, four-fingered hand—fingers thick, unnailed, blunt at the tips like river-worn chert—slides an inch across the silt and sets its palm flat atop the upper course of fieldstone. Five water-drops later, the elbow straightens. Over the course of ten minutes it brings itself upright onto wide, flat-soled feet whose heels extend well behind the ankle, giving it a stable, tripodal poise when it squats or stands. Upright, it is tall enough that its crown nearly clears the six-foot mark, slack-bellied and slope-shouldered, its head carried slightly forward on a thick column of neck as though perpetually listening to the floor.

Its eyes are wide set and shallowly socketed, without lashes or heavy brows, since nothing ever blows into them and the sky never glares. They are the exact flat, liquid grey of the oval overhead, with pupils so large and slow to contract that the iris is no more than a pale rim of chalk. Because the air at the bottom of the hollow holds a constant, cistern-like dampness, it almost never blinks; once in a quarter-hour, a pale, translucent inner lid sweeps sideways from the bridge of the nose to clear a speck of airborne dust, wiping the cornea clean without breaking its gaze.

Nothing else lives on the floor of the hollow because nothing small is permitted to remain unclaimed. The lowland plain thirty feet above is full of grass seeds, midges, ground beetles, wolf spiders, and wind-carried ants, and inevitably a few of them tumble over the inward-curving lip every day. They never establish a foothold. The creature’s broad soles and palms feel the slightest tremor in the packed silt, and its wide, unmoving ears—mere oval apertures set low behind the jaw—register even the dry tick of a beetle landing on its back twenty yards away on the bare rock. 

When something falls over the northern rim onto the slumped marl ramp, the resident turns toward it with the unhurried certainty of a tide coming in. It walks without lifting its feet high, setting the heel down and rolling slowly forward across the broad pad so that not a grain of grit snaps beneath it, ascending the four chiseled bedrock steps at the pace of a shadow crossing a dial. Those shallow hollows in the center of each tread were not worn by sandals or boots in a hurry; they were polished smooth by these damp, mildly acidic soles lingering three minutes on each step, day after day across spans of time that wore away timber and rusted iron down to nothing. Reaching the fallen insect—half-stunned from its drop, or weighed down by the heavy air—the creature lowers a warm, broad fingertip, presses gently so the thing adheres to the damp skin, and draws it back to a wide, lipless mouth. Even blown grass husks, spider silk, and stray bits of chaff from the plain above are gathered in this way, rolled slowly against a rough, rasping palate until every trace of nitrogen and salt is dissolved out of them, leaving the hollow’s floor scoured to its clean equilibrium.

Around the native plants, however, it moves with an ingrained, meticulous avoidance. It never steps within a yard of the hart's-tongue ferns in the central damp pocket, never disturbs the dark leaf mould slowly darkening beneath the sparse wild fig. Without wasps in the hollow, the fig cannot set true seed, yet twice a year it pushes out small, pale, hollow syconia along its flat branches, which swell partway and drop sterile to the talus. The creature waits until they fall. It will sit folded at the base of the boulders for an entire afternoon beneath a ripening node, gray eyes turned mildly upward, hands resting open on its thighs, waiting for the stem to part rather than reach up and risk snapping a twig of the pale bark. Even the dead leaves are left untouched until months of damp warmth have softened their veins into a dark, papery paste, at which point it licks them cleanly from the stone, leaving no litter behind for mold-gnats or mites to find.

To drink, it goes to the low, arcing wall at the base of the western cliff. It does not scoop with its hands or lap with its tongue, which would cloud the lime-rimmed pocket and break the silence. Instead, it kneels outside the unmortared slabs, lays its heavy forearms along the coping stones—their upper faces worn glassy and slightly concave where its wrists always rest—and lowers its face until its parted mouth just touches the surface tension of the basin. There it stays for half an hour at a time, drawing water in through the narrow gap of its teeth at the exact rate the overhang above replenishes it. Occasionally a drop detaches from the rock overhead and strikes the crown of its smooth skull instead of the pool, making a duller, softer *thock* than usual, and running down the shallow flutes behind its ears to leave a faint, powdery track of white carbonate when it dries. Along the base of its spine and across the knuckles of its toes, thin rinds of this mineral crust have built up like sinter on an old pipe, flaking away only when it rubs its back slowly against the undercut alcoves.

Whether its own ancestors quarried the flat slabs and laid out the rectangular shelter before their tools wore out and their language dried up for lack of anything new to name, or whether it came up through some long-sealed karst fissure at the bottom of the bowl and inherited the stonework from softer, shorter-lived builders, the distinction has ceased to matter. The architecture fits its body now, not a human’s. The two remaining courses of the shelter are not the ruin of a higher wall that fell down; there is no rubble around them because, over ages of absolute shelter from wind, upper walls were simply unnecessary. Stone by stone, spaced years apart, the higher courses were lifted down and laid neatly into the recesses of the northern ramp or fitted around the catch-pool to steady its rim, paring the room down until it was nothing more than a low kerb to hold a shallow bed of fine silt and keep a sleeping body from rolling onto cold bedrock.

When the flat grey oval overhead darkens through slate toward black, the air in the hollow does not cool so much as thicken slightly. The resident withdraws from the wet wall, crosses the wide paving-smooth expanse between the ferns and the fig, and steps over the low threshold of its rectangle. It turns once, slowly, smoothing the inch of silt with the outer edge of a foot, and lowers its heavy limestone bones back into the shallow impression of itself. Its eyes stay half-open in the dark, reflecting nothing. Outside its low wall, three seconds pass, then four, and the drop strikes the basin again—the water, the stone, and the breather holding one another at the exact same level until morning.
