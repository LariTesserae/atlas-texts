Forty yards down the dry scree on the leeward side of the spine, entirely out of reach of the spray and the roar, there is a hollow scooped into the rubble and roofed over with three overlapping horsehides pinned down by slabs of dead, weathered granite. You could walk past it at ten paces and mistake it for a collapsed cairn.

Three people are folded inside it, sitting shoulder to hip because there is no room to do otherwise, warming their hands around a soapstone pot of smoldering juniper charcoal carried up from four thousand feet below. None of them speaks. Even out here in the thin, ordinary mountain quiet, they cannot hear a human voice unless it is shouted directly against the mastoid bone behind the ear. Years of going headfirst into the freezing hydraulic thunder of the basin have made the bone inside their ear canals thicken and knob inward—smooth, ivory-hard growths that have narrowed the passages to pinholes. It is the body’s slow armor against bursting eardrums. Down in the timbered valleys where they winter, people think them dim or sullen because they stare at throats and lips instead of eyes, and never turn when called. Up here, the deafness is the only reason they can work at all.

The oldest of them—a woman in her forties with a neck as thick as a pine bole and white frost-scars across the bridge of her nose—has her bare left heel planted firmly against a spike of bedrock protruding through the floor of the shelter. She is reading the mountain while she chews dried horse-fat. Every few minutes, a faint, sharp shudder travels fifty yards through the solid granite spine and taps the bottom of her heel: another submerged plate shearing off inside the basin. She doesn't look up for those. What she is waiting for is a change in the steady, underlying tremor—a drop in pitch that means the deep fault-throat has choked briefly on fallen rock and then blown clear, slinging fresh stone up from the belly of the massif.

Beside her sits a younger man with his right arm bound tight against his ribs in strips of rawhide. Three days ago he was twenty feet down, hanging from a waist-loop on a bone piton, driving a fresh peg into a hairline crack, when a sheet of feldspar the size of a door split off the opposite wall. The shockwave didn't touch him with stone; the water itself hit him like the flat of an oak plank, snapping the ash haft of his maul clean across and turning his arm from wrist to collarbone the color of dark slate. He can wiggle his fingers today, which means nothing is broken inside, only blood forced out of the vessels into the muscle by pure acoustic violence. Across his knuckles, and across the forearms and shins of all three of them, the skin is crosshatched with hundreds of hair-fine, dead-straight white scars. Freshly split quartz and feldspar underwater are as sharp as volcanic glass, and in thirty-four-degree water you never feel yourself open up; you only realize you’ve brushed the wall when you haul yourself over the dry rim and find bright, hyper-oxygenated blood sheeting off your pale skin onto the scree.

They are quarrymen, though they have never swung a pick at dry rock in their lives. They come up twice between the June melt and the first hard blizzards of September, leading stubby, barrel-chested ponies as far as the lower talus apron two miles down, then carrying everything else on their backs: stripped pine poles, coils of green rawhide, bundles of split valley willow, rendering-pots of marmot tallow, and heavy leather sacks full of smooth, round river cobbles.

Nothing native to this ridge is round. Everything up here slices rope. Without the river cobbles from the old, soft valleys below—stones worn gentle by ten thousand years of silt—they could neither weight a basket nor sink themselves into the well.

When the tremor under the woman’s heel shifts into a cleaner, faster thrum, she taps the third member of the crew on the knee: a lean, broad-backed youth barely twenty, whose ear canals are only half-closed yet and who still winces when the big gas bubbles detonate. Outside in the thin noon glare, he strips completely naked in the dry wind, shivering violently before he has taken a breath, and the older woman begins working warm marmot grease and rendered fish-oil over him with both hands, troweling it thick into his armpits, groin, neck, and over the scarred domes of his ears until he shines dull yellow in the sun. Over his eyes he ties a pair of snow-goggles carved from mountain-sheep horn, blackened on the inside with charcoal soot and pierced with narrow vertical slits; without them, looking straight down into that shaken prism of noon light and micro-bubbles blinds a diver faster than a glacier field. Around his waist goes a wide rawhide belt hung with four twenty-pound river cobbles tied on slip-knots, and in his teeth he clamps a thumb-thick toggle of oiled pine.

Two paces from the lip, the silence breaks off like a cliff.

Stepping up to the wet granite rim is like stepping into the chest cavity of something immense and frantic. Thick, glassy ribbons of high-tension spray rear up two feet above the sharp stone lip, hang suspended in the thin air a fraction of a second too long, and snap flat with the crack of whipped canvas. Before the youth goes over, the woman kneels at one of the V-notches and presses a short stick of seasoned larch against the butt of a submerged pine pole, biting the other end of the stick firmly between her back molars. 

Through her jawbone, the bass roar of the water drops away into background pressure, and what comes sharpest up the wood is the bright, hard, staccato *clack-clack-clack* of the dark slate pendants hammering inside the willow cone five feet down.

To an outsider, that willow basket looks like an instrument of superstition or a bizarre trap. To her teeth, it is a gauge. When the currents rotate evenly, the slate chimes strike in rapid, ragged bursts—*clack-clack-clack, pause, clack-clack*. When a dangerous gas surge is gathering sixty feet down in the throat, the rising column stiffens and pins the pendants flat against the willow ribs, and the clacking suddenly stops. If the wood goes silent between her teeth while the boy is under, she will haul twice on his braided horsehair lifeline, and he will pull the slip-knots on his river cobbles and let the updraft shoot him out of the middle like a cork, praying he clears the walls.

The slate is clattering fast and crisp. She slaps his greased shoulder once. He steps off the rim.

He does not dive—diving into an erratic rotating column would slam him straight into a razor-edged buttress of pale feldspar. Instead, weighted by eighty pounds of valley stone, he drops feet-first down the sheer northern corner where they have hammered a ladder of split elk-bone pitons directly into a fault seam. The cold hits like an iron band cinched around the skull, squeezing a reflex gasp that he bites down into the pine toggle. Inside the water, the noise is no longer sound; it is a continuous mechanical battering that blurs the vision by shaking the eyeballs in their sockets and rattles the air inside the lungs like peas in a gourd.

Yet through the slits of his horn goggles, the clarity is terrifying. There is no top and no bottom, only sixty feet of blinding, fractured diamond light tapering down into a jagged blue-white slit where streams of tiny bubbles scream upward like blown snow, chased by wobbling, melon-sized domes of raw tectonic gas that gleam like quicksilver.

And the basin is not as empty as it looks from the rim. Once your head is under the snapping surface chop, the margins come alive.

Right beside his greased knee, woven into the frayed, pale wood-fibers of the broken maul haft resting on its narrow ledge, three tiny creatures no longer than sewing needles are clinging tight. They are larval arrowhead-gliders, newly hatched and entirely transparent save for two pinpoint specks of black pigment where their eyes are forming. Because the granite walls shear off every few days, nothing in this basin can safely cement its eggs to the stone; for centuries the adult gliders laid their tough, spiral-horned egg purses exclusively around the base of the metallic-green *wire-kelp* tufts vibrating further down. Since the quarrymen began wedging pine poles and iron-hard rawhide into the cracks, the gliders have taken fiercely to the wood and hide, gluing rows of rubbery, amber-colored cases into the underside of the willow cone and along the coarse two-foot net. The quarrymen never scrape them all off. In late August, before hiking down, they harvest a dozen adult gliders with barbed gaffs—not for the rubbery, ammonia-tasting cartilage flesh, which even dogs won't touch, but for their oversized, pale yellow livers, which yield half a cup apiece of thin, odorless oil that remains liquid in sub-zero gales and keeps rawhide bindings from turning brittle under ice.

Ten feet away in the open, blinding center of the chamber, two forearm-long adults flick past him, riding a spinning thermal of thirty-five-degree meltwater with minute, twitching adjustments of their scythe-fins. They are hunting upward. Every minute or two, high-altitude winds sweep stray insects—numbed snow-fleas, migrating moths, blown spiders carried miles up from the lower valleys—over the mountain's crest. Anything that crosses this courtyard-wide bowl is snatched out of the air by those strange, viscous ribbons of leaping spray and slammed down through the surface tension. The arrowhead gliders hang suctioned to the sheer quartz faces, pinprick eyes fixed straight up on the dazzling roof of the water, waiting for dark specks to punch through the mirror. When a moth breaks through, three gliders peel off opposing walls simultaneously, intersecting in the thrashing blue center with a silent snap of cartilaginous jaws before vanishing back into separate eddies.

Below the boy’s hanging boots, down past the twenty-foot mark where no human diver has ever survived the pressure spikes, the ecology grows stranger and older than the wind-blown surface trade.

Those solitary tufts of *wire-kelp* anchored in the fresh winter micro-fractures are not true plants at all, or at least not wholly photosynthetic; their stiff, copper-colored strands are armored in precipitated silicates and dissolved heavy minerals drawn straight from the fault gas, humming at a pitch you can feel in your fingertips from a yard away. Clinging flat around their holdfasts—invisible from the rim because they match the wet glint of dark mica—are palm-sized, blind grazers shaped like overlapping plates of hammered horn. They have no heads to speak of, only a muscular grey foot capable of clamping to polished quartz with five hundred pounds of suction and a rasping tongue toothed with raw iron oxide, scraping microscopic skins of sulfur-eating bacteria off the copper-stiff fronds. 

Further down still, past the sixty-foot point where even noon light dissolves into white, boiling fizz, the inverted pyramid pinches down to a roaring throat scarcely wider than a chimney. Down there in the dark, where glacial meltwater forced miles beneath the range meets hot, mineral-thick gas squeezing up from the grinding roots of the massif, thick curtains of pale, stringy fleece coat the trembling stone lips of the vent. Every time a melon-sized gas bubble tears loose from the fissure, it carries shredded wisps of that deep-earth grease upward inside its un-popping skin. When the bubble blasts apart at the surface with a wet slap, those microscopic films of fat spray outward onto the dry upper lip—feeding the pale, rubbery crusts of translucent lichen that swell and flatten in the wind. Everything here eats violence in one form or another.

The youth doesn't look down into the chimney. Hand over hand down the bone pitons, fighting currents that try to peel him sideways off the wall like a strip of bark, he reaches the wide-mesh rawhide net stretched across the fault line.

He wraps both legs around the anchor-peg and grabs the gleaming white lump caught in the center of the webbing.

This is what they endure the deafness, the broken mauls, and the marrow-deep cold to harvest. Down in the deep throat, miles of granite weight are actively crushing an ancient hydrothermal vein, tearing loose chunks of deep-earth quartz that have never known frost, surface air, or gradual weathering. Ninety-nine pieces out of a hundred have microscopic internal flaws; as the violent updraft hurls them forty feet upward and drops them back down to grind in the vent, they shatter into sharp gravel and wash back into the dark. Only a nodule of absolute, flawless structural purity survives being hammered in the throat and flung high enough to tangle in the two-foot mesh, where it hangs vibrating like a trapped heart in the blast.

He wrestles the wet rawhide cord loose with numb, clumsy thumbs—he has roughly ninety seconds from leaving the rim before his fingers stop obeying his brain entirely—and shoves the heavy, jagged chunk into a deer-hide pouch slung across his chest. Before letting go of the net, he reaches into a second pouch and pulls out two fist-sized blanks of dark valley slate, rough-chiseled down at camp, and hooks their horsehair loops onto the bottom strand of the webbing to season for three days in the sonic battering. What doesn't split apart by Friday will hold an edge sharp enough to shave horn and tough enough to strike fire for a lifetime.

His vision narrows at the edges into grey tunnel-frost. Time is up.

He yanks the two rawhide toggles at his hips. The four river cobbles drop away from his belt, plummeting down into the dazzling blue funnel—two seconds later they hit a tapering granite plane forty feet down with dull, muffled *thuds* before tumbling into the throat—and instantly his greased body surrenders to the rising column of gas-charged meltwater. He tucks his chin to his chest, crosses his arms over his goggles to guard his face from stray wall-strikes, and lets the mountain spit him upward through thirty-five-degree prism-light.

He breaches waist-high in a burst of snapping, glassy ribbons, catches the thick pine pole wedged into the rim notch before the back-wash can drag him down again, and is hauled bodily over the razor-sharp lip by the older woman and the one-armed man, who hooks a wrist under his armpit with his good hand.

They don't linger on the lip—not even for five seconds. Carrying the dripping stone, all three scramble two paces back over the granite crest and drop flat onto the dry, sun-warmed scree, back into thin air and high-altitude quiet.

While the youth curls trembling under a horsehide, coughing pink froth from the cold-shock and spitting out the scarred pine toggle, the woman pulls the white lump from his pouch. In dry daylight it doesn't look like common mountain quartz. It is clear as winter ice right to its core, heavy for its size, its fractured planes so fresh they smell sharply of gunflint and bruised iron. When she strikes its corner lightly with a pebble of ordinary granite, a pale, greenish-yellow spark jumps *inside* the translucent stone itself—the trapped, restless strain of twelve thousand feet of rising mountain still locked in its lattice, prized across five river valleys below by lens-grinders, surgeons, and winter shamans who pay four ponies for a piece the size of an apple.

Hours later, when the sun drops behind the western crags and the three quarrymen seal themselves back into their low hide-hut down the slope, the courtyard-wide bowl up on the spine loses all its blinding noon geometry. The surface turns the color of cold lead, then pitch black under thin, dry stars. Nobody comes near the rim in the dark; one slip on spray-glazed feldspar would mean vanishing forever into the roaring chimney.

Yet the basin does not go wholly dark. Every few minutes through the freezing night, as the massif continues its slow, agonizing uplift and fresh slabs shear off deep down in the sixty-foot point, raw quartz grinds against raw quartz under tremendous hydraulic weight—sending brief, silent blooms of cold amber and electric-green lightning flashing upward through the clear, thrashing water, illuminating for a split second the copper-stiff kelp, the wooden frames drinking the shockwaves, and twenty pale arrowheads suspended motionless in the updraft, staring straight up at the stars.
