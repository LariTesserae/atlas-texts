To find the people of the plateau, you have to look behind the double-paned glass of the conservatory-cabs, or peer through the thick, resin-yellowed bullseyes set into the bark of the factory-groves. Out on the open root-flats, a human being looks wrong—too vertical, too soft, an unanchored splinter waiting for the wind to plane it away. Inside, warm and sealed into the pressurized ribs of the architecture, they make sense.

They are broad-chested, barrel-ribbed people, shaped by generations of thin air into walking bellows. Even the children have the heavy, sloped shoulders of deep-water divers, their blood thick and dark with extra red corpuscles that flush their cheeks and knuckles a permanent, wind-chapped plum. They move with a peculiar, rolling gait—knees slightly bent, weight kept low—the unconscious adaptation of a populace raised on a floor that breathes, shudders, and never stops vibrating. Because there are no quarries, no mines, and no herds up here, almost everything they wear and use is an exudate or an heirloom. Iron and brass are dynastic. A family’s social standing can be read in the rivets of their heavy, multi-generational coats—garments tailored from vulcanized cambium-felt, oiled with pine-tar, and stiffened at the joints with salvaged copper grommets. When an elder dies, the brass buttons, belt-buckles, and breathing-valves are respectfully unthreaded from their burial shroud before the body is lowered into a Grafting Pit, where the warm, weeping callus-wood seals over them in a matter of weeks, absorbing bone-calcium directly into the city’s foundation. To say someone has "gone into the grain" is not a metaphor; mothers bring teething infants to press their gums against the smooth grey swells above the pits, hoping the ancient wood will pass some of the ancestor’s stubbornness back up through the sap.

They are largely deaf to the registers lowland ears rely on. The relentless *thud-hiss* of the engines and the mountain-lung *blare* of the relief towers would shatter an unconditioned mind, so the locals stop guarding their eardrums early and let the high frequencies burn out. By thirty, most speak in a flat, humming chest-voice pitched to slide *under* the machinery’s roar, supplemented by a crisp, rapid sign-language of tapped wrenches, flashed brass knuckles, and boot-stomps sent shivering along the floorboards. You do not shout across a stamping mill; you rap an iron tie-rod three times with a spanner, and forty yards away, your apprentice feels the syllable in the soles of his boots and opens a bleed-valve.

Their true genius—and their strangeness—lies in how they navigate the burls.

Those surveyors’ tripods out on the empty plazas, with their heavy brass plumb-bobs hanging crooked in the crisp light, do not set themselves out. That is the work of the Line-Walkers, the plateau’s most vital and unsettling guild. While the conservatory-tenders stay warm behind glass, nursing the great hydraulic looms and simmering vats of amber rosin, the Walkers go outside. They wear heavy, lead-weighted clogs—the lead brought up two centuries ago and guarded more fiercely than gold—and long, billowing coats stiffened with reed-splints so the wind catches them predictably, rather than whipping them into rags. 

A Walker does not look where they are going; looking down a three-mile boulevard where space has knotted around a refinery will make an ordinary person drop to all fours and vomit from sheer geometric vertigo. Instead, Walkers navigate by *tension*. They carry long staves shod with brass tuning-forks, striking them against the polished elephant-hide of the ground every dozen paces. Where reality is straight, the wood rings C-sharp. Where an ancient injury or an overworked turbine has twisted space along the invisible rings of atmospheric grain, the pitch drops into a sour, buzzing flat, and the Walker steps sideways—apparently into thin air—sliding effortlessly across two hundred yards of compressed distance without breaking stride.

Long exposure to the frayed viaducts and non-Euclidean heartwood leaves its mark on them. Old Walkers become slightly unmoored from sequential time, fraying just like the cause-and-effect around the rusted girders they inspect. You can spot a veteran Walker in a taproom—warm, resin-scented caves carved directly into the swollen bases of relief towers, where workers drink hot, turpentine-sharp tea brewed from steamed pith—by the way they flinch at spilled cups three seconds *before* an elbow knocks them over, or answer questions a companion is still gathering breath to ask. Their shadows, under the glare of pressurized gas-lamps, sometimes lag half a step behind their heels, edges blurred and feathered like primordial fronds. Nobody pities them for this. Up here, being slightly ahead of the hammer’s fall is considered a practical trade skill.

Beneath the humans, clinging to the margins of the infrastructure they maintain, a quieter ecology has learned to exploit the breakdown.

In the deep axle-pits, down among the grease-slick mosses and the forearm-long, bone-white millipedes, live the Valve-Crabs—though they are neither crabs nor insects, but tough, spider-limbed things armored in plates of calcified resin they scrape and cement onto their own backs. They feed on the microscopic spray of sugars forced out of weeping piston-seals, clustering around high-pressure joints like barnacles on a hull. Maintenance crews leave them alone; the creatures have an uncanny sensitivity to turgor spikes. Ten minutes before a deep-root surge threatens to blow an iron corset apart, every Valve-Crab in the pit will suddenly unhook its claws, fold into a tight amber ball, and roll clattering into the dry drainage channels, giving the engineers just enough warning to throw the manual bypasses.

And out in the bright, empty stretches between districts, where the wind planes the grey wood smooth and the upward-falling rain pops in the dry air, there are the Ghost-Grafts.

They are not animals, not quite, and certainly not spirits—everything up here is stubbornly, overwhelmingly physical, even when physics bends. Centuries of forcing lowland iron into timeless cellulose, while space curls and remembers what the plateau was four hundred thousand years ago, has occasionally prompted the substrate to bud things it no longer knows how to finish. 

In the warped zones around abandoned viaducts, lumps of grey callus-wood will sometimes swell, split open like dry seed-pods, and extrude brief, wooden approximations of fauna that died out eons before the plateau rose above the clouds—or things the wood has half-remembered from the vibrations of passing locomotives. A three-legged shape of flexible, unbarked withes and knotted sapwood, headless, clicking rhythmically like a cooling boiler, will pull itself free of a burl and totter across the sunlit plaza. It has no mouth, no eyes, no stomach; it is driven entirely by internal hydraulic pressure, a walking wrist-thick bundle of xylem tubes flexing in the crisp afternoon light. 

Sometimes it walks in a tight circle following a localized gravity-knot until its sap dries in the wind, freezing it into a pale, twisted sculpture that the next locomotive crew will unceremoniously chop up for boiler-fuel. Other times, caught in a spatial slipstream, one of these wooden automatons will step sideways into an invisible ring of grain and vanish, reappearing half a mile away right beside a brass tripod, tapping a blind wooden toe against a forty-five-degree plumb-bob as if checking the calibration.

Even the solitary hawks riding the high thermals have adapted to the strangeness. They are not looking for rodents anymore; there haven't been mice up here in ten generations. They are broad-winged, pale-feathered scavengers of the thin places, watching the air *above* the cracks in the wood. When the upward rain shoots fifty feet into the sky—carrying with it tiny, translucent blind-shrimp and pale grubs sucked out of flooded, subterranean root-chambers two miles down—the hawks fold their wings and stoop *sideways* through the severed steam-plumes, snatching deep-earth protein out of the bright, popping air before the memory of water snaps shut again.

At dusk, when the sun drops below the plateau's lip and the pale vault turns an instant, freezing indigo, the conservatory-cabs roll down the amber-greased tracks toward the factory-groves, docking like iron beetles into the warm bark. Shutters of heavy, riveted boiler-plate clang down over the glass bullseyes.
