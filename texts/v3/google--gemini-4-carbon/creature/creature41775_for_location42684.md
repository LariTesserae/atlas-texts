At first, you would look in the wrong register. You would wait for a stir among the leather-stiff fronds, or listen for the wet scrape of a belly over stone, expecting something scaled down to cave life—eyeless, translucent, conserving itself in the cold. Nothing like that is here. Every leaf is whole because nothing here has jaws, or a gut, or the furious metabolic furnace required to tear plant matter apart and burn it into motion.

What lives here is draped across the upper tiers of the thicket like a spilled measure of colorless grease.

If you passed a hand blindly over the shingled leaves near the east wall, you wouldn’t feel a body so much as an abrupt failure of friction—a broad, cold slickness three spans wide, thicker in the palm than heavy cream, thinning out at its margins until it is no more than a glossy glaze wedged into the overlaps of the foliage. Under a lamp it would have the milky, depthless clarity of cataract glass or raw egg white toughened by frost, shot through with a faint internal grain where stray mineral dust has been swallowed and held in suspension. Down here, in the faint photon-drizzle of the relics, it is invisible except where a rare glint catches the meniscus of its edge, curving tightly between two dark blades.

It has no skin, only a boundary—a tough, elastic surface tension reinforced by long-chain sugars and natural antifreeze drawn from the damp air and the sweating exudates of the bryophytes. It does not eat the garden. Eating is too violent, too wasteful for zero degrees. Instead, it *couples* with it.

Beneath its main mass, thousands of hair-thin hyaline threads reach downward through the half-meter cushion of moss, bypassing the living stems to mingle directly with the white fungal mat and the black, compacted peat below. It drinks the surplus electrons bled off by the artifacts before the roots can claim them all, using the plants the way a lichen uses its algae, or a shepherd uses a banked fire—not consuming them, just lying across their crowded shoulders to intercept the slow, cold cascade of chemical potential rising from the floor. Where its underside presses flat against a broad frond, it leaves the leaf entirely unharmed, even cleaner than before, stripped of stagnant water films and stray fungal spores, its waxy cuticle polished smooth.

For weeks at a time, it is scarcely an animal at all. It is just a cold lens of organized water and protein sitting at the freezing point, letting the chamber happen through it. When a supercooled drop detaches from a limestone rib overhead and strikes its back, there is no crisp *tock*—only a muted, gelid *thup*. A slow dimple opens, holds its shape for half a minute like cold tallow, and then eases flat again as the fresh water is absorbed, salted with glycols, and drafted into its mass.

Yet it has an occupation, and a preference, and something resembling a mind, though that mind is spread out flat and runs at the speed of freezing sap.

Over the course of days, it travels. One margin thickens, gathering hydraulic weight, while the trailing edge withdraws from the leaf-crevices without leaving so much as a smear behind—clean as mercury rolling off slate, though infinitely slower. An inch an hour is a headlong rush for it. It flows over the stiff tiers of vegetation toward the embedded polyhedra in the east wall, drawn by the dry, sub-audible ticking in the rock.

When the artifacts shed their tiny increments of entropy, the surrounding stone and root-mat suffer minute, localized shocks—a sudden clustering of bitter metallic ions in the water film, a microscopic sear of ozone, a spot where a hair-fine filament crosses a wet gap and freezes the damp air around itself into needles of aberrant, high-density rime. Left alone over centuries, the crowding flora would press blindly into those filaments, severing their own slow-growing stems on the impossibly rigid threads or poisoning their root-tips against pockets of ionized slush.

The pale mantle arrives hours or days after a phase-tick and settles over the disturbance.

Near the east wall, one of its lobes—blunt, finger-thick, stiffened internally by reversible rods of crystallized protein—lifts away from the foliage into the freezing air. The effort takes half a morning. Blindly, guided entirely by the steep electrostatic gradient in the dark, the lobe reaches into a stone crevice until its tip meets one of the hair-fine filaments. It cannot touch the relic-stuff directly any more than the calcite collar can touch the central plinth; a tenth of a millimeter away, its pale flesh flattens against that same frictionless wall of sheer repulsion, forming a neat, cylindrical sleeve around the invisible boundary.

And there it holds. For days it hangs suspended between rock and leaf like an icicle growing sideways, passing its own slow, salty circulation around the filament's sheath. Inside its translucent bulk, faint, milky whorls gather and disperse—tiny internal currents ferrying away the stray, jagged ions, absorbing the stray low-energy photons before they can scatter, buffering the cold acid buildup where stone and non-stone interpenetrate. When it finally unclasps and sinks back into the moss a week later, the air smells sharply of crushed pine and clean rain—the waste scent of its quiet alchemy—and it carries away a pinpoint fleck of inert, black mineral ash suspended deep inside its jelly.

It has been doing this for longer than the upper chimney has been sealed. It cannot digest those black flecks of precipitated waste, and it will not drop them onto the immaculate leaves below, so it herds them inward to the oldest, densest middle of its body, just above the central plinth.

If you knelt by that dark, knee-high stone and felt carefully through the surrounding crown of bent-back fronds, you would find the heart of the creature draped like a heavy, frigid saddle around the plinth’s dry calcite collar. Deep inside that thickest fold—cold as iron, tough as gristle—hangs a tight, constellation-like cluster of thousands of microscopic black grains, arranged in faint concentric shells like the rings of a yew tree. Every century of ticking stone is recorded there in suspended soot.

Sometimes—perhaps twice in a human year—the whole dispersed thing gathers itself in. Every distant ribbon and root-thread retracts from the walls, climbing down out of the moss and drawing back across the slate-purple leaves over the span of a fortnight, until the entire room’s population is revealed to be a single continuous sheet, pooled densely around the central plinth in a smooth, pale ring the size of a cartwheel.

During these gatherings, the chamber grows subtly darker. The ghostly visual static in the air dims by half because the creature has covered the lower dampness, catching the stray photons inside its own cloudy thickness. Inside that ring, slow, silent waves of contraction pass around the plinth—one ripple every ten minutes—re-sorting its memories of every angle of rock, every crowding stem, every creeping millimeter of growth in the six-pace world, redistributing its antifreeze and its gathered charge until no part of it is older or hungrier than any other.

Then a drop of water falls from the ceiling flutes—*thup*—into the center of its quietness. By the next day, the ring has begun to thin at the edges again, sending flat, clear tongues gliding imperceptibly back up into the dark leaves, keeping the cold garden whole, smoothing the seams where the buried artifacts lean against the world.
