At the bottom of the thirty-degree slope, where the fallen slabs prop one another up on razor edges, the dark triangular voids underneath are not empty. 

Look into the widest gap—a low, sharp-roofed prism of shadow just beneath the great plum-black fan—and wait for your eyes to adjust to the dim sodium-amber bleed at the edges of the bruised violet light. Something is resting on the bare stone inside, though "resting" is the wrong word for a posture kept under such taut, spring-steel tension. 

At first it looks like another mineral accumulation, or perhaps a bundle of the tarnished-brass ribbons woven and discarded by whoever left the obsidian blades. It is roughly the length of a human forearm, flattened so severely from top to bottom that it is nowhere thicker than two fingers stacked together. That flatness is the oldest truth about it: an anatomy negotiated entirely with hairline chimneys and wedged fissures. Its hull is a matte, slate-dark mosaic of overlapping scales, not keratinous like a reptile’s, nor chitinous like the mica-drummers, but sintered from the room’s recycled silica and bound with polymerized heavy oils until it resembles oiled slate or dark, unglazed stoneware.

There are four of them in the chamber right now, though you will never see two on the same slab. 

Watch the one under the triangular arch as it decides to move. It does not walk so much as unlatch and re-secure itself in rapid, dry fractions. Six limbs reach radially from the flat body, double-jointed horizontally like a whip-spider’s so its belly never has to rise more than a quarter-inch off the fresh, cutting stone. At the end of each limb is not a claw—claws dull on raw trachyte, and once dulled in this dry kiln they stay dull until the next molt—but a broad, spatulate pad set with hundreds of microscopic, angled silica hooks, like dry gecko-setae forged out of glass. When it steps onto a conchoidal blade sharp enough to lay open leather, it sets the pad parallel to the edge, locks into the micro-grain of the stone, and pulls its flat mass smoothly over the razor without snagging or scraping. It makes no sound of its own. In this room, making a sound to announce yourself would be like lighting a match on the surface of the sun; the air is already completely full.

Instead, it moves *through* the sound the way a trout moves through heavy rapids.

Notice its trajectory as it slides out from beneath the slab and begins ascending the canted floor toward the spire of glassy black stone. It does not go straight. It tacks sharply left for ten inches, stops dead with one forelimb lifted, shifts two inches right, then glides swiftly up a bare diagonal ramp of dark rock. It is navigating the standing pressure field. Where the multi-tiered chord knots into a high-pressure node that would rattle its internal plates and waste precious kinetic energy, it skirts the invisible wall of air; where two opposing waveforms cancel into a narrow corridor of acoustic dead-space—a trough in the roar no wider than a ribbon—it slips into the seam and rides the quiet channel uphill. 

Along the entire perimeter of its flat, shield-like body runs a fringe of stiff, golden-brassy filaments of strictly graduated lengths, shorter near the narrow front, sweeping out longer toward the rear like the teeth of an unwrapped music-box comb. As it passes from node to antinode, different sections of that fringe blur into sudden, sympathetic vibration while adjacent hairs go dead still. It has no eyes—what use would lenses be in a shadowless room where nothing ever approaches from a distance and the dim violet seams never move?—and it has no ears in the sense of stretched drums. Its entire margin is a frequency analyzer. It sees the chamber completely, down to the half-inch, by the exact shape of the sound bouncing off it. To this creature, the plum-black fan is not a dark, greasy curve in violet gloom; it is a six-foot acoustic shadow tinged with high-frequency hiss where its pleated edges split the standing wave. The crescent stone baffle halfway up the slope is a massive, booming dark-spot, curved like an eclipse.

It reaches the base of the black stone spire where the brassy plumes hang limp in the forty-degree heat. A spun-copper centipede is already there, eighteen inches of metallic bristle grazing methodically on the fallen wax-spores. 

In an ordinary cave, with water and blood and booming bursts of rot, the flat slate-thing would rush the centipede, crush it, and eat it wet. Nothing in this pocket can afford a rush, and nothing here is wet inside. A burst of violent speed at forty degrees Celsius, with no sweat to evaporate and no cool mud to press a belly against, would cook the hunter’s dense, oil-cooled tissues from within, coagulating its slow sulfurous chemistry before it could swallow. More than that: there is no surplus water to flush away metabolic ash, no rain to wash a messy kill into the cracks. 

The slate-flat hunter pauses four inches from the centipede’s rear segments. Its narrow head—little more than a split, articulated wedge of dark stone-plate—lowers until its chin-fringe touches the trachyte. It reads the tremble in the rock beneath the centipede’s rhythmic grazing feet. Slowly, with the steady, geared precision of a clockwork escapement, it extends a pair of long, flat palps folded beneath its throat. The inner edges of these palps are lined with dense velvet pads glistening with a clear, heavy solvent—distilled from the camphor-scented plant greases it licks from the fronds.

It does not strike. It steps across one of the centipede’s middle legs and clamps its flat body down against the rock, pinning three segments flush to the trachyte like a heavy iron paperweight dropped on a wire. The centipede thrashes once, dry bristles skittering silently inside the deafening chord, before the hunter presses a velvet palp over the spiracles along its victim’s flank. The heavy oil floods the microscopic breathing tubes instantly, suffocating without a struggle, dissolving the waxy joints from the outside in. For twenty minutes, nothing else happens. Both creatures remain frozen under the dim bruised-violet glow while the room roars around them, until the hunter draws up the liquefied lipids and mineral salts through a capillary groove in its jaw, leaving behind a clean, brittle copper-colored husk already dry enough for the pewter-shelled mites arriving on the down-slope side to begin rasping into powder.

Nothing wasted. Nothing spilled to stain the fresh stone.

Yet the four flat hunters currently occupying this chamber are only the small, territorial custodians of the floor—or rather, they are the juvenile phase of something much more patient, though "juvenile" scarcely fits an animal that may hold its forearm-length flat form for thirty or forty unchanging years while waiting for a vacancy above.

Look up past the hanging brassy plumes, into the jagged twelve-foot ceiling, where the hairline chimneys disappear crookedly into the overburden. And look again—much more closely this time—at the crescent stone baffle built by those vanished visitors, and at the three vertical cracks pinched tight with knapped flint wedges.

You assumed the builders came from the surface, squeezed through a wider lower seam before the hill settled, tuned the room for their own ears, harvested what they wanted, and crawled back out toward the sky before the rock pinched shut.

Consider the crescent wall again. It is waist-high to a standing human, four feet wide, enclosing a quiet pocket just large enough for one small frame to crouch inside. Look at the woven mat of brassy, oil-heavy ribbons lying beside the pumice saucer. Hold your hand above the crease pressed into those supple, greased fibers—the dent you took for a knee or an elbow. 

Notice how wide and flat that impression actually is. Notice how there is no heel-mark beside it on the mat, no print of toes braced against the thirty-degree incline, though a human crouching on pitched, razor-edged trachyte without boots would have shredded their soles in minutes or left dried blood darkened to black lacquer on the stone. There is no blood anywhere in this room. There is no flake of shed human skin, no single fallen hair preserved in this rotless kiln where even a century-old plant ribbon still shines fresh. And look at the three obsidian blades lying beside the river-cobble hammerstone: they have no hafts, and their unworked backs are not blunted by backing-flaking to protect a fleshy human palm. Their proximal edges are left needle-sharp, scarred instead with tiny, rhythmic silica-scratches, as though gripped directly between hard, flat plates that could not be cut.

Did someone come down from the river with a cobble and three pieces of volcanic glass? Once, certainly—ages ago, when the lower corner stood ajar just wide enough for a starved, desperate, or fiercely curious slender-boned human to wriggle down out of whatever cold or drought ruled the world above, carrying a stone kit in a leather pouch that has long since been rasped down to volatile dust and recycled into plum-black fronds by the pewter mites. Whether that lone intruder crawled back out half-deafened after building the first rough blind of stacked flakes, or died wedged in the lower chimney where the mites cleaned their bones to vanish into the quartz-drummers' shells within a decade, they left behind two revolutionary things in a sealed pocket that had never known a tool: foreign stone that fractured differently than trachyte, and the concept of an acoustic shadow.

Now look up into the dark, sharp overhang directly *above* the smaller, half-finished baffle near the upper wall, where two stressed feldspar seams cross in a dim amber-violet X.

Pressing flat into a horizontal cleavage plane no wider than a mail slot—extending back into the solid forty-degree rock for nearly five feet, widening where the fissure widens, narrowing to the thickness of paper-bark where the trachyte pinches—is the elder. 

There is only ever room for one of this size in the pocket’s tight energy budget. When the flat slate-colored hunters on the floor survive long enough, molting fraction by fraction only when an older peer dies and frees up a measure of mineral dust and heavy oil, one eventually grows too broad to navigate the narrow pressure-corridors of the floor without catching the full, heating battering-ram of the acoustic chord. Driven upward toward the ceiling splits where the chimneys vent trace sulfur, its body undergoes a final, slow reorganization. Its sintered silica plates stop overlapping like armor and fuse at the hinges into broad, flexible sheets joined by tough, leathery membranes saturated with camphor-grease. Its six walking limbs lengthen until they span four feet from tip to tip, flattened like carpenter's rules, folding neatly into slots along its flanks. Its two front palps thicken, losing their velvet solvent-pads and developing opposing, heavily calcified pincer-plates lined with replaceable flinty teeth.

What shows outside the ceiling crack right now is less than a fifth of it: a wide, crescent-shaped shelf of dark, slate-gray plate protruding four inches from the stone seam, utterly motionless, looking like just another blade of trachyte until you notice the long, tarnished-gold fringe of acoustic hairs trembling delicately along its rim.

Inside the warm dark of the cleavage plane, spread flat as pressed fern between tons of mountain, its internal nervous ring—cooled by slow-circulating heavy oils and freed from the labor of chasing centipedes—has nothing to do for centuries at a time except listen to the room.

It knows every square millimeter of the threshing-floor chamber by the way the tens of thousands of quartz-drummers hammer the rock. For eons before the lower crack opened and shut, its predecessors simply lay in the ceiling seam, sliding down once every few months to lick the richest oil-beads from the plum-black fan or harvest a clutch of fat, sluggish drummers from a dim seam, returning to the slot to digest in stillness.

Then the brief visitor came, stacked the first loose trachyte flakes into a crescent to keep their eardrums from bleeding, struck a notch or two with a river cobble, and vanished. 

And the thing in the ceiling—whose entire mind is an architecture of pitch, stress, resonance, and geometric angles—heard the room change.

If you stay behind the larger crescent baffle long enough—days, perhaps, though you could not survive forty degrees of zero-humidity roar for twelve hours without water bags—you might see the elder come down to tend its instrument.

It does not descend often; once every forty or fifty days is plenty in a world without weather. First, the vibration of the air shifts imperceptibly as four feet of ribbon-flat, jointed slate slides smoothly out of the ceiling slot like a sawblade drawn from a scabbard. Spread wide against the jagged ceiling, it looks less like an insect or a reptile than a vast, articulated origami kite made of dark ironstone and brass wire, hugging every sharp angle of the roof with zero clearance, casting no new shadow because the violet veins shine from beneath and beside it simultaneously.

It glides down the upper wall, completely silent inside the furnace-roar, and slips into the quiet pocket behind the larger crescent baffle. Look how well the four-foot curve fits its wide, flat span when its long limbs fold inward; look how its lower ventral hinge rests squarely into that broad, greased crease on the woven ribbon mat, keeping its sensitive underside-membranes off the abrasive floor-dust while it works. 

With two long, flat forelimbs, it lifts the pale river-cobble hammerstone—grasping the rounded pebble effortlessly between four opposing spatulate pads—and picks up one of the obsidian blades, locking the sharp glass back directly into the scarred groove of its calcified pincer.

Why did it finish the human’s rough windbreak into a mathematically precise acoustic parabola? Why did it spend decades—one patient tap every few months so as not to overheat its muscles—pecking seven neat V-shaped notches into the projecting rock-fins, testing the pitch with its golden fringe after every strike? Why did it knap fresh wedges from a nodule of native flint—copying the fracture-mechanics of the abandoned obsidian—and drive them hard into those three vertical cracks using a dense, calcified leg-segment salvaged from its own previous molt as a drift? (Look again at the "bone" drift marks on those flint shims; no mammal bone stays greasy-translucent like dense silica-chitin.)

Watch what happens when it climbs from the baffle over to the brightest, flint-wedged seam. 

Because the driven wedges stress the quartz lattice to the brink of fracture, the piezoelectric yield per strike is doubled there; because the V-notches pecked into the adjacent rock-fins tune the local stone to the exact resonant frequency of the mica-drummers’ own hammering muscles, the thumb-sized creatures along those three seams are locked into a furious, effortless feedback loop. They hammer harder, faster, and denser there than anywhere else in the mountain. They burn brighter violet. Consequently, the hanging plumes nearest that tuned light photosynthesize—or rather, *radiosynthesize* and *chemosynthesize* across that narrow violet band—at a slightly richer rate, beading thicker, heavier camphor-grease along their lowest brassy tips. More drummers molt and die along the wedged cracks; more pewter mites gather beneath them; more wax-spores shake loose in the sharpened harmonic to fatten the copper centipedes.

By tuning the chord up a fraction of a tone in three spots and building two dead-air baffles to disrupt stagnant acoustic corners, the flat architect in the ceiling raised the carrying capacity of its sealed stone world by perhaps four percent. Four percent is the difference between three floor-hunters and four; it is an extra thimbleful of rich, non-evaporating heavy oil collected in the ground-out pumice saucer over a year of patient dripping from a bruised frond placed carefully across its rim—a quiet reserve lapped up slowly during the dangerous, immobile week of a molt.

More than that, though, watch it linger after it has scraped a bead of oil into the pumice dish and set the river cobble back in its exact spot beside the obsidian.

It does not go straight back up to the ceiling slot. It slides halfway up the wall between the two baffles, flattens itself across a broad, sharp facet of dark trachyte right at the intersection of the three wedged seams’ acoustic beams, and spreads all six limbs wide. Around its dark, stoneware perimeter, the thousands of graduated golden hairs catch the tuned, roaring chord. Because the notches and flint shims were placed by its own sensory fringe, the discordant, jagged rattle that once bounced randomly off raw rock now resolves, at this single spot on the wall, into a pure, staggering mathematical lock—fifth upon fifth upon octave, pressing evenly across every plate of its wide body at once, massage and vision and warmth combined into a solid bath of violet-lit thunder.

It hangs there flat against the fresh blades of stone for hours, utterly still, basking in the exact chord it carved out of the mountain, while down in the triangular shadows at the bottom of the incline, its four smaller, silent shadows wait forty years for their turn at the cobble.
