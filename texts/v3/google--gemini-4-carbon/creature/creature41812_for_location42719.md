Look again at that solitary bull drinking by the snowmelt tarn. Wait until your eyes adjust to the pewter gloom and the dim amber wash beneath his sternum. 

High up behind his massive, piston-driven shoulder, tucked deep into the greased folds of his wool where the dorsal spiracles vent their warmest, dampest exhaust, a mat of grey fleece shifts against the grain. Five blunt, articulated fingers—plated in dull, calloused horn the exact color of wet shale—reach out from the tangle, test the temperature of a rising curl of sulfur-steam, and draw a flap of heavy, oiled felt tighter across a buried shoulder.

There are people here, or what became of people when the plateau’s patient, grinding logic finally took hold of them. 

They do not cross the scree on foot if they can help it. To walk the gravel is to bleed heat into a world with an infinite appetite for it, and the distances between one pocket of iron-sedge and the next would kill a naked mammal before it was halfway across a basin. Instead, they live *on* the migration. They are an arboreal species whose trees happen to be sixty-ton, six-legged biological refineries moving north at two miles an hour.

A single bull might carry a household of three or four; a vast, venerable matriarch with a broad back and four centuries of mineral-crust thickening her boiler-ribs might carry a dozen. You could stare directly at one from fifty yards away and see nothing but the beast’s own winter coat. They weave their shelters directly into the living wool—low, streamlined blisters of felted hair, cured hide, and vulcanized gut-rubber, lashed to the great dorsal spines with braided wire-sinew so they roll smoothly with the slow, piston-heave of the animal’s gait. 

When one of them emerges to work along the flank, descending the beast’s side on climbing-loops braided into the matted pelt, you see how thoroughly the plateau has edited them.

They are short, immensely broad through the chest and pelvis, built like smooth river-boulders wrapped in dark leather. Their skin has a faint, plumbago sheen—a metallic grey-blue accumulated over lifetimes of absorbing trace minerals through grease and steam—and it is coated deliberately in a thick layer of warm tallow rendered from the beasts' lubricating glands. Beneath the skin lies a dense, rubbery mantle of brown fat that rounds off every human angle. Their necks are nearly nonexistent; their heads sit low between heavy trapezius muscles to protect the great vessels of the throat from the raw air. 

Because the air is so thin, their ribcages are barrel-deep, housing lungs partitioned like bellows. Because the air is freezing and laced with fine sulfur-ash and metallic dust, their noses have grown wide, flat, and fleshy, curving downward over their upper lips. Inside those broad nasal vaults sits a complex labyrinth of warm, blood-rich turbinates lined with stiff, oily cilia that trap particulates and heat the thin air before it ever touches their throats. When they exhale, no white plume escapes their mouths; they reclaim almost every drop of moisture and degree of warmth on the way out.

Their faces are startlingly calm, framed by heavy, overhanging brow ridges and thick epicanthic folds that narrow their eyes against the rime and the occasional silent, blinding flash of a scavenger-kite discharging into a hillside. Their irises are huge and very dark, drinking in the shadowless pewter light, with a faint tapetal gleam at the back that catches the amber glow of the beasts' sternums like wet slate.

They have no fire. Up here, there is nothing to burn that isn't worth infinitely more as insulation, and open flame consumes oxygen the thin air cannot spare. Instead, they are masters of stolen heat and silent maintenance.

Watch a woman working along the lower ribs of a walking cow. The animal does not break stride; the woman moves across the vast, warm wall of breathing hide with the slow, sure-footed economy of a spider on stone. Slung across her thick torso are tools grown and harvested from the ecology itself: scrapers of tungsten-bone dropped by molting slag-grazers, wrenches of precipitated bog-iron, pouches of mineral salve, and hollow reeds of rigid cartilage. 

She pauses beside an intake valve behind the third leg where a crust of yellow sulfur-salt and frozen grit has begun to foul the seal. If left alone for a year, the valve would develop a wheeze, lose pressure, bleed heat, and eventually slow the cow until she fell behind the herd's invisible spacing. With slow, deliberate leverage—never striking, never hammering, because a sharp metallic *clang* feels obscene here, an evolutionary error that wastes energy—the woman applies a warm acid-paste milked from rock-lichens, waits five minutes while her own body sways in rhythm with the piston-tendons beneath her knees, and peels the softened mineral scale away in one quiet, leathery curl. 

She tucks the scale into her pouch. It is rich in refined nitrates and trace metals; later, steeped in warm meltwater inside a gut-flask wedged between two of the beast's dorsal vents, it will feed the dense, symbiotic yeast-curds that form half their diet. 

In return for keeping the spiracles clear, lancing oil-cysts, paring away split laminations on the iron hooves while the beasts stand twenty minutes drinking at the tarns, and scraping parasitic stone-barnacles from the translucent sternum plates so the internal dynamos can radiate excess heat properly when spinning down, the beasts tolerate them completely. More than tolerate: an unmaintained bull on the outer edges of the migration will sometimes drift half a mile off his vector toward a tended cow, rumbling at a frequency far below human hearing, waiting patiently in the freezing fog until two adolescents climb across on a cast line of braided gut to clean his fouled vents and claim him as a new hearth.

They are as quiet as everything else on the plateau. 

Speech, as lowland creatures understand it, died out among them epochs ago. Shouting into thin, damp, micro-condensed air is useless past twenty paces, and opening one’s mouth wide to form vowels lets the cold directly into the core. Close up—huddled inside a wool-blister where the ambient temperature stays a humid, musky ninety degrees against the beast’s spine, smelling richly of lanolin, hot grease, wet iron, and peat—they communicate through a low, closed-mouth hum modulated in the throat and nasal cavities, pressed directly against one another’s collarbones or foreheads so the sound travels through bone rather than air. 

Out in the open, across the vast spaces between one walking beast and the next, they speak with light.

Each adult carries a lantern-organ—not a manufactured lamp, which would require fuel, but a fist-sized, living capacitor harvested from dead scavenger-kites and kept alive in a warm pouch of nutrient-gel against the groin. At night, or in the deep, days-long twilight of a passing fog bank, a hunter standing atop the wool-matted ridge of a bull’s shoulders will draw the organ from beneath his greased cloak, squeeze its muscular poles gently between horned palms, and expose a slit of cool, steady indigo luminescence. 

Miles away across the dark gravel, another tiny point of indigo opens in answer above the slow, rhythmic amber pulse of a distant sternum. Two squeezes. A long pause while the beasts take twelve steps. One squeeze, shuttered downward. *Water-basin ahead crusts thick. Bearing three degrees west. Widow-cow calving.*

When a beast finally dies—which happens once in a human lifetime, after centuries of slow wear, when the boiler-heart calcifies and the internal dynamos spin down through amber, to dull red, to black—it is the closest thing this world has to a festival, and a catastrophe. 

Word travels outward by indigo blinks across forty square miles of spaced-out herd. Over the next three days, figures detach themselves from passing animals—a rare, perilous trek across the open scree, wrapped in inflated bladders of warm exhaust-gas under triple layers of oiled felt, walking with a slow, rolling, flat-footed glide that never raises a sweat. Two dozen people might gather at the cooling mountain of flesh and iron, the largest crowd any of them will ever see. 

They work in absolute, reverent quiet while the freezing fog slides over them. Nothing is wasted. Before the core heat vanishes into the basalt, they harvest the great curved ribs of iron-bone, the flexible intake trunks, the lenses of the translucent sternum, the heavy valves, the gallons of warm, amber lubricating oil, and the rich, metallic meat that tastes of blood and wet slate. Marriages are arranged there in the diminishing warmth of the carcass—two young people pressing their greased foreheads together, humming resonances into each other's skulls to test the lung-depth and calm of a prospective mate before parting to return to different vectors of the northward drift. 

Within a week, the scavengers and the slag-grazers have arrived. Within a decade, nothing remains on the gravel except six neat depressions where the flanged hooves stood last, and perhaps a curved arch of boiler-bone left deliberately upright—not as a monument, because they have no interest in defying the plateau, only as a scratching-post for passing calves and a grounding-rod for the kites.

And if you watch the plateau long enough—not for twenty minutes, not for a day, but for a season—you begin to realize something stranger still about the quiet people tucked into the wool.

Watch what they do when they descend to the gravel while their host-beast drinks at a tarn. Before climbing back up into the warm pelt, an old man walks thirty paces out onto the barren shale, kneels slowly on padded knees, takes a tungsten-bone chisel from his belt, and chips three small, shallow channels connecting a puddle of black meltwater to a dry, isolated mat of iron-red sedge. Half a mile away, on the next beast over, a child drops a handful of crushed slag-grazer pellets—rich in soluble oxides—around the base of a grey cushion plant that won't reach full size for another two hundred years. Further north, a woman uses a kite-bone capacitor to coax a tiny, silent spark into a vein of dull ore exposed by frost-shatter, oxidizing its surface just enough that the rotary-jawed grazers passing next autumn will smell it and pause there to feed.

There are no fences, no roads, no mines, and no ruins because they outgrew the clumsy, noisy habit of building things outside of living flesh. They are gardening at the speed of geology. 

Tucked warm against the breathing boilers, rocking slowly through the pewter dark with grease on their cheeks and bone-hums in their chests, they steer ten thousand locomotives by a fraction of a degree a week—clearing a valve here, salting a tarn there—keeping the whole vast, silent engine of rock, fog, iron, and blood turning smoothly over on itself, forever, without a single wasted motion.
