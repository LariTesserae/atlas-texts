To find the rest of what lives here, you have to stop looking at the open water and look into the leeward shadows—not shadows of light, which barely exist under the high, clinical sun, but shadows of force and sound. 

Behind every snapped hexagonal column of basalt, behind the concrete silos of the turbine-anchors, and even behind the thigh-thick steel cables, there is a narrow, turbulent wake where the twelve-knot current briefly trips over itself and eddies. In the deafening, uniform roar of the basin, these wakes are pockets of acoustic shade. Things gather there.

**The Grease-Thieves and the Cable-Walkers**

Clinging to the concrete flanks of the turbine stations are creatures that look like bundles of stripped copper wire and pale leather. They have no swim bladders—any pocket of gas in this environment would be vibrated to froth by the acoustic pressure, pulping the tissue around it—so they are denser than stone, built entirely of gristle, heavy chitin, and leaden, salt-heavy blood. 

Locally, if anyone were left to name them, they might be called *clamps* or *turnbuckles*. They are twelve-legged, flattened completely against the substrate like dropped rags, with joints that lock mechanically rather than relying on muscular effort. A clamp does not hold onto the rock by flexing; it wedges a hook of glassy chitin into a microscopic fracture in the obsidian or the seam of an iron bolt and snaps a biological pawl into place. It could die and remain locked to the steel for fifty years before the current finally shook it loose.

They exist in the basin because of the stations, or rather, they existed in tiny numbers around rare hydrocarbon seeps before the stations were built, and have since inherited an empire of industrial grease. When the massive flywheels turn inside their housings, the pressure-seals inevitably weep microscopic beads of heavy, synthetic lubricant—formulated to stay viscous at sub-zero temperatures. In a calorie-starved void, that grease is a feast of complex carbons. The clamps crowd around the seal-rings in tight, overlapping rosettes, scraping away the extruded grease with rasps of iron-hard teeth. 

To reach the next station twenty miles west without being swept out of the caldera entirely, they use the high-tension cables. A migrating clamp wraps six of its hooked legs around the vibrating steel braid, unlocks its friction-joints just enough to let the twelve-knot current push it, and *slides*. It rides the humming wire like a zip-line across the blue-white abyss, trailing a hair-thin wake of cavitation bubbles, deaf and blind, until it slams into the concrete mooring of the next silo down-current. Sometimes two clamps collide mid-span—one stalled, one sliding—and remain locked together in a knot of tangled hooks until one of them starves, freezes solid in the brine, and has its brittle limbs snapped off by the other’s patient, ratcheting pry-bars.

**The Shaving-Eaters**

Down in the glassy scree, where the pickled carcasses of the twenty-foot white swimmers come to rest, there is an entire, glacial economy based on friction.

Nothing rots, and the flesh of a dead swimmer—cured instantly by the hypersaline chill—is as tough and dense as vulcanized rubber. No small jaw can tear it. Instead, the carcass lies wedged across the razor-sharp edges of freshly fractured obsidian. Even down in the trenches, where the twelve-knot current slows to a mere crawl of two or three knots, the water still pushes relentlessly from east to west, and the bass roar of the singing ridges vibrates the stone floor like a sanding block. 

Year by year, millimeter by millimeter, the dead swimmer is planed away against the volcanic glass. Fine, translucent curls of pickled blubber and white muscle peel off the underside of the corpse like wood shavings from a carpenter’s bench.

Waiting three feet down-current of every carcass is a neat, fan-shaped garden of worms. They do not burrow—you cannot burrow into solid volcanic glass—so they cement their bases to the stone using a rapid-curing glue refined from the piezoelectric shells of dead spade-creatures. Their bodies are flat ribbons of grey, rubbery tissue, held stiffly upright in the slow bottom-drift like strips of wet parchment. They have no mouths; instead, their entire forward-facing surface is a sticky, digestive mucosa. When a microscopic curl of shaved, salt-cured meat drifts off the obsidian blade under a dead swimmer, it hits the ribbon-worm and adheres. Over weeks, in the freezing cold where chemical reactions crawl at an agonizing pace, the worm dissolves the speck of meat directly through its skin.

Because a single twenty-foot carcass takes forty or fifty years to plane down to bone, a colony of ribbon-worms will grow, bud, and reproduce entirely within the twenty-yard wake of one dead animal. When the last of the white meat is finally shaved away and only the heavy, dense skeleton remains—too hard even for the glass to cut quickly—the entire fan of worms slowly starves, turning clear as cellophane before dissolving into the brine, save for the few larval spores that drift westward to hit another corpse miles away.

**The Glass-Splitters**

Look closely at the occasional papery smudge of sulfur-eating bacteria on the obsidian floor, and you will notice that the rock beneath it is always freshly broken. That isn't coincidence, and it isn't geology. Something is breaking the glass on purpose.

In a freshly cooled basalt and obsidian basin, tiny pockets of volcanic gas—hydrogen sulfide, methane, trapped thermal minerals—remain sealed inside the black stone like microscopic bubbles in amber. The bacteria need that sulfur, and the *splitters* need the bacteria.

A splitter looks less like an animal and more like a geological tool: a flat, oval slug the size of a man's boot, armored on its back with laminated plates of scavenged mineral and piezoelectric cartilage stolen from drifted spade-shells. Beneath its mantle, instead of a soft foot, it possesses a single, terrifying organ—a muscular, hydraulic piston tipped with a conical spike of pure, biologically precipitated magnetite.

The splitter crawls slowly across the pristine obsidian floor until it senses, through faint acoustic resonance, a gas-rich inclusion trapped just beneath the surface of the volcanic glass. It clamps its edges down using hyper-saline suction, creating a vacuum seal against the smooth stone. Then, it stops fighting the roar of the sea and starts *using* it. It tunes the tension of its armored back-plates until its whole body resonates in exact sympathy with the local frequency of the singing basalt ridges. 

Vibrating like a jackhammer driven by the endless twelve-knot current overhead, it drives its magnetite spike against a single point on the obsidian. *Tick-tick-tick-tick*, four hundred times a second, drowned out entirely by the blast-furnace howl of the water. Glass is hard, but it is brittle, and under high hydrostatic pressure and freezing cold, it holds immense internal tension. Within an hour, a clean conchoidal fracture snaps outward beneath the slug. A flake of black glass as sharp as a surgeon’s scalpel pops loose, releasing a tiny puff of trapped volcanic sulfur into the water beneath the splitter's skirt.

The splitter stays clamped over the wound for months, farming the pale grey bacterial film that blooms instantly in the trapped, sulfur-rich pocket under its belly, grazing it down with a soft, velvety tongue before moving six inches to the left and cracking the world open again. Millions of acres of scree on the trench floors aren't volcanic rubble at all; they are tailings—piles of uniform, palm-sized obsidian flakes chipped neatly off the bedrock over centuries by slow, vibrating slugs.

**The Listeners in the Amber Light**

And then there is what lives inside the acoustic shadow of the yellow inspection lamps.

The twenty-foot white swimmers aren't the largest things in the caldera; they are merely the most visible because they have substance, muscle, and white skin to catch the sun. Above them, and around them, occupying the vast, terrifyingly clear mid-water, are the *veils*—though you could look straight through one from ten yards away in full daylight and see only the crisp, black basalt ridge two thousand feet below.

They have the exact refractive index of hypersaline water at minus-four degrees Celsius. In water with no silt to dust their skins and no plankton to cloud their guts, invisibility is trivial; anything made of clear, dense gel simply ceases to exist to the eye. You only ever see a veil when it drifts directly between you and one of the wire-caged yellow inspection lamps at the base of a turbine silo, and even then, all you see is a momentary warping of the amber circle on the black rock, as if heat-shimmer were rising through ice water.

Yet to the blind white swimmers, a veil is as solid and unmistakable as a brick wall.

Where everything else in the caldera endures the sound, or eats the sound, the veils *architect* it. A veil is a colony organism, or perhaps a single splayed-out nervous system, shaped like a vast, ruffled parachute forty yards across, gossamer-thin, weighted at its hems by smooth nodules of dense cartilage. Running through its transparent membrane is a latticework of fluid-filled canals containing heavy, metallic salts in suspension. By flexing microscopic sphincters along millions of intersections, the veil alters the density and tension of its own skin across fractions of a second.

It is an acoustic lens. 

Hanging slackly in the current miles away from any station, moored to nothing, the veil drifts westward at the exact twelve-knot speed of the sheet-flow—meaning that relative to the water around it, it experiences dead calm. As the deafening, bone-shaking roar of the basalt ridges passes upward through its forty-yard span, the veil bends the sound waves.

To a blind white swimmer holding station by a steel cable, the world is painted entirely in the steady, unchanging hum of the rock and the wire. A veil drifting half a mile up-current will subtly warp its own membrane, focusing the ambient roar of the sea like a magnifying glass focuses sunlight, and project a *false shadow*—the exact acoustic silhouette of a concrete turbine-silo, or a tall basalt spire, or a thick, dense cloud of clicking spade-shells—into empty water fifty yards away from the swimmer.

The swimmer, trusting the eternal, unchanging geometry of the sound-field, turns from its cable to investigate the sudden shelter or the sudden feast. The veil tightens its lattice. It takes the blast-furnace roar of forty miles of rushing sea, gathers it across twelve hundred square yards of resonant membrane, and focuses all of that acoustic pressure into a single focal point no larger than a clenched fist, right inside the swimmer’s heavy skull.

There is no struggle, no blood clouding the crystal water. The focal point hits with the force of a lithotripter dialed up to industrial scale. Inside the swimmer's head, the dense acoustic organs shatter; capillaries rupture instantly into the pale meat; the spine decouples at the brain-stem from sheer, focused vibration. The white fuselage simply stops flicking its crescent tail, turns slowly broadside to the twelve-knot current, and begins its long, clean diagonal sink toward the obsidian miles below.

The veil never touches it. A veil cannot eat cured, heavy meat; it has no jaws, no stomach. Instead, as the stunned or dying swimmer spasms in the instant of its death, it vomits the contents of its crop—hundreds of crushed, half-digested piezoelectric spade-shells, already broken out of their iron-hard carapaces, already ground into a rich, cold slurry of lipids and charged cartilage. 

The veil relaxes its lens, goes acoustically transparent again, and drifts seamlessly through the dispersing mist of pale slurry, absorbing the rich droplets across forty yards of invisible, ruffled skin before continuing west in the blindingly bright, roaring emptiness—leaving the rest of the twenty-foot body to fall cleanly down to the black glass and the waiting worms.

**The Wrench-Keepers**

And if you go down to one of the brutalist iron silos—past the humming cables shedding their silver dust of collapsing bubbles, into the unnecessary amber glow of a caged inspection lamp—and reach down to pick up that unrusted steel wrench lying in a basalt cleft, it will pull back.

Not fast. Nothing down on the stone moves fast, because cold brine steals heat from working muscle faster than digestion can replace it. It will pull back with a slow, hydraulic, implacable dreadfulness, scraping across the volcanic glass with a shriek of metal on stone that you will feel in your wrist bones rather than hear.

Wedged deep inside the cleft beneath the wrench is something like an octopus that gave up softness ten thousand generations ago: a knot of flat, segmented straps covered in pale grey calluses, with two cluster-eyes that actually work—tiny, pinhole apertures lined with mirror-scales, evolved specifically in the last few centuries to read the dim, unchanging amber circles of the turbine lamps. 

Refined steel is the rarest mineral in the caldera. Unlike obsidian, which chips away when struck, stamped vanadium steel holds an edge without shattering. The strap-things collect every dropped bolt, every sheared cotter pin, every length of flanged pipe within five miles of a station, dragging them inch by inch across the razor ridges back to the warm—half a degree warmer, from the friction of the gears above—concrete plinths of the silos. 

They use the wrenches to pry clamps off the grease-seals. A pale, segmented strap wraps thrice around the handle of a stamped *Drop-Forged 24mm* spanner, wedges the jaws under the locked chitinous hook of a feeding clamp, and leans its dense, lead-blooded body weight against the lever until the clamp's leg snaps with a dull *crack* and the creature tumbles into waiting beaks below. When the meal is done, the strap-thing pulls the wrench carefully back into its cleft beneath the yellow lamp, wiping the clean steel free of grey blood with its own leathery mantle, hoarding its inheritance while the concrete silo shudders overhead and forty miles of clear, freezing water pours endlessly toward the unseen west.
