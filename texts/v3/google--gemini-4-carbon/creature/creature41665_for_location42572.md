If you keep perfectly still between two of the basalt fins—though there is nowhere for a human body to stand, so you must imagine your gaze alone wedged into that hot, buzzing slot—you begin to realize that the stillness of the vault is an illusion of scale. The equilibrium is ferocious, and it has teeth. 

Look first at the plate-backs, that continuous white pavement of porcelain shells tiling the knife-edges of the rock. Because the dead never rot and never drop away, the oldest ledges have swollen into thick, calcified reefs of fused ceramic, twenty or thirty generations deep, narrowing the gaps between the stone plates to mere finger-widths. Live current runs only across the top layer. Down in the suffocated, bone-dry dark beneath the living crust, among the hollow shells of ancestors, moves the **solder-worm**. 

It is not a worm in any soft, wet sense; nothing down here could afford hydraulic flesh. It is a ribbon of articulated bismuth-gray scales, flat as a feeler gauge and thirty inches long, edged with hundreds of hooked silica setae. It slides through the hairline interstices of the dead ceramic reef with a dry, metallic whisper. The solder-worm is a short-circuit predator. Its flattened head ends in two heavy, insulated mandibles lined with pure, native copper stripped from the bodies of its prey. Emerging partway from a crack beneath a row of humming, St. Elmo’s-lit plate-backs, it reaches across three or four shells at once and clamps down. The sudden, catastrophic bridge of conductivity dumps the accumulated static of the entire row straight through the worm’s heavily shunted dorsal nerve-cord—a blinding, violet snap that smells sharply of burnt hair and ozone—cooking the bypassed plate-backs in their shells while the worm drinks the violent rush of amperage like warm blood. Afterward, it unhinges its jaw-plates and rasps out their desiccated, sulfur-rich tissues with a tongue like a jeweler’s file, leaving the empty white domes neatly in place so the circuit of the ledge is not broken for the neighbors.

Sometimes a solder-worm misjudges the charge of a particularly fat reef. You can find their mistakes fused permanently across the porcelain ridges: rigid, blackened lightning-scars of melted scale and carbonized meat, already being climbed over by young plate-backs eager for the elevated perch.

Above the reefs, in the papery thickets of slag-silk where the stridulators saw away in phase with the great B-flat roar, lives something that hunts by cancellation.

The stridulators’ survival depends entirely on maintaining exact sympathetic resonance with the iron pillars; if one falls out of phase, the brutal acoustic pressure of the room batters its brittle, parchment wing-cases to splinters within minutes. So they rasp in lockstep, blind and rhythmic. Drifting among their layered curtains is the **null-moth**—though it has no wings to beat, and could never fly in air thickened into invisible bars of standing sound. Instead, it resembles a tangled hank of gray fiberglass wool wrapped around a narrow, segmented thorax of porous carbon. It moves hand-over-hand along the mineral-silk threads on eight ridiculously long, hair-thin legs of flexible quartz, stepping delicately between the suspended ribs of floating dust without disturbing a grain.

When a null-moth settles over a stridulator, it spreads a broad, ribbed cloak of sound-baffling, aerated pumice-chitin over the victim’s back. Then the hunter begins to vibrate its own thorax—not with the room’s B-flat,ht, but precisely half a cycle *out* of phase. Right under its cloak, a pocket of dead, terrifying silence opens up. Deprived of the room's acoustic scaffolding, the stridulator’s rhythm stutters; its fragile, dry-bellows body instantly crumples under the external wall of sound hammering against the sudden vacuum. The null-moth doesn't even need mandibles to kill. It simply waits three seconds for the room's roar to crush its prey into neat, dry flakes inside the baffle, then sweeps the shattered chitin and powdery yellow fat into its static-charged belly-fur.

Even the standing ribs of airborne dust—those horizontal, shimmering bars of levitating mica and ash where the salt-grain mites swim—have their leviathans. 

If you peer closely into one of those three-foot-wide acoustic shelves, backlit by the dull cherry glow of a distant nichrome cage, you will see a ripple in the suspended grit that is not caused by the iron cores. Threading through the center of the dust-bar, never falling out the bottom and never rising through the ceiling of the sound-node, is a **chladni-skimmer**. It is no heavier than cigarette ash, four inches long, built like a microscopic fleet of linked snowshoes or a skeletal fern frond beaten out of gold-leaf-thin muscovite. It has no eyes—vision is useless inside a blinding fog of glittering flakes—and no legs that could bear weight on stone. Born aloft in the acoustic pressure wave, mating aloft, dying aloft, it rows through the dry, levitating silt with slow, undulating ripples of its translucent frame, seining thousands of pale mites into a sticky electrostatic basket beneath its throat. If a stray spark from a stone fin ever disrupts the standing wave for a microsecond, or if two skimmers collide and tumble a fraction of an inch below their acoustic shelf, gravity claims them instantly. They plummet straight into the razor-sharp talus piles below and shatter like dropped watch-glasses.

Down on that floor, among the glittering shrapnel of cleaved basalt and flaked stone, life changes posture again. Here the constant mechanical shudder of the vault is direct, transmitted straight through the bedrock from the buried iron casings. The talus piles are never still; they seethe with a microscopic, high-frequency chatter, every razor-edged flake tapping sixty times a second against its neighbors, grinding fallen slag-silk and shattered skimmers into flour.

Nothing soft could cross that floor without being ribboned in three strides. What walks the talus is the **shrapnel-crab**—a low, radial creature that has abandoned the hassle of growing its own armor entirely. At birth it is little more than a tough, leathery knot of carbon-fiber tendons and piezoelectric muscle, shaped like a dried starfish. As it grows, it reaches into the shivering heaps of rock and cements razor-keen flakes of basalt and translucent mica directly onto its limbs using a glassy, vitrified spit smelted by tiny electrical arcs between its palps. By adulthood, a shrapnel-crab is completely indistinguishable from a fist-sized clump of fallen cleavage—until the clump rises on six silent, knife-bladed stilts and picks its way through the labyrinth. They are the undertakers of the floor, grazing on the black, stove-wire thickets of piezoelectric plants near the roaring iron pillars, snipping off the graphitic stems with basalt shears that strike tiny orange sparks in the amber gloom, puffing gray clouds of carbon soot over their glittering backs.

And then there is the thing inside the leaning brick switch-house.

You will not see a second one. Perhaps there *were* two or three once, centuries ago when the pegmatite slab first dropped and crushed the roof in, or perhaps it is not a single animal at all anymore, so much as a vast, sprawling clonal colony that has found the absolute summit of the vault’s dry hierarchy.

Around the drooping copper busbar outside the door, the tall, tripodal violin-scavengers stand in their motionless, concentric rings, passing pellets of ash from mouth to mouth. Watch the innermost ring—the largest, oldest tripods, whose brass-wire legs have grown thick with verdigris-free copper oxides and whose pumice bodies buzz with induced eddy currents. Every few hours, without breaking the ring's geometry, one of those inner elders turns its tuning-fork antennae toward the cracked, brick doorway of the switch-house, steps forward on stiff joints, and reaches through the broken glass of a ruined control panel to drop a single, densely compacted bead of pure, stripped metallic dust inside. It is not scavenging there. It is paying tribute, or feeding a brood-mother, or grounding itself before it burns out.

Peer through the arc-scarred glass of the leaning wall, past the brass needles welded forever to their high red numbers.

The interior of the switch-house is packed from floor to buckled ceiling with a single, immense, cathedral-organ structure of spun glass, salvaged copper wire, and porcelain plates stolen from a million dead chitons—the **loom-warden**, or **the governor**. At the heart of this dry, ticking web hangs a body the size of a butchered calf, though it looks nothing like meat. It is a series of great, translucent amber bladders—not filled with fluid, which does not exist here, but inflated with hot, ionized gas—caged inside ribs of blackened, graphitic bone. Hundreds of fine, filamentous limbs, long as fishing lines and tipped with tiny pincers of fused quartz, radiate outward from its body through every crack in the brickwork, threading out into the wider vault.

Follow those threads into the dim, violet-edged alleys between the basalt fins, and you begin to see what the governor actually does.

One thread stretches forty feet through the air—carefully routed through the quiet troughs *between* the acoustic dust-ribs—to touch the edge of a ceramic plate-back reef that has grown too thick, threatening to bridge the gap between two stone fins and trigger a massive, vault-searing arc flash that would vaporize fifty square feet of life. With tiny, surgical snips, the quartz pincer chips away the dead calcified base of the reef, dropping measured flakes to the talus below, keeping the spark-gap precisely an inch and a half wide. Another filament wraps around one of thecherry-red nichrome cages of the cast-iron pillars, scraping off a crust of windblown mineral slag that had begun to choke a ventilation slot and overheat the neighborhood. Three more filaments reach up into the slag-silk curtains overhead, plucking a strand here, severing a guy-line there, tuning the tension of the stridulators' webs the way a blind harper tunes strings, ensuring the reflected sixty-cycle roar never focuses hard enough on the fractured ceiling to shake loose another slab of pegmatite.

It eats very little—only the rich, metallic beads passed through the broken window by the tripods, and the continuous, gentle trickle of current bled off the back of the dead switchboard’s slate panel, where its glassy anchor-roots are sunk deep around the main copper lugs. It has no eyes, no ears that could survive the B-flat thunder, and probably nothing you would recognize as a thought. Instead, its body is an immense, delicate map of resistance, capacitance, and vibration. Every tremor of the four iron cores, every shift in the electrostatic fuzz along a thousand basalt blades, every slight dampening of the standing sound-waves comes home along its quartz-tipped threads as a tiny change in tension or voltage across its hot, gas-filled bladders. When an imbalance itches in its dark brick room, a distant pincer twitches in the maze and trims the world back to true.

Nothing knows it is being tended. The plate-backs glow blue and pass their charge down the line; the solder-worm bridges four shells in a sharp violet snap; the null-moth opens its tiny umbrella of lethal quiet; the gossamer skimmers row endlessly through levitating bars of mica; the stove-wire stems drink the shaking of the iron. Down here in the dry amber heat, wedged into stone too narrow for a ribcage, life has become another set of coils and condensers wired seamlessly into the abandoned grid—taking the raw, blind fury pouring down from the dead world overhead and stepping it down, spark by spark and rasp by rasp, so the chord never breaks.
