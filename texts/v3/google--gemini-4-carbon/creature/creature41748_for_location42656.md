To find what lives here, you have to stop looking for things that walk on the catwalks or build nests in the rafters, and look instead at what happens when you put a bare hand against the brick wall of a rotary-converter shed. 

Before your skin even registers the thirty-four-degree water sheeting down the vitrified glaze, your bones take up the sixty-cycle hum. Stand there for three minutes and your teeth ache; stand there for ten and your vision blurs slightly at the edges as the fluid in your eyeballs begins to set up a standing wave. Anything with a delicate nervous system, an inner ear, or a reliance on blood vessels thin enough to burst under continuous acoustic shear would go mad here in an hour and die of exhaustion in a day. Furthermore, there is nothing to burn for fuel. The plants are unchewable vulcanized mats of antifreeze and heavy-metal chelates; the air is thin enough to starve a hot-blooded lung; the rock will slice leather boots to ribbons in an afternoon.

What lives here does not fight the vibration, does not eat the foliage, and does not breathe in any way a lowland animal would recognize. It uses the city the way a waterwheel uses a millrace.

If you watch the glazed ceramic insulators where the forty-mile transmission cables anchor into the basalt—specifically at the point where the braided steel enters the strain-clamp—you will sometimes see what looks like a thick, amber-colored grease slowly welling up out of the cable’s core, defying the wind. 

Wait an hour. In the flat, shadowless glare, the grease gathers itself into a translucent blister the size of a butcher’s block. Inside it, suspended in a stiff, piezoelectric jelly, are hundreds of fine, coppery filaments arranged in concentric rings, remarkably like the stator windings of the municipal generators humming forty feet away. 

These are the **line-leeches**, though they take nothing the grid misses. They are bulk-phase colonial organisms, closer in structure to a vitreous fungus or a giant siphonophore than a slug, devoid of eyes, gut, or brain. They feed directly on magnetic flux and mechanical tremor. Drawn to the ferocious corona discharge and the sixty-cycle shudder of the tension towers, a colony wraps itself around an unshielded bushing or packs itself into the narrow air-gap beneath a roaring transformer bank. As the alternating field passes through the jelly, those internal metallic filaments—scavenged ion by ion from the mineral-rich water running off the creepers—oscillate violently. That microscopic friction warms the colony’s core to a comfortable seventy degrees against the freezing gale and drives a slow, weird, electrochemical metabolism that fixes carbon straight out of the wet wind and dissolved basalt dust.

Most of the time, a line-leech is simply an architectural lump: a turbid, citrine collar wrapped around a warm pipe, slick with icy runoff, pulsing internally with faint, pale-blue static sparks whenever the wind slaps the forty-mile cables hard enough to spike the line load. When a colony grows too massive and its outer rinds cool in the thirty-four-degree wash, it calves. A fifty-pound lobe of stiff, rubbery gel detaches, slides seamlessly over the wet burgundy moss without tearing a single frond, and pours itself over the razor-sharp basalt edge—not falling to its death, because it has no bones to break, but catching one of the lower cables or lodging in the cast-iron ribs of an under-street truss, where it immediately flattens out, anchors against the sheared stone via suction, and begins humming again.

For a long time, an observer might think the leeches are the whole story: passive blisters riding the municipal grid. 

Then one of the gantry cranes moves—not the hook, not the rusted iron trolley frozen on its rails, because the control cab has been empty forever, its brass levers locked in green verdigris. What moves is something *under* the crane's main girder. 

Hanging upside down from the dripping iron flanges, gripping the rivet-heads with sixteen radially symmetrical, caliper-like limbs, is something the size of a draft horse and as flat as an iron manhole cover.

To exist in a forty-mile gale on rock sharp enough to sever tendons, life had to abandon softness on the outside, or abandon hardness altogether. The leeches chose jelly; the **calipers** chose hornfels and iron. 

They are wide, low-slung, heavily plated hexadecapods—or perhaps not arthropods at all, since nothing in their ancestry ever swam an earthly sea, though convergent necessity has forged them out of the exact materials lying at hand. Their dorsal shells are laminated plates of biological iron sulfide—fool’s gold laid down in dense, cross-hatched matrices—grown directly over an impact-absorbing cushion of tough, glycol-saturated cartilage. They have the dull, gunmetal-green sheen of wet boiler plate. When one clamps itself down onto a narrow basalt ledge in the teeth of a seventy-knot gust, its bevelled rim seals flush against the ninety-degree stone, turning the creature into a mere bump in the masonry over which the wind shears without purchase and the icy water sheets unbroken.

A caliper has no head, no front, and no back. It cannot afford to turn around on a six-inch iron flange twenty thousand feet above the mist. It moves equally well in any radial direction, swinging one set of hooked, hardened-chitin grapples forward to lock onto a line of rivets, a crack in the hornfels, or the braided twist of a steel guy-wire, before hauling its heavy, discoid body along with the slow, ratcheting deliberateness of a chain hoist.

Watch where it goes. It leaves the crane girder, steps effortlessly across a right-angled corner of vitrified brick where the mortar looks laid yesterday, and crawls straight up the vertical wall of a stamp mill whose interior iron shoes have been dropping onto empty anvils ten times a second for an unmeasured eternity. The noise here would liquefy a bird’s organs. The caliper climbs right up onto the cast-iron housing of the main drive bearing. It settles over the bolts, drops its flanged rim tight against the metal, and locks its sixteen grapples.

Inside its flat shell, resting on tuned suspension ligaments, sits a massive lithic organ—a free-floating block of dense barite crystal enclosed in rows of muscular ratchets. As the stamp mill hammers beneath it, that heavy internal stone bounces violently. Every bounce throws tiny internal pawls forward, winding immense, spiral springs of biological elastin coiled throughout the creature’s flat torso. 

It does not eat to move; it winds itself up on the industrial din. 

Once fully wound—a process that takes six or seven hours of sitting motionless on a roaring turbine housing or boiler feed-pump—the caliper unlocks from the machinery and goes prospecting. Its only actual chemical need is structural repair: carbon, sulfur, iron, and silicate to patch its abraded shell and replace the metal-tipped hooks that inevitably chip against the unweathered basalt. To get them, it walks down into the waist-high thickets of blue-green fronds and bladder-ferns. It still does not bite a single leaf. Instead, it slides its flat underside *beneath* the three-foot-thick root-mats where the warm substation water runs over the raw rock, extrudes a stiff, bristled pad from its central ventral slit, and scrapes up the thin, heavy sludge of precipitated iron oxides, leached copper, and dead line-leech slough that collects at the stone-water interface. Having licked the stone clean without harming a hair of the filtering plants above, it ratchets back out into the bright, freezing glare.

And then it crosses the void.

You can see them if you look carefully along the six sagging steel transmission cables through field glasses, though at first you will mistake them for anti-vibration dampers bolted to the wire by forgotten linemen. Every mile or two along that dizzying, forty-mile catenary over nothingness, a dark, bevelled disc is clamped around the braided steel. 

A fully wound caliper can unspool its internal springs over four days of steady, rhythmic clicking, walking hand-over-hand—or hook-over-hook—out into the empty cubic leagues between the spires. Halfway across, miles above the pooled white mist and miles from the nearest needle of black rock, the wind drives the cable into wild, singing oscillations, whipping sheets of condensed water off the steel in bright, sunlit arcs. The caliper simply clamps four opposing hooks completely around the braid, tilts its armored shell ten degrees into the gale so the wind presses it *down* onto the wire rather than tearing it off, and keeps ratcheting forward at three feet per minute. When its springs run low ten miles out, it stops, locks down, and lets the aerodynamic howl of the cable itself vibrate its internal barite weight until it has enough tension stored to make the far ceramic insulators. 

Why cross forty miles of freezing sky when the stamp mill behind you is identical to the rotary converter ahead? Because different wards run on slightly different mineral diets. A powerhouse spire fed by deep hornfels fissures precipitates nickel and zinc into its cistern mats; a switching-yard spire perched on pure columnar basalt yields magnesium and iron. To build a proper shell, a caliper has to visit three or four neighborhoods over the course of a century.

Yet the leeches and the calipers are only the heavy machinery of this ecology—the bulk consumers of magnetic flux and kinetic rattle. Neither of them notices the third thing that lives here, though the third thing notices everything, and in a quiet, entirely blind fashion, keeps the city from tearing itself apart.

Look inside the control rooms. 

The promptness with which the observer notes *no operators behind the blurred glass* is natural; you are looking for coats on pegs, skeletons in swivel chairs, or hands on rheostats. Look closer at the glass itself, and at the slate switchboards behind it. The glass isn't blurred by dirt—there is no soil to blow around at twenty thousand feet—nor by frost, since the waste heat from the resistor banks keeps the room at fifty degrees. It is blurred from the inside by millions of microscopic, clear silicate tubules cemented edge-to-edge in branching fractal arrays, looking like frost-ferns drawn with a diamond stylus.

Follow those glassy traceries down the window frame, across the wet tile floor, and up the back of the black slate control panels, right into the knife switches, circuit breakers, and governor linkages.

Out in the open air, they look like fine tufts of spun glass or mineral wool wedged into the brickwork cracks, streaming stiffly in the wind without ever snapping. In the sheltered warmth of the turbine halls and conduit trenches, however, you can see what does the spinning. Scuttling through the glassy tubes at blistering speed—driven not by legs, which would jam, but by electro-osmotic pumping of the thin film of water lining the tubules—are tiny, lozenge-shaped beads of ruby-red chitin no bigger than flaxseeds. 

Call them **shunt-mites**, though there are trillions of them on every spire, acting less like individual insects and more like the mobile corpuscles of a single, spire-wide organ made of spun quartz.

Like everything else here, they are completely deaf and blind in the ordinary sense, because ordinary senses are useless inside an invariant wall of sound and unchanging noon light. Instead, every glassy tubule they lay down across a bearing cap, a steam-pipe flange, or a brick pilaster acts as an interferometer and a strain gauge. The mites feel *phase*.

In this city, invariance is life. Every organism from the trembling bladder-ferns—whose cell walls are tuned to absorb nutrients only at the exact resonant frequency of their local pump house—to the calipers and leeches depends on the machinery never stopping, never surging, and never throwing a rod. If a forty-ton flywheel in an open shed develops a hairline imbalance and begins to wobble at four cycles per second against the sixty-cycle baseline, it will eventually shatter its cast-iron pillow blocks, smash through the vitrified brick wall, sever the cooling pipes that warm the cabbage-rosettes, and drop an entire spire's grid offline, starving the line-leeches into frozen, brittle amber within hours.

To the shunt-mites, an off-rhythm knock in a bearing or an arc-fault hissing at the wrong pitch in a switchgear cabinet is an agonizing tear in the texture of their world—an intolerable discordant screech cutting through the smooth, eternal chord of the city.

They swarm toward disharmony the way white blood cells swarm toward a splinter, or termites swarm toward a draft in the mound.

Suppose a brass steam-whistle corrodes slightly at the lip after five hundred years of continuous screaming, dropping its note by a quarter-tone and setting up a destructive rattle in the adjacent exhaust stack. Within twenty minutes, millions of ruby beads are streaming up the outside of the scalding brass pipe inside hastily extruded ribbons of wet silica. At the lip of the whistle, thousands of mites sacrifice themselves directly into the roaring steam jet—flash-cooking instantly, their silica-heavy shells fusing under the heat and pressure into a hard, opalescent ceramic glaze right on the corroded brass edge. Layer by microscopic layer, they build the lip back up until—*snap*—the whistle hits its original resonant pitch again, the rattle in the stack vanishes back into the uniform wall of noise, and the surviving mites calmly retreat down their glass highways into the dark warm cracks beneath the boilers.

They do the same inside the machines. Where a heavy iron shaft begins to score its journal because the ancient grease traps ran dry eons ago, the mites pack the gap with a continuous paste of their own crushed, molted shells mixed with glycol-mucus wicked up from the creeper roots outside—a self-replenishing, glassy-smooth wet lubricant that keeps forty-ton armatures spinning on mirrors of biological silica without a human oiler in sight. Where hairline fractures try to propagate through the sharp basalt foundations under the endless vibration, the mites pump mineral-saturated water deep into the stone cleavage, growing dense veins of artificial chalcedony that stitch the needle-peak together tighter than when it first rose out of the crust. Where high-voltage contacts pit and burn, mites carry flecks of pure copper reduced by the creeper roots and pack them into the crater until the current flows cleanly again.

They have no idea they are maintaining a city. They do not know what a rotary converter is for, any more than a coral polyp knows it is building a breakwater that keeps a lagoon calm. They only know that when the hum is smooth, warm water flows, mineral paste precipitates, and their glass galleries stay intact; when a harmonic goes sour, they plaster it over until the peace of total, deafening monotony returns.

And if you stand at the very edge of a cantilevered iron promenade—careful not to touch the sharp basalt parapet—and look out past the solitary, steadily burning streetlamp into the forty miles of bright, freezing emptiness between the wards, you will notice one last thing.

The air between the spires is not quite empty after all. 

Catching the high, flat slant of the sun, drifting horizontally on the screaming wind from one needle of black stone toward the next, are occasional glinting threads—long, hair-thin filaments of pure spun silica, fifty or sixty feet long, strong as piano wire and light enough to ride the gale for leagues without dropping into the white mist below. Clinging to the center of each glass thread, sealed inside a tiny, aerodynamic bead of hardened antifreeze jelly, are a dozen ruby mites and a microscopic spore-packet of line-leech tissue. 

Most of those threads will miss. Ninety-nine percent of the volume here is empty sky; they will blow clear past the distant tooth of iron and brick, sail out beyond the planetary curve where the crags give out, and vanish. 

Eventually, though, one thread whips across a wet brass whistle or snags on the sharp ninety-degree edge of a powerhouse roof fifty miles downwind. The jelly dissolves in the thirty-four-degree wash streaming down the stone. The tiny red beads feel the familiar, bone-shaking roar of the turbines coming up through their soles, find a crack in the mortar where a bolt has worked a millimeter loose over the last three centuries, and begin spinning glass to wedge it tight.

Nothing decays here; nothing sleeps; nothing waits for evening or spring. Behind the frosted windows and beneath the slick, trembling leaves, in a roar so complete it has become a kind of silence, the city and its vermin hold each other forever at full load.
