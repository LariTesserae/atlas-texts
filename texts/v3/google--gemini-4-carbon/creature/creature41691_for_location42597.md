You have to look down into the tight, wet triangles where three swollen petioles wedge together, or run a thumb along the warm underside of a copper manifold where the condensation hangs in fat, trembling bellies, before you realize the hollow is not nearly as empty of animal life as it first pretends to be.

What is missing is *jointed* life. Anything built out of rigid chitin or brittle bone—anything with dry spiracles to clog, or stiff legs that need clear purchase, or nervous systems calibrated to snap and twitch—is excluded by the sheer, relentless squeeze of the place. A beetle wedged between two hydraulic stems at noon is a flat husk of shell by two o'clock, and by nightfall the pale hair-roots have already stitched it shut and drunk the meat out of its thorax.

What thrives here instead has given up on skeletons altogether.

Wait twenty minutes beside that slow, nine-foot flywheel, watching the rust-pitted iron rim creep upward out of the strap-ferns, and you will notice that the greasy black moss coating the lower housing is not entirely moss. Patches of it—dark, olive-brown, glistening like wet liver—are creeping slowly *down* the iron against the wheel's rise, holding precisely still relative to the shaft. They are *velvet-leeches*, though they draw no blood. Each is a flat, muscular ribbon four or five inches long, scarcely thicker than heavy wool felt, with no distinct head save for a paler, scalloped leading edge that tastes the metal ahead of it. They graze on the dense, metallic biofilm that cooks itself onto warm iron where stray current meets oxygen-rich condensate. If you try to peel one from a casing with a fingernail, it does not curl or thrash; it simply flattens harder, spreading its hydraulic suction across the pitted cast iron until it feels like a vulcanized rubber patch cemented to the metal. Let it go, and five minutes later it has resumed its imperceptible slide toward the grease-cup on the main bearing, where tallow-like plant waxes weep out of a brass collar.

Those three-finger-wide mottled slugs on the porcelain insulators are only the public face of a much larger, hidden mollusk economy down in the root-mat. Follow the warm, clear water squeezed up around your boot-soles: down below the sunlit surface, three and four feet deep in the permanently flooded dark where ancient rhizomes have compressed into a living sponge-work of vascular pipes, live the *valve-clams*. You never see the adults unless a piece of old plumbing bursts or a section of root-wall tears open under its own weight. They wedge themselves as tiny, drifting larvae into the narrowest, fastest-flowing clefts between main water-roots—or directly inside the cast-iron return flumes—and grow to fit the cavity like poured lead. An old one has no symmetrical shell left at all; its calcium carbonate valves, leached and rebuilt out of dissolved mineral scale, twist and flute around brass bolts and root-knuckles alike, locking it permanently in place. They have no need to move. The basin’s own pressurized circulation pumps warm, sugar-tinged, mildly camphoraceous fluid straight through their gills twenty-four hours a day. In return, they filter out the drifting sloughed cells and microscopic silt that would otherwise score the buried turbine blades or foul the osmotic membranes. Sometimes, when a pressure valve *chuffs* at the surface and sends a brief hydraulic hammer shivering back down through the lines, you can hear a faint, muffled *clack-clack-clack* underfoot—dozens of buried shells snapping shut in unison down in the dark, momentarily spiking the pressure on the nearest brass dial by half a pound before they slowly yawn open again.

Even the blind white shrimp inside that glass sight-gauge are not prisoners trapped by accident. Peer closely at the brass fittings at the top and bottom of the glass tube and you can make out microscopic, thread-like channels etched through the gray cambium gaskets where plant flesh meets metal. The shrimp—translucent, flat-backed creatures scarcely longer than an oat-grain, with oversized, paddle-shaped second antennae—inhabit the entire low-pressure condensate return network, both the copper pipes and the hollowed-out pith cavities of the older gourd-vines. They breed in the warm, turbulent dark behind the check-valves, grazing the bacterial slime off brass seats so clean that the valves continue to seal tight after fifty years without a wrench laid on them. Their eggs are tough, buoyant capsules no bigger than pollen grains, designed to survive forty pounds of head pressure and the slow shear of a piston cylinder, drifting smoothly from iron manifold to living stem and back again.

And then there is the thing that moves the hoses.

If you visit the hollow twice, separated by three or four days, little inconsistencies begin to prick at the eye. A braided copper grounding strap that hung in a slack loop beside the factory-blue dynamo on Tuesday is tucked neatly into a dry fork of wood on Friday, well clear of a newly swollen water-bladder. A weeping pinhole leak in a lead-soldered elbow—one that had been spraying a fine, hot needle of mist onto a yellowing fern frond—has been packed tight with a thumb-sized wad of chewed, fibrous pith mixed with waxy resin, already hardening like gray putty while neighboring cambium creeps up to make the repair permanent.

Nothing with hands did it. Stand on the central cast-iron flange just as the afternoon sun slides entirely off the rim and the hollow drops into sudden, blue-green shadow, and stay utterly still while the cooling leaves creak and resettle around you.

As the sixty-hertz drone of the dynamo softens and the sump-engine drops a full third in pitch for the night, the tight hydraulic stiffness of the lower stems relaxes just enough for gaps to open in the packed green wall. Out of a warm, cavernous pocket beneath the western buttress-roots—right where the dynamo’s exhaust heat keeps the root-mat at ninety degrees—something pours itself upward between two brass conduits.

At first you mistake it for a thick, mottled root-tendril slipping free of its truss, or an extraordinary snake. It is the thickness of a man’s forearm where its main mass flows, four feet long when stretched out along a pipe, colored the exact oxidized verdigris-and-wet-bark blotch of the lower basin. Only when it reaches the ceramic manifold does the illusion of a reptile break: its forward third splits effortlessly into five, then seven tapering, muscular arms—not segmented tentacles with suckers, like an octopus, nor fingered hands, but smooth, prehensile trunks of firm, rubbery flesh lined along their undersides with microscopic, ridged friction-pads like the belly of a gecko soaked in mucilage.

Set back behind the cleft of those arms sits a smooth, boneless mantle-hood with two small, dark, lidless eyes filmed over by clear nictitating membranes against the constant spray, and beneath it a heavy, overlapping beak of dark, horny keratin strong enough to shear through a copper wire or nip a calcified twig clean in two.

There are perhaps four or five of them in the entire seventy-yard basin—*sump-tenders*, if anyone ever came down here to name them, descended from some tropical semi-terrestrial cephalopod or giant carnivorous land-planarian line that found this drowned mechanical conservatory generations ago, or perhaps placed here deliberately when the last human engineers pulled up their ladders and let the rim-canopy close. Whatever their deep ancestry, the hollow has remade them in its own slow, unhurried image. They breathe through a rich, vascularized mantle-cavity lined with folded wet gill-lungs that must stay damp, drinking oxygen directly out of the warm, mist-saturated air and the trickling guttation water.

Watch one work in the blue dusk, and you understand why no insect survives and why no machine seizes up.

It does not dart or pounce. Its metabolism runs at the exact deliberate, high-torque tempo of the basin around it. Pressing three thick arms flat against the warm dynamo casing—apparently relishing the sixty-cycle electrical tingle that drives everything else away, using the stray current fields to map the health of the buried lines the way a shark reads weak voltage in dark water—it reaches two slenderer tips deep into a cramped, steaming gap behind the ceramic gourds. Its skin ripples briefly with a slow, involuntary blush of dull brick-red, matching the heat-pads pressed against the cooling fins.

Deep in that cleft, a woody vine has grown crookedly across a brass latch-lever, threatening to jam the sixty-second piston at the top of its stroke. The creature wraps two muscular coils around an iron stay-bolt for purchase, wedges its rubbery shoulders between the gourds until its own boneless bulk deforms like a water-filled inner tube, and brings its heavy keratin beak down onto the offending stem.

*Snick.* Slowly, methodically, grinding with steady hydraulic leverage rather than snapping jaws, it severs the tough, pressurized vine. Immediately, warm, camphor-scented sap wells out of the cut end—sap that would blister human skin after an hour and poison an ant on contact. The creature does not flinch; instead, it clamps its mouth-parts directly over the bleeding stub and drinks for three minutes straight, its mottled mantle pulsing with slow, greedy waves as it draws down the bitter, sugar-heavy fluid and sequesters the volatile camphor straight into its own skin-mucus. When the pressure in the severed stem drops, it rasps a strip of gray, fibrous bark from a neighboring dead frond with a rough radula, chews it into a gummy bolus mixed with thick salivary wax, and smears it firmly across the raw plant wound with the flat of an arm-tip so the line won't bleed pressure all night.

Before moving on, it pauses beside a porcelain insulator where one of the broad, three-finger slugs is halfway across its afternoon yard. One pale-tipped arm snakes out, delicately taps the slug's mantle—tasting its slime for age and sickness—and then withdraws, leaving the healthy grazer to its work. Two feet lower, however, where a patch of black velvet-leeches has crowded too thickly around a grease-cup and begun to choke the weep-hole, the tender curls an arm-tip like a spoon, scoops up three of the flat brown ribbons in a single smooth wipe, and tucks them beneath its hood into its beak with no more drama than a gardener pinching off suckers on a tomato vine.

By full dark, when the overhead oval holds only humid stars and the buried sump-engines have slowed to a deep, sleepy, two-minute *thump... chuff*, you cannot see the tenders at all. You only hear, every quarter-hour or so beneath the steady applause of falling water-drops, the soft, wet *squelch-slap* of ten pounds of firm, boneless muscle pulling itself effortlessly through a two-inch gap between warm iron and wet bark, checking the seals, drinking the leaks, keeping the pressure whole until morning hits the upper leaves again.
