If you follow the inspection collar for forty minutes—walking if you can find a place between the tortoises to set a boot, though it is easier to simply crawl along the top of the third pipeline, letting the chilled steel numb your knees and palms—you will eventually see the green pinhole of its LED strike something that does not shift aside, and does not have eyes to reflect it, yet breathes.

At first, in the zero-visibility dark, it registers only by feel and by a slight change in the smell of the air: the rich, iron-and-fig stink of the mud gives way to the sharp, vinegar-sweet tang of cured silicone, mixed with old sweat and crushed pennyroyal. 

Wrapped over the gap between one of the hot crude lines and the sweating cold-water pipe is a broad, low canopy. It is not pitched like a tent, which would require stakes driven into solid limestone, nor hung from the squat trees, which are fifty feet back from the cleared right-of-way. Instead, it is draped directly across the pipes themselves like a heavy saddleblanket—stitched together from stiff, black EPDM pond-liner, shed anaconda skins as wide as stove-pipes, and wide sheets of vulcanized rubber salvaged decades ago from the crane-mats left to rot at the basin’s rim. Weighted along its hems with river-rounded chunks of limestone tied in nylon gill-netting, the canopy traps a long, narrow pocket of air cooled twenty degrees below the surrounding soup by the sweating return-line.

Inside that pocket lives the Pipe-Tender, though the company that owns the pumping station twenty miles north has no record of employing anyone since the nineties.

He—or she; in the dark, coated in the same grey, talc-fine limestone paste and red clay that cakes the peccaries, sex has mostly weathered away into pure anatomy suited for heat—is old in the way tortoises are old: leathery, slow-pulsed, creased deeply at the joints, and profoundly indifferent to the passage of hours. He lies naked except for a wide kidney-belt of woven copper grounding-wire worn not for modesty, which would be absurd in a pitch-black valley where nothing looks at anything else, but because the copper bleeds off the faint, prickly static charge induced by the high-voltage lines sagging overhead. Without it, the hair on a human’s arms stands up straight all night and draws biting midges by the thousand.

You cannot see him until the green collar rolls up to his boundary. He has built a clever, lazy ramp over the fourth pipe out of two curved slabs of ironwood bark greased with tapir tallow; when the ultrasonic crawler reaches his camp, its rubber feelers touch the bark, and instead of nudging him, the machine simply climbs four inches up and over his shins, its polyurethane rollers humming softly against the wood while its sensors complain briefly to a server three hundred miles away on the coast about a recurring, six-foot-long anomaly in the steel’s acoustic signature. He doesn't even wake when it crosses him. A big yellow-footed tortoise wedged against his hip pulls its scaly head an inch back into its shell, sighs like a punctured tire, and goes back to sleep.

To live in the basin is to surrender the mammal’s frantic insistence on keeping a separate temperature from the world. Over thirty or forty years down here, the Tender’s metabolism seems to have dropped a gear to match the boas. During the blinding, lead-gray afternoons when the basin hits a hundred and fourteen degrees, he lies completely motionless in the black mud trench directly beneath the cold pipe, flat on his back with only his nostrils, cheekbones, and knees breaking the slick surface, letting the *plink, tup, plink* of the fifty-degree condensation hit the center of his sternum and run down his ribs. Blind caecilians, bluish-gray and slick as peeled roots, loop lazily over his ankles and under the small of his back. Land crabs walk across his collarbone, picking dead skin and salt crystals from the hollow of his throat with delicate, unhurried claws; he lets them, the way a crocodile lets plovers work its gums, keeping his fingers curled loosely so he won't accidentally crush one in a dream.

At night, when the air drops to ninety-eight and the chilled trench begins to draw too much heat out of old marrow, he crawls three feet sideways and wedges himself into the lukewarm clay *between* the hot crude line and the cold return, adjusting his distance by inches until the fifty-hertz thrum of the oil pumps vibrates squarely against his spine and the radiant heat balances the cool draft on his face. 

He is never alone in that narrow alley, and he has long since stopped distinguishing between furniture and wildlife. When he wants a pillow, he slides an arm under the barrel-thick midsection of a twelve-foot *Boa constrictor* digesting a brocket fawn on the bitumastic coating, lifts the heavy, muscular coil gently—it feels like a warm firehose filled with wet sand—and tucks his neck into the bend of it. The snake does not strike; striking takes explosive oxygen, and nothing in the basin wastes oxygen. Besides, the old human smells exclusively of wet clay, cured rubber, and the crushed, bitter leaves of *Piper* shrubs he rubs into his groin and armpits twice a day to keep the ticks piled under the hot pipes from climbing him. To the boa, he is merely a strangely shaped tapir with remarkably flat feet.

He is not the only person down here, though "community" is far too brisk and noisy a word for how they exist. 

Spread out along the four-mile flat where the pipes cross the deepest pan of the ancient riverbed, spaced roughly half a mile apart like beads on an iron wire, are six or seven others. Some are washouts from the old construction crews who walked away from the coastal slums during the currency collapses twenty years back, following the right-of-way over the limestone rim because they knew the pipes meant water that never stopped dripping and heat that never required chopping wood. Two were botanical poachers who came down into the bowl looking for rare sinkhole orchids in the dark, lost their flashlights to the relentless damp within forty-eight hours, sat down by the sweating steel to wait for a break in the clouds that didn't come for four months, and gradually forgot why walking back out into the bright, loud, starving world above the rim had ever seemed urgent. One—half a mile east, where a shallow karst sinkhole dips right under Pipe Two—was born here, or at least came down strapped to a mother’s chest so young she has never seen a horizon farther away than her own outstretched hand, nor heard a sound louder than thunder muffled through three thousand feet of muggy cloud.

They can go weeks without crossing paths, even though a straight steel rail connects every one of them. Moving half a mile in ninety-nine-percent humidity at ninety-eight degrees, stepping carefully over a continuous carpet of sleeping peccaries, tangled aerial roots, and stacked cane toads in pitch darkness, takes two hours and costs a quart of sweat. 

So they communicate through the iron.

If you lay your ear flat against the fourth pipeline—the empty utility spare where the green crawler runs, unmuffled by heavy oil or rushing water—you can hear the whole four-mile string of them beneath the fifty-hertz drone of the pumps. They use short lengths of brass brazing rod or heavy river pebbles, tapping not in Morse code, which is too sharp and military for how their minds work now, but in slow, rhythmic cadences scraped and struck against the steel. A long, lazy drag of stone across a weld seam, followed by two soft taps: *I am awake; the crawler just passed me.* Ten minutes later, ringing faintly through two miles of hollow forty-eight-inch steel from the east: *scrape, tap-tap.* Then, softer, from the far western terrace where the tapirs wallow thickest: a double-knuckle thump, dull and resonant. *Still here.*

When they do gather—usually once every month or two, drawn together when a giant fig tree collapses out in the dark and brings down a windfall of fermenting fruit, or when one of the overhead transmission insulators fouls with bat guano and drops a dead, roasted buzzard or a shower of fused ceramic shards onto the limestone—they meet without speaking aloud. Voices sound grotesque in the basin. A human shout tears the velvet quiet like sheet metal ripping, startling a hundred tons of sleeping mammals into blind, trampling panic for fifty yards around, setting off a frantic flapping of heavy bats that fills the air with suffocating musky dust and dry ticks. Even a whisper feels weirdly theatrical when you are touching shoulders in total blackness.

Instead, when the young woman from the eastern sinkhole crawls down the cold pipe to sit under the old Tender’s rubber canopy, she announces herself from twenty feet out by clicking her thumbnails together—*tik, tik, tik*—an imitation of a beetle’s tarsal claws on bark. He answers by slapping two fingers wetly against his own collarbone. 

She slides under the black pond-liner, slick from head to foot with cold pipe-sweat and red mud, carrying a netted gourd full of shelled Brazil-nut cousins gathered by feel from the forest floor, or six huge, pale corydoras-like blind cavefish hauled up on a hair-line from the flooded phreatic fissures beneath her sinkhole. Their greeting is entirely tactile, slow as sloths grooming. She presses her cool, wet forehead against his shoulder; he lays a broad, calloused hand over the crown of her cropped skull—they all shave their heads and body hair clean with flakes of bottle-glass or sharp chert, because hair holds fungal rot in perpetual dampness—and reads how she has been keeping by the plumpness of the muscle at the base of her thumb and the steady, forty-five-beats-per-minute thud of her carotid artery.

You might expect people living blind in warm mud to waste away into feverish wraiths, eaten alive by foot-rot and parasites. A newcomer would be, within ten days. If you walked down from the ridges tonight in boots and cotton trousers, your skin would macerate white and slough off inside your socks by Thursday; ringworm would map your torso by Sunday; the *fer-de-lance* vipers coiled near the hot welds, confused by the erratic, thrashing heat-plume of a panicking mammal stomping through the brush with a headlamp, would hit you above the ankle before you made a mile.

The basin-dwellers survive because they have become curated ecosystems themselves, as heavily colonized and carefully balanced as the squat barrel-trees. 

If you shone a light on the young woman’s skin—she would flinch violently and clamp her forearms over her eyes, which have dilated over twenty years to gather the faintest violet prickle of the corona discharge overhead—you would see she is mottled in faint, velvety geometric blooms of pale gray and ochre. They cultivate a specific, benign surface yeast found on the waxy undersides of the dinner-plate shrubs, rubbing the bruised leaves over their shins, toes, and skin-folds until the harmless flora crowds out the aggressive red-clay fungi that eat raw flesh. Their skin smells faintly of sourdough bread and crushed green stems. Around their ankles and wrists they wear loose rings braided from shed viper skin soaked in raw crude oil wept from a tiny pinhole gasket-seep half a mile down Pipe One; nothing with six or eight legs will cross a band of sour Venezuelan heavy crude.

Their diet is absurdly rich, effortless, and requires neither fire—which cannot be lit here anyway, where dry tinder is as mythical as snow—nor hunting tools. Protein sits waiting in stupors everywhere. Yet they rarely kill the warm-blooded sleepers around them; butchering a peccary or a deer in ninety-eight-degree stagnant air creates forty pounds of putrefying offal within four hours, which draws army ants out of the root-mats in blind, million-jawed rivers that scour everything down to the bedrock. 

Instead, they graze the abundance in small, neat, bloodless mouthfuls. They collect tortoise eggs laid warm in the red clay seams; they pluck giant silk-moth pupae as big and buttery as avocados straight from the leaf-ribs; they gather fist-sized land crabs from the cold trench, cracking the carapaces silently between two smooth limestones and sucking the sweet, chilled white meat raw while tossing the shells immediately back to the millipedes, which vanish every scrap before sunrise. When they want cooked food, they wrap palm-hearts, toad legs, and wild figs tightly in broad, waxy *Calathea* leaves tied with vine-bast, and wedge the packets into the scorching V-shaped crease where the two heated crude pipelines touch. Left against the hundred-and-fifty-degree steel for six hours, the bundles slow-braise in their own juices until they turn soft as marrow, infused with a faint, smoky-mineral undertone from the bitumastic tar.

Water is cleaner than anything in the cities on the coast: atmospheric moisture condensed directly onto chilled steel, pure distilled dew, caught before it hits the mud in shallow troughs folded from tough tree-spathe and hung from the belly of Pipe Three with loops of copper wire. It tastes faintly of iron dust and bat-fur, cold enough to make your teeth ache in the velvet heat.

What is strangest about them, if you sit beside them long enough in the dark for your own breathing to slow down and your clothes to rot off your back, is what happens to a human mind when sight is subtracted and struggle becomes unnecessary.

Above the rim, human life is organized around edges—horizons, walls, clocks, distances between here and there. Down in the pan, where sight ends at your own elbow and ninety-nine degrees feels identical to skin, the boundary between your body and the rest of the basin dissolves completely. Sitting under the rubber tarp with the old Tender and the sinkhole woman while the *plink, tup, plink* marks off time that doesn't go anywhere, you cannot tell where your hip ends and the warm clay begins, or whether the slow rise and fall against your ribs is your own lung, the woman’s flank, or the sleeping tapir wedged three feet away in the mud wallow, sighing through its prehensile nose.

They have enormous, intricate inner lives built entirely of touch, smell, and vibration. They know every weld-bead, flange-bolt, dent, and corrosion-pit on all four pipelines for four miles as intimately as a blind monk knows an illuminated manuscript; crawling along the iron in the dark, their calloused fingertips read the geography of the valley through steel typography—*here is the double-repair patch from 1998 where the giant katydids lay their eggs in rows; sixty feet past the sunken flange is where the albino boa sheds every third month; ten yards beyond the crooked roller-guide, the limestone dips into cool sulfur mud that cures an aching tooth if you pack your jaw with it.*

They know when it is raining fifty miles away in the mountains because the pitch of the chilled water rushing inside Pipe Three shifts half a semitone higher twelve hours later. They know when the world outside is having a boom or a war or a strike by the heat and urgency of the oil lines: sometimes for months the crude pipes cool down to body temperature and the fifty-hertz thrum fades to a sleepy murmur, and then the snakes scatter back out onto the limestone flats, the ticks go dormant in the dust, and the six or seven humans drift closer together along the cold pipe, sharing body heat under one long stitched-together rubber awning like hibernating bats. Then, without warning, far-off valves open, the unseen pumps twenty miles north spin up to a deep, chest-rattling roar, the steel swells fractionally with fresh heat, and within two nights the great braided ropes of boas, vipers, and iguanas flow silently back out of the dark thickets to reclaim the welds.

Sometimes—maybe once every three or four years—the green inspection collar detects a genuine thinning in the wall of Pipe Four, or a weld seam weeps more crude than the automated pressure-sensors on the coast will tolerate.

The basin-dwellers always know twenty-four hours before anything arrives. First, the induction hum in the overhead cables sharpens, setting the violet corona fuzz crackling fiercely in the wet air so that if you look straight up, the black ceiling seems dusted with faint, greasy purple embers. Then the ultrasonic crawler breaks its eternal six-feet-per-minute routine; it stops, reverses, rolls back and forth over the same ten-foot stretch of steel twenty times in an hour, its tiny green eye frantic.

Long before the heavy tracked swamp-buggy grinds over the northern limestone rim five miles away—splintering the ancient quiet with diesel clatter and stabbing sweeping, blinding white LED flood-bars through the stagnant mist—the canopies are already gone. 

Rolled into tight black bundles, weighted with stone, they sink without a bubble into the bottomless, ink-black water of the deepest karst sinkholes. The bark ramps are tossed into the shrub-tangles, where aerial roots and sleeping moths obscure them in ten minutes. The people do not run; running in the basin is impossible anyway. They simply roll three yards off the right-of-way into the nearest clay wallow between a pair of five-hundred-pound Baird’s tapirs and four dozen stacked tortoises, smear two handfuls of fresh red mud over their shaved scalps and pale yeast-mottled shoulders, close their eyes against the coming glare, and drop their heart rates.

When the three-man maintenance crew finally churns up to the coordinates inside a pressurized, air-conditioned cab—wearing thick yellow Tyvek suits, snake-gaiters to the thigh, and full-face respirators fogging instantly on the outside with condensation—they hate every second they are on the valley floor. Their floodlights turn the immediate twenty yards into a steaming, nightmarish diorama of glistening eyes, coiled scales, and drooping, insect-heavy leaves, while beyond that twenty-yard circle the humid dark swallows a ten-thousand-lumen beam like black felt. Everything smells suffocatingly of musk and iron. Their boots suck in the tacky clay; boas hiss sluggishly as hydraulic tongs shove them off the hot weld; moths the size of dinner-plates plaster themselves silently against the floodlight lenses until the glass smokes.

The engineers clamp a bolted steel split-sleeve over the weak pipe-section in forty frantic minutes, sweating rivers inside their chemical suits, cursing the heat, the snakes, the mud, and whatever lunatic routed four trunk-lines across this primeval sump without an access road. One of them swings his headlamp beam across a nearby limestone depression to check for bushmasters, illuminates a tangle of mud-caked hides rising and falling in slow, synchronized waves—two tapirs, three brocket deer, a wedge of giant tortoises, and a curved, mud-slicked flank tucked seamlessly between them—sees nothing that looks like a vertical, clothed, tool-bearing human, and swings the light back to his wrench.

Then the diesel roars, the tracks churn a twin scar into the waxy ground-vines that will grow back over in nine days, and the white lights crawl slowly back toward the invisible rim five miles north.

For an hour after the rumble dies out of the bedrock, nobody moves. The disturbed bats settle back into breathing curtains on the horizontal boughs. The displaced boas pour slowly back onto the warm new bolts of the repair sleeve, tasting the fresh factory grease with black, forked tongues and finding it acceptable. The air closes back over itself like warm oil in a pan.

Down in the clay wallow, two muddy fingers reach out in the absolute dark, find the edge of Pipe Four, and tap twice with a thumbnail against the iron. *Tik-tik.*

Half a mile east, and a mile west, soft through the warm steel under a million sleeping bellies, the pipe taps back.
