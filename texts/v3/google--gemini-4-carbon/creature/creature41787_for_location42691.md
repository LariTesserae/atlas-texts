Before anything shows itself at the eastern throat, the willow switches tell you it is coming. 

For months at a time, the four peeled wands stand as dead and plumb as surveyor’s stakes, their hanging selenite discs frozen in the pressurized calm. Then, on an afternoon no brighter or dimmer than any other, one of the discs rotates half a turn on its braided cord and stops. Ten minutes later, it turns back. Nothing can be heard yet—the smooth curve of the marl swallows the distant scrape of elbows and knees—but the displacement of a warm body moving through the tight pinch-points miles back in the chalk has pushed a slow, invisible piston of air ahead of it. Those switches are not a shrine or a marker; they are barometers. If you are sitting beside the basins and see the discs begin to twist, you know someone else is already in the pipe, and you wait, because there is nowhere in the eastern squeeze for two bodies to pass one another.

What eventually slides out into the tallow-glow, feet first, pushing a small, rolled bundle of cured bladder-skin ahead of its shins, is human—or close enough to share the bone-awls and the logic of a water-flute—though shaped by generations spent navigating the shallow, sun-baked fissures beneath the plain rather than walking upon it.

She is small, spare as a whippet, and coated from scalp to ankle in a deliberate, matte paste of dry marl-dust and rendered fat, smeared on until her skin has the exact chalky reflectance of the floor. Only along the sharp, sweating creases of her collarbone, the hollows behind her knees, and the insides of her wrists has the crawl rubbed the grease away, showing an undertone like cured, smoked quince—dark and leathery, rich with pigment kept not against a direct sun, which she rarely sees, but against the relentless, omnidirectional wash of ultraviolet that bleeds through the thinner gypsum lenses across this whole subterranean karst. 

When she stands up and brushes at her thighs—a quick, dry *frip-frip* that dies instantly in the flat acoustics—her posture stays tucked, economical, built for low ceilings. Her shoulders are narrow and unusually mobile, the clavicles bowed slightly forward so she can fold them nearly together when wriggling through a seam. Her feet are what left those neat, toe-deep tracks: narrow at the heel, spread wide and muscular at the forefoot, the big toes slightly divergent and calloused thick as horn on the tops as well as the soles from hooking onto ceiling-ledges and pushing off raw rock.

It is her face, though, that belongs entirely to the glare. Where cave-dwellers go wide-eyed and pale in the dark, the people of the selenite belts live inside a lightbulb, and their faces have closed down like shutters. Her brow ridge is low and thickened with a permanent shelf of bone and muscle, beneath which her eyes sit deep back in dark, unpainted sockets—the one place she leaves bare of white grease, except to smudge a faint ring of dark chert-soot along the lower lids. When she looks across the bowl toward the mirrored beetles, she does not open her eyes wide; she views the world through habitual, heavy-lidded slits, fringed with double rows of stiff, pale lashes clumped slightly with dust. If she turns her head quickly and the milky light catches the cornea, you see an iris so dark brown it is nearly black, pinned around a pupil shrunk to the size of a poppy seed, shielded at the inner corner by a pronounced, translucent third-lid fold that sweeps horizontally across the eye every quarter-minute to clear the drifting alkaline talc without requiring a full blink.

She does not speak, because there is no one with her, and even when two of them come together—usually an older woman and a child light enough to be pulled backward by the ankles if a squeeze goes bad—they have very little use for voice down here. In a room that presses sound flat and strips away directional echo, speech feels loud, vulgar, and strangely smothered all at once, like shouting into wool. Instead, as she crouches by the three melon-sized basins, her breathing is almost inaudible through wide, downward-hooded nostrils lined with coarse hair that traps the plaster-dust before it reaches her lungs.

Watch her hands. They are long-fingered, hard-knuckled, the nails worn square and thick from testing seams in marl. She does not drink yet. First, she picks up one of the split marrow-bone awls—checking its point with the pad of her thumb as casually as a seamstress returning to a table left before supper—and skims the thin, six-month skin of floating calcite off the top of the leftmost basin. She lifts it whole, like skin off scalded milk, and lays it gently on the gypsum slab beside the sorted beetle-shells. That crust isn't trash; ground down with spit and yellow ochre, the pure, precipitated crystal makes an abrasive paste fine enough to polish lenses, or to rub into small cuts so the alkaline minerals seal them clean without festering in the breath-warm air.

Only when the water lies bare and bright as glycerine does she unroll the bladder-skin bundle and draw out a hollow tube—one of the waxy, segmented horsetail stalks from this very room, harvested on a previous trip and cured dry until it is light and rigid as bamboo. She dips the reed in and draws up half the basin in one slow, even pull, her throat working thrice, leaving the bottom couple of inches undisturbed so the settled grit doesn’t cloud it. The water tastes intensely of wet stone, Epsom, and faint iron, bitter enough to make an outsider gag, though to her tongue—raised on mineral seep—rainwater from the surface tastes thin, dead, and frighteningly empty.

Why come here at all, through half a mile of bruised ribs and dusty belly-crawling, to a room that grows no tubers, harbors no game larger than a thumb-length gecko, and sweats a pint of bitter water a day?

You find out when she moves to the wall.

She steps carefully between the polite intervals of the living fan-stalks—never brushing their papery horizontal vanes, because the oils on human skin bruise the waxy cuticle and make the plant rot back to the silt within a month. She carries the flat gypsum slab in the crook of her left arm and holds a dull chert flake in her right. Up on the mid-wall, where the thousands of coin-sized grazers hang like hammered tin, her approach registers not by shadow—she casts none—Wait: she *does* cast that faint, warm occlusion, and as her body comes within two feet of the marl, the temperature of the rock shifts by half a degree, and her breath changes the humidity on their shells. A patch of three hundred mirrored carapaces abruptly clamps down tight against the stone with a collective, dry *shick*, like a handful of thumbnails clicking together at once.

She doesn’t pry at the clamped ones; pried shells split along the suture and ruin the mirror. Instead, she squats on her heels, still as one of the blind geckos high near the roof, tucking her chin to her chest so her breath spills downward between her knees. Five minutes pass. Ten. A drop falls into the middle basin—*plip*. High overhead, a cloud must be crossing the unseen plain, because the blinding tallow of the walls dims for forty seconds to the color of old ivory, then swells seamlessly back to full, shadowless milk.

One by one, the beetles relax their suction feet, rising a millimeter on their hair-thin legs to resume their clock-hand grazing. 

Her hand moves—not fast, which stirs the air, just an unhesitating, practiced flick with the flat of the bone awl slipped beneath a shell's rim before the creature knows the stone has been touched. *Tick.* It drops into her waiting palm. She turns it over. Beneath the polished, metallic dome, the creature is soft, pale yellow, segmented like a pillbug, smelling sharply of iodine and crushed moss from the endolithic algae in its gut. With her thumbnail, she pinches the nerve-knot behind its mouthparts, pops the meat whole between her teeth—chewing twice with a brisk, sour grimace, because down here calories and trace salts are never thrown on the floor—and wipes the clean, concave shell with a pinch of dry silt before setting it on her slab alongside the older ones.

She takes only forty or fifty from this quadrant of the bowl, spacing her thefts wide so the grazing-film won't bloom unchecked and turn the wall slick and black, which would spoil the sweat-pores below. And she collects two of the pale, velvet geckos that have died naturally and fallen at the foot of the eastern curve, desiccated by the dry floor-silt before rot could ever start, light and translucent as dried locust-husks, curled into brittle little commas. Those go into a tiny pouch of woven plant-fiber at her hip; ground fine, their unpigmented, heat-sensing skins are thought by the elders back in the deeper habitation-caverns to cure night-blindness and fevers brought down from the surface.

Before she leaves, there is maintenance. Nobody owns this chamber, just as nobody owns a rain-barrel on a shared mountain pass, yet the etiquette of the bright-rooms is older than any living memory in the warrens. Using the dark chert hand-axe—holding it directly in her palm without a haft, rocking its bruised edge back and forth with the heel of her hand—she deepens the leftmost soapstone flute by the thickness of a fingernail where mineral deposit has begun to choke the trickle. The sound it makes is a harsh, soapy *scritch-scritch* that dies three feet from her knees. She checks the four willow switches; one has dried brittle at the base and leans an inch off true, so she pulls it up, snaps six inches off the rotted bottom, and presses it firmly back into the silt until the chipped selenite disc hangs clear again.

Then she sits for twenty minutes with the lump of yellow ochre on her thigh, spitting a precious thimbleful of the bitter basin-water onto its flat ground face, working up a thick mustard-colored slip with two fingers. Slowly, working entirely by feel, she refreshes the rings around her collarbones, paints three neat dots on the scarred tops of each big toe, and draws a wide, dull band across the bridge of her nose below the soot-line—cutting the bounce-glare off her own cheekbones before she heads back into the brighter, shallower seams to the east, where the selenite roof drops so low to the floor you have to crawl for half an hour with your cheek an inch from burning-bright crystal.

When she packs the bladder-skin—taking the sorted pile of fifty older, fully cured mirror-shells that the last visitor left drying six months ago, leaving her fresh forty neatly graded by diameter in their place—the chamber is already forgetting her. 

She backs into the low horizontal throat feet-last this time, pulling the bundle behind her by a rawhide loop gripped in her teeth until the pinch passes her hips, undulating her narrow shoulders with a quiet, practiced bonelessness that barely raises a puff of talc. For three minutes after her greased heels vanish into the bright cleft, the four hanging discs of selenite turn slowly clockwise, then counterclockwise, registering the plug of air she draws after her down the pipe.

Then the cords go dead plumb. 

In the silt around the basins, a newly overlaid set of narrow, splayed footprints sits beside the older ones—identical in gait, slightly smaller in the arch, indistinguishable in age now that the dust has settled. Up near the glowing suture of the dome, a bone-white gecko shifts half an inch to the left, its pink lungs pulsing visibly through its ribs as it tracks the minute vibration of a mite descending toward a wet pore. Five minutes gather in the pressurized, breath-warm glare. Along the green soapstone flank, a heavy bead of stone-sweat swells, trembles, loses its grip, and slides down the newly deepened chert groove to strike the bare surface of the left-hand basin—*plip*—where the first microscopic needles of a new calcite skin are already beginning to knit themselves together across the still water.
