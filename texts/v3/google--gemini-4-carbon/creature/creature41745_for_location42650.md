If you watch the thumb-width channels between the river-granite cobbles for an afternoon, nothing happens. If you watch them for a week, still nothing happens, and it becomes easy to believe that the arrangement is an impossible fluke of hydraulic equilibrium—a five-thousand-year-old machine that runs itself.

It is not. Cushion moss does not respect history; left alone, those emerald-black bolsters would swell across a thumb’s breadth of granite in three years and drink the channel dry. Calcite, leaching continually from the weeping dolostone overhead, would glaze the soapstone bowls in flowstone rind within a century. And the sweet, pale biofilm that clings to the chipped edges of the chert only thrives on raw, freshly fractured silica; once the microscopic teeth of a stone edge dull and oxidize in the cold seepage, the film starves and peels away. 

For the channels to remain open, a thumb has to pass through them.

It does not come down through the juniper roof. Overhead, the trembling brown ceiling of harvestmen hangs unbroken from wall to wall, millions of six-inch legs hooked together in a net so delicate that a falling pinecone would tear a rent a yard wide. There is no rent. Nothing climbs down, and nothing climbs out. 

Instead, look five feet back from the edge of the pool, where a stunted rhododendron bends horizontally under an enormous, sodden bolster of moss and beard-lichen. Look at the way the slate-colored cave-salamanders are braided there. Everywhere else along the limestone ledges, they lie in single or double heaps; on the flank of this particular hummock, they are stacked four deep, packed tight as bundled roots, pressing their slick bellies inward toward the mound’s core. 

Put a palm through the cold jelly of salamanders, push past three inches of wet moss and pale, tangled hair the texture of juniper bark, and what your fingers meet is not stone. It gives. It is dense, heavy subcutaneous fat under skin as thick and poreless as mushroom cap, and in the forty-six-degree gloom, it burns at a steady, luxuriant fifty-three.

There are three of them in the basin. There have almost always been three of them—sometimes two after a hard century, briefly four when one is very small and another is very close to dissolving—because three is the exact number of large, slow furnaces the calorie budget of the sinkhole can sustain without tipping into famine.

If you hauled one out into the thin alpine sun above, washed the green velvet algae from the creases of its knuckles and scraped the liverworts from the shelf of its brow, an anatomist would know within a minute what genus it belonged to, though the species would start an argument that lasted decades. The pelvis is broad and bowl-shaped; the femur, bowed and massive, was built in some forgotten Pleistocene valley to carry weight upright up steep talus slopes. The skull is long, low-vaulted, and thick-walled, with heavy, rounded supraorbital ridges overhanging shallow sockets where eyes no longer bother to finish forming—just smooth, seamless pads of pale skin sunken beneath bone, scarred faintly where embryonic lids fused shut before birth.

They came down when the mountain was shaped differently, before the upper dolostone finished collapsing, when the sinkhole was still a steep cleft where shy, broad-chested toolmakers could shelter out of the wind with an armful of river-cobbles and blocks of soft soapstone. Perhaps an earthquake dropped the juniper mat across the lip and sealed the chimney; perhaps the winters outside grew so sharp during an ice advance that leaving simply stopped making sense. Mammalian flesh usually dies in persistent wet cold, burning through its own muscle in frantic shivering until the heart stops. Why theirs did not—what freakish latent capacity for torpor they carried in their marrow, shared with marmots and cave-bears and fat-tailed lemurs, allowing the hypothalamus to turn the thermostat down instead of up—is lost. What remains is what the sinkhole made of them afterward.

They do not walk through the room because walking costs heat, wakes the toads, and bruises the ferns. They lie wedged into the foundational forks of the ancient rhododendrons, half-submerged in the peat-moss sponge, folded on their sides like sleepers in cold bedrooms. Their hearts beat three times a minute. Between one breath and the next, forty seconds of wet static pass; when their ribs finally expand, the inhalation is so slow and shallow through wide, flat nostrils that the mist in front of their faces hardly eddies. 

A single gesture takes an afternoon.

Watch the largest of the three—an old female whose spine has grown gently curved to match the lobe of dolostone behind her, her shoulders shingled in tiny translucent snails that graze harmlessly on the sloughing keratin of her skin. Around noon, though noon means nothing here, some faint shift in the taste of the seepage—a microscopic thinning of the sugar-film scent reaching her upper lip—registers in the vast, unhurried dark of her brain. 

Her left arm is already resting along the moss-floor toward the pool. Slowly, at the pace an hour hand creeps across a clock face, the thick fingers uncurl. A glass frog sitting on her wrist does not leap; it merely shifts its sticky toes a fraction of an inch to stay level as the tendon tightens beneath it. Over two hours, her forearm slides forward through the black porridge of amphipods without raising a ripple. Her thumb—broad as a walnut, capped with a horn-thick, yellow nail worn square at the tip—settles into the granite channel.

With one continuous, glacial push, she draws the thumb six inches back through the groove. A year’s worth of encroaching moss-threads and precipitated limestone grit peels smoothly away under the nail. The clear wall-water, freed from a minute damming, slips a fraction faster over the slate bed. 

She does not pull her hand back. Tomorrow, or the day after, her fingers will close around one of the chert nodules resting in the water and lift it an inch above its neighbor. Then comes the one fast motion left in their repertoire: a short, practiced drop of the wrist. 

*Tock.*

Underneath the quarter-inch of water, stone strikes stone. It is no louder than two billiard balls kissing through a blanket, yet in the breathless hush of the sinkhole it lands like thunder. Half a dozen salamanders twitch along the walls. A fresh, razor-sharp flake of grey chert shears off the core, exposing virgin silica to the current, and settles at the bottom of the soapstone bowl. Within days, the sweet, pale biofilm will bloom across the new fracture like frost on a windowpane, feeding the gelatinous bryozoans, drawing the blind amphibians in to lay their leathery strings of roe. She is not making knives. Nobody down here has needed to cut anything since the glaciers retreated from the lowlands. She is plowing a field two inches wide, refreshing the stone so the stone will feed the slime so the slime will feed the eggs.

Before her hand retreats—a journey back to her ribs that will last until tomorrow—her scooped palm passes through the soapstone bowl, gathering two ounces of dense, gelatinous roe, a cluster of bryozoans, and a thick skimming of blind white amphipods. When her fingers reach her mouth, her heavy lips part underwater-slow and take the mass in whole, without chewing, letting her tongue press the cool protein against the roof of her palate until it slides down. 

Nothing is stolen by this. The accounting is exact. In the matted, lichen-threaded hair behind her ears, where the skin is warmest, two sightless shrews have woven a dryish pocket to sleep off the chill between hunts, dropping tiny, nitrogen-rich pellets that wash down her collarbone to feed the spleenworts rooted in the mud beneath her hip. The salamanders braided across her belly incubate their eggs three weeks faster against her fifty-three-degree hide than they can on the forty-six-degree rock. Even the bones are merely on loan. You will find no skulls buried in the peat, no ancient graves under the ferns, because calcium carbonate is precious in bog-water: when one of the three finally stops breathing—after two hundred years, or three, when the slow heart simply forgets the next beat—the shrews and fungi clear the tallow and muscle within a week, and then the pale snails converge in thousands, rasping the ribs and skull with microscopic radulae year after year until the entire skeleton has been dissolved and rebuilt into tens of thousands of tiny, coiled shells clinging to the undersides of leaves. Every ancestor she has ever had is still in the room, gleaming faintly on the backs of snails.

It would be easy, looking at that mossy, immovable bulk with frogs cobbled across its knuckles, to assume that the mind inside the thick skull has guttered out—that tool-maker has subsided entirely into turnip, running on brainstem reflex alone.

Look closer at their feet.

In the center of the sinkhole, beneath two feet of saturated cushion moss where the twisted rhododendron roots cross and graft together into a single woody floor, the long, prehensile toes of all three individuals are hooked around the same thick root-stock. They cannot see each other ten feet away through the solid green labyrinth, and their vocal cords thickened into silent pads of gristle millennia ago, because speech wastes breath and breath wastes heat. Instead, they hold the wood, and they hold each other’s ankles where they overlap in the peat.

What did all that big hominin cortex do when light vanished, when horizons shrank to sixty feet of wet stone, and time stretched out into an endless, forty-six-degree pause? It turned entirely to touch. Without eyes to drink up eighty percent of the brain's attention, their tactile nerves have colonized the whole quiet cathedral of their skulls. Lying still in the sponge, each of them feels the sinkhole in high-relief, continuous three-dimensional detail through ten square feet of skin and the resonant wood under their soles.

They feel the minute, rhythmic shift of pressure when the younger male, fifteen feet away under a curtain of spleenwort, flexes two toes against the shared root—not words, nothing so clipped and frantic as words, just a long, chorded modulation of grip that lets the other two share the exact shape of his wakefulness, his satiety, the tickle of a beetle crossing his shin. They feel the weight of snow piling onto the mountain peak a thousand feet overhead by the hair-thin compression it sends groaning down the dolostone ribs. Three days after an August thunderstorm batters the ridge outside, when the rainwater has finally filtered down through the high aquifers to sweat out of the walls, they taste the trace of lightning-fixed nitrates on their wet lips and tighten their toes around the root together in something vast, wordless, and pleased.

Right now, tucked deep into the warm fold between the old female’s belly and her drawn-up thighs, completely buried under a living blanket of wrist-thick salamanders, there is a fourth thing—hairless, blind, no larger than a loaf of bread, clamped onto a nipple that yields one slow, rich drop of seal-thick milk an hour. It has been that size for twelve years. It is in no hurry to grow. It will wait in the warm dark until the eldest male across the basin—whose breathing has lately stretched to fifty-five seconds between rises, whose skin has cooled to forty-nine degrees—finally lets go of the root beneath the moss and gives his calcium back to the snails. Only then, when the budget opens by one slot, will hormones stir in the dark, and bones lengthen, and a small, broad thumb begin reaching blindly through the peat to learn the shape of river-granite.

Above the juniper roof, hawks wheel in thin blue air, empires redraw their borders, timberlines advance and retreat, and hikers pass within half a mile complaining of the fog. Down in the stone lung, the old female’s hand finally settles back against her ribs. A glass frog hops onto her knuckle. Forty seconds pass. Her chest rises a quarter of an inch in the dark, tasting broth and sweet flint, holding the root, wanting nothing.
