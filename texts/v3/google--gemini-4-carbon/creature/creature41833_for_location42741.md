To understand what lives in the blister, you have to watch the floor. 

The carpet of spent glass and cracked carapaces is four inches deep in the basins, shifting constantly like wet gravel in a sluice, yet not a single creature bleeding into the scalding runoff is bleeding from its feet. The pale, terrier-sized climbers haul their heavy, musculature-knotted bodies straight up Vertical needles of fresh obsidian, bracing their weight on edges a molecule thick, and their hide does not part. It merely dents, whitening briefly under the pressure like hard tallow, then rebounds. 

Their skin is dead long before it reaches the surface. Heated to the brink of cooking by the geothermal draft, the outer epidermis cures into a dense, vulcanized rind impregnated with silica absorbed from their diet. They do not shed this armor; they let the room plane it down. A climber squeezing through a jagged basalt slot leaves behind translucent, curled shavings of its own flank, pared away cleanly like horn under a drawknife. Beneath that grey-white rind, their bodies have no rigid skeleton—no ribs to snap when three rivals pile onto their backs, no long bones to lever out of sockets when wedged into a narrow fault. Internally, they are hydraulic: bundles of dense, gristly muscle wrapped around pressurized bladders of hot fluid. That is why their six limbs look jointless, bending in smooth, sickening arcs wherever leverage demands, flattening to the thickness of a handspan to worm into a crevice, then pumping rigid again to pry rock apart.

They have no faces. A face is an arrangement of fragile things brought forward to meet the world, and in a cistern packed this tightly with razors and desperate neighbors, anything brought forward is immediately sheared off. Instead, their sensory cluster sits recessed in the thick saddle between the first and second pairs of limbs, sheltered behind hooded folds of gristle. Three pairs of gill-slits—more properly heat-exchangers, lined with bright violet vascular fringes that dump excess body warmth into the slightly cooler pockets of air—flare and snap shut in uneven rhythms. Ringing those slits are twenty or thirty pinhead eyes, hard as quartz beads. 

If you watch those eyes, you realize quickly they are not looking at shapes. The crisp, shadowless daylight makes everything in the pocket visually flat—black glass on black rock under colorless water—while the broken acoustics make everything dark with impact. When a climber drives a stone chisel into a grazer’s shell, the missed sound blooms in the air as a fist-sized smudge of indigo-violet bruising. Multiply that by ten thousand tiny collisions a second—claws on slag, shells clicking, jaws snapping, water ticking—and the air inside the blister is constantly stippled with flickering, dark-purple shock-blots. 

The pale climbers hunt by watching the bruises. 

An ambush-worm shifting its coils deep inside an opaque crack makes no noise, because noise doesn't carry, so its scrape against the interior basalt bleeds straight through the solid rock as a faint, plum-colored stain on the surface. Immediately, a climber’s bead-eyes lock onto the discoloration. Its three-fingered forepaw—split down to the wrist like a woodcock’s bill, lined with rough, calloused pads—selects a long needle of glass and drives it two inches to the left of the stain, compensating effortlessly for the way stone bends light-bruises. When the needle pierces the worm, a bright, dense bead of pure black pops into existence at the tip of the skewer: the muted scream of ruptured hydrostatic flesh, converted entirely into brief local darkness.

Down underneath the tangle of their slick limbs, the smaller tiers of the cistern aren't merely enduring the climbers; they are feeding on the industry.

The knuckle-sized grazers heaped along the sulfur-slime ledges have translucent shells made not of calcium—there is almost no lime in this young volcanic thrust—but of cross-linked proteins plated with raw iron sulfide precipitated out of the vent-steam. Near the bottom of a heap, where the water runs hot enough to poach an ordinary animal instantly, the shells tarnish to dull brass. These grazers have no eyes at all, only a fringe of stiff bristles around their skirts that feel the micro-tremors of stone flaking off the walls. When a climber overhead snaps a fresh blade from a ridge, the shock-darkness doesn't reach the grazers, so instead they react to the sudden drop in local tension within the rock face itself. The entire heap clamps down at once, sucking flat against the basalt with muscular feet so fierce that a climber’s obsidian chisel often shatters before breaking the seal. 

Between the grazers glide the palm-wide leeches, though they aren't true annelids. Peel one off the obsidian and its underside is an intricate, fleshy labyrinth of tiny suction-cups that pass scalding water backward in rhythmic waves, skimming off invisible mats of hyper-thermophilic bacteria and fine glass-dust. That glass-dust doesn't pass through them as waste. Inside their flat, liver-colored bodies, specialized glands cement the microscopic obsidian splinters along their outer margins, giving a soft-bodied slug a rim as sharp as a circular saw. When a pale climber steps carelessly on a leech, the flat creature whips sideways with terrifying speed, trying to slice through the tough, vulcanized pad of a digit to drink the pressurized, copper-smelling lymph inside. Half the climbers in the chamber are missing the tips of one or two fingers, the stumps healed over into smooth, blunt nubs that they use as hammers to tap the backs of their stone chisels.

Even the displaced spaces have their own distinct populations, cropped and tended like orchards by the crowd in the room.

That dinner-plate puddle in the center of the floor—the one still holding the white smear of five-minute-old cirrus on its skin—is an unhealed fold from when the mountain tore upward through the crust, pinching a pocket of subterranean thermal brine miles deeper in the range and leaving its mouth open right here on the cistern floor. What lives down inside that non-geometric depth never sees the sky, and cannot survive the oxygen of the alcove. The scaleless, thrashing beasts hauled out of it are blind, corpse-white eels as thick as a man's thigh, with mouths like pleated funnels meant for gulping mineral mud under immense pressure. The moment a climber drags one up through the two-inch threshold into the low atmospheric pressure of the high peak, the eel begins to swell and rupture internally, its dissolved gases boiling out of its tissues. The climbers don't even have to kill them; they just pin them down with two rear limbs until the decompression stops their thrashing, then butcher them fast before the meat turns spongy. 

Around the lip of that impossible puddle sits a permanent ring of specialized scavengers—spidery, long-shanked things with boiled-pink shells and claws shaped like sugar-tongs. They never touch the climbers, and the climbers largely ignore them. Their entire existence consists of reaching delicately into the rim of the puddle to pluck parasites off the skins of the deep-brine eels *just* as they are dragged across the threshold, snatching tiny, pressurized lice before they pop in the thin mountain air.

Along the western wall, where gravity tips thirty degrees sideways and the boiling sheet-wash skates horizontally across the glass before remembering how to fall, the physics shear creates a trap that requires no tools at all. 

The hooked scavengers hanging there in dry clusters look like dried figs bristling with black thorns. They aren't holding onto the air; their thorns are hooked into the gravitational crease itself, hanging from the sharp thirty-degree angle in space the way a bat hangs from a rafter. Anything washed down from the upper rim—dead grazers, dropped scraps of eel-meat, chipped obsidian with ribbons of slime still clinging—hits that tilted gravity-band and suddenly shoots sideways at high speed, slamming directly into the waiting basket-thorns of the fig-creatures. They feed passively on the sideways river, swelling fat and tight-skinned, until a climber braces three limbs on true-vertical rock, leans two limbs into sideways-rock—its body twisting horribly across the invisible boundary as half its weight tries to fall toward the north wall and half toward the floor—and rakes the fattest ones loose with a long flake of slag.

And then there is what happens at the top, just under the ragged lip where the pale afternoon sky looks in.

Watch the climbers closely after they pry open one of those midair seams and lap up the colorless, ozone-stinking fluid that bleeds out of the misaligned geometry. 

The ones who get a mouthful do not go back to prying grazers off the wall. Immediately after swallowing that cold, metallic-smelling drip—which steams in the chamber not because it is boiling, but because it is catastrophically cold, condensing the wet air into instant white fog—a visible spasm runs through their hydraulic bodies. Their violet gill-fringes turn briefly black. The bead-eyes around their collars roll inward, disappearing beneath their tough lids. 

For ten or fifteen minutes, a climber that has drunk from a spatial seam becomes slightly unmoored from the sequence of the room. 

You can see three or four of them resting on a narrow shelf just below the stuttering water-drops, shuddering faintly. When another animal jostles one of them, the drinker reacts half a second *before* the shoulder hits it, flinching out of the way of pressure that hasn't arrived yet. When one of these ozone-drunk climbers picks up a triangular shard of black glass and reaches toward a rock-cleft, its split paw stutters just like the falling water overhead—appearing in three slight, overlapping positions at once—and pulls out an ambush-worm that was still six inches too deep to reach, catching the prey where it was going to be a moment later. 

It takes a terrible toll on them. The cold geometry burns their insides. You can tell the oldest, most dominant climbers in the cistern—the slightly larger ones who always muscle their way to the high ledges near the rim—by the frostbite-like scarring around their mouths and the way their outlines occasionally blur at the edges, like heat-haze over tarmac, even when they are holding completely still. Their tough, silica-cured hides develop hairline cracks that don't bleed lymph, but instead leak tiny, silent puffs of bruise-purple darkness whenever they flex too hard.

When one of those old ones finally gives out—usually right after a seam snaps shut and splinters a stone wedge against its chest—it doesn't fall cleanly. It tumbles down through the tangled mass of its peers, hitting the sideways-gravity band on the west wall, shooting five feet horizontally through a curtain of scalding sheet-water, dropping out the bottom edge onto the carpet of broken glass, and immediately vanishing under a surging blanket of grazers, saw-rimmed leeches, and younger climbers with fresh, sharp flakes already in their paws. 

No battle cry goes up; no death-rattle echoes off the sharp walls. As the tough hide is sawed open and the pressurized bladders collapse, the air above the carcass merely dims for ten seconds into a thick, localized twilight—a silent, purple-black fog of absorbed tearing and furious chewing—while clear sunlight pours straight through it to glitter on the wet glass underneath, and within four minutes there is nothing left on the gravel at all except a handful of blunted obsidian blades dropped by the butchers when they finished.

Nobody sleeps here. There is nowhere flat enough to lie down without sliding into scalding water or being trampled by twenty wet feet, and besides, the geothermal vent never slows its breathing, and the mountain hasn't finished settling. Every hour or two, deep beneath the cistern floor, the young andesite shifts by a millimeter. No rumble shakes the chamber. Instead, every single shadowless facet of rock inside the room turns pitch-black for one heartbeat as the stone absorbs its own groan, a fresh crop of razor-flakes pops loose from the walls into waiting three-fingered paws, a new hairline seam opens crookedly near the ceiling, and the silent, steaming crowd surges upward to wedge it open before the sky can heal it shut.
