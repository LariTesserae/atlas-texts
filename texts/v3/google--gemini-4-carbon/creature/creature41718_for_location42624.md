At first, you think it is only a snag of pale, dead bladder-wrack caught where a dry wind-ribbon shears against the amber brine. 

It hangs motionless in the dense current, draped over the lip of a wide, granite-carved bowl. Then the thermal river shifts, dragging a veil of smoke-silt across the clearing, and the snag does not drift with it. Instead, it reaches out a long, ribbon-flat limb, hooks two boneless fingers around the invisible, rubbery skin of a passing vapor-mirror, and pulls itself forward without making a ripple.

They have no name for themselves, because names require sound, and this ocean eats sound before it can cross the width of a hand. If an outsider were to see them, they might call them the Binders, or the Skin-Tenders, though at first glance they barely seem separated from the syrupy medium they inhabit. 

Up close, a Tender is roughly the mass of a tall human, though arranged with none of a terrestrial creature’s anxious, load-bearing rigidity. Bone would be an absurdly heavy luxury here, yet pure jelly would be torn apart where the physics fray. Instead, their bodies are built on a lattice of flexible, translucent silica-cartilage—glass that bends like green willow—wrapped in flat, overlapping shawls of muscle the color of smoked tallow and bruised plum. They have no true head, only a thickening at the crest of the mantle where the flesh grows clear as oiled parchment. Inside that pale dome, you can dimly see a dark, branching cluster of sensory nerves swaying in its own internal fluid, shaped remarkably like the maroon hydrocorals outside. 

They do not have eyes for looking straight ahead; in a world lit like the inside of an amber stone, straight lines are useless. Instead, two rows of wide, shallow, gold-rimmed pits run down the margins of their upper shawls, drinking in the scattered twilight and the warped, upside-down reflections bouncing off the overhead gas-lenses. A Tender never looks *at* you. It reads the way your bulk dims the ambient glow from six directions at once, and it watches your approach ten miles before you arrive, upside-down in the silver belly of a drifting cathedral of steam.

Watch one work the boundary of a cairn, and you begin to understand what they actually are.

The Tender slides down the whale-backed swell of rose granite, moving not by swimming—swimming is too loud, too wasteful in water as thick as warm oil—but by *peeling* and *laying* itself against the invisible architecture of the sea. Where warm brine meets a denser, cooler current, the boundary has a slight, resistant rind. The Tender’s broad, cleft extremities press against these thermoclines like a mime’s hands against glass, finding purchase on pressure alone. Beneath its skirts, pale, muscular creeping-soles secrete a slick, heavy mucus that grips polished stone where nothing else can root, allowing it to walk leans of forty degrees through a roaring silt-storm as casually as a goat on dry scree.

It pauses beside a five-sided arch of pale bone-lime. Half of the arch sits in warm, wet broth; the other half stands inside a stray ribbon of dry highland wind that smells of ancient, sun-baked stone and thin violet sky. 

A fish, or a silk-worm, would recoil from that dry gap as if from fire, or suffocate in it. The Tender simply parts its outer shawls. Beneath its heavy, wet skin, tucked into deep pleats along its flanks, are wide, velvety gill-books lined with stiff silica combs. When the Tender steps out of the brine and into the dry wind-ribbon, the sudden drop in pressure snaps those pleats shut, trapping quarts of syrupy water inside while exposing a tougher, waxy outer hide that gleams dryly in the dusty draft. 

Standing completely dry on the seafloor, the wind whipping dead silt around its ankles while forty stories of ocean lean quietly against an invisible wall two feet to its left, the Tender reaches down and picks up a basalt pebble.

The pebble is almost round, but not quite. It has a slight ridge left from some forgotten fracture eons ago. The Tender tucks the stone into a deep, muscular pouch at the center of its chest. If you were close enough—pressed practically skin-to-skin, which is the only way anything hears anything down here—you would feel a faint, rhythmic *grit-grit-grit* vibrating through the creature’s cartilage. Their gizzards are lined with precipitated quartz teeth. They do not eat the rampant, mucilaginous jungle directly; they graze the mineral glaze off the kelp-tubers, swallow rough stone, and spend decades rolling basalt and granite in their bellies, digesting the trace iron and silicates while grinding the rock down into flawless spheres.

*Nobody knows what built the cairns,* an observer might think, looking for chisels or mortar or clever, jointed hands. They do not think to look for a mason that swallows a cobble when a century begins and coughs up a polished black pearl the size of a fist when it ends, laying it neatly onto a low circular wall before gliding away.

Why do they build them? Not for shelter; nothing hunts them, and the storms down here are soft as smoke. 

They build them to farm the glitches.

A Tender cannot survive on dissolved stone alone. It needs the rich, complex fats and sugars of decaying flora, and in an ocean this warm, thick, and crowded, rot is strangely hard to catch—everything is immediately reabsorbed by the endless, hungry mats of bryozoans before it can ferment into the heavy broth the Tenders crave. Worse, the plateau is monotonous. Without edges, currents wander aimlessly. 

But stack enough perfectly round, dense basalt spheres in a ring within a granite hollow, and the weary, ancient habits of the plateau snag on the geometry. The stone ring trips the bottom currents. A pocket of silver-clear, heavy rain begins to pool inside the basin like mercury. Or a ribbon of dry air catches on a five-sided bone-lime arch—built from the calcified, fused skeletons of Tenders who died standing linked together in a deliberate pentagon three thousand years ago—and holds open a dry meadow where underwater ferns shrivel, toast in the phantom mountain breeze, and crisp into sweet, brittle wafers rich with concentrated salts.

Most prized of all are the time-pools.

Follow the Tender as it leaves the dry wind-ribbon, stepping smoothly back through the wall of brine without raising a single bubble, its gill-pleats unfurling with a long, luxurious shudder into the warm soup. It hauls itself hand-over-hand up a hanging curtain of maroon bladder-wrack until it overlooks a deep, polished trough between two granite swells.

Down in the hollow sits a stalled storm. A waist-high front of grey colloidal silt hangs utterly motionless, curled like woodsmoke frozen in amber. Half of a massive, fleshy tuber-bush has grown across the invisible border of the pool. On the outside, its pale fronds twitch and shed spores in the warm current; five inches to the right, inside the pool, its sister-branches are locked in a century-old afternoon, plump with sugars that have not aged a second since the surface swells above rolled a million times ago.

Three other Tenders are already gathered at the rim of the slow-hollow. 

Seen together, they look less like distinct animals and more like a single, sprawling tapestry of plum and tallow velvet draped across the kelp. Because sound dies in yards, their entire social life is tactile and chemical. They press the flat, sensitive margins of their shawls together, passing complex ripples of muscle-twitching down the line—a silent, skin-to-skin conversation made of shivers, pressure-pulses, and sudden flushes of warm blood. At the same time, they release thin threads of heavy, tinted mucilage that do not dissolve in the brine, hanging between them like floating handwriting in flavors of copper, bitter iodine, and sweet lime. Through these slow, lingering ribbons, a solitary Tender wandering thirty miles away can cross a current weeks later, taste a drifting thread, and read the exact mood, health, and recent masonry of a cousin it hasn't touched in forty years.

Right now, the three at the hollow are doing something very delicate.

One of them—an immense, ancient individual whose silica-cartilage has grown so thick with age that its flanks chime faintly, *tink, tink*, inside its own flesh when it bends—is anchored firmly to the living kelp outside the time-pool. Its lower shawls are braided tightly with the shawls of the second Tender, who holds the ankles of the third. 

The third Tender leans far out over the stalled silt-storm and dips a single, long, ribbon-like arm into stagnant time.

It has to be extraordinarily careful. If a Tender slips entirely into a deep time-pool, its mind and flesh lock into the amber stillness along with the silt; you can occasionally find one down in the deepest, oldest trenches, crouched over a stone bowl, looking completely alive and supple, waiting to finish a single step it began before the continents elsewhere on this world had grass. 

As the third Tender’s arm crosses the boundary, the limb abruptly stiffens. Blood stops flowing past the elbow. Nerve impulses vanish into the slowness. From the elbow down, the arm becomes as rigid and immovable in the water as an iron bar, pinned in the stalled Tuesday of three weeks ago. Using its own frozen forearm as a lever—and braced by the braided strength of the two cousins hauling backward in normal time—the Tender pries against a boulder of salt-crust wedged at the lip of the pool, nudging the geometry of the hollow by half an inch.

The adjustment takes four hours. Nobody hurries. Above them, a house-sized lens of silver steam drifts past, showing them their own braided bodies upside-down amid a reflected forest ten miles to the west. A fifty-foot bolt of pale, many-eyed silk glides right over their backs, brushing their mantles like cool satin, tasting their mucus-threads and finding them far too mineral-tough to absorb beforeghosting silently through a five-sided arch.

Finally, the salt-boulder shifts. It drops two inches into the granite trough—a silent *thump* felt only in the cartilage of the chest.

The shape of the hollow changes just enough to break the snag. A tiny spill of stagnant time leaks out of the basin like cold syrup, washing over a cluster of thrashing hydrocorals and slowing their frantic growth to a drowsy crawl, while a fresh tongue of warm, fast brine curls into the stagnant pool, thawing two feet of frozen silt into sudden, billowing motion and waking up a century-old patch of ripe, unrotted tubers. 

Slowly, millimeter by millimeter as the boundary retreats, the third Tender’s arm thaws. Dark blood flushes back into the tallow-pale flesh; the boneless fingers curl and flex. It reaches down into the newly wakened patch of flora, plucks three fat, mineral-sweet bulbs that existed in two different centuries ten minutes ago, and passes them back along the living chain of its kin.

They do not celebrate, at least not in any way a noisy, short-lived creature would recognize. They simply stay braided together on the warm granite swell as the twilight deepens almost imperceptibly toward night—though down here, night only means the amber bruise overhead turns the color of dark tea. 

One by one, they tuck their sensitive crests beneath their heavy shawls. From deep inside its chest-pouch, the eldest Tender coughs up a newly finished sphere of black basalt, smooth as an eyeball, warmed by fifty years of body heat. Without looking, feeling only the subtle pull of the plateau's weary gravity and the way the dry highland wind is beginning to gather and whisper inside the nearby arch, it rolls the stone across the velvet silt. The sphere settles with a tiny, intimate *click* into a gap between two glazed rocks, holding the dry wind open for another thousand years, while the masons flatten themselves against the stone and go to sleep in the warm, silent dark.
