To find one, you cannot look for movement, and you cannot look for heat. At thirty-four degrees Celsius, anything warm-blooded would cook in its own skin within an hour of exertion; their internal chemistry runs at the exact ambient temperature of the basalt, neither radiating into the dark nor drawing from it. If you shine a light into a pitch-black chimney of fractured stone—an act that would blind and scatter the smaller, velvet-skinned gliders—you will likely look directly at one and mistake it for an intrusion of darker schist wedged between the selenite needles. 

Wait four hours. Wait six. At the end of the sixth hour, a single limb will unlock with a sound like a dry seedpod splitting, shift three inches to a fresh purchase on a razor-edge of quartz, and lock again.

They are built like siege engines made of slate and horn. Though vaguely hexapodal in the way they distribute their weight when climbing, two of the six limbs are held high and tucked tight against a narrow, vertically keeled thorax, folded back like a mantis’s, ending not in claws, but in four-thumbed, opposing clamps surfaced with calloused, stone-hard pads. The lower four limbs are long, double-jointed struts that bow outward from the body, keeping their center of gravity slung low over the jagged scree or pressed flat as a lichen against a vertical sheer. 

Nothing about them is soft. In a world where every surface is freshly broken glass and unweathered flint, exposed flesh is a death sentence; a single slice would spill the irreplaceable internal oil that cannot be drunk from any stream. Instead, they are armored in overlapping, articulated plates of dense, keratinous mineral-shell, dull grey and banded with rust-brown from the iron-rich crusts that sit at the bottom of their food chain. Where their joints flex, there is no supple skin, only tight, concertinaed bellows of dry, fibrous leather, dusted permanently with rock flour that acts as a dry lubricant so their movements do not squeak.

They have no mouths that open to the air, and they do not breathe with lungs. Breathing requires wet membranes, and wet membranes would crisp to ash in three breaths of the kiln-dry vault. Along the undersides of their thoracic plates run narrow, baffled slits lined with electrostatic bristles that trap dust while allowing the stagnant, low-oxygen air to diffuse slowly through dry, chitin-lined tracheae directly to their tissues. Because diffusion without pumping is slow, they cannot sprint. They do not know what a rush of adrenaline is. Their lives are lived in hydraulic, ratcheting increments.

What passes for a head is hardly separate from the shoulders—a low, wedge-shaped cowl of bone-hard carapace that protects their most delicate instruments. They have eyes, surprisingly, though not for seeing the landscape. Set deep in recessed pits are four wide, lensless patches of silvered retina, hyper-sensitized solely to register two things: the dim, static amber of stressed quartz—which tells them where the tectonic pillars bear the greatest weight and where the stone might unexpectedly shear—and the sudden, cold-blue flash of a predator’s lure. Otherwise, they navigate by wire and sound. Sprouting from the brow and the wrists are sweeping, six-foot filaments as thin as drawn glass, constantly, imperceptibly trembling, reading the electrostatic charge of nearby rock faces and the body-capacitance of the motionless fauna crowded around them.

When one of them enters a flaking floor to make a blade, the true strangeness of their nature emerges.

It does not strike the obsidian the way an upper-world hominid would, swinging a hammerstone through the air. Swinging generates heat, wastes precious joules, and sends sharp shrapnel flying unpredictably against neighbors or one’s own sealed joints. Instead, the maker squats low in the six-inch drift of ancient glass chips, bracing an obsidian core against a basalt anvil with its lower feet. It brings its two heavy, four-thumbed hands down and sets the tip of a chitin-hafted copper punch against a microscopic ridge it has found solely by touch. Then, it engages a set of ratcheting tendons in its shoulders. For ten minutes, nothing seems to happen. The creature is entirely still, cranking tension into its own skeletal frame like a crossbow being drawn by a windlass. 

*ink.* 

A sound sharp and high as a snapping needle rings out and dies instantly in the crowded stone. A six-inch ribbon of black glass, one molecule thick at the edge, falls away into the dust. The maker waits two minutes for the tiny fraction of muscle-heat generated by the release to dissipate through the broad, fin-like radiators on its back, feels the new face of the core with a thumb-pad sensitive enough to read the ripple-marks of the fracture, and resets the punch. A single hand-axe takes four days to finish. The maker does not get bored. Boredom is an adaptation for creatures that have seasons to beat or daylight to waste.

This same terrifying patience governs their hunting and their husbandry—for the two are barely distinguishable. 

Out in the selenite thickets, where the hound-sized colonial arachnids hang in their miles of metallic webbing, a pair of makers will work a single curtain of silk for decades. They do not raid the webs; they edit them. Moving slower than the spiders’ vibration-thresholds can register as prey—a millimeter per minute, pausing whenever an arachnid shifts its legs—they clip anchor-lines with obsidian shears and splice in their own braided cables, gradually rerouting the blind colony’s architecture to funnel wandering, oil-rich millipedes and velvet gliders into dead-end stone chutes. 

When they take a spider, or a heavy, loom-shaped grazer, there is no struggle. Two makers will pin the creature against the rock using forked struts of selenite, wedge its carapace open a fraction of an inch with a slate pry-bar, and insert a hollow, bevel-tipped needle knapped from tubular crystal or rolled from native copper. They do not eat the meat yet. First, they uncoil a muscular, retractable proboscis from behind their sternal plates, lock it hermetically over the butt of the needle, and draw out the three or four ounces of heavy, viscous, amber-colored solvent that served as the prey’s blood. Not a single micro-drop touches the air. Only when the husk is pumped dry do they crack the brittle shell and grind the desiccated, sulfur-rich organs between external mandibles set low in their chests, swallowing the dry powder in slow, peristaltic waves.

Oil is everything. Oil is blood, hydraulic fluid, memory, and soul.

Because they cannot grow in a continuous, soft way, they must molt four or five times over a lifespan that—at this glacial metabolic crawl—stretches easily past seven hundred years. This is the purpose of the slate lean-tos. You wondered why the domes have no doors, why the dry-stone courses are fitted so tightly a knife blade cannot slip between them. They are not houses; they are kilns of stillness, built around a single occupant from the *inside*.

When an individual’s shell grows too cramped, or too scored by centuries of brushing against mica blades, it gathers a mound of dry, powdered grazer-flesh and seeks a junction of giant crystals. With one or two kin passing flat slabs of cleaved slate down to it, it stacks a dome over itself, fitting the final keystones from within until it is sealed in absolute, hermetic isolation, safe from the millions of opportunistic scavengers waiting inches away on the ledges outside. There, in a darkness even purer than the vault’s, it splits down the back. For a year, or two, while its exposed, damp-oily new skin hardens into mineral horn, it lies utterly defenseless. When it is hard again, it does not dismantle the whole shelter; it carefully levers out three stones at the base, crawls backward into the world, and leaves the rest of the cairn standing. Some of those intact domes you pass are not abandoned; there is someone inside right now, halfway through their second year of hardening, listening to your footsteps crunch in the rock flour outside. 

Others are tombs, sealed from the *outside*, holding an elder who grew too brittle to split their shell one last time—though only after their kin gathered around them with copper needles to draw their ancestral oil into their own bodies, ensuring the precious fluid stays in the walk of the living.

And their young? Nothing so wasteful as an egg left in the open, or a soft infant losing vapor to the air. A parent carries a single larva internally, budding off a portion of its own solvent reserve until the offspring is the size of a clenched fist and already encased in flexible flint-grey mail. Birth is a dry, mechanical unlatching of a ventral brood-plate; the miniature creature drops onto the obsidian chips of a flaking floor, immediately freezes for twelve hours to let its joints set, and then reaches out a hair-fine antenna to touch its parent’s shin.

This touch is how they speak. 

Out in the open, they never make a sound if they can help it; noise draws the blind things that hunt by the *tick* of a slipping claw. Yet they have a vast, topographical language. Watch two of them meet on a narrow, braided-silk bridge spanning a black crevasse. Neither flashes a light; neither clicks a mandible. They halt face-to-face, raise their sweeping, glassy brow-filaments, and cross them. 

Through subtle modulations of static electricity—generated by rubbing dry silica pads together deep inside their thoraxes—and microscopic, high-frequency taps along the crossed filaments, they converse in complete silence. They trade maps. Their minds are almost entirely spatial, immense three-dimensional archives of overhangs, loose scree, piezoelectric glow-points, spider-curtains, and the exact friction-balance of ten thousand quartz markers stretching hundreds of miles through the vertical dark. Those three-shard quartz tripods balanced tip-to-tip on prominent boulders are not mere trail blazes; they are readable. To a four-thumbed hand passing lightly over them without knocking them down, the exact size of the shards, the angle of their cleave, and the specific mineral impurities on their faces spell out sentences left centuries ago: *Good slate fifty yards down-chimney. Spider-silk weak to the left. Here we tapped an elder dry; three needles’ worth, rich with iron. Walk flat.*

Occasionally—very rarely, perhaps once in a human lifetime in any given cubic mile of the labyrinth—something happens that requires them to speak across distance. A massive ceiling block shifts, threatening to shear a quartz pillar; or a nomadic pack of large, scissor-jawed centipedes pushes into a farmed thicket.

Then, a maker reaches up and plucks one of the flint chimes hanging outside a shelter, not by accident, but with a calibrated flick of a horn claw. 

*Tink. Tink-tink.*

Fifty paces away, in the pitch dark, perched upside down beneath a basalt overhang among forty motionless crab-scavengers, another figure registers the crisp, high ring before the millions of stone angles swallow it. It waits ten seconds. It plucks a single strand of a nearby spider-guywire it tuned thirty years prior. A dry, metallic *thrum* answers through the rock itself. Farther up the chimney, a third taps an obsidian flake twice against a selenite needle. Within five minutes, across half a mile of impassable, razor-edged vertical maze, twenty hidden figures have triangulated the disturbance, coordinated a response, and begun to unlatch their limbs—moving through the crowded, breathless dark like shadows made of slate, converging at a blistering top speed of forty yards an hour.

If you were down there right now, standing in a drift of grey rock flour with your lamp turned off to save the battery, feeling the black wool of the thirty-four-degree air pressing uniformly against your eyeballs and your skin, you would hear the normal ambient world: a claw shifting on glass to your left, mandibles rasping iron-crust above your head. 

You would not know that four feet to your right, standing knee-deep in struck obsidian chips beside a three-stone marker, one of them has had its six-foot sensory filament hovering a quarter-inch from your carotid artery for the last twenty minutes. It is feeling the frantic, terrifying *wetness* of your breath, the roaring, wasteful furnace of your thirty-seven-degree skin, and the rhythmic, hydraulic hammering of the incredible, impossible gallons of liquid sloshing carelessly inside your soft hide—wondering, with the slow, cold geometry of stone, what kind of leaking, burning monster you are, and how long it will take you to dry out and stop moving so you can be properly tapped.
