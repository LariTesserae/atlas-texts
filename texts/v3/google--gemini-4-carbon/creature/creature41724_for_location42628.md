If you stare long enough at a mile-wide junction where six railway-thick drive-shafts converge in a nest of screaming bevel gears, you will eventually realize that some of the dark, grey-black smears around the collar housings are not graphite paste. They are moving against the spin.

They look at first like flat, articulated ironwood splinters, or perhaps scraps of blown leather pinned to the bough by the hurricane. Then one detaches its trailing edge, swings across a six-foot gap of empty, four-mile-deep air on a braided tether, and clamps onto a reciprocating rocker arm with a dry *snick* that vanishes instantly into the roar of the continent.

You might call them Joiners, though they have no mouths with which to name themselves, and no ears that have not long ago calcified into solid bone to save their brains from being liquefied by the noise. 

To live in the Great Weave—not merely to scramble across it like the wicker-hounds or ride its exhaust like the shard-wings, but to *inhabit* it with deliberation—requires surrendering everything soft, wet, and hesitant. A Joiner is built on the logic of a rigging-block and a box-kite. Standing upright—which they rarely do, preferring to crouch flat in the two-inch boundary layer of slower air hugging the smooth ironwood—a mature individual is perhaps five feet long, wide through the chest, and impossibly thin from front to back. Their hides are overlapping plates of horn-dry keratin, polished to a matte pewter sheen by constant abrasion against airborne wood-flour, beveled at the edges so a ninety-mile-an-hour crosswind presses them *down* onto the timber rather than peeling them off. 

They have no eyes as wet worlds understand them. Moisture on an exposed eyeball would freeze or sublimate in seconds, and grit blasted from a rupturing spore-pipe would sand a cornea opaque in an afternoon. Instead, across the swept-back crest of a Joiner’s wedge-shaped skull runs a curved band of solid, amber-colored crystal—faceted silica salvaged by their distant evolutionary ancestors from the last pulverized memories of the planet’s bedrock. Behind these dry, immovable lenses, they see the blinding, chalk-white world in razor-sharp, polarized monochrome, effortlessly picking out the faint heat-shimmer of an over-stressed bearing half a mile down into the violet shadow-vaults.

Watch a pair of them working a main arterial truss. 

They do not walk; they *index*. Every limb ends not in fingers or toes, but in opposing, spring-loaded calipers lined with replaceable pads of cross-grained cork-callus. When a Joiner moves along a vibrating beam, at least two limbs are always locked in a dead-man’s mechanical clamp. Even if struck dead by a flying shard of shattered turbine blade, a Joiner will hang from its last handhold for decades, slowly sanded down to white dust by the wind until nothing remains but the locked calipers themselves.

Between the two individuals stretches a flat, thumb-wide ribbon of braided tendon—not grown from their own bodies, though it looks much the same, but harvested from the taut musical fibers of the forest and spliced into their harness-ribs. They are communicating. Because the air belongs entirely to the catastrophic pipe-organ scream of the flora, sound is useless to them; the air is a solid wall of noise. Instead, a Joiner talks through *knock* and *tension*. 

One shifts its weight, planting its sternal keel—a heavy bone plate down the center of its chest—flat against the shuddering wood of the bough. With a stubby, hammer-headed forearm, it strikes a rapid, syncopated triplet against the grain, right between two giant *clack-boom* beats of a nearby drop-forge cam. Half a second later, eighty yards down the curving rib, its partner feels the crisp, high-frequency transient cut cleanly through the sloppy bass rumble of the machinery, reads the tug on the safety-ribbon, and leans out over four vertical miles of sunlit nothing.

What is inside them is as dry as what surrounds them. If you cut a Joiner open—though you would need a hacksaw—nothing would bleed. Their biology abandoned water ages ago in favor of heavy, non-volatile, silicon-chained oils and dense, waxy lipids stolen from the forest’s own pneumatic fuel-lines. Their hearts do not beat with a lub-dub; they cycle with the stiff, precise *chuff* of a grease-gun, forcing thick, amber-colored fluid through braided vessels at immense pressure. They do not breathe air into wet lungs to trade gases across a damp membrane. Along their flanks sit rows of slotted gill-cowls facing backward into their own slipstream. High-velocity wind blasts in beneath their collar-plates, swirls through dry cyclone-chambers in their ribs to centrifuge out the abrasive wood-dust, and passes over miles of electrostatic, papery pleura before shooting out the vents at their hips. When a Joiner works hard, its exhaust vents puff faint, rhythmic wisps of grey mineral talc—spent waste-ash, dry as chalk.

For a long time, if you watched them, you might think they were merely exquisite parasites, clever ticks on the hide of a continental engine. 

You see them tap the pressurized nutrient-pipes, certainly. They carry heavy, bone-handled augers grown from the shed horn of teardrop crawlers, wedging themselves into the screaming crotch of a truss to bore neat, tiny bleed-valves into the arterial wood. Instead of prying a junction open and risking decapitation by an automated slammer-valve like the wicker-hounds, a Joiner threads a hollow quill into the high-pressure line, letting it hiss softly into a woven silk filter-bag until it collects a brick-hard cake of yellow lipid-powder and carbon-flour. They eat by unhinging a narrow ventral slot beneath their chins and slotting the dry fuel-cake directly into a muscular gizzard filled with granite grit—the most precious heirloom a family possesses, passed down from gizzard to gizzard for ten thousand generations, because there is no more stone left in the world to swallow.

You see their rookeries, too, though you have to know how to read the shadows down in the second mile of depth to spot them. They do not build walls; walls catch the wind, and anything that catches the wind without moving is torn loose and cast into the lower dark. Instead, inside the giant, hollow hubs of the slowest-turning great wheels—wooden drums sixty feet across that rotate once every four minutes to winch entire ridges into the shifting jet stream—the Joiners hang gimballed cradles of woven vine-tendon. Weighted with heavy knots of ironwood at the bottom, these latticework spheres stay dead-level as the vast wooden room turns endlessly around them, smeared in slick black graphite so the wooden axles of their home turn without a whisper. Inside these swinging, twilight cages, their young hatch from leathery, six-sided cases wedged into warm friction-grooves, emerging already equipped with locking calipers and crystal eyes, instinctively clamping onto the woven walls the moment their shells split.

It is only when something goes wrong in the Weave that you realize what the Joiners actually are.

Three miles down-wind from the rookery, a main drive-shaft—a twisted column of ironwood forty feet thick, transmitting the combined scream of two hundred ridge-top Pinwheels—begins to wobble. A century of cross-gales has eroded its lower collar-socket by four inches. The shaft is hammering against its housing. Left alone, the forest’s blind, evolutionary clockwork has only brutal answers for this: the friction will spike, a score of ash-bladders will burst in a blinding grey cloud, the wood will char, the shaft will shatter like a dropped glass rod under ten million foot-pounds of torque, and the flailing, miles-long end will whip sideways through the latticework, smashing arches, rupturing spore-mains, and dropping three square miles of sunlit ecosystem down into the crushing, lightless dust-sump at the bottom of the world. Eventually, over five hundred years, the forest would regrow the span. The forest does not care.

The Joiners care. That drive-shaft powers the cam-banks that pump lipid-spores past their rookery hub.

Within twenty minutes of the first irregular shudder travelling down the grain, forty of them have gathered along the bucking, shrieking housing. They come skimming down the tension-cables like flat stones skipped across water, brake-plates flared on their backs, shedding seventy miles an hour of airspeed in a shower of grey sparks just inches before colliding with the collar. 

There is no leader shouting orders; there is only the sudden, furious geometry of forty minds reading the exact same vibration through their chest-keels. 

Working inches from a forty-foot blurred cylinder of spinning ironwood whose surface velocity would instantly vaporize them into red wax and dust-motes, they move with terrifying, insectile calm. Half a dozen anchor themselves above the bucking joint and unspool heavy, braided tendon-lines, tossing weighted grapples *through* the gaps in a nearby spoked wheel—timing the throw to the millisecond—to catch a rising reciprocating beam. As the beam slams upward on its forest-driven stroke, the Joiners lock their ratchets, stealing ten tons of lift from the continent’s own muscle to haul a massive, curved shim of seasoned deadwood up out of the violet abyss below.

Others are down at the lip of the smoking socket, completely invisible inside pulsing clouds of black graphite dust and white fire-ash coughing from triggered heat-galls. Using pry-bars of fire-hardened horn, they wait for the monstrous, rhythmic *bounce* of the failing shaft. 

*Thump-SCREAM. Thump-SCREAM.*

At the exact zenith of a four-inch bounce—a window of perhaps a tenth of a second—eight Joiners kick the heavy ironwood shim into the gap. The forty-foot shaft slams back down. Instead of shattering the housing, it seats itself with a bone-jarring *thud* onto the fresh, curved cradle. Instantly, three younger, smaller individuals dart beneath the spinning monster, slashing open harvested grease-galls they carry slung across their backs, smearing slick, silver-black graphite paste directly onto the screaming interface so the fresh wood catches polish instead of fire.

The deadly, ragged wobble smooths out. The shriek drops two octaves back into the deep, healthy, chest-rattling hum of the planetary engine. 

For a long moment, the forty Joiners stay clamped around the mended collar, sternal keels pressed flat against the warm wood, feeling the restored trueness of the spin run clean and straight through their dry bones. They do not celebrate; they have no faces to smile with, and the gale is already tearing away the ash of their work, leaving everything chalk-white and silver again under the freezing cobalt sky. 

One of them taps a sharp, double-beat on the housing with its forearm. *True. True.*

Then, one by one, they unlock their calipers, tilt their flat, pewter bodies edge-on into the eighty-knot updraft, deploy stiff leather membranes between their ribs, and drop sideways off the bough—falling a thousand feet into the brilliant, roaring latticework before catching a cross-cable with a dry *snick*, already listening through their feet for the next bearing five miles away that needs grease.

Down in the deepest vaults they rarely go, though they know what lies four miles beneath the bright ridges, down where the lattice grows so dense the sunlight dies into violet, then slate, then absolute, choking pitch. Everything the wind sands off the upper world falls down there eventually. Millions of tons of dry wood-flour, shattered Pinwheel blades, spent mineral ash, and the dry husks of shard-wings drift down into the windless, lightless sumps, forming treacherous, shifting dunes a thousand feet deep where huge, blind, auger-snouted worms swim through the powder, digesting the fallen dust back into the deep, ancient root-pumps that still churn in the dark. 

When a Joiner grows very old—when its crystal eye-band is finally frosted milky white by decades of grit, and its joints stiffen as its internal oils polymerize into hard amber, and the precious granite pebbles in its gizzard have worn too smooth to grind its food—it does not wait to die locked to a sunny beam. First, it climbs back to the swinging cradle inside the great hub and coughs up its tiny, smooth-worn stones into the waiting calipers of its grandchildren. 

Then it crawls out onto an open span where the sky is widest, faces the blinding white horizon where ten thousand louvers are slamming in the sun, unlocks all four limbs at once, and lets the continent blow it away.
