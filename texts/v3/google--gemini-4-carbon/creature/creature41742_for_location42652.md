Wait another ten minutes in that slate-colored hush, watching the south wall where the clay meets a stepped overhang of the split basalt, and a patch of the architecture detaches itself from the damp mud and shifts its weight.

She has been sitting with her shoulder blades pressed flat into a wide concavity she smoothed for herself weeks ago while the lower courses were still soft as tallow. When she leans forward, her back pulls free without a sucking sound—she peels away at an angle, slow as the vine snake, leaving behind the faint, glossy negative of her spine and ribs beside the crescent palm-prints of the masons who raised the shaft.

She is human, though shaped for an interior life that belongs neither entirely to the sun-baked terraces overhead nor to the blind water table beneath. She is small-boned and compact, built to fold easily into acute angles, perhaps four and a half feet tall if she stood fully upright—which she rarely does here, where the basalt ledges slice at careless shins and equilibrium is better kept low. Her skin is the deep, warm umber of river silt, matte where it is dry over her collarbones and shoulders, dusted across the knees, shins, and forearms with the pale gray rock-flour of the floor. In the twice-reflected twilight, that mineral powder gives her limbs a velvety, ghostly bloom, like the skin of a dark plum.

She wears no woven cloth. Fiber rots in eighty-eight-degree stillness, sours with mold within days, and snags fatally on freshly fractured stone. Around her hips she wears only a supple apron cut from a cured, oil-rubbed calf-size leaf—one of the older aroid blades harvested before it yellowed, boiled in bark-tannin somewhere topside until it turned to silent green leather, tied at her flank with braided reed-pith. Across her left hip hangs a flattened gourd with its neck plugged by rolled sedge, and tucked behind her ear is a stylus whittled from fire-hardened palm wood, six inches long and polished slick by skin oils.

Look at her hands and feet first; they explain why she is down here while the thousands above stay on the roofs.

The soles of her feet are extraordinary—thickened from childhood into broad, pale-gray pads of dense horn, dry enough not to slip on wet clay yet tough enough to bridge the razor ninety-degree arrises of raw basalt without parting. When she steps down from her perch onto a fractured block, she does not slide her foot; she places it straight down like a stamp, toes splaying wide to test the angle before committing her weight. There isn't a single nick or scar on her ankles. Her fingers are long, flat-tipped, and stained almost black around the nails from iron-rich mud, except for the outer edges of both thumbs, which carry smooth, deliberate calluses from testing stone planes and turning clay coils.

Her eyes are very large, dark almost to the edge of the sclera, the pupils dilated wide in the permanent amber-slate gloom. She blinks seldom. Like the frogs in the leaf axil and the marsupial on the shelf, she possesses the stillness of creatures that hunt or work by peripheral motion; she does not turn her head to look at the bronze vine snake drinking from the stone six feet away, though her gaze registers the dip of its neck. The marsupial up on the raw ledge doesn't even pause in its turning of stone flakes as she rises. To the small inhabitants of the shaft, she is simply the largest, warmest piece of the mud wall—a slow-moving thermal mass that occasionally alters the geometry of their hunting ground and leaves edible scraps of marrow-paste near the ant trails as toll.

She is one of the *Sump-Keepers*—or, in the older lowland gesture-speech used down in the vents where voices would carry straight up the shafts into twenty sleeping households, a *Wall-Listener*.

A city built of wet, unreinforced river mud directly across a live tectonic split cannot simply be stacked and forgotten. Up in the sun and wind, the outer terraces bake fast, shrinking and cracking like dry lakebed while their deep foundations, forty feet below in the humid dark, remain plastic and heavy as dough. If a lower wall dries too quickly on one face, the whole four-story mass above tilts; if water rising from the fissure softens the basal courses unevenly, a seam shears silently and drops three families into the earth. And if the brand-new bedrock shifts by even the breadth of a fingernail along its glassy fault, someone must know before the reed eaves begin to groan.

She steps across the empty stone toward the north wall, moving in a low, fluid crouch that keeps her center of gravity six inches above her heels. Not a breath of displaced air disturbs the hanging midges. Reaching the snow-white apron of fresh travertine at the base of the wall, she kneels—setting her knees down carefully onto two smooth river-clay pads she left curing on the basalt three days ago, spare cushions scattered around the floor like stepping-stones so she never has to kneel on flint-sharp corners.

She leans in until her cheek is an inch from the paper-thin calcium crust.

For two full minutes she does nothing at all. Her breathing slows until her ribs barely move, matching the stagnant rhythm of the shaft. She is feeling the radiant temperature of the seep with the thin skin beneath her eye, smelling the rising vapor from the hairline seam. Good water—settled ground-table vapor—smells solely of wet iron, chalk, and the green peppery sweat of the giant aroids. Bad water, water squeezed upward from deeper down before a fresh shudder in the plain, carries a faint, sharp tang of bruised flint and rotten marsh-gas, and comes up three degrees hotter.

Satisfied by the cool, chalky breath of the seam, she draws the palm-wood stylus from behind her ear and turns twenty inches to her left, where the dark clay wall meets a glassy basalt step.

Pressed across that joint is a ribbon of pure, fine-washed slip—pale yellow clay brought down from a hillside pit miles away, smoothed across the boundary of stone and mud like a wax seal on a jar, three fingers wide and thin as an eggshell. There are a dozen such yellow ribbons bridging rock-to-wall and wall-to-wall around the enclosure. They are her gauges. In this motionless air, yellow slip dries to a brittle satin skin in forty hours; if the bedrock settles a hairsbreadth, or the curing wall creeps inward under the weight of the town's upper tiers, the pale ribbon parts in a microscopic threadlong crack long before a human eye could see the dark wall bulge.

This ribbon is whole. With the tip of her wooden stylus, she makes a tiny semicircular prick at its right-hand margin—today's mark, joining eleven identical crescent punctures marching neatly from left to right. No dust falls when she pierces it.

Then she turns her attention to the dampness of the east wall itself.

High above, three geckos scatter silently sideward—not fleeing in panic, just parting like water around a wading heron—as she reaches up as high as her compact frame allows and presses the flat of her inner wrist against the hand-coiled tiers. Here, the builders laid the mud slightly too fat; moisture rising from the fern-fissure has lingered in the deep crescent hollows of their palm-prints, threatening to turn the clay sour and slumped rather than leather-hard.

She unstoppers the flat gourd at her hip. Inside is dry, finely sifted wood-ash mixed with roasted, powdered freshwater mussel shell—white, alkaline, hungry for water. Dipping two fingers in, she pats a ghosts-breath of pale powder directly into the wettest grooves of the builders' handprints, rubbing it gently in circular motions with the ball of her thumb until the slickness turns to a stiff, velvety matte. Where the clay is too dry near a warm thermal draft rising toward the second-story vents, she does the opposite: she walks over to the nearest giant aroid, cups her palm beneath the downward-pointing drip-tip of a calfskin-sized leaf, waits patiently while four fat silver beads slide down the petiole groove and merge into a cool pool in her hand, and carries that silent spoonful of water back to anoint the parched mud.

She tends the room the way an embalmer tends a body, or a potter tends a kiln from the inside. Every square foot of those four leaning walls is known to her by moisture, resistance, and smell. In truth, the builders who slapped these heavy mud balls together two months ago only rough-hewed the shaft; she is the one finishing it, ounce by ounce, guiding forty tons of wet earth through its dangerous infancy into stone-hard permanence without letting it split or rot.

Halfway through her circuit, she pauses beside the solitary wax-leaved vine on the west wall.

One of its pale adventitious roots has wandered out of the shallow palm-prints and found a microscopic cooling-check in the clay, pushing its succulent white tip half an inch deep into the structural mass. Left alone in this greenhouse vigor, that root would swell to the thickness of a thumb by next moon, wedging the uncured courses apart as surely as an iron chisel.

She doesn't rip it free. Tearing a vine here would bruise the stem and spill sharp green sap that brings swarms of ferment-flies down from the eaves. Instead, using the flat bevel of her wooden stylus, she gently pries the root-tip out of the pore, seals the tiny hole behind it with a pinch of clay scraped from the floor-joint, and tucks the living root sideways under a small, damp dab of fresh mud three inches over on solid wall—steering the plant's hunger upward toward the light-gap while keeping its fingers out of the mortarless seams. As her shoulder brushes a broad wax leaf, the bronze vine snake coiled two nodes higher freezes, flattens its head slightly, tastes the warm salt of her collarbone with two flicks of a black tongue, and resumes its slow descent toward the tree frogs. She neither flinches nor interferes. The frogs keep the midges down; the snake keeps the frogs from crowding the leaf-axils and snapping the stalks with their weight; the ants clean up whatever misses its footing on the razor basalt. Balance down here requires very few actors, provided none of them gets clumsy.

When the amber-gray light overhead cools fully into late-afternoon slate and the fresh bloom of micro-droplets mists the black rock faces, her work in this pocket is nearly done.

Before she climbs, she walks to the edge of the central fissure where the young tree-fern uncurls its fuzzy brown fiddleheads in the rising warm vapor. She squats on her tough gray heels at the very lip of the split, looking down into the black throat of the earth where the water table breathes just out of sight. From a pouch folded into her leaf-leather apron, she takes a single dried clay pellet the size of a pea—a tiny hollow rattle baked hard in a surface fire, holding one grain of basalt grit inside. She doesn't drop it into the water. She wedges it delicately between two opposing, razor-sharp jaws of the split bedrock twelve inches down in the crack, pinching it just tight enough that friction holds it suspended over emptiness.

There are fifty such baked-clay peas wedged at skin-splitting tension all along the new fissure underneath the town. If the plain groans in the night—if the young fault widens by half a millimeter while the settlement sleeps—the stone jaws will part, the hollow pea will drop onto the resonant basalt shelf below with a single, sharp *tick*, and if the fault pinches inward instead, the brittle shell will crush with a crisp *snap* audible fifty feet up the clay-lined shaft to whoever sits watch on the reed-eave parapet.

Nothing snaps. Nothing drops. Only the silver condensation rims swell along the flinty ledges around her bare toes.

To leave, she doesn't call down a rope or haul out a ladder; wooden poles knock chips off fresh rock and gouge soft walls. In the northeast corner, where two inward-leaning clay faces meet at a tight eighty-degree angle, the builders left a staggered chimney of deep, rounded thumb-and-heel pockets molded smoothly into the mud as they worked upward, hardened early with shell-lime so they wouldn't deform.

She wipes the gray rock-flour from her palms onto her thighs, plugs her gourd tight, and steps straight up into the corner.

Bridging the angle with wide, sure feet and flat palms, moving with the unhurried, three-point certainty of the whip spiders below, she climbs forty feet of vertical twilight without dislodging a single crumb of dry earth. Halfway up, passing one of the round ventilation holes two stories above the floor, a faint, dry rumor of the upper world touches her skin for the first time in hours—the smell of roasting tubers, river wind, hearth-smoke, and the low, blurred murmur of voices passing along an alley just beyond the two-foot-thick mud shell. Her wide dark pupils contract by half in the marginally brighter grayness.

Near the bundled reed eaves at the top, where a narrow crawl-slot opens onto a shadowed maintenance catwalk beneath the crowded house-terraces, another figure is already waiting in a crouch—an older boy with the same thick, horn-soled feet, the same pale dust on his shins, a fresh gourd of shell-ash slung at his hip for the night watch, when the cooling desert sky above will draw faster drafts up the warm chimney of the shaft.

They do not speak as they pass in the slot beneath the reeds. He extends two fingers and touches the back of her wrist—asking the state of the bottom. She taps his knuckles twice, slow and flat—*steady, damp, curing true*—and holds up three fingers angled downward to tell him she reset the vine root on the west face and marked the north gauge.

He slips headfirst over the lip and vanishes silently down the corner pockets into the warm, damp stillness below, descending into the company of geckos, travertine, and silent frogs, while she crawls out into the dry evening breeze of the settlement, blinking slowly against the vast, noisy, messy glare of the open sky.
