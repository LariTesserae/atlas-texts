You usually find the signs of them before you catch them moving: a brass valve polished mirror-bright by the dry pad of a thumb, where everything else for ten miles is matte with chalk-dust; a neat, deliberate stack of stripped hex-nuts balanced on a vibrating housing, sorted by thread-pitch, untouched by the wind. 

Then, fifty yards down a maintenance trench, a section of pale ironwood bark and braided copper wire stands up, shakes a quart of fine rust from the creases of its elbows, and walks sideways through a gap between two pounding stamping-rams without breaking stride.

They are called—if there were anyone left with wet vocal cords to shape the vowels—something like the *Fibrils*, or the *Tenders*, though in truth they have the aspect of dry-rotted wickerwork that has learned to read a schematic. Up close, a single individual stands roughly the height of a tall man, though its proportions belong to an adjustable drafting lamp. They have no skin, because skin is a bag meant to keep water in, and water here is an unimaginable vulgarity. Instead, they are composite: flat, laminated strips of pale heartwood and vulcanized fiber-board bound together by dry, tense ligaments of braided zinc-wire and cured phloem. Their joints are exposed, exquisite dry-ball-and-socket assemblies of polished silicate, lubricated continuously by a pinch of grey graphite dust shed from specialized glands in their collarbones. 

When one moves, it makes the sound of an old wooden folding-ruler being snapped open and shut, very fast, in another room.

They have no faces in the mammalian sense. Where a head should be, there is a flattened, shield-shaped cowl of layered mica and horn-like parchment, tilted slightly upward to deflect the relentless overhead Glare. Beneath the cowl’s eaves sit three or four wide, horizontal slits packed with dry, hexagonal facets of smoked quartz. They do not blink; instead, tiny stiff brushes of pale bristle sweep sideways across the quartz every few minutes, flicking away accumulated chaff with a faint *tick-tick*. They have no mouths, no lungs, no gut. To breathe air is to expect oxygen to dissolve in blood, and nothing dissolves here. 

Instead, they eat torque and static.

Watch one at rest, and you see how they feed. A Tender will walk up to a massive, vine-choked lathe that has been spinning a fossilized crankshaft for three centuries. It will reach out with a long, four-thumbed hand—two thumbs on either side of a flat, caliper-like palm—and press its bare, calloused wrist directly against the spinning iron collar. It locks its knees into the twenty-foot duff, braces its laminated spine, and simply holds on. Friction builds instantly. Smoke does not rise—the wood of their bodies is far too mineralized to char—but the piezo-crystalline plates layered deep within their forearms drink the ferocious mechanical vibration and the screaming static charge stripped from the spinning metal. Across the creature’s back, a ridge of flat, tarnished-brass fins rises like the hackles of a hound, trembling in sympathy with the machine, bleeding off excess heat into the crisp, cool air while dry salt-ions snap back and forth through their fibrous nerves, recharging them. For twenty minutes the creature stands frozen, buzzing violently in your teeth if you stand within ten feet of it, completely silent to the ear. Then it steps back, flexes its fingers, and goes back to work.

What that work *is* takes a long time to understand, because it serves neither the original intent of the factory nor the blind appetite of the forest, yet prevents either from choking the other to death.

They are creatures of maintenance without memory. They carry tools that are half-grown out of their own forearms and half-scavenged from the duff: long, balanced hooks of spring-steel wrapped in grey twine; feeler-gauges carved from petrified bamboo; heavy, dead-blow mallets packed with loose iron shot. A Tender will spend six hours crouching beside a vine-driven conveyor belt, carefully prying out a wedged, calcified gourd that is causing a drive-chain to skip a tooth every forty revolutions. Once the gourd is free, the Tender does not throw it away; it walks half a mile through the silver-briars to a dry-mill hopper and drops it in, dusting its hands together with a dry *scritch*. Then it returns to the vine that was straining against the jammed chain and uses a sharp, crescent-shaped thumb-claw to prune back a cluster of parasitic salt-galls from the plant’s bark, ensuring the creeper doesn’t snap under the resumed load. 

They do not worship the machines, and they do not cultivate the plants for harvest. Rather, they seem to possess an unbearable, bone-deep allergy to *seizure*. In a world that runs entirely on friction, static, and dry tension, a jammed gear or a throttled root-stock creates a dead spot—a pocket where vibration stops and electrostatic charge pools until it threatens to arc hot enough to start a smolder in the twenty-foot duff. A fire in this dry, continental tinderbox, fanned by the league-wide breathing-gales, would burn from horizon to horizon without smoke or roar, reducing the basin to white ash under the shadowless Glare. So the Tenders roam, driven by an ancient, inherited itch in their quartz-crystal ears, hunting down the faint, discordant shudders in the floor that mean a bearing has run dry of talc or a strangler-fig has pulled a gantry out of true.

Yet the most unsettling thing about them is not their industry, but how they navigate the broken geometry of the basin.

A visitor with wet eyes looks down a trench, sees parallax tear in half, and feels their mind revolt as a distant harvester slides behind a nearby stalk of bamboo. The Tenders do not suffer from this, because they do not believe in distance; they only believe in *sequence* and *register*.

In those uncanny pockets where the landscape stutters—where eight hundred square yards repeat the exact same cracked brass pipe and six-leafed ivy forty times over—you will often find what looks like a crowd of forty Tenders working in uncanny, clockwork unison. Step into the pocket, and you realize with a cold prickle at the base of your skull that there is only *one* of them. 

They have adapted to the spatial slips the way a mountain goat adapts to sheer shale. A solitary Tender will deliberately step sideways into a modular repeat, splitting its own presence across forty adjacent twelve-foot cells at once, using the broken space as a force-multiplier. Forty identical wicker-and-brass figures will lean down, forty sets of four-thumbed hands will grip forty identical corroded valves, and with a single, shared heave, the creature leverages its own multiplied weight to break loose a rusted main-stem that ten of its kind could never budge in normal space. If you toss a stripped bolt at the third figure from the left, it will catch it without looking—and suddenly thirty-nine other bolts, Plucked from nowhere or everywhere in the stuttered light, are held in thirty-nine identical palms, until the Tender steps backward out of the spatial fault, collapsing smoothly back into a single, gaunt figure holding one rusty bolt, which it promptly drops into its woven-wire hip-basket.

Because parallax means nothing to them, they travel the middle distances by riding the visual errors. You might see two of them standing atop a low transformer housing a quarter-mile away, tiny and bleached against the chalk-green scrub. They will wait until a massive, wind-blown sheet of silver chaff sweeps between you and them, flattening the depth of field under the shadowless ceiling. When the chaff clears three seconds later, they are standing six feet in front of you—not having run, not having leaped, simply having stepped from a far layer of the flat white light onto a near one while the seam between the two was obscured. 

Up that close, you can smell them: the sharp, clean tang of ozone, old dry paper, pulverized slate, and warm brass.

They are largely solitary, crossing paths in the endless thickets with minimal ceremony, yet they are not wholly isolated. Because the air kills low-frequency sound and the Glare washes out visual signals past a hundred yards, they speak through the floor and the canopy. A Tender will pause, unclip a heavy iron tuning-bar from its ribs, and strike it hard against an exposed root-beam. You hear nothing—just the tiny *tink* of metal touching metal—but through the soles of your boots comes a complex, syncopated packet of tremors, traveling miles through the fused iron-and-wood crust at the speed of sound in steel. Half a minute later, an answer shudders back up through your ankles from somewhere beyond the false horizon.

And when a silent, violet-white sheet of static prepares to discharge across the canopy, they gather—if two or three happen to be within a league of one another. Just before the air ionizes, they climb up into the high, bleached rafters of the ancient cranes, hooking their wire-wrapped toes around copper bus-bars alongside the fluttering flocks of blueprint-moths. As the multi-mile sheet of purple lightning washes silently through the leaves, snapping open a billion seed-pods at once, the Tenders throw their four-thumbed arms wide. Their brass back-fins flare, rimmed in pale blue St. Elmo’s fire. For five dazzling seconds, caught in the storm of rising silver chaff, they twitch and dance in rigid, ecstatic semaphore—shedding old, brittle scales of dead wood, annealing the micro-fractures in their silicate joints, and trading long, rapid-fire bursts of electrostatic memory directly from cowl to cowl across the charged air.

They do not die in the way wet things die; they do not rot, and nothing eats them, for there is nothing in them worth digesting that cannot be scraped more easily off an I-beam. Instead, over centuries of dry friction, a Tender simply sands itself down. Its joints grow loose despite the graphite; its laminated ribs wear thin as onion-skin from brushing through a million miles of silicate-bamboo; its quartz eyes turn milky and blind from the endless sandblasting of rust-devils.

Eventually, there comes a day when an old Tender walks up to a slow, heavy gear-train or a flexing cradle of ironwood roots, reaches in to clear a jam, and finds its worn-down body fits the gap better than any tool.

You find them all over the basin if you know how to look. At first, you think it is just an unusually intricate truss, or a bundle of pale roots that happened to twist into the shape of a ribcage around a leaking sulfur-chute. Then you see the four-thumbed hand, long ago mineralized and fused seamlessly into the bark of a hawser-vine, acting as a permanent tensioner-pulley. You see a curved mica cowl wedged neatly beneath a cam-shaft, worn into a smooth, saddle-backed bearing surface over decades of silent, heavy strokes, shedding a microscopic pinch of grey graphite with every pass of the iron lobe. 

The forest grows through their wicker ribs; the rust cements their feet into the root-plate; the machine keeps turning through the socket of their shoulder. Nothing gleams, nothing mourns, nothing makes a sound louder than a turned page. Fifty yards away, a younger figure—freshly woven in some deep, dry nursery-loom tucked beneath the twenty-foot duff, its brass fins still bright and unscratched—steps sideways out of a repeating patch of briars, checks the tension on the vine running over its predecessor’s collarbone, nods once with a faint *click* of dry wood, and walks on into the glare.
