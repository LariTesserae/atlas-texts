To see what lives here, you have to ask first where the dead things go.

Outside the chalk lip lies high, wind-scoured country. Dust blows across those uplands; dry grass-heads snap off in gales; chilled beetles, craneflies, and exhausted migrants are carried continually across the ridges. Yet none of their husks ever litter this forty-pace bowl. Nor do the plants leave untidy deadfall of their own. When one of the trunkless palms sheds an old, rubbery frond, or when the spent, translucent skin of a rooted bladder-fern sloughs away, no brown mulch collects in the corners of the pools. No film of rot ever clouds the water.

Even the emptiness of the sky directly overhead has a boundary to it. A high-country bird that angles too low across the chalk collar meets a sudden slackening of lift where the warm, wet air sits pooled in the stone. The basin is a cup not only of heat, but of heavy gas: the warm groundwater, sweating perpetually out of the limestone and laying down fresh travertine, breathes off a slow, invisible ballast of carbon dioxide as its minerals settle. The gas pools densest near the floor, warm and odorless beneath the green, vegetal dampness. It is why the solitary mosses and palms grow so swollen and extravagant, gorging on rich air without wind to dry them. It is also why no insect skates the pools. Any beetle or midge blown over the rim drops through the warm shear, takes two breaths above the water, folds its legs, and falls still onto a pan without kicking up a ripple.

If you watched that fallen speck of chitin from the rim, nothing would seem to happen to it for hours. By the next morning it would look slightly softer, pressed flat into the stone. By the third day there would be nothing left at all—not a wing-case, not a stain—only the bright, clear sheet of water sliding silently over pale rock.

To find the edge of what took it, look beside the giant moss mound where the cold-hammered wedge of meteoric copper sits in its shallow dish.

Where the verdigris leaches downward in a blue-green ribbon toward the roots of the yellow-leaved shrub, the pale rock immediately bordering the stain is genuinely bare—chalky, matte, slightly rough. About three fingers’ breadth out on either side of that poisonous copper streak, however, runs a hair-thin seam under the water. It casts no shadow in the bright, bounced light, and only betrays itself because the sliding film of water climbs half a millimeter over it, bending the light just enough to trace a faint, colorless hem pulled tautly back from the taint of the metal.

Once the eye catches that single retracted border, the whole floor of the basin shifts into focus.

Almost none of the submerged terraces are bare stone. Spread flat across the bottom of nearly every ankle-deep pan lies a continuous, hyaline mantle—colorless, utterly motionless, matching the refractive index of the warm, mineral-heavy water so closely that sunlight passes straight through it onto the white travertine beneath and bounces back unaltered. Were you to touch it, it would not feel slimy like pond-scum, nor yielding like jelly, but taut, warm, and resilient, like soaked parchment or very thin horn, anchored by millions of microscopic root-threads into the pores of the stone below.

These are the aprons of the sleepers, and they are the reason the basin has the clean, unhurried spacing of a gallery.

Follow any one of those invisible sheets out of the open pan toward the perimeter of the floor, where the weeping walls slump down into rounded, wax-like bolsters and shallow alcoves. Look closely among the pale shoulders of rock at the waterline. Most are solid limestone, dissolved and reglazed over millennia. Five or six of them—wedged into the warmest, wettest curves where the groundwater sweats thickest—are only stone in their foundations.

Their upper domes, each the size of a curled sheep or a drifted sack of meal, are dense, calcified tunic, crusted in fine white lime precipitated directly from the glaze of water sliding over them. They have no heads, no eyes, no shells that hinge or snap, and no muscles built for haste. Long before this chalk plateau was lifted into the cold sky—when the stone of the walls was still soft, warm sea-ooze under a shallow tropical reef—their ancestors lived among coral flats, half-buried in carbonate mud, spreading clear digestive veils across the tide-pools at slack water. As the seabed rose over epochs into frozen mountains, everything swift or fragile perished; only here, trapped inside a warm karst vent that slowly freshened from brine to sweet mineral spring, did a remnant settle into geological patience, trading every unneeded organ for longevity and lime.

In the bright, shadowless light of the sinkhole, only one feature distinguishes a sleeper’s body from the slumped travertine beside it: running horizontally across its pale flank, looking exactly like a natural dissolution-crack in melted wax, is a narrow slit-seam lined inside with motionless, pleated folds the color of skimmed milk.

For twenty or thirty minutes at a stretch, that seam stays nearly closed. Warm water holds very little air, and the sleepers need almost none, burning their fuel at the pace of lichen. Then, somewhere across the basin, condensation gathers at the tip of an overarching palm frond, swells until its surface tension fails, and falls.

*Thuck.*

A single circular wave travels lazily outward across the pans, carrying a minute charge of cooler, fresher air stirred down from above the gas-layer into the water film. As the ring reaches the wall and washes over the slumped shoulder, the horizontal seam parts by the breadth of a wheat-straw. Inside, the milky pleats flare softly, drink the passing ripple, and take ten minutes to ease shut again.

Nothing else moves—not at a speed an eye can register.

Yet on the clock of the ferns and the mosses, the sleepers are restless architects. Beneath their transparent aprons, their undersides are constantly busy with the chemistry of the stone. Where a pan grows too shallow, the sleeper breathes a mild carbonic acid downward through its anchor-threads, melting the travertine beneath its belly into soft paste; where water sheets away too quickly at a terrace edge, the margin of its apron precipitates dense, interlocking crystals of aragonite, raising the calcified lip of its pool by the thickness of a thumbnail every few seasons. The lily-pad terraces of the sinkhole floor are not accidents of geology. They are furniture, leveled and maintained over centuries so that every sleeper stays bathed in an unbroken, laminar skin of warm water.

When an apron intercepts a stunned highland moth, a blown seed, or a rotting fern-skin, there is no snap or ripple. Over half a day, the clear tissue beneath the speck softens, dimples, and folds up over it like warm tallow closing around a wick. Enzymes break down shell, fiber, and wing without releasing a bubble. Whatever the sleeper cannot use—dark, insoluble humates, woody lignin, coarse dust—is nudged along branching, hair-thin gutters *beneath* the apron and expelled neatly at the boundaries where two sleepers’ territories meet. Over centuries, those tidy middens have become the isolated pockets of rich black silt wedged in the stone.

Even the placement of the plants is negotiated with them. If a moss spore or a fern bulb tries to root in the middle of an open pan, the apron simply digests it, keeping its light-gathering floor unobstructed. Only in the neutral silt-pockets between aprons—or at the dry pedestals above the water—is vegetation permitted to stand. In return, the palms shade nothing for long and drop the heavy, periodic droplets that aerate the pans, while the bladder-ferns offer something stranger.

The sleepers have no eyes, but their pale skins and wide aprons are crowded with heat- and light-receptors, tuned to the slow wheel of the sun across the circle of sky above. As the melon-sized water bulbs of the bladder-ferns swell over months, hanging out over the pools, they act as liquid magnifying glasses. Every clear midday, wobbling, concentrated rings of warm light slide across the submerged aprons. The sleepers lean their metabolism into those bright coins, using the focused warmth to lay down their hardest, purest stone. As a fern’s bulb grows heavier and bows week by week closer to the floor, its focal ring tightens and heats on the apron below—warning the sleeper long in advance where the bulb will eventually touch down. If the sleeper wants no plant there, it thickens its digestive skin at the hot spot and consumes the bulb the week its skin dissolves. If the terrace edge needs shade or drip-water, the sleeper pulls its transparent hem quietly back an inch a month, leaving a bare cup of etched stone and black silt waiting for the root.

When a sleeper grows too broad for its pan—once in two or three human lifetimes—it does not breed or spawn. It simply builds a calcified ridge straight up through the middle of its own apron, walling itself slowly in two until each half sits in a separate dish of water, forgetting the other.

And once in a long while, something upright comes down over the chalk collar from the cold world outside.

Whoever visits this place understands its rules, though whether they think of the basin as housing silent animals, touchy spirits, or merely living rock scarcely matters; their manners are impeccable. They leave their boots, hides, and flaking trail-mud outside on the rim, descending barefoot so no sharp highland grit scratches the wet travertine. They never step into the middle of the clear pans—not since whatever distant ancestor first set a heel onto what looked like wet white rock, felt the warm, drum-taut skin yield slightly underneath, and saw the bruised spot turn chalky and rough for three years afterward. Instead, the visitor steps slowly from dry pedestal to black silt pocket, moving without haste so they do not pant down in the heavy, head-thickening air, staying an hour at most before climbing back into the wind.

They do not come to hunt or gather food. They come to ask the sleepers to finish their tools.

The copper wedge beside the moss mound is a fence-post. Set in its dish of water seasons ago, its narrow ribbon of verdigris holds the nearest sleeper’s apron politely at bay so the yellow-leaved shrub will not have its roots walled over in stone before its mineral-tinted leaves are ready for plucking.

And at the center of the floor, where the warm spring wells up without a ripple, the visitor used a smooth river-cobble to deepen a notch in the rim, coaxing the mineral water—and with it, the creeping, crystal-laying margin of the basin’s oldest and broadest apron—to sheet leftward across four scraped paddles of tough upland wood. They wedged those paddles directly beneath the bowing stalks of four bladder-ferns, right where the noon sun lenses through the swelling water-bulbs in bright, hot rings.

Stimulated by the concentrated warmth and irritated mildly by the rough, fibrous wood intruding into its pan, the great central sleeper has crept a glassy lobe entirely over the four spatulas. Season after season, trying to smooth this foreign splinter into part of its clean floor, it has sheathed the wood not in brittle, porous lime-scale, but in cross-woven, pearlescent layers of living aragonite—hard as jade, grainless, polished by water, binding tough plant-fiber core and stone skin into blades no hammer could ever flake without shattering.

Right now, the heavy fern-bulbs hang scarcely a hand’s breadth above the submerged paddles, their lensed rings of light tight and hot on the pearlescent swellings beneath the water. Soon—perhaps within the month, before the bulbs touch down—a shadow will cross the bright lip of the sinkhole. Bare feet will press once more into the warm black silt. A splash of green water dipped from the copper dish will be drizzled gently across the stems of the paddles; over half an hour, without haste or hurt, the invisible hem will draw itself back an inch from the bitter taste, letting four seamless stone tools be lifted cleanly from the pan before the basin settles back into its warm, bright, breathing quiet.
