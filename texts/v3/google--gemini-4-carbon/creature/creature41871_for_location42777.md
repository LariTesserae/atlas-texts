The maintenance door three miles off does not close behind a machine. A minute after the dark rectangle vanishes back into the glare of the pump-house wall, a figure steps out onto the perforated steel landing and begins the long walk down the elevated causeway toward the distillation towers.

Seen from a distance through the rising heat-shimmer, the town’s builders look narrow and strangely doubled, their dark reflections stretching out beneath them across the silver flats like stilts of their own. Up close, they are human, or began that way long enough ago that the basin has finished editing them. They are tall and spare, built with the attenuated proportions of people whose bodies have spent generations trying to shed heat into air that is already warm and thin. Their chests are deep and barrel-vaulted behind lean ribs to haul oxygen out of eight thousand feet of altitude, while their limbs are long, dry-muscled, and mapped with prominent surface veins that sit just beneath skin the color of cured saddle leather and dark teak. 

Against the doubled glare—sun above, mirror below—their faces have settled into a permanent, sculpted economy. Heavy brow ridges overhang eyes shielded behind double-tiered, dark-tinted glass goggles seated in frames of vulcanized rubber and boiled horn. Nobody goes out on the walkways bare-eyed; even a minute of looking down at the travertine shelves without smoked glass leaves crescent-shaped burns across the retinas. Most have lost the habit of blinking horizontally altogether, or rather, they blink rarely, their tear ducts producing a thick, lipid-heavy film that resists instant evaporation in the alkaline updrafts. Noticeable, too, is the paint: thick, greasy smears of pale zinc and ground rust-ochre worn not solely across the cheekbones, as snow-travelers wear it, but painted carefully along the *undersides* of the jaw, the chin, the nostrils, and the undersides of the earlobes, where the relentless golden bounce-light strikes hardest from the water underfoot.

They wear wide, flat-brimmed hats woven from spun basalt fiber, stiffened with ceramic glaze so they ring faintly if tapped with a fingernail, though no one taps them. Below the neck, their work clothes are loose, multi-layered wraps of unbleached, mineral-treated linen hung over light frames of split reed—garments that do not touch the skin except at the shoulders and waist, creating personal chimneys of shade around the torso so the rising heat off the walkways pulls a constant draft across their sweat. Their boots are knee-high, molded with thick wooden and cork-composite clogs at the sole to insulate against the hot steel grates, though when they step down off the causeways into the flooded flats themselves, they swap these for wide, snowshoe-like pattens of perforated aluminum that distribute their weight so they won't punch through a fragile travertine shelf into a scalding pocket beneath.

Two of them meet at a junction where three copper slurry viaducts cross beneath a monorail pylon. Neither makes a sound. 

In a valley where thin air swallows shouts and where the background hum of existence is a fragile, crystalline silence, the human throat has largely retired from outdoor work. Speaking aloud in the dry, alkaline heat wastes moisture and feels, to people raised inside this acoustic glasshouse, vaguely obscene—like throwing stones at a mirror. Their mouths stay closed, breathing slowly through noses lined with protective salve. Instead, as the two workers approach within twenty yards, their hands come clear of their linen wraps. 

Their fingers are very long, hardened at the nails, and banded from knuckle to wrist with small, flat rings of polished tin, blued steel, and dyed porcelain. In the blinding light, a flick of the wrist sends sharp, tiny semaphores of reflected sun and dark contrast across the causeway. It is not a crude trade-sign; it is a dense, rapid grammar of angles, shadow-shapes cast against the pale front of a tunic, and flashes of metal—half hand-talk, half borrowed instinct from the ultraviolet-blinking gliders overhead. They converse at a full walking pace without breaking stride, three sentences passing between them in four seconds of fluttering shade and glinting tin: *Bladder four on the south siphon is lagging its stroke; mineral rind in the collar; going down with the vinegar lance to bleed it.*

The other answers with a two-finger tilt that catches the white glare off the water below—*Watched it on the gauge. Take the wide pattens; the shelf under four laid down soft chalk overnight*—and continues toward the residential blocks without turning his head.

To understand them properly, you have to follow one indoors, through the double-sealed vestibule of one of those squat, windowless ceramic-brick blocks sitting isolated on its iron pilings.

The outer door swings shut on heavy, oiled brass hinges that make no squeak, sealing against a gasket of grey silicone. Inside, the glare vanishes instantly, so completely that for ten seconds the interior seems pitch black. Then the eyes open wide, and the true climate of their domestic life reveals itself. 

There are no lamps burning. Light enters from *beneath*. Built into the thick insulated floors are vertical shafts lined with polished silver plate that drop down between the iron pilings, catching the intense upward bounce-light off the flooded flats below and funneling it up through thick slabs of pale green and amber soda-lime glass set flush into the floorboards. The rooms are lit like deep-water grottos, bathed in a cool, wavering, upward luminescence that throws the faint patterns of rippling brine onto the vaulted brick ceilings. Every time a five-legged dome-grazer wades beneath the building outside, its soft, pulsing silhouette drifts huge and blurred across the glowing green glass of the living-room floor, watched lazily by toddlers lying on their bellies.

Here, out of the sun, the garments and goggles come off. Inside the double walls of glazed brick, cooled by cold-water pipes drawn up from the deep basalt beneath the caldera’s thermal cap, the air is sixty-five degrees and kept deliberately damp. And here, finally, there is sound—though it is the sound of people who treat vibration as an intoxicant and a craft.

They speak indoors in low, resonant murmurs, pitched right at the bottom of the chest, voices worn velvet by generations of quiet. Nobody raises a voice in anger; in a windowless brick vault shared by twelve or twenty kin, a shout is considered a kind of physical assault. Instead, the rooms are full of small, deliberate contact-noises. While elders sit on slung leather hammocks repair-weaving basalt hats or lapping brass valve-seats with fine emery paste, someone usually has a thumb resting against a *taut-board*—narrow soundboxes of thin, seasoned wood bolted directly to the cast-iron structural pilings that go all the way down into the stone of the caldera. By plucking tiny tines of tempered steel set into the board, they play music that barely enters the air at all; you hear it by pressing a bare heel or an elbow against the cool brick wall, feeling the deep, humming chords travel through your bones, mingling with the faint, sub-audible tremor of the Stirling flywheels turning half a mile away through the bedrock, and the subtle scrape of copper-shield creatures latching onto the pilings directly underneath the house.

Children born here learn to read the building’s pilings with their palms before they can walk. Touching the main iron column in the center of the kitchen, a seven-year-old can tell when a utility tram has passed the quarter-mile switch, when the automated scraper-gantry in the nearby trough has hit a knot of hard silica, or when a flock of heavy copper-shields has clustered thick enough on the household’s gray-water radiator fins to slow the afternoon cooling cycle.

Their whole civic life is organized around the management of crusts, gradients, and slow flows. They do not think of themselves as miners, though three hundred tons of refined lithium salts, borates, pure precipitated silica, and rare-earth slurries slide out of the caldera every week down a gravity-fed pipeline notched into the low eastern pass. Ask them what they do—in the patient, indoor murmur over bowls of pale pink, vinegar-cured biofilm noodles and poached brine-slug white-meat, rich and dense as scallop—and their word for their profession translates roughly as *tenders of the skin*.

Everything in the basin wants to skin over. Left alone for forty years, the town would not rust away; it would petrify, entombed in smooth apricot and cream stone until the distillation towers became strange cylindrical buttes and the viaducts turned into natural aqueducts. Left too clean, stripped too aggressively by the diamond wires, the chemistry of the terraces shifts, the water channels carve too fast, the pink microbial mats tear loose in ragged sheets, and the dome-grazers starve or migrate away from the sectors where their rasping mouths keep the intake grates clear of slime.

So the people live inside a continuous, slow-motion choreography with the precipitate.

Every household has its hereditary *reaches*—specific acreage of flat, pipe, piling, and lagoon whose mineral accretion they know down to the fraction of a millimeter. Out on the flats in the late afternoon, before the glare begins to tilt, you can watch the kiln-masons and weir-setters at work. Moving on wide aluminum pattens across water hot enough to scald a careless wrist through a torn glove, they place small, curved baffles of unglazed stoneware onto the travertine terraces. They don't build walls; they let the valley build them. By setting a line of three-inch ceramic wedges at a precise angle to the sheet-flow today, a mason knows that in six months the water will have laid down a smooth, solid travertine curb four inches high, gently turning ten thousand gallons an hour away from a subsiding transformer foundation and pooling it instead into a new shallow nursery pan.

They grow their furniture and spare parts the same way. Beneath the shaded undersides of the residential blocks, hanging from frames into the richest mineral outflows beneath the pilings, sit wire-mesh armatures in the shapes of chairs, washbasins, tabletops, pipe-collars, and burial urns. Over two or three seasons, the steaming sheet-water coats the wire in dense, translucent bands of banded travertine and opalized silica—layered in cream, pale apricot, and rust depending on which fissure was running strong that winter. When the piece reaches thickness, they haul it up, polish the seating surfaces with pumice until they gleam like wet bone, and carry it upstairs. Every house is furnished with the dated geology of its own lifetime; an old woman’s armchair shows the thin dark band of iron-rich water from the tremor year she was married, polished smooth by forty years of her shoulders.

Between these people and the silent animals of the basin, there is neither domestication nor wilderness, only a dense, opportunistic familiarity.

No one fences out the five-legged dome-grazers. On the contrary, the town’s plumbers rely on them. Where low-pressure cooling pipes cross the flats just above water level, workers smear the undersides of the conduits with a paste of crushed sulfate scavengers; the grazers gather beneath to rasp it clean, their pulsing, blood-cooled domes brushing against the pipe-bellies and absorbing stray heat while their five calcified legs stir the sediment just enough to keep it from setting into hard scale. When a worker needs to cross a crowded lagoon, he doesn't shoo them; he simply flashes a small matte-black paddle hung at his belt, imitating the shadow-Drop signal of a diving glider, and the domes smoothly part three yards to either side without missing a rasping beat, rippling pale violet across their translucent tops as he passes.

Even the dead stay in the gradient, though never in the flats where their minerals would contaminate the salt-pans. High up on the grey basalt shoulders of the caldera’s rim—dry, cold, fifty feet above the highest upwelling—stand long rows of open stonework cribs. When someone’s lungs or heart finally tire of the thin air, their kin carry them up on an induction tram at twilight, wash them in the last warm condensate from the family still, and lay them out under the bleached sky. Within days, the membrane-winged gliders spiral down from the thermals in utter silence, flashing slow, solemn washes of metallic blue across their flanks as they land on the basalt cribs. Beneath the sun and the dry wind and the clean, surgical mouths of the gliders, a body turns to white bone in a week. Afterward, the family brings the bones back down into the valley and sets them into a quiet niche beneath their home’s main piling, where the weeping capillary creep slowly drinks them in, wrapping ribs and skull in smooth, pearlescent stone until the ancestor becomes part of the bell-shaped pedestal holding the house above the scalding mirror.

Now, late in the day, the light begins to change, and the valley’s hidden hour approaches.

As the sun drops behind the western shoulder of grey basalt, the direct glare cuts off across twelve miles of water in a single sweeping line. For twenty minutes, the basin is lit *only* from beneath—the hot water and pale travertine still glowing with stored radiance while the sky overhead turns a deep, sudden indigo. Shadows invert completely; the undersides of the viaducts and the bellies of the gliding trams gleam pale gold against a darkening vault.

And then the air cools past its saturation point.

All at once, without wind or thunder, the invisible humidity that hung suspended all afternoon surrenders. Fog does not roll in from elsewhere; it *precipitates* everywhere at the same instant, ten feet thick right off the skin of the water, turning the entire twelve-mile mirror into a sea of luminous, warm pearl. Visibility drops from twelve miles to twelve paces. 

Up on the perforated steel causeways, just at chest height with the top of the sudden mist, the doors of the ceramic-brick blocks swing open. Out come the people of the town—without hats now, goggles pushed up onto their foreheads, bare skin drinking the wet dusk, pale zinc smears ghostly on their jaws in the twilight. Below their knees, their boots vanish into the warm white cloud rising off the brine. Around the iron pilings beneath them, invisible in the steam, hundreds of copper-shield creatures unlatch from the cooling radiator fins and slide back into the hot outflows like coins slipped into oil. Overhead, the last gliders drop onto the warm tops of the distillation towers, folding their oiled-paper wings, blinking one final, faint ripple of violet along the rooflines before going dark.

Along three miles of walkways, small tin lamps with amber lenses are hung on hooks, glowing softly through the steam. People stroll between the widely spaced blocks to visit, carrying covered stoneware crocks of cold cellar-water and warm fermented mats, leaning together on the railings above the unseen water. Out here in the damp dark, where the air is finally gentle on the throat and the blinding mirror is covered for the night, you can hear them at last: dozens of low, chest-deep voices murmuring quietly across the mist from landing to landing, light as condensation dropping onto stone, careful never to wake the silence underneath.
