You will not see it at first because you are looking for something that displaces water. 

Look instead at the low curve of hand-dressed limestone near the lower cleft, the one fused to the raw basalt. Watch it when a Dinner-plate shard of greenstone shears off the ceiling and hurtles across the vortex straight toward the masonry. Before the shard can strike, something unpeels itself from the lee side of the limestone courses, intercepts the spinning blade of rock with an audible, meaty *thwack* that is instantly swallowed by the roar, and drops back behind the wall with the shard clutched against its underside.

At first glance, you would take it for a bundle of waterlogged timber and torn hide that got caught in an eddy and somehow knotted itself together. It has the mass of a butchered hog, low-slung and wide, flattened by the relentless hydraulic hammer until it possesses almost no profile at all. Anything tall here gets snapped; anything buoyant gets shredded in the centrifuge. So the *mason*—for there is no better word for what it spends its eternity doing—is built entirely of dense, solid, airless weight. 

It has no swim bladder, no lungs, no hollow cavities behind a face. If there were air inside it, the cavitation and the wildly fluctuating pressure thresholds would rupture it from within in minutes. Instead, it is solid all the way through: a rubbery, shock-absorbing matrix of grey muscle and flexible gristle, wrapped in a hide that looks and feels like wet slate layered with calloused leather. Where a stray razor-edge of basalt slices across its back, no blood clouds the water. The cut simply opens pale and dense, like a slice in a boiled turnip, and slowly knits shut while small, gravelly bits of rock trapped in the wound are absorbed straight into the skin as fresh armor.

Count its limbs and you will get a different answer depending on how hard the water is pulling. Fundamentally, there are six heavy, radial appendages fanning out from a central, tortoise-wide core, each as thick as a quarryman’s thigh. None of them have true joints—knees and elbows have levers that the cross-currents could wrench backward—so they bend anywhere along their length like thick lengths of braided hawser. Each limb terminates not in claws, which would snap on the glassy basalt, nor in suckers, which fail when a rock face suddenly splits into a double-exposure and drops its seal. Instead, the underside of each broad foot is a dense plush of millions of microscopic, greased fibers—the exact structural trick of a gecko’s toe, scaled up to survive a hydraulic press. It does not grip the rock; it *adheres* to the molecular surface of it, sliding across slick greenstone in the teeth of a forty-knot gyre as easily as oil across a skillet.

Wait for a fresh fissure to tear open in the far wall, bleeding that brief, exhausted violet-grey light through the silt, and look closely at the creature’s margins. 

It has no head. A neck is just a place to be broken. Its mouth is tucked squarely underneath its center of mass, scraping flush against whatever stone it is currently clinging to. Around the rim of its body runs a thick, fleshy valance fringed with short, stiff bristles of pale horn. These bristles are constantly twitching, not feeling the water, which lies about direction every three seconds, but reading the *sound*. 

Because the noise in this chamber is an absolute, battering physical force, the mason uses the acoustic violence the way surface creatures use sunlight. Everything in the basin casts a sonic shadow. When a wobbling, silver tornado of mist whips down from the upper fractures, it muffles the brass-horn crackle of cavitation behind it; the mason feels that sudden dead-spot on its left flank and flattens itself harder against the stone before the air pocket can hit it and strip its oxygen-absorbing skin dry. When a woven reed basket tumbles lazily out of nowhere into the center of the nave, the sub-audible bass grinding of the mountain reflects off the basket’s weave in a distinct, hollow patter. Instantly, two of the mason's thick limbs unspool, stretching astonishingly thin as they reach out—not through the boiling water, which would tear them off, but *along* the spatial stutter where the left side of the vortex fails to meet the right. 

It knows the geometry is broken. More than that, it relies on it. Watch one of its forelimbs reach into a shearing seam of water three feet from its body and emerge simultaneously twelve feet higher up, just below the inverted chimney where the doghouse-sized cobble shrine sits wedged. The broad, plush-bottomed foot wraps deftly around a loose river cobble that the horizontal spin has rattled half-out of the shrine’s wall, shoves it firmly back into its dry-stone socket, holds it there until the hum of the arch catches and locks the stone’s weight back into place, and then retracts back through the seam. 

This is what it does. It maintains the impossible.

Why a blind, deaf, cartilaginous slab of deep-mountain meat cares about pre-iron masonry is a question that only makes sense if you assume the masonry came first and the creature found it. Look closer at the pale river cobbles of that shrine when the mason pulls its limb away. They aren't held together by mortar, true—because there is no lime here—but wedged deep into the chinks between the dressed stones is a tough, fibrous caulking made of chewed reed-pulp, splintered timber, and shredded pale-ochre antler-weed, spat into the gaps and hammered tight until the acoustic vibration of the walls welds it hard as bone. 

The mason isn't an animal squatting in ruins. Or rather, it was not always just an animal, or these are not entirely ruins. 

Consider what happens when a whole, quiet world—a valley of split-timber halls, cobble shrines, and limestone sheepfolds—doesn't merely flood, but falls sideways into the unmade marrow of a rising mountain range. The timber beams shred in ten seconds. The reed baskets turn to chaff. Anything with hollow bones, red blood, lungs, eyes, or eardrums dies on the first concussive beat of the cavitation, crushed and deafened and scattered into silt before it even realizes up has disappeared. 

Unless, perhaps, the mountain didn't tear open all at once. Or unless time in this chamber is just as badly sheared as its left and right walls, so that a stone-axe beam dropped six thousand years ago only arrives in the vortex *now*, and whatever fell in along with the first course of limestone has had ten thousand generations of furious, compressed, non-linear time inside the gyre to strip away everything that didn't work, keeping only the blind, heavy, stubborn impulse to keep the walls standing.

Watch it move out from behind the limestone curve now. There are two of them in the basin—never near each other, because two heavy things in the same current become flails that beat each other to death, yet always working in an eerie, syncopated tandem across the sixty-foot gulf. 

The larger one glides down the raw basalt toward a jagged, brand-new fracture still leaking dim purple light. A solitary tuft of pale antler-weed has already rooted in the razor-sharp lip, flexing wildly. A forearm-long translucent flatworm is sliding down a water-shear toward it to graze. The mason doesn't eat the flatworm—the worm is mostly water and slick gristle, poor fuel for something so heavy. Instead, the mason slides directly *over* the fresh crack, clamping all six massive limbs down around the bleeding light, sealing the seam under its wide, slate-grey belly. 

For three, four, five minutes it stays clamped there while the boiling water hammers its domed back, trying to pry it loose, and spinning plates of sheared greenstone glance off its thick hide with dull, stony clacks. Underneath its body, sheltered from the current, its rasping ventral mouth isn't eating the weed; it is scraping up the raw, glowing mineral slush bleeding directly out of the new basalt—drinking the trapped, violent energy of the mountain’s breaking—while carefully pruning only two outer tines of the ochre weed and tucking them into a muscular fold beside its mouth.

Then the current shifts. The far wall stutters, doubling itself in the violet gloom, and a massive wedge of greenstone the size of a haycart tears loose from the upper groin of the nave, caught immediately in the roaring centrifuge. 

Before the boulder has completed half a turn, both masons are already moving. They feel the catastrophic change in the room's bass note through every bristle on their fringes. They do not flee into crevices—crevices here are just guillotines waiting to shut. Instead, the smaller mason high up near the mist-tornados lets go of the wall entirely. 

It doesn't swim. It curls its six limbs inward until it becomes a dense, streamlined discus of grey gristle and slate-skin, angling its body like a skipping stone against the invisible boundary between two colliding walls of warm water. It shoots across the roaring nave at terrifying speed, skipping cleanly off a wobbling, ten-foot sphere of fractured water, dropping instantly through a spatial fold, and slapping down with bone-jarring force onto the remnant of the vanished stone staircase just a heartbeat before the tumbling greenstone boulder arrives.

It cannot stop the boulder. Nothing could. What it does is wedge its dense, rubbery bulk diagonally into the angle of the remaining limestone steps, puffing its muscular core hard and rigid, turning itself into a living fender. 

When the haycart-sized wedge of greenstone clips the staircase, it hits the mason instead. The impact would liquefy anything with a skeleton. The mason compresses to half its thickness—a flat, hideous pancake of grey muscle absorbing tons of kinetic shear—and its greased-fiber feet screech half an inch sideways across the dressed limestone, shedding a brief, milky cloud of torn bristles. Then the boulder glances off the living bumper, spinning away harmlessly into the purple dark to smash open a fresh line of light on the barren basalt opposite.

Slowly, in the continuous, deafening crackle that sounds like burning forests blown through brass, the flattened thing on the staircase reinflates its dense meat. A six-inch shard of glassy greenstone is embedded deep in its flank; already its grey skin is puckering tight around the rock, turning the wound into a new piece of shingle. 

Unhurriedly, it reaches underneath itself, pulls out a half-chewed wad of antler-weed and splintered axe-cut oak it caught an hour ago, and begins to pack it tightly under the corner of the top limestone step, which knocked slightly loose in the blow. When the stone hums again—that low, impossible harmonic that means the step still remembers how to be a step—the mason unpeels two limbs, tests the violent water for the next spatial break, and goes back to waiting in the dark for the next piece of the quiet world to fall through.
