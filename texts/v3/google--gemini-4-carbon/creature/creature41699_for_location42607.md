To live at the bottom of the split is to have two completely different sets of nerves: one for the ear, which learns to ignore almost everything, and one for the soles of the feet and the palms of the hands, which miss nothing at all.

A visitor lowered down the western hoist-way spends their first three days with their shoulders hunched around their ears, flinching at every two-second shriek of a pressure valve and shouting themselves hoarse trying to order soup across a counter. By the second week, they notice the locals never raise their voices. When two mechanics stand six inches apart beside an open-air transformer, they speak in flat, unhurried chest-tones that vanish completely three feet away; they are watching each other’s throats and lips, reading the shape of the words as much as hearing them, their hands flashing quick, greasy shorthand for numbers, thread-gauges, and tensions. Across any distance greater than an arm’s reach, speech is abandoned altogether. The whole settlement talks with its wrists and elbows.

A woman leaning over the iron railing of a fourth-floor tenement in Tower Two doesn't call down to her husband crossing the blinding grey flat fifty yards below. She raps the heel of her wooden clog twice against the cast-iron drainpipe—a sharp, clean *tock-tock* that travels straight down the metal into the bedrock—and when he looks up, she turns an open palm toward the eastern cliff and taps two fingers against her collarbone. *Going to the bathhouse; back in an hour.* He tips his cap with two fingers and keeps walking at the basin’s universal pace: a long, flat-footed, rolling stride that matches the stroll of the electric tram, chosen because running on sharp-stepped, freshly cleaved slate is a fast way to open a shin to the bone.

You can tell someone born in the cleft by their footwear and their wrists.

Every boot in the basin is resoled locally with thick laminated pads of vulcanized rubber and boiled felt, trimmed flush to the leather welt so nothing catches on an eighth-inch lip of fractured greystone. Beneath that soft tread, however, the residents are exquisitely tuned to the floor. They feel the town’s clockwork through their ankles. When the main pawl in Tower Seven drops at two in the afternoon, nobody looks up from their lathe or their soup pot; yet if a secondary reduction gear in Tower Nine develops a microscopic burr on a brass tooth, half the people in the nearest plaza will stop dead in the sunlight, tilt their heads slightly toward the brick wall, and wait four minutes for the slow wheel to bring the bad tooth around again so they can confirm the hitch through the stone.

Their wrists are thick, ropy, and scarred with pale, crescent-shaped nicks from slate edges and hot copper filings. Almost nothing in the cleft is done swiftly, and almost nothing is done with light tackle. Even domestic chores borrow from the heavy torque of the engine-houses. Laundry isn't wrung by hand; wet sheets are fed through massive, slow-turning mangle-rollers bolted to the tenement balconies, driven by thin take-off shafts geared down from the building’s main vertical drive. A child of eight can engage a bronze clutch with one thumb and watch a winter quilt pressed bone-dry with five tons of borrowed tectonic pull, though every child is drilled from toddlerhood never to wear loose sleeves, trailing scarves, or unbuttoned coats anywhere outside their front door. Clothing in the cleft is close-fitting, heavy-woven, and fastened with flat horn toggles designed to snap cleanly apart under twenty pounds of pull—a quiet concession to the fact that the machinery around them cannot be stalled by anything softer than eight inches of cold-rolled steel.

The population is small—fewer than four hundred souls spread across the eleven green-tiled towers—and weirdly stratified, not by wealth, which barely fluctuates here, but by *elevation and damp*.

The ground-floor and cantilever-level people are the Tallow-Coats: the anchor-smiths, gear-scrapers, cable-walkers, and condenser-tenders. They smell permanently of lanolin, mutton fat, and crushed greenery. Because the lower thirty feet of every tower is wrapped in a suffocating, dripping curtain of giant tree-ferns and liverwort, the mechanics work half-submerged in tropical botany while dressed for a cool, high-plains autumn. To check the anchor-bolts on the western rock face, a rigger puts on greased canvas overalls, slings a brass-bound spirit level and a long machete from the same belt, and steps off a dry sunlit plaza straight into ten feet of black-green twilight.

Inside the fern-skirts, the roar of the basin is muffled to a thick, wet hum, replaced by the deafening, metallic *tink-tink-tink* of thousands of copper frogs vibrating against the ribcage. The riggers move through the wet foliage with slow, deliberate sweeps of long-handled hooked knives, clearing strangler-fig roots out of rivet heads and scraping plate-sized, lacquered beetles off the warm brass condenser unions as casually as a gardener brushing aphids off a rosebush. They don’t kill the beetles—the heavy insects are scooped into wire baskets and dumped at the base of the cliff, because they eat the lime scale faster than vinegar and brass brushes ever could, leaving the pipe-joints bright and bare without scoring the metal.

Higher up, in the glazed green-tile tenements of the fourth, fifth, and sixth stories, the air is crisp, dry, and glittering with wind-blown mica dust and bird-down. Up here live the porcelain-turners, the armature-winders, the bakers, the glass-grinders who make lenses in the vibration-proof stone cellars and polish them in the bright upper rooms, and the retirees whose knees can no longer take the iron spiral stairs.

Their lives are bound up in a permanent, unsentimental truce with the wildlife that shares the high wires.

The big blue-backed cliff birds—locals call them *clackers*, or *brass-throats*—have zero fear of human beings. They weigh four pounds apiece, smell faintly of ozone and warm feathers, and treat a cast-iron fire escape precisely the same way they treat a granite ledge. A woman stepping out onto her balcony to check her root-boxes of carrots and cellar-onions has to shove two or three heavy, indignant birds off the turnip greens with the flat of a broom before she can water them. The birds don't even fly when pushed; they simply lean back, snap their hooked yellow bills once against the broomstraws with a sound like oak blocks clapping together, and sidle six inches down the iron rail.

Nobody harms them, partly because their eggs—pale blue, thick-shelled as stoneware mugs, and laid right on the warm brick cornices above the engine-house arches—are collected every Tuesday with long-handled wooden spoons and baked into the dense, saffron-colored custards sold at the Tower Four bakery; and partly because the birds are the town’s best insulation inspectors. Clackers love warmth, and they hate leakage. They will pack themselves shoulder to shoulder along a sixty-thousand-volt busbar rack in the cool morning air, preening calmly, until the exact afternoon a porcelain insulator develops a hairline crack and begins to bleed stray current across its glaze. Long before an instrument panel inside the transformer shed registers the fault, a three-foot gap opens up in the row of sitting birds. The high-wire linemen don't even bother with binoculars half the time; they just stand in the sunlit flat with their thumbs hooked in their belts, squint up against the bright sky, and look for the empty white porcelain spool where no bird will put its feet.

With the long-tailed gray climbers—the *grease-monkeys* or *turnbuckle-thieves*—relations are considerably more watchful.

Roughly the size of a big housecat, with hands as dexterous and cold-fingered as a raccoon’s, the climbers have adapted entirely to the industrial ecology of the rift. Their gray fur is permanently slicked dark along their bellies from sliding down tallowed tension cables, and they have figured out the town’s pulley systems to an infuriating degree. Groceries, mail, freshly sharpened chisels, and hot loaves travel from tower to tower or floor to floor in lidded wicker baskets slung from endless hemp loops. If a latch is left unpinned for thirty seconds while someone answers a kettle, three gray shapes will drop twenty feet off a copper ground-strap, hit the swaying rope without making it bounce, flip the hasp with their thumbs, and vanish into a dripping wall of pitcher-plants carrying a whole salted mackerel or a bundle of wax candles.

Why a creature that eats snails and fig-pulp wants wax candles was a mystery for twenty years, until an anchor-crew clearing an old exhaust flue found a dry rock chimney packed four feet deep with stolen tallow stubs, brass washers, lost thimbles, and porcelain fuse-plugs—chewed over, arranged by color, and meticulously greased by three generations of bachelor males using industrial salvage to impress females during the autumn rut. Now, hardware shops hang shallow tin trays of defective brass nuts and scrap mutton-suet out on the steel cantilever tips just to bribe the climbers away from the domestic lines.

And then there is the third tier of life in the cleft—the things that were never on the lowland plateau above, or if they were, existed only as starving, fingernail-sized scraps hiding under flat rocks until the rift tore open and humans bolted warm, sweating engines into the wound.

Down in the deep sump-pits beneath the twenty-foot cable drums—places where the cold rock cleft narrows into pitch-black slots descending hundreds of feet toward the blind mechanics of the earth, and where warm, oily condensed water drips continuously down raw, unweathered mineral faces—something else has moved in.

The mechanics call them *plate-backs* or *rift-crawlers*, though strictly speaking they are a kind of blind, pale crustacean, flat as a carpenter’s square and wide as a dinner trencher, with calcified shells the exact chalky, micaceous white of the freshly split bedrock. On the dry plateau above, their ancestors were tiny, translucent spring-shrimp living in deep groundwater seams. Brought up into the rich bath of warm condenser runoff, flaking rust, and organic detritus washing down from the fern-jungles above, they have grown massive and weirdly leisurely, matching the tempo of the engines overhead.

If you shine an electric hand-lamp down through the iron floor-gratings of Tower One, the wet stone walls ten feet below appear at first to be tiled in overlapping slabs of pale slate. Hold the light steady for two minutes, and one of the slabs will slide half an inch sideways across the slick rock, grazing on the velvety black iron-bacteria that bloom where pig-iron counterweights drip grease-water into the dark. Their long, hair-fine antennae—four feet from tip to tip—trail upward into the cool air drafts of the intake shafts, quivering delicately in the sixty-cycle hum of the transformers above. The sump-tenders swear the crawlers navigate by the town’s electrical phase; if a generator is taken offline for brush-cleaning and the hum in that tower stops, every pale plate in the shaft below unhooks from the wall within ten minutes and sinks silently back down into the lightless crack beneath the town, waiting days or weeks in the deep water until the brass governors overhead resume their stately turn.

At night, the cleft becomes another country altogether.

Sunset happens all at once, like a lid sliding shut over a box. One moment the eastern cliff face is blazing gold, every mica chip winking sharp and hot above the dark green skirts of the ferns; four minutes later, the sun drops below the western rim and the whole stone trough plunges into crisp, cold indigo shadow while the sky overhead is still bright afternoon blue.

Immediately, the strings of solitary cast-iron streetlamps click on across the bare bedrock plazas, throwing sharp pools of yellow incandescent light onto clean stone where nothing grows. Up on the cliffs and the high steel trusses, the four thousand blue-backed birds tuck their yellow bills beneath their scapular feathers and go silent within twenty minutes—a sudden, breathtaking subtraction of treble from the air.

In that newly hollowed space, the wet pockets wake up.

The copper frogs shift from their daytime *tink* to a slower, bell-like *glock-glock-glock* that echoes off the ninety-degree rock corners until the whole basin sounds as if water were dripping into a thousand bronze bowls of different sizes. Out of the black-green curtains of broad-leaved climbers creep pale, nocturnal tree-snails with shells coiled flat like watch-springs, sliding out onto the dry, night-cold brick of the towers to lick the salt efflorescence from the mortar. Moths the span of a mason’s trowel—heavy, furry things colored like oxidized copper and grey slate—flutter lazily out of the warm exhaust plumes to circle the streetlamps, casting huge, wheeling shadows across forty yards of empty stone floor while night-jars dive silently down from the plateau rim to snatch them mid-turn, banking hard between the braided steel cables without ever touching a wire.

This is the hour when the people of the cleft come down onto the stone.

In the daytime, the plazas are only corridors to be crossed between tasks. After dark, with the birds asleep and the raw rock radiating back the modest warmth it soaked up during two hours of direct afternoon sun, families carry folding canvas chairs, woolen lap-rugs, and heavy ceramic jugs of hot spiced chicory out into the middle of the bare grey flats, thirty yards from the nearest building.

They sit in small circles under the solitary lamps, twenty or thirty yards from the next group, surrounded by acres of clean, sharp-edged stone and framed by towering walls of wet, invisible jungle steaming faintly into the cold night air. Beneath them, felt through the wooden legs of their chairs and the thick rubber soles of their boots, runs the deep, unhurried chord of the sixty-cycle transformers and the slow, planetary pull of the earth widening beneath them by the thickness of a sheet of paper since Tuesday.

Nobody talks much even then. Two old men play dominoes on an upturned tool-crate, setting the bone tiles down on the wood with crisp, deliberate snaps. A girl repairs a wicker pulley-basket with soaked willow switches, pausing every few minutes to toss a snippet of bark toward the dark wall of ferns ten yards away, smiling when two small, bright amber eyes catch the lamplight from a turnbuckle overhead. Every forty minutes or so, deep inside one of the warm brick halls behind its tall, gleaming windows, a heavy iron ratchet reaches its mark and drops home—*CLANK*—sending a single, clean tremor across the slate floor, rattling the spoons in the chicory mugs for half a second before the brass governors take up the slack and go right on turning, unhurried, condensing bright new beads of water out of the dark.
