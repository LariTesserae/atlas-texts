If you stand on one of the woven-reed plazas and stare across the bright, booming cylinder of air to the opposite wall, it takes a very long time to realize you are being looked at in return. 

There are perhaps sixty people in the entire chime—enough for ten or twelve small households, though they possess housing for two thousand. Right now, three of them are in plain view on the far galleries, entirely motionless. One sits on the outer edge of a slate cantilever, knees drawn to his chest, wrapped in a stiff, overlapping mantle of cured bladderwort skins greased with bird-fat. From two hundred paces away, glistening with condensation, he looks exactly like a parcel of bundled slate left out to weather. Above him, a woman leans with one shoulder against a living root-cable, the pads of two fingers resting lightly on a hairline seam in the basalt. She has been standing in that exact posture since the morning cloud tore itself on the eastern rim and drifted out, two hours ago. 

They are human, though the altitude and the stone have pared them down to essentials. They are compact, barrel-chested for the thin air, with heavy-knuckled hands and broad, flat feet encased in thick-soled sandals made from stacked layers of green-hide—the only material tough enough to absorb the razor bite of unweathered rock without slicing through. Their skin is wind-chapped to the color of red clay, pale around the eyes where they squint against the blinding pearl-glare of passing clouds. None of them wear loose clothing. A fluttering hem here will snag on a glassy spur of basalt and rip clean away, or worse, pull the wearer off-balance on a foot-wide stair over the abyssal draft. Everything they wear is strapped, wrapped, or laced tight to the calf, forearm, and ribs, armored at the elbows and knees with boiled leather plates scored by dozens of fine, white, superficial cuts.

They move slow not because they are sluggish, but because velocity is fatal here. To hurry on wet, unweathered slate slick with highland condensation is to slide off into two hundred paces of nothing; to stumble into a doorframe of fractured obsidian is to open an artery. So they have adopted the cadence of the cliff-cranes. When the sitting man finally rises, he shifts his weight over his heels with deliberate, liquid precision, testing the wet reed-matting with his big toe before committing his hip, rising smoothly into the bright air without a single jerky motion. 

To live inside a stone horn that roars like a waterfall of gravel is to abandon the throat. A human shout cannot cross ten paces against the deafening, crisp clatter of ten thousand water drops hammering tensioned slate, and the low, sub-audible hum of the wind over the rim swallows chest-tones whole. 

Consequently, they are silent. Their faces have grown extraordinarily mobile, muscular around the brows and mouth, capable of throwing clear, stark expressions across narrow alleys. For speaking across the two-hundred-pace void between neighborhoods, they carry *clappers* at their belts—pairs of dense, cured hornbeam slats or polished antler tines—and small discs of flaked, silver-backed mica. 

The woman touching the rock seam draws her mica disc and tilts it into the sharp, cold shaft of high-altitude sun. Across the gulf, a dazzling white spark leaps onto the face of the man who just stood up. He looks at her. She does not wave; broad gestures look like falling. Instead, she strikes her hornbeam slats together three times. 

*Tak. Tak-tak.* 

Wood-on-wood has a dry, flat, mid-range snap that cuts cleanly through the wet, ringing higher frequencies of water-on-slate and the bass drone of the wind. Across the cylinder, the man pulls a heavy wooden mallet from his belt-loop and taps the slate cantilever beneath his sandal once—sending a dull shudder into the cliff—then holds up four fingers, splayed wide against the dark wet green of the bergenia behind him. 

They are discussing the seam under her fingers. Two hours ago, when the mountain let go one of its gunshot *cracks*, the stress distribution of the entire western wall shifted by the breadth of a fingernail. A walkway four tiers below her has suddenly gone slack in its root-bindings, while the slate shelf above her is now pinched so tightly by the closing rock that it will shatter like spun glass when tonight’s frost wedges ice into the joint. 

This is why there is a city for two thousand and only sixty souls inside it. They are not the remnants of a vanished metropolis, nor are they waiting for an army of settlers to arrive. The sixty *are* the city’s full measure, and they need every empty street of it.

You cannot live in one house here for a year. The mountain is too young, too restless, and the plants are too hungry. A terrace wedged together in the spring is habitable by autumn; by the following winter, the braided binding-roots have swollen so thick with constant moisture that they begin to lever the slate floor upward at a tilt, or else the rock face behind it shifts, turning a dry bedroom into a sluiceway for a shivering sheet of ice-water. Conversely, a neighborhood that has been unlivable for five years—too loose, or pelted daily by glassy shards from an active fracture overhead—will suddenly lock tight after a minor tremor, its dangling stone counterweights finally pulling the calcified vines taut into an iron-hard bridge, its drip-catchers brimming with sweet water, its eaves newly sheltered by a fresh calving of rock high above.

The inhabitants are sedentary nomads, migrating thirty yards at a time around the inside of a stone barrel. 

At any given month, nine-tenths of the tiered galleries are *ripening* or *resting*. A family of five might occupy three dry, stable rooms on the north wall while simultaneously tending twelve future houses scattered clockwise around the chasm: hanging a forty-pound chunk of basalt on a vine-rope here to draw an awning straight by next summer; wedging an antler-shimmed slate step into a fresh crack there so the clubmoss will felt over it before the child learns to walk; dismantling the lashed reed-screens of a parlor on the south rim because the mountain’s settling has aimed a cold draft straight up through its floorboards from the abyssal dark below.

Inside the few occupied dwellings, life is astonishingly dry, quiet, and dim. To step off a sunlit, deafening terrace through a low, woven-willow hatch is to enter a basket slung beneath stone. The walls are triple-layered mats of dried clubmoss stuffed between wicker frames, hung a hand’s-breadth shy of the weeping cliff so the rock-water sheets harmlessly behind them. That thick, springy moss drinks the endless percussive roar of the exterior, turning the shattering *clack-clack-clack* of the drips into a thick, muffled hush, like rain heard from deep underground. 

Here, in the peat-warmed dark smelling of lanolin, crushed green stems, and tallow lamps, they finally use their voices. They speak in astonishingly soft, breathy registers, bare whispers rich with sibilants, pitched just louder than their own breathing. Someone raised in that moss-muffled quiet finds raising their voice physically painful, even vulgar—like striking flint indoors. Around tiny, smokeless braziers fueled by dried bladderwort bladders and resinous root-shavings, old men with scarred knuckles braid endless lengths of green-hide thong while children play intricate cat’s-cradle games that are actually structural blueprints, learning at four years old how a three-strand living hitch tightens when wet and slackens when frozen.

What do they eat, suspended in an air-well with no dirt? 

Follow the boy who emerges onto a lower gallery near noon, descending a switchback stair that has no handrail—only a taut, vertical vine he touches lightly with his palm for balance, never gripping it tightly lest a sudden rock-shift whip the line and break his wrist. He carries a long-handled wooden scoop and a basket lined with broad, waxy leaves. 

The chime is a giant funnel trap set directly in the teeth of the mountain passes. Every cloud that snags on the jagged basalt rim dumps not just water, but the high-altitude drift of the lower valleys: stunned swarms of fat, cold-numbed midges, wind-tossed pine mast, stray migratory finches baffled by the blinding pearl-fog, and clouds of highland moths. All of it washes down the sheer, ungrooved rock faces in the shivering films of water, caught by a thousand woven-linen drip-catchers, reed-sieves, and wide-mouthed clay pots arranged along the lower tiers.

The boy moves along the wet pot-rows, skimming rich paste of drowned insects and wind-blown seeds to feed into wicker pens behind the walkways, where pairs of domesticated cliff-cranes—their leg-scales clipped so they do not wander up to the rim—turn the drift into heavy, grey-shelled eggs and dense, oily meat. From the undersides of the wettest slate overhangs, he harvests pale, translucent tubers of rock-succulents that taste sharply of sorrel and iron, and scrapes thick, gelatinous crusts of sweet lichen off blocks that split from the crust three winters ago. Nothing rots here; the air is too cold, too crisp, too full of sharp ozone. Split crane-meat hung under an outer eave where the abyssal updraft blows ceaselessly cures into black, translucent flint-hard jerky in four days, tasting faintly of wet stone.

Beneath the lowest galleries—fifty yards down where the clear sunlight loses its nerve and blue shadow thickens into perpetual, roaring mist over the wedged blocks at the bottom of the chasm—none of them go, except the bone-tiers. 

There is no dirt to bury the dead, and no timber spare to burn them. When someone dies in the moss-lined rooms, they are wrapped tightly in newly braided, living root-nets alongside three or four sharp basalt wedges, and lowered on long green-hide lines down into the booming twilight where the great calved boulders lock together over the bottomless draft. They are wedged straight into the widest structural chasms at the base of the chime. Within two seasons, the cascading clubmoss and calcifying vines swallow the bundle completely, knitting bone, hide, and wedge into the very keystone blocks that hold the perimeter walls apart. It is not seen as grim; it is considered the final, weight-bearing carpentry of an elder, holding the foundation open against the mountain’s squeeze so their grandchildren’s balconies ten tiers up won’t pinch shut.

High in the sun again, the woman on the western gallery slips her mica disc back into her belt-pouch. Down below, the man with the mallet disappears behind a curtain of glistening silver-green leaves, returning a moment later dragging two long, pale poles of seasoned crane-bone and a coil of wet hide-rope. He begins the twenty-minute walk halfway around the perimeter ring to help her re-tension the fourth-tier walkway before evening frost. 

As he reaches the middle of the northern bridge—a narrow arch of wedged slate held purely in compression between two jagged ribs of black glass—the wind shifts above the rim. The vast hollow cylinder catches the gust and hums its sudden, chest-shaking bass note. Every hanging stone weight throughout the empty plazas sways half an inch in unison, a thousand taut vines creaking in a single chord beneath the roaring applause of the drops. 

The man pauses, lifting one thick-sandaled foot, holding it poised in the sparkling air just like the grey birds overhead, waiting with complete, unhurried patience to feel whether the stone beneath his other heel intends to settle, before setting his foot down and walking on.
