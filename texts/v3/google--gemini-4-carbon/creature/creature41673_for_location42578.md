At first glance, the avenues belong entirely to the fish and the frogs. You could stare down through the celadon clarity for an afternoon, watching the silver curtains of oxygen pearls rise past the market-ring, and swear that whatever built the town is gone. 

Then a section of the braided root-mat on the lower terrace detaches itself, uncurls six pale, disproportionately long limbs—actually four limbs, though the fingers and toes are so attenuated and splayed for grip that they read as a cluster of pale roots—and pulls itself hand-over-hand along the underside of a reed aqueduct.

There are perhaps nineteen of them in the basin. Never many more, never many fewer; the cold and the size of the sinkhole keep an iron ledger.

If they share blood with the people who first knotted the willow shoots above the water line centuries ago, the water has long since rewritten the contract. To live in snow-seep just above freezing without fleeing it requires an abandonment of haste and, eventually, an abandonment of heat. They are not sleek like otters or seals. Sleekness is for things that chase. These are creatures that wait and tend, built like leather wineskins packed tightly with pale, dense tallow. An adult is shorter than a terrestrial man, scarcely four feet from crown to heel, yet weighs nearly twice as much—heavy enough to walk upright down a spiral avenue without floating away, leaning slightly into the slow turn of the current like a pedestrian leaning into a winter wind.

Their skin is the color of washed lard, thick and strangely loose at the joints, folding in heavy, velvety creases around the neck, wrists, and groin. Where the cold mountain sun strikes their backs and shoulders most often, faint mottlings of dull olive and slate-blue sit deep in the dermis, matching the carapace shrimp that inevitably crawl over them when they sit still too long. They have no hair at all; hair traps dead water and breeds rot. Instead, from the base of the skull down to the small of the back, and fanning out behind the upper arms and calves, the skin has been coaxed by generations of cold, hyper-oxygenated water into broad, ruffled aprons of thin flesh. 

In the celadon light, these frills are a dark, startling plum-red, packed with surface veins. When one of the tenders rests—which it does for hours at a time, wedged into the crook of a thickened archway—the plum-colored aprons drift outward in the slow current, drinking dissolved oxygen straight from the water, pulsing once every forty or fifty seconds as the heavy, sluggish heart turns over.

Up toward the surface, where the wind scuffs the dark glass of the tarn, you will sometimes see a wide, flat face rise into the shadows under the overhanging rim-roots to take a true breath. They still have lungs, reduced to single leathery sacs used mostly for ballast and long, strenuous bouts of pruning. A surfacing tender breaks the tension line without a splash, exhales a slow, stale sigh that smells intensely of crushed willow-bark and cold fat, draws in a single vast draught of thin mountain air, and sinks back into the green vault like a dropped stone, letting out tiny silver beads from its slit-like nostrils to trim its buoyancy until it settles onto the exact terrace it aimed for.

To live inside that relentless, bone-shaking roar—the frying-pan crackle of ten thousand snapping shrimp, the chest-thumping *booms* of the loaf-sized frogs in their wooden bass-drums—would shatter a delicate ear. Looking closely at a tender’s broad, tallow-smooth head, there are no ears at all. The sides of the skull are uninterrupted, seamless curves of dense bone covered in thick hide. Their eyes are small, dark, and set behind second eyelids as clear and tough as horn, untroubled by the sharp brightness of the water. 

Instead of ears, their throats and the broad, flat plates of their breastbones are taut and slightly concave, drummed over with thinner, paler skin. They feel the town through their chests. They read the basin’s acoustics the way a blind spider reads its web. 

Watch two of them meet on the curved steps below the market-ring. Neither makes a sound of its own. One lifts a heavy, spade-nailed hand—the fingers webbed halfway to the middle knuckle, the palms crosshatched with pale grey calluses—and places its thumb against the hollow pillar of the seventh market post. With its other hand, it reaches inside the round doorway of a nearby pavilion where a great, pale-bellied frog is booming, and gently shifts a slab of slate lean-to by three inches. 

Instantly, the pitch of the frog’s next *boom* drops by a quarter-tone. Thirty yards away, across the open liquid nave, the second tender pauses on an aqueduct line, feels the altered chord press against its breastbone, and turns its head. They communicate by tuning the city. Every hollow tower, every taut reed pipe, every creaking burl-wood paddle is an organ stop they have known since birth. By cupping a hand over a vent-hole in a reed pipe or shifting their heavy bodies to block a doorway, they throw slow, resonant shadows of altered sound across the water.

They are still builders, though an observer from the dry ridges above would scarcely recognize the work as labor because of the glacial patience required. A single task can take a season; a single gesture takes ten seconds.

Slung diagonally across their heavy torsos, most wear a baldric of braided vine fibers that have been stripped down to raw, rot-proof cellulose. Tucked into this belt are tools that have not changed in forty generations: slender wedges of split char-rib, scrapers of black mountain slate ground to a razor bevel against the exterior scree before being brought down, and heavy spools of living willow-root kept supple in little pouches of woven moss.

Their work is the maintenance of the equilibrium. The drowned town only looks effortless because the nineteen never stop subtly correcting it. 

A tender will spend three hours crouched upon a torso-thick reed aqueduct, its plum-colored back-frills undulating lazily among the rising curtains of oxygen bubbles, listening through its feet to the groan of a burl-wood paddle below. Finding the rhythm slightly staggered—perhaps a winter’s growth of inner callus has narrowed the water-pipe by a fingernail's breadth—the tender unlimbers a slate knife. It does not hack or saw. Moving with the heavy, deliberate grace of a sleepwalker, it presses the stone edge into a specific node on the grafted collar four feet upstream, making a shallow, crescent-shaped score just deep enough to bleed pale sap into the cold water. 

Instantly, a cloud of silver-sided char abandons the street below, drawn lazily upward by the faint chemical tang, while half a dozen slate-blue shrimp skitter out from under the tender's own ankles to snap at the drifting threads of sap. Over the next six months, the wounded reed will swell locally to heal the cut, bowing outward just enough to widen the interior channel and restore the paddle’s three-minute sigh to its proper, ancient measure. The tender knows this the way a dry-land orchardist knows which bud will throw a fruiting branch two summers hence.

Their diet is as slow and cold as their pulse. They do not hunt the char—chasing a fish, even a sluggish one, burns precious tallow. Instead, when a tender needs meat, it drifts into one of the resonating willow-chambers, settles its bulk beside one of the massive, booming mountain frogs, and places both broad, warmish hands gently over the amphibian's flanks. Frog and tender sit together in the booming green twilight of the wooden room until the frog's cold stupor deepens; sometimes the tender strokes the creature's throat, coaxing out a series of low, rolling vibrations that seem to please them both. Most times, the tender simply plucks a few parasites from the frog's pale belly and leaves. Once in a great while, a thumb-nail slides neatly behind the frog's skull, severing the spine without interrupting the room's stillness, and nothing is wasted: the pale meat is shared out raw in thin, waxy strips at the market-ring, the long bones are tucked into crevices for the shrimp to strip clean into future needles, and within a week, another heavy young frog from the lower ledges has claimed the empty resonator and taken up the bass line.

Mostly, though, they eat the town itself, or what the town makes for them. Inside the hollow pillars of the market-ring and within certain sun-facing domes on the upper terraces, the tenders cultivate inner linings of sweet, starchy cambium and gelatinous bladders of clear fern-gum that swell to the size of plums, packed with sugars distilled from the crisp mountain daylight. A tender scrapes a palmful of pale inner bark, rolls it with three or four stunned carapace shrimp for salt and shell-lime, and chews it slowly while watching the light shift across the opposite wall.

As for the spherical cage tethered to the market pillar—the grown-shut globe of living wood holding the white river stone on its bed of moss—they never touch it with a blade, though every slate knife in the basin could part those bars in a minute. 

Instead, it is the one place where their slow lives gather into something like ceremony, or perhaps merely calibration. When the afternoon sun stands directly over the sinkhole’s throat and the celadon light reaches its sharpest, most shadowless pitch, you might see four or five of them—old ones whose tallow-skin is scarred with pale, keloid ridges from decades of scraping against bark, alongside a single round, buoyant infant tethered to its mother’s belt by a soft umbilical leash of braided sedge—resting in a loose circle on the cobble-smooth steps below the pillars. 

They sit completely motionless as the char rasp past their knees and the oxygen pearls bead up not just on the walls around them, but on their own slate-mottled shoulders, turning them momentarily silver before detaching toward the sky. All five pairs of dark, horn-shielded eyes are fixed on the white stone inside its seamless wooden globe. 

Down at the very bottom of the sinkhole, sixty feet below their broad feet, the deep thermal seep lets out its long, upward breath. The water shifts. High above them, a burl-wood paddle groans once, a deep wooden *creak* that shudders through every ribcage in the water. Inside its cage, the white stone lifts a fraction of an inch off its moss bed, hangs weightless for three slow heartbeats, and settles back down. 

The eldest tender reaches out a webbed hand, taps two knuckles once against the root-floor—*all plumb, all tight*—and slowly, without kicking up a single grain of silt, leans into the circular drift and lets the street carry it onward to the next archway.
