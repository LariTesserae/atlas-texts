For most of the day, and through the entirety of the night, you could thread half the narrow corridors of the stone forest and remain certain that whoever built the rigs died or climbed down years ago. 

The mistake is looking for motion at a lowland pace. Nothing with lungs can hurry under a violet sky and live past a second sunset. Up here, any sudden effort tears at the lining of the chest and fills the throat with thin, rusty froth. Survival requires adopting the cadence of the rock itself: long stretches of locked, crystalline stillness broken by brief intervals of deliberate work when the light demands it.

Tucked into the deepest of the dry-stone crescents on the western side of the nave—where three thick limestone fins overlap to block the direct noon glare while trapping a pocket of reflected afternoon warmth—lies what looks at first like a discarded bundle of hides wedged against the back wall. Only if you stand very close in the cobalt shadow, holding your own ragged breath so the rush of blood in your ears subsides, will you hear the other sound beneath the faint *tink* of popping ice. It is a slow, dry rasp, occurring perhaps five times a minute.

A person is curled on the flat gravel floor, knees drawn high toward the chin, folded inside a stiff shell of pelts taken from the pale, coarse-haired cliff-goats that graze the scree two vertical miles below the cloud deck. The hides are worn fur-inward; their outer leather has been rubbed over seasons with mutton tallow and fine limestone silt until it has cured into a grey, windproof rind almost indistinguishable from the frost-split slate overhead. 

Around the mouth and nose is lashed a thick pad of loose, unspun goat hair, crusted solid on the outside with rime. In air this dry, an uncovered mouth exhales half a pint of body water between dusk and dawn, and water up here costs heat to make. When the sleeper breathes out, the moisture catches and freezes immediately in the coarse fibers of the muzzle-pad; on the slow inhale, the cold dry air passes back through that lattice of frost, drawing a fraction of the moisture and warmth back into the throat. Periodically, a gloved thumb reaches up without waking the rest of the body, cracks a wafer of ice off the wool, and slips it between the teeth rather than let it sublimate into the breeze.

When the central sheet of beaten bronze begins its dull, arrhythmic *clack—clack* against the five leaning spears at the heart of the cleft, the bundle stirs.

They do not stand upright all at once. They unfold in stages, waiting between movements for their heavy blood to climb to the brain. Fully risen, they are short and startlingly broad through the middle—not with fat, which the altitude strips away in weeks, but with the permanently sprung, barrel-hooped ribcage of someone born high near the snowline who has spent half a lifetime climbing higher still. Even beneath layered pelts, the torso looks oversized for the spare, sinewy limbs, built almost entirely to house immense lungs and a slow, muscular heart the size of two fists.

You never see the whole face at once. From brow to cheekbone it is covered by a curved strip of dark horn scraped thin and pierced with two hairline horizontal slits—wide enough to show the footing in a thirty-inch corridor, narrow enough to keep the ferocious upward bounce-light off the corneas. Around the edges of this mask, where skin meets the reflected ultraviolet glare, the cheeks and the bridge of the nose are plastered with a greasy, chalk-yellow paste ground from tallow and the very sulfur-salts harvested from the bowls. Where that paste has cracked along the deep creases beside the eyes, the underlying skin is leathery, dark as smoked walnut, and netted with tiny burst capillaries. When the breathing-wool is pulled aside to test the air, the lips and gums underneath are not pink, nor blue with transient chill, but a deep, permanent mulberry-plum—the exact bruised violet of the zenith, darkened by blood so thick with red cells it flows like cold oil.

The bronze clacks faster. Over the open lip of the amphitheater, the first white tendrils of the thermal plume tear loose from the cloud floor and boil upward into the maze.

Within ninety seconds, sight becomes useless. The whiteout does not dim the cleft; it turns the air itself into a solid, glowing lamp that erases edges, distances, and shadows alike. A stranger caught out of shelter would freeze in place or step backward over the three-thousand-foot drop within ten paces. The salt-tender steps *out* of the stone crescent and straight into the blinding milk, moving sideways through the narrow gaps with a crabbed, sliding gait that never lifts either boot more than an inch off the scree.

Their boots are soft-soled envelopes of double hide stuffed with dried sedge from the lower valleys and wrapped round and round the instep with rough animal-hair cord, which bites securely into wet glare-ice where bare leather or iron nails would skitter. They do not look where they are going. One hand—wrapped in a fingerless mitten, the exposed fingertips clubbed wide like lizard-toes and stained permanently verdigris-green at the nail beds—trails lightly along the stone needles at shoulder height. Every spire in the labyrinth has its own tactile signature: three deep solution flutes wide as thumb-grooves on the sunward cheek, a perforation the size of a bowl at waist level, a rough flint nodule jutting out where dolomite meets a harder vein. 

By touch and by the muffled, deadened *clack* of the bronze vane orienting the center of the nave, the tender threads the whiteout while the rime grows. This is the hour for checking the dry rigs. While moisture loads the air, the brittle animal-hair cords slacken just slightly before freezing stiff again, allowing a stiffened thumb to nudge a river-pebble counterweight half an inch along its notch without snapping frayed fibers that would shatter like glass in the noon dryness.

Then the thermal chimney collapses. The mist drops away or vanishes into thin air; the bruised violet sky slams back down like a lid, and the sun hits the frosted labyrinth like a hammer blow.

For the next forty minutes, the cleft belongs to water, and the tender works with quiet, practiced economy.

Every limestone pillar has its own chemistry, known not by name or assay, but by taste and habit. As the newly grown feathers of rime hiss into steam or bead into clear drops that race down the solution flutes, the tender moves from station to station along a single corridor—one person has the lung-capacity to work perhaps six of the two dozen rigs in a given week, leaving the rest dormant under their flat anchor-stones like fallow fields. As the rime-load melts off the soapstone troughs, the river-pebble counterweights draw the cords taut again, tilting the carved channels by fractions of a degree so the brief trickle runs true rather than spilling down the rock face when the drip weakens.

At a fresh overhang where frost-shattering split off a pale flake of rock earlier in the spring, the tender catches a single sunlit bead on a thick, violet-gray knuckle and touches it to the tip of the tongue. Sweetish and puckering: alum-heavy, good only for the yellow leather-curing bowls. Three spires over, the runnel tastes fiercely bitter and metallic; beneath it, sitting in the sharp cobalt shadow where water freezes before it can splash, the hammered native-copper bowls are already furred round their inner rims with delicate crusts of pale sky-blue carbonate and sharp green vitriol, drawn out of the impure metal itself by the biting mineral load of the meltwater and concentrated day after day as the ice sublimates directly into the dry sky.

Where a shadow is creeping too fast toward a running flute, threatening to seal a trough in clear ice before the afternoon trickle finishes, the tender reaches into their furs, draws out a palm-sized plate of polished scrap-copper, and wedges it between two sunny needles opposite—throwing a single bright coin of reflected glare onto the lip of the soapstone for twenty crucial minutes longer, buying half a cup more brine before the blue shade swallows it.

There is no fire here. Even if brushwood were hauled up on an aching back through the clouds, thin air starves a flame down to a lazy, heatless orange lick that scarcely warms cold fingers. Instead, inside the pelts, slung squarely against the breastbone by a sinew loop around the neck, hangs a flat, kidney-shaped flask of thin hammered copper. Between tasks, the tender slips ice chips into its narrow neck; body heat melts them over hours into lukewarm water tainted faintly green by the vessel. For food, tucked into an inner pouch where skin-warmth keeps it just soft enough to score with an incisor, is a dense dark brick pounded together months ago in the lower pastures: rendered marrow-fat, parched barley meal, and dried goat blood, entirely without salt—because salt draws water out of the tissues, and up here thirst is the beginning of stupor.

Nobody lives in the cleft permanently; human marrow eventually forgets how to mend itself at this height, teeth loosen in their sockets, and cuts refuse to knit. Yet the cleft is rarely empty for more than the worst midwinter moons. They come up one at a time from a handful of stone villages clinging to the timberline far below the clouds—places where a single goat-skin pouch of pure sky-boiled blue crystal or cautery-salt trades for four winters' grain, sought by lowland smelters, corpse-washers, and dyers who have no other source for compounds that only precipitate where native copper, acid snowmelt, and fierce ultraviolet light meet in near-vacuum.

Only one in twenty from the high villages has the chest and the temperament for the nave. Those who do climb up in late spring, ascending through the blinding cloud-sea with forty pounds of fat-brick, spare hair-cord, and smooth river pebbles balanced on a pack-frame, and stay alone until the autumn gales begin tearing the rime-troughs loose.

What happens to a mind kept for four months at a time in thin, iron-tasting air is not madness, precisely, so much as a severe paring-down. Hypoxia makes complicated thoughts heavy and irritating to carry; silence and glare bleach away indoor habits. After the second week, the tender stops thinking in words altogether. Memory becomes wholly spatial and percussive. Time is measured by the *tink* of ice lenses detaching from the eastern fins at mid-morning, the twenty-minute whiteout, the slow sweep of cobalt wedges across the scree, and the subtle thickening of the blue rime inside bowl number four. 

When they do pause at dusk—after the horn scraper has shaved dry powdery ribbons of pale blue salt from an evaporated bowl into a stiff leather pouch, and the pouch has been pinned carefully back under its flat stone—they sometimes squat motionless at the edge of a shaded silt hollow before retreating to their gravel bed. In the sudden forty-degree plunge that follows the vanishing of the sun behind the amphitheater wall, they watch the salt-and-ice flowers extrude from the freezing mud: curled, translucent horns pushing silently upward out of dead silt, building intricate, bone-white gardens in the blue twilight that no eye below the clouds has ever seen, and that tomorrow’s noon shaft will collapse with a tiny sigh. Behind the dark horn slits, the bloodshot eyes watch this without superstition or loneliness—only the quiet recognition of one slow, mineral patience observing another.

And they are not entirely without predecessors.

At the very back of the tilted nave, where the enclosing walls of the massif overhang so deeply that neither direct sun nor afternoon mist ever penetrates, three narrow fissures run twenty feet back into permanent, twenty-below-zero shadow. If you peered inside once your eyes adjusted from the painful outside glare, you would find where the hands went that never returned for those bleached, wood-stiff pouches still pinned beside the older bowls out in the maze. 

When a tender’s thickened blood finally throws a clot during sleep, or an ankle snaps in a thirty-inch corridor three thousand feet above help, the body does not rot. There is nothing up here to eat it, not even mold or decay-bacteria capable of waking in dry permafrost. Whoever climbs up through the clouds the following summer simply carries the lightened body back into the dark rear fissures, props it neatly upright between the vertical dolomite ribs to save space, and leaves it wrapped in its grease-cured pelts. 

Over decades, sublimation has drawn every drop of moisture cleanly out of them through skin and wool alike, leaving small, weightless figures cured to the exact pale, fibrous stiffness of the freeze-dried pouches outside—horn masks still lashed across sunken cheeks, broad ribcages intact beneath stiffened leather, green-stained clubbed fingers folded over empty copper flasks. A newcomer checks their kit without ceremony or dread: untying a length of good animal-hair cord if a winter storm snapped a rigging, borrowing a river pebble of the right heft, leaving a pinch of fresh yellow crust on a dry leather knee in fair trade before turning back toward the glare, just as the afternoon mist spills over the lip and the bronze sheet high in the center begins to clack.
