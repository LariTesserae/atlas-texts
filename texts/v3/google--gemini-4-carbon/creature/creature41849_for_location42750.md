You do not hear them coming, of course. You feel them in your shins. 

If you press your bare heel against a primary load-bearing tendon—avoiding the loose, rattling whip-roots that skitter with the wind—you can pick out their gait through the endless, buzzing surf of the cicadas. A herd of grazers strikes the wood in a frantic, pattering scatter, like dry peas poured onto a drum. A heavy raptor landing three hundred yards off sends a single, dull *thump* down the grain. People feel different. Their step is a slow, rolling *thud-drag, thud-drag*, deliberate and cushioned, transferring weight from heel to outer edge to toe without ever snapping the knee straight. They walk the way sailors walk a pitching deck, except the deck has been pitching in slow motion for ten thousand years and the water is three miles of empty sky.

When they finally crest the silver curve of a ridge, moving single-file along the crest of a barge-thick root, they look at first like weathered knots detached from the wood itself. 

They are low-slung, broad-hipped people, compact and heavy in the calf and forearm, with skin burned by the unshaded high-altitude sun and the constant, reflective glare of the pale bark to the color of oiled saddle leather, dusted silvery-ash at the elbows and knees. Nobody stands fully upright in the open; to stand straight is to offer yourself to the canyon shear. They move in a permanent, fluid crouch, torsos canted forward at the hip, long arms hanging loose and slightly wide for balance. Their feet are bare—shoes would be suicide on slick, curving ironwood when the morning sweat rises—and the soles have spread over generations into wide, leathery pads, calloused half an inch thick and cracked along the flex-lines like dried river mud. Their big toes hook inward slightly, muscular and prehensile enough to pinch a thumb-thick twine of root without breaking stride.

Look at their faces, and the first thing that strikes you is the stillness around their mouths. They do not shout. In a five-mile wooden amphitheater vibrating at a hundred decibels, trying to out-scream ten billion plum-sized cicadas will tear your vocal cords to rags before you reach puberty. Their throats are quiet. Most adults keep their ears plugged entirely while out on the open weave, stuffing the canals with twist-wads of shed lizard skin greased with fat and yellow resin, leaving only a dark, waxy nub sticking out behind the tragus to pull them free. 

Instead, their hands never stop moving, and their eyes are crow-footed into tight, permanent squints behind smears of black charcoal-grease rubbed across the cheekbones to cut the blinding updraft glare.

Even at a dead walk across a six-inch span over nothing, a pair of them will carry on a brisk, dry conversation entirely with their fingers, forearms, and shoulders. It is a language built for three-mile sightlines and violent wind. Close up, talking about the grease-content of a batch of roasted beetles or whose turn it is to scrape the resin-poles, the signs are small, clipped flicks of the knuckles kept tight against the chest so as not to throw off their balance. Across a gap—say, from one sweeping structural bough to a hunting party crouched fifty yards below in the dizzying blue gulf—the grammar expands into crisp, angular semaphores of the whole arm, utilizing flashing paddles of white-bleached bone worn strapped to the wrists. Flip the wrist out: a bright, sun-catching flash against the blue abyss. Two flashes, a drop of the elbow, a fist struck twice against the thigh: *Herd turning left below the split; wait for the shadow.*

They do have voices, though they save them for the insides of the blisters. 

When five or six of them crawl headfirst through the low, ground-level lip of a wedged burl and pull a heavy mat of woven bark-sinew across the hole, the physical blow of the outside noise drops away. Inside, it is dim, smelling fiercely of old woodsmoke, sour tannin, roasted insect fat, and damp human hair. The walls are alive—warm, faintly damp to the touch, swollen into smooth, tumorous curves around the dark river-stones driven into the floor by someone’s great-grandmother. The whole chamber hums in a deep, chest-loosening baritone, vibrating sympathetically with the cicada-choir outside. 

Here, unplugging their ears with a wet *pop* and a sigh, the people murmur. Because the walls carry every vibration, they speak with their chins tucked down, voices pitched into a gravelly, resonant register that harmonizes rather than fights with the drone of the wood. A child who shrieks in a burl is immediately hushed with a firm palm over the mouth; high pitches bounce painfully inside the tight wooden dome. Instead, they hum, click their tongues, and speak in breathy, rhythmic bursts that sound remarkably like wind rubbing two heavy branches together. When they laugh, their shoulders shake violently, mouths wide, making almost no sound at all—just a sharp, hissing rush of air through the teeth and a rhythmic slapping of an open palm against the curved floor so everyone else in the room can feel the joke in their tailbones.

Everything they own, wear, and eat is negotiated with gravity and the living tree.

You cannot dig a pit here. You cannot drop a tool without watching it tumble downward through pale blue space, bouncing off a cross-bough two hundred feet down with a faint spark and vanishing into the sunlit haze forever. Consequently, everything is tethered. A person out on the root-plain wears a harness of braided grazer-hide slung low across the hips and ribs. From it hang short, waxed cords tied to bone-handled scrapers, horn-tipped chisels, pouches of grey hearth-clay, and heavy throwing-bolas weighted with priceless, smooth river pebbles. If a woman drops her adze while trimming a mist-pole, it falls three feet, jerks taut against her hip, and swings over the three-mile drop until she reels it back in without even looking down.

Children are tethered the same way until their sixth or seventh year. Before they can walk, babies are strapped snug against a parent’s chest or hip inside rigid, boat-shaped cradles woven from split cicada-wing and tough sinew—armored baskets, really, meant to take the shock if a parent has to drop flat to avoid a diving pelican-jawed raptor, or if a herd of panicked six-legged grazers comes clattering over the rise like a runaway rockslide. By four, the toddlers are let out on twenty-foot leashes tied to a central peg driven into a broad bough-junction. You will see three or four of them scrambling on all fours around the amber puddles after the morning mist, naked except for their harnesses and ear-plugs, quick as monkeys, snatching flat sap-colored lizards out of the crevices and biting the heads off before the tails stop whipping. If one slips through a gap in the weave, nobody gasps or scrambles. The nearby adults just watch the braided cord snap tight through the floorboards, wait three seconds for the kid to stop swinging in the open sky beneath them, and watch the small, brown hands come reaching back up through the hole to haul themselves onto the sunny wood, furious and embarrassed.

Fear of heights does not exist among them, not even as a concept to mock. What scares them is *rot*, and what scares them is *fire*.

Look closely at the forgotten adze by the cold hearth, and at the clay-lined scar on the wood. Clay does not belong three miles in the air on a living scaffolding of root. Neither do the dark, dense river-stones wedged into the huts, or the flint knives tucked into their belts. 

Somewhere down below—three miles straight down through the dizzying geometry of spaced, sweeping ironwood tendons, past the cloud-deck where the light turns green, down where the ancient granite dust finally settled or washed away—there is a bottom. None of the small bands moving across the sun-bleached valley live down there; they say the air at the bottom is thick as tallow, black as an unlit burl, wet enough to rot the soles right off your feet in a week, and full of slow, pale things with too many joints that climb up the lower trunks to feed on what falls from the sky. 

Yet once in a generation, or when a clan’s chisels have been ground down to nubs and their hearth-clay has blown away to grey powder, a party of young adults goes down the verticals. 

You can tell who has made the Drop. They are the older ones with the broad, pale keloid scars across their chests and inner thighs—burn-marks from lowering themselves down miles of curving, bark-plated pillars on thick sinew ropes, hanging suspended in empty space for days at a time, sleeping lashed into the undersides of solitary cross-boughs while the updrafts howl past their ears. Those who come back up—hauling skin-sacks of wet, heavy bottom-clay and river cobbles, taking weeks to climb out of the gloom back into the roaring, sunlit basin—walk with a particular weight among the clans. A three-pound wedge of black basalt is a family heirloom. When a camp packs up to follow the herds or let a section of scarred bark heal over, they leave the huts behind, leave the resin-poles standing, leave piles of cracked bug-shells, and sometimes even leave a worn wooden handle—but they knock the stones out of the minor splits and carry them slung in leather slings between two people, resting the weight on their collarbones. Without stone, you cannot split the ironwood to coax a new blister. Without clay, you cannot light a fire to boil resin or roast meat without setting the floor of the world alight.

Fire is handled with an almost suffocating ritual care. Before a spark is struck, two people haul up a gourd of amber tannin-water from the shady underside of the weave. A thick pad of wet bottom-clay, rimmed with scraped cicada-chitin, is packed onto a dead knot—never over thin weave, never near the dry, fibrous windward side of a ridge. They burn only dried grazer dung and the oily, hollowed thoraxes of the giant cicadas, which burn hot, fast, and smokeless once caught, leaving minimal ash. While the fire lives, someone sits with one bare foot resting directly on the wood six inches from the clay rim, feeling for heat creeping into the grain. When the meat is seared, the whole hearth is drowned, scraped clean, the damp clay rolled back into a ball and wrapped in reptile skin to keep its moisture, leaving only a faint black smudge for the morning mist to wash olive-green again.

They are nomads, not because the valley is poor, but because the floor is alive and easily irritated. 

Stay in one blister too long, drive too many poles into one junction, let too much human waste and grease soak into a single acre of the weave, and the great root-mass reacts. Over months, the pale silver wood flushes a permanent, angry bruise-purple. It stops drinking the mist. Instead, it begins to exude a thick, bitter, blisteringly caustic sap that smells like crushed ants and eats right through calloused skin, slowly swelling up to swallow stones, poles, and huts alike in smooth, impenetrable calluses of iron-hard wood. The tree heals them out.

So they move. Four or five families together—twenty people at most, so their combined footsteps don't spook every grazer within two miles—drifting in wide, multi-year loops around the five-mile bowl of the sky. 

If you wait hidden behind a row of resin-poles as the afternoon mist begins to rise through the gaps in the floor, turning the blinding daylight into a warm, luminous pearl-grey soup, you might see how they hunt. 

They do not chase the six-legged grazers; nothing on two legs can outrun hooks on hollow timber. Instead, they hunt *through* the floor. Half the band—usually the lighter, long-limbed teenagers and leanest adults—slips feet-first through a gap in the three-foot weave, vanishing beneath the ground. Underneath the sunny meadow of the upper canopy, the world is a shadowed, roaring catwalk of pale wooden undersides, thick encrusted mats of singing copper cicadas, hanging water-gourds, and fifty-yard drops to the next solitary tendon below. Clinging upside-down and sideways like spiders in braided harnesses, hands chalked with dried ash for grip, the under-hunters move silently along the belly of the floor, brushing waking cicadas off the bark in buzzing, furious sheets that fall away into the gulf. 

Up top, through the warm grey sweat rising out of the floorboards, two beaters walk out wide across the curve of the ridge and strike the wood with heavy stone mallets. 

*BOOM. BOOM-BOOM.* 

Through the roar of the insects, the shockwave travels straight down the tense grain of the ridge. Half a mile off, five hundred hound-sized grazers freeze, hooves clacking nervously, then wheel and bolt down the main structural highway—straight toward a narrow neck where three giant limbs fuse together. As the thundering, clattering herd pours across the gap above them, the hunters clinging beneath the weave thrust long, barbed bone-gaffs straight *up* through the narrow chinks in the floorboards, hooking the trailing animals cleanly by the hock or pastern and jerking downward, bracing their own feet against the rafters of the world. Above, in the mist, a grazer drops instantly onto its chin with a broken stride while the herd splits and vanishes into the grey; ten seconds later, three crouched figures emerge out of the fog on top, dispatching it with a quick thrust behind the shoulder before its thrashing draws the heavy raptors circling overhead.

Nothing is wasted, and what must be discarded is dropped through wide gaps far downwind of camp, watched intently as it plummets. Sometimes, before a gut-pile falls two hundred yards into the open blue, a shadow as wide as a hut folds its wings, drops out of the upper glare like a thrown spear, and takes the offal mid-air with a clap of leathery pinions you feel in your teeth. The people watch this with flat, professional approval, flicking two fingers toward the bird before turning back to their skinning knives. It is good to keep the big jaws fed away from the leashed children.

At dusk, when the sun drops behind the smooth, bone-curved western rim of the wood-mountains, the temperature falls twenty degrees in ten minutes, and the warm updrafts slacken. 

For about a quarter of an hour—just as the light goes violet and the gaps in the floor turn from pale blue to bottomless indigo—the cicadas stop. Not all at once, but in massive, cascading sections, ridge by ridge, cooling down for the night. The metallic surf recedes across the miles, echoing off the far timber walls one last time, and then cuts out. 

To an outsider, the sudden silence is terrifying; your ears ring violently with phantom static, and without the wall of noise pressing against your skull, you suddenly feel how thin the three-foot mat beneath you really is, and how unimaginably deep the dark air drops away beneath your heels. 

That fifteen-minute window of twilight quiet is when the smoke three ridges over goes out, and when the people stand up straight. 

All around the five-mile bowl, emerging onto the highest, smoothest knuckles of the bleached ridges before the night chill drives them into the blisters, small dark silhouettes rise out of their lifelong crouch. They pull the waxy plugs from their ears. Stretching their broad backs against the violet sky, tiny as splinters on the ribs of a vanished mountain, one band will cup their hands around their mouths and let out a single, long, wavering call—not words, just a clear, open-throated *oooo-ahhh* pitched low enough to roll across the empty gulf. Four seconds later, faint and pure across miles of darkened, hollow sky, another ridge answers, letting the neighbors know how many made it to dark, and which way the herds are sleeping, before the night-beetles wake up in the crevices and the wooden mountain begins to roar all over again.
