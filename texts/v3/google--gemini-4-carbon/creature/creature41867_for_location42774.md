You see their feet before you see their faces, because in the dim pewter light your eyes stay dropped to the slick, calcifying ledges where a misstep opens a shin to the bone on fresh basalt. 

Those feet are astonishing things: wide as paddles, splayed, and shoeless, because rawhide rots in three days if walked through warm mineral water and stone sandals would slide like oiled glass on the ochre bacterial slicks. From birth, the skin of their soles drinks dissolved silica and lime while simultaneously being abraded by rough sinter. By adolescence, a citizen’s heel and ball have cured into grey, horn-hard pads half an inch thick, deeply fissured like dry river-clay so they grip wet rock by suction and friction alike. Watching an elder come down a steep, steaming sluice-stair, toes gripping the sharp white travertine lips without looking, is like watching one of the pale gutter-crabs move. Every few days, sitting on the warm rims of the communal boiling-troughs, people take rough pumice blocks and grind their own soles flat again, blowing away the damp white dust of dead skin and stone that has fused into a single substance.

Above those heavy ankles, they go nearly naked. The air is thirty-seven degrees year-round, saturated to the point where sweat cannot evaporate; clothing is not warmth here, only a trap for fungus and rot. Instead of cloth—for there is no flax, no cotton, no wool-bearing beast—they wear grease. Every morning, from little squat pots of carved soapstone kept warm near the floor-vents, they rub themselves down with rendered rail-fat and pressed fish oil mixed with fine, grey thermal clay. It gives everyone in the basin a uniform, dull-pewter sheen, as if they were cast from wet lead. Water beads on their shoulders and runs off in quick, silver rivulets just as it runs down the basalt columns. When they must carry things, they wear harnesses and kilts of cured fish-skin, split bird-leather, or linked plates of flat snail-shell stitched together with sinew—materials that rattle softly against their thighs, though nobody hears the rattle under the roar of the northern cataract.

Hair is the only crop they harvest from themselves, and they treat it as industrial timber. Men, women, and children past their seventh year keep their scalps shaved to pale stubble with flakes of black volcanic glass—partly to deny purchase to the blind white midges that otherwise mat in damp locks, and partly because human hair does not rot in the wet. Every shorn lock goes into the ward’s hair-vats, washed in hot alkaline spring-water and twisted on drop-spindles weighted with pierced toad-skulls. A family’s wealth hangs in heavy, glistening black hanks from ceiling pegs: three generations of grandmothers’ and uncles’ hair braided into hoist-ropes thick as a wrist for levering basalt prisms, or spun thread-fine to net the scaleless fish, or woven with split swift-quills into stiff, indestructible sieve-baskets. To have thick, fast-growing hair is a civic virtue; baldness is a mild, lifelong embarrassment, compensated for by spending extra hours scraping guano on the high parapets.

Look at a face up close—which is the only way anyone here ever really looks at a face—and the basin’s permanent twilight and relentless noise have reshaped every expression. Their eyes are wide, dark, and unblinking, the pupils permanently dilated to drink in the shadowless slate glow, ringed with heavy lashes greased to catch mist droplets. Yet their mouths are strangely still, almost vestigial in public. Because shouting into the fused wall of waterfall-thunder and amphibian stridulation shreds the throat within an hour, an open mouth in the street looks to them either childish or deranged—additionally foolish because a gasped breath in the lower courts inhales a dozen midges. 

Instead, their faces have migrated into their collarbones, shoulders, and hands. 

Watch two sluice-wardens arguing at an aqueduct junction while bronze toads pulse deafeningly between their ankles. Their lips stay pressed in calm, greased lines, breathing evenly through flared nostrils. Between chest and chin, however, eight lean fingers and two broad, double-jointed thumbs flicker like striking lizards. The sign-tongue of the basin is compact—designed to be used in alleys barely two shoulders wide without striking stone—and intensely percussive to the eye. Because background stone is black basalt and white sinter, people stain the palms of their hands bright rust-red with sulfur-ochre skimmed from the bacterial pans, leaving the backs of their hands greased dark grey. As they sign, the red palms flash open and shut against the dimness like semaphore shutters: *flash-cut-turn-press*. Nuance lives in the eyebrows, the tilt of the shaved skull, the puffing or hollowing of the cheeks. Anger is stiff, wide-elbowed, slow; wit is tiny, rapid-fire flicking down near the ribs where outsiders cannot catch it; grief is hands held flat, grey backs outward, hiding the red palms entirely against the breastbone.

Voice is not absent; it has simply become intimate, almost erotic in its privacy, or else purely biological. You never hear another human voice until their nose is brushing the wet shell of your ear. Inside the low-linteled houses—dark vaults smelling densely of tallow lamps, wet chalk, and boiling crab—mothers press their lips directly against the soft spot behind an infant’s jaw so the child feels the hum through skull-bone before it ever parses pitch. Lovers speak mouth-to-throat, hands resting on each other’s ribs to read the vibration of vowels that drown three inches away in the ambient din. What vocal sounds they do make out on the terraces aren't speech at all, but sharp, piercing glottal clicks and two-finger whistles pitched precisely to slice into the narrow frequency gap above the bass roar of the cataract and just below the metallic trill of the millones of carapaces.

Because there is no night, no winter, no dawn, and no star, these people have no rhythm in their blood that comes from the sky. Ask one of them their age and the concept must be translated into stone. 

Time in the basin is a thickness. Everyone knows that an undisturbed sheet of warm spring-water lays down the breadth of a thumbnail of white travertine in what elsewhere would be called a year. Along the central piers of every neighborhood stand the Gauge-Stones: pristine hex-columns of black basalt over which a measured, unchanging trickle is diverted. An elder is "three fingers deep on the Seventh Pier." A marriage contract or a loan of obsidian cores runs "until the channel lip meets the peg." Even their bodies aren't synced to a twenty-four-hour pulse. Without darkness to herd the city to bed at once, sleep happens in rolling shifts. Walk down any narrow, steaming corridor at any moment and two-thirds of the city is awake—chipping risen footpaths flat with cobble mauls, harvesting snails, shifting sluice-blocks—while behind oiled bird-skin doorway flaps, the other third lies curled in the dry warmth around clay floor-vents, sleeping four hours at a stretch twice or thrice between stretches of labor, utterly deaf to a clamor their brains edited out in the womb.

This ceaseless, unseasonal steadiness breeds a peculiar mind: profoundly patient, anti-metaphorical, obsessed with flow and accretion, horrified by stagnation.

They have no gods in the clouds; the clouds are merely the grey stone lid of the world, sweating water back down so the springs don't empty. If anything approaches religion, it is Hydraulics. In a city that cements itself together by three millimeters a season and feeds on bacterial mats grown in running mineral water, a blocked channel is both famine and ruin. Divert too much water over a neighbor's roof and in ten moons the extra weight of solidifying sinter cracks his rib-bone vault; starve his wall of seepage and his dry-stacked basalt loosens, shifts, and spills his children into the fish-court below; let a warm pan stagnate and the rust-red slick dies, turning black and sour, killing the midges, starving the toads, driving the lizards away.

Consequently, the supreme authorities in the city are not warriors—for there is nowhere else to conquer and no metal for swords, though an obsidian-flaked butcher’s blade will take an arm off at the joint with terrifying ease—nor priests, nor hoarders of dried fish. They are the **Chisel-Masters** and **Weir-Keepers**, recognizable by the heavy necklaces of polished, resonant phonolite bars clacking softly against their greased collarbones, and by the pale, scarred knuckles of hands that have worked hot lime-water for forty thicknesses of stone. From raised platforms, holding horn mallets and bleached pelican-like rail-pelvis paddles, they conduct the city’s water the way an orchestra leader conducts sound. A triple ringing *CLACK-CLACK-CLACK* from an upper terrace phonolite slab cuts cleanly across half a mile of croaking din; down on three lower tiers, crews immediately lever slate gates into slots, sending fifty thousand gallons of warm, silica-heavy water sheeting down a newly stacked tenement row to begin welding it into the reef, while drying an older causeway just long enough for the maul-gangs to hammer away five years of blistered white crust before the street rises above the lintels.

Watch a household at their boiling-trough during a waking-meal and you see how seamlessly they sit inside the machinery of animals. Nobody flinches when a broad-headed, bead-scaled lizard the length of a forearm crawls across a toddler’s thigh to reach a warmer vent-stone; the toddler merely rests a fat, greased elbow on the reptile’s rough back as if it were a warm cushion, until the mother reaches down, checks the lizard’s belly with two practiced fingers to feel if it is gravid with leathery eggs, and lets it stay. Toads sit on the rims of clay stew-pots, snapping midges drawn to the red tallow-wick; only the largest, oldest bull-toads—whose bronze skins carry bitter alkaloids—are left strictly alone as pest-traps, while the plump yearlings have their hind legs skinned with a flick of volcanic glass and dropped straight into the geothermal broth alongside cracked channel-snails and flaky white chunks of whisker-fish. Nothing is wasted because nothing grows to replace waste. Fish-spines become needles; crab-claws become tongs for lifting scalding stones; crushed snail shells are calcined over the hottest dry vents to make caustic lime-paste for curing bird-skins; human waste and swift-guano are carefully sluiced down dedicated lower-tier channels into the vast, steaming turbid flats at the basin’s belly to fertilize the ochre bacterial meadows that feed the midges that feed the world.

And when a citizen dies, they do not burn them—there is no fuel—and they cannot bury them, because there is no dirt and chipping a grave out of raw basalt with stone hammers takes weeks.

Instead, they give them back to the water’s habit. 

High along the outer, steepest shelves of the basin, nearest the northern fault-line where the spray of the great cataract hangs thickest in the air and the mineral springs run hottest and richest with dissolved chalk, lie the **White Courts**. The dead are washed of their protective grease for the only time since birth. Their heads are shaved one final time for the family rope-chest. Then they are carried up the slick stairs on bone litters and laid on wide, stepped travertine shelves in the posture of sleep, knees drawn up, hands folded flat over the breastbone—red palms inward, hidden at last. A Weir-Keeper opens a dedicated warm runnel above the shelf so a thin, tepid film of mineral water sheets continuously over the body.

In the hot, alkaline rush, the small pale crabs and water-snails clean the frame to gleaming ivory within days, long before putrefaction can sour the air. And then the stone begins. Month by month, finger-breadth by finger-breadth, the dissolved silica and lime settle onto collarbone, rib, and eye-socket. Within a few thicknesses of the Gauge-Stones, the sharp white cage of skeleton softens, swells, blurs into crystalline bosses and smooth alabaster curtains, and fuses solidly into the terrace itself. 

Ten generations deep those shelves rise now: an immense, glistening white amphitheater of bone-cored stone facing the roaring black wall of the waterfall. Living grandchildren walk barefoot across the calcified shoulders of their ancestors to adjust the sluices, chipping flat a grandfather’s overly thick rib-ridge with a cobble maul when it threatens to divert a trickle the wrong way, entirely unsentimental, entirely at home—another layer of the reef holding up the terrace above, deaf and warm under the motionless pewter sky.
