Look past the clatter of the stilt-crawlers and the brassy, percussive booming of the gliders. Look down into the half-inch of sheeting water itself, where the runoff slides over the razor-sharp steps like clear oil. 

What appears at first to be a localized thickening of the current—a palm-sized lens of water refusing to obey gravity—is actually a flat, clear-bodied hunter. They have no pigment and almost no bone, only a cartilaginous rim shaped like a hydrofoil that presses flush against the oiled-glass silicate. By flexing their undersides, they turn themselves into living suction cups, immune to the constant wash that sweeps every loose scrap over the edge. When a mantle-glider snaps a radial crawler in half, sending pale, twitching legs skittering across the wet polygon, the water itself seems to detach and slide uphill. The clear-body glides over the debris, traps it beneath its bell, and dissolves the soft meat out of the glassy shell in minutes, spitting the empty, polished cylinder into the current to click-clack away over the precipice.

Everything that lives on the terrace lives on a clock measured in heartbeats, because the floor is rising. A creature that sleeps deeply here becomes a fossil before morning. 

Yet if you crouch near a milky quartz vein—careful not to slice your knees on a micro-riser—and wait for one of the canted rings to pulse its deep ultraviolet flash through the stone, you will see shadows trapped just beneath the surface. Not fossils. *Swimmers.* 

They look like flattened silverfish made of beaten tin, no longer than a thumb, and they have evolved entirely for the three-second window between gel and rock. Most of the time, they lie dormant inside hairline cooling-fractures, wedged tight in the dark. They do not listen for sound; they feel the subtle spatial tightening in their metallic lateral lines a fraction of a second before a plumb-bob fires. The instant the ambient light implodes and the glowing silicate gel precipitates overhead, they shoot out of their cracks and *into* the molten phase-state. For two, perhaps three seconds, before the rifle-shot crack of the crystal lattice locking shut, they thrash wildly through the incandescent slush, gulping down exotic, short-lived isotopes and unbonded ions that never survive to touch the air. Just as the stone snaps solid with a chorus of glassy *pings*, they breach the surface like flying fish, skittering madly across the scalding new crust in a cloud of steam to dive into the nearest newly formed tension-crack. Sometimes they misjudge by half a heartbeat. When the ultraviolet light travels under the terrace, you can see the losers: immaculate tin silhouettes suspended forever in clear quartz, curved mid-thrash, half an inch beneath the feet of the living.

Nothing here builds a nest, because there is nothing to build with and nowhere that stays still. To leave an egg on the stone is to see it cooked by the next precipitation sheet, washed into the plum-colored abyss, or rasped to paste by the diamond radulae of the grazers. 

So the terrace wears its nurseries in the air. 

Look closely at the four-foot acid-stalks—those whip-thin pioneers leaning into the warm updrafts, trailing their pale yellow microbial nets. Near the top of almost every stalk, just beneath the billowing membrane, hangs what looks like a knot of wet, gray twine. Separate the strands and you find six or seven tiny, spindle-shaped amphibians with oversized, lidless eyes the color of bruised plums. They have long, prehensile toes coated in a sticky mucus that resists even the stalk's own defensive acids. They never touch the ground until they are fully grown. Instead, they cling together in a tight, shivering braid four feet above the frantic violence of the stone, snatching up the fat, sluggish cloud-mites and airborne spores combed out of the fog by their host's yellow net. When a nearby tech-spindle fires and a wet wave of exothermic steam rolls upward, the whole knot of them uncurls at once, spreading thin, translucent throat-fans to drink the heat before balling tight again against the altitude chill.

Even the bulky, velvet-backed slugs huddled on the vertical risers—those pulsing, orange-and-violet islands of slow flesh—are not as stationary as they appear. Watch a cluster of them for twenty minutes and you realize the whole colony is rotating in slow motion, peeling one wet, muscular foot free at a time to climb steadily upward, millimeter by millimeter, staying perpetually one step ahead of the rising floor. 

And living *in* the deep, velvety pleats of their backs is an entire secondary tier of minuscule, hook-clawed mites, pale gold and blind. They feed on the sloughed velvet and the condensed mineral salts that crust on the slugs' skin. When the slugs flush warning-orange in synchronized waves, the mites respond to the sudden rush of subcutaneous blood-heat by standing straight up on their hind legs in the thousands, turning the slugs’ dark violet valleys into shimmering, golden fuzz for half a second before vanishing back into the folds.

Then there are the things that do not live *on* the terrace at all, yet belong exclusively to it—the reason nothing that washes off the edge ever reaches the unseen depths below.

Walk to the snapped perimeter. Stand right where the greenish-black silicate stops dead and the water pours off in a smooth, trembling curtain into the boiling purple vapor. Do not lean too far out. 

Hanging thirty feet down in the warm, vertical updrafts, invisible until a pulse of ultraviolet light spills over the cliff-lip, are the Drift-Maws. They are immense, radially symmetrical parachutes of pale, veined gristle, some twenty feet across, billowing upward like inverted jellyfish tethered to nothing. By subtly tilting the scalloped margins of their bells, they surf the continuous thermal column created by the mountain’s hot, artificial birth, holding station effortlessly just beneath the rim of the world. Their upward-facing mouths are wide, circular pits lined with inward-pointing bristles of flexible horn. They never hunt. They never fight. They simply hang open beneath the waterfall of this eighty-yard sky-island, catching the whole relentless harvest of the ledge: the shattered shells of radial crawlers, the dislodged acid-stalks whose disks were undercut by fresh gel, the clumsy young gliders knocked off balance in territorial brawls, and the occasional velvet slug that failed to climb fast enough when a step snapped sharp beneath its belly.

Only around the nine artifacts themselves does life hesitate, and then grow strange.

Where the air shimmers like underwater heat haze and the puddles lock into rigid, motionless ripples, the furious clatter of the oasis drops away to nothing. No glider lands within six feet of a suspended plumb-bob; the spatial distortion scrambles the fluid in their inner ears and sends them cartwheeling sideways across the slick rock, vomiting half-digested crawler shells. Even the frantic grazers skitter wide around the frozen geometry of the puddles.

Yet right at the boundary of that motionless water—half in the normal runoff, half pressed against the rigid, solid-seeming ripples—grow small, asymmetric clusters of something neither entirely animal nor entirely crystal. They look like tiny organ pipes spun from smoked glass and dark red cartilage, no taller than a finger joint. When the rest of the terrace screams with steam and castanet-clicking feet, these chime-polyps sit utterly still. They do not eat the stone, nor the microbes in the mist. Thin, root-like filaments of conductive nerve-tissue trail out of their bases and dip directly into the frozen puddles beneath the canted rings. 

When the twenty-second mark approaches and the space around an artifact begins to tighten, the chime-polyps lean inward toward the gray matter, trembling with ecstatic tension. At the silent implosion of ambient light, just before the gel appears, every red-veined pipe flares wide at the tip, drinking the raw phase-bleed straight out of the warped air—glowing briefly from within like tiny blown embers, feeding at the mouth of the forge itself before the thunderclap of new stone drowns them in steam.

And then there is the Visitor. Or perhaps the Inspector—though it has no tools, and no hands, and may only be an animal grown weirdly clever in the cracks of a machine.

There are never more than two of them on the eighty-yard green at once. Most of the time, they hang beneath the razor-sharp overhang of the outer drop, clinging upside-down to the dry, vertical cleavage planes out of the wind, folded up like long bundles of wet umbrella ribs wrapped in dark, pebbled oilskin. 

When one climbs onto the terrace, the mantle-gliders fall abruptly silent, deflating their brassy throat-bladders and shuffling sideways toward the cliff edge with nervous, wet slaps of their fins. The creature stands waist-high—precisely the height of the artifacts—balanced on three long, multi-jointed limbs arranged in a tripod, each ending not in claws or suction cups, but in broad, soft pads of dense micro-setae that grip wet, oiled glass through sheer van der Waals molecular attraction. It moves across the surgical steps with an eerie, liquid smoothness, its central body no larger than a melon, slung low beneath the high arch of its three knees. 

It has no eyes for the dim gray overcast. Instead, its entire upper carapace is a smooth dome of cloudy, milky quartz—stolen, dissolved, and biological re-precipitated over years of molting—that acts as a single wide-angle lens for the ultraviolet flashes below and the spatial distortions ahead. 

It does not hunt the crawlers, though it will casually pluck a velvet slug off a riser with a sudden, whip-like uncoiling of a pale, ribbon-flat proboscis, rolling the toxic creature in a puddle until it vomits its warm belly-water before tossing the limp body back onto the rock unharmed. What it actually wants is *prediction*. 

You can watch one stalk across the gleaming pewter mirrors between the rings, pausing every few paces to press one dry, seta-padded foot flat against a quartz vein, feeling the deep harmonic hum of the mountain's unfinished roots. Slowly, deliberately, it will walk to a completely bare, cold polygon of greenish-black stone yards away from any grazer herd, fold its three long legs beneath itself, and wait. Ten seconds later, twenty yards away, a canted ring shimmers—and the patch of stone *directly* in front of the tripod’s nose flashes into glowing gel. 

Before the rifle-shot crack has even finished echoing into the clouds, before the radial crawlers have even begun their frantic sprint across the flats, the tripod is already moving. Its ribbon-proboscis darts across the scalding, half-set crust like a calligrapher's brush, flicking up the tin-colored stone-swimmers the exact millisecond they breach the surface, plucking them out of the steam one after another—*snip, snip, snip*—five or six in two seconds, swallowing their hot, isotope-rich bodies whole before stepping primly backward onto cold rock just as the first wave of clicking, diamond-toothed grazers crashes in around its ankles.

It stands there for a moment amid the steam and the *scritch-scritch-scritch* of a hundred hungry mouths, its quartz dome glowing violet in the after-flash of the veins underfoot, utterly unbothered by the sheeting water sliding past its three molecular pads. Then, feeling some subtle shift in the tension of the shelf, it turns away from the frenzy, strides smoothly to the sheer, snapped edge of the village green, and steps straight off into the plum-colored void—catching the dry underside of the cliff three feet down without a sound, folding its long limbs tight against the newborn mountain to digest in the dark while the floor overhead climbs another half-inch toward the sky.
