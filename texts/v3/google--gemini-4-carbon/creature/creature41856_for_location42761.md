Stand beside one of the straight-edged gutters for twenty minutes, watching the warm, clear sheets of runoff slide past your boots, and you will notice a momentary glitch in the hydraulics.

Down in the beveled trough, a six-inch section of the rushing water stops moving. The rest of the current parts cleanly around it, throwing up a tiny, V-shaped bow wave against what looks like a submerged pane of glass. Then the pane lifts itself out of the sluice on four stiff, right-angled legs, shakes a mist of sweet-smelling droplets from its back, and walks up the ninety-degree curb onto the flat esplanade.

Up on the dark, wet horn of the paving, it is slightly easier to see, though only by its edges. It has no curves at all. Its body is a flat, articulated lozenge of clear, cartilaginous jelly—the exact refractive index of the sugar-water—reinforced internally by hair-thin struts of pale yellow lignin that meet in crisp, triangular trusses. It looks less like an animal than like a piece of architectural drafting instrument: a set-square or a crystal caliper that has worked itself loose from a drawing board.

Watch that single spot for another five minutes and you realize the plaza is not empty. Dozens of them are moving across the wet hexagons within thirty yards of you, detectable only by the faint, bright lines of sunlight catching their beveled margins and the tiny, rhythmic *tick-tick-tick* of their hardened tips striking the cellulose plate.

They are cold-blooded in the literal, physical sense: they have no internal furnace of their own, and if one were tossed thirty feet straight up into the true mountain air, it would stiffen and freeze before it hit the deck. Down in the thirty-foot greenhouse layer, though, bathed in the wet, forty-degree fever of the pavement, they move with rapid, clockwork precision. They do not eat in any way an outside creature would recognize; they have no mouths, no gut, no waste, which is why the water stays so relentlessly clean. Instead, as one pauses straddling a bright green seam of meristem between two hexagonal slabs, a needle-sharp proboscis folds down from the underside of its chassis and slips directly into the dividing tissue. For ten seconds the little caliper pulses faintly as pressurized sap—already filtered, warmed, and heavy with dissolved cane-sugars—pumps straight through its translucent vascular lattice and vents out a rear valve as a tiny puff of steam. It is an organism plugged directly into a municipal main.

Yet if that were all they did, they would be mere aphids on a gigantic stem, and the plateau would grow soft and shapeless. Look closer at what the caliper does *after* it drinks.

Before withdrawing its stylet from the green seam, it rocks forward on its front legs and runs two broad, flat palps along the growing edge of the slab, pressing down with surprising leverage. A microscopic curl of pale wax—a chemical stop-order—transfers from its palps onto the surging plant-flesh. Instantly, along that exact millimeter-straight line, the frantic outward push of the cells halts and begins to cross-link into hard horn. Three inches away, a second caliper is doing the same thing on the perpendicular seam, and a third is walking the mitered corner between them, trimming away an errant blister of soft cream-colored wood with a pair of scissor-like mandibles and flicking the shaving into the gutter, where a tiny flap immediately opens to swallow and dissolve it.

The ninety-degree curbs are not in the plant’s nature. Left to itself, the colossal, nameless root-mass filling this two-thousand-meter crater would be nothing more than a boiling, shapeless blister of green callus, heaving blindly above the timberline on geothermal water and mountain sun. The geometry belongs to the inhabitants—except that the inhabitants are only half on the surface.

Drop to your knees beside a newly rising plinth, press your ear through the thin film of warm runoff against the feverish mahogany plate, and listen past the heavy *thud-clack* of the deep check-valves. Just beneath the horn skin, inside the parallel xylem conduits and the flooded, resonant chambers of the foundation, something much larger is sliding through the dark.

There is an entire pressurized, subterranean canal-system beneath the deck, miles of smooth-walled, square-bored galleries flooded with hot, rushing sap, and the things that live down there have never seen the pale-blue mountain sky. You only glimpse them during a catastrophic failure.

Walk out toward the eastern flat where that cantilevered aqueduct is still groaning under its own overextended weight, or wait by one of the solitary staircases just before it splits down the middle. When a tension cable finally parts—in that brief, violent second after the *crack* of tearing heartwood, before the scalding spray clears and the digestive tendrils surge up from the floor—look directly into the ruptured, octagonal beam-ends.

Coiled inside the broken wooden pipes, gripping the ribbed interior walls with rows of flat, suction-cupped plates, are pale, ribbon-bodied worms as thick as a man’s forearm and twenty feet long. They are blind, milk-white, and segmented like flexible wooden rulers. The crash has caught three of them in the open air. They do not thrash in panic; they recoil with smooth, muscular coordination back down into the standing pier, pulling behind them a tangle of fine, silver-white nerve-threads that were, until a moment ago, spliced directly into the growing collar of the arch.

These are the Valve-Keepers, the deep-water caste, though *caste* implies a single species and a single plan, and what lives in this basin is neither.

What lives here is an argument.

Long ago—perhaps when this high valley was still cold granite and scree around a cluster of warm mineral vents—a mat of hyper-metabolic alpine flora and a lineage of segmented, fluid-dwelling worms closed their life cycles around one another so tightly that the outside world was crowded out completely. Every spore, beetle, moss-speck, or wind-blown seed that drops out of the cold sky onto this wet slate is dissolved within seconds by the deck’s immune-tendrils and turned into clear fuel; birds that dip below the thirty-foot thermal boundary into the rising plumes of thin, superheated steam lose their lift, strike the slick masonry, and vanish beneath the hinged flaps before a feather can drift down a sluice. The plateau keeps itself sterile because it cannot afford interference.

Down in the dark, flooded galleries beneath the paving, millions of those heavy white ribbons hang suspended in the rushing, sweet-smelling currents. Their bodies act as living slide-valves and variable baffles. By inflating their muscular segments to block one conduit and collapsing flat to open another, a knot of fifty worms can redirect thousands of gallons of turgor pressure into a single forty-yard square of the surface deck. Why do they do it? For territory, for mating-chambers, for cooler eddies or richer sugar-streams—for reasons that are entirely dark, hydraulic, and three-dimensional. When two rival knots of Valve-Keepers wrestle for dominance inside a subterranean manifold, shunting immense rams of boiling sap back and forth to flush each other out of a favored junction, the surface above them erupts.

A staircase shoots forty feet into the clear afternoon air because a colony down in the dark needed a pressure-relief chimney to keep their brood-gallery from bursting. An amphitheater rises in knife-sharp, mucilage-slicked tiers because twenty concentric rings of deep worms are pulsing in slow, rhythmic synchrony around a central upwelling, each pulse driving a step of pale cream tissue into the sun.

And the moment that raw, feverish mass breaks the plane of the deck, the surface calipers—which are not a separate animal at all, it turns out, if you watch one of the sunken square pools long enough, only the crisp, sunlit, short-lived adult phase of the very same worms—swarm over the rising bulk in transparent thousands.

Look into that sunken pool left behind by the collapsed amphitheater, now brimming with hot, glass-clear water from its square-mouthed spouts. Down on its submerged bottom, a trapdoor has unlatched from the plumbing below. Half a dozen small, juvenile ribbons, no thicker than shoelaces, have swum up into the blinding daylight. Already the ultraviolet glare of two thousand meters is working on them: their milk-white skins are clearing like boiling gelatin, their soft rings are stiffening and creasing along sharp geometric folds, and stiff, yellow-strutted legs are unfolding from their flanks like pocketknives opening. Within an hour they will haul themselves up the vertical wall of the pool, completely dry-edged and faceted, driven by a single, furious, baked-in compulsion that is the exact opposite of their parents' hydraulic sprawling: *flatten, square off, seal, drain.*

To the tiny, crystalline walkers on the deck, any raw protrusion is a wound that lets the cold mountain wind catch hold and chill the pavement. Left unchecked, rough towers would create turbulence, break up the shallow blanket of hot air, invite frost, and kill the whole basin in a single winter night. So the moment a fresh blister of plant-flesh heaves up from below, legions of invisible, sunlit draftsmen rush onto its soft cream flanks. Running along taut guidelines of spun wax, snapping tactile signals through the wood with their hard heels, they plane its curves into octagons, bevel its risers to shed water at maximum speed, cut slit-valves to vent excess steam, and try desperately to cap it off into a flat, safe roof.

Meanwhile, twenty feet below, the blind white giants in the pipes feel the back-pressure of that capping, interpret it as a blockage, and throw open three more subterranean rams to punch right through it.

That is why the cantilevered aqueduct outgrew its counterweight. Beneath its eastern piers, a massive braid of deep ribbons was shoving forward with every ounce of exothermic sugar-burn the surrounding quarter-mile of floor could generate, trying to extend their pressurized territory across the flat; up on the leading green collar, ten thousand glittering, thumb-sized calipers were frantically squaring the extrusion into octagonal beams, bracing it with trusses, trying to make an impossible surge structurally sound enough not to tear open their warm world—until the math failed, the tension cable snapped, and both halves of the creature pulled back to let the floor clean up the mistake.

If you stay until late afternoon, when the sun drops toward the jagged stone peaks beyond the rim and long, blue-black shadows stretch for miles across the wet slate, you finally get to see them clearly.

Low-angle light transforms the plateau. Suddenly, the empty esplanades ignite with millions of tiny, prismatic sparks. Every moving bevel, every crystalline leg and clear, lozenge-shaped back catches the horizontal orange rays like spilled crushed diamond. You can see them massed in shimmering drifts along the outer parapets at the basin's edge, stacked three and four deep on the wind-baffles where the freezing mountain gales slam hardest against the mahogany bulwarks. Out there on the cold boundary, the little draftsmen die by the thousands every evening; as the shadow of the ridge hits them, their sap-joints stiffen, their grip fails, and instead of tumbling into the abyss, they lock their legs together along the ninety-degree eaves, letting the plant-flesh surge over their stalled, glassy bodies and laminate them straight into the wall as extra reinforcement against the night.

Walk back inward from the rim before full dark, though, because the night shift changes the rules on the deck.

Once the sun vanishes and the sky turns the hard, dry indigo of high altitude, the radiative cooling is immense. A ceiling of thick white fog forms instantly at the thirty-foot boundary—a flat, solid-looking lid of cloud resting directly on the tops of the taller arches and tripod-chimneys, sealing the valley in like the cover on a simmering pot. Beneath that low, glowing roof of mist, the heat actually rises, trapped and dense, smelling overwhelmingly of hot cane-syrup and green wood.

With the ultraviolet glare gone, the surface flaps do not merely open to swallow debris; wide, rectangular sluice-gates unlatch along the major parallel gutters. Up out of the flooded basement, into the warm, pitch-black steam-room of the covered plaza, slide the great white ribbons themselves—hundreds of yards of pale, blind muscle hauling half their length onto the slick hexagons to mate, swap chemical packets, and test the surface grades with broad sweeps of their eyeless heads. As they glide across the wet masonry, millions of tiny, clicking crystal-walkers climb onto their slick backs, scraping away old cuticle, splicing fresh nerve-threads, and grooming the very parents whose architectures they spent all afternoon trying to curb.

By dawn, when the first cold, ordinary ray of sunlight slices over the mountain rim and burns the fog-lid away in tattered ribbons, the big valves will have slipped back down into the dark conduits and latched the gates shut behind them. Half the monuments you mapped yesterday will be gone, melted down into smooth esplanades; half a mile north, a brand-new colonnade of squared yellow pillars will be steaming in the crisp morning light, rising an inch every four minutes, covered in a nearly invisible sheen of living glass calipers clicking furiously along its edges—forever correcting, squaring, and balancing a pulse from the dark that refuses to stop rising.
