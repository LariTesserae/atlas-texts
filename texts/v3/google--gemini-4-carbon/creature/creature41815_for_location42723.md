At first, if you were somehow folded small enough to crouch on the razor-sharp diamonds of the floor grating, you would swear you were alone with the liverwort. The promptness of the cold and the sheer, crowding density of the ironwork leave no room for a body, and certainly no room for a life. 

You would watch the four droplets from the horsetails strike the sump. You would watch the rainbow film of cutting-oil split into concentric rings of violet and brass, shiver against the galvanized walls of the basin, and slowly draw back together to seal the black water underneath.

Except the oil does not draw back together evenly. 

Near the base of a sweat-slicked return pipe, a patch of the rainbow slick thickens, pulls against the flat geometry of surface tension, and climbs out of the water.

It does not climb like a worm or a snail; there is no muscular ripple, no peristalsis. It moves upward along the zinc-plated steel the way solder wicks into a heated copper joint—a smooth, silent, capillary draw. For an inch or two it looks merely like a dark, iridescent grease-stain defying gravity, fringed with microscopic bristles of black magnetite swarf it has gathered from the sump. Then it reaches the first bolted flange, gathers itself into a bead the size of a walnut, and *clicks*.

Inside the translucent, amber-black jelly of its mass, three tiny helical curls of bright steel—leftovers from the tapping of the screw-holes overhead—rotate into alignment. They interlock like a makeshift planetary gear, brace against the hex-nut of the flange, and lever the bulk of the creature upward onto the braided copper grounding strap.

There is no beast with legs or lungs here, because legs require corridors and lungs require an atmosphere meant for squandering. What lives in this cistern is something native to tolerances—an interstitial inhabitant, born in the narrow gap between mineral chemistry and machine-shop physics, thriving in the exact space where gloved hands were never meant to fit.

Follow the grounding strap upward into the cross-hatched gloom behind the manifolds, and you begin to see more of it—or more of *them*, though the boundary between an individual and a colony is as fluid here as heavy gear-oil. 

They might be called the Gaugers, though there is no one down here to name them, and they do not read the dials. They do not need to. 

Resting in the two-inch clearance between a cast-iron handwheel and the solid steel bulkhead it faces is a larger specimen, stretched flat as a carpenter’s shim. In the oxblood glow of a nearby pilot jewel, its substance looks like clear, dense silicone grease marbled with colloidal silver and pale threads of living fungal hypha—a quiet theft from the spores that brought the moss. Suspended within that chill, viscous membrane are dozens of scavenged industrial crumbs: stray brass filings, a flake of white porcelain glaze, minute spheres of wept lead from the pipe-joints, and crisp curls of high-carbon swarf. 

The creature is draped across the gap between the hub of the handwheel and the cold hull-plate. Every few minutes, when the hidden internal ratchet ticks and advances the valve stem by a fraction of a degree, the creature undergoes a silent, crystalline stiffening. You can see the shear-stress travel through its translucent body as a brief, milky opalescence—a wave of polarized light, like stress lines seen through a machinist’s test-lens. 

That mechanical strain is its meal. Whether piezoelectric, triboelectric, or some stranger galvanic trickle harvested between zinc plating and damp cast iron, the creature drinks the torque. As the ratchet settles, the milky cloudiness fades back to clear amber, and a tiny, bubbleless pulse moves through its core, echoing the rhythmic rise and fall of the fluids in the sight-glasses beside it.

They are creatures of pure maintenance and quiet parasitism, utterly adapted to a world of pristine, unrusted parts. Look closely at the gauges that face blindly into the backs of the I-beams, half an inch from the web of the steel. Pressed directly against the beveled glass of a pressure dial is a disk of clear, firm gel, clear as an optical lens. Fine, branching filaments—half metallic dendrite, half pale rootlet—spread from its rim to grip the knurled brass bezel. As the needle beneath the glass trembles against the sixty-cycle hum of the room, the creature’s body vibrates in microscopic sympathy, warm by perhaps a tenth of a degree against the freezing air. It has lived its entire slow life in that half-inch cleft, bathed in the radium-green faintly painted on the numerals, feeling the needle twitch like a heartbeat beneath its belly.

When the relief valve sighs overhead and the three-minute weather begins, the whole population shifts into a different phase.

As the warm, damp exhaust rolls down through the labyrinth of armored hoses and the amber lights blur into fog-halos, the chill stiffness leaves their bodies. Their viscosity drops. Along the undersides of the step-down transformers—where the sixty-cycle hum is thickest and the baked enamel is almost lukewarm—sheets of the glistening, silvery-amber film loosen their grip and begin to travel.

They glide through the tightest clearances with the slow, deliberate elegance of mercury navigating a maze. One slips seamlessly through the crescent-shaped shadow between two touching hydraulic hoses; another threads itself directly along the helical groove of a greased worm-gear, riding the *tuck-tuck-tuck* of the ceiling gantry’s drive train without ever being crushed, flowing out of the path of the bronze gear-teeth a millimeter before contact and closing back over the freshly compressed grease behind them.

They are fastidious. Rust would ruin their conductivity; dust would foul their slick, frictionless skins. As the ninety-second metallic rain patters down through the pipes, the creatures spread wide to catch the fresh, mineral-free condensate, flushing excess salts from their membranes. Wherever a stray fleck of gray mastic has fallen from the ceiling gantry onto a polished hydraulic ram, two or three smaller beads converge on it, envelop the grit, carry it to the edge of a flange, and drop it neatly through the grating into the sump below—*plink*—keeping the chrome mirror-bright so the seals will never score.

Their relationship with the sparse green life of the chamber is neither predatory nor wholly benign; it is the careful, measured intimacy of a machinist tending a wick-oiler. 

Near the warm solenoid block, where the miniature horsetails lean toward the amber jewel-light, a broad, ribbon-like individual lies coiled around the damp mineral-wool lagging at their roots. Its body is richly veined with copper-bright filaments it has drawn over months from the grounding straps. By shifting those internal metallic threads closer to or farther from the humming solenoid housing, the creature acts as a living induction coil—warming itself with eddy currents just enough to keep the freezing air from killing the pale stems above it. In return, as the horsetails and the emerald-glass liverwort respire in their tiny, rationed pool of orange light, microscopic traces of sugars and organic acids leach down into the damp wool, where the creature absorbs them to repair its tough, rubbery skin.

Near the fist-sized cushion of silvery moss on the lower flange, a smaller, paler variant—almost solid in the near-freezing cold, more like chilled tallow or vulcanized caulk—forms a low, neat retaining wall around the frost-crisped skirt of the plant, guiding the slow drip of mineral condensate directly into the moss’s soft, lit core so not a drop splashes wasted onto the dry zinc floor.

Nothing hurries. Nothing makes a sound of its own. They have no eyes to see the yellow-and-black warning stripes, though they avoid the yellow paint slightly because the cadmium pigment tastes wrong to their edges; they have no ears to hear the groan of the hull-plates settling against the deep rock outside, though every time the great curved steel skin shifts under the pressure of the earth’s crust, every individual in the cistern freezes simultaneously, flashing briefly opaque white in the dimness like a constellation of tiny, startled moons before relaxing back into clear grease and shadow.

High near the crown of the chamber, the radial gantry clicks forward another notch, pressing a fresh white porcelain tile into its bed of wet gray mastic. 

Right behind the mechanical arm, trailing it by less than four inches, moves the oldest and largest of them. It is the size of two open hands, though never shaped like hands—just now it is molded cleanly into the concave fillet where the curved wall meets the newly tiled ceiling, looking for all the world like a bead of translucent, silver-flecked sealant applied by an obsessive craftsman. Inside its slow, clear mass, dozens of bright steel swarf-curls turn lazily over one another, catching the dull orange glow of a caged bulkhead lamp like tiny clock-springs adrift in glycerine. 

It presses its cool, slick body along the fresh seam between two porcelain plates, smoothing a microscopic ridge of wet mastic, absorbing the faint chemical warmth of the curing cement, tasting the brand-new glaze. It has been following this gantry at three inches an hour since the first tile was laid. When the last seam is sealed—next week, or next year—and the gantry motor finally clicks against its limit-switch and goes still, the creature will simply flow downward along a conduit, settle into the warm, humming crease behind a transformer box, and keep its quiet watch over the tightening bolts, waiting to see what the room decides to become next.
