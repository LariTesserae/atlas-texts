Look straight at a limestone pier for twenty minutes in that shadowless glare, letting your eyes water against the blast of alkali air until your vision adapts to the micro-relief of silver-grey on bone-white, and one of the stones will un-wedge itself.

It does not crawl out of a crack; there is nothing soft enough here to crawl. Rather, a flat, rounded slab the size of two open hands—a piece of what you took to be the smooth, soap-worn face of a corbelled arch—draws its margins inward by a fraction of an inch, breaks the dry friction holding it between two courses of rock, and slides sideways across the face of the pillar with the dry, muted *shhhk* of chalk drawn across slate. Even that sound is instantly obliterated by the roaring chord of the flues.

There are four of them in the twelve-foot pocket. There have only ever been four, or perhaps three and two halves, depending on how one counts the slow, centuries-long budding by which an older individual splits along a cleavage plane when it grows too wide for the wind-shadows.

To call them animals feels imprecise, though they have muscle of a sort, and intent, and a slow, deliberate circuit they make through the packed architecture. They are built on the principle of the *chiton* or the limpet, if a limpet were forged out of porcelain, horn, and dry tendon, and taught to live in a sandblaster. From above—or rather, from the front, as they cling vertically to the piers—each is an oval shield of dense, laminated calcium carbonate and bound gypsum, indistinguishable in color and polish from the surrounding limestone. The blast has worked on their backs just as it works on the masonry: their shells are subtly grooved and parabolic, sculpted by the exact same drafts they inhabit, so that when one settles into a leeward scoop on a buttress, it completes the aerodynamics of the stone. The howling wind slips over it without a hitch. Rather than fighting the gale, its convex back uses the rushing air to press itself tighter against the rock, pinned fast by aerodynamic lift in reverse.

Beneath that heavy, pale carapace, there is no wet foot—mucus would freeze dry in seconds, and soft flesh would be flayed by the salt-dust. Instead, when the creature shifts, you catch a glimpse of a dense skirt of overlapping, slate-grey scales, finer than mica, ringing its underside. Inside that sealed gasket are hundreds of short, stiff, horn-plated struts, arranged in tight concentric rows like the teeth of an iron comb. Each strut ends in a microscopic, hooked spatula of black chert—mineral gathered from the blown dust and secreted into the tips of its limbs. 

It walks by stone-mason’s physics. Half the struts hook into the microscopic grain and pit-marks of the dressed limestone, pulling the shell forward a millimeter; the other half reach ahead, find purchase in a hairline seam or the edge of an upright floor-flag, and lock. Its musculature is nearly anhydrous—bundles of tough, collagenous fiber as dry as cured rawhide, actuated not by pumping blood, which would require water it does not have, but by slow, electrostatic ratcheting and dense, oily lipids that never evaporate. It cannot fall. Even if pried loose with a crowbar—an effort that would snap steel before the creature’s grip failed—the tight spacing of the piers means it would only drop six inches before wedging neatly between two opposing faces of stone.

It has no eyes. In a room where every surface reflects blinding light at every angle, where nothing ever approaches from a distance and no shadow ever heralds movement, vision is a useless expenditure. Instead, it reads the chord.

The creature’s shell is an ear. Pressed flat against the vibrating piers, its dense, crystalline body conducts the immense, invariant roar of the room directly into a labyrinth of dry, taut membranes strung across the underside of its carapace. To an outsider, the sound in the cistern is a single, deafening wall of noise. To the *ashlar-back*—for lack of a name—that chord is an intricate, three-dimensional map of standing waves, acoustic shadows, and vibrating stone courses. Every block in the dry-stack has its own resonant tension under the colossal weight of the buried walls. A pier near the center hums a slightly flatter pitch than a cantilever near the outer flues; a seam choked with pewter-stem vibrates differently than a bare joint. When one of the other three individuals shifts its weight two pillars away, the microscopic change in mass alters the resonance of the dry-stacked stone beneath it, sending a faint harmonic tremor through the tight-wedged masonry, down through the upright book-packed flagstones of the floor, and straight into the listening belly of its neighbor. They know exactly where the others are at every instant, felt as slight adjustments in the tuning of their world.

They are the reason the flora stays flat. 

Follow one for six or seven hours—wedge yourself sideways between two smooth ribs until your ribs ache and your lips split from the cold, roaring dryness—and you will see it feed. It slides at the pace of a clock’s hour hand along the curve of a buttress until its leading edge meets a grey-green rosette of the sharkskin scrub, or one of the rigid, pewter-colored stems bridging a gap. It does not bite; jaws would open its interior to the desiccating blast. It simply glides its heavy margin *over* the edge of the woody growth, clamps its mica-scaled skirt down tight against the surrounding rock to seal out the wind, and lowers a broad, flat rasp of flint-hardened plates from its center.

For three hours it sits motionless, looking entirely like a misplaced cornice-stone. Beneath the seal of its shell, in total acoustic and atmospheric isolation from the gale millimeter away, it files away two millimeters of calcified bark and scaly leaf. Inside that sealed pocket, its slow metabolism cracks the dense resins and extracts the bound water molecules that the plant stripped from the night air over the preceding decade. It turns woody cellulose and juniper-scented sap directly into lipid, horn, and shell-paste. Nothing is wasted. There are no droppings because there is nothing wet to expel, and no indigestible bulk left unchecked; what little mineral ash remains—pure silica, excess alkali, ground limestone—is mixed with a microscopic trace of waxy secretion and extruded not onto the floor, where the wind would tear it away, but *into the joints*.

Look closely at the mortarless seams where the creature has just rested. Deep between two pale slabs, tucked behind the wind-baffle of a stone lip, is a fresh, neat bead of pale grey mineral cement, hard as porcelain within minutes of touching the air. Over millennia, while the wind has scoured the exterior faces of the piers smooth, these four slow-moving masons have been unconsciously grouting the deepest, finest hairline fractures of their own sky, packing their dry, calcified waste into the microscopic voids where stone meets stone, reinforcing the grip of the pewter-stems’ roots, keeping the compression true.

Sometimes, two of them meet on the narrow saddle of a low corbelled arch where the pillars nearly kiss.

There is no fight—territoriality makes no sense when the entire universe is nine feet by twelve and its larder grows by a tenth of an inch a century. Nor is there any hurried mating. When their beveled edges touch, click-solid like two curling stones meeting on ice, they halt. They align their margins so the two ovals form a single, longer aerodynamic streamlined blister on the face of the arch, splitting the screaming draft cleanly around them both. Beneath the double-sealed rim where their skirts overlap, safe from the glare and the desiccation, they open tiny, valve-like spiracles and exchange threads of dense, waxy genetic packet, dry as pollen. They may stay locked like that for forty days, immovable as keystones, humming internally at the exact frequency of the limestone arch supporting them, while the blinding silver light pours down from the collar-gap without dimming—even through the brief outside nights, it seems, the pale stone holds a cold phosphorescence or catches high, cold starlight off the salt-pan, though in truth when darkness does drop like a guillotine at sunset, nothing in their behavior changes at all. Light and dark are irrelevant to stone that listens.

Near the base of the easternmost pier, wedged upright among the vertical flagstones of the floor, there is a fifth shell. 

It died perhaps four hundred years ago, or two thousand. Because its hooked chert struts locked automatically as its dry fibers cured into solid horn at the end, it never let go of the wall. And because it sits in a tight, screaming venturi between two low buttresses, the gypsum-laden blast has continued to work on it. Half its thickness has been planed away, revealing the exquisite, nacre-dense laminations of its interior architecture—hundreds of paper-thin layers of silver and chalk-white mineral laid down one dry year at a time, tighter than bog-oak rings. A wire-thin stem of pewter-brush has grown right across its worn back, bracing itself against the dead shell’s beveled edge to span the gap to the next stone over. The shell holds the plant; the plant breaks the wind just enough for a tiny grey-green rosette to root in the socket where the creature’s rasping mouth once pressed the rock; and thirty inches higher up the same pier, a living descendant—smooth, pale, deafeningly resonant, and broad as a platter—inches down the groove at a millimeter a minute, coming around on its multi-year circuit to rasp the edge of that very rosette.

If you reach out a gloved hand and press your knuckles hard against the back of a living one, it does not flinch. It feels precisely as cold as the limestone around it. Beneath your skin, you feel only the massive, singing vibration of the buried walls—and then, if you hold very still for ten minutes until your arm goes numb in the chill draft, a single, minute *tick* beneath your palm, like a watch-gear slipping one tooth, as it tightens its hooks into the rock and waits out your soft, wet, transient presence.
