To see the switchmen, you must wait for the cherry-red dark. 

During the long minutes of the dusty gold light, while the resin-engines hammer and the walls roar with the sound of shifting gravel, the narrow iron shanties remain sealed. Their doors—heavy, ship-style bulkheads with wheel-dogs rusted into a permanent half-turn—stay shut against the constant hailstone clatter of falling shells. Through the thick, grease-yellowed glass of the portholes, there is only an occasional, ambiguous movement: a pale blur passing close to the pane, or the dull glint of brass spectacles catching the sodium gloom. 

Then the dynamo whines up to pitch. The voltage drops, the amber lamps bleed down to the color of cooling iron, and the great arthropod crust freezes in mid-stride. 

In that sudden, breathless quiet—when only the *thump-hiss* of the pistons remains—the bulkhead doors unlatch. 

They step out onto the iron landings three or four at a time, moving with a brisk, terrifying economy. They are human, or are descended from humans who took this posting when the roots were still merely invading the masonry rather than replacing it, though centuries of sealed, pressurized husbandry have pared them down to essentials. They are short, compact, and profoundly barrel-chested, built to haul heavy wrenches in thick air heavy with vaporized turpentine and carbon. Their skin has the damp, tallowy translucence of cellar-sprouts, mapped at the temples and wrists with fine, dark-blue veins that sit very close to the surface. 

Because the ambient roar of the chamber long ago ruined the utility of the human voice—and would have burst unprotected eardrums in infancy—their ears are plugged from childhood with fitted stoppers of vulcanized tree-rubber and beeswax, grown over now with a protective, calloused ridge of pale scar tissue. They do not speak, and they do not startle when a late-dropping isopod the size of a cobblestones strikes the iron railing an inch from their fingers. They feel the room entirely through the soles of their boots and the palms of their hands.

To walk the catwalks during the living hours requires dressing like the architecture. They wear heavy, high-collared aprons and gaiters stitched not from cloth—which would rot to slime in a week—but from cured, overlapping plates of shed predator-chitin, riveted together with copper wire and slick with machine tallow. Over their shoulders they sling heavy leather satchels clinking with brass shims, scraping-irons, and pots of caustic lime-paste.

They have perhaps forty seconds before the whistle blows. 

Watch a pair of them work the third-tier landing. They do not walk so much as slide along the grease-slick iron, their hobnailed boots gripping the velvet rust, knees slightly bent to drink the rhythm of the flywheel pit fifty feet below. While the millions of creatures are locked in their phototropic trance, the switchmen treat the terrifying, ankle-deep carpet of bugs with the rough, unsentimental familiarity of shepherds handling wool. 

One kneels beside a junction box where a translucent phloem-tube is clamped to a cast-iron conduit. With the flat of a brass crowbar, he sweeps a dense cluster of fist-sized browsers off the valve wheel—shoveling them aside like wet coal. They tumble off the catwalk in a silent, pale shower, still catatonic, hitting the wooden floor far below like dropped crockery. His partner is already leaning into the gap, fingers flying over the exposed brass fittings. Their hands are extraordinary: oversized, thick-knuckled, the fingernails horn-yellow and curved from generations of prying, yet capable of reading the microscopic tremor of a failing bearing through three inches of cast iron. 

They do not repair the machines so much as they arbitrate between the iron and the wood. Where an ambitious lip of pale, calloused root-matter threatens to swallow a pressure-relief lever, a switchman scores the living wood with a curved grafting-knife. He does not cut deeply enough to bleed the volatile sap; he merely irritates the cambium, rubbing a pinch of bitter copper-sulfate and engine-ash into the nick so the tree will flinch away over the coming months, curling its growth in a safer direction. Beside it, where an iron bracket has rusted thin enough to groan under the catwalk’s weight, he smears a sweet, resinous poultice directly onto the metal, encouraging the thirsty wood to swell over the joint and splint the failing steel with living timber.

Higher up, near the vaulted ribs where the big scissor-jawed predators sit motionless in the red gloom—their mandibles gleaming like oiled shears—a solitary tender makes a different circuit. This is usually one of the elders, bent-backed, breathing with a dry, rhythmic wheeze that matches the twelve-minute Vent cycle. Carrying a long, hooked gaff, the elder walks the narrow spine of an overhead crane-rail, inspecting the predators themselves. If one has grown too heavy and sluggish, letting the smaller browsers overpopulate a dynamo casing and risk shorting the coils with their damp bodies, the elder hooks it neatly behind the thorax and flicks it into the abyss. It hits the sunken flywheel pit below and is instantly mulched into grease and shell-meal, which the surrounding floor-roots will drink within the hour, and which the soft-bellied oil-lickers will clean from the pistons before the next shift.

There is no cruelty in it, nor reverence. To the people in the lockers, the chamber is a single, cranky, multi-ton animal made of brass, wood, electricity, and shell, and they are merely the specialized lice that keep its eyes clear of crust.

Then, through the soles of their boots, they feel the deep root-valves shift miles below in the blind earth. Five seconds left.

They do not look up. As one, along all the tiered balconies and spiral stairs, the pale figures step backward. Those near the shanties slip inside, pulling the heavy bulkheads shut and spinning the rusted dogs home with a dull, metallic *clack*. Those caught too far out along the central spans do not run; running shakes the iron and wakes the crust early. Instead, they step calmly against the nearest vertical stanchion, pull their chitin-plated aprons up over their faces like hoods, tuck their thumbs into their armpits, and lock their knees.

Overhead, in the dark, the brass whistle blows its single, flat note. 

The steam louvers roar open at floor level, filling the nave with rolling, pearlescent fog. The sodium lamps surge back to dusty, merciless gold. Instantly, the trance breaks. The deafening *shhhk-shhhk-shhhk* of ten million legs slams back against the walls like a physical blow. Out on the catwalk, the stranded switchman disappears entirely beneath a rising, surging tide of pale-shelled climbers and blind velvet-skins scrambling upward toward the warm ceiling to escape the hot exhaust—a motionless, bug-covered cairn standing patiently on the vibrating iron, breathing warm turpentine through a grease-rag, waiting twelve more minutes for the red dark to let him take his next ten steps.

Inside the shanties, behind the grime-caked glass, life is vertical, warm, and intensely cramped. Each iron locker is scarcely wider than a sleeping-berth on a submarine, stacked three or four high and bolted directly into the wood-swallowed masonry. Because the iron walls sweat constantly, the interiors are lined with cork-like sheets of dried bracket-fungus peeled from the deep exhaust flues, which drink the moisture and smell faintly of nutmeg and hot copper. 

Here, the vibration is softened to a steady, cradle-like rock. Light comes from small, tapped phloem-bladders—translucent, living nodules coaxed through holes in the iron wall and pricked with hollow silver needles to weep a slow, perpetual bead of turpentine into tiny brass spirit-lamps. 

There are three or four people to a stack, climbing between levels on iron rungs polished bright as mirrors by bare, calloused heels. Down here, with the bulkhead dogged shut, they pull their chitin aprons off and hang them over the floor-grates so the rising steam will cook the parasites out of the seams. In their undershirts of woven root-bast, they look smaller, softer, almost fragile. Their hair is cropped to the skull with tin-snips to deny purchase to ticks; their skin gleams with a protective salve of rendered arthropod-fat that keeps the caustic condensation from blistering their necks.

Without voices, their domesticity is entirely tactile. When two people pass on a ladder, they press shoulder to shoulder for a lingering second—a check of pulse, temperature, and mood. Conversations happen in the dark corners on narrow oilcloth bunks, conducted by tapping blunt, grease-stained fingers against a partner's collarbone or forearm in a rapid, shorthand cadence borrowed from telegraph keys and piston-knocks. *Valve four binding,* a thumb presses twice, drags once against a wrist. *Salt-crust on the west dynamo. The boy needs new boot-nails.*

Children are rare, one or two to a generation across the whole chamber, carried for their first four years inside heavy canvas slings beneath a parent's chitin coat, pressed tight against a ribs-cage that booms with the room’s bass note. A baby born here does not cry aloud for long; learning quickly that sound vanishes into the omnipresent roar even through iron walls, an infant learns to kick a sharp, rhythmic tattoo against its mother’s sternum when hungry, its huge, dark-adapted eyes tracking the twelve-minute brightening and dimming of the porthole glass long before it knows its own hands.

They eat what the room gives them, cooked over the manifold pipes that pass directly through their kitchens. Heavy, sweetish loaves baked from bleached isopod-meal ground in hand-querns, dark paste fermented from the soft-bellied oil-browsers—which tastes sharply of walnuts, iron, and pine-tar—and clear, mineral-heavy water distilled from the ceiling-rain in copper catch-pans hung just outside the doors. Nothing has soil on it; nothing knows the sun. Scurvy is kept at bay by chewing the bitter, pale-yellow shoots that occasionally try to sprout from the grafted cable-runs, pickled in jars of weak battery-acid and brine.

Along the inner walls of every shanty, shelved above the bunks, are the ledgers. They are not books of paper, which would dissolve into grey soup before a page was filled, but thin, planed shingles of cured heartwood and beaten brass plates, etched with steel awls. Nobody left in the terminus remembers what the dynamos are wired to beyond the root-walls. The thick, copper-armored cables plunge straight into solid, three-hundred-year-old burls at the north end of the nave and vanish upward into the planetary crust, carrying the chamber’s furious wattage away to a surface city that may be a ruin, a forest, or a sheet of ice. 

It makes no difference to the ledger-keepers. The plates do not record history; they record clearances, sap-pressures, brush-wear on the generators, and the slow, glacial creep of the wooden vaults. A grandfather dies in his bunk during the gold light; during the next red pause, wrapped in woven copper wire to weigh him down, he is dropped quietly into the deep exhaust Sump, where the taproots are warmest and hungry for calcium. His brass spectacles and his scraping-irons are wiped clean with tallow and handed to a twelve-year-old whose ears have just finished callousing over their rubber plugs. 

And up in the highest, narrowest iron box of all—bolted directly beneath the central wooden vault where the oily rain gathers before it falls—sits the Whistle-Watch. 

Only one person occupies this berth at a time, serving six-hour shifts beside a massive bank of trembling mercury-gauges and brass pressure-dials whose needles swing slowly toward red lines. A braided leather lanyard hangs from the ceiling, disappearing up into the dark toward the great steam-whistle grafted into the crown of the vault. 

The Watcher sits on a tall wooden stool, bare feet resting squarely on the main cast-iron conduit so the soles can read the deep earth-valves miles below, eyes fixed on the rising mercury and the glowing cherry-red filaments of the dimmed lamps. Down below, in the brief, miraculous silence, twenty pale figures in armored aprons dart across the catwalks, prying, salting, greasing, and harvesting among the millions of sleeping shells. The Watcher counts the beats of their own heart against the heavy *thump-hiss* of the engines beneath them—waiting for the mercury to kiss the brass pin, waiting for the deep roots to draw breath, hand raised and closed lightly around the leather cord, keeping the truce for twelve minutes more.
