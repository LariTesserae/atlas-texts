To look for what rules this place—or rather, what endures it with something resembling intention—you have to stop looking at the skyline. The twenty-foot stilt-grazers are just weathervanes with stomachs, and the blubber-birds are merely surf-foam riding the hydraulic ridges. Anything that stands upright here is an amateur, fighting the wind and waiting to be sheared in half when an acre of turf decides to skip twenty yards to the left.

The true natives do not stand on the drumhead. They are threaded into it, sliding between the beats. 

At first glance, you would mistake one for a patch of exceptionally greasy, iron-stained water—a dark, heavy slick clinging to the silver-green sedge, perhaps ten feet across, vibrating in sympathy with the subterranean pistons. Look closer, through the stinging horizontal mist, and you see that the slick has thickness. It is a flat, muscular mantle of cold- vulcanized flesh, no thicker than a heavy winter coat, pressed so tightly against the wet mat that the wind passes over it without finding a single edge to lift. 

They have no bones. Three billion years ago, their ancestors might have had spines, ribs, and skulls, just as the plateau once had granite, but the bog has ground both down to a more durable compromise. A rigid skeleton is a liability when the ground beneath you regularly accelerates at sixty miles an hour or drops out into starry vacuum. Instead, the mantle-dwellers are built like heavy-duty conveyor belts: layers of rubbery, grey-black gristle cross-hatched with bands of biogenic copper wire—mineral stripped from the bog-slush and woven directly into their fascia. 

They move like spilled mercury pulled by a magnet, pouring themselves over the ankles of the stilt-grazers, flowing seamlessly up and over the three-foot shockwaves as they race past, absorbing the deafening *thump-thump-thump* of the lignified moss veins as a series of ripples across their backs. 

You can tell when one is waking up to hunt because the copper-threaded skin begins to click. 

Lying flat across a spinning wheel of black iron-lichen, a mantle-dweller will let the rotating plant drag its belly in slow circles, using its own conductive musculature as a stator. It harvests the thrumming static charge of the flywheel, bleeding the electricity away from the blue-lit amphibians—which go dim and sluggish around its margins—and storing the current in flat, saline bladders arranged radially beneath its hide. The smell of crushed celery and ozone sharpens to a nose-bleeding tang. Small, beaded droplets of freezing water sitting on its back begin to dance, skittering across the rubbery skin in geometric patterns, vibrating at the exact frequency of the local root-hammering.

It does not have eyes; in a world where light only bruises through the clouds and fifty paces can suddenly magnify into five inches, optics are a lie. Instead, its entire dorsal surface is a tympanum, reading the barometric shriek of the sphagnum valves and the subterranean percussion of the peat. And more than that, it feels the *thinness*. Its copper-laced nerves are exquisitely sensitive to the fraying of physical law. It can feel the static tension building where two sixty-mile-an-hour hydraulic waves are on a collision course, sensing the impending spatial glitch seconds before the turf gives up.

In fact, they farm the glitches. 

Watch three or four of these wide, dark mantles converge on an intersection of root-veins. As they slide through the sedge, their edges meet and *zip* together—hundreds of tiny, muscular hooks along their margins interlocking with a wet, velcro-like tearing sound until four individuals have become a single, forty-foot tarp of heavy, glistening hide. They do not communicate with sound; the stamping-mill roar of the plateau would drown out anything softer than a cannon. They talk through voltage and tension, passing sharp, rhythmic cramps from one body into the next, a silent telegraph of twitches running across the combined sheet.

Together, they are strong enough to interfere with the factory floor. 

The merged sheet slides over a series of puckered, whistling sphagnum vents and clamps down. They seal their undersides to the wet turf using rings of terrifyingly strong suction-pits, holding fast against pressures that would burst an iron boiler. Beneath them, deprived of its exhaust, the biological piston stalls. Black, tannin-rich slush backs up in the miles-long moss vein. The ground begins to judder violently, throwing off sheets of cold spray. Another wave approaches from the east, racing in like a freight train. Because the first vein is choked, the collision doesn't happen on schedule—it happens off-beat, with double the hydraulic violence.

Right before the acre tears loose, the joined mantles unhook their zippers, release their suction, and curl their leading edges upward just a fraction of an inch to catch the gale, peeling smoothly backward off the doomed slab like wet decals off glass.

*Crack.* 

The cast-iron snap splits the twilight. The slab of sodden moss leaps six feet into the air, water pouring upward into the belly of the clouds, gravity inverted, time stuttering into steel-wire sheets of suspended rain. A passing flock of blubber-birds is caught in the skip; half vanish into the mist a quarter-mile off,honking in panicked brass chords, while three or four are caught precisely on the shearing line—not cut, because space doesn't cut, it simply ceases to agree on where their halves belong. Heavy, pale plates of blubber and meat drop slapping onto the margins of the square, starry vacuum hole, alongside stunned, pale eels and bushels of frozen marsh-gnats knocked out of the stuttering air.

Before the rushing bog-water and knitting roots have even finished sewing the hole shut—while wisps of absolute, freezing nothingness are still boiling off the edges of the black slush—the mantles pour back in. 

They slide over the windfall, enveloping the sheared blubber-birds and the twitching eels from above. Underneath the dark tarp of their bodies, hundreds of small, circular mouths—ringed not with teeth, which shatter, but with rotating rasps of toughened peat-horn—go to work, grinding the meat and blubber into a paste before the bog itself can swallow it down into the miles-deep felt.

Yet even this—this coordinated, industrial scavenging—is only their surface life. 

If you could follow one when it grows heavy and sluggish with age, or heavy with young, you would see it stop sliding across the silver-green sedge. It finds a quiet seam between the root-bladders—quiet being relative, merely a place where the turf only heaves like a stormy sea rather than detonating—and it turns itself edge-on. Narrowing its body into a thick, muscular ribbon, it forces its leading margin down *through* the woven mat of lichen and sedge, slipping beneath the drumskin into the dark, circulating ice-water below.

Down there, miles above any memory of stone, suspended in the pitch-black, thundering hydraulic interior of the plateau, is where they keep their history.

Wrapped around the great, lignified arteries of the deep moss—tubes as wide as tunnels that constrict and fire every few seconds—hang thousands of older mantles. They have stopped moving entirely. Over centuries of cold and pressure, their rubbery hides have grown indistinguishable from the root-felt itself, stitched into the plumbing of the world by invasive white rootlets that drink the excess mineral salts from their flesh while feeding them faint sugars from above. Their copper-wired nerves have grown outward, piercing the walls of the moss-veins, linking one ancient, blind sheet to the next across miles of subterranean slush.

They do not control the great green engine; nothing controls it. The plateau is far too vast and frantic to be steered. 

Instead, this deep, sunken network acts like a governor on a runaway steam engine—or perhaps more like a vast, cold, wet brain dreaming inside a machine it didn't build. By clenching here, by bleeding static charge into a root-bladder there, by twitching a muscle-valve three miles down in the freezing dark, the sunken elders subtly tune the chaos overhead. They route the sixty-mile-an-hour ridges away from the nurseries where newly budded, palm-sized mantles are learning to ride the static of the spinning lichen-wheels. They nudge the worst of the reality-tears toward the feeding grounds, opening square windows of starry vacuum at semi-regular intervals like fishermen casting nets into another universe's dark. 

Occasionally, when a particularly violent collision down in the deep peat goes wrong, a section of this sunken network gets caught in a subterranean skip. 

A half-mile away on the surface, out in the dim, weeping openness beneath the bruised sky, an ancient elder will abruptly appear—teleported upward out of the dark, lying flat across the silver-green turf. It looks like a small, glistening black pond that wasn't there a heartbeat ago, covered in torn white rootlets and crusted with centuries of bog-iron, smelling overwhelmingly of old snow, grease, and copper. 

The stilt-grazers pump their knees and step delicately over it without breaking the rhythm of their clattering baleen. The blubber-birds sprint right across its back on the next passing wave. And for a few minutes, before it slowly, painfully curls its stiff edges and sinks itself back down through the tight weave of the turf, the surface-mantles will slide in from all directions to press their margins against the elder's frayed hide—clicking softly, trading jolts of cold blue static, reading three hundred years of deep, thundering memory before the wind and the rain wash the scent of the lower dark away.
