If you drop into a crouch behind that engine just after it crests the grade, and hold your breath until the *shhhk-shhhk* of its treads fades to a tremor in the soles of your boots, you will hear a second sound trailing fifty yards behind the stern.

It is softer than the grazers’ hooves. It is the sound of braided leather soles—soled with rawhide peeled from the bellies of dead gliders and greased with tuber-tallow—placing themselves flat and deliberate onto the warm, damp corduroy left by the steel tracks.

Nothing has ever been built on the plateau, because anything anchored to the stone is stranded in a lean, spread-out world. If you pour a foundation, you starve within a month once you have dug every bloated root within walking distance and driven the herds three basins over. To live up here, a thing must move continuously, or let something else do the moving for it.

The people who walk behind the rig call the machine a *bellows-back*, though they rarely say the word aloud. Speech with the vocal cords carries poorly in heavy, damp air unless you shout, and shouting draws the dish-eared hunters out of the dark washes. Mostly they speak in clicks of the tongue against the back teeth, faint whistles shaped behind closed lips, and dry, two-fingered taps against the hollow wooden staves they carry—a close, brisk patter that sounds to any listening predator like loose shale settling or beetle-carapaces knocking together in the wind.

There are twenty-six of them trailing this particular engine. Twenty-six is close to the upper limit of what one condenser pipe and one wake of disturbed gravel can sustain without forcing the band to fan out too wide and lose touch in the dark.

At first glance, in the momentary violet flash of an internal electrostatic discharge, you might mistake them for upright, skinny grazers. They are pale—not the sickly white of cave-things, because the faint, constant plum airglow and the mineral dust work on their skin, leaving it the matte, waxy grey-umber of putty or old tallow. Their eyes are wide, dark, almost entirely pupil, lidded with heavy, oily lashes that keep the fine ironstone silt and damp mist from fouling the cornea. Most of the elders have lost their sight anyway to cataracts or grit-scarring by forty, and it slows them down very little. Watch an old woman walking the outer flank of the band: her chin is tilted slightly up, nostrils flared wide to sort the warm chemical layering of the air, while the long, calloused toes of her bare left foot—she wears a greased shoe only on the right, for kicking loose scree—read the exact grain and moisture of the ground before she commits her weight. Their ears are slightly cupped and mobile at the base, though nothing like the great cartilaginous dishes of the quadruped hunters; instead, they rely on their staves. Each staff is six feet of cured, fibrous root-wood, shod at the bottom with a salvaged scrap of sintered bronze or hardened ironstone, and pressed lightly against the jawbone or collarbone whenever the band halts. Through the wood, the plateau stops being empty and becomes a tight drumhead: they can feel the heavy, liquid *thud-thud* of a grazer column two miles crosswind, the light, skittering scratch of armored scavengers fighting over a dry marrow-bone, and the deep, steady, reassuring fifty-hertz purr of their bellows-back ahead.

Right now, though, they are not trailing fifty yards behind. Only six of them are on the gravel—the outriders and the water-catchers, jogging lightly behind the stern with slung bladders of stitched gut held under the condenser drips before the grazers can crowd back in to lick the stones.

The rest of the twenty-six are *on* the rig.

The machine was never designed for passengers. It has no catwalks, no handrails, no ladders, and its builders—whoever they are, down below the great boundary rifts where the refined isotopes eventually go, or dead ten thousand years in some sealed vault—gave it smooth, sloped flanks to shed dust and rain. Yet over centuries of walking beside these moving mountains, the bands have learned every bolt-boss, every louver-lip, every cool dead-spot between the roaring heat-exchangers, the way cliff-martins know a sheer granite face.

Thirty feet up the unpainted alloy flank, slung across a shallow recess where two hydraulic housings meet a structural rib, hang seven wide hammocks woven from tough, waxy shrub-fibers. Inside them, children and off-watch foragers sleep curled on their sides, strapped in loosely against the slow two-degree roll of the bogies crossing an ironstone swell. The metal beneath their backs is forty-two degrees Celsius, vibrating with a continuous, soporific bass hum that gets into the marrow of an infant’s bones before it is an hour old. People born on the flanks of a bellows-back cannot sleep properly on the ground; if stranded on the stone, they lie awake shivering in eighty-degree air because the earth is too still, too quiet, like floating in dead water.

Higher up, near the spine where the warm, scrubbed exhaust plumes vent upward into the dark, three hunters sit clipped to a louver-strut by braided leather harnesses. They wear wide wraps of dark, mist-combing felt oiled to shed condensation, and over their noses and mouths sit thick pads of crushed lichen to filter the ozone bite that collects near the precipitator towers. They are utterly motionless, holding long, Y-shaped throwing frames strung with twisted sinew.

 *Thump.*

Deep inside the louvers, static discharges. For a tenth of a second, violet lightning stabs outward through the vents.

In that instantaneous freeze-frame, the air twenty feet above the rig’s spine is revealed: a swirling chimney of blind midges, and banking through them, four leather-winged gliders with their wide jaws agape. One of the hunters does not aim during the flash—no human nerve is fast enough for that. Instead, the flash merely confirms the map she has already built from the faint *whuff* of displaced warm air and the tiny, dry clicks of the gliders’ wing-thumbs adjusting to the exhaust turbulence. Half a second after the dark slams back down, her frame snaps forward with a muffled *twock*. A heavy bone-headed dart, tethered by forty yards of fine waxed cord, takes the lead glider cleanly behind the broad ribcage. She hauls it down hand-over-hand before its thrashing can foul the intake vanes, breaks its neck with a practiced twist of her heel against the alloy plating, and slips it into a mesh sack to bleed out over the side—away from the clean hydraulic seals.

That is the first rule of the bands, older than any remembered name: *nothing fouls the engine*.

Nobody pries up an inspection plate. Nobody wedges a bone chock into a bogie hinge to slow it down, or scrapes grease from a bearing housing to salve a burn, or hangs wet hides over an intake grate where they might choke the airflow. Every child is taught, by having their palm pressed flat against the warm hull while an elder taps out the rhythm on their collarbone, that the bellows-back is neither a god nor a beast of burden. A beast cares if you beat it; a god cares if you pray to it. The engine does not know they are there. If a toddler slips from a sling between the third and fourth bogies, the eight-foot steel treads do not hesitate, do not rise an inch; they press child and greywacke gravel down together with the exact same quiet *shhhk-shhhk*, and forty minutes later the small, clicking scavengers come up out of the dry washes to finish what the mineral salts haven't taken.

And if a band were ever stupid enough to blind a sensor cluster with mud or jam a skimmer-arm to pry off its copper coils, the machine would simply execute a diagnostic halt, seal its condensers, vent scalding steam from its belly-valves to clear the obstruction, and alter its heading toward the distant, unseen drop-off at the continent’s edge for servicing—walking straight out of their world at two miles an hour and leaving twenty-six people sitting on dry gravel forty miles from the nearest damp clay pan, with the slow, dish-eared things already turning their heads in the dark to listen.

So they keep the hull cleaner than the wind does. Between sleeps, older children scramble along the narrow ledges above the treads with stiff brushes made from bound bundles of charcoal-black shrub twigs, sweeping caked iron silt out of the bogie joints and flicking away the pale, thumb-sized beetles when they cluster thick enough around a warm valve to bake into a crust. In return for this invisible grooming, the engine gives them distilled water that doesn't gripe the gut with heavy salts, warmth that never wavers, high refuge from the things that hunt on four silent feet, and a slow, tireless drift across fresh foraging ground every hour of their lives.

Watch the flank-walkers down on the gravel as the rig begins the long, gentle climb out of the basin.

Four men and women fan out two hundred yards to port, pacing parallel to the hull, keeping station by the sternum-deep hum and the single pale amber marker lamp high on the prow—the only star their sky possesses. Whenever the amber point dips behind their own shoulders’ silhouette or grows fuzzy with condensation haze, they angle slightly inward. As they walk, they use long, curved digging-irons—forged cold and patient over years by hammering rare, heavy meteoric nodules or shed tread-pins between basalt anvils—to hook beneath the leathery rust-colored lichen without tearing the whole mat loose. Beneath the mat, the silt is wet as dough and warm from the rock below; they scoop up handfuls of blind, fat white grubs and slip them live into clay-lined pouches, then pat the lichen skin carefully back down so the wind won't peel and kill it. When they pass a mist-combing shrub, they never uproot the whole tuber. They probe the gravel with thin bone needles to find the bloated flank of the root underground, slice off a single wedge the size of two fists, smear the wound inside the earth with dry wood-ash and iron dust to stop rot, and jog lightly to catch up with the stern. They know—though the circuit never quite repeats—that their grandchildren might cross this exact basin behind another rig in twenty years, and an empty grid of dead gravel kills whoever comes behind.

Most of a lifetime passes in this single, intimate pocket of warm noise: forty yards wide, two hundred yards long, drifting endlessly over whale-backed stone. You know every scar and mole on the twenty-five bodies around you—mostly by touch, during the long dark hours huddled in the hull-recesses while a twenty-minute horizontal drizzle slicks the alloy too dangerously to walk the ledges, braiding cordage by feel, greasing leather, rubbing tallow into cracked heels, murmuring close into one another’s collarbones so the bone conducts the low hum of an old story without spilling sound into the air.

Only once in a great while—every two or three hundred sleeps, though without sunrises they count time in shed tread-pins or pregnancies or the slow wear on a digging-iron—does the world open wider.

An outrider presses his staff to his jaw, freezes, and drops to one knee to lay his bare ear directly against a buried greywacke slab. Through the bedrock comes a beat interference: a slow *wah—wah—wah* as a second fifty-hertz hum crosses their own from thirty miles northeast.

Two circuits are grazing tangent.

The Engines do not acknowledge each other. They pass three miles apart in the dark, one angling down into a silt pan, the other climbing a long basalt swell, their two amber lamps hanging briefly in the plum-black vault like ship-lanterns on a dead sea. Neither slows.

In the three hours while the machines are closest, six figures detach from one moving shadow and eight from the other, running hard and silent across the open scree between them. There is no time for long ceremony; the hills they live on are walking away in opposite directions at four miles an hour combined. They meet in the middle of the bare gravel, breathing hard in the thick kiln-air, smelling sharply of different mineral basins—one group dusty with red ochre, the other rank with wet sulfur from the western rifts. Hands go straight to faces, tracing cheekbones, feeling scars, measuring the height of teenagers who were toddlers at the last crossing. Bundles change hands in quick, whispered bursts of lip-whistles and finger-taps: two pouches of dried grub-meal, a precious coil of shed copper wire found tangled on an electromagnetic skimmer, packets of dried bitter-fungus for fever, three bone-headed darts of a tighter balance.

And people change hands, too. A young man of seventeen presses his forehead briefly against his mother’s, shifts his staff and sleeping-sling to his left shoulder, and turns away toward the strangeamber light receding upslope; a woman in her twenties with a gift for stitching watertight gut-bladders steps into the gap he left. Without these dark, breathless sprints across three miles of predator-haunted gravel, twenty-six people would become one bloodline within three generations and wither.

Twenty minutes later, the gravel between the ridges is empty again. The young man trots behind the stern of a strange bellows-back whose exhaust smells slightly hotter, slightly sharper with ozone than the one whose hum shaped his skull in the womb. An older man drops back from the rear of the new band, taps him twice on the wrist in greeting, takes his hand, and guides his palm up the dark, sweating alloy flank to show him where the handholds are, which bolt-bosses run hot enough to blister skin, and where the hammocks hang clear of the violet flash.

He climbs up into the warm iron dark, clips a strange harness around his waist, leans his cheek against the vibrating rib of the hull until the new pitch settles into his teeth, and closes his eyes while the plateau slides endlessly away beneath him.
