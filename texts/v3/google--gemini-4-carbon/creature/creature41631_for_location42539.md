In that cable-car, swaying gently above the floating lens of black water, four people sit on slatted wooden benches, and none of them are breathing very often.

At twenty thousand feet, haste is a fatal defect. Those who live in the scattered wards are barrel-chested and broad-ribbed, built around enormous, bellows-like lungs and hearts that beat perhaps thirty-five times a minute. Their skin has the bluish-undertoned pallor of skim milk, crossed at the temples and wrists by veins dark with thick, iron-heavy blood. To an outsider, a crowd waiting on a tramway platform looks like a gathering of statues, or sleepers standing upright. They do not fidget. They do not pace. When a foundryman turns his head to watch a flock of crag-fowl vanish into a spatial crease, he moves his neck with the smooth, greased deliberation of a turret tracking a target. Every calorie of heat and every mouthful of thin, wet oxygen is budgeted before it is spent.

You can tell how long a family has lived in the cordillera by their boots and their hands. 

New stone has no mercy for soft things. Where there is no erosion, every fallen shard of quartz is a scalpel, and every basalt ledge is a sheer, glass-hard blade. Leather soles are sliced to ribbons in a week. Instead, the inhabitants wear heavy clogs forged from malleable pig-iron or shod with thick plates of vulcanized clinker-rubber, which ring like horseshoes on the wet brick alleys and strike dull sparks off the raw granite stairs. Their coats are oiled canvas or felted crag-fowl down, armored at the elbows, knees, and shoulders with riveted strips of boiled hide and scrap copper—not against weapons, of which they have none, but against a slip of the foot in the fog. To brush carelessly against an unweathered doorframe hewn from fresh rock is to open a vein; given the sluggish, pressurized thickness of high-altitude blood, they wear tight, buckled cuffs at wrist and ankle to act as ready-made tourniquets.

Their hands, when stripped of chain-mesh work gloves, are scarred in parallel, ruler-straight white lines—never ragged tears, always the clean, surgical slices of geometric stone.

Because the valley rings like a cistern, amplifying every dropped wrench and cracking ridge into a rolling four-minute avalanche of echo, the citizens rarely speak aloud outdoors. Shouting is considered both vulgar and exhausting. Instead, they carry the noise of the city inside their skulls like weather, plugging their ears with tufts of lanolin-soaked moss, and talk with their thumbs on brass telegraph keys wired into the pockets of their coats, or across short distances by tapping calibrated iron rings against the nearest girder. Walk onto any suspended catwalk and put your bare palm to the wet steel: beneath the sixty-cycle hum of the transformers and the deep, sub-audible *thrum* of the grounding-flywheels, the metal is alive with dry, clicking conversations—shift changes, marriage proposals, warnings that Ward Fourteen has drifted six inches north since Tuesday and the aqueduct joints need slacking.

When they do speak, behind the triple-glazed windows of the brick tenements where the air is thick with coal-smoke and soup steam, their voices are astonishingly deep, slow, and resonant. A sentence takes a long time to arrive. They leave long, companionable silences between clauses, waiting for the distant drop-forge to strike so they don't have to compete with its echo.

Their entire civic life is organized around a single profession, broken into a hundred guilds: keeping the city in one piece while the world underneath it finishes being born.

Watch the *Surveyors of the Seam*—men and women who spend twelve-hour shifts dangling in wicker harnesses beneath the cantilevers, armed with brass theodolites, plumb-bobs weighted with depleted lead, and cages of copper-backed beetles. A plumb-bob does not always hang down here; near an extrusion zone, it might pull sideways at thirty degrees toward a cliff that hasn't arrived yet, or spin in lazy, indecisive circles. The beetles know first when the geometry is thinning. When the insects stop clicking in sixty-cycle unison and begin to scatter blindly over a worker’s knuckles, it means a spatial fault is migrating toward the masonry.

Then come the *Pin-Drivers*. They are the aristocracy of the turbine halls—huge, silent figures greased in mutton tallow against the damp, tending the forty-foot flywheels and the pneumatic pile-drivers that sink wrought-iron grounding rods fifty yards into the newly extruded rock. Nobody entirely understands why massive, cold-forged iron driven under slow, rhythmic pressure forces the overlapping, half-real cliffs to decide on a single location and stay there, any more than the pale ungulates understand why the gravity turns upside down under the shelves. It simply works. A rod goes in an eighth of an inch per hour; as it bites, the ghostly double-vision of a drifting ridge shudders, snaps into focus with a sound like a cannon-shot, and becomes ordinary, solid basalt, ready to take rivets.

And because nothing has been here long enough to rot into dirt, the dead do not go into the ground. There is no ground—only crystalline depth and empty air. 

In the residential spires, the upper stories are warm greenhouses heated by flue-pipes, where families tend shallow iron troughs filled with the only soil in twenty miles: a dark, rich humus compounded of crushed clinker, bird guano scraped from the smelter roofs, composted black fern, and the cremated ash and ground bone of their own ancestors. Every radish, every pale tuber, every tough, bitter cabbage eaten in the tenements grows directly out of the grandfathers and grandmothers of the ward. To spill a handful of potting-earth over a balcony into the five-mile gulf is a crime punishable by exile to the unpinned ridges. People carry small brass lockets at their throats containing not hair or portraits, but a pinch of black, crumbly dirt—proof that they belong to a lineage old enough to have broken down.

As the cable-car nears the foundry complex, it slows to match the creep of the docking platform, which is currently winching itself three feet to the left on greased chains as its host needle of granite settles into the mist. 

Before the car touches the buffers, a boy of about twelve steps out onto the open iron apron of the car. He wears heavy iron-shod clogs, ear-tufts of green moss, and a coat stiff with tallow. In his hand is a long hooked pole; on his shoulder sits a tame, oil-plumaged crag-fowl, hooded in scrap leather. He does not lean out excitedly; he plants his feet with the slow, heavy certainty of a monument settling onto its plinth. 

Fifty yards below him, two cliffs are passing silently through one another in the pewter light. Where their ghostly planes intersect, a waterfall of cold condensation splits in two, half falling straight down into the abyss, half running horizontally along an invisible crease in the air to splash against the hot brick wall of a boiler-house, sending up a sudden, violent billow of white cloud. 

The boy watches it without surprise. He checks the hang of the chain at his belt to see which way "down" is leaning on this side of the chasm, taps twice with his iron ring against the handrail to tell the winchman inside the car to let out four inches of slack, and waits—breathing once, then waiting ten seconds to breathe again—for the steel to meet the steel.
