If you stand at the edge of the ankle-deep pool and wait for your eyes to adjust to the pewter gloom, you will first notice an anomaly in the cymatic grid. 

Three feet out from the eastern wall—the wall closest to the buried engines, where the stray ground-current bleeds strongest—there is a patch of water the size of a dinner plate that refuses to shiver in diamonds. While the rest of the floor is locked in tight, geometric cross-hatching, this single circular patch is glassy and dead flat. Around its perimeter, the standing waves pile up against an invisible barrier, breaking into tiny, silent whitecaps three millimeters high.

Something is lying on the bottom there, absorbing the kinetic and electrical bleed before it can reach the surface.

At a glance, you would take it for a slab of fallen greywacke coated in the pale, wet stone-flour that clings to the overhangs. It is roughly the length of a human forearm, flattened like a skate or a river-stone, and pressed so tightly to the bedrock floor that no water passes beneath it. Only when the twenty-two-second hydraulic thud shakes the chamber does the illusion break. As the pressure wave hits, the slab doesn't rattle like the loose slate shards around it; instead, a ripple passes through its pale grey mass from front to back, like wind moving through thick pudding, and a rim of translucent, cartilaginous skirt lifts for a fraction of a second to vent a cloud of fine, silvery silt.

There are eleven of them in the chamber—three large ones anchored in the pool, and eight smaller, younger ones plastered vertically into the wet, weeping fractures of the eastern wall, wedged into the stone like soft mortar. Nothing like them exists on the coastal shelf above, and nothing like them lived in the groundwater before the rock broke. Either they were shaken loose from some flooded, lightless fault-line deeper down when the shelf collapsed, or they were ordinary coastal flatworms and mud-leeches whose developmental biology went wildly sideways in the soup of constant sonic agitation, dissolved heavy metals, and weak DC current. Whatever their ancestry, three winters in the dynamo-cleft have remade them entirely into creatures of pure physics.

They have no eyes—sight is useless in stagnant mist—and nothing you could properly call a mouth or a gut. If you peeled one off the slate (it would take a pry-bar; their underside is a muscular suction-mat ribbed like the tread of a boot, slimed with a conductive hydrogel that smells fiercely of wet copper), you would find an underside threaded with branching, dark-gold filaments. 

They haven't grown those filaments entirely out of flesh. Over months of pressing themselves directly over the snapped veins of quartz and iron pyrite, they have incorporated the raw mineral dust and the iron-oxidizing bacteria directly into their ventral skin. They are living electrolytic plates. When the stray voltage leaks out of the eastern bedrock, it doesn't split the water anymore; it passes directly into the creature’s belly, running along those biologically smelted pyrite tracks to power a metabolism that barely bothers with calories at all. They don't eat the midges or the diving beetles. They graze on the stray amperage of the unseen power station, using the silver fuzz of electrolysis—the microscopic beads of pure hydrogen and oxygen gas—to oxygenate their pale, dense blood without ever needing to surface or draw a breath.

Watch the largest one in the pool when the high turbine whine spins up under load. 

As the pitch climbs, the creature’s upper surface changes texture. Smooth skin dimples and raises into hundreds of stiff, fleshy papillae, spaced with uncanny mathematical precision to match the wavelength of the acoustic roar bouncing off the walls. It is tuning itself. By shifting the spacing of its dorsal ridges, it creates a localized phase-cancellation right above its own back—an umbrella of stillness in the roaring water. 

It doesn't do this for comfort. It does it to farm, and to build.

Because the water directly above its back is acoustically dead, everything suspended in the agitated pool eventually drifts into that quiet column and drops out of suspension. Silt, microscopic flakes of mica, pale rock-flour, dead springtails, and the chitinous husks of midges discarded by the diving beetles all snow gently down onto the creature’s back. The animal doesn't shake the debris off. Instead, it secretes a clear, alkaline mucus that reacts with the calcium and silica in the stone-flour, curing slowly in the cold water into a flexible, rubbery cement. Over months, each creature builds a custom-fitted carapace of bonded slate-dust and insect-shell—armoring its soft, gelid body against the razor-sharp shards of rock that occasionally shear off the ceiling forty feet up.

Look higher up the eastern wall, six feet above the water line, where the bladder-ferns quiver in the spray. You can see how the younger ones—no bigger than thumbprints, still translucent as jellyfish—use the twenty-two-second clockwork of the room.

They cannot hold onto the dry stone, and they cannot survive without the current, so they migrate along the hairline cracks where the groundwater surges out. Twenty seconds of stillness: the young ones are wedged tight in the seam, invisible except for a faint violet-and-gold sheen where their symbiotic bacteria catch the dim light from the sky-slit. Twenty-first second: just before a human ear can register the coming hydraulic thud, the stray voltage in the rock spikes by a few millivolts as distant relays throw. The creatures feel the electrical precursor instantly. They let go of the rock half a beat *before* the thud arrives, puffing their flat bodies into little parachutes just as the pressurized sheet of frigid groundwater blasts out of the fracture. 

They ride the surge laterally across the sheer slate face—sliding six, eight, ten inches over the emerald hook-moss on a lubricating cushion of water—and clamp back down onto a fresh pyrite seam the exact millisecond the flow ebbs, long before a salamander’s tongue can register them as anything other than wet froth.

In fact, the rest of the chamber’s frantic ecosystem has already begun to organize itself around these silent, rubbery anchors, without realizing they are alive.

The diving beetles lay their eggs exclusively around the edges of the big ones in the pool, tucking pale clusters under the creature's cartilaginous skirt where the water doesn't violently oscillate and tear the delicate casings apart. On the walls, the salamanders use the palm-sized juveniles as resting pads; a salamander parked on top of a phase-tuned crawler doesn't have its inner ear battered by the relentless bass roar of the bedrock, allowing it to sleep for hours in the cold mist without sliding off the vertical slate. Even the wrens, plummeting down out of the coastal wind, aim their vertical drops at the glassy, un-rippled patches in the pool to judge their altitude above the water before flaring their wings—using the creatures as dark, reliable mirrors in a floor of blinding, shivering geometric glare.

Only during the idle shifts—those violent four-second stretches, three times a day, when the distant engines drop their load—do you see what this life costs them.

When the roar cuts out and the cymatic grid collapses into chaotic, sloshing rings, the creatures do not freeze like the salamanders. They convulse. 

Suddenly robbed of the pressure-wave they have been pushing against with every muscle fiber, and starved in an instant of the stray ground-current trickling into their bellies, they lose their grip on physics. Their dorsal ridges flatten into slick, helpless jelly. On the walls, one or two of the smaller ones always lose suction during the sudden shower of heavy fern-rain, peeling away from the black slate like wet leaves and slapping softly into the pool below. 

Down in the water, the three large ones curl their edges inward in the sudden, terrifying silence, exposing a flash of their iridescent, metallic-gold undersides to the pewter light. For four seconds, in the ringing hush while frost-wedged slate ticks in the corner, they look completely out of place—like deep-sea organs dropped onto a quarry floor, twitching weakly in plain, uncharged rainwater, slowly suffocating without the hum.

Then the distant relays slam shut. A faint, sharp whiff of ozone precedes the sound by a tenth of a second. Down in the pool, the curled edges snap flat against the bedrock before the thunder even hits your collarbone; the dorsal papillae bristle back into geometric formation; the glassy dead-zones punch cleanly up through the surface of the water; and as the massive turbines spin back up behind a hundred yards of solid stone, the creatures settle back into the roar, drinking the leakage of an engine they will never see, waiting for the walls to sweat again.
