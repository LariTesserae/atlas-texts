The long-legged creature reaches the V-shaped cleft, pauses where the stone smells of boiled quartz, and draws its limbs tight against its slick sides. Then it tips forward into the tarnished-mercury polyhedron. There is no sound of impact, no scorch of flesh. Where a wire-thin strut intersects its narrow torso, the creature simply parts—not torn, not bleeding, just neatly displaced across an unseen seam—and reassembles half a meter higher on the shaded rock behind the object, already lowering its feet to the cooler wet slate. It does not hurry. It has taken this shortcut forty times since the sun cleared the ridge.

Nothing in the basin truly *belongs* to the mountain, and nothing belongs to a single elsewhere, either. Everything here arrived as dust, spore, or stray egg blown upward from valleys a thousand kilometers apart, caught against the invisible boundary wall when the wind sheared off, and dropped down into the wet heat. What survived did not merely tolerate the four objects; it folded them into its anatomy.

Look closely at the melon-sized bladders wedged into the crevices. They are not entirely plant. Beneath the tough, translucent cellulose of the rind, suspended in the clear, hot fluid that magnifies the fractured basalt underneath, something drifts. At first it looks like a detached root-ball or a clot of pale amber resin. Wait for the quarter-hour saturation flash—wait for the ten seconds when the air turns milk-white and the sun disappears—and the clot uncurls. 

It is a soft, blind, multi-lobed thing the size of two clenched fists, lacking bones or shell, trailing pale fringes like unspun silk. When the flash-rain hits the outer skin of the bladder with a dull, drumming *thrum*, the creature inside pulses once, driving the warm nutrient broth through its fringes, and presses a wide, lipless mouth against the inner wall to rasp away the sugars the plant has spent the last fourteen minutes photosynthesizing in the brutal high-altitude UV. The plant does not resist; in the absence of soil, the creature’s nitrogenous waste is the only fertilizer in the nave. Every thicket is a closed, steaming marriage of things that have never shared a continent before this shelf cracked open.

There are two more anomalies on the floor, less obvious than the white plumb line and the mercury polyhedron. 

Near the back wall, where three razor-edged steps form a shallow, flooded corner, lies what looks like a comma of absolute, light-drinking black—a missing chip in space, flat on the surface of the pool, no larger than a thumbnail. Water runs into it from all four sides and never fills it. Around this tiny sinkhole sits the largest single organism on the shelf, though at a glance you would mistake it for a deposit of travertine or boiled salt.

It is a colonial sponge-coral, plated in overlapping scales of precipitated silicate it pulls straight from the hot, mineral-heavy runoff. It grows in a low, terraced ring around the black comma, shaping the water’s approach. Over months—or however few seasons this fresh-cracked shelf has existed—the colony has built tiny, razor-sharp spillways and baffles of glassy stone, tuning the current so that water accelerates just before it vanishes into the black flaw. In those fast, hot micro-rapids, the colony extends thousands of microscopic, rust-red feeding fans. When one side of the ring grows too high and chokes the flow, the colony secretes a weak acid, dissolving its own skeleton back into slurry to widen the channel. It is a blind stonemason, endlessly adjusting a plumbing system built around a hole in the world.

The fourth object is neither white, nor metallic, nor black. Six paces from the cliff’s edge, about knee-high in the empty air above a dry slab of slate, a fist-sized volume of space simply repeats a gesture. Every four seconds, the air itself twists ninety degrees clockwise, snaps back without a sound, and rests. If a drop of rain falls through that spot during the twist, it is flung sideways as a crescent of spray.

Beneath this invisible torsion-point lives the closest thing the basin has to a deliberate mind.

At midday, while the sun hammers the black rock, you will not see it. You will only see its architecture: a sprawling, low-slung canopy of bottle-glass fronds that have been pulled out of their natural vertical uncoiling and woven together while still soft and mucilaginous. Their wet tips have fused where they were knotted, forming dense, living domes over the deepest fissures in the center of the floor. 

Wait until the twelve-minute mark, when the air grows thick and heavy, pressing down on the eardrums just before the fog flashes.

From beneath one of the fused green domes, an arm emerges. Then another, and a third, though none of them are arranged symmetrically. The body that follows is low, broad, and roughly pentagonal, about the size of a sleeping hound, covered in skin like wet chamois leather the exact blue-black of the surrounding basalt, dusted with a pale, chalky bloom that mimics the unweathered snap of fresh-broken stone. When it stops moving on a dry slab, it disappears completely into the geometry of the floor—just another crisp, flat-topped block of rock.

It is an evolutionary castaway from some deep, cold alpine tarn—perhaps a distant, monstrously hypertrophied cousin of a planarian or a shell-less mountain mollusk, forced through ten thousand generations of hyper-accelerated division and mutation by whatever radiation or bruised physics leaks out of the four errors at close range. Its body has no fixed bones, only hydrostatic chambers it pumps full of hot water to stiffen its five stubby, muscular limbs into rigid stilts, lifting its soft belly clear of the scalding slate.

It has no eyes—eyes would boil or blind in the glare off the cloud-sea below—so it navigates by heat-shadow and the sharp, crisp *ticks* of the expanding basalt. Across its flat, angular back runs a network of shallow, geometric grooves that look uncannily like the fracture-lines in the stone floor. In those grooves, it farms the chartreuse velvet moss, carrying its own cooling system; water wicked up from the puddles runs through the moss on its back, evaporating in the sun to keep its core three degrees cooler than the lethal threshold of the open rock.

Now the fog flashes. Instantly, the cathedral nave vanishes into dense, warm milk. 

The five-limbed thing moves with sudden, startling briskness. While the smaller jointless scorpions freeze in the whiteout and the copper-veined grazers clamp their rims tight against the vertical walls, the stone-backed creature gallops smoothly through the blindness—*slap, slap, slap* go its cool, wet pads on the slate—heading straight for the cliff’s edge.

It knows the count of the seconds. Just as the ten-second fog collapses into vertical rain, it reaches the invisible thermal wall at the lip of the drop-off. 

Outside, two thousand meters above the trees, a freezing gale is tearing across the mountain. Occasionally, small, high-altitude things—wind-blown spiders on gossamer threads, migrant moths swept too high over the pass, tiny, exhausted snow-finches—strike that unseen, shimmering boundary. Instantly drenched by the sheet-condensation and stunned by the forty-degree jump in temperature, they tumble down onto the wet rim of the shelf.

The stone-backed creature walks the razor edge of the drop-off with terrifying indifference to the pale abyss inches to its left. Its broad, flat head sweeps the wet rim like a squeegee, feeling for the twitch of cold-stunned wings. It finds a moth, large and grey and sodden, folded into a prism of fractured slate. Rather than eating it whole, the creature tucks the insect beneath its belly-fold and turns back toward the center of the shelf, aiming for the fourth anomaly—the spot in the air that twists every four seconds.

It halts beside the dry slab, judges the rhythm by the faint whiff of ozone and the periodic scatter of water droplets, and lifts two of its hydrostatic limbs. With a neat, practiced flick, it tosses the sodden moth into the fist-sized knot of twisting air just on the fourth beat.

The air snaps ninety degrees and back. What drops onto the slate beneath is no longer a recognizable insect, just a warm, thoroughly milled paste of chitin and protein, sheared apart without effort. The creature lowers its broad head, laps the paste cleanly from the hot rock before it can bake dry, and pauses.

The sun is diamond-clear again. Steam curls off its chartreuse-velvet back in thin, vanishing threads. High on the blue-black wall, one of the dinner-plate grazers has reached the upper limit of the mist-zone; slowly, it lets go of the stone, curling its flat edges inward like a falling leaf so that it glides down through the heavy, windless air, landing with a wet *smack* in an angular pool at the base of the cliff to begin its month-long climb all over again.

The five-limbed thing turns away from the clouds. As the rock beneath it begins to heat back up toward scalding, it walks back to its woven dome of bottle-glass fronds. On the way, a rivulet of warm runoff shifts course where a fresh hairline crack has just *ticked* open in the young basalt, threatening to divert water away from one of the melon-bladders near its shelter. The creature stops. It lowers one heavy, flat-angled limb into the stream, blocking the new crack, and pumps fluid until that limb locks solid as stone. Then it tucks its other four legs beneath its slate-colored mantle and goes entirely still, becoming part of the basin's raw, sharp floor, diverting the hot water back toward the roots until the air turns white again.
