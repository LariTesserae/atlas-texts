Wait long enough by one of the spinning quartz bowls—long enough for the cold spray to numb your cheekbones and the mechanical applause of the valley to flatten into white noise—and you will realize that one of the wet slate pillars forty feet away is not stone, and it is not forty feet away. 

You are looking at the back of someone’s shoulder through a fold in the air. They are actually standing less than six feet to your left, entirely out of sight behind an invisible seam, though you can suddenly smell them: the sharp, struck-flint ozone of fractured quartz, mixed with the heavy, cellar-damp stink of the black grease from the deep crevices.

When they step sideways through the fault, they do not walk on legs. Nothing with legs lives here; the prompt was right about that. In a place where gravity reverses along hairline invisible planes and an ankle stepped two inches too far to the right will shear cleanly from the shin, long, jointed limbs are fatal. 

Instead, they move on a broad, calloused muscular foot—not wet and slimy like a snail’s, but dry, leathery, and heavily segmented, like the overlapping belly-scales of a snake or the gripping pads of a gecko, gray-white and tough as cured rawhide. Above this wide, gripping base rises a squat, dense, barrel-like torso wrapped in thick, heavy aprons of woven mineral wool—that flexible, asbestos-like crystal fiber, matted with stone-dust until it looks like dirty felt. They have no discernible neck. Their head is a low, smooth dome of pale cartilage and heavy bone, sloped like a limpet’s shell to shed falling rock and high-velocity water. 

They do have arms, though they keep them tucked tight into the folds of their mineral-fiber wraps until needed. When one reaches out to test the spin of the water in the pecked bowl, the limb uncoils like a thick, pale root—boneless, or rather filled with hundreds of tiny, grinding gravelly ossicles instead of long shafts, allowing the arm to bend safely across a spatial shear without snapping. The hand splits into four opposing, spatulate thumbs, tipped with thick, blackened nails as hard as chert, worn flat at the edges from chiseling.

They have no eyes. In that shadowless, bone-white glare, an eye would only burn out, and vision here is a lie anyway; looking across the basin only shows you folded distances and puddles full of subterranean dark. Instead, around the rim of their low, domed heads runs a fringe of stiff, calcified bristles, shivering constantly in the hydraulic clatter. They read the basin entirely by its percussion. Every *crack* of water hitting a fault, every change in the grinding hum of a rock fin planing against a seam, maps the invisible geometry of the air for them in real time. 

Watch this one work at the catch-frame. 

Before moving an inch, they tap a heavy flint baton twice against the dry gravel, listening to how the click bounces off the hovering ribbon of water three inches above the ground. Satisfied that the seam hasn't crept since their last visit, they glide forward—a swift, low, hitching skate across the slick dolomite—and reach into the furious, drainless vortex of the stone bowl. Their arm disappears up to the elbow before it hits the spatial skip inside the bowl; through the water, you can see their pale, four-thumbed hand suddenly materialize *outside* the rim, hovering dry in mid-air three feet away, yet dripping violently as their fingers close around a finished, milky sphere of quartz. 

They pull their arm back, uniting it with itself, holding the smooth stone ball. 

Why the spheres? Watch what they do next. The worker skates over to the base of a leaning, perforated arch where the low, grinding hum is rising steadily in pitch—a sign that an invisible spatial tide is rising through the rock, threatening to shear the arch cleanly away from a cluster of inverted huts clinging to its underside. 

The worker takes three quartz spheres from a woven asbestos sling at their hip and drops them, one by one, onto an invisible shelf in empty air four feet off the ground. The spheres do not roll off; they settle into a sharp, V-shaped trough where two spatial planes intersect, pinning themselves in the invisible crease. Immediately, a stray, upward-falling ribbon of water catches on the spheres, whips around them, and locks into a tight, screaming hydraulic collar. The sudden weight and gyroscopic torque of the trapped water acts as a wedge—or a ball bearing. The rising pitch of the grinding rock drops an octave, sighing back down into stability. The arch holds. The slate doors of the upside-down huts above stop shuddering and go back to swinging lazily in their vertical draft.

They are plumbers and masons of broken space. The entire basin is a shifting millstone, and they live inside its gears.

If you follow this one back toward the huts—carefully, stepping only where the dry-scuffed trail of their muscular foot proves the ground is continuous—you begin to notice how crowded the stone forest actually is. What you took for thick, pale mineral encrustations clinging to the undersides of overhangs, or gray boulders wedged into vertical flutes, are dozens of them, resting motionless in the cold spray. 

They are cold-blooded, or something very close to it, powered by a slow, lithic metabolism. When they aren't shoring up a fault or resetting a loom-trap, they lock their segmented bases onto the wet dolomite—sometimes sideways on a pillar, sometimes upside down under a ledge where local gravity permits—pull their arms inside their heavy mineral-wool mantles, and go entirely still for hours, letting the silver sheets of water run directly over their sloped backs like boulders in a stream.

Their lives revolve around two harvests: the spheres, and the grease.

In the upside-down huts—which smell powerfully of wet slate, ozone, and old sulfur—there is no fire. Nothing here burns. Instead, the interiors are lined with thick, insulating mats of dry mineral fiber to trap the faint, ambient friction-heat radiating from the grinding spatial seams in the walls. Stacked in neat rows along the ceiling-floors are slabs of dark slate smeared thick with the black bacterial film scraped from the lightless, miles-deep crevices. 

To get that film, they don't climb down into the earth. They use the puddles. 

Down on the basin floor, three smaller individuals—younger, no bigger than overturned buckets, their mineral wraps newly woven and stark white—are crouched around a shallow pool no wider than a wagon wheel. Looking into it, you don't see the sky; you see the wet, black, glistening throat of a vertical fissure three miles underground, where the pressure is immense and the rock sweats warm, sulfurous brine. The three workers lower a long, weighted rope of braided asbestos crystal into the two-inch-deep puddle. Sixty feet of rope vanishes straight down into two inches of water. They haul it back up hand-over-four-thumbed-hand, heavy and dripping with thick, tarry clots of black bacterial slime scraped from the deep rock, which they wipe onto slate platters with flat bone-mineral knives. 

They eat it raw, mixed with fine dolomite silt to grind it in their gizzards. As they work, they communicate—not with voices, which would be shredded and lost in the roaring applause of the valley, nor with gestures no one can see. Instead, each wears a resonant plate of thin, glassy dark slate strapped across their chest. With a blackened thumbnail or a flint striker, they tap sharp, syncopated rhythms against the slate. *Tink-tink-chak. Tink-chak.* 

The sound cuts cleanly through the lower-pitched roar of the water. Instantly, from high up on a slick spire, from behind an empty wall of air, and from inside the hanging huts, other slate plates answer—*chak-tink-tink*—a dry, brittle chorus of clicks running through the petrified crowd, updating the collective map of the basin. *A seam is rising by the third spire. The water skipped left at the split fin. Bring four large spheres to the west catch.*

And then, the tallies.

Near eye level on the wet walls, one of the oldest among them—broad as a millstone, their pale hide scarred with clean, healed linear seams from old brushes with shifting faults, their mineral cloak stiff with centuries of precipitated chalk—is at work with a stone chisel and a heavy lump of chert. 

They press the flat of one spatulate hand against the weeping dolomite, feeling the microscopic tremble of the rock, while the bristles around their domed head flare wide to catch the exact interval between the metallic *cracks* of a nearby waterfall hitting an aerial shelf. *Crack... one, two, three... crack.* The shelf is dropping. Slowly—an inch a year, perhaps, or an inch an hour during a heavy spatial tide—the broken geometry of the basin drifts. 

*Chink. Chink.* The elder strikes two fresh white marks into the gray stone beside thousands of older ones, recording the migration of the invisible plane. 

Look closely at the older tallies further along the wall, and you realize why the cairns are built across shear-lines, why the huts hang where they do, and why thousands of quartz spheres lie rolled into neat rows across the gravel. They are not merely surviving the broken rules of the valley; they are tracking a vast, glacial pattern in the faults. The dry-stone cairn fifty feet up, sheared six inches to the left of its own base, is a gauge. When its top half drifts another two inches and finally topples, the crash will ring out at a specific pitch, warning the entire basin that a major seam has uncoupled. 

Even as you watch, the elder freezes. Their calcified bristles flatten tight against their head. On their chest-slate, their thumb strikes a rapid, urgent roll—*tink-tink-tink-tink-tink*—that echoes instantly from fifty hidden places across the stone forest.

All around you, the pale boulders move. Workers unhook from the pillars, drop their scraping ropes, and skate swiftly into very specific, worn hollows in the gravel, or tuck themselves tightly behind thin fins of slate, pulling their mantles down like limpets sealing themselves to tidal rocks. 

Two seconds later, the air ten feet above your head turns briefly into the dark, starless interior of deep stone—a black horizontal slit opening in the blinding glare—and forty tons of cold, subterranean water drops out of nowhere, hits an angled fault line with a sound like a cannon firing inside an iron tank, and shears sideways across the exact ledges where three workers had been crouching moments before, scouring the rock clean before vanishing into a dry wall with a sharp, metallic *snap*.

Silence follows for half a beat, save for the ringing in your ears. Then the normal, clattering applause resumes. 

Fifty feet away, the elder unseals their foot from a dry patch of gravel, glides out onto the newly scoured ledge while the water is still skipping into new, erratic ribbons, and pulls out their chisel. They tap the wet rock once to test its ring, find the new edge of the invisible seam with a careful, boneless sweep of one four-thumbed hand, and strike the first mark of a new column into the stone.
