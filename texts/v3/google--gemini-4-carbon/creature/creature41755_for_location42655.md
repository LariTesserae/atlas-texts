If you stay on your knees beside one of the hair-thin filaments and keep looking through its terminal bladder—accepting the slight leftward displacement of the rock behind it—you begin to see what your unaided eyes edited out. 

The dark, two-dimensional seams hanging an inch above the slate are not empty. Draped across the hairline faults like wet cigarette paper are flat, translucent organisms no thicker than a fingernail’s paring and wide as a saucer. Seen straight on, they are invisible; their refractive index matches the damp air. Seen through the angled water-lens of the plant, they show a faint, oily iridescence. They have no mouths and no undersides. Half of each body hangs on this side of the spatial tear and half extends into whatever misjoined plane lies on the other, anchoring the creature in the seam the way mortar sits between bricks. They feed on the condensation that crowds the faults and on microscopic grit shaken loose—not from the rock, which never erodes, but from the air itself, where tiny flakes of grey keratin drift down from five miles overhead.

Those flakes come from the ribbons against the pewter ceiling. 

Up where the thin air gives way to near-vacuum, the valley’s trapped roar cannot travel any farther; the acoustic energy hits the void and reflects back downward like surf hitting a seawall. The pale ribbons ride that boundary. They are neither birds nor worms, though if one ever tore loose and fell—which happens perhaps once in a lifetime—you would find a strip of tough, dry cartilage forty feet long and an inch thick, ribbed lengthwise like corduroy and entirely hollow. Alive, they are colonial membranes without heads or eyes, spread flat on the standing wave of the thunder. The continuous vibration flexing their milliards of microscopic chambers generates just enough piezoelectric or mechanical warmth to keep their dry, light cells dividing, shedding dead skin from their upper surfaces as they crawl endlessly from rim to rim along grooves in the noise.

Down on the floor, nothing gets its warmth for free. 

Watch one of the dish-eared ungulates long enough—hours, until the cold seeps through the knees of your trousers and the roar has numbed your jaw—and you realize why its legs are so long and thin, and why it never bends them. Its joints are almost entirely fused. The animal’s skeleton is less a set of levers than a tuning fork: dense, heavily mineralized pillars in the limbs supporting a ribcage of flat, overlapping cartilage slats that flex silently with the valley’s *shhhhhhhhhhh-krrrrrr* rather than resisting it. Its wide, downward-angled ear-dishes are not listening for predators. Lined with tiny, fluid-filled pits, they read the interference patterns where the sourceless thunder passes through the slate floor and snags on the valley’s broken geometry. 

To walk across this basin step by step is to drag flesh across hairline fractures in distance—places where an inch is covertly three yards, or where two planes shear past one another. A running animal would eventually misstep into a seam and leave a leg behind, or exhaust its meager body heat spending forty minutes crossing what looked like fifty paces. The ungulates do not cross space; they wait for it to line up. Standing motionless over a bruised-plum rosette, the animal angles its ear-dishes until it locates an adjacency—a fold where the patch of slate six feet to the east touches the patch beneath its hooves along an unseen crease. A sharp contraction of its flank muscles, a greasy optical smear as the wet air parts and snaps shut, and the animal is simply *there*. Even its grazing relies on this. It does not tear the tough, rubbery rosettes with teeth; it closes its mouth over a leaf and shifts three inches sideways, letting the discontinuity shear the plant cleanly at the stem before chewing with slow, horizontal grinds of flat molars.

When one dies, it does not rot. Bacteria barely function in the thin, near-freezing damp. Left on open slate, a carcass would remain intact for centuries, slowly glazing over with beads of motionless water. Yet there are no carcasses in the valley, only the small spirals of pale knucklebones laid out on the floors of the cairns.

Which brings you back to your own bootprint in the wet grit outside the stone shelter. 

If you sit beside that shelter long enough for the basin’s visual tricks to settle—ignoring the horizon peeling upward at the edges of your vision, ignoring the conviction that you are sitting at the bottom of a bowl—you notice something wrong about the ground around the low doorway. Your bootprint is crisp, filling slowly from beneath with clear, cold seepage. Nowhere else in the grit is there another print. Yet the slate tablet just inside the dark entrance has fresh water beaded *inside* its parallel grooves, as though a damp thumb ran down them twenty minutes ago, disturbing the rows of surface tension.

Whoever uses the cairns is still here. You have probably looked directly at one of them three times since entering the pass.

Look again at the row of upright stone slabs near the eastern shelf—seven from the south, five from the north, with no gaps where the missing two belong. The space containing those two slabs does not vanish when viewed from the north; your sightline simply skates over the pleat it sits in, stitching the fifth slab seamlessly to the edge of the basin behind it. A strip of ground forty feet wide and half a mile long lies folded inside that northern blind spot. There are dozens of such pleats crisscrossing the five miles of slate, shifting depending on where an observer stands, and the cairns were not built as houses. They were built as survey monuments. Spaced half a mile apart, rounded to match the bedrock, they allow anyone who knows their alignments to tell at a glance which pleats are open and which are pinched shut from any given spot on the floor. 

There are fewer than twenty of the cairn-people alive in the basin at any time, though the shelters number over a hundred, accumulated over millennia in a place where dry-stone masonry never tumbles. Whether their ancestors climbed up from some vanished lower world before the altitude thinned, carrying that single wooden bowl before wood became an impossible memory, or whether they were caught here when the ancient cataclysm packed up its rock and left its noise behind, human taxonomy hardly applies to them anymore.

You see one first not by movement, but by breath—except there is no steam in air that already holds all the cold moisture it can manage. Instead, twenty paces away beside what looks like a kneeling boulder, a row of water droplets clinging to grey texture abruptly lets go all at once, sliding two inches down and stopping.

The boulder has exhaled.

They are short, scarcely four and a half feet tall if they ever stood upright, though uprightness wastes heat and snags the eye. Broad-hipped, barrel-chested to wring oxygen from the starved air, they wear coats made from ungulate pelts layered four deep and stitched with sinew. Because nothing decays and nothing dries, a coat outlives three wearers; over centuries of cold sweat, marrow-grease, and mineral seepage, the pelts mat and cure into a flexible, slate-colored carapace an inch thick. Pulled down over tucked knees, with the hood drawn forward, a person sitting still on the stone has the exact matte luster and weathered curve of the cairns. 

Beneath the hood, the face is wide, flat-featured, and pale as tallow, smeared with fat to keep the permanent damp from blistering the skin. There is no hair—hair traps water against the scalp and steals warmth—only thick pads of cartilage around the brow and temples. Most unsettling to an outsider is the mouth. At rest, their jaws hang half an inch open, lips slack, a thick, dark-purple tongue resting high against the palate to warm the thin air before it hits the throat. They never let their teeth touch except briefly when chewing. In a world where a sub-audible avalanche vibrates perpetually inside the skull, clenching your jaw turns your own molars into hammers; within ten years, touching teeth would grind one another down to raw nerve. So they live slack-jawed, breathing twice a minute in slow, cavernous draws, eyes dark and heavily lidded against the shadowless glare of the pewter sky.

They do not speak. Nothing with lungs could out-shout the valley, and even inside the shelters the roar arrives undiminished through the soles of the feet and the base of the skull. Instead, when two of them crawl into the same horse-sized cairn and pull a flat stone across the doorway—not to block the noise, which is impossible, but to block sightlines—they sit knee to knee in the pitch dark and press their bare foreheads or sternums together. 

Bone against bone, the continuous roar inside one chest meets the roar inside the other. By subtly tightening the diaphragm, filling one lung higher than the other, or shifting the heavy tongue against the hollow of the roof of the mouth, a person alters the resonant chamber of their own torso. Against another person’s ribs, this creates slow, thrumming beat-frequencies—tiny cancellations and swells inside the thunder. A pulse of relative quiet behind the breastbone; a sharp buzz in the collarbones; two slow phase-shifts rolling up into the sinus cavities. It is a language with no vowels, no air, and no range beyond skin-contact, patient and immensely dense. Two people who have not crossed paths in forty sleeps will sit locked sternum-to-sternum for six hours without moving a finger, catching up on which spatial tears have widened, where the plum rosettes are thickening, and which ungulates are heavy with marrow.

For distance, they leave the tablets and the bones. 

The parallel grooves scored into slate are read with the side of the thumb when light is deceptive, recording sightline counts and step-ratios. The spirals of ungulate knucklebones are reckonings of folded ground: which path walks true, and which path will swallow an afternoon without bringing a boulder an inch closer. And the black water in the ancient, petrified wooden bowl—and in shallow stone dishes kept in other cairns—serves the same purpose as the lens-bladders of the filament plants, only wider. Set at a precise tilt on a cairn’s floor, the oil-dark surface reflects the doorway at a grazing angle, showing the valley outside stripped of its dish-shaped optical illusion. Before emerging, a keeper crouches over the bowl, checks the reflection to see what stands in the pleats invisible from the threshold, dips two fingers to wet the grooves of a slate, and steps out.

They do not run down their prey. Nothing here runs. Hunting is an act of patient architecture. 

Weeks earlier—though without sun or stars they measure duration only by sleeps and the slow refilling of water-prints—two keepers will carry flat stones out onto the shelf and set them down in specific spots, six inches high, utterly unremarkable. To an ungulate’s downward-facing ear-dishes, however, a dense stone placed squarely on an acoustic node spoils the interference pattern of the bedrock beneath it. That patch of ground goes "sour"; the animal can no longer skip onto it. Over sleeps and sleeps, shifting one stone a handspan north, turning another on its edge, the keepers quietly herd an ungulate’s teleportation habits across a quarter-mile of slate without ever spooking it. 

Eventually, the animal shifts six feet eastward to browse a patch of rosettes and arrives between the fifth and seventh upright slabs on the eastern shelf. 

From the south, it walked in freely. From the north, two keepers step out of a blind fold and drop a single heavy lintel stone flat onto the gravel, completing an acoustic dead-zone. Suddenly the space around the animal has no adjacencies left. Every seam is pinched shut. When the ungulate contracts its flanks to skip away, nothing happens; the air does not smear. Trapped in ordinary, continuous three-dimensional distance for the first time since it was dropped wet from its mother’s side, the creature panics and tries to *walk*—lifting one long, fused-jointed leg after another in a stiff, rocking-horse stagger across the slick slate. It makes three clumsy strides before its hooves slip out from under it. A keeper steps in, never hurrying, never raising their heart rate faster than the thin air can feed it, and presses a long, ground-slate blade behind the ear-dish.

Nothing is wasted. The blood is drunk cold before it can bead and roll away. The meat, stringy and dark as liver from extreme altitude, is sliced into strips and laid directly across one of the black, two-dimensional spatial seams; the flat, iridescent seam-dwellers swarm the cut edges to drink the serum, sterilizing and freeze-drying the flesh into grey leather that keeps forever inside a coat pocket. Cartilage ribs become splints and thatch-pins for pelts; fat goes onto skin and leather; marrow is scooped raw with bone spoons. Only the dense, pale knucklebones—too hard to chew, useless for blades—are carried back to the nearest cairn and added to the spiral on the floor, recording one less grazer in the eastern sector, until new fawns appear in the pleats.

If you stay in the basin, they will not attack you. Violence takes explosive breath, and explosive breath brings black spots before the eyes and fluid into the lungs within two minutes. 

Instead, they simply remain in the missing fractions of the compass and wait to see what you are made of. At some point while you are walking forty minutes toward a boulder that looked fifty paces off, someone thirty yards away in a northern fold steps cleanly across a two-step adjacency, squats beside your crisp bootprint at the cairn, and studies it—measuring the depth of your weight in the grit, touching the side of a calloused thumb to the weave-mark of your rubber sole, looking into the black water of the stone-hard bowl to watch your tiny, distorted back receding across the tilted floor of the valley. 

If you collapse eventually from hypothermia or altitude sickness, they will wait until your chest stops fighting the roar before coming out of the angle to see how your coat is stitched, and what strange, rust-prone iron or brittle glass you carried up from a world where things still rot and shadows still turn around stones. If, on the other hand, you stop walking wildly across broken sightlines, sit with your back against a rounded rib of basalt, let your jaw drop slightly open so your teeth stop chattering against the thunder in your skull, and begin drinking the cold rows of water beads with your tongue rather than wasting effort cupping your hands—then, after an uncounted stretch of pewter gloom, you may look down at the slate beside your knee and find three pale knucklebones arranged in a line pointing four degrees left of the nearest cairn, showing you the way in that only takes six steps.
