To see what truly lives in the basin, you have to stop looking at the sky and start looking at the knots.

Underneath a great, heaving mat of staghorn ferns and barrel-orchids—three miles out from the nearest solid cliff, suspended over nothing—the root-tangle is dark, bruised-looking, and densely packed. Down here, away from the blinding sun, the noise is different. The high, tinny clatter of the waxy upper leaves is muffled, replaced by a deep, creaking groan, like the hull of a wooden ship in a heavy sea. Every time an updraft punches the underside of the mat, the whole world rises swiftly, pressing everything inside it down with heavy, sudden gravity; then the gust shears away, the mat drops forty feet, and everything inside goes weightless for two full seconds before the main anchor-vines snap taut with a sound like a muffled gunshot.

In that dark, heaving interior, unaffected by the sickening drop, something the size of a terrier uncurls its six limbs and reaches for a crab.

They are called *gale-apes* by the people of the distant rim, though they are neither apes nor true six-limbed beasts; the fifth and sixth "arms" are actually modified, bone-plated tails split at the base of the pelvis, each ending in a horny, three-pronged hook. They have no fur to mat in the flash-mists. Instead, they are covered in overlapping, shingle-like scales of soft, oily keratin, dull olive and mottled brown, slick enough that water beads and shoots off them instantly. Their eyes are huge, yellow, and set flat into the front of narrow skulls, covered by clear second eyelids that stay shut almost permanently against the stinging debris and sideways spray. 

They never walk. There is nowhere to walk. They move through the interior of the root-mats with a fluid, horrifyingly fast hand-over-hand scuttle, always keeping at least three hooks locked onto the woody tendrils. When two of them meet in the swaying dark, they do not vocalize—nothing with lungs the size of walnuts could compete with the organ-roar of the stone arches. Instead, one reaches out and grips the wrist of the other's anchor-limb, tapping a rapid, percussive rhythm directly against the bone of the forearm. They talk through tremors. They gossip, threaten, and mate entirely by vibration, hanging upside down over a mile of bright blue emptiness while stripping the pale, armored crabs from the fern-bases with narrow, crushing jaws.

Out on the sunlit skin of the canopy, life is flatter, tougher, and stranger.

Clinging to the primary suspension cables—those ancient, braided vine-trunks as thick as hogsheads—are things that look like blisters of gray bark until the mist hits. When a shred of cloud tears through a sector, turning the air white and drenching the rigging, the blisters swell, split down the center, and extrude wide, feathery fans of vivid crimson gill-tissue. These are the *cloud-grazers*: shell-less, muscular slugs the length of a man's forearm, held to the thrashing vines by suction feet so powerful that if you tried to pry one loose with an iron bar, the vine would tear before the foot let go. For the eight minutes the mist lasts, they comb the sideways-shooting vapor for wind-borne algae, fungal spores, and the microscopic, translucent shrimp that breed inside the water-filled cups of the giant bromeliads. The moment the sun breaks through and the steam begins to whip off the leaves, the crimson fans snap back inside, the tough gray mantle seals shut with a greasy *pop*, and the creature becomes dry bark again before the heat can bake it.

And then there are the hunters of the open air, which do not fly so much as they weaponize falling.

The stingray-like gliders you see riding the thermals are merely the grazers of the open gulf, peeling lichen from the high needles and snapping up bursts of wind-tossed insects. What hunts *them* hangs motionless beneath the collar-bone arches of pale quartzite, tucked into the dead-air pockets on the leeward side of the stone. 

It looks like a bundle of dead, dried vine-husks, six feet long, hanging by one immensely long, sinewy leg. It has wings, of a sort, though they are folded so tightly against its narrow ribcage they look like sleek, polished shields of bone and tough black leather. It does not beat them. Nothing with hollow bird-bones could survive the shearing crosswinds here; one bad vortex would snap a seagull's wings like dry twigs. This creature’s bones are solid, dense, flexible cartilage, heavy as wet oak. 

It waits until a glider, or an unlucky gale-ape flung loose from a snapping orchid-mat, goes tumbling past in a thermal column. Then the bundle of dead husks simply lets go of the rock. 

Tucked tight like an iron plumb-bob, it drops—or if caught in a boiling updraft, rockets *upward*—steering with minute shifts of its dense, heavy body, cutting clean across the chaotic currents because of its sheer mass relative to its narrow profile. It hits its prey like a thrown spear, wraps all four hooked limbs around the target, and only *then* pops open a small, thick-skinned braking chute between its hind legs, spilling just enough air to swing the both of them on a long, pendulum arc straight into a hanging curtain of moss fifty yards below, crashing deep into the tangle before they can fall into the abyss.

And then, moving through the roaring green rigging like spiders across a torn web, come the chisel-bearers.

If you are waiting inside one of the woven-bone bunkers when a party of them arrives out of a flash-mist, your first impression is that they are horribly deformed—hunchbacked, short-legged, with arms the thickness of a blacksmith’s thighs and faces made of brass and thick leather. 

Then they unclip from the safety lines, crawl through the narrow, flexing sphincter of woven reed that serves as a door, and begin peeling off their gear in the dim, creaking shelter.

They are human, though three thousand years of working the rigging have pared them down to essentials. Nobody tall survives long in the basin; long bones are just long levers for the wind to snap, and high centers of gravity get plucked off the stone pedestals. These people rarely clear five feet, compact and dense as river-cobbles. Their legs are slightly bowed, the big toes widely spaced and muscled like thumbs, calloused to the texture of horn from gripping rough quartzite and wet vine-bark—for they go entirely barefoot even in the fiercest gales, needing to feel the micro-tremors in a cable that warn when an anchor-root half a mile away is beginning to tear loose from its spire.

Their upper bodies are staggering. Shoulders, lats, and forearms are corded with thick, slow-twitch muscle built from a lifetime of hauling their own weight—plus forty pounds of stone counterweights and bronze tools—hand-over-hand across bucking three-hundred-yard spans. Every adult’s hands are permanently curved; even asleep on the floor of the bunker, their fingers never fully uncurl, resting in heavy, scarred hooks.

When they strip off their wind-masks—snouted things of boiled glider-hide fitted with slit-goggles of thin, smoky quartz to keep the sixty-mile-an-hour mist from flaying their corneas—their faces are broad, flat-featured, and deeply lined around the eyes and mouth, their skin burned a rich, uniform mahogany by the fierce, thin-air sun and the bright glare reflecting off the white clouds below. 

Notice the silence inside the bunker. Even with the woven walls cutting the wind down to a dull, shuddering roar that vibrates the teeth in your skull, they do not speak aloud. Many of the older ones couldn't hear you if you shouted an inch from their ear; the constant barometric hammering of the turbulent air and the concussive *boom* of snapping vine-bridges rupture their eardrums before they reach twenty. 

Instead, they sit close together in the cramped, swaying basket of the shelter, knees touching, and speak with their hands. Not in the air, where a sudden drop of the floor might throw a gesture off, and not where eyes are needed to watch the rigging—they sign directly onto one another’s bodies. A woman leans back against her brother’s chest while mending a fraying harness; his left hand rests against her collarbone, fingers tapping, sliding, squeezing in a rapid, fluid grammar of pressure and rhythm, while her own spare hand answers against his shin. Four of them can hold an intricate, overlapping argument about weather patterns in complete darkness while chewing dried slug-meat, passing jokes that make their heavy shoulders shake with silent, wheezing laughter.

Why are they here? 

Because the basin is a barrier four hundred miles long between two halves of a continent, and the abyss below is an unnavigable soup of crushing heat, poisonous swamps, and blind predators that never see the sun. To go around the basin takes four months through arid, bandit-ridden badlands. To cross *through* the canopy takes six days—if you know how to read the chords.

They call themselves something that translates, roughly, to *The Tuners*, though the merchants waiting at the rim-towns call them the Line-Wardens. They do not live in the bunkers permanently; infants born in the constant, whipping zero-gravity drops of the canopy mats develop inner-ear sicknesses and fail to thrive. Their true villages are tucked into deep, quiet clefts in the solid rock of the rim, five miles back from the edge, where the ground doesn't move and the children find the silence terrifying at first. 

Out here in the green gulf, they work in rotations of four or five weeks, moving constantly along the spiderweb of routes. They are half-ferrymen, half-arborists, and half-organ-builders.

Watch a team of three working on a narrow quartzite pedestal in the bright, blasting afternoon. They move low to the pale stone, bellies nearly scraping rock, clipped together by thick braids of oiled hair and plant-silk, heavy river-stones slung low at their hips in leather aprons to keep sudden updrafts from lofting them like kites. 

One of them—an old man with white stubble and forearms scarred white by decades of acid-sap burns—lies flat at the very lip of the drop, bare feet wedged into chiseled toe-holds. He isn't looking at the view. His eyes are shut, and he has his jaw pressed hard against the haft of a bronze chisel driven into a seam of the quartzite. 

He is listening with his skull.

Half a mile out, across a whipping green sea of broad-leaved creepers, a stone arch has been carved over twelve generations to hum in a deep, thrumming minor third whenever the crosswinds shear from the north-west. Today, the chord is flat, and underneath it, traveling through the taut vine-cables anchored to this very pedestal, there is a rhythmic, stuttering *thrum-thrum-catch*. 

A primary vine—one of the great highway cables—has caught a massive, drifting mat of dead bromeliads torn loose from an upper spire. The added sail-area is catching too much wind; the anchor-roots are groaning, preparing to blow out and take six acres of established bridge-canopy down into the haze with them.

The old man pulls his jaw off the chisel, crawls backward two yards, and taps three sharp clusters of pressure against a young woman's ankle. 

She doesn't hesitate. She checks the toggle-locks on her harness—heavy, curved hooks of dark meteoric iron polished mirror-bright by friction—straps a curved, saw-toothed pruning blade to her wrist, and steps straight off the edge of the pedestal.

She doesn't fall; she drops twenty feet until her slack-line snaps taut against the main highway vine, taking the bone-jarring yank across a wide, padded leather girdle around her pelvis. Instantly, the wind catches her, swinging her horizontal like a flag in a hurricane, ninety degrees out over a vertical mile of sunlit air. She doesn't even glance down. Waiting for the three-second lull that always follows a double-gust, she swings her compact body up, locks her prehensile bare feet onto the coarse, wrist-thick bark of the cable, hooks one iron toggle over the guide-vine, and begins pulling herself out into the roaring blue void—fast, rhythmic, economical, looking from a distance like nothing more than another armored, indifferent beetle crawling out onto a thrashing stem to clear the rigging before the next cloud hits.
