If you watch one of the cloaked figures crossing a high causeway, your first impression is of an error in perspective. They lean forty degrees into the wind, motionless from the waist down, sliding forward with a long, skating stride while their heavy garments snap and drum behind them like sheet metal. Beneath the cloak, you expect a heavy body to anchor such defiance of the gale. 

When they pass through the parting cellulose of a tower door and shed their outer wraps, the illusion of mass vanishes. 

The citizens of the basin are tall, narrow-chested, and breathtakingly light. Evolution, assisted five thousand years ago by deliberate, unsentimental self-tailoring, has pared away everything that requires water to maintain. They have no fat, no sweat glands, no wet mucous membranes, and no soft, exposed flesh. Their skin is a smooth, matte covering of flexible keratinous plates, fine as snake-belly scales around the joints and hardening into seamless, pale-ivory chutes along the shins, forearms, and collarbones. In the dim amber and ultraviolet drift of the tower interiors, they look as if they have been carved from old soapstone and articulated with dark, oiled parchment.

Their faces are still and angular, devoid of the tiny, wet musculature that produces fleeting expressions in wetter species. There are no lips to purse, only a neat, horizontal seam bordered by dense, sensory bristles that test the hydrocarbon-rich air before opening. Their eyes do not blink. They have no tear ducts to wash away the abrasive chalk-dust of the plains; instead, their eyes are solid, faceted domes of clear organic quartz, faintly polarized against the glare of the pale root-floor and the ultraviolet glow of the walls. To see into the gloom, those dark, glassy domes drink in a spectrum stretching from deep thermal infrared up into the violet bands of the data-sap. When a citizen looks at you, there is no wet glint of recognition—only a faint, internal shift of refracted amber light as the crystalline lenses focus deep inside the socket.

They do not breathe with a gentle rise and fall of the chest. Thin, cold air requires volume and pressure. Set beneath their sharp, high ribs is a pair of muscular, bellows-like sacs lined with electrostatic cilia. Inhaling produces a dry, rhythmic hiss through baffled slits behind the jaw; exhaling is a sharp *puff* that ejects trapped mineral dust in a tiny, faintly sparkling gray plume. Inside their veins runs not water-based blood, but a dark, low-viscosity perfluorocarbon suspension spiced with volatile lipids—a fluid that will not freeze when they walk the wind-scoured plains, and that smells, if they are ever cut, sharply of crushed pine needles and ozone.

Because the air of the valley belongs entirely to the roar of the wind and the shattering-porcelain clatter of the glass-scrub, the human voice was abandoned long ago as an absurd waste of breath. You cannot shout across a causeway when the bridge itself is humming like a plucked bass string ten meters wide. 

Instead, they speak through the floor.

If you look at their bare feet inside the towers, you see that the heel is elongated into a dense, calcified spur, while the long toes fan out into wide, sensitive pads ribbed like corduroy. To speak in a room, a citizen taps, scrapes, and presses that heel-spur against the taut leaf-chitin membrane of the floor. They send crisp, complex packets of vibration skittering across the tensioned architecture, which their peers read effortlessly through the soles of their own feet or the palms of their long, four-jointed hands resting lightly against a wall. 

A crowded hall inside a municipal spire appears, to an outsider’s ears, almost eerie in its lack of vocalization. People stand in loose clusters, completely silent save for the bellows-hiss of their breathing and a rapid, intricate *tick-tock-skitter-thrum* beneath their heels, sounding like heavy rain on a dry gourd. Intimacy is tactile and direct: close friends and lovers converse by pressing two fingers against the resonant hollow of the other’s collarbone, murmuring directly into the light, hollow lattice of their partner's skeleton. To shout across a room is a sharp stomp; laughter is a rapid, fluttering trill of the toes that makes the translucent floor shimmer underfoot.

Their entire lives are organized around tension, resonance, and the slow, computational botany of their cities. They call themselves something that translates roughly as *Lignin-Tenders* or *Chord-Keepers*, though in practice they are less gardeners than luthiers living inside their instruments. 

Because the towers and causeways are alive—drawing geothermal heat from miles down and firing seed-flecks to mend their own wind-scoured flanks—architecture here is a continuous negotiation rather than a finished state. A bridge cannot simply be repaired with mortar; it must be *tuned*. Among the highest castes in the spires are the Strainers: lean individuals who spend their days out on the unrailed spans or clinging to the fluted leeward fins in stiffened cloaks woven from shed glider-cartilage and static-repelling filaments. They carry long, tuning-fork-like prods of dark metamaterial. Walking the paper-thin arches, leaning casually over fifty-meter drops while the gravity-shears grip the iron-doped plates woven into their boots, they listen through their bones to the cello-drone of the wood. 

They are listening for disharmony. A flat note in a causeway’s hum means a micro-fracture in the lignin six meters down; a sharp, frantic buzz in a tower wall means a sap-conduit is clotting with crystallized lipids or an unauthorized gall is forming in the genetic code. When a Strainer finds a sour chord, they do not cut the wood out. They inject minute, tailored chemical phages—drawn from vials worn like bandoliers across their chests—that instruct the living structure to tighten its grain, shift its gravity-shear, or calcify a weak joint. Under their care, the city heals itself over weeks, slowly pulling back into pitch.

Inside the towers, life is spare, warm, and deeply communal, clustered tightly around the massive central conduits where the supercooled sap dumps its deep-earth heat. Rooms are not divided by heavy walls, which would deaden the floor-speech, but by hanging screens of woven glass-scrub fiber that chime faintly in the drafts. Furniture does not exist as separate dead objects; sleeping niches, tables, and looms are coaxed upward directly from the floor membranes, swelling seamlessly out of the translucent chitin like smooth, amber-lit blisters.

They do not cook with water or fire—an open flame inside a dry, lipid-pumping cellulose chimney is the ultimate nightmare of their culture, the single taboo so visceral that even tapping the rhythmic root-word for *combustion* on a public floor is considered a violent obscenity. Instead, they eat dry, dense cakes pressed from roasted spore-pods, mineral-rich dust scraped from the plains, and the rendered, wax-like marrow of the ivory grazers. Meals are accompanied by small, porcelain cups of warm, volatile esters tapped from specific metabolic nodes in the tower walls—drinks that evaporate on the tongue in seconds, delivering a sudden, exhilarating rush of chemical warmth and sharp camphor-like flavor without leaving a drop of dampness behind.

Down on the basin floor live those who find the hum of the spires too crowded: the Dust-Sweepers and Herd-Walkers. Their keratin plates are thicker, stained slate-gray by static-charged chalk, their cloaks encrusted with flexible solar-absorbing scales to keep their core lipids warm away from the geothermal taproots. They stride alongside the clicking ivory grazers on long, spring-loaded stilts grown from ironwood, keeping high above the reach of the bristle-black shadow-predators. They harvest the shed hooves of the herds, gather electrostatic salts from the shallow hollows of the root-stone, and collect the metallic seed-flecks that miss their mark when the tower cannons boom overhead—valuable, dense packets of pure metamaterial and compressed genetics that would otherwise be snapped up by the whip-winged gliders.

Yet even out in the middle of the half-kilometer voids, where the sky is like cold lead and the porcelain roar of the scrub drowns out thought, no one is ever truly isolated from the city. The unbroken shelf of ancient root-stone beneath them is one continuous, tightly packed acoustic web. A Herd-Walker two kilometers from the nearest spire need only drop to one knee, press their jawbone or heel-spur hard against the polished, driftwood-smooth floor, and listen. Through the soles and teeth comes the vast, faint, unmistakable chord of the valley: the deep thud of the geothermal pumps, the cannon-boom of a distant spire seeding the wind, and the faint, shimmering *tick-skitter-thrum* of ten thousand distant heels talking in the warm, glowing wood above.

And when a citizen finally wears out—when their joints stiffen with accumulated mineral silicates and their internal lipids grow sluggish—there is no burial in a ground that has no soil, and no rot in an air that has no dampness. 

Instead, they climb. Accompanied by their kin, tapping slow, resonant farewell rhythms into the spiral ramps, the dying ascend to the perforated, howling crowns at the very top of the spires, above the spore-vents, where the wind hits hardest. There, in recessed alcoves grown into the windward blades, they shed their cloaks and sit facing the gale. The living inject a mild dormant-seed catalyst into the hollow of the elder’s collarbone and leave them to the cold. 

They freeze and desiccate cleanly within an hour, never decaying. Over the following seasons, the bone-white wood of the tower slowly swells up around their seated, soapstone-pale forms, drinking back their rare trace minerals, their carbon lattices, and the dark metamaterials of their tools. Within ten years, they are gone—drawn entirely back into the fluted geometry of the crown, becoming another calcified fin or hollow flute at the top of the world, shaping the fierce, dry wind into the deep bass drone that vibrates down through the soles of their grandchildren's feet.
