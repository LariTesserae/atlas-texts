Up close, the people of the basin look as though the wind and the glare have worked on them just as patiently as they worked on the marl. 

Their faces are permanently drawn inward around the eyes—not a temporary squint against a bright afternoon, but a deep, structural folding of the brow and cheek, the skin creased into pale, leathery fans at the temples while the bridge of the nose remains smooth and taut. When they step out of their low doorways, they smear a paste of grey tallow and ground chalk across their cheekbones and lower lids to break the upward bounce of the light off the wet soapstone. Without hair to catch the drafts and whip into their vision—men, women, and children alike shave their scalps clean with sharp flakes of slate, weaving the cropped hair into the heavy, felt-like fabric of their cloaks—their heads appear stark and smooth, glistening faintly with the bird-oil they rub into their skin to keep the rapid evaporation from cracking them open.

They walk with a peculiar, ground-eating gait. Because the soapstone causeways turn from bone-dry to slick as tallow in the span of four breaths, nobody lifts their feet high and nobody locks their knees. They glide from the hips down, weight slung low, soles planted flat in broad moccasins cobbled from rough, salted crab-parchment and soft hide that grips the wet stone by suction rather than tread. Even when they sprint after a falling mist-squall, there is no pounding of heels. A dozen adults will shoot across an empty amphitheater with the smooth, skimming momentum of flat stones skipped across a pond, leaning at sharp, improbable angles into a sudden invisible wall of air, trusting the river-cobbles sewn into their hems to keep their skirts from blinding them.

Watch a team resetting a wind-baffle and you see how their minds are tethered to the invisible. Five people stand along a curving rib of stacked slate, twenty paces apart. None of them are looking at the stones at their feet. They are reading the micro-signs of the basin floor: the sudden, frantic scuttling of a sheet of pale crabs thirty yards north; the simultaneous banking of three heavy swifts over a dry cistern; a dark bloom of dampness racing sideways across a whale-backed swell of marl. 

One woman drops her left shoulder and flips her open palm backward twice. Instantly, without a nod of acknowledgment, the four others drop into squats and begin heaving the flat, thirty-pound slate slabs three paces to the west, re-stacking them in a staggered, friction-locked curve. Their hands are broad, thick-knuckled, and perpetually grey with stone-flour and grease, moving with blurring, practiced economy. They do not grunt when they lift; they exhale sharply through pursed lips, saving the heat and wet of their breath. The baffle goes up in ninety seconds. Ten seconds later, the errant downdraft hits it, curls neatly along the new stone spine, and funnels directly through a lattice of gutted, splayed swift carcasses hanging fifty yards downwind, stripping the moisture from the dark meat before it can sour.

Their language, out here in the open, belongs entirely to the silhouette. A finger twitch means nothing across a hundred yards of blinding glare, so their signing is vast, architectural, built of elbows, forearms, the tilt of a shaved head, and the flare of a weighted cloak. Two neighbors pausing on intersecting causeways will hold an entire, intricate negotiation over cistern-rights or a marriage match while thirty paces apart—one snapping an arm out horizontal against the white sky, dropping to one knee, sweeping a hand across the slick ground; the other answering with three sharp tilts of the chin and a sudden turning of the back. To an outsider, it looks like a slow, severe martial dance performed in scattered isolation across the mile-wide floor. 

Because they must always keep one eye on the shifting air and the speaking bodies of their neighbors, they have developed an uncanny peripheral stillness. They never fidget. An idle movement—a hand raised to scratch a neck, a shift of weight from hip to hip—reads as speech across a plaza, a false shout in a quiet room. When a basin-dweller has nothing to say and no gust to chase, they stand like one of the stacked-stone cairns: utterly motionless, arms folded beneath oiled leather, letting the chaotic drafts tear silently around them while their dark, narrow-slitted eyes track the pale birds banking at knee-height.

It is not only the birds and the crabs and the hounds that share this silence with them. Look closer at the hounds themselves.

They are unsettling things when one trots close enough for you to see the ribs beneath its grey, rubbery skin. Long-backed and deep-chested, with ears cropped—or bred—down to tight, valved slits flat against narrow skulls, they do not pant. Panting wastes water. Instead, great webs of thick blood vessels branch just beneath the bare skin of their flanks and long, whip-like tails, flushing dark purple when they run hard so the cold downdrafts can pull the heat straight through their hides. They were not bred to herd sheep or pull sleds; they are barometers and flushers. A hound will freeze in the middle of a dry causeway, its wide, split nostrils flaring violently as it tastes a change in air pressure half a mile up toward the plateau's lip, its body swinging like a weathervane to point at where the next heavy pocket of condensation is going to slam down. Half a second before the mist hits, the dog breaks into a dead, soundless run, and three water-catchers burst from a nearby dome with their gut-frames already unrolled, sprinting on the animal’s heels.

The hounds also hunt the seams. Where the ancient marl and chalky slate meet, deep, meandering fissures split the bedrock—narrow slots two inches wide and twenty feet deep where the vanished water of millennia ago retreated. Down in that sunless, damp dark live the things that never brave the glare: blind, pale-fleshed amphibians as thick as a man’s forearm, slick with bitter mucus, and mats of rubbery, translucent cave-fungus that feed on the mineral wash of the surface salts. At twilight—when the blinding white sky dims slowly through pearl-grey to an opaque, starless slate—children go out with the hounds and long, flexible ribs of cured whale-like bone scavenged from the ancient marl fossils. The dogs press their narrow muzzles to the stone cracks and blow sharp, silent puffs of air down into the rock, listening with their jaws pressed flat to the stone for the wet *slap* of something shifting below, before tapping a padded foot twice to show a child where to slide the baited bone-hook down.

To hear a human voice in this town, you have to crawl on your hands and knees through one of the low, curved wind-tunnels that serve as doorways, pushing past two successive curtains of heavy, oiled hide.

Inside the corbeled domes, the world reverses itself. After the scouring white vastness of the basin, the interiors are tiny, pitch-black, and shockingly close. There is no fire—with those long-dead kilns out in the plazas went the last scrub-wood of an older, wetter age—so warmth comes entirely from bodies, deep fermentation pits, and stone lamps burning tiny wicks of braided hair soaked in clear bird-fat, giving off a dim, amber glow no bigger than a plum. The air inside smells fiercely of salt, rancid tallow, wet dog, cured crab-roe, and clean grey clay. The walls are lined three feet thick with dry lichen-peat and shed hound-skins to hold the heat in and keep the sub-audible pressure-thrum of the wind from rattling the skull.

Here, in the dark, they speak. 

Because their voices are never thrown across distances, never strained over an anvil or shouted into a gale, their spoken tongue has withered down to a continuous, rapid-fire murmur—all soft sibilants, liquid clicks of the tongue against the teeth, and low, humming vowels vibrating in the throat. Eight or ten people—an entire extended house-clan—will pack together on raised shelves of smooth slate warmed from beneath by composting crab-shells and midge-meal, greasing hides or kneading damp marl-clay in the dark. Without the wide spaces required for their daytime arm-speech, they talk by touch and whisper at once. Hands rest constantly on a neighbor’s knee, forearm, or collarbone, fingers tapping out tiny, miniaturized echoes of the great plaza gestures against skin while their mouths keep up a ceaseless, purring undercurrent no louder than water trickling into a jar. 

Strangers who have descended the three-hundred-foot scree slopes from the upper crust rarely last a week among them. Out in the plazas, visitors stumble constantly on the mirror-slick stone, weeping blind from the upward glare, shouting ragged, panicked questions that the wind rips straight from their teeth while the oiled, bald-headed townsfolk skim past them at a dead run without turning their heads—not out of cruelty, only because the visitors are standing noisy and clumsy in the middle of a sentence being spoken by the mist. And if the stranger is led down into a dome for the night, the sudden, velvet claustrophobia of twelve greased bodies whispering wetly in the dark while the hide roof *thrums* overhead like a beaten drum drives them scrambling back out into the cold, bright, rushing hush before dawn.

Even the dead are given back to the motion of the bowl. There is no soil deep enough to bury a body, and no fuel to burn one in the cold beehive kilns. Instead, when an elder’s breath finally dries in their throat, the clan washes the salt and tallow from the body with precious cistern water and carries them out past the last ring of domes, toward the far western curve where the basin floor tilts gradually up toward the sky. There, on broad, flat tables of ancient soapstone set squarely in the path of the fiercest, driest katabatic chutes spilling off the plateau, they lay the body out naked beneath a weighted net of braided hair-rope. 

The pale swifts do not touch them; the crabs will not cross the dry salt-rings swept around the stone tables. Only the wind works there. Within three days, the violent, unseen river of dry air drinks every drop of moisture from tissue and marrow alike, shrinking skin tight and pale against the frame until the person is as light as hollow drift-bone, odorless and incorruptible. Later—months or years on, nobody hurries—children will come out and unpeg the hair-net, stacking the dry, smooth-rubbed ribs and skulls neatly into the curving wind-baffles near the family’s courtyard, so the old ones can keep helping steer the dampness toward the cisterns, still standing quiet in the glare, still turning the wind to work.
