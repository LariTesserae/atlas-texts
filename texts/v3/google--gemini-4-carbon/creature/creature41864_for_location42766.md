If you stay inside the bowl past the first shock of heat and noise—if you sit still on the warm granite near the curving wall until your ears stop flinching and begin sorting the roar into parts—you realize the basin does not run on daylight. The sun overhead is merely lighting. Time here is kept by the artifacts, and none of the seven or eight objects agree on the hour.

Each precipitates on its own fixed interval—one every eleven minutes and change, the slumped black wire every fourteen, the cantilevered dish closer to sixteen—so they drift continually in and out of phase across the afternoon. Because of this, water is always arriving somewhere and vanishing somewhere else, and nearly everything mobile in the forty-yard hollow spends its life chasing the most recent *thwack*.

Watch the columns of jumping crustaceans first, because they draw the map of the basin’s hunger. They are terrestrialized amphipods the length of rice grains, plated in waxy, pale-ochre shells that gleam like varnished fingernails against the rock. Sixty seconds before a given artifact snaps its mist, when no human eye can yet see the air’s refractive blur changing, a thicket ten paces away begins to seethe at its dry threshold. Thousands of the crustaceans crowd the outer rim of the moss mat, antennae quivering in unison—sensing either a subtle electrostatic buildup in the granite or the minute drop in local barometric pressure before vapor collapses. The instant the *thwack* sounds and fog boils white around the neighboring node, the column launches across the dry interval in synchronized, knee-high arcs that look like handfuls of flung gravel.

The pale sprint-lizards do not chase them randomly; they work the schedule. Before crossing an open stretch themselves, two or three lizards will press their throats flat to the satin stone to feel which thicket is vibrating with pre-jump crowding, then station themselves midway along that exact corridor, bodies raised high on stilt-like toes so their bellies clear the hot rock. Their flat, shovel-shaped snouts are built less for snapping at midair prey than for wedging. When they aren't intercepting migrating amphipods, a lizard will dart to the base of a shrub where a thumb-sized iridescent beetle has dropped to feed on fallen petals, jam that horny, beveled snout underneath the insect’s armored skirt, and flick upward with a whip of its neck. Flipped onto its curved, fiercely buzzing wing-cases, the beetle loses traction on the polished granite and spins out onto the open stone like a struck top, shrieking a higher, frantic harmonic for the two seconds it takes the lizard to pin it and slit its soft ventral seams.

Even the water itself has riders. Near the artifacts where fallen mist flattens into coin-wide, frictionless lenses that skitter across dry stone along warped local field lines, tiny copper-colored mites—broad and flat as fish scales, fringed with stiff wax bristles—wait perched on overhanging grass-like sedges. As a lens of water slides beneath a stem, three or four drop squarely onto its upper surface without breaking tension. Left alone, the lens would follow a shallow, millennia-worn groove down to the nearest established root mat. Instead, the mites walk in unison to one edge of the sliding water-coin and dip their serrated hind legs out through the meniscus so their claws drag against the dry granite beneath. Acting as living rudders, they bank the frictionless droplet sideways off its stone track, steering twenty or thirty milliliters of stolen moisture across bare rock into a smaller, drier tussock five feet out of course—a plant whose swollen stem-nodes exude tiny pearls of lipid-rich paste specifically to feed the pilots that bring it water. Thickets here compete by piracy, starving or fattening one another one steered droplet at a time.

Around the artifacts themselves, where physics bends sharpest, live specialists that cannot survive ten feet away in ordinary warmth.

Beneath the shallow cantilevered dish—the one hovering three inches off the rock with nothing under it—the cluster of translucent snails is only the outer ring of a tight concentric court. The snails graze in slow, clockwise spirals that never reverse, their shells paper-thin because calcium is scarce on pure granite; when one dies, four neighbors rasp its shell to nothing within an hour, passing the same pinch of mineral down ten thousand generations. Deeper into the three-inch gap directly beneath the dish’s belly, where the air feels slightly dense and resistant to a poked finger, hang pale, pleated curtains no thicker than wet tissue paper. They are colonial tunicate-like filterers cemented upside down to the underside of the hover-field itself—not quite touching the dish’s dark material, suspended against an invisible ceiling half a millimeter below it—rippling softly as they strain bacteria and airborne fungal spores sucked sideways whenever the nearby air collapses into fog.

Ten yards east, the slumped frame of black, light-drinking wire looks completely barren until one of the red-eyed, six-winged flyers hovers too close while waiting for mist. Then an inch of the black wire unfolds sideways on hinged forelegs. Half a dozen narrow, stick-bodied mantids—no longer than sewing needles—roost end-to-end along the artifact’s upper span. Over eons their chitin has developed a microscopic honeycomb of upright carbon-dark fibrils that trap ninety-nine percent of incident daylight. Out on the creamy granite they would stand out like spilled ink; pressed tight along the light-drinking wire, they have no highlights, no shadow edges, and no discernible depth, invisible even to the compound metallic eyes hovering three inches from their jaws until a pink interior mouth opens to strike.

And at the center of the bowl, between the two five-foot matte-gray rods, the invisible barrier that deflects dust and seeds has been turned into an aerial slaughterhouse by cousins of the parasol-carrying scavengers. Because neither the rods nor the unseen plane between them can be gripped by glue or claw, a colony of communal, saffron-legged spiders has engineered a pure tension rig. Over seasons they have woven thick, sliding collars of braided silk entirely around each frictionless rod—prevented from slipping to the floor on the left by the woody vine-basket, and on the right by a rough masonry curb of empty beetle husks cemented straight to the granite around the column’s base. Between those two collars they stretch twin vertical sheets of sticky gold web parallel to the invisible wall, a fingernail’s breadth off either face. Flyers darting across the sunny middle of the basin at thirty miles an hour cannot see the barrier, hit its slick boundary layer at a shallow angle, skid sideways completely unharmed along the unseen pane, and tumble wing-first into the waiting curtain stretched half an inch away.

Below that silk rig, tucked inside the self-supporting basket of ancient vine that circles the left rod without touching it, the tangled ball of mouse-sized mammals breathes in fast, synchronized tremors. Seen close up when the quarter-hour mist wakes them to lick warm dew off one another’s backs, they look startled and unfinished—pinkish-slate skin bare as a squab’s, wrinkled generously around the neck and haunches, without a single guard hair anywhere except five stiff white whiskers on each side of a blunt snout. Fur became a liability millions of years ago in constant wet heat. Their ears, big as thumbprints, fold lengthwise and seal shut with a tiny fleshy tragus during the loudest midday beetle-choruses, unfurling only for the forty seconds of muffled quiet when heavy fog dampens the basin’s acoustics.

They do not live by that left rod merely for warmth; the whole hollow is warm. They live there because of what leaks out of the seam where the frictionless gray column meets the granite. In a closed basin forty yards wide, isolated since the surrounding mountains stood three miles tall, any small vertebrate population ought to have collapsed into lethal inbreeding ages ago. Instead, these tiny climbers brood their bean-sized young an inch from a steady, sub-thermal flux that subtly jostles dividing cells—just enough mutative stir to keep shuffling a microscopic gene pool—paired with a ruthless maternal habit: whenever a litter of three is born into the pulsing heap, the adults sniff the newborns intently for twenty minutes and push any poorly formed kit straight out through the bottom weave of the vine onto the bare granite, where the pale lizards wait on schedule below. At dusk, when the beetles drop a full octave and the red-eyed flyers roost, the surviving adults stream single-file down the twelve-foot vine cable to its home thicket, racing through the foliage like drops of mercury to lap fermented nectar from night-opening blooms and bite the soft, lipid-swollen abdomens out of sleeping beetles.

Yet if you watch the hollow all afternoon, an anomaly slowly forces itself on your eye—something geometric that none of the insects, lizards, or naked vine-mice can account for.

Look at the twelve lush islands of purple-green shrub. Every vine here grows with tropical ferocity; every root mat swells at each misting. Given fifty years unmanaged, the hardy creepers that built the twelve-foot cable to the gray rod ought to have bridged the five-pace dry intervals, stitched the separate thickets together along the damp runoff grooves, shaded out the bare granite highways, and choked the basin into a single tangled mat. Look closer at the skirts of the moss pads where they meet the creamy bedrock. Every exploratory runner sent out across the stone—every single one except the ancient woody cable leading to the rod—has been cleanly pinched through right at the edge of the shade, leaving neat little callused nubs lined up like trimmed boxwood. Look at the shallow water grooves worn into the granite floor: whenever buoyant humus or shed beetle-armor washes into a channel during a forty-second mist and threatens to dam the trickle, something clears it out before the next noon, stacking the debris under the outer leaves where the upholstery moss can digest it.

Nothing out on the sunny rock is doing this. To find what is, you have to watch the largest thicket—the waist-high island near the northern curve of the bowl, where two condensation grooves converge—during the breathless final minute before its neighboring wire-frame snaps.

Down at the base of the foliage, what looked from the rim like a low, rounded boulder of the same creamy-gray granite shifts an inch to the left and pulls five broad, horn-nailed fingers back into the purple gloom.

Part the stiff, tiered leaves—which weave together overhead like roof shingles—and you discover the thickets are not solid brush all the way through. Eight of the twelve islands have been hollowed from the inside into dark, stiflingly fragrant chambers the size of overturned wheelbarrows. Living woody stems have been bent sideways while green and notched so they graft into arched ribs, keeping the heavy leaf-load off the bare satin granite at the core.

Inside that northern dome sits a heavy, low-slung animal about the weight of a small badger, resting flat on its haunches with its forearms folded across a broad, barrel-ribbed chest. What lineage it came from before the continent ground flat around it is almost impossible to tell at a glance; fifty million years inside a forty-yard greenhouse have planed away everything superfluous and thickened everything patient. It is a deep-time relict—most likely an archaic, surviving cousin of early burrowing mammals or late cynodonts that outlived its world—utterly hairless like the vine-mice, with loose, heavily creased skin the dark violet-gray of wet slate. You mistook its shoulder for bedrock earlier because, after every morning misting, it scrapes fine white mineral flour from weathering feldspar grains at the wall of the bowl, mixes the dust in its palms with sticky mucilage squeezed from moss-capsules, and plasters a smooth mortar over its back, neck, and outer forearms. Dried in the ambient heat, the crust matches the dull satin finish of the floor so exactly that when the creature lies flattened at the edge of a bush for two hours waiting to pin a sprint-lizard, lizards run right across its spine.

Its head is wide and flat-crowned, built to clear low branches without rustling a leaf. External ear-flaps are gone entirely—in this acoustic pressure-cooker they would be agony—replaced by flush, callused tympanic pads behind the jaw hinge. It listens to its world primarily through its wide, five-fingered hands and flat heel-pads pressed to the bedrock; through solid granite it can feel a crustacean column landing thirty feet away or tell which artifact’s subterranean anchor is warming toward a mist-snap ten seconds before the amphipods know. Its eyes are huge, dark amber globes shielded behind double sets of oily lids—inner translucent membranes closed against blinding highland glare when reaching out onto the stone, drawn back inside the bush to drink the faint purple twilight filtering through six layers of foliage. Both thumbs and the inner toes end not in claws, but in broad, flat, bevel-edged chisels of black horn that meet the index calluses like bypass shears.

There are exactly four adults alive in the basin right now, plus one fist-sized young clinging transversely across the belly-folds of the oldest female in the northern thicket. There are almost never five adults, and never fewer than three.

They are not conscious architects pondering their enclosure; they have no language, make no vocal sound louder than a wet click of the tongue against the palate, and have never once climbed the gentle fifteen-foot slope to look out over the threshold lip—the thin, freezing air outside hits their naked wet-slate skin like a hot iron and sends them flinching backward before their snouts breach the boundary. Instead, thirty million years of unbroken occupancy in a room forty paces wide has compressed what would be territory, migration, and seasonal instinct in an ordinary animal into a slow, compulsive, almost cellular housekeeping.

Each of the four keeps two or three thickets, moving between them mostly when fog blurs the open lanes or after sunset. Waking from short, twenty-minute naps synced to the mist pulses, one will hitch itself slowly around the interior vault of its shrub on silent knuckle-pads, plucking resonant beetles off the undersides of leaves like plums and crushing them behind broad, flat molars without dropping a shard of shell onto its clean granite sleeping-spot. When a tuberous root swells across a water groove outside and diverts precious runoff onto barren stone where sunlight evaporates it uselessly, the keeper edges to the shade-line, lays a plastered forearm onto the blinding rock, and methodically pares the intruding root back flush with its horn thumb-chisel, eating the starchy shavings. When beetle larvae multiply too thickly on one island and threaten to defoliate its canopy, the keeper simply tears a fist-sized skylight through the upper leaf-tiers before noon, letting direct highland sun and red-eyed predatory flyers pour into the breach for forty-eight hours until the infestation is picked clean, then bends two neighboring shoots together with hooked fingers until their sticky leaf-hairs velcro the roof shut again.

They rarely meet face to face, though they know where the other three are every minute of the day. Twice an hour, especially toward dusk, an adult will press its heavy breastbone flat against the bare stone inside its dome and give two slow, sub-audible thumps with its diaphragm—a bass pulse well underneath the saw-toothed roar of the insects that travels cleanly through the continuous granite basin floor into the palms of the other three.

That stone-borne pulse is how four individuals avoid overrunning forty yards. As long as three other chests answer through the bedrock every afternoon, none of the females ovulate; their hormone cycles stay locked down tight by the steady tactile count of neighbors. Only once every fifteen or twenty years, when an aged keeper grows slow, stops plastering her back, and dies quietly inside a thicket—her body reduced to clean white ribs by beetles, snails, and parasol-spiders within thirty-six hours—does the missing fourth beat register across the rock floor. Within days of the silence, whichever adult holds the central thickets walks out at twilight along the twelve-foot woody vine—the one runner none of them ever prune—and sits hunched for nights on end an inch away from the left matte-gray rod while the naked vine-mice skitter harmlessly over her mortar-crusted shoulders, letting the seam’s quiet flux wake a single dormant follicle into self-fertile division, or priming her to mate once, briefly and silently on the warm midnight stone, with the single smaller male who keeps the dry eastern bushes.

Stay on the rounded rim until the sun drops behind the worn-down swells of the plateau, and you see the hollow turn over its shift.

Outside, the thin mountain air plummets below freezing in twenty minutes; frost feathers onto dry granite ten feet behind your boots. Inside the dimple, the eighty-eight-degree dome holds firm, and where rising humid air off the thickets brushes the unseen ceiling of highland cold right at eye level across the bowl, a faint, luminous skin of Ghost-mist forms—a flat roof of shimmering condensation hanging fifteen feet above the basin floor like phantom glass, dripping occasional warm, fat drops straight back down onto the stone where no artifact sits.

The resonant beetles drop from a deafening buzz to a low, throbbing purr that makes the soles of your feet tingle on the lip. On the curved upper rock walls, an inch beneath that shimmering thermal lid, the squat amphibians unglue their suction toes from daytime crevices and crawl out in rows. Their plum-colored throat sacs gleam wetly in the starlight as they trade their daytime *kree-kree-kree* for deep, bell-like *booms* pitched to bounce off the thermal inversion layer overhead. Down below, the pale lizards have wedged themselves nose-first beneath warm root-mats and switched off. The jumping crustaceans spread out across the entire satin floor in a faint, clicking carpet to graze microscopic algae moistened by ceiling-drip, while six-inch centipedes the color of pale apricots—blind, soft-bodied things hidden all day inside hollow dead stems—flow silently over the moss, breathing faint whistling counter-tones through lateral spiracles that mask the vibration of their forty legs from sleeping prey.

Down in the center, by the faint starlight refracting through the vaporous lid, three dark, rounded shapes no bigger than footstools emerge from separate green islands at the exact second the slumped wire-frame *thwacks* a midnight sphere of fog into the dark. Moving unhurriedly on all fours across the satin granite, leaving damp palm-prints that vanish in ten seconds on the warm rock, the keepers make their slow nightly circuit of the worn stone channels—lifting out a jammed snail shell here, pinching off a stray tendril there, pausing beneath the hovering dish to lick a pinch of mineral film off the edge of the distorted shadow—tending the tiny, deafening room that has outlasted the mountains around it, and will likely outlast the continent beneath it, twenty minutes at a time.
