If you stand at the western end of one of the raised causeways and watch the green stripe behind a granary tower for long enough, a section of the stone wall will eventually step out into the sun.

They are easy to miss not because they hide, and not because they are camouflaged in any deliberate, deceptive way, but because their pace belongs to the masonry rather than to the animals—and even the animals here barely move. What looks from fifty paces away like a buttress of stacked grey slate capped with a bundle of dried mountain grass resolves, over the span of ten or fifteen minutes, into an upright, broad-shouldered figure shifting its weight from one wide foot to the other.

There are not many of them, which explains the vast emptiness of the avenues. A city miles across, capable of housing a hundred thousand cramped human souls, was built here for perhaps five or six hundred individuals. For them, this *is* crowded. Each compound—three or four low, timber-roofed halls grouped around a shaded cistern, separated from the next cluster by hundreds of yards of open,sharp-edged scree—holds no more than two or three residents. They require the empty courts around them the way a tree requires a root-zone, not out of hostility toward their neighbors, whom they pass on the viaducts with mild, lingering attention, but because every upright wall casts a permanent ribbon of wet forest toward the east. To build too closely is to drown your neighbor’s sunlight in marsh, or drink the moisture out of the air before it reaches his eaves. The city is spaced as precisely as an orchard; it is an engine made of stone and geometry, and its builders live inside the calibration.

Up close, a builder stands somewhat shorter than a tall man, though immensely wider through the chest and pelvis, built around the lung capacity required by the thin highland air. Their ribs form a deep, barrel-vaulted cage beneath skin the color and matte texture of dark, unpolished basalt—dry and finely creased like elephant hide where it faces the sun, cool and smooth as wet soapstone in the creases of the joints. They have no hair in the mammalian sense, only a dense, short cape of stiff, hollow, slate-grey filaments growing across the crown of the head, the nape of the neck, and the broad shelf of the shoulders, lying flat like bird-plumage or cedar-shingles to shed the chill of the shade while absorbing radiant warmth when they step into the glare.

Their feet are wide, splayed, and bootless, soled with two inches of dense, fibrous grey horn—not unlike the spongy pads of the silver-fleeced grazers, though tougher—allowing them to walk directly across fresh, razor-edged basalt scree without leaving a drop of blood or making a sound louder than stiff leather flexing. Their hands are huge, four-fingered, and astonishingly asymmetrical in texture: the backs are soft and dark, while the palms and thick, spatulate fingertips are plated in translucent, amber-colored keratin so hard they can pinch a flake off a core of slate by sheer grip strength, or press a sheet of native copper flat against an anvil stone using the heel of the palm as a burnisher.

Look at their eyes, and the strangeness of the unmoving sky makes immediate anatomical sense. Beneath heavy, projecting brow-ridges of bone and stiff grey filament—natural visors grown for a sun that never leaves its forty-degree perch—their eyes are large, dark amber, and divided horizontally by a narrow, twin-lobed pupil. When one turns its head from the blinding glare of a dry limestone court into the sudden, drenched twilight of a wall-shadow, there is no squint, no momentary blindness; the upper lobe of the pupil stays contracted against the sky’s brilliance while the lower dilates instantly to drink in the emerald gloom of the ferns. They see both halves of their striped world at once, with equal clarity, and have no concept of squinting into the west without tilting the stone-visored head a quarter-inch downward.

Because there is no night, they do not sleep—at least, not all at once, and never lying down in the dark. Inside the houses, there are no beds, no hearths, no lamps, and no doors to shut out a winter that will never arrive. Smoke would only smudge the clean timber and hang foul in the windless air; fire is unneeded by bodies insulated with high-altitude density and fueled by slow, steady heat-exchange. Instead, the interiors of the compounds contain only waist-high resting-rails of polished resinous pine and stone benches set precisely astride the shadow-lines cast through open colonnades.

When a builder rests, they stand or lean against one of these rails along the razor-edge of a shadow, facing north or south so that the western half of their body rests in the steady, forty-degree sunlight while their eastern half stands inside the cool, damp air of the shade. You can find dozens of them across the city at any moment, motionless as caryatids along the causeway parapets alongside the copper-green lizards, one shoulder warm and dry, the other beaded with fine mist. Internally, their blood circulates slowly from the sun-warmed flank to the shadow-cooled flank, maintaining a flawless thermal equilibrium while half their brain slips into a deep, ruminative slumber for three or four hours, one amber eye closed, the other open and calmly watching the slow drift of a grazing herd across the court below. Then they turn completely around on their wide, quiet heels, presenting the cool side to the sun, and let the other half of their mind rest.

This absence of hurry—the total lack of daybreak, dusk, winter, rot, or storm—shapes everything they do. They have no agriculture of grain, despite the tall towers that look like granaries; there is no autumn in which grass goes to seed in a sudden panic before frost. Those towers are curing-chimneys and shade-silos. Inside them hang thousands of woven racks holding pale, broad leaves harvested from the shadow-corridors, translucent sweet-tubers pulled from the liverwort mats, and strips of shed velvet moss. By opening or plugging square stone vents along the sunny western face of a tower, a builder alters the interior temperature by fractions of a degree, coaxing the damp air to condense or dry at exact rates, fermenting tough shade-foliage into rich, sweet, nutty cakes that keep indefinitely without salt or smoke.

Watch two of them working at the eastern edge of the city, where a new aqueduct-channel is being extended forty paces farther out onto the virgin basalt scree—not because the population is growing, perhaps, but because a particular patch of dry rock has offended someone’s sense of proportion over the last century, and ought to have forty yards of weeping cloud-trees behind it.

They carry no baskets of tools, only a pair of heavy, oval hammer-stones slung in loops of braided fiber at their hips, and short wooden levers tipped with cold-beaten copper. One kneels beside an angular slab of slate as big as a dining table. For twenty minutes, she does nothing at all except run those hard, amber-plated thumbs along the hairline grain of the rock, feeling the microscopic tension left in the stone when the basin was lifted up. There is no chatter between her and her companion. Why speak aloud when there is no emergency, when your partner is standing four feet away, and when the air is so still that a sharp intake of breath sounds rude?

When they communicate, it is mostly through slight tilts of the broad head, a shift of the grey shoulder-cape, or a series of low, resonant clicks and hums produced deep in the throat with the mouth entirely closed—a sound scarcely louder than the *drip* of the copper spouts, vibrating through the soles of their feet into the dry-laid flagstones so the other feels it in the ankles as much as hears it in the ear.

*Click-hmmm.* A thumb rests on a cleavage plane.

Her companion steps forward, sets the copper-shod toe of a wooden lever under the slab’s corner to change its resonance by a hair, and leans his heavy hip against it. She raises a riverless maul eight inches—no higher—and taps the edge once. *Tock.*

The slate parts along a three-yard seam as straight as a carpenter’s rule, opening like a heavy dark book. Neither of them shows triumph or relief. They lift the fresh, razor-edged block together, their movements smooth, slow, and immensely strong, setting it onto the dry-laid wall where its planes lock against the stone beneath with zero wobble, zero dust, and no need for mortar. The instant the block settles into place, a crisp new rectangle of shadow leaps forty inches eastward onto bare gravel that has known nothing but dry sun since the mountains rose.

Both builders stand still for a long while, watching that new square of shade. Already, invisibly, the air inside the little dark box is cooling by three degrees. Within ten minutes, the basalt facets inside the shadow darken from pale charcoal to glistening black as the first microscopic film of dew settles out of the sky. Before they walk away, the second builder reaches into a woven pouch at his belt, pinches out a tiny green crumb of liverwort spore and three pale fern-bulbils, and sets them gently into the newly dampened crevice at the base of the stone.

Their relationship with the abundant creatures of the valley is built on this same quiet architecture rather than on fences, leashes, or slaughter. Nothing in the city flees when a builder passes. As one walks slowly down a broad flagged avenue, the rows of copper-colored lizards do not scatter from the coping stones; if a builder wishes to lean on a sunny parapet, he simply slides a wide hand slowly beneath two or three lizards and lifts them onto his own sun-facing shoulder or forearm, where they flatten out again with a faint dry rustle, completely satisfied with the substitute perch.

Down in the emerald corridors behind the compound walls, an older resident—distinguishable only because the amber plates on his palms have thickened to deep mahogany and tiny tufts of silver highland moss have actually taken root in the dry, undisturbed creases of his shoulder-quills—moves among six of the silver-brown grazers. He does not herd them; the shadow itself is their pasture fence, for their thick fleeces overheat within twenty minutes if they wander out into the dry glare of the plazas. Carrying a split-slate blade as sharp as surgical steel, he steps beside a grazer that barely pauses in its slow chewing of fern-fronds. With long, unhurried strokes, he parts the animal’s dense wool and trims away only the longest, oldest locks from its flanks—never shearing it bare, taking just enough fiber to braid twenty yards of the rotless cordage that lashes the city’s roof beams, leaving the beast cooler and grooming away matted dampness in the process. From a roost overhead, one of the heavy, goose-sized slate birds drops with a soft *whuff* onto a low copper-capped post beside him, waiting politely until he plucks two ripe, waxy seed-pods from a sunlit crevice and offers them on his flat palm; in return, he reaches beneath the bird while it eats and slips a single warm, unfertilized slate-speckled egg from the mossy depression built into the eaves—an eave the builder himself constructed at just the height and overhang these birds prefer.

Nothing is killed in blood if equilibrium can yield the harvest instead. To spill blood on dry-laid porous slate where no rain ever falls to wash it away would leave a foul crust forever; in a windless, rainless city, cleanliness is as strict and structural as gravity. Every waste—shed feathers, grazer droppings, trimmed fern-stalks, even a builder’s own sparse, dry leavings—is gathered daily in copper-lined panniers and buried deep inside dark, stone-capped composting vaults built beneath the thickest cloud-tree groves, where pale, blind beetles and translucent creepers reduce everything silently back into rich black humus without releasing a trace of odor into the crisp mountain air. Even their own dead do not go into the ground passively: when after centuries a builder’s slow heart finally stills, the body is carried up above the city to the jagged western rim, laid out on high dry basalt tables directly beneath the forty-degree sun, where the thin, dry glare desiccates flesh cleanly into light leather and clean bone while the great slate-birds and high-altitude beetles return whatever is soft back into the valley’s closed loop, until the dry bones are brought back down and fitted neatly into hollow bonding-courses inside the viaduct arches—literally becoming part of the load-bearing geometry of their home.

If they have art, or religion, or philosophy, it is indistinguishable from surveying.

Go out into one of the vast, empty plazas that seem at first glance to have no purpose—two hundred yards square of swept, fitted grey flagstones containing nothing at all save a low copper plinth and a shallow stone basin. Around the margins of such a court, you will sometimes find four or five builders standing fifty paces apart in the cool morning-that-never-ends, motionless for what in human reckoning would be two or three days.

Look closely at the plinth in the center. It is a vertical fin of split dark slate, six feet tall, capped with a thin crescent of cold-hammered copper whose oxidized green edge has been honed to a wire. Its shadow stretches eastward across the flagstones for precisely eight feet, four inches, and half a thumb’s breadth, ending directly across the center of the shallow stone basin.

In the western half of that basin, exposed to the pinned sun, water evaporates molecule by molecule into the crisp air. In the eastern half, covered by the slate fin’s shadow, condensation trickles down a hairline groove in the stone—*drip... four seconds... drip*—replenishing the water at the exact rate the sunlight drinks it. Half a millimeter of water stands across the flat stone bottom, trembling slightly every four seconds with a concentric ring that catches the pale sky and vanishes before the next drop falls. It never spills over the lip. It never retreats from the dry stone edge.

The builders standing at the corners of the court are watching the ring.

Perhaps once in ten thousand drops, an unusually heavy bird gliding overhead casts a momentary, moving blot of shade across the basin for half a second, or a distant rockfall high on the unweathered mountain peaks sends an imperceptible shudder through the bedrock of the basin, or the distant, pinned sun itself—for reasons no eye could measure in a single lifetime—flutters in brightness by a thousandth part. When that happens, the water film creeps a hair’s breadth across the dry boundary line onto the warm slate before hissing silently away, or pulls back just enough to leave a pale dry crescent along the wet lip.

When it does, a soft, subterranean *hmmm* passes through the flagstones from one wide, horn-soled foot to the next around the vast plaza. One of the builders walks slowly out into the glare, kneels beside the plinth with a pebble of fine-grained basalt no larger than an almond, and strokes the copper edge of the shadow-caster three times—shaving off a curl of green tarnish thinner than a spider’s silk, shifting the permanent shadow eastward by the width of an eyelash—until the water’s edge settles back into absolute stillness, balanced between sky and rock, light and wet, while all around them the striped city breathes without a sound.
