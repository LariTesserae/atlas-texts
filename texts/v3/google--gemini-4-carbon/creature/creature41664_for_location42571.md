If you sit down on a dry parapet with your back against the sun-warmed greywacke and stay completely still for three hours, you will realize the masonry is still moving.

A quarter of a mile down-slope, where a sunken plaza steps down into a series of flooded catch-basins, one of the dark corbelled domes changes its outline. A rough grey slab near its crown lifts two inches, pivots smoothly on a balanced point, and settles back down at a slightly shallower angle. In the alley below it, the glassy sheet of water sliding down the center channel hesitates, divides around a newly placed wedge of schist, and sends half its volume shimmering sideways through a low doorway that was dry ten minutes before. 

Nothing steps out into the sun to inspect the work. Inside the dark threshold, something heavy shifts its weight, producing a single, dull *clack* of stone bedding into stone—a sound so brief and deadened by the surrounding walls that it carries less than forty yards.

What built the plateau has not left it, though there were never millions of them here at once. A single builder, over a lifespan of two or three centuries, lays an immense amount of rock when it has nothing else to do and nowhere else to go. Across the entire eighty-mile sweep of the city, there are perhaps four or five thousand of them alive today, scattered at vast, solitary intervals through the labyrinth, maintaining a stone carapace ten thousand times too large for their numbers because the carapace is how they eat, breathe, thermoregulate, and remember.

At first glance, emerging from a trapezoidal doorway into the shadow of an alley, one of them looks like a weathered basalt boulder wrapped in heavy, damp felt, walking low to the ground on four short, pillar-like legs. Stand closer—if it lets you, though it has no natural predators at this height and regards an upright visitor mostly as an odd, warm updraft—and the anatomy resolves into something neither quite mammal nor reptile, though warm-blooded in its own slow, massive way. 

It is roughly the bulk of a highland yak, though slung much lower and wider, shaped to clear a shoulder-width passage without scraping its flanks or ducking its head. Its back and broad, domed shoulders are covered in a dense, grease-thickened pelage the exact blue-grey of wet slate, matted purposefully into broad, overlapping plates like heavy Felt or corded wool. In those ancient, damp mats of hair, pale ochre and chalk-white crustose lichens actually grow, turning the creature’s spine into a living continuation of the courtyard walls. Beneath the heavy skirt of this coat, its limbs drop straight down: thick, jointless-looking columns ending not in hooves, which would slip on wet algae and chip the fitted thresholds, nor in soft pads that slate would slice to ribbons, but in broad, four-lobed feet shod in tough, calloused horn with the gritty, gripping give of vulcanized rubber. When it steps onto an angled, water-slick roof dome, the lobes spread slightly, squeezing the water film out from underneath to lock directly onto the grain of the rock.

The working end of the animal—or person, though that word sits strangely on something so silent and self-contained—is its forequarters. It has no long neck; its broad, flat-crowned head sits tucked directly between massive scapular humps of slow-twitch muscle. Where a grazer would have a muzzle, its face ends in a blunt, steeply sloped plate of dark keratin over bone, scarred with fine white scratches from a lifetime of testing rock cleavage. Tucked below the jawline and folded cleanly back against its chest when walking is a third pair of limbs: shorter than the walking legs, immensely thick at the elbow, terminating in flat, spatulate hands of black, insensitive horn backed by three opposing digits as stout as iron pry-bars.

Watch one work a slumped wall after frost-heave has spoiled the water flow, and you see immediately why there are no quarry marks, no chisels, and no mortar anywhere on the plateau. 

It does not strike stone. Striking makes sharp edges, flying splinters, and noise, and wastes calories that forty-five-degree air and thin algae cannot easily replace. Instead, it slides a spatulate horn-hand beneath a three-hundred-pound block of greywacke, rocks back onto its broad hind pillars, and lifts the stone effortlessly against its armored chest, using its own low center of gravity as a counterweight. It turns the rock over slowly in the bright highland light, peering at the bedding planes with wide-set, horizontally slit eyes the color of tarnished pewter—eyes shielded by heavy, translucent nictitating membranes against dust and glare. It presses its sensitive lower lip to a hairline seam in the rock, exhales a slow, warm plume of vapor, and listens with the bones of its jaw to how the dampness inside the fissure expands and ticks. 

To split a slab, it simply sets the stone across two projecting fulcrums in the wall, places a small, hard nodule of quartz precisely on the fault line, and leans the deadweight of its shoulders onto the overhang. There is a muffled *thock*, like a thick branch breaking underwater. The slab parts cleanly along its natural grain. The builder lifts the two halves, tests their rock-to-rock friction by rubbing them together once—producing a brief, gritty sigh—and keys them into the dry-stone batter so tightly a knife blade couldn't enter the joint.

Why build eighty miles of rooftops to catch the rain? Because on a treeless, soil-starved plateau above the timberline, raw bedrock sheds storm-water too violently, scouring away silt and plunging off the cliffs in flash floods before life can use it. By lifting the surface of an entire mountain range and relaying it into millions of gentle domes, shallow pans, and capillary channels, the builders turned sterile stone into a slow, giant digestive tract.

Everything the city does is agricultural, though nothing is planted in rows. When that builder up-slope tilted its roof shingle to divert half an alley's water through an empty house, it was irrigating. Two hundred yards further down the grade, that diverted film of water emerges through the floor seams of a wide, sun-facing courtyard, spreading out an eighth of an inch deep across warm flagstones. By mid-afternoon, the dark green algae mats resting dormant in that pan have swollen into thick, gelatinous velvet, bubbling silently with oxygen in the sharp sun. Tiny rust-red crustaceans hatch from dry cysts in the stone joints; charcoal-backed diving beetles drop out of the bright sky to hunt them; dust blown up from the distant lowlands settles onto the wet film and is trapped as rich black silt at the notched stone sills.

Toward dusk, when the cloud fleets thin out and the sky turns a hard, freezing violet, the builder emerges from the dim vault where it spent the afternoon conserving heat. Moving at a deliberate, fluid amble that never splashes, it walks down into the irrigated courtyard. Lowering its broad head to the flagstones, it opens a wide, baleen-fringed mouth behind that tough keratin face-plate and draws its lower lip across the wet slate like a window squeegee. In a single ten-foot pass, it skims up a rich, cold slurry of sun-warmed algae, diatoms, mineral silt, crustacean roe, and sweet rainwater, leaving a clean, damp track of bare greywacke behind it. It swallows without chewing—its gizzard, ballasted with smooth river pebbles, will grind the silt and cell walls all night while it sleeps—and steps sideways to skim the next lane, careful never to scrape deep enough to strip the starter-spores from the pores of the rock.

They do not need hearths because their bodies are built like subterranean cisterns: huge, slow-burning thermal masses insulated by four inches of dense fat and lichen-crusted felt. Inside the corbelled vaults, out of the evaporative chill of the wind, three or four of them will sometimes share a dry chamber during a week-long sleet squall, folding their legs beneath them until they look like four dark boulders arranged around the floor. Within an hour, their combined breath and radiant body heat bring the small stone dome up to sixty degrees, moisture condensing on the cold outer courses and trickling harmlessly away through the perimeter floor-rills without ever wetting their bellies.

That shared warmth in winter is almost the entirety of their social life, yet they are profoundly aware of one another across miles of stone. 

Having no voices—vocal cords would have nothing to push against in an acoustic baffle that swallows sound in twenty paces—they read the watershed. A builder drinking from an ankle-wide channel in a sunken alley tastes the water with a grooved organ in the roof of its mouth. In that cold, clear sip, it reads the chemical signature of the lanolin-like grease washed off the coat of another individual half a mile up-grade; it tastes whether that neighbor is old, pregnant, sick, or shedding its summer undercoat; it tastes the fresh mineral bite of newly split schist, telling it that someone three terraces up is rebuilding a collapsed cistern. 

Sometimes, one will deliberately drop a pinch of crushed, bitter saxifrage or iron-rich ochre dust into a specific rill, sending a narrow, silent ribbon of scent threading downward through forty junctions and six courtyards directly under the nose of a mate resting in a chamber below. Hours later, without a single call having broken the immense quiet of the plateau, a second heavy shadow will detach itself from an archway down-valley, climbing the slick slab staircases with effortless, slow-motion grace, following the faint trace of crushed leaf upstream against the glassy *shhh* of the water.

When one of them finally dies—usually alone, inside a deep, dry ground-course chamber during the bitterest clear nights of midwinter—nothing goes to waste, and no grave is dug. Over the following seasons, the small granite-colored rock mammals slip through the wall-chinks to strip the dense coat for nesting felt in the hollow thickness of the masonry; beetles and cold-loving molds patiently return the flesh to clean bone and black, fertile humus that washes slowly out through the floor capillaries, feeding a sudden, lush flush of rusty sedge and bright green saxifrage along the alley outside. 

Years later, another builder passing down that lane will notice the rich tuft of sedge, peer through the low trapezoidal doorway, and see the clean, massive white ribs arched under the dim vault like a smaller dome within the dome. It will not haul the bones out or carve a sign above the lintel. Instead, if the roof above is still sound, it may carry in three flat slabs of greywacke, key them neatly over the remains to level the floor, adjust a lip-stone outside so a thin, clean thread of water glides across the new threshold, and move on up the slope—leaving the ancestor built seamlessly into the foundation, indistinguishable from the mountain underneath.
