You do not hear the line-tenders coming, because you cannot hear anything over the mountain, and you rarely see them until you nearly step on them, because they move at the exact height of the vegetation. 

To stand upright at fifteen thousand feet in a seventy-mile-an-hour wall of horizontal sleet is to turn oneself into a sail, and sails get torn away. So the tenders move low. A four-person crew crossing a flooded granite saddle looks less like a party of engineers and more like a pod of heavy, dark-shelled seals hauling out over a reef. They travel in a crouched, wide-legged gait, hands frequently touching the stone or gripping the leathery crowns of cushion plants, leaning their shoulders at ridiculous angles into the wind. 

Everything they wear is designed to shed momentum and hold heat. Their outer shells are vulcanized, multi-layered black rubber, seamless across the shoulders and back, slick with lanolin and silicone grease so the driving water sheets off them without finding a purchase. Beneath the rubber, they are packed in dense felted wool harvested from the wild ungulates—the only fiber up here that retains its loft when soaked in sweat and condensation—and beneath the wool, a thin, humming mesh of copper-threaded long-johns plugged into heavy, brick-sized battery packs worn low at the small of the back. They are literally wired into their own grid; before leaving a bunker, they plug their hip-packs into a transformer on the wall and drink forty volts of Pelton-wheel fury to keep their blood from thickening in the plunge pools.

Look closely at their extremities and you see how the work has rebuilt their silhouette. Their boots are monstrous things: thigh-high waders ballasted at the ankles with segmented lead collars to keep their feet from being swept out from under them in the sheet-flow. The soles are interchangeable plates of spongy, cross-cut synthetic rubber laminated with crushed garnet, mimicking the grip of the mountain goats, strapped over steel-toed clogs. Their gloves are equally bizarre—thick neoprene lobster-claws tipped with curved, replaceable brass hooks at the palm and thumb, allowing a tender to snag a braided steel guy-wire or wedge a hand into a two-inch basalt fissure and hang there effortlessly while a squall blows itself out over their bowed helmet.

They have no spoken language out on the domes. Shouting into the freight-train roar of a gorge only fills your lungs with cold spray and shreds your vocal cords by thirty. Even radio headsets inside their brass-rimmed, polycarbonate-visored hoods are mostly useless—the static of the rain and the ferocious electromagnetic bleed of the unshielded, high-voltage ground cables turn wireless frequencies into soup. 

Instead, they speak through the bones of the mountain and the skin of their infrastructure. 

Every tender carries a short, heavy spanner forged from vanadium steel on a lanyard at their wrist. When a crew is working along a power line, they tap out sharp, syncopated rhythms directly onto the cast-iron stanchions or the thick rubber-sheathed cables themselves. Vibrations travel fast and clear through taut steel and dense stone. Fifty yards downstream, entirely swallowed by a grey curtain of mist, another tender rests two gloved fingers against the cable and reads the Morse-like cadence in their knuckles: *Slack here. Watch the surge. Gate three dropping.* When they are within arm's reach, they press the fronts of their helmets together—forehead to forehead, visors clicking—so the vibration of their voices passes directly through the polycarbonate and bone, turning a conversation into an intimate, forehead-bruising huddle in the middle of a churning cataract.

Watch a crew work a jammed sluice gate, and you realize they do not fight the water; they use it the way a judo master uses an opponent's weight. When a bloated mat of torn kelp and drowned sedge fouls a worm-gear, they don't try to haul it upstream. Two tenders clip double-tethers onto the rusted iron eyeballs bolted into the weir’s concrete crest, lean backward directly into the foaming ten-foot drop until the rushing water pins them tight against their harnesses, and go to work underwater by feel alone. Their visors vanish beneath the white boil. For two, three minutes at a time, only their hunched rubber shoulders break the surface, shuddering under tons of hydraulic pressure as they slice through tangled stalks as thick as a man's wrist with serrated gutting-knives. When a gate finally breaks free with a groan that you feel in your teeth, and a million gallons of water abruptly shifts channels, the tenders don't scramble away. They tuck their chins, cross their arms over their chests, and let the new, thundering avalanche wash them twenty yards down the newly flooded chute until their safety lines snap taut, swinging them neatly like pendulums onto the sudden, eerie quiet of the freshly drained moss-slope beside it, where grounded trout are already flopping furiously in the wet weeds.

Yet the humans are only half the ecology of the grid. The infrastructure has been bolted to these domes long enough that the mountain’s native life has figured out what fifty hertz of alternating current feels like.

Follow the three-foot stanchions through the waist-deep bamboo and into the submerged tarns, and you find that the heavy black cables are never bare. Electrical resistance generates heat—not much against an ocean of forty-degree rain, just a degree or two above ambient—and in a hyper-saturated freezing world, two degrees is paradise. Thick, velvety cuffs of rust-red thermophilic moss encase the rubber sheathing, thriving in the faint electromagnetic hum. Inside that moss live millions of specialized stonefly nymphs and blind, translucent freshwater shrimp. 

Because the shrimp are there, the cables have become highways for something much stranger: the *stanchion-eels*, though they aren't true eels at all, but monstrously elongated, scaleless relatives of the suction-bellied trout, five feet long and thick as an inner tube, mottled the exact greasy rust-and-black of weathered cast iron. They wrap themselves around the submerged power lines in tight spirals, anchoring by their pelvic discs, letting the faint hum of the current vibrate through their lateral lines while they Vacuum invertebrates off the warm rubber. They are completely fearless of the tenders; a worker checking a frayed splice under three feet of churning tarn-water often has to physically pry two or three sluggish, forty-pound fish off the junction box like stubborn wet tires, tossing them back into the kelp where they immediately swim right back between the worker's leaded ankles.

Around the turbine bunkers themselves, the concentration of warmth and vibration turns the bleak concrete aprons into raucous, crowded rookeries. The leeward walls of the steel-plate houses—the one place on the entire mountain range where the wind drops below a gale and the air smells faintly of ozone and hot axle-grease—are packed solid with the bowling-ball waterfowl. Hundreds of them wedge themselves between the guy-wire turnbuckles, preening their oil-drenched feathers in the radiant warmth bleeding through the rivets. The tenders have long since given up trying to keep them off the roofs; instead, they build sloping wooden baffles over the ventilation louvers so the guano washes cleanly away in the rain, and occasionally reach into the squawking mass to grab a fat, unbothered bird by the neck for the evening stewpot—a tax the flock barely notices.

Even the herds of cord-wooled ungulates have mapped the rhythm of the automated gates into their grazing patterns. When the upstream pressure sensors trip and the massive worm-gears begin their thirty-second, metallic shriek of warning before redirecting a cataract, the elders of the herd lift their dripping, shovel-nosed heads from the sedge. Instead of fleeing, they trot briskly *toward* the spillway that is about to be choked off. The moment the iron baffle slams down and the ten-foot wall of white water collapses into a trickling black chute, three dozen half-ton beasts are already plunging down into the polished stone trough, snorting eagerly as they gorge on the sudden windfall of stunned snails, stranded kelp-bulbs, and mineral-rich silt before the moss-mats can claim it, completely ignoring the amber strobe blinking steadily over their backs.

And when the daylight finally thins—turning from pale zinc to wet slate to absolute, roaring pitch-black—the tenders go inside. 

To step through the submarine-style hatch of a turbine bunker, spin the heavy iron dog-wheel shut, and feel the rubber gasket bite down is to experience a violence in reverse. The wind vanishes. The tearing static of the rain vanishes. Your ears ring violently in the sudden vacuum of high frequencies, leaving only the immense, maternal, chest-filling *thrum* of the Pelton wheels spinning in the dark bedrock beneath the steel floorplates. Everything inside vibrates slightly: the tin mugs hanging on brass hooks, the surface of the tea steeping on the induction plate, the needles on twenty-four round, brass-bezeled pressure gauges marching across the slate switchboard.

Inside, the tenders peel off their skins. The cramped vestibule smells overwhelmingly of wet sheep, ozone, boiled cabbage, hot machine oil, and human feet. Rubber suits hang upside down from ceiling pulleys over grated drainage sumps, dripping steadily back into the tailrace below. Stripped down to their copper-mesh base layers, the people who live here look startlingly pale and surprisingly broad, with thick, overdeveloped trapezius muscles from years of leaning into the sky, and skin permanently pruned at the fingertips, scarred across the knuckles with grey flecks of granite dust and grease tattoos. Nearly all of them are hard of hearing in the upper registers; even in the quiet of the bunker, they speak in flat, resonant baritones and altos pitched unconsciously to harmonize with the fifty-hertz floor, touching each other constantly on the elbow, shoulder, or knee as they pass in the narrow galley—a lingering habit of keeping track of one's crewmates when visibility is twenty feet.

They do six-month rotations up here, two or three tenders to a bunker, strung out every four miles across the range. Nobody comes up from the dry lowlands who quite fits down there anymore, and after three seasons on the domes, few of them want to go back. Down in the valleys, they complain, the air feels dead and thin—like breathing dust—and the silence of a dry room makes their skin crawl with phantom pressures, waiting for a sluice gate that never drops. 

Before turning into their narrow, gimbaled bunks bolted directly to the vibrating steel bulkheads, one of them always checks the main glass sight-tube on the intake pipe—a thick cylinder of armored quartz showing a slice of the mountain’s fury shooting past at eighty miles an hour under immense pressure, silver with trapped air bubbles, occasionally flashing with the pale belly of a pulverized trout. Satisfied that the wheel is fed and the needles are steady in the green, they kill the overhead bulbs. In the dark, warm belly of the iron boulder, tucked beneath millions of tons of rushing cold water, they press their palms flat against the humming steel wall, feel the faint, rhythmic *tink-tink-tink* of a neighbor crew four miles away tapping goodnight onto the main trunk line, tap twice back with a bare knuckle, and go to sleep.
