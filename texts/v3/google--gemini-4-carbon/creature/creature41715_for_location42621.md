You notice them first not by sight, and certainly not by sound, but by an absence in the vibration. 

If you stand near one of the half-submerged turbine drums, your ribs and teeth tune themselves quickly to the fifty-cycle hum of the copper lines and the deep, hydraulic *thud-thud-thud* of the pistons underfoot. It becomes the baseline of the world. Then, occasionally, the rhythm changes. A single pylon leg stops ringing quite so brightly; the steady, three-second sheet of shaken dew from a span of cable falters, dripping raggedly instead. 

Fifty yards away, what looked like a slump of rusted bog-iron and grey lichen detaches itself from the side of a cast-iron housing and begins to walk.

Whatever laid the grid across the tableland is long gone—or perhaps it never had hands and feet at all, dropping these seamless drums from the sky or extruding them up through the slate—but the grid is no longer unattended. Something else has grown up around the maintenance of it, or settled into it so long ago that the wet altitude and the fifty-cycle current have reshaped their bodies entirely. If you called them people, you would have to stretch the word until it creaked, though there is an undeniable, patient craft in the way they move.

They are built broad, low, and heavy, sharing the same squat center of gravity as the wool-coated grazers and the tripod pylons. Standing fully upright—which they rarely bother to do, preferring a rolling, knuckle-braced slouch that keeps them below the wind-shear—one would barely clear a tall man’s chest, yet it would weigh three times as much. To survive on a three-foot mattress of waterlogged sphagnum without sinking to the groin at every step, their lower limbs have broadened into wide, spatulate pedestals. Their feet are immense, splayed things, encased not in stitched leather, which would rot in a week here, but in thick, flexible boots of cured bog-rubber and layered sedge-matting, bound round the ankle with twists of tarnished copper wire. When they walk, they do not punch holes in the moss; they roll their weight across the surface like wide-tired marsh buggies, leaving only momentary, pale depressions in the silver-green sponge that fill with tea-colored water and rebound within minutes.

Up close, it is difficult to tell where their clothing ends and their skin begins. They wear heavy, seamless cowls and layered mantles woven from the shed underwool of the bog-grazers, felted so tightly with dark tallow, peat-tar, and mineral oil wept from the machine joints that the fabric has become stiff and slick as seal-hide. Over the shoulders and down the spine, tufts of live, pale lichen and tiny rosette plants have taken root right in the grease of their coats. Mist hits them and beads instantly, running off the eaves of their hoods in clear, silent rivulets.

Beneath those heavy eaves, their faces are wide, flat, and startlingly pale—the bloodless, tallowy grey of slate kept permanently underwater. The thin, cold air of the high plateau has given them immense barrel chests and broad, cavernous noses bridged with heavy cartilage, warming the sharp oxygen before it hits their lungs. They have no eyebrows or lashes to catch the freezing condensation; instead, a thick, rubbery ridge of brow overhangs eyes that are dark, wide, and surfaced with a slow-blinking, translucent inner lid that wipes away mist like a windshield blade.

Like everything else on the tableland, they have abandoned air-borne sound. A shout up here is eaten in twenty yards by the three-foot acoustic sponge underfoot and the wet wool of the clouds; besides, making noise wastes warm breath. Their mouths are narrow, tight-lipped seams that rarely open except to feed or to draw a slow, whistling inhalation when winching something heavy.

Instead, they live inside the hum.

Watch one approach a turbine casing. Before it does anything else, it ungloves a hand. The hand is extraordinary: huge, thick-palmed, four-fingered, with knuckles like walnuts and skin calloused to the texture of dry horn. The fingertips are unusually broad and flattened, almost spatulate, packed with dense, specialized nerve-clusters. It lays that wide, pale palm flat against the wet, ribbed cast-iron of the seamless shell and closes its eyes.

For three or four minutes, it does not move at all. Two slate-black amphibians, plastered to the warm iron inches from its wrist, do not even bother to slide away; their throat-sacs keep pulsing rapidly against its cuff. The creature is listening with its bones. Through the thick palm, up the heavy radius of the forearm, and straight into a dense, calcified plate behind its jaw, it is reading the interior of a machine that has no doors. It feels the tiny, rhythmic cavitation of a water-wheel vane sixty feet down in the bedrock; it reads the viscosity of the mineral oil by the drag on the recessed flywheel; it traces the distant, faint stutter of a short-circuit four miles west, where a wet willow root has finally chewed through an insulator collar.

When two or three of them work together—which is the largest gathering you will ever see, though there are thousands of them spaced evenly across the subcontinent—their conversation happens entirely through the infrastructure and through touch. One will tap the iron leg of a pylon twice with a heavy brass knuckle-ring. Half a mile down the line, where the braided copper sags across a shallow saddle, its partner pauses, lifts two broad fingers to the green-glazed wire, catches the tiny mechanical transient riding underneath the fifty-cycle current, and turns back through the waist-high heath. When they meet at the drum, they simply press their heavy shoulders or forearms together while they work, passing subtle, rapid shivers of muscle-tension back and forth—a silent, somatic shorthand as complex and dryly practical as a telegraph operator’s code.

They are not the masters of the machines; they are closer to symbiotic grooms, or stone-age millwrights tending a geologic feature that keeps them alive. 

They cannot open the cast-iron housings, nor would they ever try. Those seamless drums are sacred only in the way a riverbed or a beating heart is sacred: if you crack one open, the warmth stops. Instead, their entire material culture revolves around keeping the external joints greased, the pressure valves clear of choking moss, and the heavy copper lines balanced above the creeping wet.

Slung beneath their oiled mantles, so tightly lashed that nothing ever clinks against anything else, they carry tools scavenged from the slow, century-by-century shedding of the grid itself, married to the dense bone of the plateau’s megafauna. A pry-bar forged cold from a fallen strand of work-hardened bronze ground-wire, hafted in the solid, mineral-heavy jawbone of a bog-grazer. Scrapers flaked from dark greywacke slate, sharp enough to shave verdigris off a live bus-bar without drawing a spark. Thick bladders sewn from the stomachs of the great rails, swollen not with water, but with rendered tallow mixed with black graphite scraped from dry tarn-beds.

When a brass pressure-valve nested in the sedge gets fouled with quick-growing liverworts—threatening to choke the drum’s breath—one of these quiet keepers kneels in the cold seepage. Water wells up over its shins and vanishes into the waterproof folds of its leggings. With slow, deliberate movements that never startle the rows of owl-feathered raptors watching from the chest-high wire six feet away, it scrapes the brass clean. When the valve finally triggers, letting out its long, soft sigh of white steam, the keeper does not pull back. It leans its broad, pale face directly into the hot, metallic cloud, closing its eyes as the condensation streams down its tallowy cheeks, breathing in the wet heat and the sharp smell of ozone like a man taking a draught of strong spirits.

Their homes are invisible from twenty paces away. They do not build huts or dig pits—digging six inches down here only gives you an ice-cold well. Instead, where two or three buried feeder-conduits cross beneath the peat, creating a perennial patch of sixty-degree soil, they weave the living vegetation over the warm spot. Over decades, generations of keepers coax the tough, rubbery rosette shrubs and creeping willow into tight, domed arches, braiding the living roots and branches together and packing the interstices with living sphagnum moss and shed grazer-wool. 

Inside one of these low, living mounds, the air is dark, thick, and fiercely warm, smelling of bruised bog-myrtle, lanolin, and the omnipresent, comforting iron-tang of the buried conduit curving up through the floor like the root of a great metal tree. There is no fire; fire is virtually impossible on the tableland, where every twig is saturated to the pith and peat would take a summer that never comes to dry. They do not need it. They cook by wrapping strips of dark, oily rail-meat, tubers, and clutches of midge-fattened amphibian eggs inside slick, broad perennial leaves and wedging the parcels tightly into the hot, forty-five-degree crevices under the turbine flywheels, leaving them for a day to slow-poach in the rhythmic *thud-thud-thud* of the casing. 

In the dark of those woven domes, whole families—rarely more than four or five—sleep piled together directly over the warm iron pipe, their broad palms resting lightly on the metal or on one another’s chests, dreaming inside the fifty-cycle cradle. Their young are born small, remarkably fat, and entirely voiceless; a newborn does not cry when it hits the cool air, only shudders violently once, clears its wide nostrils with a wet snort, and immediately reaches blind, wide-tipped fingers toward the nearest source of vibration.

Perhaps the strangest thing about them is how completely the rest of the plateau’s dense fauna accepts them as part of the apparatus. 

Because they smell of tallow, ozone, and wet wool rather than sharp, predatory musk, and because they move at the slow, steady pace of weather, the herds of hundreds of badger-backed grazers barely part around them. A keeper walking a five-mile line inspection through a drifting cloud-bank will wade straight through the middle of a feeding herd. The beasts only ripple their pale ear-tufts in mild acknowledgment, stepping aside with a soft *shuck-shuck* of splayed hooves, sometimes even leaning a heavy, wet flank against a keeper’s hip as it passes, mistaking the greased mantle for another warm pylon leg. Even the silent, grey-furred curs give them a wide, indifferent berth; the keepers are too heavy-boned, too thick-coated with bitter mineral oil, and carry heavy copper-headed staves that can drop a lunging hound with one soundless, crushing sweep.

Yet they are not animals, and their relationship to the endless, horizon-spanning grid has a quiet, unfathomable depth that goes beyond mere warmth-seeking.

Every so often—perhaps once in three or four circuits of the mist-squalls—an old keeper, stiff in the hips from eighty years of high-altitude damp, its greased mantle grown so heavy with grey crustose lichen that it looks like a walking cairn, will stop maintaining its local stretch of valley. It hands its bone-hafted scrapers and its tallow-bladders to a younger pair with a long, firm press of its forehead against theirs. Then it turns and begins to walk *along* the cables.

Not looping around its home basin, as they usually do, but following the verdigris-stained copper lines straight out toward the dropping horizon. 

You might see one if you stood on a whale-backed ridge during a clear interval, when the bright, shadowless light stretches twenty miles across the olive and rust-red swells. Down in the gleaming wet hollow, moving parallel to the endless row of squat steel tripods, a single dark, low-slung figure rolls steadily forward over the sponge. Every hundred paces, without breaking stride, it reaches out a pale, heavy hand and brushes its fingertips across the sagging, chest-high wire—feeling the massive, silent river of electricity pouring smoothly away through the copper, checking the pulse, wondering, perhaps, in a slow, wordless architecture of thought shaped entirely by currents and resistances, what titanic, unseen thing beyond the curve of the earth is drinking all this power, and never, in ten thousand years, sending a single signal back.

Then a grey squall drags its curtain across the ridge. The mist swallows the line, the herds, the gleaming tarns, and the walker alike, leaving only the breathy hush of wind over oiled leaves, and the deep, tireless *thud-thud-thud* beneath your boots.
