If you watch the glass-boned waders and the barking sump-seals long enough, you realize they are only the surface traffic—opportunists skimming the spilled milk of a machine they don’t understand. The real life of the basin is much deeper, much stranger, and much closer to the bone of the architecture itself.

Look down into the pale, milky silt beneath the root-mat—you can’t see it directly, of course, unless a drainage bladder dilates wide enough to show a glimpse of the blinding white soup three feet down, churning with dissolved minerals and exhausted, dead nanites. Something lives in that blind, warm under-slurry. You only ever know it’s there by the way the floor moves when there *isn't* a hydrostatic wave. A broad, heavy track will press upward against the underside of the root-felt, raising a firm, flat ridge two yards wide that travels purposefully from the base of Tower Four to the base of Tower Nine. 

If you stand on that ridge as it passes, it doesn't feel like water pressure; it feels like standing on the back of a giant, rubbery skate gliding through mud, sliding its flat, muscular body tight against the underside of the ceiling-mat. Where it pauses, the root-pores above it don't sweat minty water—they clench tight, turning pale yellow from the pressure, while a low, resonant purr vibrates right up through the soles of your boots, rattling your teeth. These are the **Silt-Dredges**, or perhaps the basin's forgotten maintenance crew gone feral and whale-like in the dark. They are vast, blind, unsegmented mats of living cartilage and magnetic cilia that swim endlessly through the milky abyss, straining the dead silt for heavy metals, compressing them into nodules, and pressing their broad backs against the root-mat's tap-nodes to vomit refined, conductive paste directly back into the vascular towers. Nobody sees them. When one dies—which happens maybe once a century—the root-mat simply drops a curtain of digestive tendrils down around it, holds it fast while the floor above bulges into a warm, foul-smelling hillock for three weeks, and drinks it dry until the turf settles flat again.

Up in the bright, shadowless air, the life is defined entirely by the *revision*—by the fact that no perch is permanent and the town is constantly trying to optimize itself for ghosts.

Watch one of those waist-high balcony railings that has just finished growing, gleaming wet and smooth, waiting for an elbow that isn’t coming. Before its hour is up, before it decides to untwist into crimson heat-fronds, something will come to cheat it. 

Out of a stairwell steps a creature the size of a greyhound, though built nothing like one. It walks upright on two disproportionately heavy, broad-soled hind legs that slap the wet xylem with the suction-cup *thwack* of a tree frog's toes. Its upper body is narrow, hunched, and weirdly asymmetrical—one long, jointed arm ending in a broad, calloused paddle of flesh, and two smaller, hyper-dexterous feeding hands tucked tight against a translucent, pulsing throat-sac. Its skin has the exact matte, fibrous texture of wet papier-mâché, marbled in chartreuse and bruising purple. 

It walks right up to the fresh railing, steps onto the exact spot a human would stand, and lays its heavy, warm, paddle-arm flat along the polished wood. 

And it waits. 

Because the symbiotes in the wood are very old and slightly stupid, they register the eight pounds of downward pressure and the ninety-nine-degree body heat. *Occupied,* the tower thinks. Instead of dissolving the balcony into heat-sinks, the tower holds the geometry locked. Better yet, following ancient hospitality subroutines, the railing extrudes three little dimpled cups just ahead of the creature's paddle-arm and fills them from an internal capillary with thick, golden, high-glucose nectar—meant, ten thousand years ago, as a afternoon pick-me-up for a contemplative citizen. The biped’s two tiny chest-hands dart out, scoop the jelly into its sideways-hinged mouth with frantic, neat little motions, and wipe the cups clean. 

There are maybe forty or fifty of these **Loiterers** in the basin at any given time. They are evolutionary counterfeits, animals whittled by ten millennia of natural selection into the rough ergonomic footprint of a missing species. They don't have minds to speak of—their heads are barely more than flattened sensory dishes crowned with four swiveling, black-bead eyes—but they possess an uncanny, inherited somatic memory of human posture. You will see three of them sitting in a row on a newly grown bench, upright, knees together, staring blankly across the fizzing plaza while the bench dutifully warms their backsides and drips clean, sweet water onto their tongues. When an aqueduct begins to shudder and unbraid, they know the structural cues better than anything else in the courtyard; ten seconds before the hawser-snap, every Loiterer on the span will drop to all threes and bound away in long, rubbery, silent leaps, landing forty feet away on the bouncing turf just as the structure tears itself in half.

They aren’t alone in gaming the city’s senile generosity, though other things play a rougher game with the packet-network.

Look closely at the mid-heights of the braided towers, just below the swelling gas-pods of the seed-relays. Clinging to the shifting latticework like patches of dry, silvery lichen are the **Splice-Weavers**. They aren't spiders, though they use silk—or rather, they pull raw, unhardened optical cellulose straight out of the tower’s growing veins and spin it into taut, glistening harps stretched between two adjacent xylem struts. 

The Weaver itself is no bigger than a dinner plate, flat as a crab, with eight multi-jointed legs radial around a central carapace that gleams like tarnished copper—actual copper, stripped from the metallic sheaths of the airborne spores. When a relay pod detonates with that sharp *crack*, flinging a glittering packet-spore across the plaza at eighty miles an hour, it usually hits its target receptor on the opposite tower. Usually. Sometimes, a Weaver has strung its wet, elastic harp directly across the firing line, calculating the tower’s slow rotational drift with terrifying precision.

*Thwip.* The net stretches three feet back, smoking slightly from the friction, and holds the screaming, buzzing spore intact. 

Immediately, the Weaver scurries to the center of the web. It doesn't just eat the spore; if it simply cracked the casing, the symbiotes inside would wipe themselves inert. Instead, the Weaver presses its translucent belly over the metallic seed and plugs four needle-sharp ovipositor-stylets straight into the spore’s data-ports. For thirty seconds, the crab-thing shudders violently, its copper shell flushing with waves of frantic bioluminescent static—blue, white, violent magenta—as raw municipal code dumps straight into its nervous system. It is getting high on zoning laws. It is metabolizing ten-thousand-year-old water-pressure tables, architectural schematics for spiral staircases, corrupted love letters, and diagnostic pings, burning the pure, dense energy of the symbiote-cluster while the ghost-data twitches through its legs. When it’s done, it drops the dead, blackened husk to the floor (where an optic-egret immediately swallows it for the copper) and scuttles ten feet higher, suddenly compelled by the stolen code to chew through three specific structural tendons on the tower's western flank—accidentally triggering the growth of a brand-new, utterly pointless archway that wasn't on the tower's schedule at all. Half the weirdest, loopiest architecture in the courtyard is the result of Weavers sloppy-drunk on intercepted mail, chewing edits into the woodwork.

And then there is the thing in the northern sluice-wall—or rather, the thing that *uses* the northern sluice-wall, though it belongs to the whole basin.

If you stay in the courtyard through six or seven flood-cycles, your eyes eventually adjust to the hyperactivity. You stop flinching at the relay-cracks; you learn to step smoothly over the fizzing mint-water domes; you stop tracking the shimmering castanet-clouds of the gliders. And once the frantic foreground blurs into white noise, you notice the **Warden**—not because it is hiding, quite the opposite, it is in plain sight in the center of the open green room—but because it moves at the exact speed of the architecture’s revision.

At first glance, you think it’s a collapsed spire that hasn't been digested yet, or a low, tangled pavilion wandering across the turf on creeping banyan- stilts. It is about twelve feet tall and twenty feet long, a walking, self-grafting cage of dark, polished heartwood, slick crimson muscles that look entirely botanical, and weeping bundles of exposed, glowing-white nerve-root. 

It doesn't walk on legs; it rolls and strides by growing. Its front end extrudes thick, braided crutches into the wet root-felt, locks them into the floor's pores, hauls its heavy, bulbous central mass forward with a wet, creaking sigh, and simultaneously dissolves its rear crutches back into sap, re-routing the biomass forward through pulsing external arteries as thick as firehoses. It crosses the three-block plaza at a steady two miles an hour, leaving no footprints, only a faint trail of warm steam and rearranged turf.

Tucked deep inside the protective ribs of its wooden cage is a wet, glistening collection of things that shouldn't be together: three different types of drainage bladders wired in series, a cluster of un-detonated relay pods pulsing like a multi-chambered glass heart, the calcified skulls of two dozen sump-seals fused into a bony keel, and gallons of thick, silver symbiote-slurry sloshing inside a translucent amniotic sac stretched between the ribs.

This is what happens when a self-revising, programmable ecosystem runs without an administrator for ten thousand years: eventually, a piece of the municipal repair code breaks loose from the grid, realizes it can move faster if it detaches from the plumbing, and starts collecting hardware.

The Warden isn't hostile, not even to you, provided you don't stand directly where it intends to plant a crutch. It has no eyes, reading the entire courtyard instead through the hydrostatic pressure of the floor and the airborne ping of the spores—in fact, every few minutes, one of the internal relay-pods in its chest fires *upward* through a gap in its wooden spine, shooting a rogue packet straight into the nearest tower. Seconds later, that tower will shudder, halt whatever frivolous balcony it was growing, and dutifully dump fifty gallons of nutrient-heavy pith right onto the Warden's back as it lumbers past. 

The barking, six-limbed amphibians follow behind it in a glistening, respectful wake, snapping up the droppings. Three or four glass-boned egrets ride high on its shifting wooden shoulders, swaying like masthead ornaments as its frame twists and re-knits, picking parasitic mites out of its exposed vascular bundles. Even a Loiterer will occasionally trot alongside it, matching its slow, creaking pace, reaching up to lay its broad, warm paddle-arm against a smooth patch on the Warden’s flank—and the Warden, bound by the oldest, deepest laws of the basin, will pause for just a beat, shift its heavy ribs so it doesn't bruise the little impostor, and bleed a single, thick tear of golden sugar down its side before tearing its crutches free of the turf and creaking onward toward the southern curve.
