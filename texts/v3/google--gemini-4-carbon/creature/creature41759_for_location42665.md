If you watch the east wall long enough, letting your eyes adjust to the dim green drop-off beyond the four amber cones, one of the structural shadows detaches itself from the brickwork and slides downward against the grain of the masonry.

At first it reads only as a trick of the current—a dark, heavy slick of silt or old grease caught in an eddy along the submerged crane rail. It moves with the slow, deliberate economy of something that has spent its entire life negotiating forty feet of pressurized water where a single misjudged angle will tear you off your hold and tumble you south against the outflow grates. Only when it crosses the outer fringe of the third bulkhead lamp does the light catch the pale, muscular margin of its underside, and the faint, oily iridescence of skin that has never known sun, wind, or dry stone.

It is an amphibian, or the distant, subterranean revision of one—descended, perhaps, from some giant river-salamander or mudpuppy washed down through the municipal intakes before the surface world forgot this place existed, though generations in the dark acoustic press of the nave have reshaped it entirely to the architecture. Full-grown, it measures nearly seven feet from its broad, shovel-flat snout to the tip of its deep, oar-bladed tail. Its body is profoundly dorsoventrally flattened, built low and wide like a heavy leather apron so that when it presses itself flush against the soapy oxblood brick or the satin-smooth floor plates, the scouring current slips right over its back and pins it tighter to the wall rather than prying it loose.

Its coloring is the exact, bruised register of the chamber: wet slate, dark cast iron, and stagnant bronze, mottled along the flanks with pale grey patches indistinguishable from the smears of iron-bacteria in the sheltered mortar seams. Down the center of its wide head and along both sides of its trunk run deep, pitted troughs—hypertrophied lateral-line organs that look like rows of countersunk stitch-marks. Its eyes are tiny, sunken beads milky with disuse, barely capable of registering the difference between the amber glow of the carbon filaments and the pitch dark of the corners. It does not need to see. In a room where sound has nowhere to bleed away, the creature wears the entire acoustic volume of the hall on its skin.

Every vibration in the chamber maps its world in high relief. It feels the steady, sixty-cycle hum of the lead-sheathed cables as glowing lines of warmth and buzz pinned to the walls; it reads the three central columns by the singing twin vortices that peel off their iron flanks; it knows the exact shape of the silver cloud of dace hanging in the middle water by the faint, papery hiss of four hundred small tails sculling in unison. Above all, its nervous system is clocked to the great bronze scroll-case on the floor and the carriage-door flap valve at the south bulkhead.

There is only one of this size in the basin—the chamber’s strict budget of watts and drifted flesh could scarcely support a pair—though two smaller, paler juveniles, no longer than a man’s forearm, haunt the high, quiet dead-spaces inside the flooded switchgear housings near the ceiling. The big one claims the whole nave, moving through a slow, territorial circuit that takes hours to complete.

Watch how it travels. It rarely swims across the open middle water; the pike and the big brown trout own the fast, empty volume, and fighting the main current wastes calories. Instead, the creature walks the steel. Its four legs are short, thick as dock-lines, and crooked outward at the elbows and knees, ending in wide, fleshy, fingerless pads ribbed underneath like the tread that vanished centuries ago from the deck plates. By flexing the muscles in the center of each pad while sealing the rim with heavy mucus, it creates a vacuum lock against polished iron or glazed brick. One foot releases with a soft, inaudible peel, swings forward through the boundary layer, plants, and locks. It climbs down the vertical drive-shaft on the east side headfirst, spiraling around the turning steel rod, letting the slow mechanical rotation carry its bulk around like a hand sliding down a banister.

Near the bottom, it pauses on the curved, buzzing roof of the Francis turbine’s bronze scroll-case. Here the vibration is immense—a deep, wooden-bearing rumble that thrills straight through its flat belly bones. For twenty minutes, thirty minutes, it simply lies plastered to the metal, completely motionless. Its external gills do not sprout in wide, feathery bushes behind its head like a pond salamander’s; the heavy flow would shred delicate filaments in a week. Instead, they are tucked beneath two tough, muscular cowls at the base of the throat—dense, dark-crimson pleats packed tight like the pages of a waterlogged book, through which it pumps cold, hyper-oxygenated water with slow, rhythmic gulps of its broad floor-mouth.

While it rests on the bronze casing, small crayfish occasionally click across the dished iron floor plates within reach, claws up, antennae whipping the current. Sometimes the creature takes one—not lunging, just opening one corner of its wide, rubbery mouth to create a sudden, localized low-pressure void. A sharp *thuck* of displaced water, and the crayfish vanishes inward, crushed instantly between broad plates of flat, vomerine teeth evolved to crack mussel shell and chitin alike.

Mostly, though, it waits for the valve.

It knows when the southern culverts are backing up long before the fish do. The pitch of the intake sluices at the north wall drops by a fraction of a tone; the water grows fractionally denser with static pressure; the bronze runner beneath its belly labors with a heavier, Tighter groan. Two minutes before the release, the creature detaches from the turbine housing, drops down into the flanged trough of a floor girder, and creeps smoothly eastward into the deep shadow behind the third cast-iron column, just shy of the tight, razor-edged carpet of mussels. It flattens its chin to the dished iron, folds its thick tail-blade tightly along its flank, and opens the sensory pits along its snout.

Down at the south bulkhead, the brass hinges creak. The giant cast-iron door lifts, vents its roaring white column of trapped air and relieve-water, and drops back.

*Boom.*

The granite-seated shockwave punches northward through forty feet of water. In the middle distance, the pale cloud of dace and bleak shatters outward in momentary panic, flashing tarnished gold in the dusty cones of the four lamps. An eel, startled half out of a rusted conduit box on the catwalk overhead, whips backward to regain its coil. Down near the bottom, a two-foot pike that had been hovering in the slack water near the outflow grates darts sideways to clear the back-wash, disoriented for half a second as its own lateral line rings like a struck bell from the concussion.

Half a second is the entire window.

The creature does not care about the shockwave; its broad skull has no swim-bladder to compress, no delicate inner-ear drum to stun. It rides the *boom* the way a hawk rides an updraft. Before the echo has finished bouncing off the vaulted brick ceiling forty feet overhead, all seven feet of dark, flattened muscle uncoil from behind the iron column. One tremendous, sideways snap of that deep sculling tail launches it horizontally across the polished floor plates. For three heartbeats it ceases to be a creeping piece of the masonry and becomes a projectile—silent, frictionless, parting the cold green gloom in a blur of slate-colored skin.

Its jaws open nearly a hundred and eighty degrees, turning its entire shovel head into a sudden, cavernous intake. Water, sand from the dished bowls in the iron, and the tail-first bulk of the bewildered pike all rush inward together. The heavy jaws clamp shut with a dull cartilage snap; excess water jets out violently through the gill-slits under its throat-cowls; and its momentum carries it skidding across the satin steel until two broad foot-pads slap down onto a rivet-smoothed plate-girder and lock hard against the undertow.

The pike thrashes once, twice, a muffled hammering inside the giant’s wide throat, its scarred snout protruding briefly from the corner of the rubbery lips before a slow, undulating swallow draws it down into the flat gut. Out in the middle hall, the dace have already realigned themselves north, tails flicking steadily again, none the wiser.

After a kill like that, the creature will not need to hunt again for two or three weeks. Digestion in cold water is a slow, meditative enterprise, and there is one place in the chamber marginally warmer than the rest—and one requirement left in its quiet, closed-loop life.

Slowly, hauling its heavier belly up the smoothed brickwork of the west wall, past the bare expanses of oxblood glaze and the grey films of iron-slime, it climbs toward the submerged catwalk. Where the wooden planks rotted away centuries ago, it weaves its long body over and under the naked steel stringers with fluid, boneless grace, careful never to brush the fragile green beards of moss and algae ringing the four lit lamps. Even a blind thing in this sparse hall seems to possess an instinctual, physical caution around those brass cages—not reverence, simply the learned geography that where the faint warmth buzzes on the glass, the little silver fish congregate below, and the eels come to hunt them, and if you tear away the warm glass, that station goes cold and barren forever. It gives the glowing amber globes a wide, respectful berth, letting only the outer edge of the dusty yellow-green light slide across its mottled back and pick out the pale old scars where sharp mussel beds or falling catwalk bolts caught it decades ago.

Higher still it climbs, past the dead switchgear, past the spiral stairwells leading up to hatches bolted shut before its great-grandsires hatched, all the way up into the curve of the vaulted brick ceiling.

Up here, forty feet above the floor, the acoustic roar of the sluices softens into a hollow, echoing wash. Along the very crown of the arch lies that narrow, two-foot crescent of trapped air—misty, cold, smelling faintly of wet lime, ozone from the sealed dynamo nearby, and crushed water-moss. Though its tucked crimson gills can pull all the oxygen it strictly requires from the torrent below, old ancestry lingers in its blood, or perhaps the skin needs periodic relief from the endless fungal pressures of the deep water.

The creature hooks its front pads into a shallow recess where an expansion joint meets the bricks just below the waterline, and slowly pushes its broad, flat snout up through the trembling meniscus.

Bubbles drifting up from the sluices pop continuously against its slate-dark nostrils. Fine, chilly condensation drips from the brick crown onto its tiny milky eyes. With a wet, cavernous hiss that sounds oddly small inside the booming masonry vault, it opens its throat and lets out a stale bubble of spent gas, then draws in a single, long, rasping draught of the trapped, ancient air—air that has been washed clean a million times over by cold tumbling water, sealed tight under the earth.

It hangs there at the top of its sunken cathedral for half an hour, maybe an hour, chin resting on the boundary between water and mist, feeling through its palms the distant, tireless *thrum* of the bronze turbine forty feet below, keeping the dark warm, keeping the four coals lit, keeping the river moving home. Then, slowly, without leaving a ripple larger than the bursting sluice-bubbles, it unhooks its pads, slides backward down the curve of the soapy brick, and sinks back into the green twilight.
