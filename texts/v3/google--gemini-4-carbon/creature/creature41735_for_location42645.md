If you sit long enough in the knee-deep tallow-rosettes—waiting until the ringing in your ears stops fighting the chamber and begins to sort it into layers—you will notice a gap moving through the wall of sound. 

It is not a silence, exactly. Nothing can make silence here. It is a soft, traveling dent in the echo, roughly the volume of a crouching boar, sliding slowly across the cushioned floor at the pace of a clock’s minute hand. Where it passes, the stridulating crawlers do not stop singing out of fear; their rasping is simply swallowed before it can reach the open vault above. The brassy *booms* of the loaf-frogs strike this moving mass and die without bouncing back. To the sharp, rattling sonar of the ceiling gliders, it must read like a hole torn in the room—a patch of black velvet laid over a mirror.

What makes the dent has no eyes, no pigment, and very little bone that you would recognize as rigid. Under the slick, wet-wool creepers, where space is tight and every movement risks bruising the sap-bladders that feed the basin, hard skeletons are an encumbrance. Instead, these things—there are only ever seven or eight of them alive in the dome at any one time, sharing seventy paces of abundance without ever crowding—are built like heavy, muscular tongues wrapped around a cradle of pliable cartilage.

Up close, you smell them only because they smell slightly cleaner than the loam: an odor like warm buttermilk and rubbed slate. Touch one, and your hand sinks an inch before meeting resistance. Their outer hide is not smooth like the frogs, nor plated like the crawlers, but densely pleated into thousands of wet, fleshy baffles—deep, labyrinthine folds resembling the underside of a morel mushroom or the acoustic foam of a recording booth. Moisture collects in the grooves, held thick with a slick secretion that breaks the surface tension of falling ceiling-drops so that water hits their backs without a *plock*. 

In a world made entirely of echo, invisibility is softness. They are acoustic ghosts.

They graze on everything and hurry after nothing. With six broad, unjointed stubs—more like the muscular pedestals of snails than legs, though tipped with flat, hooked pads of horn for levering apart braided roots—they haul their pale, eighty-pound bulk through the perimeter tangle. Underneath, set a third of the way back from the blunt prow of the head, sits a circular, grinding mouth rimmed with sensitive, finger-like barbels. They eat the sweet, fermented jelly of dissolving leaves; they lip up the blind snails whole, crushing the thin carbonate shells internally with muffled, wet *crunches* that never escape their own heavy blubber; when a bloated amphibian grows sluggish with age, they simply flow over its niche in the rosettes and pin it gently down until its booming stops. 

Yet if you take them for mere slugs—blind mouths digesting in the dark—you miss what the broad, blunt head is actually doing.

They do not click like the gliders, because they do not need to make their own light in a room already blazing with sound. The chamber itself is their lantern. Every drop falling forty meters from the sweating stone vault strikes a leaf and sends up a crisp, spherical splash of pressure; every rasping crawler pours a steady beam of high frequency across the basin; every frog-boom washes the curving walls in long, low waves. The room is constantly, violently illuminated from every angle by noise.

And the baffles on the creature’s hide are not uniform. Along the broad, flattened hood of its front end, the pleats narrow into exquisitely tuned canals, lined with stiff, microscopic sensory hairs bathed in heavy oil. It does not just absorb the room’s racket; it *focuses* it. By subtly flexing the cartilage beneath its hood—widening a groove here, pinching a fold shut there—it reads the phase-shifts and shadows of the chamber’s din with the effortless precision of a hawk scanning a meadow. It knows the exact shape of a sap-bladder ten paces away by the way the crawler-hum bends around its swollen curve. It knows when a ceiling glider peels loose from the stone overhead by the instantaneous, two-millimeter shift in the ceiling’s acoustic reflection, tracking the silent, leathery drop across sixty meters of black air simply by listening to the moving shadow the glider casts against the ambient roar.

They have lived in this single bubble of stone long enough to outgrow haste, rivalry, and panic. Their hearts beat perhaps four times a minute, pushing thick, pale-copper blood at the exact ambient temperature of the room. Because the five frictionless stones never falter, these creatures have discarded the expensive furnace of warm-bloodedness, yet they enjoy all the quick, rich neurology that constant warmth allows. Behind that baffled hood sits a brain heavy and convoluted as a walnut loaf, entirely devoted to three-dimensional wave-mathematics, memory, and touch.

You see what that brain is for when two of them meet near one of the artifacts.

They gather around the five stones—particularly the one that hangs two fingers’ breadth above the settled bedrock. That two-finger gap is the cleanest acoustic lens in the hollow. Sound slipping under the floating stone is sheared into flat, razor-thin planes that skim across the soap-smooth floor pan. An old adult—perhaps two centuries old, scarred only by pale, knotted seams where a crawler once pinched it decades ago—will ease its heavy prow right up to the artifact. Its barbels reach out, approach the surface, and stop abruptly as they hit that strange, frictionless boundary where even water refuses to bead. It cannot touch the stone, not really; its flesh slides off without purchase just as yours would. Instead, it tucks its front pads underneath itself and settles into the warm metabolic bath, letting its cells drink directly from the quiet field the object bleeds, giving its slow gut a rest from digestion.

When another approaches through the tallow-cushions, neither stiffens. There is not enough room in seventy paces for territorial war, and over hundreds of thousands of years, evolution here has ruthlessly pruned away high-strung nerves; anything that thrashingly fought or bred out of measure fouled its own small bowl. They keep their numbers steady by some quiet chemical consensus washed through the tepid puddles—no juvenile ever matures until an elders' scent fades from the water and its body melts back into the fungi.

Instead of fighting, the two press their pleated flanks together. 

For hours—while outside in the freezing dark of the thin plateau air, a winter storm may be scouring a millimeter of dust off the whalebacked peak—the two heavy shapes lie braided among the coriander-smelling roots. And then they do the one thing in the cavern that alters the room’s sound rather than hiding from it.

They do not have vocal cords. They do not make noise of their own. Instead, lying side by side, they begin to open and shut the muscular baffles along their backs in slow, coordinated ripples. 

Instead of swallowing the chamber’s din, they start to bounce it back. 

A frog booms ten yards upslope; the two pale masses catch that brassy wave in stiffened, cupped pleats of hide and throw it upward at a sharpened angle, making it ring off a dry patch of the upper vault it hasn't struck cleanly in an hour. A curtain of high crawler-stridulation washes over them; they ripple their grooves in unison, combed to a half-wavelength, splitting the steady hiss into a sudden, cascading arpeggio of overtones that sweeps across the sixty-meter void like wind through chimes. They play the room’s own relentless noise back into the vault—bending it, delaying it by milliseconds, tossing phantom shapes of echo high into the empty black center where nothing hangs. 

Up on the wet ceiling, the colonies of gliders twitch their ears and chatter faster, mapping sudden, impossible geometries that bloom in mid-air—hollow pillars of phase-canceled quiet, spinning rings of concentrated insect-hum, ghost-surfaces that swell to fill the church-sized dome and vanish in a breath.

Down in the knee-deep wet wool, the two creatures shift an inch closer, reading each other's minds by the shapes they carve out of the roar. When the atmospheric pressure drops over the course of a long week and the ceiling begins to weep faster, they tilt their hoods upward together, letting the fat, warm beads fall harmlessly and silently into their pleated skin, listening as the whole mountain’s single, buried bell drops half a semitone and keeps on ringing.
