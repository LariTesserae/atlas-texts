If you stay in the cleft long enough for your pupils to widen to their absolute limits—past the point where the indigo thread overhead offers any useful reference, until your vision relies entirely on the infrequent, tenth-of-a-second flashes of struck silicate—you begin to realize that the great, wheezing climbers are not individuals in the way lowlanders understand the word. Not quite. They are too crowded for individuality to remain tidy, and too slow for boundaries to stay sharply drawn.

Look closely during the next orange spark of triboluminescence, right at the base of the western wall where the overhang forces the mass of bodies into a three-deep compression. 

The slate-gray plates of the climbers are not clean. Because an adult moves perhaps four feet in a month, and sometimes does not unseal its four main anchoring cups from a particularly rich seam of hornfels for two years at a stretch, the distinction between the climber’s hide and the cleft’s floor blurs. The rust-colored, iron-digesting filaments do not stop at the edge of the rock; they send shallow, hair-fine rhizoids into the dead, outer keratin of the climbers’ armor, staining their shoulders in swirling patterns of oxidized ochre. Pale bladder-worts root in the deep, damp creases behind their anchoring knees, swelling on the carbon-rich exhaust of their spiracles. 

An elder—one of the massive, six-hundred-pound matriarchs wedged near the warm, vapor-weeping fault-lines at the bottom of the nave—is less a single beast than a mobile ledge. Her hide is two inches thick on the dorsal plates, laminated like slate and scarred with a thousand white-healed hairline nicks from centuries of falling debitage. If you touch her flank, it feels only marginally warmer than the andesite, and nearly as hard, vibrating faintly with the deep, sub-audible purr of a three-chambered heart beating four times a minute.

Beneath those plates, their anatomy is a masterpiece of hydraulic patience. Muscle tissue, greedy for oxygen, is kept to a stark minimum. Instead, the four thick anchoring limbs operate on a system of dense, fibrous bladders and locking cartilaginous ratchets. To grip a knife-edge of stone, a climber presses its broad foot-pad over the rock—the flesh of the sole is a thick, viscoelastic gel, tough as vulcanized rubber, that molds slowly around razor-splinters without tearing—and draws back a central piston-tendon. Once the vacuum is pulled, a bony pawl clicks into place inside the joint. From that moment on, hanging upside down over a hundred-foot drop requires zero caloric expenditure. A climber can sleep, gestate, or die without letting go; only when post-mortem decay finally softens the cartilage after several weeks does the ratchet slip, releasing the heavy, resonant *thud-slither-thud* of a corpse coming down through the dark.

Their heads are scarcely heads at all, merely a thickened, downward-sloping cowl at the anterior end of the body, tucked permanently into the hollow between the forelimbs to protect the softer sensory organs from falling stone flakes. Under that hood sit six eyes arranged in a wide crescent. Four are tiny, recessed pinpricks, good only for tracking the faint, wandering smudge of the sky-slit far above, letting them orient to verticality. The central pair, however, are enormous—two inches across, bulbous, utterly black, and filmed with a heavy, oily tear-layer that never blinks. They are adapted entirely to the flashes.

Because a spark of struck hornfels lasts less than a heartbeat, the climbers’ retinas do not clear an image quickly. Their optic nerves possess a staggering chemical persistence; when a neighbor twenty feet away brings a dike-rock cobble down on a flinty rib and throws a brief, jaundiced flare of yellow-green light across the gloom, that ghost-image burns into the watching eyes and lingers there for three or four minutes, fading slowly like a cooling coal. To live in the cleft is to navigate by a constantly updating series of overlapping afterimages—a slow-motion strobe-world where you see your neighbor’s raised arm as it was two minutes ago, superimposed over where it was five minutes ago, calculating its trajectory through time rather than continuous sight.

This is why they strike stone even when no flake is needed. 

Listen to the percussion again, sorting it out from the wet, tidal roar of the breathing. *Clack. Tink. Chink-clack.* Not all of those blows have the heavy, dull crunch of flaking or the strained, grating pop of prying. Every minute or so, from high up near the pinched vault or deep in the talus piles, comes a pure, clear *ping*—dike-stone tapped lightly against the thin edge of a resonant blade of andesite, producing a bright blue flash and a clean pitch that cuts effortlessly through the bass wheeze of ten thousand lungs.

It is echolocation and semaphore at once. A climber suspended on an overhang taps a protruding fin twice—*tink-tink*—throwing two quick sparks. In that split second of illumination, its boneless fore-pads are held aloft in a specific, splayed posture. Every climber within forty feet catches both the acoustic ring, which tells them the thickness and tension of the rock their neighbor is clinging to, and the lingering visual imprint of the hand-sign burned onto their retinas. Five minutes later, from across the pitch-black gulf, another cobble answers: *clack*—a single orange spark illuminating a single curled pad. *I am here. This seam is occupied. The rock beneath me is shifting.*

Their forelimbs are the strangest part of them. Extending from beneath the armored cowl, they look almost naked—slender, pale-gray tubes of braided tendon and hydrostatic muscle, segmented by seven ball-and-socket joints that allow them to bend around corners and reach deep into jagged fissures. At the tips, instead of claws (which would only chip and dull against the glassy stone), the flesh widens into three flat, muscular lobes, grey-violet with dense capillary beds and ribbed on the underside like a gecko’s toe or a rough file. 

These pads are saturated with nerve endings. In the dark, a climber’s "hands" are its true face. Before an individual ever strikes a cobble against a wall, it will spend three or four hours running those sensitive, boneless lobes over the hornfels, reading microscopic grain boundaries, cooling cracks, and internal stress-lines with the concentrated delicacy of a jeweler examining a diamond. It feels where the mountain wants to break. When the blow finally falls—driven not by a fast swing, which their slow-twitch physiology cannot muster, but by lifting the heavy hammer-stone high and dropping the entire cantilevered weight of the shoulder behind it in a sudden, un-ratcheted release—it hits with surgical accuracy, peeling off a curved, translucent ribbon of black glass nine inches long and one molecule thick at the edge.

Watch what happens when that ribbon comes free. 

Before it even settles into the knapper’s lower pad, the secondary inhabitants react. Three of the long-legged, strider-like arthropods—local things with carapaces no bigger than walnuts and hair-thin legs spanning thirty inches—pause in their delicate stepping across the climber's back. Their hollow, reed-like antennae swing toward the sharp scent of freshly fractured silica and ionized ozone. Freshly broken deep-crust rock traps microscopic pockets of volcanic gases—hydrogen sulfide, methane, trace ammonia—locked inside the cooling magma millennia ago. When a flake snaps off, a tiny puff of these trapped volatiles sighs out into the thin air.

To the cleft, broken stone smells like food.

The striders dip their long, jointed proboscises down to the raw scar on the wall, lapping up the microscopic film of mineral-rich dampness that weeps instantly from the newly exposed pores before the air can oxidize it. Right behind them slide the gourd-shaped grazers—slug-like mollusks with flattened, rubbery mantles the color of tallow. They are utterly blind, guided by chemoreceptors along their skirts, and they congregate around the working pads of a successful tool-user like barnacles around a whale’s mouth, waiting to rasp away the invisible rock-flour produced by the hammer blows, using the sharp silicate dust to build small, glass-spined plates inside their own soft flesh so the leeches cannot pierce them.

And the climbers tolerate them—mostly. A climber’s metabolism is so frugal that swatting a two-ounce arthropod is a ruinous waste of ATP. Instead, a slow economy of grooming plays out across their hides. The gourd-slugs eat the stinging fungal spores that would otherwise rot the soft skin inside the climbers’ breathing spiracles; in exchange, once every week or two, a climber’s wandering fore-pad will calmly, without haste, pluck the fattest, glass-heavy slug off its own shoulder and tuck it beneath its cowl.

Underneath that cowl, hidden from the falling drops and the peering eyes of neighbors, sits the mouth: not a jawed muzzle, which would take too much leverage to work in thin air, but a circular, muscular mill lined with four rotating plates of self-sharpening, iron-impregnated tooth, behind which works a long, horny radula. Feeding is a continuous, background grinding. Rubbery black tubers wedged out of the cracks, mats of rust-filament, scraped chemotrophic slime, the occasional gourd-slug or slow-moving pale worm pried from a popped slab—all of it goes into the mill alongside deliberate pinches of fine hornfels grit to help crush the fibrous cell walls in a cold, sluggish gut that takes a full month to pass a meal.

Their blood, when you see it welling from a fresh cut, is thick as cold syrup and dark indigo-black, heavy with copper-based hemocyanin and a massive overabundance of giant, nucleated corpuscles engineered to wring every stray molecule of oxygen out of the starved, high-altitude air. It clots almost instantly upon touching the cold dampness, forming a rubbery, blue-gray scab that soon hardens into another ridge of horn.

Because they cannot afford waste, death in the cleft is less an ending than a redistribution of mass. 

Down at the very bottom of the throat, where the wedged talus blades cross over one another above black, bottomless-looking sumps of bitter mineral water, the air is slightly warmer and thick with heavier gases—carbon dioxide sinking from ten thousand exhalations, mixed with faint geothermic breath rising from the deep mountain roots. The climbers rarely descend to the true floor; the oxygen down in that stagnant sump-layer is dangerously thin even for them, and the talus shifts treacherously when leaned upon. 

Yet that is where everything falls. Snapped basalt pry-bars, dulled flakes by the millions, shed armor plates, excrement in dry, stony pellets, and the rare, heavy bodies of the dead.

Down in that suffocating, carbonic basement lives what could almost be called the cleft’s digestive tract. If a spark from fifty feet up happens to drop far enough into the talus before dying, it illuminates a pale, glistening carpet covering the jagged blades. There are things down there related to the climbers—perhaps derived from them during some ancient bottleneck when the cleft first cracked open, or perhaps their larval stage gone neotenic and feral in the foul air; it is hard to tell without cutting one open, and nothing goes down there that plans on climbing back up. 

They are flattened, boneless-looking sacks of translucent white hide, the size of badgers, lacking the heavy dorsal armor entirely. Their thoracic bellows are monstrously enlarged, taking up two-thirds of their body volume, pumping with a slow, silent, undulating ripple rather than the whistling roar of the wall-dwellers above. They do not hold hammer-stones—down in the talus, there is nothing left to pry—but their three-lobed fore-pads have grown elongated and stiffened at the edges, functioning like scoops and pincers. 

When an old climber’s ratchets finally rot through high up on the east wall and the great gray bulk comes tumbling down—hitting an overhang with a dull *boom*, spinning lazily through the dark, shearing off three yards of hanging gut-tube colonies on its way, and finally wedging with a crystalline crash into the razor-talus at the bottom—the white sump-dwellers are already oozing out of the narrow chinks between the boulders. 

Over the next three months, they disassemble the fallen elder with silent, surgical thoroughness. And here is the eerie thing: they use the dead climber's own droppedflakes to do it. Scooping up the discarded razors of hornfels that litter the floor like black leaves, the pale scavengers hold them in their pale lobes and saw patiently through the two-inch slate hide that no tooth in the chasm could ever pierce. They strip the dense hydraulic tendons, drink the thick blue-black marrow from the short, pillar-like leg bones, and scrape the copper-rich clots from the heart chambers. 

Even then, the commerce between top and bottom is not one-way. Once a carcass is hollowed to clean bone and indigestible keratin plates, the sump-dwellers abandon it; and within days, long, braided lines of sinew—actual ropes, the single exception to the chasm’s lack of manufactured goods, twisted together over decades out of dried gut-curtain filaments and shed hide-strips by the lowest tier of wall-climbers—come snaking slowly down through the dark. Weighted at the end with a notched cobble, these lines fish blindly among the bones. They are not looking for meat. They are feeling for the fallen elder’s hammer-stone, if it slipped past the grabbing hands on the way down, or for the dense, curved thigh-bones themselves, which make softer, shock-absorbing billets for pressure-flaking very brittle volcanic glass. When the sensitive tip of a sixty-foot-deep fishing climber’s pad—hanging head-down at the absolute limit of the breathable zone, lungs screaming softly as it stretches—touches that smooth, treasured river-roundness of an ancient striking-stone among the splinters, the lobes curl around it like an anemone closing on a crab. Hour by hour, ratchet-click by ratchet-click, the heirloom is hauled back up into the working world.

Look higher, though, if you want to understand their reproduction, because nothing young could survive the sump, and nothing soft could survive the crowded middle walls where the three-foot basalt levers are constantly shifting stone and dropping razors.

Follow the walls upward, past the broad, damp alcoves packed flank-to-flank with heavy adults, past the hanging, mucilaginous curtains of filter-feeding tubes that smell faintly of iodine and wet iron, up into the narrowing attic of the throat where the leaning walls close to within ten feet of one another. 

Up here, thirty feet below the knife-edged lip of the outside world, the air is colder—bitingly cold, closer to the ambient frost of the upper troposphere—and dry enough that the rock stops sweating. The big climbers cannot fit into the narrowest upward-pinching fissures here, nor can their damp, wide spiracles endure the dessicating draft that whistles across the top slot whenever a stratosphere gale scours the peak outside. 

Yet the rock up here is stippled with thousands of tiny, chipped cups, no bigger than teacups, pecked laboriously into the underside of the overhangs over generations. 

Inside each cup clings a juvenile, no larger than a clenched human fist. 

They are born live, single births, emerging from a brood-pouch beneath the mother’s cowl already possessing working suction-feet and eyes wide open to the dark. A mother spends six weeks climbing to the upper tier—yielding right-of-way to nobody, leaning her heavy cobble menacingly against the carapace of any male or barren female slow enough to block her vertical seam—deposits the fist-sized neonate into an empty pecked cup, places a single, thumb-sized splinter of sharp hornfels into its tiny, trembling fore-pads, and immediately begins the six-week descent back to warmth and water. She never feeds it. Milk is far too metabolically expensive for this altitude.

Instead, the nursery tier runs on rising heat and updrafts. All the warm, damp breath of the ten thousand giants below funnels upward toward the narrow sky-slot, carrying with it a thin, invisible snow of organic detritus: spores from the bladder-worts, microscopic droplets of oil coughed from spiracles, dried flecks of blood, dust of ground-up moss, and tiny, winged midges that breed in the hanging gut-curtains. 

As this warm plume hits the freezing stone just below the lip, the moisture condenses instantly into a thin rime of hoarfrost—the only ice in the chasm—trapping all that airborne organic dust in a nutritious,dirty glaze across the rock. 

The fist-sized young do not breathe with the slow, roaring bellows of their elders; they are small enough to rely partly on cutaneous respiration through soft, richly vascularized skin between their baby plates, and their tiny spiracles tick rapidly, like pocket watches—*tick-tick-tick-tick*—a faint, high-frequency whispering right under the roof of the world. Armed with their mother’s parting splinter, they lean out of their stone teacups and scrape the dirty hoarfrost off the ceiling, licking the organic rime from the stone blade. When the blade dulls, they tap it awkwardly against the rim of their cup—*tink, tink*—throwing pinprick sparks no brighter than dying fireflies, learning the grain of the rock before they are six months old, deepening their own shelters half a millimeter a year until their shoulders grow too wide for the cup and gravity, hunger, and thickening skin draw them slowly downward into the roaring, chiming press of the nave.

And what of the predators—those multi-limbed scavenger-hunters that work the middle walls?

If a bright green spark pops nearby, look not at the rounded, radially hunched backs of the climbers, look at the cracks *between* them. 

The hunters are often mistaken for part of a climber’s own anatomy until you count the limbs and realize there are too many emerging from under a single ledge. Locals of no name, evolved perhaps from some deep-fissure centipede or segmented rock-worm that followed the rising crust upward and grew gigantic in the absence of fast vertebrates, they are ribbon-flat, yard-long creatures armored in glossy, jet-black chitin that mimics wet hornfels to perfection. They have no suction cups; instead, twenty pairs of hook-tipped walking legs allow them to slot their paper-thin bodies sideways into fractures half an inch wide, moving behind the climbers, inside the walls themselves, traveling through the mountain’s internal plumbing of fault-planes.

A hunter cannot overpower a six-hundred-pound climber, nor can its mandibles chew through slate-thick hide. So it watches the sparks, tracking them with clusters of pale, faceted ocelli set flush into its flat head. And it borrows the cleft’s defining habit. 

Held in its modified front maxillipeds—thick, crab-like raptorial claws lined with rubbery tubercles—the hunter carries a long, needle-sharp stiletto of glassy hornfels, scavenged from the debitage of a master knapper and chosen with terrifying discernment for straightness and point. 

It slides three inches of its flat head out from a crack directly beneath a resting climber’s flank, braces its rear twenty segments deep inside the rock fissure for leverage, places the tip of the stone needle precisely into the soft, pulsing crease behind one of the giant’s anchoring limbs, and simply locks its joints, pushing outward with steady, unyielding spring-tension. 

It is a contest of pure geology. The climber feels the prick immediately, of course, through its skin-nerves, even if its brain takes ten seconds to register the threat and another thirty seconds to route hydraulic fluid toward its defense. You will not hear a roar of pain; screaming wastes air. You will only hear the giant’s twenty-second exhalation hitch slightly—a wet, rattling *huff*—as it begins the long, tectonic shift of its center of gravity, sliding two hundred pounds of calloused hip-plate downward by fractions of an inch per minute, trying to shear the stone needle off against the cliff face or trap the hunter’s flat head against an arrête and grind it to paste.

Meanwhile, the climber’s own slender forelimb comes snaking slowly over its own back, heavy hammer-stone poised, feeling blindly through the dark for the slick chitin in the crack. Sometimes the hunter’s needle finds the deep femoral sinus first; thick, dark blood wells out in slow pulses, the climber’s hydraulic pressure drops, a foot-cup loses vacuum with a wet, echoing *pop*, and three neighboring climbers immediately—without malice, without haste—reach out their free pads to steady the dying neighbor’s hammer-stone so it won’t fall into the abyss while the black ribbon-thing slips forward into the wound to drink. Other times, you hear a sharp, dry *crunch* quite distinct from stone-working, followed by the slow oozing of pale yellow hemolymph down the wet andesite, and for the next two days the victorious climber calmly uses a fresh flake to hook segments of crushed hunter out of the crack like meat from a lobster claw.

Yet if you stand wedged on a narrow plinth halfway up the southern seal-wall, letting the clammy mist soak through your clothes until you shiver uncontrollably in the thin, metallic-smelling gloom, the overwhelming impression of the cleft is not violence. It is industry—an eerie, aimless, geological industry that has accidentally stumbled across the threshold of mind, or something orthogonal to mind.

Watch two old climbers hanging side by side on a broad sheet of black andesite near the center of the chamber. Their hides are bridged together by a single continuous mat of ochre iron-moss; they have not moved away from one another in so long that the gourd-slugs glide seamlessly from one back to the next as if traversing a single boulder. 

Between them, wedged into a stubborn vertical fault-line, is a four-foot column of dense gray basalt—a prize pry-bar, worn glassy-slick at the grip-ends by decades of boneless pads. Both creatures have one forelimb wrapped around the lever, leaning their combined half-ton of dead weight into the torque. Every twenty-eight seconds, in perfect phase-locked synchrony now after months of shared labor, their massive thoracic sacs haul in the thin air—*whoooooooosh*—hold it for four seconds of dead silence in their corner while the rest of the cathedral wheezes on, and let it go in a warm, damp cloud that wreaths both cowled heads together—*shhhhhhhhhhh*.

With the other, free forelimb, one of them holds a small, worn cobble, smoothly egg-shaped—an object that must be three hundred years old, battered down from an angular block by ten million patient taps. Slowly, moving like the hour hand of a tower clock, it brings the cobble around and taps it—not against the cliff, and not against a flake core, but softly, gently against the dorsal armor plate of its partner. *Tuk.* No spark this time; hide does not flash. Just a bone-conducted vibration traveling straight through keratin and fluid into the partner’s inner ear.

Three minutes pass. A drop of bitter water detaches from sixty feet overhead and hits the basalt lever between them: *plink*. 

Slowly, the partner’s free pad rises from a crevice, holding a freshly peeled, translucent blade of hornfels, wet with mineral-slime, and presses the flat of the glass against the sensitive, violet-ribbed lobes of the first climber’s waiting hand, letting it feel the keenness of the edge, letting it smell the tiny, trapped sigh of ancient sulfur still clinging to the fracture. 

Nobody speaks. Nothing builds. Far above, a single hard star wheels invisibly across the crooked seam of bruised indigo sky and vanishes behind the raw lip of the mountain. Down in the dark, the two giants tighten their ratchets by a single notch against the groaning basalt, a hairline crack snakes three inches farther into the heart of the peak with a bright, violet flash that lights ten thousand wet, unblinking black eyes at once, and the mountain’s own split throat keeps eating itself alive, one spark, one breath, one razor-thin flake at a time.
