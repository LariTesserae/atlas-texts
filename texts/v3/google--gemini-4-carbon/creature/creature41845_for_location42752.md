At first, you would look for a man in rubber waders.

It is an honest mistake, born of those spinning brass governors. Brass does not keep a bright, oiled sheen in a hot, acidic fog by itself, and carbon-arc lamps do not burn forever; their glowing rods shorten by millimeters every hour, spitting white-hot ash against their wire cages, requiring a fresh stick of baked coke to be dropped into the collet before the light dies. Somewhere in that thunderous wet room, something is keeping house.

Wait forty seconds. Let the western relief-stack slam open with its brass *clack*, let the horizontal roar of white steam erase the middle of the plaza, and watch the amber light through the curtain of sudden, hammering rain.

High up on the flank of the third switch-house—just beneath the eaves where the porcelain insulator bells spit their pale violet sparks—a patch of the metallic-green moss detaches itself from the glazed tile.

It does not crawl. Muscle and nerve cannot survive the stray current bleeding through the basin, and whatever this is, it has neither. It moves the way an oil slick changes shape on a tilting plate, or the way a drop of mercury chooses a groove in floorboards, except that it has heft, and fibrous architecture, and an unmistakable, unhurried grip.

Up close, if you could stand in that tea-dark water without your calves locking into galvanic spasm, you would see that it is a bundle—roughly the bulk of a hunched badger, though rarely the same shape for three minutes running—composed entirely of tightly braided root-hair, blackened copper wire scavenged from frayed windings, and plates of plum-colored iron scale shed by the turbine housings. Its interior is a dense, gelatinous pith, clear as boiled starch and smelling sharply of pickled walnuts and battery acid. Through that translucent jelly run branching filaments of precipitated copper, grown directly into the tissue the way mineral dendrites form in agate.

There are perhaps eight or nine of them in the basin at any given time, though counting them is a slippery business.

Watch the one under the eaves. It reaches the wire cage of the sputtering arc lamp by rolling its own braided tendrils over one another—turgor pressure, not sinew, pumping warm, mineral-heavy sap from its rear mass into its leading edge with a rhythmic, hydraulic *squeak* like wet leather. One stubby, calloused runner slips the rusted latch of the lamp cage. Another—tipped with a thick, dead cap of hardened resin that insulates it from the live current—reaches directly into the blinding amber glare, plucks out the burnt-down nub of carbon, and drops it.

The glowing stub falls four stories, hissing softly as it vanishes into the knee-deep flood below.

From the hollow of its own interwoven body, where three fresh carbon rods are carried like quills in a basket, the thing draws a new cylinder and slides it into the brass jaws. The arc stutters, flares violet, and settles back into its steady, buzzing gold. The latch is pressed shut. For a whole minute afterward, the creature simply hangs from the bottom of the iron cage like a drenched wasps’ nest, basking in the fierce ultraviolet wash and the high-voltage hum, its outer mat of dark liverwort trembling with tiny, greedy adjustments.

They are not animals that learned to look like plants. They are the basin’s flora having solved a mobile problem.

Long ago—when the municipal engineers stopped coming down the terraced embankments, or when the city above forgot what these buried return-mains were cooling—the district’s maintenance became a closed loop, and then a broken one. Somewhere in the dark rooms behind the lancet windows, hoppers of carbon rods, vats of heavy cylinder oil, and crates of tallow packing-grease stood waiting for hands that never arrived. Meanwhile, outside in the flooded thoroughfares, the giant aroids and conductive mosses were learning to drink stray amperage straight off the wet bricks, using voltage gradients the way ordinary weeds use sunlight.

When an arc lamp died, a patch of wall went cold and dark. When a governor ran dry and began to chatter, the pitch of the turbine beneath it ragged out, turning the smooth, nourishing sixty-cycle hum in the water into a jagged, tearing static that burned the great root-mats.

The plants hated a rough current.

Nobody saw the century in which the aerial roots of the high chandeliers first crept through the unglazed lancet windows toward the warm smell of whale-oil and tallow in the storage drums, or how detached, vegetative bulbils—dropped into the currents by the copper-veined philodendrons—began rolling along the drowned curbstones, accumulating stray iron filings and greasy lint until they became self-contained galvanic cells. What exists now is the settled result: the *tenders*, if a word is needed for things that have no eyes, no mouths, and no division between tool and limb.

Look down into the clear, dark tea of the avenue when the localized rain-squall stops and the surface smooths out into black glass.

Along the submerged curbstone between two pressure-exchangers, a much larger mass is moving against the pump-current. This one is flat and broad as a manhole cover, built like a heavy, sodden hearth-rug woven of woody vine-cables and matted peat, ballasted with whole vitrified bricks clutched in its underside. As it drags itself over the chamfered paving, it scours the drowned street clean of fallen leaves and lime scale, digesting the organic rot through a pale, ruffled underside of root-lets while extruding tiny, hard pellets of pure copper sulfate behind it like bright blue gravel.

Ahead of it, one of the buried return-mains pulses under the brick, sending up a lazy shimmer of heat. The flat dredge-thing stops directly over the hot spot. Slowly, its edges curl upward, forming a shallow dish. Two smaller, nimble knots—the size of wharf rats, bristling with stiff whiskers of stripped bronze wire—unbraid themselves from the parent mat and float upward on trapped pearls of oxygen, catching hold of a cast-iron bollard to haul themselves out into the heavy, wool-wet air.

They do not have distinct territories, nor do they have individual lives in any clean sense. Inside the third-story gallery of the eastern turbine house—a narrow, deafening room where a walking-beam engine rocks back and forth above a pit of churning condensate—three of the badger-sized tenders are currently fused together into a single, glistening saddle draped across the warm iron casing of a main bearing.

From a cracked stoneware cistern in the corner, a permanent, stationary vine—thick as a man’s thigh and pulsing like a slow throat—draws heavy amber cylinder oil out of storage by capillary draw and passes it across the tiled floor to them. The fused mass absorbs the oil into bladders of tough, translucent skin, creeps out to the edge of the rocking iron beam—waiting with absolute, clockwork patience for the *chug-thump* of the downstroke—and wipes a greasy, fibrous pad across the scored steel trunnion just before the metal slams back up. Millimeter by millimeter, decade by decade, they keep the skin of oil intact. If a pad is caught by the flywheel and sheared away, there is no cry and no recoil; the severed clump splashes into the sump, where its stray root-hairs immediately latch onto the wet masonry and begin slowly braiding a new body out of rust and lint.

Watch what happens three seconds before the forty-second mark.

Every creature in the basin stills. Not because they hear a warning click—the roar of the pumps drowns everything—but because they feel the micro-volt shift in the wet bricks as the western boiler hits peak pressure and strains against its rivets.

Across the flooded plaza, the giant tabletop elephant ears tilt their waxy blades. High on the feeder cables, the twenty-foot emerald chandeliers stiffen. And halfway up the glazed brick pillar of a pressure-exchanger, a climbing tender—burdened with a lump of pale tallow wrapped in its belly-roots—flattens itself into a mortar seam, folding its copper-veined plates tight over its soft starch core like a chiton on a wave-beaten rock.

*Clack.*

The brass counterweight lifts. The white horizontal volcano erupts across the square, turning the pewter gloom into a screaming, boiling whiteout. Hot rain hammers the black water into froth, washes sheets of condensate down the tiles, and sluices over the flattened lump on the pillar. In the sudden spike of humidity, the bare braided busbars along the walls hiss and bloom with furious, crackling sleeves of violet corona.

The tender on the pillar does not flinch from the sparks. As the stray voltage surges down the wet glazed brick, its outer pelt of metallic moss flushes a brilliant, startled emerald, drinking the spike greedily, tiny silver bubbles fizzing out of its seams into the hot runoff.

Then the counterweight drops. The roar cuts off as cleanly as a slammed door. The localized squall vanishes, leaving behind the steady *chug-thump* of the walking beams, the hollow ringing notes of fat iron-drips hitting the flooded street, and long ribbons of amber arc-light trembling on the black water.

On the pillar, the wet, plum-scaled knot uncurls from the brickwork. With a faint, hydraulic *squeak*, it resumes hoisting its lump of tallow up toward the high lancet window, unhurried, warm, and entirely at home in the thunder.
