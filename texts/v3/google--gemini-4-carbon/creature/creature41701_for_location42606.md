You will know one is near before it touches anything, because the echoes stop making sense.

Move through a thicket of heat-fronds and you carry a wake of displaced air and spattering droplets; click your tongue or snap a knuckle and the world comes back to you as a crisp, wet geometry—the hard, flat bounce of limestone underfoot, the fuzzy, absorbent mush of hanging root-mats to your left, the sharp, ringing *ping* of a copper condensate pipe thirty paces ahead. When a warden passes behind that pipe, the *ping* doesn’t merely cut out, the way it would if a heavy grazer had wandered into the line of sound. Instead, the space behind the pipe suddenly sounds like a hundred yards of empty, rain-swept marsh. A second later, as the creature shifts its weight, that marsh folds inward and sounds like a dense wall of kinetic trunks. It wears misdirection the way a cuttlefish wears color, shrugging false distances across its hide as casually as breathing.

If you could lay hands on one—and you would only ever do so if it were dead, or if it were curious about the shape of your ribs—you would find something heavy, low-slung, and disturbingly warm, running several degrees hotter than the mouth-warm rain. Fully stretched across a stone lip, an adult weighs perhaps four hundred pounds, though its mass is slung low between six limbs that jut slightly outward before dropping straight to the stone on wide, pneumatic pads. There is no head in the terrestrial sense. Evolution here never had a reason to bundle eyes onto a high, swiveling turret; instead, the brain sits deep inside the armored barrel of the chest, insulated from concussion by thick cushions of oil-rich fat and suspended behind a double breastplate of dense, resonant bone.

What extends forward from the shoulders is a muscular, prehensile prow—half neck, half trunk—ending in a wide, scalloped rosette of mobile cartilage. When relaxed, this rosette folds inward like a closed fist to shield its delicate interior from flying grit and scalding exhaust steam. When listening or sniffing, it flares open to the size of an umbrella, a plush, wet dish of radially pleated flesh lined with millions of microscopic cilia, thermal pits, and chemoreceptive grooves. Behind it, sweeping back over the ribs and haunches like a heavy scalloped cloak, hangs the mantle: hundreds of overlapping, muscular dermal flaps, some inflated with lymph to absorb high-frequency clicks without an echo, others stiffened with keratin plates that can be tilted and cupped in milliseconds to catch ambient noise and throw it back at a slightly altered angle.

Beneath the mantle, the limbs divide their labor. The rear four are squat, tireless pistons built for wading thigh-deep spillways, bracing against sixty-knot gusts, and clamping onto slick, moss-furred cast iron. Their soles are concentric rings of ridged, weeping muscle that can stamp down on smooth wet limestone, squeeze the water out, and pull a vacuum strong enough to let that four-hundred-pound bulk walk calmly upside down beneath an overhanging karst arch.

The front pair have largely given up the ground. Folded tightly against the chest when traveling, they unfold to nearly twice the length of the walking legs—triple-jointed, whip-strong arms ending in hands of five radially opposed fingers. Two of those fingers are thick, hooked talons sheathed in horn, strong enough to pry apart rusted inspection louvers or hook over a moving haulage cable. The other three are long, boneless-seeming tapers of packed nerve endings, tipped with calloused fingernail-like picks. When a warden investigates an unfamiliar object, those three fine fingers move over it at a blur, drumming, scraping, pinching, and measuring thermal conductivity, building a three-dimensional map of internal density from the way the object rings under the fingernail-taps.

They did not build the machines. Whatever built the machines—or dropped the seed-factories from orbit above the lower crushing basins, eons ago, to harvest rare earths and pump volatile brines up to the high cableways—designed the pin-patterns on the hatches for a hand with four parallel digits and a single thumb. You can feel the discrepancy if you trace the ancient cast-iron plaques yourself: the raised braille-like grids are spaced slightly too tightly for a warden’s wide talons, slightly too coarsely for its trembling sensory tapers.

Yet the machines belong to the wardens now, in the oblique, non-negotiable way that coral reefs belong to parrotfish, or human cities belong to rats and falcons—except these tenants have crossed the threshold into engineering.

Listen to the six-second *clank-hiss* of the barge-sized pump-jack straddling the mineral seep. Stay beside it in the warm, dripping mist for an hour, letting the rhythm sink into your teeth until any deviation jumps out like a shout. On the four-hundredth stroke, you will hear a tiny, extraneous *tink-tink-chirr* riding just underneath the hiss of the steam-exhaust.

That is a tool hitting a grease-nipple.

Clinging to the underside of the walking beam, riding the twenty-foot vertical rise and plunge in effortless, counter-weighted silence, a juvenile warden is wedged into the angle of a steel truss by its four suction-cupped hind feet. Held in its two heavy talons is a length of salvaged spring-steel whisker boom snapped off a wrecked crawler miles away, cold-forged and ground against abrasive basalt until its tip fits the recessed hexagonal valve on the pump’s main bearing housing. With its three long sensory fingers pressed flat against the hot iron casing beside the bearing, the warden is *listening* to the grease inside. It feels the microscopic chatter of steel rollers beginning to run dry, hears the pitch of the bearing creep a quarter-tone sharp, and taps the valve open just long enough for pressurized lubricant from the central reservoir to bleed into the race before snapping it shut again.

Ask why it does this, and you have to follow the juvenile when it drops off the beam into the fern-mats and lopes back down the terraced spillways toward the condenser tower.

The tower is forty stories of roaring, sweating iron, vibrating like an organ pipe planted in the bedrock. Down around its concrete footings, where boiling mineral runoff spills into terraced cooling basins, a sept of roughly thirty wardens keeps its rookery. They do not build walls—walls trap stagnant air and block sonar—so their architecture is an intricate web of acoustic and thermal plumbing woven directly into the bones of the installation.

Using stripped haulage wire, braided kinetic-tree root, and slabs of acoustic-baffle vine kept alive by grafting their stems into nutrient-rich condensate drips, the sept has partitioned the lower three tiers of the tower into distinct sonic microclimates. There are nurseries slung directly beneath massive cast-iron return pipes, swaddled so thickly in foam-lined creepers that inside them the deafening roar of the tower drops to a womb-like, sub-audible throb, warm as blood and safe from the needle-beams of hunting flyers. Twenty feet higher, out on the windward girders, hang curing racks of stretched wire where butchered plates of marsh-grazer meat and clusters of sulfur-rich bladder-weed spin slowly in the dry, oily blast of an auxiliary exhaust vent, smoking and dehydrating in air too hot for rot-spores to settle.

At the center of the rookery, bolted to the main structural column, sits an inspection hatch whose raised pin-pattern was worn smooth ten thousand generations ago. The wardens have pried off its heavy cover and replaced it with a resonant drumhead of cured grazer-hide stretched over a rim of bent copper tubing. Every pipe, cable, and girder in a five-mile radius feeds vibrations down into the bedrock and up through that main column; by pressing its flared nasal-acoustic rosette lightly against the hide drum, an older, heavy-mantled matriarch can feel the heartbeat of the entire local industrial grid.

She knows that four miles east, the barge-sized pump-jack’s main bearing has quieted back down to its proper, buttery hum. If that bearing had seized, the pump would have tripped its mechanical overload breaker and gone silent. Within six days of silence, the bedrock tremors would cease; the blind subterranean worms would scatter off their migration path; the warm exhaust ducts would cool; the three-foot mats of heat-tubers would blacken and slough off into the mud; the crab-jawed scavengers that the sept traps by the dozen would abandon the seep. Worse, two weeks after a pump-jack dies, the central automated network registers the drop in line pressure and dispatches a heavy demolition-and-refit rig down the unseen valleys—a deafening, six-tracked juggernaut that rips everything living off the ironwork with high-pressure steam lances, unbolts the entire station, and leaves behind nothing but scoured limestone and the stench of ozone.

To keep their reefs alive, the wardens have become mechanics, ranchers, and saboteurs.

They cannot smelt iron—fire is practically impossible to sustain on a plateau where the air is forever supersaturated with warm mist and every surface beads with water—so their metallurgy is cold, patient, and parasitic. They harvest shed crawler-track pins, snapped cable strands, and corroded copper shims, working them in the natural hydraulic presses of the machinery itself. Walk past an ore-bucket pylon twenty minutes before a bucket is due, and you might hear a sharp, rhythmic scraping high up in the fog: a warden crouched on the cross-arm, carefully wedging a lump of scavenged bronze alloy onto the steel guide-rail, held in place with resinous plant-gum. When the multi-ton bucket screams past on its pulleys, its flanged wheels mash the bronze flat in a shower of unseen sparks, rolling it out into a thin, work-hardened blade that the warden retrieves from the wet ferns below once the cable stops singing.

With these blades, bone picks, and tuned Tuning-Forks—forked iron rods struck against stone to test the internal flaws of pipes or stun small pool-dwelling amphibians—they shape both machine and jungle. Around their home towers, they prune the acoustic flora with ruthless precision. False-echo dishes are snapped off along their foraging trails and replanted in dense, bewildering rings around rival territories or predator dens, turning a simple limestone gully into an acoustic hall of mirrors where an unfamiliar intruder hears solid stone cliffs inches from its nose while walking straight off a thirty-foot spillway.

Their language is terrifying to stand inside.

They have no vocal cords in the windpipe; breathing the thin, wet upland air while hauling themselves up forty stories of vertical iron requires an unobstructed bellows. Instead, they speak with their chest-plates, their mantles, and whatever they are standing on. For intimate talk—grooming a mate’s sensory-tapers, guiding a blind-drunk larva toward a warm condensate trickle—they flutter the edges of their nasal rosettes while clicking their horned talons softly together, spinning rapid, gossamer streams of high-frequency whisper that die out in the wet air within six feet, completely private.

For anything more serious, they couple their double-layered breastbones directly against a surface—a stone lip, a water-filled basin, a taut steel cable—and fire muscle spasms across ossified striker-ribs inside their chests.

When two wardens argue across a terraced marsh, you don’t merely hear it; it comes up through the soles of your boots as rapid, syncopated volleys of sub-bass *thud-rips* that make the standing water jump into shimmering standing waves, overlaid with rapid-fire harmonic chirps thrown sideways off their flared mantle-flaps. They do not use nouns the way creatures of vision do. A visual mind names a thing as a static noun and then attaches verbs to it: *the crawler moves through the reeds*. A warden’s utterance is a miniature, compressed acoustic model of an event’s resonance, density, and rhythm. To name the maintenance crawler, a warden strikes a hollow, metallic *clack-whirr* in its chest while fluttering three mantle-plates to mimic the scatter of millimeter-wave radar hitting wet leaves—a single half-second chord that simultaneously states the crawler’s weight, how worn its third left track-bogie is, which direction it is pushing the brush, and how long ago its grease was hot.

Watch what happens when an autonomous crawler actually rolls into a sept’s territory.

Hours before the six-tracked rig crests the rounded karst ridge, its low-frequency tread-rumble has come down the limestone strata and buzzed against the hide drum on the condenser tower. By the time the crawler’s long spring-steel whisker booms come whipping and scraping through the stands of rubbery kinetic-trunks, smelling of hot grease and wet iron, the marsh ahead of it has been rearranged.

Three wardens are moving parallel to the machine through the flooded depressions, invisible inside their mantles. As the crawler’s spinning radar horns sweep the brush, the wardens tilt their keratinized dorsal plates in coordinated waves. Millimeter-wave radar isn’t sound, yet centuries of trial and crushed ancestors have taught them that dense, wet bone and stolen sheets of copper mesh—woven straight into the harness-straps across their backs—bounce the machine’s invisible beams just like rock. Working together like three mirrors on hinges, two wardens project the radar-shadow of a massive, collapsed limestone boulder directly across the spillway leading to their tower’s tuber-gardens, while a third warden runs ahead of the rig’s whisker-booms, holding a peeled branch of dense ironwood to trip the spring-steel feelers in the exact sequence of a solid cliff-face.

The twenty-ton machine halts in the warm rain, gears whining inside its sealed belly as its blind logic reconciles map with obstacle. Its radar horns spin faster, buzzing faintly against the mist; its exhaust stack coughs a plume of oily steam into the thrashing membranes overhead. Slowly, ponderously, its left tracks reverse, its right tracks bite into the smoothed stone, and it pivots forty degrees north, grinding off down an empty spillway away from the nurseries.

Before its rear hull has even cleared the brush, two juveniles drop silently onto its warm rear deck from an overhanging hummock. Moving with brisk, monkey-like economy between the whipping radar cones, they unlatch a recessed tool-bin by counting its cast-iron notches, lift out a fresh coil of copper gasket-wire and two blocks of solid tallow-grease, click the heavy lid shut so the pin-switch doesn’t register an open hatch, and roll off into the ankle-deep water without raising a splash louder than the rain.

When the continental fronts drop—when the barometric pressure plunges so hard that every air-sac under a warden’s mantle swells tight as a boil, and the horizontal deluge turns the upper world into a solid, roaring wall of white noise—the plateau belongs entirely to them and the great herds.

High-frequency sonar dies completely in that hammering water. The flyers hang torpid and deaf under the warm cowlings, sometimes within inches of a resting warden’s hind foot, ignored because hunting is pointless when a million tons of meat are surging up through the flooded spillways below.

During a great storm, the sept leaves the upper latticework of the tower and goes down into the rushing water. They collapse their delicate nasal rosettes tight as fists to protect them from the stinging sheets of rain, flatten their acoustic mantles smooth against their flanks like sealskin, and navigate purely through the soles of their feet and the heavy bone of their chests. Wading shoulder-deep through tepid torrents that spill from basin to limestone basin, five or six hunters link up by trailing a braided wire line from one talon to the next, passing sharp, Morse-like tugs down the wire so they never lose formation in the deafening roar.

They move right into the jostling, panicked herds of giant plated grazers and multi-legged amphibians, sliding between tree-trunk legs in absolute darkness, feeling the panicked hammer of huge hearts through the water itself. When they single out an old bull bogged down in a stone bowl, they don’t waste strength fighting ten tons of wet shell; two wardens slap their suction-pad feet directly onto its slick carapace, scramble over its back in the blinding downpour, and drive tuned iron spikes straight into the acoustic-nerve clusters behind its ossified throat-plates, deafening and disorienting the beast in a single heartbeat so it turns away from the herd and blunders straight up the terraced steps toward the waiting butcher-crews beneath the tower.

And higher still—above the drowning basins, above the thrashing membrane-forests, forty stories up at the very peak of the condenser tower where the wind screams across wet iron at eighty miles an hour and the rain drives sideways like warm buckshot—you might find a single old adult clinging alone beside the main haulage-cable pulley.

Every twenty minutes, through the storm that has erased the rest of the universe, the braided cable tightens its hum from a low *C* to a screaming *F-sharp*. An ore-bucket looms out of nothingness, slams across the greased guide-rail in a shriek of wet steel and ozone, and vanishes west toward the distant, unseen rims of the massif, where the cable plunges over the edge into the crushing, toxic atmospheric oceans miles below.

Sometimes—not often, perhaps once in a generation, when a rookery grows too crowded or an old mechanic’s sensory-tapers have grown scarred and numb from too many burns on steam-pipes—a warden waits for that rising *F-sharp*, times the rush of displaced air before the flange hits the rail, and throws its long hooked talons upward into the dark.

There is a heavy, wet slap of suction pads finding iron lip; a rapid flutter of mantle-flaps tucking out of the wind; and then the bucket rolls out over the flooded valleys, swinging faintly under four hundred extra pounds of warm, listening weight, carrying no light at all toward whatever machines are waiting at the other end of the wire.
