You have to watch the chamber through four or five cycles of the pipe before you realize that one of the dark, kidney-shaped valve housings near the bottom is breathing.

Your eye misses her at first because everything else in the column is busy advertising the current—the kelp streaming, the foil-fish darting, the silver gas roaring toward the dome—while she belongs entirely to the iron. Only when the forty-second valve slams shut beneath the grates, and the sudden ten-second hush drops over the room, does a seam part along the side of what looked like graphitic sponge. A wide, fleshy operculum lifts, spills a slow curl of silt-free water, and settles tight again before the next *boom*.

She is nearly four feet long, heavy-skulled and broad across the forequarters, tapering behind into a thick, laterally compressed tail edged with a stiff scalloped fin. Whatever river-bottom clingfish or stone-sucker first rode the priming water up into the spire’s plumbing centuries ago is barely recognizable in her now. Isolation, relentless warmth, and dissolved metal have remade the lineage for this single vertical room. Her skin is scaleless and dense, textured like wet slate across the back to match the eroded cast iron, breaking along her flanks into irregular blotches of dull brass and powdery verdigris-green that mimic the corroded manifolds. 

She has no swim bladder. In a narrow, forty-foot chimney where pressure shifts wildly and rising gas tears at anything buoyant, an internal pocket of air would be a liability. Instead, she is ballasted like a piece of machine-shop scrap. Her skeleton is dense, mineralized with trace iron filtered from the water, and the broad, rounded crushing plates inside her mouth are blackened with magnetite. Left in open water during the ten-second lull, she sinks like a dropped wrench.

To stay aloft, she wears the room’s geometry on her underside. Her pectoral and pelvic fins have fused into a single, oval suction disc nearly a foot across—pale, tough, and deeply grooved in concentric rings like a vulcanized rubber gasket. With the slightest flex of the muscle at the center of that pad, she can lock herself onto vertical iron, curved brass, or bowed glass, holding fast while the thirty-second updraft rages past her flanks without shifting her a millimeter.

When she decides to climb, she does not swim against the tank; she uses the clock. 

Two seconds before the solenoid fires—warned by some preliminary click or hydraulic gathering deep behind the inner bulkhead that precedes the main *boom*—she peels the front lip of her ventral pad off the iron. The silver plume erupts from the bronze throat below. As the roaring column of bubbles hits her, she spreads the stiff, wide skirts of her cheek-flaps and tail fin, angles her flat head upward, and lets the inverted waterfall haul all sixty heavy pounds of her straight toward the ceiling. She rides the center of the boil effortlessly, turning slowly in the emerald ribbons of kelp like a barge on a lift. At the exact height she wants—twenty feet up by the fern-draped ladder rungs, or thirty-five feet up near the copper-mossed piston rods—she banks sideways out of the warm core, slips into the cold downward current along the glass, and slaps her pale gasket-belly against the wall with a dull, wet *thud* just as the gas cuts off.

Then, in the ten-second quiet, she feeds.

She is mostly a grazer, which is the only reason an animal of her bulk can exist in thirty-three thousand gallons of sealed water. Fueled by blinding high-altitude sunlight, engine warmth, and whatever carbon-rich exhaust bleeds through that bottom pipe, the ribbon-kelp grows inches a day; without her, it would have choked the column into a stagnant mat decades ago. Moving in short, hunching hitches of her suction pad, she works her way along the dead pressure-gauge collars, shearing thick kelp blades cleanly near the holdfasts with the iron-rimmed plates of her jaws. Loose scraps drift away from the corners of her mouth, swirling downward in the hush while the thumb-sized foil-fish flash in to steal the crumbs. Now and then, as if wanting grit or salt, she slides over to the leaded panes, hooks her hard upper lip under one of the hexagonal bolt-mimic snails, pops it off the glass, and crunches it whole—shell and all—with a heavy, grinding *scritch* that carries clearly across the tank between engine strokes.

For a long time, she appears to be completely alone of her kind. A twelve-by-forty-foot pocket cannot feed a herd of four-foot grazers. 

Then you look closer at the chartreuse fern tiers midway up the rusted ladder, and you spot the second one—and higher still, tucked beneath a sweating piston rod, the third. 

They are less than ten inches long: slender, blotched bright chartreuse and copper-red so they vanish against the moss and fern fronds, their heads narrower, their movements quick and skittish. For years—sometimes for decades—they do not grow at all. Every forty seconds, the closed loop of the current washes the old female’s chemical signature into every corner of the room, steeping the water in a quiet hormonal brake that keeps the others locked in a sleek, low-appetite juvenile state. Higher still, darting right inside the shimmering curtain of hydrofoil fish along the glass, are half a dozen larval fry no bigger than sewing needles, silvery and transparent, eating the same microscopic shred-biofilm as the fish. 

The whole population is stacked vertically like spare parts on a shelf, waiting. Only when the great slate-backed matriarch finally dies and drops onto the floor grates—to be dismantled within days by the snapping white-and-rust crustaceans and dissolved back into the sump—will the chemical lock lift from the water. Within months, the largest of the ten-inch juveniles in the ferns will darken, broaden across the skull, mineralize its jaw-plates, and sink down to claim the kidney-shaped iron boulders near the warm pipe, while one of the needle-thin fry peels out of the fish school to take the empty rung on the ladder.

Nothing in their bodies runs on an independent rhythm anymore; the spire’s core has taken over the work of keeping time. When the matriarch rests, clamped vertically to the back bulkhead where the heat through the iron is fiercest, her gill covers do not pump. She simply faces downward into the rising blast, props her wide mouth slightly ajar, and lets the thirty-second current ram warm, saturated water straight over her gill arches. When the plume stops, her opercula clamp shut and her heart drops to three slow, heavy beats across the ten-second pause, conserving effort until the pipe breathes for her again.

Even their courtship and territorial squabbles belong to that ten-second window. Against the bass *thrum-clack* of the core engines and the high, continuous hiss of steam beneath the grates, soft sounds are useless. When one of the smaller, fern-dwelling subordinates drifts too low during a pause, the matriarch does not chase it. She lifts her heavy chin a fraction of an inch and strikes the mineralized plate of her lower jaw sharply against an eroded brass intake manifold. 

*Tink. Tink-tink.*

The clear, bell-like metallic rap cuts straight through the middle of the machine noise, travelling instantaneously through forty feet of water. Up in the chartreuse leaves, the trespasser flinches, hitches backward around a ladder rung, and stays put.

Only once or twice a day, when the unseen machinery inside the spire hits a heavy load and the inner bulkhead grows uncomfortably hot to the touch, does the matriarch leave the metal altogether. 

Riding an updraft halfway up the column, she swings across the open, sunlit middle of the room and plants herself squarely on the center of the exterior glass, twenty feet above the grates, pressing her broad pale underside flat against the chilled four-inch pane to bleed the excess heat out of her blood. 

Seen from the front like that, spread-eagled against the greenish leaded glass with the blinding glare of the upper troposphere pouring around her silhouette, she looks briefly grotesque and vulnerable: a flat, pale-bellied, pulsing flange of muscle and gill-slit pinned against the void. Her eyes—set high on her skull beneath heavy, bronze-iridescent brow ridges grown to shield her retinas from the brutal high-altitude light—turn outward. Their pupils contract to horizontal pinpricks, twin slivers of dark iron looking straight out over miles of motionless white cloud-tops where nothing rises, nothing falls, and no current ever turns back on itself. 

The snails graze in slow hexagons around the rim of her suction pad. Behind her, emerald ribbons drift lazily downward through forty feet of golden water, and flakes of rust snow toward the floor. For ten full seconds she hangs suspended between the roaring green room and the empty blue curve of the world—cooling her belly against the sky—until the deep valve slams open beneath the grates, the bronze pipe coughs silver, and the whole narrow sea surges upward behind her back.
