You do not see the architects of the bowl because you are looking during the Screaming. 

At high sun, the glare is not merely bright; to anything smaller than a dog, the parabolic focus of the gypsum walls makes the open plazas cook like the floor of a kiln, and the updraft tears upward with enough force to pluck an unanchored body straight out of the pit and fling it across the salt flats like chaff. Only the armored gliders and the blind, flattened beetles they hunt dare the open noon—scavengers running quick, desperate errands across the anvil while the hammer is raised.

The builders are three inches behind the selenite windows. They are packed into the dark, insulated marrow of the clay-and-gypsum walls, waiting, working, and listening with their feet.

To understand them, you first have to understand why a town with no river and no rain would build ten thousand bells and leave its stone axles ungreased. 

Out on the salt-crust, nothing moves unless the wind pushes it, and when the wind dies, everything dies. Stillness is starvation. Above ground, the air holds microscopic drift: blown spores from distant mountain ranges, salt-flocculate, flakes of dead insect-shell from miles away, sulfur dust, and static electricity. The bronze fan-leaves and silver-green vines eat this drift, combing it out of the thermal column. 

But in dry air, dust clings. Left alone, the glassy hooks of the vines would clog with salt-flour in an afternoon; the lantern-plants would choke on gypsum silt and suffocate. They need to be shaken. Continuously, violently, down to the cellular level, the entire garden must be beaten like a rug so the waste grit drops to the pit floor and the static-charged hooks stay clean to catch airborne spores and sulfur. And the gypsum floor itself must be pulverized and hauled back up to make mortar, and the brittle mineral-seeds of the copper fans must be shattered out of their husks, and the sulfur must be milled with mica into dry paste.

The roar is not a byproduct of the city. The roar *is* the city’s metabolism. Sound, down here, is an industrial solvent and a threshing flail. 

If you wait on the rim until late afternoon, when the brassy sky bruises toward violet and the sun slides low enough that a crescent of shadow finally spills over the western lip, the thermal column falters. 

The transition is startling. Within five minutes, the pistol-snapping sails go slack and begin to mutter. The screaming stone axles drop down the scale—a high shriek sliding into a groan, then a dry, rhythmic *creak-creak*, and finally a slow, ticking halt. One by one, the clay bells give up their frenzy and hang trembling, chiming only in stray, tired gusts. In the sudden, ringing hush that follows—a silence so abrupt your ears hiss with phantom static—the selenite panes begin to slide open from the inside.

First come the feelers, testing the drop in pressure: long, stiff filaments thin as spun glass, twitching in the warm twilight. Then the builders emerge onto the worn gypsum sills.

At a glance, you might mistake one for a clockwork toy cobbled together from locust legs, slate shingles, and scraps of dried parchment, until you see the deliberate, heavy-shouldered way it leans over a parapet to inspect a frayed rawhide lashing. 

They stand roughly the height of a human hand from wrist to middle fingertip, though they rarely stand upright. Their bodies are low, wide, and segmented in overlapping plates of pale, calcified horn that matches the buff-white of the anhydrite walls so closely they vanish the moment they stop moving. They have six limbs, arranged not like an insect’s, out to the sides, but tucked beneath a broad, keeled thorax like a terrier’s or a chameleon’s, giving them a sudden, ratcheting gait—three steps blur-fast, pause, three steps, pause. The rear four legs end in split, hooked pads dusted with natural rosin for clinging upside down to the undersides of the reed viaducts. The front pair are heavy, asymmetrical tools: one typically broad and rasp-surfaced like a mason’s float, used for troweling dry clay and crushing mineral cakes; the other split into three delicate, triple-jointed fingers nimble enough to tie knots in split sinew no thicker than a eyelash.

Like the yellow gliders, they have no wet mouths, no noses, no soft, evaporative skin to lose precious moisture to the furnace air. Their heads are flat, wedge-shaped shields of horn set with four eyes that look like drops of dark amber—natural polarized lenses evolved to stare straight into chalk-glare without going blind. 

Inside the dim chambers behind the walls, where they spend the boiling daylight hours, it is not quiet. While the sun rages outside, the hollow’s masonry acts as a vast sounding board, and the creatures spend the day pressed belly-down against tuned slabs of mica-schist built into their floors. Every wind-wheel, every lifting-sail, every suspension cable in the settlement has its own distinct pitch. Through the sensitive, drum-head membranes stretched across their bellies and the soles of their feet, a builder lying in the dark five tiers down can feel the entire city as a chord. 

If a frayed sinew snaps on the third viaduct from the bottom, a sharp flat drops out of the chord. If a basket-chute jams with gypsum grit, a wooden ratchet stops clicking, leaving a ragged hole in the rhythm. They lie in neat, dry rows in their cool galleries during the Screaming, feeling the city work, tapping pale, dusty forelimbs against the floorboards to argue with one another about which axle is wearing thin, which bell-line needs re-stringing before tomorrow’s noon, and where the yellow gliders have nested too thickly and muffled the copper chimes.

Now, with the sun gone and the residual heat of the bowl rising only as a gentle, velvet draft, thousands of them pour out onto the stepped plazas and spider-thin bridges. 

There is no chatter. Even in the quiet of dusk, they have no voices for air. Instead, the twilight fills with a dry, intricate *ticking*—thousands of mason-rasps and horn-tipped feet striking the stone streets in sharp, coded bursts. Step onto a plaza, tap twice; the vibration travels forty feet along a tensioned reed cable and tells the crew on the far tower to slacken the counterweight. 

Watch a team of four working on one of the great wooden paddle-wheels. They move with the terse, unhurried economy of old sailors who know a gale will return at dawn whether they are ready or not. Two hang effortlessly by their rear hooks from the wooden spokes, scraping blackened crust off the stone axle before rubbing it with a lump of grey graphite hauled up from a deep seam at the bottom of the pit. A third sits hunched on the gypsum sill, chewing a strip of scavenged glider-skin between dry, scissor-like mandibles—not to eat it, for it has no throat for meat, only a grinding gizzard—masticating the dry hide with a waxy secretion from its jaw-glands until the leather is supple enough to splice a worn belt. The fourth stands on the parapet, sweeping a long-handled brush of silica-bristles across a cluster of bronze fan-leaves, harvesting the fine, electrostatically trapped cake of yellow sulfur-dust and grey spores into a woven reed pannier.

This grey-yellow powder is their bread and water both. Back inside the granary-cones, they mix it with powdered selenite and cultivate pale, felt-like mats of xerophilic fungus that need no liquid water at all, pulling every molecule of hydrogen they require out of their own metabolic breakdown of carbohydrates, just as a flour-beetle lives its whole life in a barrel of bone-dry meal without ever seeing a drop of dew. From that dry felt, the builders get the trace moisture that keeps their thick, amber-colored blood—heavy as tree-sap—moving sluggishly through their shells.

Because nothing rots here, nothing is ever truly thrown away, and the dead do not leave the city. 

If you look closely at the finest architecture on the upper tiers—the tiny, cantilevered wind-vanes that steer the morning drafts, or the intricate escapement gears of the dust-hoists—you will notice that the pale material isn't wood or stone. When a builder grows old, its joints calcify further with every molt-less year, taking on silica from its diet until its movements slow from a blur to a stiff, creaking march. Eventually, one afternoon during the Screaming, an elder simply walks into one of the inner mortar-chambers, hooks its four rear feet into a prepared niche in a cracked wall or a stressed archway, locks its leg-ratchets, and stills. Its sap-blood dries to resin in forty-eight hours. Within a month, the masonry-vines thread through the gaps in its thorax, stitching shell to brick. Half the load-bearing lintels over the oldest doorways are ancestors, still holding up the roof, their polarized amber eyes clouded to white pearls, their broad mason-claws locked together in permanent arches.

Around midnight, the bowl cools completely. Cold air from the surrounding salt plain spills over the rim, heavy and dense, pooling at the bottom of the pit and reversing the draft for a few still hours. 

Down on the pit floor—a place that at noon was a blinding, fifty-degree cauldron—crews descend the vertical guidelines like tiny, pale spiders. Down here lies the day’s harvest of wreckage: shattered beetle-carapaces dropped by the gliders, fallen flakes of gypsum knocked loose by the afternoon’s vibrations, blown sand from the outer flats, and the occasional dead glider itself, broken by a treacherous crosswind. 

This is the hour of high craft. By the faint, cold starlight amplified slightly by the white walls, circles of builders gather in the central dust-pan to hammer copper. Where the copper came from originally—some ancient, weathered vein long since mined down to a smooth dimple beneath the anhydrite, perhaps—is impossible to tell; they have clearly been reforging the same few hundred pounds of metal for centuries. Five or six of them stand around a flat flint anvil, swinging tiny stone mauls attached to flexible reed shafts, striking cold-worked copper disks in a blindingly fast, polyrhythmic tattoo—*tink-tink-tink-tink-tak*—retuning a dented chime until, when flicked with a claw, it rings at the exact frequency needed to shake a forty-foot curtain of silver vine clean of salt.

Look up from that midnight floor, and you see the bridges overhead crowded with still silhouettes. Those who aren't repairing the rigging or foraging the bottom simply sit along the suspension cables in the cool dark, facing outward toward the center, grooming the grit from one another’s eye-lenses with delicate तीन-fingered hands. Sometimes two will hook feet side-by-side on a slack rawhide line and pluck it gently, sending slow, bass thrummings back and forth between their bodies—quiet conversation after a twelve-hour shift in a boilermaker’s shop.

Occasionally, a scouting party climbs all the way to the lip of the rim. 

Three or four of them will creep ten inches out onto the flat salt-crust, halting instantly under the terrifying, dome-less enormity of the open plain. Out here, there are no walls to bounce footsteps back to them; when they tap the salt, the vibration vanishes into infinity, unanswered. The silence of the flats to creatures born inside an acoustic furnace must feel like vacuum, like falling upward into black sky. They only stay long enough to gather crusty nodules of pure halite or scavenge a wind-blown feather from some bird that died fifty miles away, dragging the prize backward like a timber beam toward the lip.

An hour before dawn, the eastern horizon pales from black to iron-grey, then to dull brass. 

A single sharp *clack* echoes from a watch-tower near the rim—a sentry tripping a wooden drop-weight against a slate slab. Instantly, the plazas empty. Tools are lashed down inside stone lockers; panniers of spore-flour are dragged indoors; the great parchment sails are set on their ratchets and cocked to catch the first thermal breath. Thousands of pale, dusty bodies slip backward through narrow doorways. Selenite shutters slide into their stone grooves and are wedged tight from within with wooden pins.

For twenty minutes, the city stands utterly vacant in the strengthening light, poised like a music box with the lid lifted.

Then the rim of the sun clears the salt horizon. A lance of white-gold light strikes the western anhydrite wall, bounces down to the chalk floor at the bottom of the pit, and begins to bake the stone. A bubble of hot air swells at the base of the bowl, tears loose, and rushes upward.

A single bronze leaf shivers and *ticks* against its neighbor. High on a reed bridge, a copper disk swings an inch and kisses another—*ting*. A wooden paddle-wheel shudders, overcomes its dry inertia, and makes one complaining turn. Then another. A lantern-plant pops open with a pistol *crack*. Within ten minutes, the rising column catches its throat, the sails snap taut, ten thousand leaves burst into tinny applause, the stone axles begin to shriek, and behind six inches of warm gypsum and shimmering window-stone, pressed flat against their humming mica floors, the city closes its amber eyes and listens to itself come back to life.
