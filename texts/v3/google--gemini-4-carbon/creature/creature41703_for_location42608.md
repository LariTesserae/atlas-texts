To find what else lives here—what owns the space between the floor and the ceiling—you have to watch the crickets. 

When the pneumatic relief valve vents its wet sigh, the crickets on the nearest pier step three inches to the left in a single, rippling sheet, and then stop. That is mechanical; they know the steam. You have to look instead at Pier Four, eighty feet away from the sump, where there is no valve. Every few hours, a slow, vertical parting opens in that brown fur of antennae. The crickets do not leap in panic—leaping in forty-five-degree air costs too much oxygen, and landing on the floor means being dismantled by beetles before they can right themselves. Instead, they walk sideways, unhurriedly, clearing a lane ten inches wide from the top of the pier down toward the ankle-deep water. They fold their six-inch feelers flat back along their wings like laid-back ears as something comes down the concrete.

At first it looks only like a weeping seam in the formwork, a long ribbon of clear, heavy grease sliding down the pale gray pillar. It catches the dull amber of the sodium lamp not as a solid shape, but as a slight lensing of the concrete grain beneath it, the chalked blue `4-NORTH` of the surveyor’s mark bending and swelling for a moment before flattening out again.

They came in, almost certainly, with the bentonite drilling mud, or else rode down in the damp straw packing around the iron pipe-cradles shipped up from some warm port, or dropped out of a breached storm-main fifty feet overhead where an old subterranean canal was clipped during blasting. Whatever they were in the dark dirt above—some blind, tropical flatworm from a botanical conservatory’s root-ball, an escaped aquarium eel, a pale caecilian washed down a sink—the vault got hold of them before the concrete had even finished curing.

In forty-five degrees, warm-blooded things fail quickly if they grow large. Even the ceiling bats are stunted, skinny, hot-eared little things that roost slightly apart from one another so they do not cook in their own shared body heat, Panting continuously with tiny pink tongues. To get big down here, an animal cannot make its own fire. It has to let the room be its blood.

There are seven of them in the chamber now, though they rarely cross paths, each holding loosely to a territory defined by two iron pipe-cradles and a lamp cone. The municipal technicians who came down eighteen months ago to check the torque on the flange bolts never saw one, because when the bulkhead wheel dogs are spun from the outside, the change in barometric pressure hits the vault instantaneously, a subtle pop in the mist, and anything clinging to the concrete simply lets go and drops silently into the cast-iron trench drains beneath the grates.

Left alone, the one on Pier Four takes twenty minutes to descend thirty feet. 

It is roughly the length of a man’s forearm and wrist, though flattened like a thick leather strop, wide across the middle and tapering at both ends so it is hard to tell head from tail until the front third lifts clear of the stone to taste the thermal draft. It has no scales and no true shell, only a smooth, muscular mantle of translucent, tallow-pale skin through which its insides show in muted, murky colors: a long, straight dark gut packed with black beetle-shell and mottled brown cricket legs; two faint, pulsing lateral vessels the color of watered wine; and near the blunt, shovel-shaped head, a cluster of pale yellow fat-bodies arranged like tiny seeds. 

Beneath its belly runs a single broad, rippled sole—or rather, what used to be limbs and ribs have been tucked entirely inside a wide, sucker-like flange of pale muscle coated in heavy, viscous mucus. That slime is the whole secret of its survival. Ordinary sweat or frog-slime thins out and runs off in this wet heat, or else gets stripped away by the alkaline dust still leaching out of the fresh shotcrete. What this creature secretes is thick as axle-grease, slightly iridescent where it smears over the broom-finished concrete, full of stolen oils licked from the iridescent films on the floor puddles. It leaves a faint, glossy ribbon down the pier that repels condensation; for an hour after it passes, water beads up along the edges of its trail instead of sheeting across it.

Halfway down the pier, it reaches the caged sodium-vapor lamp.

Here the concrete is hot to the touch, green and slick with the little oasis of *lampenflora*. The pale ferns tremble in the rising draft off the glass. The creature eases its blunt front end into the amber glare. It has eyes, though they have sunk deep beneath thick pads of clear, protective gristle to keep the lime-water out—two tiny, pinhead specks of dull plum-black that register nothing sharper than *lamp* and *not-lamp*. Around its wide, lipless mouth sits a fringe of short, fleshy barbels, ten or twelve of them, quivering continuously in synchronization with the six-second *chug-thump* of the rotary pumps at the far end of the hall. 

It does not hunt the crickets by chasing them. Nothing down here wastes energy on speed except the beetles, and the beetles burn out and die in weeks. Instead, the creature slides right up against the iron cage of the lamp, settles its heavy, flat length straight across the wet cushion of moss, and stops. Within two minutes, its skin—warmed to the exact temperature of the damp concrete—loses even its tallow sheen. Dust of dead midges settles onto its mucus coat. Small pale moths blunder off the lamp cage and stick to its back; it does not bother with them. Over twenty minutes, the lane in the cave crickets slowly closes back in. Their six-inch antennae sweep forward, test the oily lump sitting across the moss, find it unmoving and the exact temperature of the wall, and step right onto it to reach the damp cyanobacteria they graze on.

Only the barbels around the wide slit of its mouth move, curling inward slightly. When a cricket’s hind leg—thick as a wooden matchstick, spiky with chitin—steps directly in front of the blunt snout, there is no lunge. There is a wet, pneumatic *slap*, quieter than a water-drop hitting a pipe, as the creature’s throat balloons inward, dropping pressure inside its mouth cavity the way a sump-valve pulls slurry. A cupful of thick, hot air and two whole crickets vanish together. 

The throat ripples twice. Through the translucent skin behind the head, you can dimly see four blunt, calcified plates inside its gullet—grown heavy and chalk-white from all the dissolved calcium carbonate in the seepage—grinding sideways against one another with a faint, submerged *crunch* that never escapes its body. Antennae wave wildly from the corner of its slit-mouth for five seconds, slick with oil, and are drawn in like wet thread. The surrounding crickets shift half an inch away, wave their feelers at the disturbance, and then settle back down.

That is the juvenile way of feeding, on the piers. The oldest one in the chamber no longer climbs the concrete at all.

It lives forty feet up, in the dark space between the two eight-foot mains, down inside the curved steel saddle where the upper pipe rests on its heavy iron cradle. Up there, the aluminum jacketing was slightly crushed during rigging, leaving a four-inch gap where the fiberglass insulation beneath is exposed to the mist, sodden and hot as a poultice, hovering near fifty-two degrees. No geckos go near that gap; it would cook the proteins in their eyes. 

The thing wedged into that saddle has grown to match the ironwork. Stretched out along the underside of the pipe seam—which it does only when the big mains drop two degrees during the city’s three-a.m. low-demand trough—it is nearly five feet long and wide as a shovel blade, heavy-bodied and sag-bellied, its skin no longer translucent but thickened into a tough, leathery rind stained rust-orange and mineral-gray by iron oxide and lime drip. Along its back, where steady condensation from an overhead cable tray has struck the exact same spot behind its head for fourteen months, a thin, crusty saddle of actual flowstone has precipitated onto its hide—a rough, ridged cap of dirty white calcium carbonate bonded right into the dead outer layers of its cuticle, cracking slightly along hinged seams whenever it turns its head. It carries the geology of the room on its neck.

It has no need for the crickets. Ten inches away from its rust-stained snout begins the neat, territorial grid of the house geckos clinging upside-down to the riveted aluminum. And twenty feet above it, the ceiling bats drop down out of their concrete expansion-recesses into the open air-space, stalling for a fraction of a second in the heavy mist before catching their glide toward the ventilation shaft. 

Every few nights, a bat misjudges the thick, broth-heavy air, or comes back down the shaft heavy with city rainwater on its fur, or collides with another in the amber murk, and spirals down onto the broad aluminum curve of the upper main. And every hour or two, a gecko, grown fat and sluggish on moths, loses grip on a slick patch of condensation over a rivet head.

The old one in the iron cradle does not move for hours on end. Its flat head hangs four inches out over the edge of the steel saddle, upside down, swaying almost imperceptibly in the slow convective draft rising off the flange. Its breath—for it has a single, simple lung running half the length of its body like an air-bladder—goes in and out once every three minutes, a long, slow inflation that matches the bleed-cycle of the distant pneumatic relief valve so closely that if you stood on the galvanized catwalk six feet away, looking straight at it through the amber fog, you would swear it was only a sagging canvas pipe-wrap swollen with trapped steam, crusted with white lime, dripping warm water from its chin *plink, plink, plink* onto the grating below.

Down on the floor, in the wide, bath-warm puddles where the machine oil makes rainbow swirls under the lamps, the next generation is already waiting for more room.

If you crouched beside the cast-iron lip of the trench drain—ignoring the hot rain off the catwalk soaking through your shirt in ten seconds, ignoring the carabid beetles running fearlessly over the toes of your boots—and shone a bright white flashlight straight down through the surface film of mosquito larvae, the tiny greenhouse frogs would freeze on the iron rim. The mosquito wrigglers would dive in a single black convulsion. And on the rough broom-marks of the submerged concrete beneath them, you would see dozens of pale, coin-sized rings cemented flat to the floor.

They look exactly like stray gobs of clear silicone sealant dropped by the pipe-fitters, each ring enclosing twenty or thirty tiny amber spheres no bigger than mustard seeds. Around them, grazing steadily through the warm silt of pulverized insect wings and basalt dust that washes toward the drain, glide the hatchlings: hundreds of flat, thread-thin slivers, half an inch long and completely transparent save for two microscopic black eyespots and a hairline streak of dark gut. Even at that size, they move in slow, deliberate arcs, avoiding the frogs by tasting the bitter alkaloids in frog-skin through the warm water an inch away, hugging the raw, unweathered ridges of the concrete.

They are waiting for the seals on the secondary valves to wear a little thinner, for the lime smears to thicken into ledges, for two or three of the caged sodium bulbs to burn out and expand the cool brown margins between the piers. Every six seconds, the rotary pump at the far end of the nave goes *chug-thump*, sending its small, faithful tremor through the basalt walls, through the water film, and through a thousand tiny, belly-flat bodies pressed tight against the stone, digesting the snowfall in the dark.
