Before you ever see one, you feel the weight shift in the floor.

Walking a minor span—say, a three-strand braid of supple fig-root, slick with wet moss and sagging gently across a half-mile pocket of fog—you learn quickly to trust the rhythm under your heels. Every living bridge has a baseline tremble: the deep, slow throb of the sap-tide, the faint twitch of a herd of knuckle-walkers cropping shoots two junctions away, the gentle tug of an off-kilter gravity pocket pulling sideways at the fringe-roots. You stop noticing it, just as a sailor stops noticing the roll of a hull.

Then the weave goes *quiet* beneath your arches. Not still—nothing here is ever still—but damped. Counter-balanced. Someone a hundred yards behind you in the pewter mist is matching their stride to the natural harmonic of the span, stepping down only as the fibers rebound, erasing their own wake so cleanly that the wet rills trickling down the bark don’t even jump their channels.

When they finally loom out of the drizzle, they do not look like masters of a continent. They look like an adaptation to dampness and thin oxygen that happened to keep its hands.

They are tall, though you rarely see them standing upright; their posture is a loose, tireless crouch that keeps their center of mass close to whatever surface currently claims to be *down*. Their chests are immense—deep, keeled barrels built to haul mouthfuls of high, impoverished air into oversized lungs—tapering down to narrow waists and disproportionately long, heavy forearms. Their skin has the matte, water-shedding texture of wet suede, colored in bruised plums, drowned olives, and pale lichen-grays, stained darker at the palms, knees, and insteps into permanent, mahogany-brown pads by centuries—no, merely lifetimes—of tannin and raw sap. Hair is liability in a place where wild creepers snare and graft to anything fibrous and stationary; they have almost none, only a fine, seal-like nap along the shoulders and spine that channels condensation cleanly down the small of the back to drip off the heel.

Look at their feet, and you understand why the center of every causeway is polished to satin while the edges remain rough. The big toe is set wide and thumb-thick, opposing the outer four, which are webbed halfway to the first knuckle and lined beneath with soft, finely ridged friction-skin. They don’t walk *on* the lattice; they hold it with every step, tasting the tension of the twill through their soles.

And they have no flint. If the ancient builders chipped stone to shape the primary arches, the stone was dropped into the ten-mile gulfs ten thousand generations ago, or else it stays back in whatever century the first spans were thrown from. What they carry instead is tucked into belt-loops of cured bladder-skin or braided directly into the loose folds of peeled-bark wraps slung across their ribs: split ribs of the giant gliders, scraped razor-thin and hardened over glowing punk-wood; curved yellow incisors pried from the jaws of dead knuckle-walkers, still hafted in their own jawbones for leverage; long needles made from the calcified thorns of hoop-rattan; and little stoppered gourds of concentrated herbivore spit, fermented with crushed scale-insects until it turns into a thick, blistering paste that makes old heartwood weep and soften like boiled turnip.

They are quiet in the way everything here is quiet. With lungs that large, they *could* bellow, though thin air steals the carry of a shout; instead, they have let speech drop out of the air altogether and put it into the wood. 

Watch three of them resting inside one of the hollow burl-pavilions while a squall dumps warm sheets of rain through the void outside. None of them looks at the others. One sits with her spine pressed flat against a structural rib, eyes half-shut against the pewter gloom, tapping the side of a calloused heel against an exposed tendon of creeper—*thup, thup-thup, slide*. Across the concave floor, an older man with milky cataracts rests two fingers lightly against the rim of a weeping water-basin and answers by flexing his toes into the tabby-weave beneath him, sending a tight, silent shiver across the floorboards. When they must communicate face-to-face in the open, away from tight fibers, they speak in wet, breathy clicks of the tongue against the palate and low, chest-resonating hums felt more in the sternum than the ear, coupled with quick, shorthand flicks of their long, flat-nailed fingers. To an outsider, a gathering of twenty of them sounds like nothing more than heavy breathing and rain hitting broad leaves.

Their thumbnails deserve their own study. On both hands, the nail of the thumb and the nail of the forefinger grow thick as horn, curved slightly inward like a hawk’s bill and deliberately notched along one edge. From infancy, they rub these nails with astringent gall-wasps and amber gum until the keratin cures iron-hard. Even in sleep, their hands twitch, the notched thumb-edge seeking a frayed fiber to strip, twist, and tuck.

They are not the architects of the continent—or if they are, they have forgotten that architecture was ever an act with a beginning and an end. To them, the lattice is simply what matter does, and they are part of its immune system.

For hours at a stretch, a small band of them—rarely more than eight or ten, bound less by blood than by shared rhythm on the lines—will hang upside down beneath a primary rib, suspended in harnesses of living liana that they splice together in thirty seconds and sever when they move on. Beneath the bridge, gravity is fickle; drop a bone awl from the underside of an old knuckle-graft, and it may fall *up* into the weave, or drift sideways fifty yards and hang spinning in a veil of mist. They work in that vertigo without seeming to notice it exists. A child of six, naked save for a smear of snail-slime rubbed onto the chest to ward off biting midges, will step off the edge of a thirty-foot causeway, drop ten feet through empty air until the local pull of the bundle catches him, flip neatly, and land four-handed against the vertical flank of the arch like a pale spider, crawling down into the shadowed clefts to dig out rot-fungus with a sharpened stick.

What they eat is what the maintenance yields. They harvest the loaf-sized, translucent snails from the damp undersides, slicing them into rubbery strips that cure in the warm updrafts; they bleed the primary ribs during the high sap-tide, catching the warm, faintly sweet, mineral-heavy liquor in folded leaf-cones before sealing the tap-hole with a graft of fresh moss; they set long, slack lines of sticky bladder-cilia across the flight paths of the almond-colored gliders, taking only two or three from a flock of hundreds, breaking their necks with a silent twist before the membrane can thrash and shake the span. Fire exists, though it is precious and terrifying on a world made entirely of fuel. They never build open flames. Instead, inside the wettest, thickest burls—places where the bark weeps constantly and the wood is too waterlogged to catch—they keep fist-sized embers of dried glider-dung and pith smoldering inside double-walled censers of wet green rind, blowing them to a dull cherry glow just long enough to sear meat or temper a bone point, watching the thin blue smoke get dragged sideways out a knot-hole toward an invisible sinkhole in the air.

It is their relationship to the spatial knots that sets them furthest apart from anything born on stone.

They do not map the world by distance or compass; there are no stars behind the tarnished upper haze, and "north" means nothing when walking straight ahead for three hours brings you out above your own starting point looking down through a triangular mesh five miles wide. They map by *tension* and *lag*.

Stand beside an elder at the rim of one of the great rattan hoops—thirty yards of braided, thorny frame dripping slow beads of water into nothing, looking through onto greener light and sideways rain. You see a portal, an impossibility. She reaches out, lays her ear flat against the wet, scarred binding of the hoop’s outer ring, and listens to the sap-creak coming through from the other side.

She is checking the phase.

Because the geometry here is tied to the turgor of the wood, the hoops do not merely bridge space; they slip slightly in duration when the weave goes slack or pulls over-taut. The inhabitants know this in their knees and teeth. They have a gesture—a slow rolling of one fist around the other while exhaling sharply—that means *over-braided*, or *stale-sap*, used for sectors of the lattice where too many nested loops have pinched the air so tight that light pools thicker there and an afternoon takes a week to drain out. 

They avoid the tightest knots, mostly. Not out of superstition, though they leave offerings braided into the thorny rims—little woven cages holding a single luminous snail, or lockets of baby-teeth plaited into maidenhair fern—but out of practical wariness. Sometimes, at the height of the second sap-tide, when the whole world groans like a ship in ice and distant hubs snap suddenly close through the fog, you will see another band across a seam that has pulled narrow enough to spit across. 

Sometimes that other band waves back—two fingers tapped silently against the collarbone—and tosses over a gourd of fresh glider-fat or a bundle of rare, straight-grained reed-shoots that don't grow in your sector, trading across six inches of damp air that will be twelve miles wide by dusk.

And sometimes, looking across a seam or through a tight, weeping eyelet in an ancient three-way graft, they see walkers whose skin is darker and coarser, whose jaws are heavier, who carry chipped black flint lashed with raw sinew to fire-hardened shafts—figures crouched on younger, greener ribs where the scars haven't yet healed silver, furiously splitting shoots and cinching raw, violent knots into empty air.

When the current folk see *them* through a hoop, they do not signal. They step back carefully, matching their weight to the sway of the path so not a single tremor crosses the threshold. If the eyelet is small enough, two weavers will squat at its base, take up green willow-whips, smear them with fermented spit, and spend the next four cycles quietly lacing the aperture shut—tucking the raw ends into the weeping bark, drawing the blinders closed so the ancestors can finish building the floor without stumbling forward into the finished house.

Nor are these quiet, suede-skinned tenders the only minds—or near-minds—the lattice has bred out of the hush.

Spend enough cycles following a single major arterial rib out toward the frayed, growing margins where the primary arches taper into mile-long, swaying whips that lash lazily at the open clouds, and you encounter the *Bag-Drifters*, or what the weavers indicate with a puffed-cheek gesture and a fluttering drop of both hands. 

Out there, the human bands rarely go; the thin air grows thinner still where the great vascular towers stop transpiring warmth, and the sap-tide arrives only as a sluggish, irregular shudder. Yet you will find basketry hanging from the whip-ends that no two-thumbed hand ever plaited. 

The velvet-furred, serpentine hunters of the inner spans have larger cousins in the outer gulfs—creatures twenty feet from blunt, heat-pitted snout to prehensile tail, broad-ribbed and flattened so they can ribbon-glide from whip to whip across steep, broken gravity wells. They live in tangled, multi-generational knots of twelve or twenty individuals, coiled for warmth inside self-woven globes of living creeper. Having no hands, they weave with their jaws and loops of their own muscular bodies, bruising young vines until they turn pliable, dragging them around one another in messy, dense, fiercely strong chaotic snarls—bird’s-nest engineering rather than tabby or twill, yet watertight and cunningly balanced. 

Between the weavers and these great, silent coils exists an uneasy, ancient truce that borders on trade. When a span’s spatial tension slips badly—when a primary cable rots through in the middle of a four-mile void where no human dare climb without losing their breath or drifting sideways into a stagnant air-cell—the weavers will hang three bruised, pungent gourd-melons at the base of the damaged line and beat a slow, heavy cadence into the wood with a deadwood mallet. Within a cycle or two, a knot of the giant gliders will slither out from the mist, drawn by the vibration and the scent. While the humans wait fifty paces back, eyes averted, the serpents flow out along the snapping, perilous cable, wrapping their own thick bodies across the gap as temporary living splints while two or three of the smaller males drag fresh, sap-wet lianas across their backs and thrash them crudely around the break—all just to claim the gourds waiting at the root. Once the serpents retreat into the fog, the weavers creep out along the stabilized line to neat-tuck the sloppy lashings and graft them permanent.

Stranger still are the elders who never come down from the hubs.

Because there is no soil in which to bury the dead, and because dropping a body into the open gulfs risks having it caught in a neutral-gravity pocket—drifting for decades in the warm wet haze, bloated with algae and trailing pale roots of mist-mold, until a thermal blows it gently back onto a walkway—the people give their dying back to the load-bearing knuckles.

When a weaver’s chest grows too stiff to pull thin air, or their cataracts thicken until they can no longer read the grain with their thumbs, they walk—or are carried in a sling—to one of the colossal, barge-thick primary joints where three or four great arches fuse. Deep inside these bulbous woody hearts, there are warm, pitch-dark chambers smelling intensely of bruised sap, cinnamon-rot, and damp skin, where the hydraulic roar of the twice-daily tide is loud enough to drown your own heartbeat: a vast, rhythmic *whoosh-creak* of ten thousand tons of water being pumped uphill through xylem tubes thick as thighs.

Into a prepared cleft of split cambium, the old one curls, knees to chest. Their band-mates do not weep; tears are just water, and everything is already wet. Instead, they sing into the wood with their heels and palms for half a cycle, filling the dying person's bones with the steady, rocking vibration of their whole lineage, while lacing thin, hungry strangler-roots lightly across the ankles, wrists, and ribs.

The air in those inner chambers is narcotic with carbon dioxide and sweet, volatile tree-esters. Death comes as a warm, heavy sleep before thirst ever starts. Within ten cycles, the fast-growing scar tissue has rolled smoothly over skin and bone alike; within ten years, the calcium of femur and skull has been drunk completely dry by greedy root-hairs—the rarest, richest mineral treasure in a stoneless sky—and pumped upward to harden the thorny rims of distant spatial hoops.

Sometimes, passing through an ancient, barrel-thick junction miles from anywhere, a solitary traveler will pause on the slick, concave path, press a palm flat against a swollen knot in the wall that still faintly echoes the curl of a spine, and feel—underneath the vast, ship-groan surge of the rising sap—a peculiar, localized warmth, and a grain pattern that turns a wandering, lost vibration gently back toward safe footing. Whether the wood remembers what it ate, or the old weavers simply chose their final clefts where their skeletons would brace a weak turn in the splice, makes no difference at all to the feet passing overhead in the pewter dark.
