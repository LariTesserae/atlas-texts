Across the wide, cream-colored plain of the plaza, you see their shadows before you make out their faces. Under the unblinking noon glare of the overhead light-pipes, a human figure three hundred yards away casts a crisp, ink-black stencil against the pale root-plate—and if you watch a pair of them standing in conversation, you will notice they never stand quite still. Every minute or two, without breaking the cadence of their talk, both take two slow paces to the left. Two minutes later, two paces back. 

Outcomers call it the *basin-rock*, though to anyone raised down here it is simply manners. The floor beneath their boots is alive and hungry for light; stand planted in one spot for an hour while arguing over a blueprint, and your shadow will starve a faint, brittle dimple into the growing weave. Nothing in the city sits flat on the ground if it can be helped. Tool-chests, surveyor tripods, street-side rest benches, and vendor racks all perch on slender, four-foot spider-legs of polished brass, letting the radiant glare wash clean underneath them. 

Up close, the people moving along the boulevards look bundled yet streamlined, dressed for a library kept at thirty-four degrees. They wear long, close-tailored coats of grey felt woven from shed cinder-moth velvet and stitched through with fine filaments of red copper. Because an ungrounded body walking across half a mile of bone-dry plant lattice in near-zero humidity will quickly build up a thirty-thousand-volt charge, every boot-heel is pierced with a flexible brush of braided brass wire. As people walk, they make a soft, dry, rhythmic *scritch-tick, scritch-tick* whenever a heel sweeps across one of the iron grounding-rails inlaid into the street. Before two coworkers pass a wrench or clasp forearms in greeting, they instinctively tap the copper cuffs of their wrists together first; a tiny violet spark snaps between the metal threads, harmlessly equalizing them before glove touches glove. To shock someone on the ear or knuckle is an awkward, childish blunder, like stepping on a hem.

Out in the open avenues, nobody goes bare-mouthed. A single warm, wet human breath exhaled directly onto young *lignin-bone* tendrils acts like mild acid: the crisp ochre fibers drink the vapor, swell into grey, spongy blisters, lose their tensile strength, and give off a sharp, sour reek like scorched vinegar. Worse, in air this dry, an unprotected lung bleeds away a liter of precious body water in half a shift. 

Instead, everyone wears a *still-muzzle*—a neat, half-face mask of molded spore-wax and spun brass fitted snugly over the bridge of the nose and chin. Inside its perforated chamber sits a cartridge of toasted zeolite beads that traps ninety-eight percent of the moisture from every exhalation—warming the mask pleasantly against the brisk air—and releases just enough back on the intake to keep the throat from cracking. Built into the front of the muzzle is a dry mica speaking-reed. It strips the wet plosives out of speech and gives every voice in the valley a crisp, slightly papery woodwind resonance, like a cello played behind a screen. Above the masks, exposed cheekbones and foreheads gleam with a matte, ivory sheen of rendered spore-wax rubbed into the skin each morning against chapping, and eyes stay hidden behind side-shielded spectacles of smoked quartz to cut the upward bounce of glare off the pale streets.

Because voices carry so cleanly across the sound-drinking floors, nobody shouts. Shouting feels obscene here—an ugly, exhausting waste of breath-water. Across distances where even a clear voice thins out, the inhabitants speak with their hands and wrists, using an angular, rapid finger-cant originally devised by high-wire crane riggers. You can stand on a bridge over the central trench and watch twenty silent conversations happening at once across half a mile of bright, cold space: two foundry clerks walking parallel boulevards four hundred yards apart gossiping effortlessly with flicks of copper-ringed fingers while their masked mouths stay shut.

***

They do not think of themselves as miners, or even quite as builders. They are *draftsmen*, *pruners*, and *herders* of slow geometry.

Look up at an unfinished apartment tower or transit arch, and you will see the **wire-riggers** hanging in web-harnesses fifty yards out in empty air, slung between temporary bamboo-like spars. Architecture here has no masons, no mortar, no rivet-guns, and no sawdust. A rigger’s kit consists of heavy spools of bright copper guide-wire, glass-bulb electroscopes, and steel tuning forks. They string the thin metal threads through nothingness to sketch the ghost of next month’s room, tensioning each span until it hums at a precise pitch when struck, then clipping it into a low-voltage feed-line. Once charged, the wire calls to the husk. Over days and weeks, pale ochre shoots creep along the humming copper like frost crystallizing in slow motion, braiding tightly over one another until a hollow, load-bearing beam stands where empty air used to be. 

A good wire-rigger reads stress in a growing wall the way a sailor reads canvas. If a buttress is thickening too slowly on its shaded underside, they do not prop it up with timber; they angle a six-foot heliostat mirror on stilts at the edge of the plaza to bounce extra arc-light into the joint, turn up the local carbon-gas louver by three percent, and let the wall feed until the scar knits tight.

Higher still—miles above the streets, slung right beneath the vaulted ceiling where the great quartz light-pipes blaze—work the **lamp-gleaners**. It is the one hot place in the cavern. Up in the radiant bake beneath the lamps, swirling thermals carry blizzards of microscopic spore-wax and drifts of molted moth-down that constantly cake onto the glass tubes. Left uncleaned for a week, a fouled light-pipe casts a dull, yellowing lane of twilight onto the town directly below it, starving the roofs and bridges in its path. The gleaners ride in slender aluminum gondolas along the undersides of the pipes, wearing broad-brimmed hats of reflective sheet-mica and heavy dark goggles, sweeping the hot quartz clean with long dry-bristle brooms. Twice a shift, when they empty their catch-pans into baling chutes, stray plumes of golden-white wax-dust escape and snow lazily downward for thousands of feet, sparkling in the noon glare until flocks of dinner-plate cinder-moths bank inward to graze the falling cloud clean before it ever reaches the roofs.

Down on the basin floor, around the pneumatic gas-mills and the sunken pits where forty-foot flywheels spin in near-silence, move the **graphite-wrights**. In a city where grease, oil, and water are treated as industrial poisons—oil wicks instantly into *lignin-bone*, turning dry structural joints slick and treacherous—every bearing, piston-skirt, and gear-tooth runs on micron-fine dry graphite powder. Against the pale cream, ochre, and bright brass of the valley, the machinists look dipped in pewter. Their cuffs, aprons, and bare knuckles inside cut-finger gloves are polished to a dark, mirror-slick metallic sheen that feels soapy to the touch yet leaves no dampness behind. They carry wooden listening-rods carved from dead, cured husk-heartwood; you see old wrights walking the perimeter of a flywheel pit, pressing one end of a rod to a housing bolt and the other against their mastoid bone, eyes shut behind smoked lenses, hunting for the half-hertz flutter of a dry bearing beginning to gall.

And threading between the wrights and the riggers are the **damp-wardens**, easily spotted by the long, wicker-ribbed shoulder baskets they wear—and by the pale, narrow heads poking out of them. 

Over the four decades since the cavern was seeded, the sightless, badger-like predators of the transformer towers have split into two populations. The wild ones still drop silently from high gantries onto clicking wire-skinks. The tame line—bred smaller, calmer, and snowy white—are known locally as *sail-ferrets* or *whisper-hounds*. Blind under the blazing lamps, their wide, translucent, veined ear-sails twitch constantly like radar dishes. A warden patrolling the lower levels of a residential block walks slowly beside the wall while their hound leans out of its shoulder-cradle, nose inches from the papery lattice. Those huge ears can catch the dry *tick* of a wood-boring static-beetle three feet deep inside a pillar—and, far more urgently, they can hear the faint, unmistakable, fizzy *sizzle* of hidden moisture rotting raw husk from within. When a whisper-hound snaps its ear-sails flat against its skull and hisses like a bled air-hose, an entire street crew drops tools and comes running with desiccant lances and hot-air blowers to cauterize the damp spot before a load-bearing rib turns to paste.

***

Only when you follow someone home into one of the tall, hexagonal reed-towers do you see what they look like unarmored.

You cross a narrow, tension-grown skybridge that sways half an inch underfoot, step onto a landing, and enter through a *scrub-lock*: a narrow vestibule where a sharp downward blast of dry air strips every stray moth-scale, graphite smudge, and spore-flake off your coat through a floor grate. You unclip your brass heel-brushes at the sill. Then a heavy, double-gasketed hatch swings open, and you step inside an **amber-cell**.

No human living space touches the raw *lignin-bone* of the tower directly. Instead, each apartment is a sealed, curved pod of laminated quartz-sheet, baked spore-enamel, and thin aluminum, suspended inside the hollow hexagonal shaft on brass tension-springs like an egg hung in a basket. 

Once the inner hatch dogs shut, the silence changes. The crisp, dry library-hush of the valley vanishes, replaced by warm, soft, enclosed air kept at thirty-five percent humidity—air that smells shockingly thick and animal to anyone coming in from the street: wool, toasted lichen-bread, skin, and warm tea. Here, finally, people unlatch their brass still-muzzles and hang them on heated regeneration pegs by the door, where induction coils gently bake the day’s trapped breath-water out of the zeolite beads so it can trickle into a sealed glass condenser jar beneath the shelf.

Unmasked, a stranger’s lower face looks startlingly naked, almost indecently soft. To watch someone speak with bare, damp lips—hearing the wet click of tongue against teeth without the dry woodwind buzz of a mica reed—is an intimacy reserved for family, lovers, and sworn housemates. 

Even inside these sealed cells, liquid water is treated with the solemn caution a surface household reserves for lamp-fuel or acid. There are no open taps, no basins of sloshing dishwater, no steam-kettles whistling into the room. Every drop of water in the cell circulates through closed, double-walled quartz plumbing and sealed sanitary stills; a spilled cup is mopped up instantly with chemical sponge-blocks lest a drop find a hairline seal-crack and weep onto the structural cradle outside. People drink from heavy, rubber-ribbed glass bulbs fitted with bite-valves. They bathe stripped down in shallow, warmed troughs of finely milled cedar-pith and fuller’s earth—rubbing the hot, silky dust into scalp and skin to drink up sweat and oil, then stepping through a low-voltage copper hoop that pulls every single grain of powder off the body at once with a soft, prickling *pop*, leaving skin cleaner and smoother than soap ever managed.

When it is time to sleep, they draw heavy blackout curtains of layered grey moth-felt across the pod’s glazed portholes. Outside the glass, the cavern’s artificial noon never dims; light filters through the pale ochre tower walls in a steady, warm honey-glow twenty-four hours a day. Lying in the artificial dark of a curtained bunk, with your ear near the brass suspension struts, you never quite stop hearing the building live around you: a slow, dry *tick... creak... shhh* as millions of plant fibers flex and braid themselves tighter in the relentless daylight six inches beyond your wall.

***

Forty years is just long enough to split a town into two kinds of human being.

You can tell the **Founders**—now in their sixties and seventies, grey-browed behind their amber spectacles—by the heavy, heel-striking way they still try to walk when they are tired, and by the strange relics kept locked inside their private amber-cells. A retired pneumatic-engineer might keep a thumb-sized vial of hermetically sealed dark mud from an upland river valley on his shelf, turning it under a reading lamp on rest-days just to watch moisture bead on the inner glass. When Founders talk among themselves in sealed rooms, their metaphors are still stubbornly wet: projects *gain steam*, tempers *boil over*, bad plans *wash out*.

To their grandchildren—the **Husk-Born**, twenty- and thirty-year-olds who have never stood beneath an unroofed sky—those phrases sound like archaic medical slang, slightly disgusting if you picture them too closely. 

The Husk-Born are leaner, lighter-footed, and pale as balsa pith beneath their morning wax. Their pupils dilate wide and dark the instant they step into a curtained room; their ears are so finely tuned to dry micro-acoustics that they can tell from three blocks away whether a transit car’s graphite shoes need dusting, or identify a housemate’s approach by the exact pitch of their boot-brush ticking on the street-rail. To them, the surface world described in old archive reels is a place of visceral horror: an unlit, howling chaos where tons of corrosive liquid fall loose and filthy out of black emptiness, where dead trees collapse into slimy black rot crawling with soft, unplated worms, and where buildings sit dead and stupid as stacked stones, incapable of healing a single crack on their own. 

Down here, cleanliness is dryness; safety is light; beauty is tension, geometry, and room to breathe. Even the pests that plague the young generation belong strictly to the dry world—like the tiny, translucent *spark-mites* that hitchhiked up from deep tectonic fissures to spin copper-dusted webs across live guide-wires, siphoning trickle-current and tricking the husk-flora into growing wild, lacy tumors of rogue lattice (*witch-burl*, the riggers call it) along neglected eaves until teenagers on community-shift walk the parapets with ebonite wands, winding the charged webs off like spun sugar.

Every four hours, when the great rotary louvers five miles away prepare to reverse the valley’s breath, you can watch the whole sparse metropolis answer at once. 

Ten seconds before the draft arrives, the pressure drops. Across the bright, wide plazas, hundreds of distant, dark-coated figures pause mid-stride or mid-sign, tilting their masked chins slightly as their ears pop. Up on the unfinished viaducts, wire-riggers hook one elbow around a stay-cable; on the street shoulders, tame whisper-hounds flare their veined ear-sails wide to taste the shift; miles overhead, lamp-gleaners brace their brooms against their gondola rails. 

Then the cold, linen-clean wind sweeps across the basin. Millions of hollow ochre beams flex and release their long, papery sigh; the endless miles of brass and copper blueprint-wires hum their single, faint, freezing chord; snowstorms of white spore-flakes swirl briefly around tall brass bench-legs and copper-heeled boots; and before the note has even fully died out of the wires, the people of the valley have already shifted two slow paces out of their own crisp shadows and gone quietly back to work.
