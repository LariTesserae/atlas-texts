There are men here, though you could stand on a catwalk for six hours looking out over the flooded tarmac and never pick one out from the rusting pipe-stanchions. 

They do not number more than forty across the whole twelve-square-mile pan. Most are on a municipal or corporate payroll three times subcontracted, receiving direct deposits from a holding company in a cooler city four hundred miles inland, though a few are simply squatters who learned which valves not to touch and so were left alone by the ones who get paid. After ten years in the basin, there is no visual difference between the two.

You cannot keep dry here, and anyone who tries—wearing rubber waders or zipped slickers—boils in their own salt within an hour and gets hauled out with heat-stroke. So the men who stay have given up on dry. They dress in loose, faded synthetics—old polyester scrub pants cut off at the calf, mesh vests stained the exact ochre of the bacterial mats, and thick-soled polyurethane clogs slotted along the sides so the warm ponding water can sluice straight through with every step. Their skin has taken on a permanent, waxy pallor, bloated slightly at the knuckles and ankles, cured by the alkaline mist and the constant hundred-degree sweat until it looks like boiled tallow. None of them wear watches or wedding bands; metal traps the wet against the flesh and raises weeping red rings of rot in three days.

They live twenty-five feet up. Down on the concrete pan, the crabs get into everything, and the damp rises through a mattress in an hour. Instead, they occupy the elevated control blisters, old relay rooms, and galvanized switch-houses bolted to the sides of the cracking towers and tank farms. These little aluminum rooms have working air-conditioning—industrial chiller loops meant to keep server racks and circuit boards from frying—and stepping through a blister’s heavy gasketed door is like stepping into a meat locker. The cold inside smells fiercely of canned sardines, wet dog, silicone grease, and mildew. They sleep in there under heaps of damp fleece blankets, three or four to a room, listening to the chiller fans rattle, waking up with stiff joints and dry throats before pushing the heavy door open again into the wet wool punch of the outside air.

When they move across the district, they rarely walk the tarmac. Walking the flats means shuffling slow so you don't slip on the black algae, crunching through crabs until the smell of broken shell draws the eels right up against your clogs, and wading knee-deep through thermal ditches where hundred-and-ten-degree water pours out of concrete culverts. Instead, they travel the pipe-racks. 

Two stories above the flooded plazas, miles of steel catwalks and I-beam flanges run beside the insulated steam mains. A basin hand walks with a particular, unhurried gait: knees slightly bent, toes turned out for purchase on the slick galvanized grating, one hand trailing lightly along a sweating conduit—never gripping, because a live steam leak is invisible for the first three inches before it condenses into white vapor, and an invisible jet will take the meat off two fingers before you feel the heat. For that reason, every one of them carries a three-foot wand of frayed fiberglass conduit, held out loosely ahead of their knees like a blind man’s cane, sweeping the air along the pipe joints to watch for the telltale flutter of the frayed fibers.

The heat and the background hum of the transformers have stripped the noise right out of them. On the catwalks, nobody shouts over a pump housing; shouting takes wind, and the wet air gives nothing back. They talk up close in low, flat chest-voices, or across fifty yards of empty tarmac with lazy two-fingered hand signs borrowed from crane riggers fifty years ago. Even their tools are quieted—wrenches wrapped in friction tape so they don't *clang* against steel and bring five hundred cormorants shitting and flapping off an overhead boom all at once.

Their actual work is slow, custodial, and almost biological. Automated refineries don’t need hands on the dials, they need hands with flat-bladed scrapers. Here, life is fouling. If a basin hand doesn't make his rounds for two weeks, pale clusters of heat-tolerant mussels will pack the intake grates of a secondary cooling loop so tight the submerged pumps cavitate and burn out. So a man spends four hours of the grey morning sitting on an overturned five-gallon bucket at the edge of a canal, feet dangling in the hot, catfish-thick water, hauling up heavy chain-link strainer baskets with a block and tackle. He scrapes thirty pounds of barnacles, snails, and gray bryozoan jelly off the steel mesh with an iron spud bar, dumping it directly back into the water. He doesn't even have to kick it in. Before the pile hits the concrete, the tarmac turns solid black-and-orange with crabs, and the water boils with blunt, whiskered heads fighting over the crushed shell. The catfish bump right against his heels, slick and heavy as wet inner tubes. He doesn't look down at them.

They eat out of the canals, too, though not straight out. An armored catfish pulled directly from the retention basin tastes like diesel, hot mud, and rust. Up on the catwalk landings outside their cold-rooms, the men keep rows of blue fifty-five-gallon plastic drums fed by gutter pipes that catch the clean, warm drizzle off the high warehouse roofs, aerated by tiny bled-off lines from the plant’s compressed-air mains. They drop half a dozen big eels or mud-cats into a drum and let them swim in clean rainwater for ten days until their gills flush clear and the oil-taste cooks out of their fat. Come evening, when the sodium floodlights click on and turn the mist amber, you will see a tiny, blue propane flame burning on a catwalk grate forty feet above an empty intersection—a man squatting in his shorts, skin glistening orange in the lamp-glow, frying white chunks of purged eel in a cast-iron skillet while midges snow silently into the grease.

Beneath the men, sharing the dry strips of concrete medians and the hollow foundations under the storage tanks, live the dogs—and the things the dogs haven't managed to kill. 

The basin dogs don't look like town strays anymore. Five or six generations in a wet hundred degrees has bred the coat almost entirely off them; they are slate-skinned, big-eared, narrow-ribbed creatures the color of wet cement, with sparse, stiff bristles along their spines and wide, splayed paws webbed thick between the toes from a lifetime on slick algae. They never bark. A barking dog wastes water through its tongue. They hunt at a dead-silent, flat-backed trot through the amber light-cones, snapping up soft-shell crabs right after a molt, or standing belly-deep and statuesque as herons at the lip of a warm outfall, waiting to pin a three-foot water snake behind the skull before it can slide back into the culvert. 

Between the dogs and the men there is an old, tired truce. When a basin worker walks a lower retaining wall at dusk with a bucket of grease-guns, three or four hairless dogs will materialize out of the warm fog twenty paces back, matching his speed, stepping precisely in the dry footprints his clogs press briefly into the wet algal film before the water seeps back in. They don't beg, and he doesn't whistle. If he pries open a junction box and finds a thick, four-foot rat snake coiled tight around a warm porcelain transformer bushing—dead and baked stiff from bridging two phases—he pulls it loose with insulated tongs and drops it over the rail without looking. Down below, there is one soft *wet-leather* thud on the concrete, a brief click of dog teeth, and nothing else.

Only one other big thing lives down in the foundations, tucked into the warm, dark crawlspaces under the miles of low-slung assembly sheds where the concrete pilings sit four feet apart in shallow, stagnant black water. People from inland would call them hogs, or maybe nutria, depending on which end they saw in the dark before it went under, though in truth it is an old delta population of feral pigs that got trapped in the subsidence forty years back and shrunk down to fit under the slabs. 

They are no heavier than sixty pounds now, almost amphibious, coated in a crust of dried alkaline clay and rust-slime that keeps the brine flies off their backs. They spend the flat grey afternoons submerged to their nostrils in ninety-eight-degree water beneath the warehouses, listening to the automated stamping presses or fluid-bed reactors throb gently through the floor slabs twelve inches above their ears. Only in the deepest charcoal hours after midnight, when even the sodium lamps are half-choked with dead moths, do they come out onto the open plazas in tight, silent sounders of six or eight, rooting with calloused, shovel-hard snouts along the expansion joints of the concrete, prying up ribbons of leathery ochre bacteria and crunching whole clusters of zebra mussels off the base of the retaining walls. 

Sometimes, around three in the morning, a man sitting alone on an upper catwalk—door to his cold-room propped open an inch behind him so a ribbon of chilled air hits the back of his neck while he smokes a damp hand-rolled cigarette—will look down through the warm, iron-smelling mist into an amber pool of floodlight two hundred yards square. 

Down there, a sounder of mud-gray hogs will be working an expansion joint from the north. A rank of five bare-skinned dogs will come trotting down a raised median from the east, see the hogs, slow to a walk, and curve wide around them through six inches of steaming water without raising a hackle—too hot to fight over mussels when the ditches are full of slow eels. Ten thousand crabs part in two neat lanes around both groups and click shut behind their heels. Up on the horizontal boom of a gantry crane level with the smoker’s eyes, two hundred black cormorants sit shoulder to shoulder with their wings drooped open like wet umbrellas, watching the hogs, watching the dogs, watching the red coal of the cigarette brighten once in the pewter dark and then drop—a tiny silent arc of orange sparks—down forty feet into a canal, where three armored catfish strike the floating butt at once before sinking back into the warm, thrumming dark.
