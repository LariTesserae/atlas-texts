At first, looking across the roaring gloom of three or four translucent city blocks, you mistake them for drifting debris—great, tattered wraps of shed bladder-vine or stray laundry caught in a thermal—until one of them steps out onto nothing at all, plants a wide, bare foot onto a shimmering shear-plane of compressed air, and leans a forty- degree angle into a wall of bass thunder that would knock an outsider off a ledge in half a second.

There are people here. Not the ones who buried the emitters down in the raw basalt and tuned the sky to hold weight—those minds either went elsewhere when the crust split, or refined themselves out of meat entirely—but the descendants of maintenance crews, acoustic surveyors, basin-squatters, and stranded biologic technicians who stayed behind in the warm din long enough for the din to remake them. Twenty generations in a place where silence is fatal and weight is an engineering hazard has turned them into something slender, rubber-ribbed, and uncanny to watch.

Up close, a resident of the tiers looks slightly over-drawn, as if pulled upward while still soft. They are tall—often near seven feet—yet they weigh startlingly little, their long bones honeycombed like a pelican’s, their joints loose and hyper-mobile to absorb the constant micro-jackhammering of the floors. If a heavy-boned outlander stands on a residential membrane too long, their kneecaps craze with hairline fractures and their kidneys bleed from the sub-audible shear; the locals have thick, gelatinous pads of dense cartilage nearly an inch deep beneath their heels, palms, and sit-bones, grey and calloused as elephant hide, which drink the structural vibration before it reaches their marrow. 

Their chests are broad and strangely supple, lacking a rigid, fused sternum. Instead, their ribs meet at the front in a flexible lattice of fibrous gristle. When a district harmonic shifts—when the great turbines down in the fissures step up a third to stiffen an avenue against a sudden crosswind—you can actually watch a local’s torso visibly ripple in sympathy, letting the pressure wave pass cleanly through their lungs rather than fighting it. They do not have eardrums anymore; eardrums ruptured in the first week and never healed right, and over decades the body gave up on the middle ear entirely, sealing the canals over with smooth, pearly drums of scar-like skin. They hear through their jawbones, their collarbones, and the sensitive, damp skin stretched across their throats. To listen closely to a faint structural flutter in a wall-filament, a citizen does not cock their head; they open their mouth slightly and press their chin or knuckles against the shimmering air.

Because speaking aloud in the basin is like spitting into a hurricane, their throats have gone quiet, used only for coughing out inhaled mist-spores or making sharp, percussive *clicks* with the tongue that register at two paces as tiny, needle-bright shockwaves against a companion's cheekbones. Everything else is said with light, geometry, and interruptions in the roar.

They wear almost nothing—a soggy wrap of woven aero-moss around the loins, perhaps a harness of braided spider-filament slung across the chest—because wet cloth sours in the balmy heat and heavy fabric drags in a jump. Instead, they cultivate luminous skin-symbiotes borrowed from the canopy: smears and crisp geometric bands of captive bioluminescent bacteria painted along their long forearms, collarbones, and fingers, fed by the salt of their own sweat. In the perpetual pewter dusk, two neighbors arguing across a fifty-yard gap of open air are a flurry of cold amber, cyan, and sharp violet hand-cant—*flick, sweep, damp-out, flare*—their long, double-jointed fingers snapping open and shut like signal shutters. Nuance lives in how quickly they mask the light with a palm, or how they turn a shoulder to let the ambient mist blur a color into a halo. Anger is rapid, staccato white-blue across the ribs; humor is a slow, rolling green wave down both arms that makes them look briefly like undulating ferns.

For longer distances, or when hands are full, they carry *shades*—hand-fans made of dried, stiffened arthropod wing-baffles. By snapping a shade open at a precise angle inside an acoustic standing wave, a person casts an instant, laser-straight "sound shadow" across three hundred yards of empty plaza. Someone standing three blocks away feels a sudden, delicious tenth-of-a-second drop in pressure tap against their shoulder blades—*ba-dump, dump, ba-dump*—a tactile Morse code punched directly out of the background roar. Walk down a crowded elevated thoroughfare at dusk, and the air is crisscrossed with invisible beams of momentary quiet, strangers tapping polite warnings onto one another’s backs before crossing an intersection: *passing left; heavy load; mind the slick.*

Living on nothing requires an intimate, physical literacy in invisible architecture. Outsiders see empty twilight between four violet pinpricks; locals read the air the way river-pilots read muddy water. They see the faint refractive curl where a floor-membrane thins near an edge; they watch the three-dimensional grids of suspended water beads to see how the city’s weight is settling. Before stepping onto an unfamiliar span, a child will flick a thumbnail of spit ahead of them—if it flattens into a spinning silver coin and skitters sideways, the floor is hard; if it drops through in a lazy tear, that square yard is dead air, a lethal trapdoor three hundred feet above the basalt knives.

Their homes have no walls to keep out weather—the warmth never changes, and rain does not fall, it simply *happens* right where you are standing when a harmonic squall sweeps the block. Privacy is visual, not solid. Families string six or seven layers of living, chartreuse bladder-vine between two hairline struts to break up silhouettes, creating billowing, glowing green rooms suspended over the abyss. Inside, there are no chairs or tables—furniture with legs would punch straight through a phase-membrane like needles through soap film. Everything is slung, inflated, or wide-footed. They sleep in deep, breathable hammocks of cured leaf-silk that sway twenty feet out over sheer drops, cradling sleepers in warm updrafts. Cooking is done without fire—smoke cannot rise properly through horizontal acoustic ceilings and lingers in choking, vibrating pancakes—so they cook with the city itself. Every household keeps a * tuning-fork kettle*: a hollow, double-walled bladder of tough gourd-skin hung at the exact intersection of two high-frequency containment shears, where the ultrasonic friction boils water and stews sliced glider-meat in four minutes flat while the outside air stays merely warm.

To get water, nobody carries a bucket. When the ten-second condensation squall hits a district and millions of warm droplets lock into shimmering geometric lattices across the living room, someone simply picks up a wide hoop strung with fine hydrophilic hair and walks once from corner to corner, sweeping five gallons of pure, suspended mist out of midair and wringing it into a hanging gut-sack before the chord shifts and the rest vanishes back into humidity.

Their economy is entirely preoccupied with buoyancy, tension, and the maintenance of the ghosts beneath them. 

The highest status among them belongs to the *Line-Tenders* and *Baffle-Cutters*—lean, scarred daredevils who grease themselves in slick glider-tallow so the shearing air won't flay their skin, clip safety-tethers of non-baryonic silk to their harnesses, and drop beneath the lowest residential tiers into the empty, roaring halls above the rock. Down there, the acoustic pressure is monstrous. A man dangling two hundred feet below the streets vibrates so hard his outline blurs into a three-inch smear; stay down an hour too long, and your retinas detach or your blood foams in your calves. They go down anyway to scrape away the heavy, calcified shells of dead violin-arthropods before the accumulated weight drags a mono-filament out of true, or to prune back stray curtains of canopy-vine whose wet hydrogen bladders are beginning to muffle a primary standing wave. 

You can always spot an old Line-Tender on the upper plazas: their skin has a permanent, pearlescent sheen from microscopic acoustic scarring, their eyes are milky at the rims, and they walk with a strange, drifting arrogance, stepping across half-inch hairline conduits over three-hundred-foot drops without bothering to clip in, trusting their calloused heels to ride the upwellings like gulls.

Yet for all their grace aloft, the whole culture is haunted by Down.

Nobody goes to the floor. In twenty generations, almost no living citizen has ever felt unyielding stone beneath their feet, and the concept of *ground*—matter that does not give, does not hum, and waits patiently at the bottom of everything to split you open—fills them with a visceral, nauseated dread. The jagged black basalt gleaming faintly through ten translucent canopies below is their hell and their graveyard. Because there is no soil to dig and fire is scarce, when someone dies in the tiers, their kin strip them of their harnesses, wash the glowing bacteria from their skin so they go dark, and carry them out to an un-gridded gap between towers. 

The funeral is brief. Twenty or thirty mourners gather on the edge of an invisible plaza, glowing dim, bruised indigo along their throats. Together, they open wide acoustic shades in unison, casting a broad chimney of deadened air over the gap. Into that sudden, terrifying pocket of reduced pressure, they roll the body. 

Without the standing waves to buoy it, the corpse plummets like lead—tearing silently through layer after layer of wet emerald silk that seals instantly behind it in green capillary sighs, vanishing down into the dark toward the fresh, sharp fractures below, where small, blind, scissor-legged scavengers—the only creatures down there among the basalt splinters, flat as razor blades and deaf as stone—wait for whatever the loud sky lets go.

Only at the very fringes of the basin, out where the needle-pylons thin and the standing waves fray into limp, heavy air, do you find the *Rim-Walkers*—exiles, heretics, and half-mad scavengers who drag themselves out of the acoustic field on bellies and crutches because their light, honeycombed bones buckle under ordinary, unassisted gravity. They crawl along the wet, sharp margins in thick leather wraps made from collapsed bladder-vines, harvesting mineral salts from the rare trickles of real runoff, trading small pouches of grit and chipped obsidian knives back into the weightless city. When they pause at the edge and look back into the twenty-mile bowl—chest heaving painfully in the thin, horribly *quiet* air of the rim, watching the distant violet knots and the miles of glowing green curtains thrashing in the warm, solid roar—they look like deep-sea fish flopped onto dry gravel, starving for the crushing pressure that used to hold them together.
