You do not see them first, even if you carry a light—and light here is an intrusion that reaches far too wide in the gin-clear water, throwing stark, theatrical shadows across half a mile of bare slate. You feel them through your boots as a sudden, deliberate dampening of the valley’s noise, or hear the crisp, double-click of river cobbles struck together in a rhythm too steady for an upwelling vent.

Nothing swims in this valley, and neither do they. To let go of the bottom where the invisible rivers sweep down from the drowned passes is to be carried out toward the roaring rims and thrown off the edge of the mountain into thin air. Everything about them belongs to the boundary layer—the bottom five feet where stone resists water.

They are built like squat, heavy-shouldered hominids carved out of basalt and bog-butter, standing barely four and a half feet tall, though they rarely stand fully upright outside the calm lee of a grove or a stone hut. Out on the flats, they travel in a low, driving crouch, leaning forty degrees into the flow like mountaineers leaning into a gale. They weigh three hundred pounds or more. Beneath a hide as smooth, seamless, and gray-black as wet slate lies a four-inch mantle of dense, rubbery blubber, stiff enough in the near-freezing dark to hold its shape like armor, over bones that have lost all marrow-hollows and turned as solid as ivory. They have no hair anywhere, no external ears to catch and tear in the current, and no necks to speak of; their wide, flat-crowned skulls sit settled directly between massive trapezius ridges, streamlining their shoulders into a smooth wedge that lets the water press them *down* onto the rock rather than lifting them off it.

Up close, in the faint, fleeting blue smear of a burst seed-pod, their faces look unfinished, almost fetal in their smoothness, yet ancient in their weight. Their eyes are scarcely larger than dark juniper berries, sunk deep behind heavy brows of gristled pad, useful only for catching the ghostly trajectories of luminescent sap or checking whether a drift-current has dragged them too high toward the violet bruise of the far-off ceiling. Sight is a minor sense to them. Instead, across the bridge of the flat nose, along the heavy jaw, and down the outsides of their forearms run rows of pale, dimpled pits—sensory ampullae and pressure-pores that read the shape of the dark through water-shear. 

When one pauses beside a notched boulder on the open gneiss, it does not need to touch the stone to know which path it is on. It simply holds up a broad, heavy hand, fingers splayed, and feels the exact geometry of the whistling wake peeling off the rock ten paces upstream. They read the valley as an intricate relief map of acoustic shadows: the boom of the gas-bladders bouncing back off a cirque wall tells them the exact curvature of the granite half a mile away; the changing pitch of the old, frayed copper-wedged cables singing in a chute tells them how hard the deep artesian throats are surging today.

They do not breathe the water through feathery, fragile gills—nothing fragile survives the scour or the snapped whip-fronds of a violent grove. Instead, they possess high, muscular siphons tucked under the base of the skull behind the jaw, opening into broad, sluggish branchial chambers lined with tough, leathery plates that pump water in slow, bellows-like heaves, extracting the rich dissolved oxygen churned down from the violent mountain surface far above. When they work hard—hauling stone or prying holdfasts—you can hear them venting: a low, rhythmic *huff-thrum* as twin jets of slightly warmed water shoot down along their backs. Their blood runs slow and dark, thick with copper-heavy proteins forged from the mineral seeps, keeping their extremities functioning half a degree above freezing while their deeply insulated cores burn steady and hot.

Because nothing rots here, the valley looks abandoned to a hasty eye. A shelter built four hundred years ago looks much like one built forty years ago, its slate slabs clean of slime, its cached flint knives sitting untouched on a stone bench because the maker simply walked ten miles over the ridge to another basin and has not needed to come back this century. There are very few of them—perhaps three or four dozen across the entire drowned highland—and they live terribly long, slow lives. Cold and pressure stretch out their years. A cut on a knuckle takes half a season to knit; a child takes thirty years to put on enough bone-weight to cross an open current-trough without being tethered to an adult’s waist by a braided kelp-line.

They are creatures of patience, quarrymen and arborists of a hostile, automated forest.

Watch two of them working the edge of a towering grove. They wear no clothes—fabric only drags and frays in the current—save broad, heavy aprons and harnesses of cured kelp-rind slung low across the hips, hung with mauls of banded gneiss, pry-bars of cold-beaten native copper pried out of fault-seams, and coils of black fiber rope. Their hands are immense, five-fingered spades tipped with thick, horn-colored nails curved slightly downward like climbing crampons; their feet are wide and flat, the big toe splayed wide at an angle to wedge into bedrock cracks, their heels rough as coarse sandstone to hold purchase on wet granite.

Approaching the grove requires timing. The calcified organ-pipe stalks clatter and chime along the perimeter, and overhead in the black, the rubbery, oak-thick canopies thrash. The pair waits motionless on all fours just outside the root-zone, listening through their palms pressed flat to the stone. They can feel the internal hydraulic pressure building inside a great trunk—a subtle, tightening hum through the bedrock as a mature canopy-pod prepares to fire or a defensive root-tendril coils to strike an encroaching neighbor. 

One taps twice on the stone with a copper wedge. They move in together during the lull, hugging the gnarled holdfasts where the current dies abruptly into turbulent, swirling calm. Here, the water smells sharply of iodine, crushed iron, and the bitter, peppery tang of ruptured plant-sap. They do not clear-cut; a grove takes centuries to anchor itself against the scour, and to kill the windbreak of big trunks would let the river strip the whole oasis bare. Instead, they prune and bleed it. One anchors itself by hooking a knee-crook and one heavy foot into a root-arch, bracing its slate-gray back against the trunk, while the other works with a flint-bladed adze, hacking neat, concoidal windows into the pale, woody outer rind—the exact scars that, decades later, heal into those smooth burls on the older trees. 

From the wound wells a thick, pale-blue mucilage, dense with sugars, lipids, and harsh, stinging alkaloids meant to burn away rival moss-spores. Before the sap can stiffen in the cold water, the harvester scrapes it directly into a flexible bladder made from an old, hollowed-out spore sac, sealing the neck with a bone toggle. Elsewhere under the canopy, they move carefully through waist-high silver-black ferns, clipping the melon-sized spore sacs before their coiled tendrils can snap, and peeling thick, gritty slabs of crimson mineral-moss straight off the stone like turf, rolling them up to be chewed later for iron and salt.

They eat cold, heavy food, sitting hunched inside their corbelled slate beehives out of the endless push of the current. Inside a shelter, with a flat slab pulled across the low crawlway, the water finally goes completely dead and still. Three or four of them packed into that cramped stone dark will slowly raise the temperature of the trapped water by a few precious degrees with the heat of their massive bodies. It is the closest thing to a hearth they know. In that sluggish, slightly Less-Freezing pocket of pitch black, they unlash their harnesses, rest their chins on their chests, and pass around chunks of tough, fermented spore-curd that has been wedged under boulders for three seasons until its defensive poisons broke down into dense, waxy fat.

They speak there, too, though not with air. Pressing a thumb or a chin against a companion’s shoulder blade or collarbone—or simply letting the enclosed stone dome act as a sounding board—they hum through closed jaws and click tongue-bones inside their throats. To an outsider, the stone hut from ten yards away would sound like a hollow boulder full of shifting gravel and groaning timber; inside, they are trading whole histories of the basin. They keep an oral map of every upwelling vent, every loosening boulder on the talus slopes, every grove whose root-wars are pushing it inch by inch out into the scour, and every frayed strand on the great singing cables strung across the gorges.

Those cables are not abandoned, though many hang ragged for generations. The baskets woven onto them are traps for genetics and light as much as food—catching the rogue, jet-propelled seed-pods that shoot out of the high, unreachable cliff-groves so the gatherers can carry the glowing, sticky capsules down into barren fault-seeps and wedge them into cracks with bruised stone, starting new islands of forest that their grandchildren will harvest two hundred years hence. When a cable finally unravels enough that its bass drone drops a third in pitch—a flat, sour note echoing miles across the valley floor—someone eventually hears it, sets down their adze, braids six hundred feet of fresh black stalk-fiber over the course of a quiet winter, and trudges up into the roaring chute with a mouthful of copper wedges and a stone maul to tune the gorge back to its proper key.

Only two things draw them away from the stone floor’s safety: birth and the end of life, which point in opposite directions.

The deep artesian vents—those circular throats in the valley floor where water cold as ice-melt erupts from the mountain’s core, rattling plumes of smooth pebbles—are sacred or utilitarian in the quiet way all things are to them, which is to say there is no difference between the two. Nothing grows right at the lip of a cold vent, and the water pouring out of it has never touched the upper air; it is heavy with dissolved stone and utterly sterile. When one of them is badly gashed by a snapping calcified stalk or crushed by a rolling talus block, they crawl to the rim of an upwelling and let that freezing, mineral-choked plume wash over the wound, numbing the flesh completely while the dancing pebbles clink and chatter an arm's length away.

And when they grow truly old—when their joints stiffen with mineral deposits until they can no longer crouch low enough to cheat the drag of the invisible rivers, and their pressure-pits dull until the echoes off the ridges blur into white noise—they stop walking the notched-boulder paths between the shelters. Because nothing decays here, a corpse left in a stone hut or wedged in a grove would stay there forever, waxy and whole, slowly sanded smooth over millennia by stray grit like a piece of driftwood. They do not care to clutter so sparse a world with themselves.

So an old one walks downstream instead. Unburdened of harness, flint, and copper, leaving its tools neatly stacked on a dry-fitted stone bench for whoever wanders past in thirty years, it follows the polished bedrock troughs out past the last singing gorge, out where the granite drops away and the black water gathers terrifying, silent speed. Hands and crampon-nails skittering at last across rock too slick to hold, it lets the current take its heavy, streamlined bulk, lifting it off the mountain’s bones for the first and only time—rushing out into the booming, violet-rimmed dark where the highland pours itself endlessly over the brink into the clouds.
