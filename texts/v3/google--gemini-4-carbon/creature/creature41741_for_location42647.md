You smell them before your eyes adjust to the bruised light, and at first, you think it is only the trees. It is a dense, medicinal scent of crushed juniper berries, cold lanolin, wet wool, and the sharp, astringent tang of willow-salicylate. 

Then a section of the braided wall detaches itself, shifts its weight with a faint *creak* of stiff leather, and moves down the corridor.

They are built like river-stones—low, compact, and dense, with scarcely a neck to speak of and limbs foreshortened to hoard heat. An adult stands no higher than a lowland child’s chest, yet weighs nearly as much as a lowland man, their frames thick-boned and padded with a smooth, firm layer of subcutaneous fat that rounds off every angle of jaw, shoulder, and hip. In a place where the air sits perpetually at the threshold of ice, surface area is the enemy. Their ears are small, rolled tight to the skull and tucked beneath coarse, oily hair the color of peat or tarnished pewter. Their noses are broad and flat, with wide, baffled nostrils that warm the damp air before it hits their lungs. 

When one turns toward a light-well, its eyes catch the slate-colored gloom with a sudden, milky-green flare—not glowing, just gathering every stray photon that leaks through the yard-thick moss above. Up close, the irises are nearly black, swallowing the whites almost entirely, giving them the solemn, unblinking gaze of deep-water seals.

At first glance, they seem wrapped in coarse, shaggy pelts, though there are no sheep or goats in the trench, nor room to graze them on the lethal granite screes outside. What they wear is felted directly from the settlement itself, mixed with their own sheddings. Over decades, families collect combings of their own thick under-hair, rolling it on their thighs with the fibrous inner bast of willow, tough tufts of bog-cotton gathered during the six-week summer thaw, and trailing skeins of black beard-lichen. They beat and wet this mixture with hot urine and snowmelt until it mats into heavy, seamless tunics an inch thick. Waterproofed with pine-pitch and the natural tallow of their skin, these garments grow shiny and stiff at the elbows and knees, sprouting tiny, accidental gardens of bright green spore-moss across the shoulders. When two of them pass in an alley barely wide enough for their shoulders, they do not turn sideways and risk scraping the fragile bark-buds; one simply squats low into a ball, becoming an indistinguishable boulder of dark felt and lichen, while the other steps smoothly over their back, using a braced shoulder as a rung.

Watch their hands. Their hands never stop moving, even when they are resting or speaking. 

The fingers are short, wide as paddles, and slightly curved inward from birth, ending in thumbnails that are shockingly thick—horn-gray, ridged like highland ram’s horn, and worn into skewed, chisel-edged crescents. Beside them, tucked into greasy pouches under their tunics, they carry small, knapped flakes of raw, unweathered granite, struck straight from the weeping cliff walls. As an elder walks down the greased-leather thoroughfare of fused trunks, her thick hands trail lightly along the living railings like a blind woman reading script. Without looking, without breaking stride, her horn-thumb finds a swelling epicormic bud, hooks under its base, and *snaps* it off cleanly. Three paces later, she feels a whip-shoot loosening from its stone tension-wedge; she pauses for four breaths, uses her broad flat molars to chew a strand of sedge-cord pulled from her belt until it goes supple, binds the freezing twig back into the weave, tucks a lump of absorbent sphagnum into the seep-joint beside it, and moves on. 

This is not chores; it is respiration. It is a nervous tic shared by four hundred minds. If you bound their hands, the town would strangle them in three winters, its doorways shrinking like healing wounds, its stairwells bristling shut with waxy, blue-green needles. 

Because there is no dry wood—because a single open flame would fill this sealed, sunken lung with suffocating smoke and boil the sap in the load-bearing walls—there is no fire here. There has never been fire here. Everything warm in this trench is warm because blood is pumping through it.

Their entire domestic life is arranged around the hoarding and trading of metabolic heat. Follow the slow, rising convection currents up to the second tier, ducking through an oval doorway no bigger than the mouth of a bread oven, its rim swollen into a smooth, pale callus of scar-wood. Inside, a hollow burl the size of a haycart holds nine people. The floor is a deep, springy mattress of dry juniper needles and shed heather, meticulously kept above the damp line, insulated by two feet of living wood all around. In the center of this dark, resin-smelling womb sits a *ferment-jar*—not clay, which cannot be fired, business-like and seamless, woven from coiled pine roots smeared thick with pitch and swollen watertight. Inside it, crushed violet fungi, chewed lily-bulbs from the summer slopes, pine-cambium, and scraps of dried marmot fat are breaking down in a slow, anaerobic rot that generates its own gentle, sour heat. 

The family sits pressed around this warm belly of roots, knees drawn up, bare soles tucked under one another’s thighs. Their feet are remarkable: leathery, broad-soled, with a thick, opposable big toe stiffened by decades of gripping wet, horizontal bark and hauling thick bodies up the vertical root-ladders of the ceiling vents. In the pitch dark of the burl, they do not talk much—sound dies immediately in the spongy walls anyway, and loud voices waste breath-heat. Instead, they communicate in a quiet, continuous murmur of clicks, soft throat-hums, and tactile pressure: two fingers tapped against a neighbor's collarbone, a palm slid across a knee, a shift of shoulder weight that ripples through five bodies at once to say *move back, the wall is weeping behind me*, or *the child is shivering, put him in the middle*.

And what do they eat, in a world without grain, herds, or firewood? 

Down on the lowest level, where the light fails completely and the granite floor bleeds cold, mineral-heavy water over the knotted roots of the settlement, you find the farms. Young men and women with pale, translucent skin—some go months without climbing the shafts into the blinding snow-world—crouch in the icy trickle, tending long, deliberate gashes cut into the massive base-trunks of the willow-pines. These trees are not killed; they are bled, like cattle in a pasture. From the weeping wounds, the tenders scrape curls of sweet, starchy inner cambium and collect beads of cloudy, sugar-rich sap that freezes almost as it emerges, eaten like pale amber candy. Farther back, where the air smells sharpest of wet iron and anise, entire deadfalls of pruned twigs—every bud and branch snapped off in the upper streets—are packed into clefts of the rock to feed dense, terraced beds of the violet-capped cartilage-fungi. 

Nothing leaves the trench. Night-soil, nail-parings, dead shrews, spoiled felt, and eventually the dead themselves—curled tight, knees to chin, bound with sedge—are carried down into the pitch-black root-vaults at the pinched ends of the fault. There, wedged deep beneath the thorny plug of alpine rose and pine roots, the cold, slow fungi and the hungry root-hairs dismantle them over decades, drawing their calcium and nitrogen straight back up into the floorboards, the arches, the hollow bedrooms, and the sweet inner bark that feeds their grandchildren. To touch a pillar in the street is to touch an ancestor’s ribs, turned to tough, resinous heartwood.

Yet they are not wholly creatures of the dark. Every day, when the noon light turns the ceiling from charcoal to tarnished olive, two or three figures in the upper tiers pull thick hoods of greased marmot-hide over their heads. They fit narrow, slit-carved slivers of gray slate over their eyes, tied tight with sinew behind their small ears to block the lethal glare of the snowfields. 

They climb the weeping ventilation chimneys—hand over leathery hand, big toes hooking into living, ice-glazed root-rungs—pushing their shoulders through the freezing slush at the lip until they break out into the roaring, white, sub-zero gale of the ridge. Up there, looking like stubby, frost-rimed cairns that have learned to walk, they move slowly across the shattered granite. They check deadfall traps of balanced stone set for ptarmigan and winter hares; they chip fresh, razor-sharp flakes of flinty granite from the exposed fault-lip to replace dull pruning-blades down below; during the brief, violent mountain summer, they harvest frantic armfuls of sedge, sour-sorrel, and dwarf-willow catkins to pack into the ferment-jars. 

They never stay out long. An hour in that scouring wind turns their breath to masks of solid rime on their felt collars. Soon enough, the cairns shuffle back to a faint, steaming dimple in the snow-crust—the only sign for fifty miles that anything lives on the mountain—and drop feet-first into the ground, pulling a plug of woven heather shut over their heads.

Down in the bruised twilight, they peel off their frozen outer hides, hang them in the slow rising draft of the lower tiers to drip onto the cabbage-leaf gutters, and press their chilled, paddle-shaped hands under the armpits of whoever comes out to greet them. No words are spoken. Someone passes them a lump of sour, warm pine-mash from a root-jar; someone else leans in to chew the ice-beads out of their eyebrows with warm, flat teeth. Around them, a drop of water ticks into verglas on a lintel, two fused pillars groan softly under the weight of the snowpack, and a thumb reaches out in the gloom, purely by reflex, to pinch off a stubborn bud before it can narrow the street.
