To live inside the bottle is to live without a horizon, and the people of the sink have bodies shaped by a world that measures distance up and down rather than out.

They are small, barrel-chested for the high thin air of the plateau above, yet sleekly muscled in the shoulders, calves, and forearms from a lifetime of three-dimensional movement. You will rarely see one of them walk heel-to-toe the way plains-people do. Even when they climb out onto the flat shield-rock to gather cobbles or harvest wild grass-seed, they move with a slightly crouched, rolling gait, knees bent, outer edges of the feet testing the stone, eyes cast down within a twenty-foot circle. Vastness makes them squint and press their thumbs into their palms; they call the open plateau *the Unroofed*, and treat it the way sailors treat the winter sea—a place of necessary harvest, barren, blinding, and lethal if you linger after the sun tilts.

Down inside the amber gloom, however, they move like water running along branches. Their bare soles are padded with horn half an inch thick, grooved and supple as warm leather, gripping the tallow-smooth granite by friction and sweat alone. Their big toes diverge slightly from the rest, prehensile enough to hook an agave-fiber cable without looking while both hands are busy carrying a gourd of warm mash or resetting a bone toggle on a storage net. Look at an elder’s elbows, hips, and shoulder blades: the skin there is buffed to a faint, hairless sheen, calloused from sixty years of chimneying up narrow stone slots and leaning back against rounded doorways to weave.

Because there is no water to waste on washing skin, they bathe in dust and oil. Every household keeps a shallow stone dish of crushed, roasted coney-fat mixed with crumbled sage leaf and the fine, astringent white powder scraped from the undersides of silver air-plants. Once a day, sitting on the edge of a catwalk with their legs dangling over forty feet of whistling, bird-crossed air, neighbors rub this paste into one another’s backs and scalps, then scrape it clean with curved ribs of bone—a dry strigil-bath that leaves their dark skin gleaming like rubbed bronze and smelling faintly of bitter camphor and warm nut-oil. The scrapings are flicked straight down into the gloom. Before a ribbon of oily dust has fallen twenty feet, moths have investigated it; within three minutes of hitting the dark mattress below, the black beetles have vanished it entirely.

Nothing in their lives is wasted because water cannot be allowed to escape the stone throat as vapor if a mouth can intercept it first.

To understand who rules here, or rather what holds the two hundred together without knives being drawn in cupboards where neighbors hear each other swallow through six inches of dry-laid cobble, you have to look at the trees. Five immense bottle-trees and three strangler figs anchor the architecture of the sink, their bloated, elephant-gray trunks wedged into the lowest crevices, rising fifty feet through the rigging like living columns before flattening their crowns beneath the overhang. Those trees are the town’s only wells. Nobody owns a house outright—houses are merely shells you maintain while breathing—but every clan holds hereditary tap-rights to specific limbs and root-buttresses.

At dawn, before the forty-minute blade of sunlight hits the east wall, the Tappers go down. They are usually the older women, slow-pulsed and steady-handed, carrying hollow bird-leg quills plugged with wax and bulbous bladders stitched from cured coney-stomachs, rubbed translucent with grease. They do not hack the trees—to bleed a fig recklessly would be slow suicide for the entire chamber. Instead, they know the swollen nodes along the trunks the way a midwife knows a belly. They pull a wooden peg from a healed scar, slip the bone quill two inches into the spongy, water-swollen cortex, and sit patiently in the dim, clicking warmth of the beetle-floor while clear, slightly sweet, mineral-heavy sap ticks drop by drop into the bladder. While they wait, big-eyed ground lizards run over their ankles, and the gray-furred conies nose at their knees hoping for a lick of salt from their shins.

That sap is their water. Nobody here has ever heard the sound of a stream, or slapped a puddle, or seen their own reflection in a flat liquid surface. Children know water only as something thick, dark-tasting, kept inside skins, gourds, or green stalks. When a person is thirsty, they do not tilt a cup and gulp; they peel a grey-green paddle of vine-leaf and chew the crisp, tart flesh until the juice coats their tongue, or they suck a measured mouthful of fig-draught through a reed tube. Even cooking is waterless: grains are parched in hot pebbles; tubers and swift-squabs and fat beetle-grubs are wrapped tightly in broad, waxy leaves and buried eighteen inches deep in the black humus of the pit floor, where the relentless biological furnace of ten million composting insects slow-bakes a meal to steaming tenderness by evening without burning a single stick of precious highland scrub.

Fire is used sparingly—tiny, smokeless embers of dried dung and woody cactus-rib cupped in thick stone bowls—mostly to roast limestone for mortar, toast grain, or feed the littlewicked oil lamps tucked into wall niches. Because smoke lingers under the stone brow before swirling out the throat, a careless fire that chokes the upper tiers is a grave humiliation. A household whose hearth smokes yellow will find four generations of neighbors leaning over the upper balustrades, clicking their tongues in sharp, rhythmic unison—*tchk-tchk-tchk*—an imitation of an angry swift-colony that silences any argument instantly.

They cannot afford shouting. In a ninety-foot granite bell that amplifies a dropped flint into a rifle-crack, loud anger is physical violence against two hundred eardrums at once. So they have evolved a split language. Across the central gulf, through the permanent, dry roar of wings, men and women speak a crisp, high-pitched tongue of short vowels and sharp whistles that cuts cleanly through the white noise like birdcall—a public shorthand for passing tools, warning of a falling pebble, or calling down that the kites have taken a drying hide. *Inside* the tiers, however, within arm's reach on the swaying agave bridges, voices drop to a breathy, rapid murmur pitched right beneath the drone of the chamber, heavily stitched together with fingers brushing wrists, chins tilting toward doorways, knuckles tapping twice against a ribcage to mean *let me pass on the wall-side*. A stranger lowered through the throat on a rope would think them eerily subdued, almost conspiratorial, until realizing that every eyebrow-flash and lip-twist is crackling with dry gossip, ancient jokes, and sideways teasing.

And they are intimately, unsentimentally entangled with their roommates.

There is no word in their speech that separates *vermin* from *livestock* or *wild* from *kept*. Everything inside the vessel is simply *the Breath*—the shared warm lung that keeps the highland freeze above the collar of stone. A boy of eight knows the individual scars on the three pairs of gray-headed kites nesting under the sunlit rim; when he skins a rock-coney on a balcony plank, he tosses the sinewy neck-scrap straight *out* into the shaft and watches a kite fold its wings, drop twenty feet through a gap in the laundry ropes, take the meat in mid-air with a clap of dry primaries, and flare back upward on the rising column of town-heat without touching a fiber. They pay that tithe gladly, because the kites keep ground-snakes and plateau-foxes from creeping down the smooth throat at night.

Watch an old weaver at her backstrap loom, braced against a rounded granite bollard on the fourth tier. Three lichen-colored geckos sleep tucked into the warm hollow behind her collarbones while she works, emerging onto her shoulders whenever a fat, dusty miller-moth flutters near her lamp-niche; she doesn't even pause the bone shuttle as a lizard sprints down her forearm, snaps the moth off her wrist, and trots back into her shawl. Above her head, packed along the cactus-rib rafters like clustered smoked pears, hang three hundred amber bats, twitching in their daylight sleep, shifting with faint, papery squeaks. Their dry, nitrogen-sharp droppings patter occasionally onto a tilted reed mat rigged specifically beneath the eaves like an awning; twice a week, her grandson sweeps that mat into a tight basket—not with disgust, though he holds his breath against the dust—and carries it down to pack around the root-collars of his clan’s bottle-tree, paying back in dark crumbs what the clan drew out in sap that morning.

Even the tiny bromeliad-frogs—no bigger than a thumbnail, marbled bronze and jade, never seen except when a woman reaches deep into a rosette of spiky leaves to harvest fiber—are treated like household gods of the smallest scale. Each giant air-plant clinging to the masonry holds perhaps half a cup of condensed breath-moisture and plant-sweat deep in its tight, overlapping leaf-axils: the *only* standing liquid in the entire world. In those hidden thimbles swim two or three transparent tadpoles, fed unfertilized eggs by their mother. To tip a great wall-bromeliad and spill its heart-cup is considered horrible luck, the carelessness of an idiot child; if a frog’s flint-sharp *tink! tink!* stops sounding outside a couple’s sleeping-cupboard at night, the wife grows uneasy and checks the leaves to see if the air around their doorway is growing too dry or smoky.

Twice a day comes *the Turning*, when human life halts completely for twelve minutes.

Everyone knows the pressure change seconds before it happens—the air in the pocket thickens slightly as the violet shadow sweeps across the plateau overhead. Mothers snatch toddlers off the outer catwalks; weavers lash down loose yarn; lamp-flames are shielded behind pierced horn screens. Then the sky-slot above begins to scream. Ten thousand cliff swifts tip out of the freezing upper dusk and plummet down the smooth stone throat at sixty miles an hour, pulling out of their dives inches above the upper catwalks in a roaring cyclone of sickle-wings, at the exact moment fifty thousand bats drop off the eaves like dark gravel, catch air ten feet down, and beat furiously upward in a tight corkscrew hugging the left-hand wall.

For twelve minutes, the town disappears inside a dry hurricane. Hanging gourds clack together; vine curtains whip horizontally; fine golden dust rises from the beetle-mattress below and turns the lantern-light into glowing bronze fog. People lean back in their oval doorways with cloths pulled over their noses, eyes crinkled shut against the gale of dander and down, feeling the rhythmic, warm *whuff-whuff-whuff* of countless small bodies grazing their bare knees and hair, listening to the granite bell ring with shrill whistles and ultrasonic ticks you feel mostly behind your teeth. Nobody speaks during the Turning. It is when tired people rest their foreheads against smooth doorframes, when lovers sitting squeezed together in storage hammocks share a peeled aloe-heart in the roaring dark.

Then, abruptly, it finishes. The last bat clears the lip into the cold stars; the last swift locks its tiny claws onto its spittle-cup overhead and tucks its beak into warm breast-feathers with a sleepy rustle that sweeps round the curved dome like settling grain. The golden dust sinks back down to the whispering black floor. *Tink,* goes a frog deep inside a wet leaf-base. *Tink-tink.* Down on the third tier, someone strikes flint to dry moss, blows an ember orange, and begins grinding parched grass-seed with a low, circular rumble against stone.

When someone dies here, they cannot be buried—the floor belongs to the roots and the beetles, and an unburned corpse would foul the warm air of the bottle in half a day. Nor is there wood enough on the barren shield-rock above to build a pyre.

Instead, wrapped tightly in an old storage hammock of agave twine, their skin rubbed one last time with sage-grease so they leave no moisture behind unless the sky takes it, the dead are carried up the longest ladder through the tallow-smooth throat at mid-morning, right when the forty-minute shaft of raw sun blazes down the east wall. Four young climbers haul the bundle out onto the blinding, wind-scoured flat of the Unroofed and carry it five hundred paces away from the sink, out where the ancient granite curves smoothly away toward nothing in every direction. They lay the body on the bare stone under the enormous, terrible bowl of pale sky, weight the netting edge with wind-polished cobbles so it won't blow before its time, and walk quickly back toward the warm musk rising out of the ground, hurrying before the cold highland gale cracks their lips—leaving the plateau kites, the sun, and the dry wind to polish the bones down as smooth and edgeless as the rock itself, until years later a grandchild gathering cobbles picks up a white rib, tests its curve against their thumb, and carries it back down into the twilight to scrape clean the living, or chink a seam in a wall.
