At first glance, looking across a sunlit span of trusswork where the fog has just burned off, you will miss them entirely. You will see a cluster of rust-colored knots wedged into the crotch of an I-beam, or what looks like a canvas tarpaulin stiffened with old grease draped over a warm condenser housing. Only when a relief valve blows twenty yards away, sending a sudden tremor through the deck plates, will the tarp lift a broad, flat head, blink a pair of dark, heavy-lidded eyes behind pale nictitating membranes, and shift its weight to let the hot condensate run underneath its belly rather than down its spine.

If they have a name for themselves, they do not speak it out loud—at least not with voices that fight the sixty-cycle hum and the shriek of the copper-winged flocks. Why shout against a boiler? Instead, if you press your ear to a iron stanchion—carefully, using a folded rag so you don’t blister your cheek—you can hear them three tiers away and two hundred feet down. *Tak-tak. Tak-tak-tak.* A knuckle rapped against a hollow pipe, a piece of scrap brass tapped twice against a rivet head, a thumbnail dragged down the threading of a three-inch bolt. They talk through the bones of the basin. Metal carries a crisp, sharp tap for half a mile right underneath the wet roar of the steam vents, arriving instantaneously in the palms and soles of anyone resting against the same web of girders.

Up close, they are people, though built to an architecture entirely dictated by thin air, verticality, and hot iron. They stand short—rarely five feet—and astonishingly wide through the ribs, carrying the deep, barrel-vaulted chests needed to pull oxygen out of an atmosphere that leaves a match gasping. Their limbs are long, corded, and slightly bowed, terminating in hands and feet that look almost interchangeable: broad paddles shod in quarter-inch pads of horn-yellow callus, cracked and blackened with embedded carbon and iron oxide. A child of six can walk barefoot along a copper busbar hot enough to sizzle spit and feel nothing more than a pleasant, sleepy tingle; an adult can hang by three fingers from the lip of a greasy flange for twenty minutes while waiting for a troop of red apes to pass overhead, their breathing slow, effortless, and deep as a bellows.

Their skin, where it isn’t dusted with gray fly ash or smeared with heavy bearing tallow against the brutal noon ultraviolet, has a dark, burnished, slightly purplish cast, rich with extra hemoglobin that shows dark plum under their fingernails and inside their lips. Their hair they keep cropped close or braided tight with strips of fibrous bark and twisted copper wire, because anything loose is an invitation to be scalped by a creeping worm-gear or snagged on a jagged gusset plate during a blind fog-out.

Watch one move through the middle tiers, where the strangler figs braid with the ladder rungs and the light falls in blinding violet shafts through shattered conservatory glass. They do not walk so much as swing, brace, and slide. Floors here are suggestions—rusted grates, narrow catwalks, the curved backs of six-foot steam mains—so these people treat gravity not as a ground condition, but as a plumb line to lean against. They wear harnesses woven from boiled liana fibers and salvaged bronze chain, fitted with wide, curved hooks made from scavenged wrench jaws or cold-hammered valve stems. When the twice-daily cloud bank crests the western baffles and turns the world into blinding, luminous milk in thirty seconds flat, nobody panics and nobody stops moving. They simply clip a hook over a familiar guide-cable, drop their weight into their harnesses, and skate along the span by touch, reading the subtle differences in temperature and vibration through their soles the way a river pilot reads currents. Every pipe has its own pulse. The main return line from the eastern condensers shudders with a triple-beat rattle; the high-pressure bypass sings in D-flat and smells faintly of hot linseed oil and crushed fern. You cannot get lost in the fog when the entire world is singing its coordinates into your ankles.

To think of them merely as scavengers living in the ruins of a machine would be to misunderstand both them and the basin. Nothing here is ruined; it is simply *settled*, and its inhabitants are less like squatters in an abandoned factory and more like tiny, meticulous crabs living in the seams of a whale’s baleen. They have no memory of who built the piers, nor any curiosity about what lies beneath the white wool of the cloud deck thousands of feet below. Down is simply where the heat and the dark oil come from, rising on that forty-second *thud-hiss* stroke that has beaten like a mother’s heart behind every breath every one of them has ever drawn.

Yet they are intimately, obsessively involved with the machinery’s moods. 

Follow an older woman—her joints thickened by decades of vertical climbing, a belt of porcelain insulators and bone chisels clinking softly at her hip—as she works her way along a sweating brass manifold three levels below the canopy. She isn’t trying to fix anything, and she certainly wouldn’t dare turn a major wheel; even their strongest men couldn't budge a main gate-valve encrusted with two centuries of lime scale, and besides, the machine knows its own business. Instead, she is gardening the leaks. 

With a hooked iron bar, she pries a wedge of calcified scale out of a weeping flange joint, widening a hairline hiss into a steady, rhythmic drip. Below it, wedged into the dished iron pan of an idle clutch housing, she has packed three bushels of rich black leaf-mold, bird guano, and crushed rust. Into this warm, dark mash she presses tubers the size of clenched fists—pale, knobby roots cultivated nowhere else on earth, bred over uncounted generations to thrive on iron-heavy muck and eighty-degree water. Around the rim of the housing, she smears a ring of pungent, bitter grease skimmed from a sump pit two tiers down; the metallic-winged beetles hate the smell, and the forearm-long lizards won't cross it because it fouls their toe-pads. In six weeks, before the great rotary valve at the valley’s center clicks over its next tooth and sends this pipe line into cool dormancy, she will harvest forty pounds of dense, starchy root, wrap them in broad fern leaves, and parboil them by wedging the bundles directly into the exhaust collar of a low-pressure steam trap.

They have no fire. At this altitude, fire is a weak, sputtering, greedy thing that eats precious wood and gives back pale heat and choking smoke. Why bother with flame when heat is the single most abundant substance in the universe? 

Their kitchens are clustering points along the great brick flues and glazed-tile chimneys. Whole extended families—twenty or thirty people, organized around the matrilineal inheritance of prime thermal real estate—camp on wide timber-and-vine platforms lashed around the warm brickwork. Here, slices of green-and-copper bird breast, fatty beetle grubs, and split fig-hearts are laid out on scrubbed slate tiles or flat iron inspection covers to slow-roast or dehydrate in the dry, bread-oven radiance. Water is caught in cascades of split-bamboo troughs and folded sheet-lead funnels hung beneath condenser coils, filtering down through beds of charcoal and moss into riveted copper cisterns to strip out the metallic tang of the pipes. 

Because everything is warm, nothing rots if you put it in the sun, and everything ferments overnight if you put it in the shade. Three tiers down, in the perpetual green gloom beneath the viaducts where the frogs deafen the ear, they keep heavy stoneware crocks—salvaged battery jars of thick green glass, mostly, or hollowed-out sections of vitrified clay sewer pipe plugged with pitch. In these they brew a thick, sour, mildly alcoholic mash of crushed bromeliad hearts, tree-sap, and toasted insect meal, kept at a perpetual blood-warm simmer by resting the jars on the humming casings of step-down transformers. The sixty-cycle vibration agitates the yeast continuously; they claim a batch brewed on a dead stone tastes flat and cowardly.

Watch them when the great central distributor valve prepares to shift. 

Long before a stranger would notice anything at all, the people feel the prelude. For three days beforehand, the tension in the mile-long overhead tie-rods changes pitch, rising a quarter-tone as deep internal pressures redistribute down in the brick-lined bellies of the basin. The frogs in the lower vaults stop chiming all at once for ten minutes at dusk—a sudden, eerie hole in the wall of sound that makes infants wake up crying from the quiet. 

Nobody hurries, because nothing in the bowl hurries, yet a purposeful choreography begins across the north terrace. Families unlash their woven vine hammocks and roll up their reed mats. Young men and women with broad tumplines across their foreheads load up the heavy glass battery jars, the bundles of dried meat, the prized bronze pry-bars, and the baskets of seed-spores and dormant moss plugs. Toddlers too small to make the long cable-crossings are tucked into wicker panniers slung beneath their fathers’ chests, peering out through the lattice with solemn, dark-rimmed eyes as their parents walk the high trusses over four-hundred-foot drops to the turbine floors.

They travel alongside the lizards and just behind the red-furred primates, sharing the wide girder-highways without malice. In a world packed this tightly, predation is polite and strictly seasonal. A hunter might take two or three young monkeys with a heavy, spring-steel crossbow bolt if a troop raids a tuber-pan, and everyone snares the screaming green birds by the dozen using slip-nooses greased with sticky fig-latex laid along their favorite warm roosting-pipes. But while moving along the trestles during a valve-shift, a truce holds. You will see an old grandfather sitting on the sun-bleached housing of a traveling crane to catch his breath, sharing the warm iron plate with four yards of iridescent monitor lizard, casually tossing the reptile a pinched-off beetle head as a troop of apes swings past ten feet below, showering them both in red rust flakes.

At the very center of the basin, where the distributor valve itself sits inside a roofless, vine-choked rotunda of vitrified brick, a few dozen people usually gather to witness the click. 

These are mostly the *listeners*—older men and women whose hearing in the air has long since been ruined by a lifetime of roaring exhaust vents, leaving them locked in a serene inner silence broken only by what comes up through their bones. They lean wooden staffs against the massive cast-iron shroud of the valve gear, pressing their chins or thumbnails to the wood, eyes closed against the hard violet glare of the noon sky. 

Down in the grease-pit, a counterweight the size of a cottage creeps downward the final fraction of a millimeter on its gleaming, self-oiling worm screw. A pawl slips free. With a deep, liquid *clunk* that hits the sternum like a swung mallet and sends ten thousand green birds exploding upward into the thin, bright air like thrown copper coins, the toothed wheel advances. 

Instantly, the pitch of the rotunda changes. A cavernous sigh travels outward through the south-side mains as live steam hits cold iron, knocking and booming through a mile of expansion loops, blowing white plumes of condensation out of a thousand dusty bleed-cocks that haven’t wept since winter. On the north side, the long, wet hiss fades to a tick, then a murmur. 

The listeners open their eyes, smile faintly with dark, plum-colored lips, and tap twice on the casing with their staffs—not in worship, exactly, though not entirely without gratitude either. Just acknowledging the stroke. Then they hoist their baskets onto their wide backs, hook their safety lines onto the south-bound suspension cables, and lean out into the warm rising mist, following the water home.
