Look closer at the concrete floor when that current shifts, right down in the four-inch expansion seams where the poured slabs meet. Before the kelp ribbons have even finished swinging to the new bearing, something else adjusts. Along the lip of the seam, spaced every ten or twelve yards like surveyor’s stakes, sit heavy, wedge-shaped sculpins the exact mottled slate-and-aggregate gray of wet Portland cement. They do not swim so much as skid. When the flow ticks five degrees south, a sculpin lifts its broad, leathery pectoral fins—whose undersides are calloused thick as boot-soles from gripping rough trowel-marks—lets the water push its blunt head onto the new vector, and clamps back down. They have grown strangely flat and dense in ten years here, bred by the sheer absence of mud; any hatchling too buoyant or too slow to wedge its chin behind a protruding pebble of aggregate gets swept three miles down-slab into the intake screens before its yolk sac is gone.

Everything that survives in this basin is learning the rules of hard edges and constant velocity. There is no mud to hide in, no weeds thick enough to confuse an eye, and no silence in which to stalk by ear. Evolution hasn't had time to build new bones, so instead it is ruthlessly sorting behaviors and stripping away old habits within three or four generations.

Consider the lateral line—that seam of pressure-sensitive pores running down a fish’s flank. In a natural lake or coastal bay, a bass or a pike uses it to feel the tiny hydraulic twitch of a minnow turning six feet away. Here, the fifty-cycle turbine hum and the hammering of check-valves flood every nerve ending with continuous white noise. The first generation of river-cod sucked through the eastern spillways went half-mad from it, swimming in stunned, erratic spirals near the casemate walls until starvation or the pumps took them. Their grandchildren are functionally deaf to anything quieter than a freight train, and entirely serene about it. They hunt strictly by geometry and light. 

Watch the packs of dark-backed mackerel hanging beneath the submerged catwalks. They have abandoned the loose, herding encirclements their ocean cousins use. Instead, they treat the crisp shadow-edges cast by the overhead sodium floods as solid walls. Five or six of them will line up nose-to-tail along the exact razor line where bright water meets grey shadow, trembling slightly to hold station against a four-knot drift. When the three-note acoustic horn rings out through the basin, the baitfish—still carrying an ancestral panic reflex—flinch downward as a single silver sheet. The mackerel don't flinch at all. For them, those three brassy notes are a dinner bell. They launch the instant the first harmonic hits their swim bladders, slicing into the momentarily disorganized cloud of herring while the bronze echo is still bouncing back off the northern bulkhead.

Around the big tension-cables—those freight-train-thick bundles of braided galvanized wire slicing diagonally through the mid-water—a whole vertical neighborhood has arranged itself around vibration. The cables hum at very low frequencies depending on the load on the retaining walls miles away, sometimes thrumming slow and tight like a plucked bass string, sometimes shuddering faintly when a sluice opens. Clinging to the grease-free patches between the wire twists are colonies of freshwater bryozoans and small, translucent shrimp that arrived as microscopic larvae in ballast water. The shrimp march up and down the steel strands in hundred-yard columns, grazing on the thin, slick biofilm of diatoms that grows where the arc-lamps strike the zinc coating. Hovering two feet off the steel, tilted at the exact forty-five-degree angle of the cable itself, are slender, needle-jawed gar and juvenile whitefish. To someone drifting past, it looks eerily wrong: dozens of fish suspended at a steep, uniform slant in the middle of open aqua space, oriented to the cable’s gravity rather than the floor’s, picking off shrimp whenever a structural shudder shakes a few loose into the slipstream.

Farther down, where the secondary sump channels drop a clean hundred feet at a ninety-degree angle, live the heavyweights—the founders. 

These are the animals that came in during the first filling fifteen years ago as fingerlings or two-pound juveniles and grew enormous in a cold, hyper-oxygenated room with unlimited herring and nowhere to spend energy except pushing against water. Down in the corner of Sector 4, tucked into the hydraulic dead-zone behind a twelve-foot cast-iron sluice housing, lies a sturgeon nearly eleven feet long. Its scutes—the bony armor plates along its back—are worn smooth and bright on the left side from a decade of leaning against bare concrete while sleeping in the eddy. There is no silt here for a sturgeon’s barbels to sift through, so it has changed its trade entirely. It waits for the *thwump* of the floor-plate air vents, or for the violent shear-squalls when the afternoon cold front collides with warm condenser water. Those cyclonic eddies act like giant broom-sweeps across miles of concrete, gathering up dead baitfish, molted crab shells, waterlogged moths fallen from the dry light-galleries high above, and torn ribbons of kelp, dropping them in neat, swirling windrows along the base of the three-foot baffle walls. Half an hour after the eastern pump-batteries cycle up, the big sturgeon pulls out of its iron corner and cruises the baffle line like a street-sweeper, vacuuming the clean concrete crease with a mouth wide as a bucket, accompanied by an escort of thick-shouldered, carp-colored suckers that dart in to snatch whatever tumbles past its gills.

Even the invertebrates have taken on an industrial cast. The long-legged crabs marching across the *CLEARANCE 80 FT* stencil are spider crabs from cold estuary mouths outside, creatures that normally spend hours gluing bits of sponge, shell, and red seaweed onto the Velcro-like hooks of their carapaces to vanish into a rocky bottom. Here, on acres of spotless black anti-fouling paint and bare grey slab, a crab covered in red weed stands out like a flare—and the diving cormorants or patrolling cod snap them up immediately. The ones still walking around the manifold blockhouse have decorated themselves with what the basin provides. One carries three bright orange flakes of bolt-rust and a curled shaving of white PVC pipe-thread tape hooked neatly over its eyes; another is tufted entirely in pale gray strings of cured polysulfide sealant picked from an expansion joint, rendering it completely invisible the moment it squats flat against the cement.

And high up—thirty fathoms above the slab, where the water meets whatever roof contains this place—there is another layer entirely, half-aquatic, half-structural.

It turns out there *is* an airspace up there: a twenty-foot gap between the restless, choppy surface of this indoor sea and the vast ceiling of riveted steel trusses, catwalks, and wired-glass light bays. It smells sharply of cold spray, ozone from the arc-lamp ballasts, and damp iron. Up on the horizontal flanges of the I-beams, dry and warm directly above the heat-radiating hoods of the sodium floods, sit the cormorants and grebes in rows of black silhouettes. They build nests up there out of stiff, dried ribbons of the brown expansion-seam kelp, woven together with lost zip-ties, copper wire scraps left by electricians ten years back, and long white wing-feathers. Their guano streaks down the steel stanchions into the water, creating the only pockets of rich chemical fertilizer in the entire sterile volume; right beneath each bird-roost gantry, the green filamentous algae explodes into thick, streaming beards twenty feet long, which in turn shelter dense nurseries of fingernail-sized fry—minnows, tiny transparent perch, baby pipefish—hugging the shadow of the trusses while the cold swells slap rhythmically against the girder webs.

Seals have found their way in, too. Nobody in the control rooms miles away quite knows how—perhaps through an unscreened overflow culvert during a winter storm surge five years back—but there are seven of them now, harbor seals gone sleek and pale-eyed in the artificial noon. They haul out on the flat steel tops of submerged buoyancy pontoons that ride just awash near the intake towers, barking back at the groan of the worm-gears, their voices ringing weird and metallic across miles of steel ceiling. When they dive, they drop straight down the two-hundred-foot vertical masonry cylinders like torpedoes, using the steel guide-rails as visual ladders into the deep glare. You can see one from half a mile off: a dark, supple comma twisting effortlessly through a stiff, rectilinear world of cable and concrete, chasing a four-pound striped bass around the square pillars of a pipe-bridge while clouds of silver bubbles from a floor-vent roar upward past them both.

What is strangest, though, is what happens at two in the morning when the grid load drops outside and three-quarters of the overhead sodium banks click off at once.

The basin doesn't go dark—pilot floods and green navigation beacons on the towers stay lit, turning the endless water into a vast, dim cathedral of bottle-green glass—and the fifty-cycle hum never stops, though it drops a third in pitch as the big impellers throttle back to idle. With the current slowing from four knots to a lazy drift of half a mile an hour, the relentless horizontal tension lets go. 

The kelp ribbons along the floor seams slowly rise from flat-out pennants and stand upright across the miles of slab, swaying gently like sparse, dark wheat in a field of stone. The great clouds of silver herring, pinned all day behind steel cross-bracing, expand outward until they fill whole cubic acres of open water with a faint, drifting stardust that catches the green beacon-light. Eels—thick, bronze-bellied things nobody ever sees in the bright noon glare—slide out of weeping four-inch drainage weep-holes in the retaining walls by the hundreds, undulating lazily out across the crisp ninety-degree corners of the sumps. Up near the manifold blockhouse, millions of tiny, nearly invisible mysid shrimp lift off the bare aggregate floor and migrate twenty fathoms upward toward the dim glow of the wired glass, a silent vertical snowstorm rising in reverse.

For four hours, until the morning demand kicks the eastern pump-batteries back into their roaring stride and the arc-lamps strike white again with a muffled *thump* overhead, the inhabitants drift freely across the sharp angles and serial-numbered iron—completely native to a sea without mud, sky, or history, waiting comfortably in the hum of the walls for the morning weather to be switched back on.
