Stand on one of the glazed lava blisters for twenty minutes without shifting your weight, and the bruised-violet shelf of leaves fifty yards to your left will slowly, smoothly lower itself by four inches. 

At first it looks as though a hollow tumulus has given way beneath the root-mat, though there has been no cracking sound beneath the steady sizzle of the chorus. Then the neighboring shelf rises slightly, parting its heavy, dark-red paddles just enough for a slick curve of pale slate—wide as a butcher’s block, rubbery, beaded with mineral sweat—to roll upward through the gap, shed a cascade of disturbed millipedes and tiled snails without hurting one of them, and sink back out of sight. Ten seconds later, the leaves settle back into edge-to-edge alignment. Every antenna goes right back to brushing its neighbor.

Nothing big here can afford to have corners, and nothing big can afford to keep its blood warmer than the rock. What moves through the lower story of the basin is built on the logic of thick dough poured slowly through a sieve.

They are called *flense-backs* only if you have never touched one; there is nothing sharp or torn about them. Imagine a creature descended from some forgotten burrowing reptile or giant caecilian that gave up digging when there ceased to be dirt, and instead adapted to swimming through a solid weave of pressurized roots, jagged obsidian, and living meat. A full-grown adult is twelve to fifteen feet long and wide as a draft horse across the middle, yet scarcely eighteen inches thick when relaxed—less a cylinder than a heavy, muscular mattress of dense gray-violet gristle. It has no limbs, and its ribs are disconnected floating splints of cartilage buried deep in gelatinous fat, allowing the entire body to fold lengthwise, pour sideways through a six-inch crack between basalt ropes, or flatten itself thin enough to slide underneath a tightly woven root-canopy without snapping a single woody cable.

You almost never see a whole one at once. You see a yard of glistening, slate-colored flank sliding silently across a pearlescent snail-highway while its head is already two yards away down a steam-vent hollow and its tail is still wedged inside a flooded lava saucer three hummocks back. 

Up close, their skin is extraordinary. Raw basalt glass would ribbon ordinary hide in ten feet, and the gastropods’ hardened mucus-highways only cover the exposed crests. Down among the root-anchors, every crevice is lined with black surgical blades. Rather than growing heavy armor scales—which would catch on roots and trap fatal metabolic heat—the great crawlers secrete a continuous, half-inch-thick mantle of dense, rubberized mucilage bound together by shed keratin fibers, something between whale blubber and vulcanized tire tread. When a razor-edge of stone slices into a crawler’s belly as it hauls its bulk over a ridge, the cut parts only this dead, elastic outer rind. Immediately, milky fluid wells from dermal pores underneath, curing on contact with the sulfur-heavy air to weld the gash shut behind it—or, if a flake of volcanic glass snaps off inside the cut, encapsulating the shard in a smooth, rubbery pearl that is slowly pushed outward over weeks until it drops harmlessly into a water-saucer. Older individuals are pebbled all along their undersides with hundreds of these dark, encapsulated obsidian splinters, wearing the basin’s teeth on the outside of their skin.

They do not breathe the scalding broth of the air more than once every quarter-hour. Lungfuls that hot bring too little oxygen for the work of pumping ribs. Instead, running down both flattened margins of the body are broad, ruffled aprons of vascularized skin, deep maroon against their slate hides, kept constantly wet by the condensate dripping from the leaves overhead. As the animal inches forward—never faster than a minute hand’s creep—it ripples these side-frills through the lukewarm water-saucers and wet bladder-mosses, trading carbon dioxide directly into the mist while two wide, valved spiracles high on its blunt, eyeless head open every ten or twelve minutes with a wet, hydraulic *huff*—a sound like a heavy bellows sighing underwater—to clear stale gas and draw a slow draft of dense air.

To watch one feed is to understand why nothing in the basin bothers to run away.

It does not hunt. Hunting requires sudden contraction of muscle, and three minutes of thrashing in air this hot and saturated would cook an animal that size from the inside out before its blood could shed the heat. Instead, the crawler simply *grazes the density*. Its head is a broad, blind shovel of sensory pits—detecting heat-gradients down to a fiftieth of a degree, tasting dissolved nitrates and hormone-slicks in the vapor—with a wide, ventral mouth tucked underneath like a bottom-feeding skate’s. As its forward edge slides over a packed saucer of coiled worms and ribless amphibians, or pushes through a curtain of heavy millipedes, it doesn't snap. It yawns open a soft, muscular vacuum-throat and takes a measured, unhurried *third* of whatever occupies that cubic foot: three or four frogs, six palm-snails shells and all, a double handful of arthropods, two quarts of nutrient jelly from a liquefying frond, and a mouthful of gelatinous liverwort. 

Then the mouth closes, and the forward lip keeps sliding on. It never strips a pocket bare. A bare patch of stone would take weeks to recolonize to peak thickness; skimming the cream off an overcrowded saucer leaves the survivors with half an inch more breathing room, triggering an immediate spate of spawning and molting behind the crawler’s tail that restores the exact weight of flesh within days. Inside its slow, cavernous gut—which functions less like a furnace and more like a warm fermentation vat running at ambient rock-temperature—gizzard-pads of hardened cartilage quietly crush snail shell into calcium paste, passing it out hours later as warm, mineral-rich chalk-ribbons that the flat-shelled gastropods immediately swarm over and rasp up to build new shells and fresh highway glaze.

Even its back is real estate. Because a mature crawler moves only forty or fifty yards in a full turn of the dim, unshifting sky, and frequently lies completely still for twenty hours digesting in a warm hollow, its broad dorsal surface supports its own traveling roof-garden. Translucent mites and pale, flattened pseudo-scorpions live permanently in the creases behind its spiracles, keeping its sensory pits immaculate of fungal mold. Across its broad, damp spine grow mats of specialized, fast-rooting liverworts found nowhere on the stationary stone—plants whose rhizoids grip rubbery keratin rather than basalt, riding slowly from vent to vent to bathe in richer sulfur plumes than stationary plants ever taste. Small, blind frogs sit in rows along the moving ridge of its spine, calling uninterruptedly as their mount glides beneath overhanging aubergine leaves, plucking heavy arthropods right off the undersides of the canopy as the crawler ferries them past like barges down a canal.

Yet the crawlers are only the muscle of the basin’s lower floor. Stay longer—until the steady, sizzling roar of the million wet clocks begins to sort itself out inside your skull, until your ears stop hearing the surface noise and drop into the sub-audible pressure beneath it—and you realize something else is using the stone itself.

Drop to your knees beside a steam-crack and press an ear against a glazed snail-ridge. Beneath the bass drone of the blister-frogs, there is a deliberate, dry *tink... tink... tink-tink* traveling cleanly through the solid basalt. High-frequency, crisp, glassy. Like someone tapping the rim of a crystal goblet with a bone needle half a mile underground.

Thefresh lava pan is only solid on top. When a basalt flood miles wide cools from the crust downward, the molten interior drains and shrinks unevenly, leaving behind an immense, chaotic honeycomb just beneath the surface: blister-caves, cooling-contraction fissures inches wide, collapsed gas tubes, and flat horizontal crawlspaces suspended between glassy strata where the air is six degrees hotter than the surface and completely cut off from the dim sky.

Down in that pitch-black sub-crust live the *glass-wrights*, though you will only ever see their fingers above ground.

They are social crustaceans—distant kin to the soft-shelled vent-fanners, diverged down into the cooling rock the moment the crust hardened enough not to boil them—built with the terrifying, attenuated precision of cave-spiders forged out of porcelain. A single individual’s body is no larger than a clenched fist, pale and translucent enough to show the slow blue-gray pulse of its copper-based blood, perched in the dark upon eight spindly, yard-long walking legs segmented with triple joints so they can fold flat inside a two-inch cooling crack. Where surface creatures wrap themselves in slime or rubber to survive the obsidian edges, the glass-wrights have gone the opposite way: their leg-tips are shod in replaceable caps of dense iron-sulfide mineral drawn from the rising hydrothermal water, harder than the basalt itself, allowing them to skate effortlessly along razor-sharp ridges in pitch blackness without a scratch.

They never come onto the surface whole; the open pan is too crowded with heavy, clumsy crawlers that would snap their brittle stilt-legs by rolling over. Instead, a colony of several thousand occupies an interconnected labyrinth of gas-blisters beneath three or four acres of surface hummocks, farming the boundary where the two worlds meet.

Look closely at any hairline crack where warm water sweats up onto the stone, or peer beneath the dense root-canvas wrapping a tumulus, and you will see their work. Left to themselves, hydrothermal cooling-cracks clog up within months—choked by precipitating silica, bacterial slime, or the muscular, strangling roots of the aubergine-leafed shrubs pushing downward for water. If the cracks sealed, the rising moisture would stop, the closed circulation of mist would collapse, and the surface above that acre would starve down to bare, dry glass.

The wrights do not let them seal. 

Part two overlapping leaves above a weeping fissure and hold perfectly still. Within half a minute, two pale, articulated forelimbs—long as knitting needles, multi-jointed, tipped with delicate, serrated shears like surgical forceps—will slide silently up out of the hairline crack in the rock. They move with an eerie, stop-motion economy that belongs to a cooler, sharper mind than anything up in the warm broth above. Blind, guided entirely by vibration and chemical taste along bristles fine as blown glass, the two pale arms feel along the stone lip of the crack. One snip severs an encroaching root-hair trying to wedge into the fissure; the root bleeds a drop of sweet, pressurized sap, which a third, sponge-tipped mouth-tendril immediately reaches up to dab dry before retreating. Next, the forceps deftly chip away a crust of mineral scale narrowing the water’s exit, widening the stone lip by a fraction of a millimeter so the warm seepage spills precisely leftward—directly into a shallow saucer six inches away where a braided ball of gill-worms is beginning to run low on water.

*Tink. Tink.*

That is the sound running through the rock beneath your boots: millions of iron-shod forceps across miles of subterranean blister-vaults, trimming stone, flaking volcanic glass to redirect condensation trickles, tapping coded acoustic tremors into the crisp, unbroken basalt to coordinate flow pressures across whole colonies. Solid volcanic glass transmits vibration better than cast iron; a sentry wright feeling the heavy weight of a fifteen-foot crawler sliding across the roof of vault forty yards north taps three sharp clicks against a ceiling strut, and instantly five hundred pale arms across half an acre retract half an inch below the surface cracks until the heavy rubber belly has glided safely overhead, then slide straight back up into the mist to resume pruning.

Down in their dark blisters, where the heat rises raw from the deeper stone, they hang from the glassy ceilings in overlapping pale chandeliers around subterranean steam-chimneys, passing droplets of root-sap and harvested surface-mites mouth-to-mouth. When an aged snail-crawler or blind frog dies near a wider vent-crevice, dozens of pale, needle-thin arms reach up out of the black stone together like stiff grass blades in reverse, systematically disarticulating the body from underneath in silence—leaving the upper skin intact like a deflated tent until the very end so surface scavengers don’t crowd the opening, drawing slivers of meat and bone down into the cracks to fertilize subterranean fungal gardens that glow with a faint, pointless greenish bioluminescence no eye down there has lenses left to see.

And then there are the blisters that do not ring hollow when tapped, and do not hold frogs.

Walk half a mile out toward the middle of the pan, where the pewter cloud-ceiling presses lowest and the horizon disappears completely into hot amber-gray fog, and you begin to notice occasional tumuli—low domes of buckled lava six or eight feet wide—where the overlapping shelves of dark-purple leaves grow noticeably darker, almost pure lampblack, and swell to twice their usual thickness. Around the base of these specific mounds, the pearlescent snail-highways curve politely wide. Even the great, blind mattress-crawlers alter their slow compass ten feet out, pouring their slate-colored bulk down neighboring troughs rather than sliding across the crest.

Nothing looks threatening about the mound. The chorus is just as loud around it; arthropods still bead the waxy black leaves, and capture-sheets still glisten between the stems. Only if you crouch beside the stone skirt of the blister do you notice that the warm water seeping out from its base does not evaporate cleanly three feet away. It smells faintly of sweet vinegar and crushed almonds, and it carries microscopic, glistening threads—not spider silk, and not plant root, and not fungal hyphae, though it borrows tricks from all three.

Break open one of those black-leafed tumuli—if you had an iron bar and the stomach to swing it in heat that makes your vision swim after three blows—and you would not find a hollow stone bubble inside. You would find a single, uninterrupted mass of trembling, amber-rose flesh filling the volcanic dome from wall to glassy wall like marrow packed inside a knucklebone.

It has no skeleton, no eyes, no front, no brain, and it cannot move an inch from the lava bubble its microscopic spore washed into fifty or five hundred years ago when a crawler tracked it there on its belly. In truth, nobody looking at it could tell you cleanly whether it started out as a colonial tunicate stranded inland from some primordial sea, or a monstrously derived slime-mold that learned how to build permanent organs, or an amphibian lineage that gave up hatching out of the egg-mass altogether and simply fused ten thousand siblings into a single, immortal digestive brain.

Whatever its ancestry, a *hearth-mass* lives entirely by chemistry and plumbing.

Filling a stone chamber just above a deep thermal crack, its core sits bathed in water near scalding, drawing heat-energy that would kill mobile tissue and using complex sulfur-binding enzymes to power a breathless, relentless chemical factory. From its gelatinous bulk inside the dark dome, thousands of muscular, pale-pink stolons—thin as capillary tubes, tough as tendon—thread outward through every porous gas-bubble and hairline fracture in the basalt blister, spreading thirty feet in every direction beneath the root-mats and snail-glaze.

It does not eat anything around its mound. Instead, it *tunes* them.

Through those hidden capillaries, the hearth-mass leaks precise cocktails of tailored hormones, amino acids, sugars, and mild alcaloids directly into the root-nets of the shrubs above it and into the shallow water-saucers ringing its stone skirt. To the aubergine-leafed plants anchored over its dome, it feeds concentrated nitrates and phosphorus scavenged from the deep mineral water; in return, its capillaries pierce right into the plants' vascular phloem, drinking half the sugars those dark, oversized leaves manufacture from the dim, directionless sky, while subtly altering the leaves’ sap chemistry so they sweat twice as much sweet exudate onto their stems.

That extra sweetness draws thick, hanging curtains of the heavy millipede-things and wingless beetles—far denser on a hearth-mound than anywhere else. They crowd the black leaves, feeding peacefully, breathing, excreting nitrogen-rich frass that melts straight down through the root-mat into waiting funnel-mouths of pink tissue tucked out of sight between the basalt ropes. 

Nor does the mass stop at fertilizer. The faint almond-and-vinegar scent in its runoff water is a calmative pheromone tuned to the nervous systems of the surrounding arthropods and amphibians. Within twenty feet of a hearth-mound, metabolic rates drop by five percent; ambush-crawlers grow sluggish and pass by; glass-wrights working the cracks underneath find beadlets of pure, extruded lipid left neatly beside the fissures for them, bribing the blind crustaceans to keep the mound’s deep hydrothermal vents scraped spotlessly open to the exact flow-rate the core prefers—not hot enough to parboil its underside, never cool enough to slow its chemistry.

When a beetle or frog living on the mound eventually wears out—after living considerably longer than its cousins fifty yards away—and drops limp into the slick crotch of a stem, fine pink threads emerge from beneath the bladder-moss within minutes, wrap the quiet body like slow fingers, dissolve it cleanly without spilling a drop of rot into the air, and draw the soup back inside the stone dome. 

Across the whole sunken pan, spaced three or four hundred yards apart like quiet governors on a steam engine, thousands of these sealed domes sit pulsing invisibly beneath the purple-black shelves. When one grows full to bursting inside its stone shell, it doesn't split the rock; for two days, its capillaries pump a milky fog of microscopic, lipid-coated buds up into a nearby steam-vent, letting the rising vapor carry them ten yards sideways before they condense onto the rubbery back of a passing crawler, riding for months through the wet labyrinth until one washes off into a newly vacated, uncolonized lava bubble where an old frog-chorus died out or a fresh cooling-crack just wept water for the first time.

Stand back up on your glazed ridge and look out across the miles of dark olive and bruised violet dissolving into pewter steam. 

Nothing appears to have changed since you looked down. The light has neither brightened nor failed. The low, wide pan still hums with its deafening, grease-in-a-skillet roar; the forty leathery crustaceans around the vent at your boots are still sweeping their feathered mouthparts in locked, clockwork unison—*left, draw, tuck; left, draw, tuck*—every mite clinging beneath their chins pulsing in time. Thirty yards off, the leaves dip four inches and rise again as another slow ton of rubbery slate slides unhurriedly over razor-sharp obsidian without spilling a drop of blood. Beneath the soles of your shoes, a crystal *tink-tink* runs sideways through black glass, widening a warm trickle by the breadth of an eyelash, while forty paces away a dark dome of stone breathes out sweet vinegar vapor to keep ten thousand crowded jaws chewing at the exact speed new leaves unroll.

There is no silt, no wind, no season, no chase, and nowhere for anything to go. The whole basin is a single, steaming animal with ten billion mouths and stone for bones, digesting water and sulfur and starved amber light in place, forever young because nothing is ever allowed to stop long enough to weather.
