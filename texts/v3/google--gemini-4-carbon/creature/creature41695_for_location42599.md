If you stay still against the northern buttress for long enough—if your boots stop scraping the oiled stone and the sharp, panicked wet smell of your own sweat begins to bake away into the ambient musk—the wall behind your shoulder blades will eventually exhale, unstick itself from your shirt, and reach around to feel the shape of your ribs.

Those tally-grooves on the basalt were not left by visitors from down the mountain. No lung grown in the cold scree outside could endure twenty minutes of the vault’s sulfurous, chalk-laden furnace without seizing in wet coughing, and wet coughing here would bring a dozen ribbon-hunters sliding up your shins before you reached the floor, drawn madly to the impossible luxury of free aerosolized water. 

The carvers never left. They are still here. There are eleven of them at present, wedged into the mid-wall tiers between the third and fourth ledges, though unless a cluster of static sparks happens to fire in quick succession along the eastern overhang, you could press your palm flat against the small of a woman’s back and think her only a larger, slightly bonier gleaner settled into a cooling cleft.

They are human, or were, three or four thousand generations of mountain-isolation ago, before the snowline crept down the massif or the valleys dried to salt-pans and drove a small band of stone-carriers backward up into the warm chimney-throats to survive an ice age that never quite let them go. Now the vault has had its way with them, just as it had its way with the blind things coiled in the offering niche and the flagstone-grazers clamped to the vents.

In the brief, violet snapshot of a static flash, you would not see faces first; you would see knees, shoulders, and broad, grey-buff backs folded together like drought-shrunken leather. They are small—scarcely four feet long if one were ever stretched out flat, though none of them has ever been stretched out flat from birth to death—and extraordinarily wide across the pelvis and ribcage, flattened front-to-back like creatures meant to live between the pages of a closed book. Their joints have a loose, boneless compliance. A grown man can fold his heels behind his own scapulae or collapse his collarbone inward to slip sideways through a gap between two sleeping brooder-mats without waking either.

Their skin has forgotten sweat entirely. Every sweat gland vanished millennia ago, sealed over into a thick, chamois-soft hide dusted with a fine, self-secreted talcum of waxy keratin that keeps them from sticking to the scalding flanks of the vent-grazers when they climb down to the floor. Over their shoulders, spines, and the crowns of their skulls grows a dense, short pile—not hair as valley people have it, but flat, overlapping filaments like mole-plush, warm taupe and iron-grey, brushed permanently downward by centuries of sliding feet-first into crevices. When two of them shift against each other in the dark, they make the exact same dry *shhh-fluff* sound as everything else in the room.

They have no eyes worth mentioning—only faint, sunken dimples of unpigmented suede beneath heavy brow-ridges that now serve purely as bumpers to protect the temples against jutting trachyte. Instead, their upper lips, cheekbones, and the backs of their knuckles are wide, pillowy pads of naked, velvety tissue, packed so densely with trigeminal heat-receptors and electrostatic hairs that a child can hold an open hand six inches above a packed ledge and read the whole tangled crowd below by its radiant warmth: the white-hot seethe of the fissure-rims, the steady forty-two-degree loaves of the pulsing brooders, the cooler, furtive ribbon of a hunter slipping underneath, and the faint, prickly static halo of a glider banking past behind her neck.

Their nostrils are narrow, muscular slits lined with dry, pleated baffles of cartilage that recapture ninety-eight percent of the moisture from every breath before it leaves the head. When they exhale, the air that comes out of them is as parched and hot as kiln-exhaust, smelling faintly of toasted tallow and bitter salt. Even their mouths seldom open to the room. Their lips seal with a waxy seam; when they eat, they press their mouths directly against their quarry—a soft-shelled gleaner plucked from a grazer’s back, or a fat-engorged mite-cluster peeled out of a rock seam—and chew with broad, flat-crowned molars inside a closed vacuum, swallowing paste, hide, cartilage, and bone-salt alike so nothing is surrendered to the air.

Because they do not speak—not with air—their throats have grown quiet and shallow, the larynx shrunk to a little clicks-and-valves stopper used mostly to keep chalk-dust out of the lungs. Yet they are endlessly, intricately talkative. 

In a world where everyone is already touching something, the whole chamber is a telegraph wire. Two of the vault-people wedged forty feet apart along the northern buttress can converse across thirty intervening yards of packed gleaners and sleeping brooders without either of them moving an inch. One presses the heel of his palm against the flank of a massive, torpid ledge-grazer and taps a rapid, syncopated flutter of muscle-tremors—*thrum-thrum, slide, pinch, pause, double-thrum*—tuned to a low frequency that travels cleanly through dense, waxy flesh without triggering a predator’s startle-reflex. Three seconds later, twenty bodies away, an elder resting her jaw against the polished trachyte ridge feels the cadence arrive through her cheekbones, filtered and softened by a dozen alien bellies along the way, and answers with a slow, rolling flex of her toes against the rock.

When they are together—which is often, knotted three or four deep inside those low, round enclosures of dry-stacked slate—their language becomes something richer and stranger than speech. Those slate wells are not pens for livestock, nor hearths; they are *coolers* and nurseries, built precisely where the sinking outer-wall downdrafts pool just enough thirty-five-degree air to keep an infant’s brain from cooking during fever-growth. Inside a slate ring, four adults and two young will lie braided together like roots in a pot, hands resting on throats, soles against bellies, fingertips tucked into the warm hollow behind a neighbor’s knee. Conversation happens across six square feet of skin at once: a dry thumb-stroke across an eyebrow for *who*, a subtle stiffening of the ribs for *wait*, a rippling five-finger chord down the forearm to describe the exact spiral-path of a shedding glider-flock overhead, a shared, slow tightening of the diaphragm that is their equivalent of laughter—a silent, rhythmic heave that sets the surrounding three yards of wall-creatures shifting sleepily in sympathy.

They are the stewards of this blind, cramped Eden, though their stewardship looks nothing like farming and everything like grooming.

Watch one move—if you could see in the infrared, where the chamber blazes like a stained-glass window of embers, golds, and deep arterial plums. An old male, scarred across the shoulders from a childhood brush with a fissure-lip, unbraids himself from the slate ring and goes down to work the floor-troughs. He does not walk. Nobody walks here; upright posture would plunge your head into the choking glider-stream and leave your ankles exposed to heat-vipers. He swims slowly downward through the living upholstery, breaststroking with flat, spatulate forearms whose outer edges are callused into smooth horn-blades. He parts two stacked gleaners as gently as a midwife parting curtains, slides headfirst down a greased trachyte chute, and hooks one broad, prehensile big toe into a cooling-crack just above the iron-red glow of a main vent.

The heat here is fifty-four degrees, dry enough to crack timber, roaring upward with a hiss of sulfur and floating bacterial ash. He squints his heat-lip away from the glare—to his face, the fissure is blinding white—and reaches out with both hands to feel along the rim-shingles of the giant flagstone-grazers.

Here is why the low slate walls were stacked, why the river-cobbles were hauled up from a forgotten world below, why the basins and tally-grooves exist. Left entirely to itself, the vault is unstable. Uric salt-crusts build up along the vent-lips until they choke the sulfur-hiss that feeds the bacterial tarnish; dead grazers occasionally bake hard in place instead of being drunk dry, sealing off six feet of precious primary pasture beneath an inedible porcelain shell; dominant ribbon-hunters grow too long and thick, eating out whole alcoves of brooders until bare, unproductive rock shows through and breaks the thermal insulation of the wall.

The old man feels a choked vent-lip with the horn-edge of his ulna. Reaching to a belt woven from twisted sinew and strips of glider-parchment—the only clothing any of them wear—he draws a wedge of dense, black river-basalt, foreign stone harder than anything native to this volcanic neck, prized above life itself because when it chips it cannot be replaced without climbing out into the lethal sky-freeze. With three practiced, muffled pries—*clack, crunch, slide*, padding the tool with his own palm so the vibration won’t draw a crowd of scavengers—he breaks free a ten-pound shelf of fused uric crystal and baked bacterial clinker. He lifts it clear of the hiss, letting fresh sulfurous breath bloom out over the rock, and immediately two young flagstone-grazers waiting on his calf-muscles inch forward over his shins to clamp onto the newly opened pasture.

He does not drop the salt-slab back into the vent. He tucks it under his chin and worms his way back up the knee-deep floor highway toward the carved buttress.

Those shallow, hand-chiseled basins in the northern wall are salt-mortars and fat-presses. Up there, two younger women sit wedged in a chimney-root alcove, grinding vent-clinker and shed skin-flakes into fine, medicinal paste with river-cobble pestles, mixing it with waxy tallow milked—literally squeezed out like ointment through natural sebaceous pores—from tame, purring loaf-brooders held across their laps. They smear this thick grease onto nascent rock-cracks to keep the mountain’s sulfur draft from leaking sideways into the sleeping tiers, or press cakes of mineral-rich bacterial ash into the mouths of old, tooth-worn kin, or rub scented musk-tallow along the lintels to steer the blind glider-whirlpool away from the nursery rings.

And the tally-grooves? Run a thumb over them carefully. They are not all straight lines. Some are shallow cups, some long bevels, some forked, polished deep in the center by ten thousand oily thumbs checking the count on the way past. 

In a closed biological treasury where every gram of water is a finite heirloom, you cannot have twelve people when the vault only holds moisture enough for eleven and three-quarters.

The grooves are the Ledger. Every living person in the mountain has a vertical score on that buttress, deepened by a fingernail’s breadth at each molt-season, cross-notched when they take a mate-braid, smoothed partly flat again with pumice when they grow small and dry in extreme age. Beside the human scores is a broader, older set of basins that tracks the great brood-mats and the heat of the four main floor-vents—a stone memory spanning centuries, telling the fingers of the blind whether the mountain’s breath is cooling over ten generations or growing hotter, whether this century can afford twelve souls, or ten, or nine.

Right now there are eleven grooves kept crisp and clean with grease-free horn scrapers, and near the bottom of the tenth groove, a tiny, newly scratched side-notch no bigger than an oat-grain.

Deep inside the third slate enclosure, underneath a quilted overhang of downy gleaners that keeps the draft gentle, a mother lies curled into a tight C, completely enclosed by the bodies of two aunts and an older brother who press their broad, suede backs outward like living masonry to hold back the crushing ton of curious wall-life. No birth here can ever happen in open air; a newborn comes into the world slick with precious fluid that the vault would drink to parchment in four minutes. So the four adults have locked limbs and bellies around a sealed hollow the size of a kettle, breathing shallowly inward, skin glued to skin with tallow-wax until their bodies form an airtight womb of bone and muscle. Inside that hot, pitch-black pocket, the infant—blind, velvet-backed, wide as a turtle—is meticulously licked and rubbed dry by four pairs of hands and lips before a single seam of flesh is parted to let the vault air touch its skin. Not one milligram of amniotic water is lost to the chimneys. What was the mother’s becomes the child’s and the aunts’ before the slate stones underneath them even feel the warmth.

And when someone dies—when one of the elders feels her blood thickening past tallow into dry wax, her breath-baffles stiffening, her heat-lips going dim so the room fades from embers to grey ash—nobody weeps. They have no tear ducts left to weep with, and even if they did, crying would be theft from that newly notched baby in the slate ring.

Instead, the dying person climbs—or is passed gently hand-over-hand through the hushed, rustling dark—over to the waist-high cairn of river-cobbles in the recessed alcove.

That cairn is the oldest thing in the vault. The first desperate refugees carried those water-rounded stones up from a vanished river in leather slings, perhaps to remember what rain and running creeks felt like when everything else turned to scree and ice. None of the eleven living people knows what a river is. To their fingers, the two pale cobbles still peeking through the synchronized, pulsing blanket of loaf-brooders are simply *Sky-Bone*—miraculously dense, cold-hearted rock polished by some unimaginable ancient crowd of smoother-bellied beasts than any alive today.

When an elder’s time comes, the whole eleven gather around the alcove, wedging the hunters gently aside with horn-blades, peel back a corner of the warm, breathing brooder-mat from the river-stones, and press their hands, cheeks, and ribs all over the dying person one last time—memorizing the exact grain of his calluses, the scarred map of his shoulders, the unique rhythm of his resting muscle-tremor, passing his lifelong memories of vent-shifts and rock-flaws into six younger nervous systems through three hours of silent, trembling skin-speech.

Then they pull the warm mat of brooders and velvet gleaners back over him, tucking him neatly against the river-cobbles, and crawl away to scratch his groove flat on the basalt buttress. Within a night and a day, without a drop spilled, without a bone left exposed for twenty-four hours, every drop of his six-thousand-year-old water has passed quietly into the brooders, and from the brooders’ tallow-pores back into the mortar-basins, and from the mortar-basins onto the lips of the newborn in the slate ring—while his dry calcium and uric salts drift down like fine white sleet to hiss against the red fissures and feed the tarnish that feeds the grazers. Even his skull, softened and scrubbed by mites, is eventually retrieved, packed with tallow, and tucked into a chink in the slate wall to bolster the nursery ring against the press of the crowd.

Only once in an age does any of them approach the upper chimneys.

Sometimes—perhaps once in three lifetimes—a river-stone pry-bar shatters beyond re-grinding, or a tremor in the volcanic neck pinches shut a vent and eleven must become ten before a natural death comes due, or a young, restless mind grows dizzy with the endless, velvet claustrophobia and wonders what lies past the knife-edge at the top of the air.

High up in the crooked throat of the rock, just below the turbulent boundary where the fifty-degree updraft meets the sub-zero mountain night, there is a narrow, soot-dry ledge worn smooth by hesitant knees. Rising gliders snap their wings shut three feet away and plummet past like falling leaves, striking faint blue sparks against the shaft walls. Up there, one of the small, broad people will sometimes wedge himself into the crack, holding his breath to spare his dry baffles, and slowly—trembling violently—extend a single velvety, heat-sensitive hand upward across the invisible threshold into the outer air.

To skin tuned to read half-degree shifts across packed flesh, the freezing mountain night does not feel cold; it feels like *annihilation*. It feels like dipping fingers into white-hot void, an infinite, shrieking sink pulling every calorie out of his knuckles into an emptiness so vast it has no thermal walls at all—no neighbors, no ceiling, no heartbeat, just a terrifying, roaring vacuum where water dies into hard stone and warmth falls upward forever without ever bouncing back.

Usually, within three heartbeats, he snatches his hand down, tucks his burned, numb fingers deep into his armpit, and slides rapidly back down the greased chimney-wall—plunging gratefully headfirst into the suffocating forty-five-degree dark, back into the smell of chalk and ozone and hot tallow, back until shoulders, flanks, belly, and soles are once again pressed tight on all six sides by breathing velvet flesh, and the mountain closes safely around him like a clenched fist.
