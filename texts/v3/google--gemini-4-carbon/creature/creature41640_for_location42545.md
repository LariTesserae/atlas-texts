To see the rest of what lives here, you have to stop looking for animals that were carried up in cages, and start looking at the tolerances of the hardware.

Watch the matte-gray beetle when its wings are finally dry. It does not fly toward the orchids or the fruit-heavy vines. It clicks its primer-colored shell shut, marches with stiff, calibrated deliberation down the flange of the I-beam, and wedges its flat head directly beneath a brass drip-valve that has begun to weep out of rhythm. With a pair of mandibles shaped remarkably like offset box-wrenches, it grips the packing nut, braces its six tarsal claws against the unpainted rivets, and *heaves*. The nut turns a sixteenth of a millimeter. The rogue drip stops. From the collar of the valve, a tiny bead of heavy, amber cylinder oil squeezes out; the beetle laps it up with a feathery copper-colored tongue, cleans its antennae, and trundles off into the begonias. 

Nothing here is feral, and nothing is merely decorative. Because there is no soil, there is no detritus—no rotting wood, no leaf-litter, no fungal decay. Anything that falls and rots would sour the chemical baths and clog the sluice gates. Therefore, the basin’s fauna does not eat dirt or dead leaves; it eats *error*. It feeds on friction, excess voltage, mineral precipitate, and runaway growth.

Look closely at the greased turnbuckles where the mooring-rope vines are trying to strangle the tension cables. Wrapped around the threaded steel rods are things that look, at first glance, like misplaced strips of oiled chamois leather. Wait until the ten-minute fog rolls in. As the warm mist hits them, the strips uncoil. They are flatworms, ten inches long and broad as carpenter’s ribbons, patterned on their backs with faint, ruled millimeter hash-marks. They glide up the encroaching tendrils and secrete a clear, painless alkaloid that dissolves only the vine's gripping hairs, causing the heavy green rope to slip harmlessly off the critical hardware and drape instead over the structural trusses where it belongs. The worms don't eat the plant; they drink the sweet, potassium-rich mucilage the vine produces to glue itself down, keeping the turnbuckles clean and visible.

Higher up, in the upper canopy where the gantry crane drags its curtain of brass chains, the static sparks have bred—or summoned—a specialized grazer. Clinging to the undersides of the glass funnels are clusters of long-legged harvestmen, their bodies no bigger than watch-gears, their eight legs spun as fine as tungsten filaments. They do not weave webs; the sudden indoor rains would tear silk to shreds every ten minutes. Instead, they link legs across the gaps between galvanized conduits and brass manifolds, forming living, shifting bridges between different metals. When the crane passes and the leaves dump their static charge, you can see a faint, shivering luminescence travel through the knotted clusters of harvestmen. They are galvanic creatures. They drink the stray potential difference between copper and zinc—preventing the galvanic corrosion that would otherwise pit the gleaming joints—and use that tiny trickle of current to twitch their hair-thin legs through the air, combing microscopic spores and high-altitude dust out of the mist before it can settle into the open grease-cups.

Down in the flooded bilges, where the blind, translucent amphibians drift among the gutta-percha cables, there is a quiet hierarchy of heat. Those pale creatures—something between an olm and an embryonic mudpuppy—are not hunting worms. If you watch one hover over a thick, braided submarine cable, you will see its feathery red gills flare and press flat against the rubberized insulation. They are thermovores, or close to it, harboring dense colonies of sulfur-oxidizing bacteria in their gill-slits that require an exact, narrow band of warmth and the 50-hertz electromagnetic hum to divide. When a cable runs hot—carrying a sudden surge of load from whatever invisible engine sits miles below—dozens of the pale amphibians swim lazily out of the shadows to pile atop the warm spot, their cool, gelatinous bellies drawing the excess heat out into the standing water like living heat-sinks, keeping the gutta-percha from softening and splitting. 

And yet, an ecosystem of pure maintenance cannot manage the sheer, explosive volume of biomass erupting from the black-pellet slag. Something larger has to read the pressure gauges behind the begonia leaves. Something has to empty the glass funnels before the clear, viscous sap overflows onto the diamond-plate walkways.

You hear it before you see it, though the sound blends seamlessly into the hydraulic hush of the room: a soft, rhythmic *cup-pop, cup-pop*, like rubber plungers pulling free of wet glass, followed by the muted chime of an empty flask being set back into a spring-clip.

Wait on the middle catwalk when the indoor rain begins to fall, when the warm fog turns the eighty-yard span into a series of shallow, grey-green curtains. 

Out of the dripping philodendrons steps a shape about the size and weight of a yearling calf, though built on no recognizable skeletal plan. It has no spine, or rather, it has too many—a radial symmetry gone slightly oblong to navigate narrow maintenance stairs. Its hide is smooth, poreless, and the exact slate-gray of vulcanized India rubber, shedding the warm rain in sheets. It moves on five thick, muscular limbs that terminate not in hooves or paws, but in broad, flanged suction-pads rimmed with tough, keratinous scrapers, allowing it to walk effortlessly up the vertical face of a wet concrete baffle or upside down along the belly of an I-beam while staring straight down through the floorboards into the blinding blue abyss without a flicker of vertigo.

It has no face in the mammalian sense. At its crown sits a turret of three large, heavily lensed eyes arranged in a triangle, each hooded by a brassy nictitating membrane that clicks shut against drips like a camera shutter. Beneath the eyes, tucked into folds of warm gray skin, is a ring of dexterous, boneless manipulators—some thick as thumbs for turning cast-iron sluice wheels, others tapering to split, sensitive tips fine enough to part the petals of a dark-violet orchid without bruising the velvet.

They are the Tenders—there are perhaps four of them in the entire basin, rarely crossing paths, moving on staggered, silent patrols through the green steam. 

Watch one pause beside a narrow stairway where a giant begonia has pressed its waxy leaf flat against a pressure gauge. The Tender does not tear the leaf away. It gently slides two thin, pale tendrils behind the stem, pinches a specific vascular bundle to make the leaf droop on its own hydraulic hinge, and leans one huge, camera-lensed eye an inch from the glass pane. The needle is twitching in the red. 

Inside the Tender’s barrel-like torso, you can hear a wet, rhythmic *slosh-click*—a biological pump shifting ballast. It reaches down to the nearest glass funnel, unhooks it from the handrail, and pours the collected, spark-milked sap directly into a breathing spiracle on its own flank. Within seconds, its gray rubbery skin flushes a warm, bruised plum-color. Recharged—or perhaps chemically instructed by whatever alkaloids were in that specific batch of sap—it walks thirty paces along the catwalk, braces three suction-limbs against the diamond-plate floor, wraps two heavy manipulators around a stiff, newly cast bypass valve, and opens it half a turn. 

Somewhere beneath the floorboards, steam reroutes with a deep, velvety *whoosh*. Five yards away, a silent turbine casing cools by four degrees; the neon-emerald moss coating it shivers, stops advancing toward a greased bearing, and pulls its spore-capsules tight. The needle on the gauge drops back into the green. The Tender releases the begonia stem, allowing the broad leaf to press gently back against the glass like a curtain falling shut.

Where did they come from? Look down into the lowest, warmest corner of the amphitheater, behind a concrete sill still stamped with the pine-grain of its wooden forms, where the 50-hertz hum is strong enough to make the standing water form tiny, geometric Faraday waves. 

Hanging from the underside of a warm copper tray are three translucent, leathery sacs, each the size of a coal-scuttle, anchored to the pristine metal by braided cords that look disturbingly like the cloth-wrapped cables rising from the abyss, yet bleed clear lymph if nicked. Inside the sacs, curled in a broth of warm condensation and mineral runoff, smaller shapes twitch in time with the cams of the misting valves. One sac is nearly opaque; through its stretched wall, you can see the faint, matte-gray gleam of vulcanized skin, and a single large eye already open, watching the ten-minute fog thicken and fall.

They were not hoisted up in crates after the scaffolding came down. They were poured with the concrete, or precipitated out of the first chemical baths the moment the steam was turned on. 

As the chamber matures, the line between the plumbing and the inhabitants is growing harder to parse, not because the machine is breaking down, but because it is finishing its assembly. The heavy, bulbous seedpods that drop onto the iron grates and roll against the bolt-heads do not sprout roots when they crack open; they extrude pale, rubbery gaskets that seal the floor-joints so the warm water stops dripping out into the sky. The worm-gear snails, when they grow too heavy for the copper pipes, crawl into empty valve-housings and calcify in place, their coiled shells becoming permanent, self-healing check-valves that let water flow up toward the orchids, never back down.

Everything here is young—only days or weeks old, blinking in the fierce, thin mountain sun, still smelling of hot linseed oil, crushed fern, ozone, and wet cement. There is no predator in the basin, no rot, and no memory of the ground miles below. There is only the ten-minute rain, the rising heat, the silent blue sparks in the canopy, and a handful of quiet, grey-skinned stewards walking upside down across the girders, listening to the hum in their bones, carefully pruning and tightening a world that has not yet revealed what it is trying to distil.
