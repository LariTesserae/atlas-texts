Riding the open rear platform of that municipal tram, leaning a broad forearm against the warm, verdigris-slicked brass of the hand-brake, is one of the builders. 

At first glance, from twenty paces through a drifting veil of condenser-steam, you might mistake him for an upright piling of bolted boiler-plate draped in wet felt, or one of the squat masonry piers come loose from its mortar. Whoever raised the first plinths on this tableland—whether they walked up from the lowlands ten thousand years ago as ordinary humans or arrived already altered—has long since been kneaded by the altitude, the damp, and the unhurried gravity of the work into something indigenous to the stone.

They are short, scarcely five feet high, with the massive, barrel-vaulted ribcages required to draw thin plateau air into slow-beating hearts. Their proportions are horizontal, settled, built for leverage rather than stride. Beneath skin the color and texture of oiled slate—thick, nearly hairless, and permanently beaded with warm mist—lies a dense subcutaneous padding of fat and fibrous gristle that insulates them against the crisp high-mountain shadows while absorbing the ceaseless bass vibration of the flagstones. Look at their bare feet where they stand ankle-deep in the flooded concourses: the soles have spread into broad, cushioned pads with short, webbed toes plated in thick horn the yellow-gray of river-shell, gripping the biofilm-slicked granite without ever slipping. They have no need of boots; iron nails and leather rot in a month here, whereas callous grows back overnight.

Because there is no flax, no cotton, no timber, and no pasture, everything they wear is either smelted, quarried, or harvested from the reef of beasts. The tram-conductor wears a heavy, sleeveless mantle felted from the shed down of the coal-colored scavengers and treated with tallow rendered from vat-grubs until it hangs like supple tarpaulin, shedding the continuous eaves-drip in oily beads. Across his chest is a harness of braided copper wire and cured siphonophore-gut, hung with heavy bronze spanners, grease-horns, and calipers whose joints are slick with animal fat. When he shifts his weight, he clinks softly, not like a soldier in mail, but like a sack of wet river-cobbles and brass fittings.

His face is wide and flat-browed, the nostrils broad and lined with coarse, filtering bristles that trap soot and drifting midges before they reach the lungs. His eyes are dark, deep-set beneath heavy, greasy folds of lid, and protected by a pale, translucent third membrane that sweeps sideways across the cornea every half-minute—*flick, flick*—wiping away droplets of warm brine and airborne lime-paste without interrupting his gaze. 

He does not shout to his crewmate at the prow. In an atmosphere saturated with the deafening, resonant roar of millions of croaking throats and chuffing atmospheric vents, high-pitched human speech is useless; thin sounds are eaten alive within ten feet. Instead, beneath his heavy jaw hangs a loose, leathery throat-pouch, faintly striated like dark pewter. When he wishes to signal that the flange-way ahead is choked with calcified shell-crust, he draws a slow, cavernous breath and lets it out through his closed lips. A low, vibrating *boom-hummm* rolls out of him—a sound pitched precisely in the narrow acoustic trough between the deep turbine-thrum in the stone and the midrange rattle of the roosting flocks. It carries for two hundred yards down the wet brick canyon, felt in the sternum before it is heard by the ear. Up at the prow, the driver’s own throat swells and answers with two short, descending notes, like a tugboat acknowledging a lock-keeper, and his heavy hand eases the throttle back one notch.

They call themselves nothing at all in the grand sense—when there is no edge to the city, there are no foreigners against whom to draw a boundary—though their working guilds have names that translate roughly by elevation and fluid: *Sump-wrights*, *Brine-walkers*, *Flue-scrapers*, *Crane-sleepers*. 

Follow the tram as it eases into a district depot—a vast, roofless basilica of weeping red brick where subterranean steam-mains vent through perforated iron columns—and you see how completely their domestic lives are mortised into the masonry and the fauna. They do not live in dry rooms behind closed doors; dry air makes their skin crack and their sinuses ache. Their tenements are stepped grottoes of glazed tile and cast-iron ribs open on one side to the warm mist, heated from beneath by the radiant flagstones under which greywater and foundry-slurry perpetually circulate. 

Inside an alcove three tiers above the flooded street, a family sits around a warm iron floor-grate. None of them are sitting on chairs—chairs belong to people with brittle knees and dry floors. They squat effortlessly on their broad heels or recline against bolsters of woven brass-mesh stuffed with oiled feathers, their backs resting directly against warm brickwork where cobblestone-shelled filterers cling like living wainscoting. An infant, plump and gray-skinned as a river-pebble, crawls across the half-inch film of warm water on the bedroom tiles, swatting lazily at a slow, fat, translucent-winged locust that has drifted in on an updraft, catching it in a stubby fist and popping it whole into its mouth while its elders watch with heavy-lidded contentment.

Their diet is the distilled richness of the industrial marsh. In the lower fermentation wards—vast, sunken amphitheaters of warm brick vats heated by waste-steam—huge mats of sulfur-bacteria and black yeast digest the endless rain of bird-guano, shed shells, dead amphibians, and mineral salts washed down from the upper terraces. From these vats, workers with long-handled bronze skimmers harvest thick curds of pale protein-paste and scoop up baskets of blind, pale crustaceans as heavy as loaves of bread. Nothing is baked with grain; everything is poached in geothermal steam-chests, stewed in warm brine-troughs, or cured in the warm, mineral smoke of the brick-kilns. At street corners, old men with pale, cataract-clouded eyes sit waist-deep in stone soaking-basins beside the hot-water mains, selling skewers of roasted vat-locusts brushed with fermented crustacean oil, and heavy stoneware mugs of hot, salty broth thick with pulverized shell-calcium that keeps the citizens’ dense bones as heavy as pig-iron.

To watch them work is to realize that they do not distinguish between maintenance, agriculture, and geology. 

Walk out toward that distant bank of low cloud where the cathedral-sized cranes are turning. High in the greased truss-work of a jib two hundred feet above the marshy pan, a crew of eight riggers lives for months at a time inside a counterweight-house made of riveted copper sheet and woven reed-like bundles of calcified worm-tubes. Swallows nest in the crooks of their knees while they sleep; bare-throated scavengers perch along their shoulders like heavy, warm shawls, preening the oil from the workers' scalp-folds with blunt, gentle beaks. There is no shooing, no disgust, no sudden movement. If a rigger needs to reach a grease-nipple covered by three sleeping amphibians, he slides his broad, horn-nailed fingers beneath their bellies and lifts them aside with the exact, unthinking care with which a mason shifts a plumb-bob, setting them down on his own thigh until he has pumped the tallow-gun twice, then putting them back.

Down in the sunken pan below the crane, forty Sump-wrights are dismantling a tenement block built nine hundred years ago that has slowly subsided six feet below the local water-line. They move in unison through thigh-deep warm water, stripped to their copper tool-belts, their slate-dark hides gleaming in the bright, pale sun. Nobody swings a sledgehammer. Sledges waste breath in thin air and shatter good stone. Instead, they drill shallow rows of holes along the ancient mortar-seams using slow, water-driven rotary augers connected by leather hoses to a portable steam-donkey chuffing patiently on a barge. Into these holes they tap dry plugs of compressed shell-lime and salt. Then they sit back on the half-submerged cornices, passing around a gourd of warm, mildly narcotic fermentation-liquor brewed from black vat-yeast, humming low, chorded work-songs that vibrate across the water’s surface in concentric ripples. 

Over three hours, the plugs drink the warm water, swell with quiet, irresistible force, and *pop*—a five-ton course of weathered granite separates cleanly along its bed. Chains thick with black grease and clinging snails are looped around it; a foreman swells his throat-pouch and sends a deep, two-note boom rolling upward into the bright cloud; high overhead, a rigger shifts an iron lever an inch, and the great chain-drives take up the slack with a wet, clicking groan. By sundown, the stone will rest half a mile away on a newly leveled terrace, its coating of yellow biofilm and clinging tunicates scarcely disturbed by the transit, already bleeding rust-ochre into its new neighbors.

They have no books—paper turns to grey porridge between morning and noon—nor do they carve grand historical reliefs, knowing how quickly the constant warm rain planes sharp edges smooth. Their memory is kept in brass and bone, and in the plumbing itself. Every district’s water-wardens carry heavy rings of stamped bronze tallies hung around their thick necks, recording the pressure- settings, pipe-gauges, and kiln-relinings of the past ten generations. More deeply than that, the city *is* their archive. A master boiler-tender, laying his broad, calloused palm flat against the sweating iron flank of a subterranean steam-main, can read the pulse of fifty upstream valves by the subtle harmonic shudders in the metal, knowing without words that three leagues away in the salt-wards a crew has just diverted flow to flush a silt-basin—a basin his great-great-grandmother helped line with glazed brick before the great crane on the eastern rim had pivoted past its third buttress.

They do not fear death, though they treat a cooling body with prompt, practical tenderness. In a world without soil, there are no graves, and fuel is too precious to waste on pyres when boilers must be fed. When an old woman’s chest finally stills in her warm, tiled grotto, her kin wash her slate-gray skin with clean condenser-water, remove her bronze spanners and tally-rings to hang around the necks of her grandchildren, and carry her up the smooth, river-worn stairways to the highest roof-parapets—above the avenues, above the brine-pipes, right up beneath the sunlit bellies of the hanging clouds. 

They lay her out upon the warm slate among the cobblestone-creatures, in the clear mountain light. Before the bearers have even descended the first flight of dripping stairs, the telephone wires and crane-cables overhead have begun to lighten as the great, coal-winged flocks drop silently down on oiled, leathery pinions. Within an hour, the flesh has gone back into the sky; within a week, the heavy, mineral-dense bones have been rasped clean by snails and dragged down the gutters into the lime-kilns and mortar-mills, ground fine to butter the joints of the newly raised terraces—holding two blocks of granite together for another thousand years while the warm water slides smoothly over them, reflecting the pale sky.
