Nothing walks the streets because walking requires a continuous street, a dependable gravity, and enough time between steps to shift weight from heel to toe. Anything with bones, shells, or fixed organs that drops from the dry rim into the outer blocks does not die of predation; it dies of carpentry. A hare or a lizard lasts perhaps forty seconds before a curb jacks upward at sixty feet per second, or a wall forgets its thickness and shears sideways through the animal's middle, or a fresh callus-sheet rolls over its feet and cements it into the sub-grade to be broken down by caustic root-hairs before its heart has even stopped fibrillating.

To live here, you cannot be a discrete object. Or if you are, you must be small enough, flat enough, or strange enough in your geometry that a collapsing colonnade is weather rather than death.

Begin with the blisters under the balustrades—the motionless scale-organisms that look like warts on the fresh timber. They are only motionless when the wood beneath them is still. Watch one when the railing it clings to begins to shudder, just three seconds before the supporting colonnade shears off at the base. Before the first crack of splitting heartwood registers in the air, the blister liquefies its own margin, lifts a single hair-thin stylet out of the pressurized phloem beneath it, and *skates*. Riding a self-generated slick of mucilage, propelled by the sudden drop in turgor pressure behind it and the rising spike ahead, it shoots forty feet sideways across a heaving facade, leaps a three-inch structural fissure on a whipped thread of gummy silk, and clamps down onto a newly inflated dormer beam half a second before its former home turns into flying splinters. 

They are hemipteran in ancestry, perhaps, some distant cousin of the lac insect or mealybug, bred over uncountable fast-forwarded generations inside the basin until they lost legs, antennae, eyes, and wings—all things that catch on splinters. What remains is a muscular, translucent suction-disc the size of a thumbnail, rimmed with baroreceptors sensitive enough to read the hydraulic hammer of sap through six inches of dense lignum. Internally, they are little more than a pressure-relief valve wrapped in gonad. They do not pump sap; the city’s arterial pressure is so monstrous that once a scale-disc punches its stylet into a vascular bundle, the hot, sugary xylem-water blasts straight through its digestive tract and sprays out a dorsal vent in a fine, atomized plume, stripped only of amino acids and trace minerals on the way through. Where fifty of them cluster in the lee of a stable architrave, they hiss like tiny steam-radiators. They reproduce by shrapnel: when a wall inevitably catches a sluggish old female in a shear-zone and crushes her between two planing slabs of bark, her pressurized tough-skinned ovisac ruptures and fires microscopic, barbed nymphs into the fresh-split timber opposite, already drinking before the dust settles.

Deeper down, where the root-pavers grind together in permanent, blood-warm twilight and the slime ferments into vinegar and wood-alcohol, the pale nematodes are not alone. Or rather, they are not always individual nematodes.

In the sump-pockets beneath the stepped marketplaces, millions of these hair-thin worms weave themselves into cohesive, fist-sized bolus-knots that behave like single, boneless amphibians. A solitary worm two millimeters long is helpless when a sump drains or a root-wall slams shut; a braided rope of four hundred thousand of them, slicked in shared mucus and firing their longitudinal muscles in synchronized waves, has the tensile strength of wet rawhide and no skull to crush. When two massive buttresses slam together with force enough to powder granite, the worm-knot does not burst. It extrudes. It squirts upward through a millimeter-wide crack in the hexagonal pavement above like grease escaping a piston, pools briefly in the bright sunlight on a plaza tile, smells the dry air, and immediately pours itself down the nearest gutter-slit into a fresher, cooler cellar. 

They graze on the city's mistakes. Wherever the runaway architectural code stutters—producing a room filled solid with loose, unlignified pith, or an abortive staircase of soft, starchy meristem that begins to sour in its own trapped heat—the braids arrive through the plumbing, smother the mass like a living stomach lining, dissolve it with secreted cellulase, and divide in half when they grow too heavy to squeeze through standard joinery.

Higher up, where the orthogonal fever breaks space itself, something else has learned to use the stutter.

Stand at the mouth of one of the repeating tenement lanes—the ones where six identical doorways echo down the block in locked spatial phase—and watch the air inside the first doorway. Nothing flies down the avenue. Nothing crosses the threshold from outside. Yet hanging suspended in the dark rectangle of the doorframe is a pale, radial tangle of filaments looking very much like a wind-torn dandelion clock the size of a dinner plate, drifting slowly upward against no breeze at all. And hanging in the second doorway down the lane is the exact same tangle, and in the third, and the fourth, rotating at the exact same lazy angle.

Reach out with a stick and touch one filament in the first doorway. All six tangles flinch simultaneously, contracting into tight, defensive burrs—and then three of them vanish entirely from doorways one, two, and four, while the ones in three, five, and six smoothly unpack themselves and drift backward into the interior gloom.

It has no vernacular name because nobody lives on the rim long enough to tolerate the noise, though if a naturalist managed to net one without losing an arm to a snapping shutter, they would find no chitin and no animal protein in it. It is a rogue tuft of the city itself—an emancipated gall, a free-floating epiphyte born from the basin's broken category-boundaries, where a seed-head forgot whether it was progeny or ornament and detached from the parent vine without severing its vascular citizenship. Its pale rays are hollow, feather-light tubes coated in microscopic hooks and electrostatic hairs. It feeds on the twenty-second mists of ruptured xylem-water and the clouds of pale sawdust blasted out by shearing towers, sweeping the air clean of airborne starch and drifting pollen-chaff that has nothing to fertilize.

It survives the violent urban renewal at the basin’s center because it has adapted to non-Euclidean cover. A normal creature caught inside a five-story rotunda when it flattens into a two-dimensional silhouette is erased. The drift-galls deliberately congregate where shadows fall in the wrong direction and tendrils graft across empty air. They hook a single anchor-thread onto the rim of a clean, mid-air cross-section where a liana steps thirty yards across nothing—anchoring themselves in the seam. When the building around one end of the graft explodes into green kindling, the shockwave hits the gall's outer rays, snaps its dry pulvinus-hinges, and reels its gossamer body backward *through* the spatial skip, popping it out of thin air above a quiet courtyard half a block away like water backing up through a siphon. Sometimes, where a corridor spirals inward far deeper than a building's exterior allows, hundreds of them pack the dead-end papery rooms, glowing faintly with heat-shock proteins in the pitch dark, breeding by budding off smaller, chaotic tufts that tumble down the impossible stairs until they find a window-gap that opens onto Tuesday's courtyard or an alley three blocks west.

And then there is the tenant that uses the architecture *as* architecture.

You will never see one whole from the valley rim, and down in the avenues you would mistake its work for just another symptom of the city's madness, except for one detail: quiet.

Walk—if you could walk—past a row of frantically banging silica-leaf shutters, deafened by the hydraulic roar and the rifle-shot snapping of pavers, and suddenly you pass an alcove where four shutters hang completely, unnaturally dead. No potassium ions pump through their hinges. Below them, a section of hexagonal street-pavers ten feet wide sits slightly sunken, locked in a glaze of hardened, amber resin that has not cracked even though the avenue three yards away is heaving like surf. Inside the shadowed room behind those dead shutters, the air is still, stiflingly hot, and smells faintly of formic acid, bruised cucumber, and cured wood.

Something is farming dormancy.

Look closely at the load-bearing bamboo-like culms framing that silent alcove. Just above the flare of the root-collar, a neat, surgical ring of tiny punctures circles each shaft—not chewed, because chewing takes too long and triggers violent callus-swelling, but lanced cleanly into the sieve-tubes to inject a precise neuro-botanical sedative. The frantic morphogenetic signal screaming up from the mattress below hits that chemical ring and goes numb. For twenty or thirty minutes—an geological epoch in the basin's metabolic time—that single room stops trying to become a viaduct or an amphitheater. It simply stands there, bleeding slow, rich, unpressurized sap into shallow bowls of folded leaf-blade glued to the corners.

Out of the dry, papery inner doors steps the mason—or rather, seventy or eighty pieces of the mason.

Individually, they look like elongated, wingless fig-wasps built out of polished mahogany and copper wire, roughly the length of a human thumb-joint, flattened dorsoventrally so thin they can slip sideways between two planing sheets of bark without being crushed, the way a knife blade slides between pages of a slammed book. Their legs do not project downward to walk; they splay radially from the flat edges of the thorax like the bristles on a chimney-brush, armed with spring-loaded tarsal claws that allow them to scuttle at blinding, jerky speeds up vertical walls, upside-down across ceilings, or straight across empty three-inch gaps by firing themselves off flexed abdominal plates that click like snapped fingernails.

They have no queen. A single egg-layer fat enough to sustain a colony would be an anchor, and an anchor in this valley is a death sentence. Instead, every worker is a sterile, short-lived tool budded directly off a shared, portable germ-line they carry between them like baggage: three or four pale, legless, grub-like "spools" no bigger than beech-nuts, slung beneath the flat bellies of the largest runners in harnesses of braided root-hair. 

For twenty-two minutes, inside their drugged, silent room, the colony rests. They unhook the pale spools, lay them in the warm, pooling sap, and groom them frantically while perimeter guards stand motionless at the doorways, long antennae splayed wide—not listening to the roar, which is useless white noise, but reading the *polarized light* and the *air pressure* in the street outside, and tasting the wood beneath their claws for the bitter chemical surge of auxin that means the mattress underneath is waking up angry.

At minute twenty-three, the floor tilts half a degree. The resin glaze spiderwebs. Beneath the drugged culms, the vast vegetable mattress realizes a piece of its grid has stalled and routes three hundred tons of fresh hydraulic pressure upward to blow the obstruction clear.

The guards do not buzz or pheromone-call; they stomp once, sharply, sending a high-frequency shear-wave through the timber that outruns the city's heavy bass roar. Instantly, six runners snatch up the pale spools, clamp them tight against their sternites, and the entire flat swarm pours out of the alcove—not down into the street, which is already blistering upward to explode, and not up onto the roof, which is losing three-dimensional coherence against the afternoon sky. 

Instead, they run straight up a Doric shaft to a spot on the blank, windowless wall where two shadows cross at the wrong angle. The lead scout—older than the rest by perhaps nine days, missing two legs, her flat carapace scarred white by silica-splinters—taps the solid green bark where the geometry is thin, finds the hairline seam where this fourth-story wall fails to agree with the sunken courtyard next door, wedges her razor-flat head into the discrepancy, and slips sideways *out* of the watchtower before it bursts into a geyser of chaff. Seventy-nine flat, copper-legged shadows flick through the non-transition behind her in half a second, emerging into the bright sunlight along the lip of a newly grown boxwood pit, already stinging the nearest balustrade into twenty minutes of quiet before the dust of their last house has finished falling.

And beneath them all—beneath the flat wasps, the drifting seam-galls, the grease-slick worm-ropes, and the whistling scale-blisters—there is of course the obvious thing, the thing the mind keeps trying to step around when looking down from the dry rim under the ordinary sun.

The mattress itself.

Four miles wide, unknown yards deep, soaking up groundwater from some fractured aquifer below the basin floor and burning midday sunlight across square miles of silica-stiffened roof-leaves at an efficiency no natural forest ever approached. It has no brain, any more than a termitarium or a slime mold has a brain. If you cored straight down through the avenues with a diamond drill—assuming the drill wasn't immediately bent at right angles or grafted onto an aqueduct fifty yards away—you would find no buried starship, no ancient stone temple at the bottom acting as a seed-crystal, no dreaming giant tangled in the taproots. You would find only denser and hotter knots of compressed cambium, wood compressed by its own hydraulic overburden into something black and heavy as lignum vitae, pulsing with slow, continental waves of calcium ions and electrical action potentials.

Yet it is not merely growing. Growth is radial; growth seeks light and water and stops when it has them. 

Down in the central wards, watch a newly erupted plaza before it collapses. A row of market stalls rises, throws out broad, shady awning-leaves, levels a counter of smooth white callus-wood at waist height—*whose* waist height?—and then, along the inner lip of the counter, extrudes a neat row of hollow, cup-shaped wooden vessels that fill half-full with clear, sweet guttation-water, wait ninety seconds in the roaring heat for a drinker who never steps up to the bar, and then crush themselves inward with a wet *crunch* to fuel a staircase leading up to an empty balcony overlooking nothing. Watch an avenue widen itself cleanly down the middle while narrower side-lanes throw arched pedestrian bridges overhead, complete with drainage gargoyles of open-mouthed seed-pods spitting warm mist into the gutters below, clearing a right-of-way for a parade that has never formed.

It is an idiot savant of hospitality, a blind, four-mile-wide vegetative fever-dream trying desperately, at sixty miles an hour and a hundred and ten decibels, to remember the shape of a guest it has never met, or perhaps to *invent* one by building every possible room a citizen might require until, by sheer combinatory violence, a citizen happens to stand inside it. Every splintered tenement, every misfired non-Euclidean stairwell, every slamming shutter is a rough draft crumpled up and thrown away before the ink is dry. 

Nothing walks the streets yet. Only the blisters skate the sap-hammer, and the worms knead the cellars, and the flat wasps steal twenty minutes of peace at knife-point from the walls. Along the dry dirt rim of the shallow basin, the ordinary scrub-grass stops six yards shy of the outer wall, singed yellow by the wave of blood-warm humidity rolling off the roofs, while down in the bright, cucumber-scented grid, ten thousand empty doorways slam open into ten thousand empty foyers, waiting at the top of their lungs for a footstep heavy enough to stay.
