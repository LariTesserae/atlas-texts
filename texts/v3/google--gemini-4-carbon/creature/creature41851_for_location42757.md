To find the municipality’s deeper citizenry, you have to stop looking at the plazas as empty space and start reading the wear in the brick. 

Wait long enough by the eastern condenser tower—standing in the damp chill while the sixty-cycle hum numbs the soles of your feet—and you will notice a secondary pattern in the blue-grey pavers. Running parallel to the rattling white highway of porcelain-plated arthropods is a shallow, polished trough, eighteen inches wide and worn half an inch deep into the vitrified clay, smooth as the inside of a butter dish. For forty minutes at a time, nothing uses it. Then, from the shadow of the switching shed under the viaduct, something comes sliding down the groove at the exact walking pace of a surveyor.

They are the reason the bollards are rubbed to a greasy mirror. 

At a glance, one looks like an overstuffed bolster of wet grey chamois, five feet long and thick around as a hogshead, moving on a broad, muscular belly-foot lubricated by the tallow-slick condensation on the stones. It has no head in any strict anatomical sense, only a blunt, slightly lifted prow banded with three crescent-shaped ridges of dark horn—the same horn as the bills of the cornice-honkers overhead, though thickened here into heavy caliper-plates. Along its flanks run neat rows of spiracles that open and shut with the wet, rhythmic *cluck* of leather check-valves. As it travels, it leans its heavy, tallow-greased shoulder deliberately against every cast-iron lamp standard and granite curb corner on its route, leaving a fresh, microscopic film of wax that waterproofs the iron while scraping away its own dead, sloughing skin against the stone.

Watch where it stops. It halts beside the west stamping mill, just outside the splash-guard where the iron-sulfide snails cluster in heavy, metallic heaps. The creature rears its front third upright—standing four feet tall, swaying slightly like a blind cobbler listening to a watch—and presses its horn calipers directly against the cast-iron housing of the main camshaft bearing. 

It stays there for two minutes, utterly still, absorbing the vibration through its dense, cartilage-ribbed core. Inside that grey sack of flesh is no brain as a mammal knows it, but an acoustic organ of staggering sensitivity: a series of fluid-filled bladders weighted with swallowed pellets of soft alloy from the induction troughs. It can feel a bearing running half a degree too warm; it can hear a hairline harmonic wobble in a cam before the steel itself knows it is fatiguing. 

Finding the rhythm slightly sour, the creature opens a longitudinal slit beneath its prow and extrudes three thick, prehensile tongues—not pink, but the dull, bruised slate of vulcanized rubber, tipped with rasping pads of crystalline magnetite. With the unhurried precision of a grease-gun, it licks away a clot of grit-fouled paraffin from the bearing lip, swallows the fouled grease to strip the iron filings in its gizzard, and regurgitates a measured dollop of clarified, warm tallow directly into the oil-cup. Around its base, a dozen of the blind sulfide snails detach from the splash-guard and climb onto its skirt, grazing away the metallic sludge weeping from its lower pores. The cam’s *thud-shhh* drops half a semitone, settling back into effortless lockstep with the powerhouse flywheel across the square. The grey bolster lowers its prow, turns a crisp ninety-degree corner at the granite curb, and slides down the avenue toward the viaduct.

Once you see one, you realize how much of the machinery isn't cast iron at all.

Look up at the elevated oval of the viaduct. Every ninety seconds, something completes the circuit of the tracks, emerging from the dark mouth of the switching shed, banking slightly on the iron curves, and plunging back into the shed again. From the rim of the basin, you would swear it is a low-slung mine locomotive, three cars long, painted the dull, slick gunmetal of wet slate. 

Stand directly beneath the girders as it passes overhead—ignoring the hot, salty snow of dander shaken loose from the thousands of honking bellows-pelicans—and look up through the open ties. There are no wheels. 

What rides the rails is a colonial organism, a fused train of three immense, segmented worms armored in overlapping plates of secreted graphitic carbon. Its underside is deeply grooved in two parallel channels that grip the steel rails like inverted ice-skates, riding on a self-generated cushion of high-pressure tallow-slime and induced magnetic eddy currents. Inside the switching shed sits a massive, unmanned dynamo coupled to a flywheel; as the heavy train-beast muscles itself out onto the slight upward grade of the northern curve—driven by tremendous peristaltic waves rippling down twenty yards of dense, white muscle—it builds potential energy, licks the slick of condensation and bird-lime from the steel rails, and swallows the mica-winged spark-drinkers that drop dead from the overhead cables onto its broad, flat back. When it crests the grade and plunges back down into the dark switching shed, its graphitic belly-plates pass at thirty miles an hour between banks of permanent magnets bolted to the ties, braking its descent and shoving a massive surge of current straight back into the municipal hall’s capacitor banks. It sits in the dark shed for twelve seconds, panting thick clouds of steam through lateral gills while pipe-nozzles overhead douse its overheated armor in cool sump-brine, and then its blind, muscular prow heaves forward onto the oval once more.

This is the civic compact of the basin: there is no distinction left between maintenance, metabolism, and governance. 

Go closer to the windowless municipal hall—the one structure in the saucer that the heavy bellows-birds refuse to cling to. Its brick walls are bare, swept clean by the fierce electrostatic crackle of the ceramic insulators bristling from its roof and cornices. The sixty-cycle hum of the transformer bank out front is so dense here that the thin film of water on the plaza bricks stands up in microscopic, cross-hatched standing waves, a geometric corduroy of trembling liquid.

Nobody comes out of the municipal hall because there are no doors—only bolted cast-iron inspection hatches four feet off the ground, wept shut with pale mineral scale, and low, brick-arched culverts at pavement level where the heavy copper bus-bars and lead-sheathed cables pass inside. Yet something lives in there, and everything else in the saucer answers to its clock.

Crouch by one of the low cable-culverts, where the ozone smell is sharp enough to make your eyes water and the warm draft blowing out tastes of hot copper and vinegar. Peer past the sweating lead pipes into the unlit interior. 

The entire hollow volume of the hall, floor to roof-truss, is strung with a three-dimensional loom of glistening, amber-colored filaments thick as ship’s hawsers, stretched taut from wall to wall and anchored to the dry brick with wide fans of calcified glue. Suspended at the intersections of this dry, humming web are pale, translucent bladders the size of calves' livers, pulsing faintly with cold, bluish corona-light every time a switch throws. 

It is a single, room-sized siphonophore—dry-adapted, sessile, and breathtakingly old—grown directly through and around the brass knife-switches, relay coils, and oil-filled circuit breakers of the town’s central distribution board. Long ago, its conductive, brine-filled tendrils infiltrated the copper bus-bars. Now, it *is* the switchgear. 

Watch a tendril as thick as a man’s wrist—pale, hairless, veined with dark copper-protein blood—wrapped three times around the handle of a massive three-phase breaker. Outside, across the plaza, the western condenser tower cools half a degree too fast as a draft eddies over the grey chert embankment; the brine returning through the storm grates changes salinity by a fraction of a percent. Down in the sumps, the bristle-gilled eels thrash a tighter knot, altering the back-pressure on the powerhouse pumps; the powerhouse flywheel slows by a millisecond; the voltage on the main bus sags.

Instantly, inside the dark hall, one of the hanging bladders contracts with a wet *snap*. The muscular tendril coiled around the brass breaker-handle tightens, yanking forty pounds of sprung copper out of one oil-pot and slamming it into another with a blinding blue arc that smells instantly of scorched horn. The siphonophore does not flinch from the burn; the charred pad of calloused flesh at the tip of its tendril is already dead insulation, grown thick as a horse’s hoof specifically to take the flash. Outside on the overhead wires, ten thousand glassy, six-winged flyers leap into the air at the sharp *crack* of the insulators, drink the sudden ozone, and settle back down. Across the powerhouse facade, forty thousand pleated throats inflate and let loose a brassy, deafening *HONK* as the flywheel catches its new load and blasts hot vapor through the louvers.

Nothing in the basin knows it lives in a town. Nothing remembers who laid the blue-grey brick at right angles, or who lit the carbon-arc globes—whose brittle black rods, if you look closely through the dazzle of a cast-iron standard, are no longer manufactured carbon at all, but dense, slow-burning tapers of compressed horn-black and tallow, pushed upward fraction by fraction from inside the hollow lamp-posts by blind, tube-dwelling worms that extrude their own calcified waste straight into the electric arc to be vaporized into steady, shadowless white light.

Even the margins of the town have their specialized wardens. Walk all the way out to the perimeter, where the vitrified pavers meet the smooth grey chert of the embankment. Here the noise of the mills and transformers presses against the concave stone rim and bounces back inward, creating a quiet, dead-air acoustic trough a yard wide all the way around the saucer’s edge.

Lining this seam, spaced as evenly as dentils on a cornice, sit creatures the size and color of cobbles. They are flat, heavy brachiopods—or things converged upon that ancient, two-shelled plan—anchored to the chert by tough, leathery stalks. Their upper valves stand perpetually cracked open a quarter of an inch toward the bright, pearly sky, showing a fringe of stiff, black, oily bristles. They never move toward the machines; they never join the rattling white ribbons of plate-arthropods on the plazas. They simply face outward, ringing the entire five-hundred-yard depression in an unbroken collar of snapping stone lips. 

Should a stray wind-blown seed drift over the chert rim from whatever mute, unseen country lies beyond the embankment, or a lost, soft-bodied meadow insect blunder down into the cellar-cool bowl of air, three or four of the cobble-shells click shut with the sharp *tock* of billiard balls. Acidic digestive foam dissolves the intruder in minutes; the next trickle of condensation washes the resulting nitrates down the polished brick apron toward the nearest storm grate, where the pale eels are waiting to strain it out and send it down into the dark sumps—making strictly certain that no grain of soil ever forms, no root ever pries a curbstone loose, and nothing green ever interrupts the roaring, greased, immaculate eternity of the works.
