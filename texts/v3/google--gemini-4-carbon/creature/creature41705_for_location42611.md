If you stand in the wide plaza long enough, your eyes adjust to the speed of the basin, and you realize it is not unstaffed at all. You were simply looking for things that dart and hurry.

What appears at first to be a heavy, hooded stanchion beside the flywheel slowly shifts its weight, breaking the silhouette of the mill-tower behind it. A person steps away from the bark. 

They wear an oiled canvas coat dyed the exact muted ochre of iron-stain, hanging stiffly to mid-calf over wool trousers the color of wet slate. When they walk across the root-plate, they make no clicking sound. Every boot in the basin is soled in thick, cross-cut slabs of boiled cork and raw crepe, stitched with waxed twine. Iron-shod heels are an unthinkable profanity here; to bruise the living floor is to invite a slow, stubborn swelling of scar-tissue that might, over a decade, throw an entire courtyard’s drainage out of plumb. So the townsfolk move with a flat-footed, rolling glide, knees slightly bent, feeling the subtle flex of the valve-roots beneath them the way a sailor reads the heave of a deck through their soles.

Up close, the basin’s keepers—they call themselves *tenders*, or sometimes *cambials*, depending on their guild—look remarkably weathered, though not by sun. Under that shadowless coastal ceiling, their skin has the cool, matte pallor of dry birch-parchment, finely netted around the eyes from squinting across bright, windy expanses of silver wood. Look at their hands, though, and you see the work. The palms and knuckles are permanently stained a deep, purplish-black—*tannate*, the unmistakable chemical marriage of raw tree-tannin and cast-iron scale. Beneath their fingernails sits a dark rim of tallow and pine-pitch that soap never quite touches. They smell clean and sharp, like turpentine, sheep’s wool, and cold rain on steel.

This one—an older woman with silver hair braided tight and tucked beneath a peaked felt cap—does not carry a hammer or a wrench in her belt loop, though a heavy spanner hangs from a strap at her hip. In her hand she holds a *listening-rod*: a three-foot spindle of dense, unvarnished boxwood fitted with a flared brass ear-cup at one end. 

She presses the tip of the rod against the scarred bole beside the flywheel, leans the side of her face into the brass cup, and closes her eyes. 

For two full minutes she does not move. Her breathing slows until the rise and fall of her heavy coat matches the *shhh-clack* of the bark-sheathed pistons. She is listening past the surface noise of the iron crank, down into the dark hydraulic vaults of the cambium, reading the deep, slushy rush of sap through chambered heartwood. She is checking for cavitation, for the faint, wheezing flutter of a tired valve-leaf deep underground, or the dull *thud* of resin crystallizing where it shouldn’t. Satisfied, she straightens, pulls a tin of mutton-fat and beeswax from her pocket, and thumbs a smear of grease onto an exposed steel pin where the wintergreen-scented vapor has begun to bead too heavily. Then she moves on, unhurried, angling across the wind-scoured court toward a switch-house.

There are perhaps four hundred people living in the parish-wide bowl, though you rarely see more than half a dozen in the open at once. The basin does not require frantic labor; it requires *presence*. 

They are a quiet, dry-humored people whose whole conception of time has been stretched out upon the rack of arboriculture. In a town where you cannot build a shed in a weekend, where adding a new room to a workshop means setting an iron collar today so your grandchildren might bolt a roof-truss to the resulting limb forty years hence, impatience is treated not as a sin, exactly, so much as a mild form of idiocy. 

You can hear this in the way they speak when two of them cross paths beneath the braided bridge-boughs. Their voices are pitched low—shouting across the hard, curved wooden plazas creates a rattling echo that muddles the sound of the pressure-vents—and their sentences are spare, full of long, comfortable pauses. A young apprentice with a satchel of copper shims might stop beside the older woman, the two of them standing shoulder-to-shoulder, looking up at the overhead hopper-vine as its switchbox clicks. Neither speaks until the hopper has cleared fifty feet of cable. Then a few murmured words about the afternoon mist, a slight nod toward a brass gauge whose needle is riding a hair high, and they part ways. Their vocabulary has no hard line between botany and mechanics: a crank is *stiff in the joint*, a sour person has *galled bark*, a well-run household *draws even*.

Follow them out of the plazas, through the low, oval doorways grown into the base of the massive trunks—every threshold has a high, curving lip of smooth wood you must step *over*, like a ship’s bulkhead, so as not to sever the tree’s structural ring—and the chill of the valley vanishes at once.

Inside the residential boles and the staffed galleries of the switch-houses, the air is startlingly warm, humid, and dim. Living wood on this scale generates a slow, metabolic heat, supplemented by copper radiator-pipes carrying warm return-sap from the working dynamos. The interiors are carved and trained into spiraling tiers of rounded, vault-like rooms, smelling deeply of cured cedar, beeswax, dried apples, and simmering broth. Nothing inside is square. Bookshelves, bunks, and workbenches are grafted directly out of the interior heartwood ribs or hung on iron brackets from old, swallowed bolts. 

Here, the tenders shed their stiff oilskins. You find small clusters of them sitting in pools of yellow light cast by low-voltage filaments powered by the town’s own slow-turning dynamos. A pair of men in sleeveless wool vests sit across a burl-table playing a quiet, intricate game with polished iron nuts and pale wooden pegs. Near the inner wall, a teenager with spectacles—a junior *grafter*—sits with a whetstone, bringing a surgical edge to a curved budding-knife while a kettle mutters on a tiny induction plate bolted to a copper bus-bar. 

Children live here, too, though they are raised with an instinctive bodily caution that makes outsiders think them solemn. There are few toddlers tearing recklessly across the plazas; as soon as a child can walk, they are given soft felt slippers and taught what can be touched and what must be left alone—never pry at amber resin, never hang from a copper conduit, never drop salt on the floor. In the warm interior galleries, three or four of them might lie on their bellies on a braided rag rug, watching one of the town’s rare house-pets.

They do not keep dogs or cats; claws and urine have no place on a living substrate, and there are no mice in a grainless town without cellars of loose earth. Instead, moving slowly along the warm, damp seams where iron beams meet the interior walls, you will find *scale-backs*—plump, fist-sized isopods with segmented shells the color of tarnished pewter. They are semi-domesticated, utterly silent, and prized by every household. They graze endlessly on wood-mites, sour mold, and the stray fungal spores that drift in on the afternoon mist, keeping the grafting-seams clean. When a child touches one gently with a finger, it does not curl up; it simply lifts its antennae, tasting the salt on their skin, and trundles steadily onward like a tiny armored tram.

Higher up in the dark, suppressed mats of waxy needles that roof the towers, something else lives, half-kept and half-wild. In the sheltered, misty crawlspaces beneath the horizontal foliage hang rows of heavy wooden skeps housing native stingless bees—small, dark, furry insects adapted to the cool valley air. They crawl over the tiny, resinous cones and hidden blossoms of the canopy-mats, gathering pitch and thin nectar, returning wax and a dark, medicinal honey that tastes sharply of pine-needles and iron-water.

And what is all this quiet, hydraulic industry *for*? 

Step into one of the four-story workshops and you see the town’s real crop. Suspended in frames of heavy timber and iron turnbuckles are rows of living blanks—axles, ship-knees, gear-wheels, turbine-blades, and arched bridge-ribs—growing directly from manifold-branches fed by the basin’s immense turgor. Under the watchful eye of master arborist-engineers, these pieces are being slowly compressed as they grow inside hinged cast-iron molds. Over ten, twenty, fifty years, the basin forces dense, silica-rich wood into those iron forms under tremendous internal pressure, starving the grain of air pockets until the timber becomes *iron-burl*: a substance heavier than water, impervious to rot, practically fireproof, capable of taking a machined thread like mild steel while absorbing vibration in a way no forged metal ever could.

In the workshop, two tenders stand on a scaffold beside a mold the size of a cartwheel. One holds a brass caliper; the other holds a ratchet-spanner three feet long. Together, leaning their modest weight onto the bar, they turn a massive tension-bolt a quarter of an inch—*creak-clink*—tightening the iron corset around the living wood inside. That is their week’s quota on that wheel. Next month, they will turn it another quarter-inch. In six years, when the grain has locked and the sap-veins have sealed themselves off at the collar, they will unbolt the mold, saw the finished gear-blank free, seal the stump with hot pitch, and hoist the piece up to the rim of the fused timber wall, where narrow-gauge railcars from the low valleys come twice a year to trade salt, wool, grain, and pig-iron for parts that will never shatter in a storm or rust in bilge-water.

As the afternoon wears thin and the coastal mist drops lower into the rectangle of sky, thickening in the needle-mats overhead, a change passes through the basin. 

The copper louvers high on the dynamo-towers click shut one by one to hold the day’s warmth. Down on the root-floor, the steady dripping from the cast-iron counterweights quickens as the fog condenses on cold metal, feeding the tiny velvet cushions of emerald moss and cup-fungi below. Across the empty plazas, yellow lights wink on behind thick, bullseye-glass portholes set deep in calloused bark. 

Outside, a last tender crosses the wide courtyard, collar turned up against the damp, carrying a brass oil-can and a hooded lantern. Beneath their cork-soled boots, the great muscular ridges of the burl give a long, slow, settling flex—a deep, sub-audible *thrum* as the entire parish shifts its hydraulic load from the day’s upward draw to the slow, pressurized rest of evening. The tender pauses for a second as the vibration passes up through their ankles, shifts the lantern to their other hand, and steps over a high wooden sill into the warm dark of a tree, pulling the heavy iron-latched door shut behind them without a sound.
