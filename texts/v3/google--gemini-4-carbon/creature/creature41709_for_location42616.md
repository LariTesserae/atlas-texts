To see the architects of the precinct, one has to look away from the violet-lit ribbons of heavy liquid and wait for a star near the zenith to be eclipsed from below. Something standing on an upper causeway will cross the reflection, blotting out the pale smear of a constellation without disturbing a single molecule of the water around itself.

There are nine of them in the seventy-meter block. There have been nine since the granite was split and the water was pinned over the scar.

That hourly, dry, clicking rasp belongs to one of them shifting its stance. Up close—if a violet node happens to drift past at the exact minute one crosses a span—the body does not suggest a swimmer, nor even an animal in the soft, cellular sense of the word. It resembles a tall, narrow armature of dark horn and leaded glass, standing roughly two meters high, though its proportions make height feel incidental, like the height of a tuning fork or a surveyor’s tripod. Its frame is built of flat, overlapping splines of a substance very close to the sintered obsidian of the towers, separated by narrow gaps through which the dark water passes freely. It does not displace a volume of water so much as it occupies a set of planes within it.

When it walks along an unsupported bridge, it moves on four slender, stilted limbs that terminate not in feet, but in blunt, chisel-like nibs of extremely hard, pale ceramic. Each step places a nib down onto the matte architectural stone—or onto the razor edge of a cleaved feldspar crystal—with microscopic precision. *Click.* A pause of forty seconds while the weight transfers without a tremor. *Rasp*, as a rear nib draws across two millimeters of raw basalt to find a microscopic facet of quartz. They walk right beside the shard of granite balanced on its point, passing within a finger’s breadth of it, and the shard does not even quiver. To leave a wake in this precinct would be the equivalent of shouting in a library or tracking mud across an altar; their entire anatomy is baffled and slotted to slip through the seven-degree water in strict laminar silence.

They have no eyes, because there is almost nothing here to see, and what little light exists is merely a byproduct of the heavy-liquid conduits shedding waste energy. Instead, the upper third of a resident’s body opens out into an asymmetric crown of thin, rigid vanes—like the plates of an old air-capacitor, or the baleen of a winter-starved whale, if baleen were forged from dull silver and grey slate. 

Through these vanes, they read the block. 

In water this still, bounded by six perfect Euclidean mirrors of phase-locked tension, every physical object casts an acoustic and electrostatic shadow. The sub-audible harmonic hum that holds the water to the mountain is not just a fence; to the residents, it is daylight. That deep, sternum-felt vibration bounces endlessly off the six walls, intersecting with the jagged talus, the twenty-four roofless courts, the drifting hound-sized mantles, and the bodies of the other eight residents. Standing motionless in an open rectangular frame on the middle terrace, an inhabitant lets that standing wave wash through its cranial vanes. It feels the entire cathedral-sized ocean at once, down to the cooling *tink* of a hairline crack in the bedrock thirty meters away, down to the slow, ten-minute reach of the three-spined iron-sulfide star beneath the bridge.

They are creatures of deep, abyssal pressure who have brought only their parlor—their quietest room—up into the thin sky, leaving the crushing weight of their native world behind while keeping its stillness, its cold, and its liquid density. 

Or perhaps they are not from an ocean at all, in the planetary sense, but from the dark, briny lenses locked beneath kilometers of ice on moons that never knew a sun, where water exists only in sealed pockets of fractured rock. It would explain why their architecture has no concept of weather, horizon, or cover, and why they chose to quarry fresh, unweathered granite high above the timberline before setting their water down upon it. Down in the valleys, water is noisy, full of mud and rot and dissolved oxygen and tumbling stones. Up here, the rock was clean when they broke it.

What they actually *do* in the dark takes hours to recognize as activity.

Near the base of the tallest sound-drinking tower, two of them stand facing one another across a sunken court, four meters apart. Neither has moved a limb since moonrise. Between them, suspended at mid-height in the charcoal gloom, they are tending a structure that isn't visible until one of the dull violet pinpricks drifts through the fluorocarbon ribbon nearby. Even then, it looks only like a faint distortion in the water—a localized pocket of altered refractive index, like heat shimmer over summer asphalt, folded into nested, polyhedral facets the size of a clenched fist.

By minutely adjusting the angle of the metallic vanes on their crowns—a tilt of half a degree, held for twenty minutes—the two residents alter the local electromagnetic and acoustic standing waves between them. They are tuning the water itself, coaxing dissolved ions leached from the fresh-cleaved basalt and feldspar to align along nodes of interference. Atom by atom, over days and weeks, a lattice precipitates out of the cold water in the space between their heads: a crystal grown without gravity’s distortion, buffered from wind, buffered from thermal drift, built out of the mountain's raw chemistry and the quiet between two minds.

This is why there are no doors, and why the buildings are frames and cantilevered ledges. The architecture is an instrument bench. Every open doorway is an aperture for the hum; every tall, sound-absorbing pylon is a baffle meant to carve out a dead zone of absolute acoustic zero behind it, a shadow of pure quiet where delicate, slow-forming things can be held in suspension without shattering.

The grey, pelagically adapted mantle—the flat, hound-sized creature hovering near the vertical conduit—is neither a pet nor a stray. Presently, one of the stilted residents descends from an upper terrace. It does not use a stair; it simply steps off the edge of a plaza, folding its four ceramic-tipped limbs inward so its slotted, obsidian-like splines align vertically, and sinks straight down like a plumb bob dropped through oil. No bubbles, no flutter. Halfway down, it spreads two narrow lateral plates, checks its descent without turbulence, and comes to rest on a fractured basalt shelf beside the hovering mantle.

The resident extends a secondary appendage from the vertical seam of its chest—not a hand, but a bundle of pale, flexible filaments finer than spider silk, resembling the feelers of the three-spined brittle star under the bridge, suggesting that everything alive in this block shares an ancient, sunless rootstock. The filaments brush the rippling margin of the grey mantle's tissue. Instantly, the microscopic sine waves along the mantle's edge change frequency, syncing with the resident’s touch.

The mantle is living memory, or perhaps a biological filter—an engineered cousin bred to drift through the orthogonal corridors and absorb stray kinetic energy, swallowing the faintest eddy left by a cooling rock or a shifting limb, converting unwanted motion into the slow metabolic ripple of its own grey flesh. And as it hangs beside the fluorocarbon aqueduct, it harvests the spent violet luminescence—which is not merely light, but tiny, geometric packets of exhausted catalytic matrix—taking them in through pores along its underside, stripping them of stray mineral fouling from the bedrock, and extruding them hours later back into the heavy ribbon at the upper terrace, dark and cold and ready to be recharged at the pylons.

As the mountain night wears on toward dawn, a third resident rises to the very top of the water-block.

It climbs the twenty-five-meter sound-drinking tower by placing its ceramic nibs into microscopic notches along the tower's flank, ascending with the patience of a clock hand. Where the tower ends, fifteen meters of open water remain below the flat, mirror ceiling. From the crown of the tower, the resident releases its hold and alters its internal buoyancy—perhaps compressing a bladder of dense oil within its dark carapace—and drifts straight upward along an invisible plumb line until the topmost tips of its silver-grey vanes stop three centimeters short of the phase-boundary.

From thirty millimeters away, the outside world is a roaring, arid chaos. Just beyond that skin of velvet-thick tension, a pre-dawn wind at minus twelve degrees Celsius is tearing ice crystals off the upper granite spire and hurling them sideways into the void. The stars above are wheeling toward a pale, threatening stain of indigo in the east.

The resident hangs just beneath the mirror, upside-down to its own reflection, its slotted dark body doubled against the stars. It does not push its vanes through the membrane; the dry air outside would rime its wet plates with frost in a heartbeat and deafen its pressure-sense into white noise. Instead, it holds its crown parallel to the skin of the ceiling, feeling the ghostly, attenuated percussion of wind-borne ice grains striking the phase-lock and vanishing into the boundary equations. 

When the first direct spear of alpine sunlight finally clears the eastern ridge and strikes the forty-meter vertical wall of water, the slate-blue curtains at the block's edges brighten abruptly to the color of glacial ice. Faint shafts of alien, yellow-white warmth lance partway into the charcoal dark before the water and the matte towers drink them down. 

The resident beneath the ceiling immediately folds its vanes tight against its narrow skull, as someone in a dark room squints against a struck match. Down on the terraces below, the other eight pause in their slow geometries or withdraw into the deep acoustic and optical shadows behind the basalt crags and obsidian frames, waiting with mineral patience inside their seven-degree dusk for the mountain to turn away from the sun again, and give them back the clean, measurable dark.
