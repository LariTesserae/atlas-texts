Watch the west wall after the valve slams shut. Wait until the crabs have finished their dry, clicking rush back toward the center, and let your eyes adjust to the pewter gloom about twelve feet up, right where the massive, dimpled greywacke blocks give way to the first stepped courses of greenstone. 

A slab of tallow-colored mineral crust detaches itself from the stone. 

It does not fall. It shifts sideways with the slow, fluid deliberation of pouring cold oil, resolves into four long limbs and a low, flattened torso, and reaches up to hook three chalk-white fingers over a slick slate lip. 

There are three of them in the vault right now—an elder female, a smaller male with a calcified lump over one shoulder where a fall broke his collarbone years ago, and a half-grown juvenile clinging to the curve of the masonry ten yards further south. There have never been more than five at a time, and sometimes for a decade there have only been two. The chamber cannot feed a crowd, and its echoing stone does not forgive carelessness.

If you hauled one out into the direct sun—though there is no door wide enough to drag them through; their ancestors came down the crooked light-chimneys generations ago, or crawled up through the dry-season drainpipes as starving children and grew too broad-ribbed to ever leave—you would know at once what they used to be. The architecture of the skull, the curve of the pelvis, the opposing thumb: all human. Still human, though pared down and remade by twenty generations of stone, cold water, and resonant dark. 

They are small, scarcely four feet from crown to heel if they ever stood upright, which they do not. Even down on the dished cobbles, they move in a wide-kneed, frog-hipped crouch, keeping their center of gravity an inch above the slick basalt, knuckles brushing the lime-ash seams. Their skin has the exact matte, waxy luster of the mineral flowstone on the walls—a pale, bloodless tallow-grey, darkened across the spine and shoulders by patches of harmless black lichen that take root in the fine creases of their hide and never quite wash off. To keep out the relentless sixty-degree chill of the mist without firewood or wool, they carry a dense, uniform padding of rubbery fat over every muscle, smoothing away the hollows of their throats and wrists until their limbs look sleek and cylindrical, like a seal’s or a giant salamander’s. When they exhale after a hard climb, their breath puffs pale grey from wide, flanged nostrils that can pinch nearly shut against a spray of ammonia-tainted water.

Watch the female climb. 

She does not scramble or heave. Every step of the corbelled dome has been mapped in the muscle-memory of her family for centuries, worn ever so slightly flatter along three invisible, spiral highways where thousands of bare passes have polished the slate. Her fingers and toes are startlingly long, splayed wide like spatulas at the tips, the nails thickened into blunt, horn-colored scoops that hook into the crumbling lime mortar between the courses. The skin on her palms and soles is deeply corrugated, perpetually pruned into deep white ridges that channel water away from the point of contact so she doesn't hydroplane off the wet glaze. She presses her pale belly flat against the weeping stone, utilizing surface tension the way the vault-newts do, ascending forty feet of overhang without dislodging a single crumb of mortar, completely silent beneath the frying-fat hiss of the room’s eternal rain.

She is heading for the third tier of earthenware jars.

As she nears the occupied vessels, the oil-dark birds sense the shift in air currents—or hear the faint, wet peel of her palm lifting from a stone—and panic erupts along the curve of the dome. Half a dozen heavy-breasted shapes burst tail-first from their clay throats, wings whistling sharply in the cold air, while twenty more duck inward and shriek into their pots. 

At three yards' distance, the amplified *boom-chatter* of twenty resonating jars is physical; it hits the chest like a mallet blow and makes the water film on the nearby slate shiver in standing waves. The female doesn't flinch. She doesn't even squint. Over her ears, thick fleshy tragi—cartilaginous flaps grown bulbous and muscular—fold neatly inward like the valves of a diving mammal’s nose, sealing her ear canals down to a pinprick. Beneath that seal, she hears the deafening roar only through the bones of her jaw and ribs, reduced to a navigable, three-dimensional map of vibrations. Her eyes, wide and heavily domed with dark irises that swallow almost all the sclera, track the swirling birds through the slate-blue gloom without blinking; clear, oily membranes sweep sideways from the inner corners every few seconds to squeegee away the dripping condensation.

She reaches a jar rimmed in thick white lime, braces her left foot on a thumb-breadth ledge of greenstone, and slides her right arm up to the elbow into the rough terracotta throat. 

Inside, it is dry, warm from the bird that just fled, and rank with centuries of compacted guano and regurgitated chitin. Her fingers walk delicately over two warm, speckled eggs—she leaves both; taking more than one from a clutch with two eggs, or any from a clutch with one, is a mistake her kind stopped making long before they forgot words—and instead scoops up a fat, blind squab from the neighboring pot, killing it instantly with a practised pinch of thumb and forefinger against the base of its skull before it can set its jar booming. Before descending, she uses her thick thumbnail to scrape a curl of nitre-rich white crust from the rim of a cracked, empty pot, popping it into her mouth like salt-candy. Calcium and salt are the currencies of survival where nothing grows in dirt.

Halfway across the chamber, high up under the easternmost light-shaft, the juvenile is doing maintenance. 

He hangs by one arm and one hooked heel from a slick cornice, seventy feet above the center drain-slates, bathed in the cold, sourceless twilight bouncing down the lime-washed chimney. In his free hand he holds a wedge of grey flint—not knapped here, for there is no flint in the masonry, but washed in through the northern groundwater conduits decades ago and cherished like a limb. With slow, rhythmic scrapes that ring *tink, tink, tink* around the vast bell of the ceiling, he clears a clogged lateral runoff groove. 

A century of tallow-glaze had dammed the channel, sending a cold curtain of seep-water pouring directly over the sleeping-ledge twelve feet below. He chips the mineral lip away until the trickle catches its ancient mason’s path again, sliding obediently sideways around the curve of the dome, away from the dry pocket where the three of them sleep heaped together like slick, breathing boulders. While he works, a hand-sized vault-newt glides right past his face and slaps wetly onto his bare thigh, mistaking his pale, lichen-splotched skin for greywacke. Before the amphibian can peel its suctioned toes free, the boy’s free hand blurs—quick and cold-blooded in its precision—pinning it flat. He bites behind its eyes, swallows the tail whole, and tucks the broad, rib-flapped torso into a pouch made from the braided, twisted gut of one of the drain-eels, slung diagonally across his chest.

Down below, the air pressure shifts. 

Neither of the climbers looks down, and the older male—who has been sitting motionless as an idol on the lowest course of ox-sized blocks near the north wall—lifts his chin. They know the flush is coming twenty seconds before the crustaceans do. Down inside the masonry behind the wooden clack-valves, the rising column of groundwater compresses the air trapped in the unseen pipe, sending a sub-audible groan shuddering through the basalt cobbles. The male feels it through his wide, flat heel-bones. 

He rises into a low, fluid lope and slides down the polished slope of the floor before the valve even moves. In his right hand is the vault’s heaviest tool: a yard-long shaft of dense, yellowed whalebone—salvaged generations ago when a fourth conduit’s rotted gate gave way and washed its hinge-pin into the room—ground against the sandstone blocks into a flat, beveled gaff with a deep notch carved near the tip.

*Clunk.*

The heavy timber slab slams upward. Frothy, ice-cold water vomits across the cobbles. The needle-legged crabs go clattering upslope in a copper wave, brushing right over the male’s ankles without breaking stride; he ignores them entirely. Crabs are shell and cold slime, starvation food for lean winters when the midges die off and the birds stop laying. He sprints—a strange, skating, four-limbed bound, pushing off the slick stones with his free knuckles like an ape on ice—straight into the rushing hand’s-breadth sheet of white water, racing the surge toward the dead center of the chamber.

He reaches the circular ring of perforated slate slabs at the exact instant the flood hits the holes and roars downward. 

He plants both wide, corrugated feet into the gaps between the basalt cobbles just outside the slate ring, drops his hips until his rump is submerged in the freezing torrent, and waits, whalebone gaff poised two inches above the churning foam. 

Up through the wrist-wide perforations come the hunters. Half a dozen slick, olive-spotted bodies erupt against the sucking current, armored skulls swinging blindly, jaws clacking wetly in the spray as they snap after drowning stoneflies and tumbling bird feathers. A thick one—nearly five feet long, thick as a man's calf, scarred around the gills from an old encounter—lunges forty inches out of the centermost hole to snatch at a stunned crab spinning in the eddy. 

The male doesn't stab; you cannot pierce those overlapping skull-plates with bone, and a wounded eel-thing can pull a grown climber’s arm down a wrist-wide drain hole and break it at the elbow before drowning him in three inches of water. Instead, he sweeps the whalebone gaff laterally along the wet slate, hooks the carved notch squarely under the flare of the creature’s armored gill-slit as it tries to retract, and throws his whole compact, fat-padded weight backward.

For twenty seconds, while the clay jars overhead boom with the excited shrieks of skimming birds and white water churns around his thighs, it is a silent, terrible tug-of-war. The eel’s tail is knotted around the stone partitions in the flooded under-channels below the floor; its slick, muscular trunk whips violently, slapping the male’s ribs with audible *thwacks* that would bruise an ordinary human black, smearing his tallow-colored skin with thick ropes of clear, foul-smelling slime. The male locks his knees, curls his prehensile toes into the calcified shell-bed between the cobbles, leans back until his spine nearly touches the rushing water, and simply outlasts the siphon. 

Behind him, the northern basin runs dry. The timber clack-valve drops shut with its deep, room-shaking *thud*. 

As the upper jars hum their sympathetic minor chord, the suction down the slate holes falters and breaks into a throat-clearing rattle. Deprived of the roaring current that tells it where down is, the eel-thing momentarily uncoils its tail in the dark below to readjust its grip. That half-second of slack is all the male needs. With a short, grunting bark—a sound pitched low and nasal, clipped short so it won't bounce endlessly around the curved walls and deafen him—he heaves hand-over-hand up the bone shaft, dragging five feet of thrashing olive muscle clear out onto the draining cobbles, rolling away from its snapping shovel-snout to bring the butt of the whalebone down three times, hard and precise, right where the skull-armor meets the spine.

Silence rushes back into the room, stitched through instantly by ten thousand dripping beads of water. *Plink. Plink. Hiss.*

Within a minute, the female and the juvenile have descended the west and east curves, sliding the last ten feet down smooth tongues of mineral glaze on their tough, calloused haunches. They gather not at the center—nobody lingers near the grates, even between flushes—but high up the gentle dish of the floor near the south wall, where the basalt is merely damp rather than slick. 

They do not speak in sentences. In an empty stone bell seventy paces wide, sibilants turn into blinding white noise and sharp consonants ricochet back from six directions at once until meaning dissolves into chaos. Instead, as they crouch shoulder-to-shoulder to skin the eel with flakes of split slate and cracked crab-shell, they press their temples or bare forearms lightly together and hum. 

They borrow the language of the room. Low, chest-deep notes—pitched deliberately in the exact hollow fifths and minor thirds of the wind-blown jars overhead—pass between them, modulated by small shifts of throat and jaw. From ten paces away, a listener wouldn't know they were making a sound at all; it would blend seamlessly into the faint, braided moaning of the upper dome as the draft shifts through the light-chimneys. Up close, pressed skin to skin, the vibrations carry everything needed: *the east groove is clear; the bird-pots on tier four are laying early; my shoulder aches with the cold rain; eat the liver first.*

Nothing of the catch goes back down the drain. They eat the dense, oily meat raw while it is still twitching, along with the warm squab and the newt, chewing the soft ribs and small bird-bones to a paste for the marrow and lime. The tough, slime-slick skin is scraped clean on the dimpled face of a greywacke block—one specific block, stained dark and greasy as old butcher’s oak by five hundred years of the exact same task—to be twisted later into cordage or chewed soft as a waterproof wrap for an infant. The heavy armored skull and spine are tucked neatly into a dry niche at the base of the wall. Over generations, those niches have become miniature ossuaries: thousands of eel skulls, bird sterna, and shed crab carapaces stacked with obsessive, geometric neatness into every gap where ancient mortar has fallen out, shoring up the lower masonry with bone where the lime-ash washed away. 

They are part of the vault's maintenance now, as mechanical and essential to its survival as the counterweighted whalebone valves. Without the juvenile chipping the tallow-crust from the upper channels, water would freeze in the winter drafts and wedge the greenstone corbels apart; without the elders wedging thousands of oiled bones into the eroded seams at the floor's perimeter, the pounding flushes would have undermined the ox-sized foundation blocks three centuries ago. They do not know why the room was built, or what the clouds look like above the crooked, lime-washed chimneys, or why they keep repairing the seams with fish skulls. It is simply the shape of the world: you keep the water moving toward the middle, you do not break the clay jars, and you never take the second egg.

Overhead, the slate-blue dusk thickens by two shades toward slate-black; outside, unseen, evening has come on, and wind is rising across the hidden mouths of the six shafts. 

The hundreds of earthenware throats high in the invisible apex catch the drop in pressure and begin to sing—a slow, immense, organ-like swell that fills the sixty yards of misty emptiness until the very air feels dense enough to swim in. Down by the south wall, the three pale figures wipe their hands on patches of damp wall-moss, hook their broad, chalky fingers into the dimples left by stone hammers before history had a name here, and climb smoothly upward into the dark, rising tier by tier into the booming chord until the stone takes them in and the floor below is completely empty again, waiting for the next *clunk* of the gate.
