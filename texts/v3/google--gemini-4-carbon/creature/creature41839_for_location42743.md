To spot someone crossing the basin, you have to watch the viaducts the way you watch the flywheels. At first, a figure two districts away looks like a surveying stanchion or an oxidized bollard standing on the vitrified brick. Only if you look away and look back five minutes later do you realize it has advanced fifty yards toward a manifold house. 

Nothing that lives here hurries, because the air will not allow it. A mile above the sea, saturated to the point of sweating and vibrating with ninety decibels of sub-bass, the atmosphere is thick and taxing. If you run, your lungs fill with warm mist and your heart stumbles against the competing rhythm of the ground. So the people of the basin walk with the *pendulum gait*—a heavy, flat-footed, rolling stride, knees slightly bent, letting their weight settle entirely through one heel before lifting the other. 

Up close, they are broad, thick-chested people, barrel-ribbed from high-altitude breathing, with skin the color of pale tea and damp clay, permanently flushed at the cheekbones and knuckles from the ambient warmth. Nobody wears cloth outdoors. Woven fabric rots in a month here, mildewing in the warm damp and fraying from the abrasive mineral mist. Instead, they wear long, sleeveless aprons and split-legged wraps of cured eel-hide and vulcanized rubber, oiled with tallow rendered from the canal crabs, fastened with heavy toggles of horn or tarnished brass. Their hair is universally cropped to the skull or braided tight and greased; loose hair catches the mist, turns heavy, and whips stingingly in the downdrafts near the Siphons. Their boots are knee-high, cast with thick, lugged soles of cork and reclaimed tire-rubber—not to keep the water out, which is impossible, warm water simply sloshing in and out through brass-grommeted weep holes at the instep—but to insulate their bones from the relentless tremor of the flagstones.

They are not deaf, though an outsider shouting inches from their face would get no reaction at all. Over generations, their ears have simply abandoned the frequencies owned by the Siphons. Children born in the basin lose their mid-range and bass hearing before they are ten, the tiny hairs of the cochlea flattening down like reeds in a flood. What remains to them is the extreme high end—a thin, crystalline band of perception far above the cataract roar—and an exquisitely sensitive somatic literacy. 

Out on the causeways, they do not waste breath. To watch two basin-dwellers meet at an intersection of wet brick is to watch a slow, deliberate choreography. At a hundred yards, before faces can be read through the sunlit glare off the puddles, they use "the semaphore"—a loose, wide-armed vernacular of posture and silhouette. An elbow raised with the forearm hanging slack means *pressure dropping in the fourth quadrant*; both hands clasped behind the neck while walking means *heading off-shift, carrying nothing urgent*. 

When they stop face-to-face, they reach out and rest two fingers lightly against the hollow of each other’s collarbones, or press the heels of their palms against the other’s sternum, or simply stand with the sides of their heavy boots touching. Sometimes, if they are standing near a copper conduit or an iron railing that runs all the way back to a boiler house, they don't touch each other at all—they both lay a flat hand on the warm, sweating metal six feet apart. They speak in low, glottal hums and clicks generated deep in the throat, pitching their voices *into* their own ribcages so the vibrations travel down their arms, into the pipe or directly into the listener’s chest. To an outsider, two mechanics standing by a sluice gate look like statues holding onto a railing for balance, mouths closed, eyes half-shut against the bright highland sun, utterly silent beneath the roaring sky, while through the rusted iron between their palms passes a dry, intricate gossip about a weeping gasket three miles east or whose cousin took a fall on the slick steps of Reservoir Nine.

For sharper, quicker warnings across fifty feet of mud—*live wire down*, *crab in the mud-trap*—they carry slender striking-rods of high-carbon steel hung from their belts. Tapped once against a vitrified brick or a brass rivet, the rod lets out a tiny, needle-sharp *ping* at twelve thousand hertz that slices cleanly over the top of the bass roar like a bird’s shadow crossing a gorge.

To hear their actual voices, you have to follow them indoors, which in the basin means going down, or going behind lead.

Their dwellings are not built separately from the infrastructure; they are parasitic pockets hollowed out of its mass. A family—usually six or eight adults and two or three quiet, wide-eyed children—will live inside the forty-foot-thick masonry pier of an aqueduct, or in the dry, vaulted cellar beneath a switching station. You enter through an airlock of sorts: double doors of three-inch-thick ironwood padded with felt made from crushed translucent reeds and tar, wedged shut with heavy throw-levers. 

When that inner lever drops into its bracket, the world vanishes. 

The sudden absence of the roar is nauseating to the uninitiated; the eardrums balloon outward, and for a full minute all you can hear is the frantic, wet surging of your own carotid arteries. Even here, behind twelve feet of basalt block and calcified mortar, the Siphons are present, reduced to a ghost—a sub-audible pressure wave that makes the oil in the shallow brass lamps tremble in concentric rings. 

Inside these chambers, it is dry, dim, and fiercely clean. Ducts of warm water diverted from the aquifers run beneath slate floors, keeping the air dry enough to store paper, salt, and wool. Only here do the basin-dwellers shed their stiff rubber and oiled hides, draping themselves in loose, soft shifts spun from imported lowland cotton—pale yellows, faded indigos, bleached whites that never see the rust-stained outdoors. And here, their voices emerge: surprisingly high, breathy, flute-like whispers, full of sibilants and soft whistles, as though even behind twelve feet of stone they are unwilling to trespass on the bass register that belongs to the valley.

What do they do here, in a city that takes six hours to open a gate by a foot? 

They are not its builders, and they are not quite its masters. If you ask an archivist in one of the dry vaults beneath the central manifold who laid the cyclopean foundations, they will show you ledgers bound in alum-tanned eel skin going back four hundred years, and the earliest entries are already maintenance logs. *Replaced third bronze baffle on Siphon 44. Chipped nine hundredweight of white scale from the West Weir.* If the city was built to power some lowland empire over the whale-backed ridges—sending gigawatts down the drooping high-tension cables—nobody in the basin talks about the buyers, or cares whether anyone is still at the other end of the lines. Perhaps cities fifty miles below still burn bright all night on the valley’s endless thermal bleed; perhaps those cables just ground out into some forgotten slag-heap beyond the horizon. In the basin, the circuit is closed. Power is generated to turn the winches that lift the gates that cool the bearings of the turbines that generate the power.

Their entire culture is built around *descaling* and *trimming*.

The water that sweats from the earth is rich with dissolved calcium, silica, and iron carbonate. Left alone for ten years, a bronze fluted baffle would choke solid with mineral travertine; a forty-foot flywheel would grow lopsided with stone crust, throw its axle out of true by half an inch, and tear its masonry housing apart over the course of a month. The city wants to turn back into a mountain. The people are what keep it a machine.

They work in "gangs" of three or four, though a gang may be spread across half a mile of flooded plaza, connected only by sightlines. Their tools are heavy, blunt, and non-sparking: phosphor-bronze chisels, lead-weighted rawhide mallets, long-handled scrapers of spring steel, grease-guns the size of churns filled with grey graphitic tallow. You will see a woman sixty years old lashed by a leather harness to the rim of a moving flywheel. She does not stop the wheel to work on it; stopping a Siphon requires four days of pressure-shunting across twelve adjacent towers and creates terrifying harmonic beats in the valley's acoustic chord. Instead, she rides it. As the rim rises forty feet into the bright amber sunlight over the course of sixty seconds, she chips rhythmically at the mustard-colored lichen and mineral rime along a single rivet-seam. As her section arcs over the top and descends slowly toward the dark, churning splash-pit below, she unhooks her lanyard, steps calmly onto the masonry lip as the iron passes level with her boots, walks around the axle housing, and hooks onto the ascending side again.

Because everything moves at the speed of geology, their minds run on long, overlapping clocks. Impatience is considered a mild form of altitude sickness, seen occasionally in toddlers before they learn better, or in someone running a fever. A basin-dweller thinks nothing of setting a brass wedge into a hairline crack in a retaining wall, giving it two solid blows with a lead maul, and walking away for three weeks while the thermal expansion of the afternoon sun and the cool shrinkage of the highland midnight slowly walk the stone back into alignment.

Their diet comes from the warm flats between the causeways. In the shallow, stepped retention basins where the water is richest in iron, families tend "rust-gardens"—acres of submerged terracotta tiles seeded with thick, gelatinous mats of burgundy and ochre thermophilic algae, harvested with wooden rakes and pressed into dense, savory cakes that taste of miso, iron, and boiled marrow. They trap the blind white eels in woven copper-wire fykes set across the flooded intersections, smoking them over smoldering bricks of dried peat dug from the valley’s ochre rims. They eat the pale, flat canal crabs, though never the ones that bask on the transformer casings—those taste bitterly of ozone and copper sulfate and are left alone, treated with a fond, superstitious tolerance as fellow technicians. When a crab grows so old that mineral scale cements its joints slow, a passing line-walker will sometimes pause, pull a bronze scraper from their belt, flick the stone off the creature's carapace with two practiced strokes, and walk on without looking back.

Nothing is louder than their funerals, which are entirely silent. When someone dies, there is no ground dry enough to bury them and no wood spare enough to burn them. Instead, they are wrapped in clean white cotton, carried out at dusk when the highland sky goes violet and the vapor plumes turn rose-gold at the inversion layer, and lowered into one of the deep, boiling calcifying pools near the central vents—waters too hot and mineral-heavy even for the eels. Within a season, the warm earth coats them in smooth, rust-red and bone-white stone, indistinguishable from the foundations of the boiler houses.

If you stand on one of the whale-backed ridges at twilight looking down into the twenty-mile bowl, you will not see a single streetlamp. They have all the electricity in the world, humming fat and hot through the drooping cables, yet they hang no lights outside; light attracts nothing here, and they know every worn brick and rounded iron step through the soles of their boots. Below you, the drowned pavements mirror the fading sky in vast, broken squares of slate-blue. Tiny, dark figures move along the causeways at their slow, rolling pace, converging on the massive stone piers as the mountain chill comes down to meet the rising steam, sealing themselves behind lead and oak, leaving the night—as they leave the day—entirely to the water, the wheels, and that colossal, unwavering chord.
