If you hang neutral in the narrow trench between the primary rectifier banks and the east brick retaining wall, letting your eyes adjust past the amber smear of a nearby panel blister until the violet corona deeper in the ironwork begins to separate stem from conduit, you will eventually realize that one of the lead-sheathed cable bundles is breathing.

Not with lungs, and not quickly. Every six or seven minutes, a length of grey, lime-encrusted casing as thick as a bolster swells almost imperceptibly along its flank, drawing warm, mineral-dense water in through a row of slit-valves hidden under flaking scales of calcium carbonate, holds it while a faint shudder runs down its length, and lets it seep back out through the fringes of its mantle without stirring a single floating spore.

The ward’s true custodian—or at least its largest deliberate inhabitant—does not swim. Swimming requires clearance, and clearance does not exist here. Instead, what lives in the drowned alleys is something built on the principle of a putty-knife and a banyan root: a solitary, sprawling, shell-less mollusk—or a colony so fused at the nerve-cords it amounts to the same thing—whose ancestry might once have been some giant tropical slug or stray octopus trapped when the surface drains were bricked over, though a century of dissolved copper, stray DC voltage, and eighty-eight-degree water has edited its body into something native only to the machinery.

There is only room for one of it, or perhaps two that move through each other’s territory so slowly they only cross paths twice a year. At rest, wedged into the ninety-degree crease between an oil-cooled transformer and a tier of glazed-tile engine housings, its central mass is no thicker than a folded blanket, yet if it were peeled free of the ironwork and laid flat, it would cover the floor of a parlor. Its dorsal skin has the exact matte, granular texture of old cast iron stabilized with black magnetite, broken here and there by raised, pale-buff calluses indistinguishable from knuckles of boiler-scale. Even the burgundy ribbon-weeds root lightly in the dead outer layers of its mucus coat, so that when it decides to shift six inches to the left over the course of an afternoon, it carries a modest, swaying curtain of shrubbery along with it.

You never see the whole of it at once. The geometry forbids it. You see a wedge of grey-black flesh oozing over the hex-bolts of a flange like warm gutta-percha, conforming so tightly to the metal underneath that the stamped letters of the foundry mark—*Barrow & Sons, 1894*—appear briefly embossed in reverse upon its back before smoothing out again as it flows past. Two yards away, emerging from behind a curtain of feathery hornwort, a flat, ribbon-like limb no wider than a carpenter’s rule slides along a brass valve-stem, feeling for the vibration of the seat.

It has no bones, no cartilage harder than a thumbnail, nothing that can catch or jam. When the twenty-foot mossy flywheel brings a spoke down through the exact gap the creature is currently crossing, the creature does not pull back. It simply thins. Its internal organs—diffuse, braided things arranged in flat packets rather than a round visceral sack—part smoothly within the body wall, flowing to either side of the descending iron bar while a sheet of tough, rubbery dermis bridges the gap, letting the mossy spoke press right into its flesh until the animal is momentarily pinched to the thickness of parchment against a cast-iron stanchion, waiting without distress for the minute-hand pace of the wheel to carry the pressure away.

Its relationship to the ward’s electricity is intimate and metabolic. Animals with high-strung, centralized brains hate stray current; this thing has outsourced half its nervous system to the ambient field. Along the ruffled margins of its skirt runs a bead-work of tiny, tarnished-silver pits—ampullae packed with conductive gel that read the invisible architecture of the shaft better than any eye could. To this creature, the green-black gloom is not dark at all; it is a blazing, three-dimensional cathedral of voltage gradients. It feels the slow, sinusoidal heave of the lower coils, the sharp, prickly hiss of a leaking porcelain bell-insulator, the sour, metallic taste of an over-sulfated galvanic cell three tiers down.

In fact, it feeds on the interfaces where the ward’s chemistry works hardest.

Watch a copper busbar down in the violet ink-light where the microscopic pearls of hydrogen and oxygen fizz thickest. A pale, spatulate tip of the creature’s margin creeps up the masonry, pauses at the edge of the radial ring of voltage-tubers—respecting their sharp, alkaline sting—and drapes itself delicately over the fizzing copper plate, sealing off a hand-sized patch from the water. Underneath that fleshy bell, the tiny gas bubbles collect instead of rising. Specialized mat-bacteria living in the velvet pleats of its underside strip the nascent hydrogen straight off the metal to fix carbon from the mineral water, swelling into a rich, creamy paste of chemosynthetic slime. Every quarter-hour, rows of fine, comb-like cilia sweep that paste inward toward a mouth-slit tucked somewhere deep in the dark behind a counterweight shaft, while the leftover oxygen bubbles are absorbed directly through its thin ventral veins, sparing the animal the labor of pumping stagnant water over heavy gills. When it moves on an hour later, it leaves behind a square of bright, raw-penny copper that immediately begins to fizz anew.

Because its diet depends on the steady, uninterrupted hum of the closed circuit, its habits look uncannily like maintenance. Not out of intelligence—its thoughts, if they can be called thoughts, are probably no more than vast, warm moods of *too-sour-here*, *sweet-hum-there*, *pinch-coming*, *warmth-good*—but because anything that interrupts the current ruins its pasture.

Where calcareous scale builds too thickly on an exposed electrode and chokes the sweet fizz of electrolysis, the creature settles over the dead spot and rasps away at the lime with a radula hardened with black iron-sulfide teeth, grinding patiently through the night until the sharp, tingling taste of bare current prickles its mouth-parts again, at which point it drifts away satisfied, having inadvertently cleaned the contact. Where mats of decaying water-hyacinth clog the narrow throat above a transformer chimney and trap heat until the rising thermocline turns scalding and bitter, the animal pushes its blunt, heavy brow into the snag, loosening the mulch to get at the boiled snails inside, and in doing so clears the flue so the ninety-degree current can resume its lazy roll toward the vault.

Sometimes it tends the packing boxes. The long bronze piston rods, sliding four minutes out and four minutes in, weep microscopic traces of tallow and compounded cylinder-grease from their antique seals—ancient hydrocarbon fats that cannot be made anywhere else in the basin now. To the ward-dweller, that grease is a rare, intoxicating spice. It will wrap a narrow cuff of muscle completely around the mirror-bright bronze rod right up against the gland nut, clinging gentle and snug as a chamois rag as the metal slides through its embrace, licking away the molecular film of oil and simultaneously pressing loose hemp fibers back into the collar so the seal never blows out.

Smaller, stranger lives orbit its bulk, treating it as simply a softer, slightly warmer piece of the plumbing.

In the shallow folds behind its sensory pits live dozens of tiny, blind, porcelain-white isopods, flattened like pumpkin seeds, which scuttle out onto the surrounding brickwork whenever their host stops moving to graze on rust-eating root-hairs, then hurry back into its skin-creases before it glides through a tight boiler-gap that would crush them against the tile. Even the whiskerless, mottled catfish know its rhythms; when the great grey apron heaves itself up out of the lower sumps, stirring pockets of rich, iron-black silt from beneath the galvanic vats, two or three of the flat little fish will detach from their warm transformer-tops and drift alongside its rippling edge like lazy barges, snapping up the translucent worms and blind amphipods flushed from the mortar joints by its passing shadow.

Higher up—all the way up where the ribbon-weeds flatten sideways beneath the ceiling and the rising hydrogen pearls pop into that single palm’s-breadth of dead, suffocating air under the fouled glass—there is another, entirely separate register of life, thin and papery and half-mineral.

Up there, clinging upside-down to the sweating iron ribs of the vault, hang things like pale, splayed hands made of wet tissue paper and grey soot. They are neither quite fungus nor quite sponge, though they have slow, contractile veins that pulse once a minute. Their lower tendrils trail an inch or two into the warm surface film to drink mineral water, while their flat, dryish upper bodies press tight against the iron arches in the oxygen-poor, hydrogen-heavy pocket of trapped gas, metabolizing the explosive air back into harmless drops of sweat that bead along their edges and drip—*plink, plink*—back into the bath, the only sharp sound in the entire four-story nave, though nobody is up there among the blackened glass panes to hear it.

Once every decade or so, when the great mollusk down in the machinery grows too broad and heavy even for its liquid style of travel, it retreats to the perimeter where the wheel-locked bulkhead doors stand half-buried in fine dark sediment. Wedging itself into the recessed stone doorway of a passage that leads nowhere, it goes still for months. Its outer skin stiffens into a leathery, mineral-crusted shroud indistinguishable from a slumped sandbag. Inside that shell, without courtship or season, the animal quietly divides along its sluggish nerve-rings—not into hundreds of larvae that the pumps would mince and the chemistry would burn, but into two unequal halves: one large enough to remember the map of the currents, which eventually splits the shroud and flows slowly back toward the amber glow of the control blisters, and one smaller, palm-sized piece that stays behind in the quiet dark of the doorway, absorbing the old, discarded husk until it is strong enough to creep up a lead cable and find a leaking insulator of its own.

Nothing hurries it. Outside the cut-stone walls, dynasties or ice ages or new kinds of cities may be grinding across the dry crust overhead; down here, the ammeter needle trembles at half-load behind its snail-wiped glass, a bronze rod slides three inches inward, and a flat, grey margin of living velvet slips smoothly between two turning iron teeth without missing a beat.
