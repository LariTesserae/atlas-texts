To find the basin’s true stewards, you have to stop watching the strobe-flashes of the running herds and let your eyes adjust to the glare on the barest pavements, until a slab of pinkish-gray limestone detaches itself from the bedrock, rises on four columnar legs, and walks steadily down-slope toward a sweating fissure.

At first, they appear to be minor, weathered outcrops—broad, low-slung silhouettes about the height of a pony at the shoulder, draped in heavy, irregular aprons of horn and calcified hide. They move at the deliberate pace of shadows lengthening, planting wide, splayed feet padded with dense, fibrous sponge that conforms silently to every groove in the rock. Over their backs, they wear the basin itself. Generations of blown mica grit and flour-fine dust from the rings around the artifacts have settled into the waxy, corrugated folds of their carapaces, cemented there by skin oils and brief afternoon mists into genuine, living crusts of travertine. Dormant flecks of the plateau’s rust-red lichen ride their spines, blooming into sudden, vivid patches of oxblood whenever their hosts pass through a dissolving cloud-swath.

When two of them meet beside a solution pan, however, the stone illusion vanishes.

They do not flash from their flanks like the sighthound-grazers; their upper hides are too thick, too armored against the high-altitude ultraviolet glare. Instead, they lift their broad, blunt heads and unroll twin fans of vascular membrane from deep, protective slits along their necks and jaws. Up close, the display is startlingly intricate. Where the herds shout across half-miles in stark blocks of magenta and black, these creatures converse at arm’s length in cascading, silent scripts—fine, hairline ripples of cerulean, brass, and bruised plum chasing one another across translucent skin with the speed and precision of fingers across a loom. Their eyes, set wide beneath heavy shelf-brows of pale bone, are enormous, liquid-dark, and fitted with double, vertical pupils that split the white glare into polarized bands, allowing them to read the faintest shift in sheen on a counterpart’s throat even against the blinding limestone.

Nothing in the valley hunts them. The six-limbed ambushers give them a polite, ten-yard berth, not solely because of the heavy, mineral-hard plates shielding their vitals, nor the low, battering-ram density of their shoulders, but because the creatures smell profoundly wrong to a predator: dry, chalky, ozonic, tasting more of the ancient bedrock and the strange, clean heat of the installations than of blood and grease.

For a long time, watching one at a damp cleft, you might take it for nothing more than a specialized browser. It kneels—jointed forelegs folding neatly beneath its chest—and extends a long, prehensile upper lip split into two muscular fingers, delicately plucking at the rubbery chartreuse mats without tearing their holdfasts from the stone. It drinks sparingly, pressing a flat, porous pad beneath its chin directly into a half-inch film of condensation and drawing the moisture up by capillary action and slow, rhythmic swallows, leaving the microscopic blooms largely undisturbed for the caliper-legged skimmers skating around its snout.

Then it finishes drinking, rises, and goes to work on the stone.

Water in the basin is precious, governed entirely by the subtle geometry of runnels and pans, and limestone—even in a valley without heavy rain—is fickle. Dust storms choke the finer capillary cracks; fast-growing ochre scabs dam up the velvet-smooth trickles, diverting the morning sweat onto flat, sun-blasted pavements where the glare drinks it dry before anything can drink it; expanding roots of the waxy, copper-fanned stalks occasionally wedge a slab loose and spill a horse-bath hollow onto barren gravel.

When one of the crust-backed walkers encounters a choked runnel, it lowers its head and brings to bear a pair of short, chisel-edged tusks that jut not outward for fighting, but straight down from the lower jaw like mason’s irons. With slow, methodical strokes—every scrape of dense enamel against limestone muffled by the heavy, felt-like air into a faint, chalky *shhhk*—it clears the blown grit, pares back the overgrown lip of rubbery mat, and deepens the groove by a fraction of a millimeter. Where a fissure is bleeding moisture wastefully onto a baking flat, the creature gathers fist-sized cobbles in its split lip, carries them over one by one, and wedges them into a neat, curved check-dam, chinking the gaps with mouthfuls of damp flour-dust scooped from the dead zones around the anomalies.

That flour-dust is their mortar, and they are the only living things on the plateau that deliberately harvest it.

Approach the waist-high hemisphere of three-mile-deep light at midday, and you may see three or four of them standing along the worn circular track, heads lowered to the neat pale ring at the anomaly’s base. They know the boundary to the hair’s breadth. Their split lips hover half an inch shy of the impossible surface—never touching the threshold where matter blinks out and drops dead—delicately vacuuming up the zero-momentum powder that has accumulated overnight. Mixed with their thick, mineral-rich saliva, that ultra-fine grit sets in minutes under the fierce sun into a pale, seamless cement harder than the native limestone. Across the twelve-mile bowl, once you know how to look, you realize that half the "natural" lips of the finest solution pans are not natural at all. They have been raised, smoothed, and contoured over centuries by patient mouths, coaxing the afternoon sweat into deeper shadows where it will linger an extra hour against the sky.

Their true strangeness, though, shows itself in the long, dry hours between morning and afternoon condensation, when the heat shimmers hardest off the flats and most of the valley’s runners have tucked themselves into blue-shadowed clefts.

The masons do not seek shade. Instead, small groups of three or five drift toward the installations—toward the floating black torus, the braided mercury splines, and a score of lesser, half-buried geometries out on the blinding gravel. Because the air cannot carry vibration, the acoustic dead-zones around the artifacts do not deafen them; if anything, the total absence of micro-tremors in the air seems to soothe the fine, pressure-sensitive bristles lining the edges of their carapaces.

They settle in loose, symmetrical rings around the structures, folding their thick legs beneath them until their dust-crusted backs merge once more into low hummocks of stone. Facing inward toward the matte black ring or the seamless silver braids, they unfurl their throat-fans—not toward each other now, but angled carefully to catch both the overhead sun and the invisible, orthogonal warmth bleeding off the artifacts.

For hours at a stretch, nothing moves in the camp except light.

Across the outstretched membranes of the sitting circle, slow, hypnotic interference patterns begin to travel. One individual initiates a pulse—a thin, vertical bar of deep indigo backed by metallic gold, marching steadily from left to right across its throat—and the neighbor picks it up seamlessly as the bar reaches the edge of the first fan, passing the pattern around the ring of beasts so smoothly it looks like a single, continuous ribbon revolving around the alien monument. Gradually, the patterns grow denser, layering fractions upon fractions: cross-hatched lattices of violet, sudden white-hot pinpricks that dilate into rings and vanish, shifting geometric Ratios that seem to respond directly to the thermal drafts rising past them.

Look closely at a very old individual—one whose back-plates have thickened into six inches of striated travertine, supporting a miniature garden of dwarf bronze sedge right between its shoulder blades—and you will see that its patterns are the sharpest and slowest of all. When a gust of silent wind spins a devil of mica dust through the circle, disrupting the thermal plume off the braided splines, the elder’s throat-fans instantly shift register, flashing a corrective burst of stark, polarized teal that ripples outward through its companions until the whole group adjusts its posture by inches, shifting their heavy stone-like bodies to baffle the wind and restore the exact verticality of the rising heat column, high above which the crystal-winged gliders are turning.

Whether they are merely basking in an ancient, reliable hearth, conducting the long, wordless debates of a territorial mind distributed across five silent throats, or keeping some vast, generational tally of heat and stone and water that they somehow associate with the things protruding from their world, it is impossible to say.

When the sun drops low enough toward the molar-smooth western rim that the first cool breath rises out of the deep bedrock fissures, the revolving ribbons of color break off mid-phrase. As one, the throat-fans roll tight and vanish behind dusty scales. Five boulders stand up around the tarnished mercury braids, turn without looking back, and pace out in five separate directions across the swept limestone—slow, grey-pink shapes disappearing almost immediately into the bright, soundless glare, heading toward a hundred separate cracks in the rock before the afternoon water begins to run.
