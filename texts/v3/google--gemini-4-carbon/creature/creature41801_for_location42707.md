You hear their breath before you ever make out their shapes—a short, rasping *huff-shhh* timed to the swing of a wooden maul, nearly lost beneath the gunshot crack of parting slate, yet distinct because it is wet and warm in the cellar-cold air. 

Turn a corner around a bulging crib of chert, expecting an empty alley because the tallow pan in the niche is burning untouched, and there is a shoulder blocking the way. At first, it looks like another timber shore wedged against the masonry, wrapped in stiff, grease-blackened canvas. Then the canvas shifts, shedding beads of cold condensation, and you realize you are looking at a back broad as a hearthstone, hunched low to clear a rough-adzed lintel. 

The builders of the basin are men and women only in the broadest, heaviest sense of the words—or perhaps they are what human stock reduces to when bred or broken entirely for leverage in the dark. They are built low to the slate, short-necked and barrel-chested, with legs bowed outward at the knee like timber A-frames taking a vertical load. Everything about them is squared off and dense. You never see bare skin save for their faces and the thick, scarred wrists between cuff and mitten; the rest is armoured against the razor-sharp stone in stiff, overlapping aprons of boiled rawhide, split-ash shin-guards bound with hemp twine, and heavy, hobnailed clogs carved from single blocks of alder wood. Walking behind one in a narrow passage, you hear the rhythmic *clack-scrape* of wood and iron on wet flagstone, completely devoid of the spring of a normal stride. They walk from the hips, flat-footed, testing every slab for tip or slide before committing their weight, moving with the deliberate, unhurried momentum of a loaded sledge.

When one turns toward a grease-flame to trim a sputtering wick with an iron thumb-pick, the orange light catches a face the color of lard rubbed with soot. Their skin is thickened by the perpetual, cold damp and coated in a fine, grey film of rock-paste that settles into the deep creases around their eyes and mouths like mortar. They squint constantly—not against the dim lamps, which barely reach five paces, but against the flying splinters of flint and slate that hiss through the air whenever a wedge goes home. Over their brows, many wear stiff visors of woven rush smeared with pitch, pulled low, leaving only a wide, flat nose and a heavy jaw visible beneath. They do not blink when a two-ton lintel booms down onto its pillars twenty feet away; their eardrums thickened or ruptured years ago, leaving them tuned not to the deafening roar in the air, but to the vibrations coming up through the wooden soles of their clogs.

Watch one work a raw block of fieldstone, and you understand why the valley seems to build itself without crowds. They do not gather in gangs because the shoulder-width alleys will not hold a gang, and because they have replaced numbers with sheer, stubborn geometry. A single builder—a woman no taller than five feet, wide as a doorway in her layered hides—stands alone in a sunken court with a block of blue slate twice her weight. She does not try to lift it. She speaks no word, grunts no complaint, merely sets the toe of an eight-foot iron-shod ash pry-bar beneath its bevel, hangs her dense weight off the high end until the timber bows, and as the stone rises half an inch, kicks a split-oak chock beneath it with a wooden clog. Pry, kick, shift the fulcrum, pry again. In ten minutes of rhythmic, scraping silence, the stone has climbed three feet onto a dry-wall course, wedged tight with three taps of a hammer against a fist-sized shim of chert. Then she picks up her clay lamp by its wire bail, hooks it to her belt, and steps sideways into a dark gap between two hurdles, vanishing before the rock-dust has settled into paste.

They do not speak in sentences. If they have a language with names for sky or root or season, there is no room for it in a wooden drum five miles wide where every syllable bounces back distorted off ten thousand sharp corners. Instead, they communicate across the blind tiers with iron on iron, and stone on wood. Walk along a terrace and pause beside a taut guy-rope humming in the gloom; presently, a builder steps out of a recess, lays three thick, horn-calloused fingers against the hemp, feels a distant tremor sent down from the invisible top of the scaffold in the soot-fog, and reaches out with a short iron bolster to tap twice—*tink, tink*—against the ash upright of the derrick. Thirty yards away across three intervening walls, unseen hands ease a windlass, and the rope slackens half a turn. They read the masonry the way an animal reads wind. When an older retaining wall two tiers down begins to groan and spit dry splinters of stone before a slump, every builder within fifty yards freezes mid-swing, heads cocked toward their boots, listening through their ankles to the shifting friction beneath them until the fifty-ton wedge settles with a thunderclap; then, without looking to see what fell, the mauls rise and drop again.

Yet there are divisions among them, specialized shapes moving through the dark maze like tools made of gristle. 

High in the timber A-frames, right up where the wet soot-cloud swallows the pine crossbeams, clamber the riggers—leaner, longer-armed figures who forego the alder clogs for bindings of rough, braided rawhide wrapped tight around their insteps, giving them prehensile, splayed toes that hook into adze-notches and rope-lashings. They smell fiercely of pine-tar and sour sweat. You rarely see them descend; they travel horizontally across the dark miles via the taut guy-lines and cantilevered poles, passing heavy pulley-blocks from hand to hand in the mist, dropping plumb-bobs on waxed strings down into the orange pools of tallow-light forty feet below to guide the masons.

Lower down, squeezing through the two-foot drainage gaps and vertical cistern shafts where the cold water drums endlessly into the dark, go the Boys—though when one hauls himself out of a floor-chute beside your boots, you see grey bristles on his chin and cataracts filming eyes as pale as the cave-crust on the basins. They are simply the smallest, twisted thin by narrow chimneys, clad in slick, greased leather like eels so they will not snag on jagged slate headers. They carry long, hooked irons and wicker creels of rendered fat. Their work is the invisible maintenance of the deep pile: clearing rock-flour paste from the weep-holes so hydrostatic pressure does not blow out a dry-stone foundation, retrieving dropped wedges from six tiers down, and scraping up the pale, soapy blister-crust from the water rims into wooden buckets—whether to boil down with tallow for the lamps or to thicken whatever grey gruel bubbles in the iron cauldrons tucked into dead-end alcoves, it is hard to tell.

For they do eat and sleep right where the stone stops. There are no barracks, no cleared squares, no home quarter where the sharp edges soften. Every mile or so, tucked beneath the massive timber floor of a rubble-crib where the overhead logs keep off the worst of the condensing drip, you come upon a recessed bay lit by three clay pans instead of one. Here, four or five builders sit wedged against unmortared fieldstone, knees drawn up, their visors pushed back. They do not take off their leather aprons; the cold would stiffen the hide if it left their body heat. An iron pot sits over a brazier of resinous pine-knots, smelling of smoked suet, boiled barley brought in from the same unseen outside as the squared timber, and bitter willow-bark steeped to keep the damp-ache out of their knuckles. 

Even here, there is no chatter. One sharpens an iron chisel on a wet slip of slate held between his clogs—*shhhk, shhhk, shhhk*—testing the bevel against a thumb-pad so thick with horn it clicks like a beetle’s shell. Another sits with eyes shut, jaw slack, asleep upright against a jagged corner of rock that would tear cloth, breathing slow and raspy while a pale, flat scuttle-thing darts out from behind his shoulder to snatch a crumb of fat from his leather knee, entirely unheeded. Near the warm smudge of the brazier, one of those heavy, obsidian-eyed slate birds often squats on an upturned wicker basket, feathers fluffed against the chill, waiting to be tossed a gristly rind—not a pet, never touched, just a fellow tenant of the crevices sharing the grease-heat for ten minutes before dropping back into a shaft.

What is hardest to look at, once your eyes adjust to the orange gloom, are their hands when the heavy split-hide mittens come off by the fire. The fingers are twice the thickness of an ordinary man’s, the knuckles swollen into hard, calcified knobs from decades of absorbing the rebound of iron striking stone. Every nail is black, split, or missing altogether, replaced by tough domes of scarred skin. Beneath the perpetual grey paste of wet chert-dust, their palms are crosshatched with hundreds of fine, white lines where razor-edged slate has sliced them open and healed shut with grey dust trapped inside the seam, tattooing their flesh with the very grain of the basin. 

They do not look up when a stranger walks past their alcove along the top of a wall. At most, a pair of small, grease-rimmed eyes tracks your boots—noting the leather soles that will shred before morning on the raw flint, noting the empty hands carrying neither plumb-bob, froe, nor wedge—and then drops back to the whetstone. Within five minutes, the one asleep against the rock stirs at some subtle change in the thunderous rhythm of the distant sledges, reaches automatically for his maul, lifts a fresh clay pan of tallow to a splinter of burning pine, and steps back out into the wet, ringing dark to lay another course upon a floor he has never seen, toward a ceiling that is only smoke.
