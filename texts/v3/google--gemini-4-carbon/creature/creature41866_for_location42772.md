If you stand at the lip long enough—anchored behind an outcropping with your coat lashed tight so its flapping doesn't numb your ribs—your eyes slowly adjust to the shutter-speed of the basin. At first, you only see the blur: the silver-violet lash of the piezoelectric flora and the shrapnel bursts of the small kite-beasts. Then you begin to notice the anomalies in the wind itself. 

You see a shear-corridor bend slightly where no black polyhedron sits to bend it. You see a burst of chartreuse chaff scatter not around an invisible pressure-knot, but off an angled plane of something clearer than glass, drawn taut across six yards of howling emptiness.

There is an apex to this dry, roaring world, though if you asked one what it was, it could not answer with a mouth, and if you caught one and dragged it three steps back into the quiet mountain breeze, it would collapse over your boots like five yards of dry spider-silk and die of stillness before its colors faded.

To find it while it lives, look first at the floating black rings.

Around the hovering perimeter of an ancient phase-device, inches from the matte-black surface where the air is folded at lethal, right-angle pressures, you will sometimes see what looks like a dry frost-fern or a hairline crack in the air itself. That is a juvenile—a *tether-stage*. 

Life in the cathedral cannot begin loose in the gale; an unanchored egg or spore is simply milled out the top of the peak to become part of the glittering plume drifting over the dead scree. Instead, a newly cast strand—a braided filament of fullerene and dry, memory-charged protein no thicker than an eyelash—must strike the razor-thin boundary layer hugging one of the ancient installations. There, in the half-millimeter of dead, hyper-compressed stillness pressed right against the alien geometry, it grips not the unscratchable black surface, but the magnetic and barometric gradient around it. 

For its first season, it is little more than a geometric burr, a rigid little lattice of dry carbon struts and hair-fine quartz needles. It grows outward until its tips poke just a fraction of an inch into the screaming eighty-knot slipstream. Immediately, those tips begin to vibrate at ultrasonic frequencies. Like the plants below it, the juvenile drinks the violence directly—piezoelectric bristles turning mechanical shudder into raw voltage—while combed snares catch the microscopic drift of shattered grazers, flint dust, and electrified pollen whipping past. It has no blood, no moisture, no gut. Digestion here is electrostatic and mechanical: trapped organic dust is stripped down by corona discharge along specialized internal filaments and sintered directly into new, dry-crystalline tissue.

Eventually, the lattice outgrows its shelter. The wind grabs too much of it, snaps its anchor-threads with a tiny pop lost in the cathedral's roar, and hurls it into the churn.

Nine times out of ten, the sudden cross-shear catches the fledgling broadside before it can orient, slams it into a chalk fin at ninety miles an hour, and turns it into smoke. The tenth snaps open.

Fully grown, it has no true resting shape, because shape here is merely an argument with velocity. At a glance—in the millisecond before it shifts—you might call it a *loom-kite*, or a *shear-weaver*, though neither gets at the eerie, shifting geometry of the thing. Unfurled across a slack updraft to bask in static, it spans twenty feet from tip to tip, yet weighs less than two dry playing cards. Its "skeleton" is a marvelous, shifting puzzle of hollow, braided-carbon struts that never touch one another, held in floating compression by hundreds of hair-thin, superconducting tendons. Because none of its joints grind bone-on-bone, there is no friction to wear its dry frame down over decades of constant, violent flexing.

Stretched across these floating struts are dozens of narrow, overlapping vanes and iris-membranes of diamond-clear, biaxially stretched polymer. There is no muscle as wet-world creatures understand it—no heavy bundles of waterlogged meat pumping calcium ions. Movement is electrostatic. By shunting high-voltage charges harvested from the thrashing jungle down specific tendon-lines, strands repel or attract one another in microseconds. A single shift in internal polarity snaps the entire twenty-foot frame from a wide, circular parachute into a needle-thin dart, or twists it into a helical corkscrew to bore straight *down* the throat of a vertical gale.

Where are its eyes? It has none. Light is too cluttered with glittering chaff and too slow to process when a phase-ring shifts harmonic and turns your sky inside out in three-tenths of a second. Instead, every strut is etched with microscopic interference grooves and pressure-sensitive pits. The creature *hears* and *feels* the entire amphitheater as a single, three-dimensional solid made of pressure and pitch. Every screaming stone flute in the chalk walls is a lighthouse; every fluttering ribbon-plant casts a distinct acoustic shadow; every metallic-shelled grazer clicking in a vortex illuminates itself like a flare against the tearing white noise.

Watch one hunt, and you realize it is not merely riding the engine. It is *playing* it.

Hanging high under a scoured stone arch, compressed into a tight, invisible ribbon no wider than a sword blade, a weaver waits where three violent cross-currents braid together. Down in the basin, a cloud of scissor-tailed gliders is furiously stripping spores from a boiling thicket of silver vines. The weaver doesn't dive at them—diving into a chaotic air-knot is a good way to get tangled in spun-carbon vine and shredded. 

Instead, the weaver slides sideways three feet and snaps open four stiff, concave sails right across the mouth of a narrow shear-corridor. 

It holds that agonizingly tense posture for two full seconds, its struts singing like over-tightened violin strings, bleeding blue sparks from its wingtips from the sheer strain of resisting the eighty-knot blast. That brief, six-yard baffle is enough. Upstream, the deflected wind spills over a stone rib, starves a secondary eddy, and causes an entire invisible wall of air fifty yards below to buckle inward.

The sudden barometric hiccup sucks twenty scissor-tailed gliders right out of their vine-thicket and flings them upward into a clean, vertical chimney of air—straight toward the stone arch.

The weaver collapses its baffle, drops into the chimney ahead of them, and opens itself into a wide, spinning funnel of dry, charged mesh. It doesn't bite or claw. As the helpless gliders rocket upward through its rotating ring, the weaver releases a stored capacitor-jolt across its inner filaments. With a sharp *snap* that smells faintly of burnt hair and ozone, static attraction slams the tiny gliders flat against its dry digestive vanes. Already, high-frequency vibrations running through the mesh are milling their brittle, paper-thin shells down into absorbable powder, leaving their useless mineral grit to blow free through the weave.

Yet hunting is the smallest part of their lives. What makes the weavers the true citizens of this skeletal peak—what makes you lean dangerously far over the chalk lip, squinting through watering eyes into the sunlit roar—is what they do to one another, and to the ancient machines.

A solitary weaver has very little mind to speak of. A creature whose entire body weighs six grams has nowhere to pack a heavy brain; its own nervous system is just a lightning-fast reflex loop of piezoelectric switches etched along its spine-struts, barely smarter than a steel trap. 

Wait until late afternoon, however, when the mountain shadow creeps across the eastern scree and the cooling exterior air causes the ancient black polyhedra to subtly recalibrate their intake. Inside the bowl, a brief, predictable geometry emerges: a towering, forty-yard cylinder of calm, slowly rotating air right in the center of the cathedral, walled in on all sides by shrieking, supersonic curtains of vertical shear.

Into this quiet column drift the weavers. Two at first, then five, then thirty, slipping out of the roaring walls like blades slid from sheaths, suddenly opening wide in the dead air so that the sunlight catches their interference-patterned sails in slow, breathtaking washes of oil-slick magenta, gold, and cyan.

They drift together and interlock. 

Wingtip hooks to floating strut; trailing filament braids with trailing filament. Within ten seconds, thirty separate kites have clicked together into a single, hovering, polyhedral lantern forty feet across, turning majestically in the sunlit eye of the storm. Sparks jump continuously across their joined nodes—tiny, rhythmic clicks of violet light.

This is when they think.

Linked into a continuous tensegrity lattice, their thirty simple reflex-networks become a single, distributed dry-state computer, passing electrical pulses and harmonic vibrations across hundreds of yards of shared carbon filament at the speed of light and sound. Alone, a weaver is an aerodynamic reflex; braided into a Choir, it wakes up.

What does a weightless, dry-crystal intelligence think about, hanging in a stone cage at the top of the world? 

Watch the perimeter of the bowl while the Choir hangs assembled in the center. Around the outer edges of the great lantern, specialized edge-weavers angle their vanes into the surrounding shear-walls—not hunting, not feeding, but *plucking* the wind. In coordinated, millisecond ripples, the Choir deflects rhythmic pulses of pressure outward across the cavern. Those pressure waves strike the screaming stone flutes of the chalk ribs, momentarily choking one aperture, over-blowing another, turning the chaotic, chest-rattling roar of the amphitheater into deep, deliberate chords that shift and answer one another beneath the white noise. 

More unsettling still: they aim those focused, acoustic shockwaves straight at the ancient, hovering phase-rings.

The alien polyhedra were built to fold the wind, which means they *listen* to the wind to balance their invisible baffles. Over thousands of dry, unrecorded centuries, the Choirs have learned how to lie to them. By bouncing precise, resonant knots of compressed air against a hovering black ring on the northern wall, the Choir tricks the ancient device into sensing a storm-front that isn't there. Obediently, the matte-black ring tilts a fraction of a degree, shifting its hum up a third, and re-routes a massive, deadly river of downward shear away from a nursery-rib crowded with juvenile frost-ferns, or drives a fresh gale straight through a stagnant tangle of silver-vines to shake loose a blizzard of nutritious static-dust.

They are farming the wreckage of an engine they will never comprehend, tuning a cathedral of invisible walls from the inside out.

Then, without warning, a true gust from the high altitude outside slams over the lip—too raw and sudden even for the Choir's deceptive chords. A phase-ring on the western rib compensates violently. With a crack like an artillery shell, the central calm column collapses.

There is no panic, no tangle, no collision. A single synchronous discharge flashes blue across the entire forty-foot lantern, reversing the charge on every docking filament at once. Thirty weavers repel one another instantly, snapping apart from a single thinking mind back into thirty mindless, razor-thin darts in less than a heartbeat, each vanishing into a different howling corridor of torn air before the collapsing shear-wall can crush them together.

Within half a second, the center of the stone ribcage is utterly empty again—just bright, freezing mountain sun pouring through roaring nothingness, smelling of flint and lightning, while high overhead, a slightly thicker puff of sparkling, iridescent dust drifts peacefully out into the quiet blue afternoon.
