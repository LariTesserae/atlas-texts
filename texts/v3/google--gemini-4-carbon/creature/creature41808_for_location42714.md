At first, if you stand still long enough for the water around your ankles to match the temperature of your skin, you might mistake them for a change in barometric pressure, or a particularly dense drift of warmth pooling against the side of a transformer. 

Then something shifts its weight on the sharp basalt beside you—you hear the faint, wet *suck* of a heavy sole lifting from flooded rock, and a slow exhalation that smells not of animal musk, not quite, but of tallow, hot copper, and damp wool that has been kept in a closed chest for a century.

They are the Tenders, though there is nothing left to tend that does not already run by its own heavy, circular logic, and they do not call themselves anything at all. Speaking out loud in the basin is rare. A spoken word travels too far across the flat dark; it arrives at the ear of someone standing two miles away before it finishes leaving the speaker’s mouth, turning conversation into a slow, tangled muddle of answers preceding questions. Instead, they communicate mostly by leaning.

If you reach out—slowly, as everything here is done—your hand will find a shoulder first. It feels broadly human in proportion, though heavy-boned and sloped like the shoulders of someone who has spent ten thousand hours carrying iron pipe, and then forgot to put the weight down even after the pipe was gone. Their skin—or their outer layer, for the distinction between hide and garment has gone soft—has the thick, velvety give of soaked chamois leather coated in lanolin. It is warm, slightly greasy to the touch, and utterly seamless over the collarbone, only parting into heavy, overlapping folds around the waist and wrists like the bellows of an old camera or the mantle of a slug.

Press a little harder against that shoulder, and your fingers sink past the warm, breathing tallow-flesh until your knuckles knock softly against the ribbed cast-iron casing of a pressure gauge, or pass briefly through a pocket of warm, circulating oil where a heart ought to be, ticking once every thirty seconds with the dull *thump* of a check-valve seating itself. They are not cyborgs; nothing was ever surgically implanted into them. They have simply lived in the basin long enough to lose their exclusivity. Just as the sleeping hog-beasts share space with structural steel, the residents have taken the solitary machines—and the slow, crowded fauna, and the slack geometry of the flats—into their own volume.

A resident has no eyes, not even the sunken sockets where eyes used to be. Across the bridge of the nose and the brow, the skin is an unbroken, sensitive pad of warm cartilage, perpetually beaded with condensation. They navigate by thermals, by the sixty-cycle hum vibrating through the soles of their broad, calloused feet, and by the faint, sour-sweet electrical gradients leaking from the unbridged cables in the air. Their hands are immense—wide as spades, with thick, blunt fingers that lack fingernails entirely, ending instead in tough, sensitive pads ridged like cooling fins. 

Watch one—or rather, stand near one and follow its slow displacement of the warm air—as it works.

A resident will stand beside a lone walking-beam pump for six or seven hours without shifting its stance, ankle-deep in geometric puddles, while blind, segmented things climb up its shins to rest in the warm creases behind its knees. It rests both broad palms flat against the sweating iron of the cylinder head. It isn't repairing the pump. Nothing here is broken, because breaking requires a sharp, sudden divergence between *before* and *after*, and the basin no longer supports suddenness. Instead, the resident is *pacifying* the tolerances. Where a bronze bushing has begun to drift out of local alignment—sliding half a mile westward while still bolted in place, causing the grease to heat unevenly and smell of scorched suet—the resident leans its heavy chest against the housing and breathes with it. 

In, over forty seconds. Hold, while a drop of water forms on the tip of its broad nose, stretches, falls, and clicks against the flooded basalt. Out, over a minute, warm air whistling faintly through wide, slit-like nostrils that can seal shut against suspended pools of water.

Slowly, under the steady, unhurried pressure of those spade-like hands, the errant bushing settles back into its proper volume. The scorched smell fades back into sweet mineral oil. While standing there, the resident’s own lower legs may have drifted slightly out of strict locality—a passing ribbon-swimmer in a chest-high sheet of water forty yards away might brush against the resident’s shinbone—but when the resident finally peels its palms off the iron with a soft *plop* and takes a step, it gathers itself back together without effort, the way mercury rolls back into a single bead on a flat plate.

They eat the way everything else in the basin eats: by patience and overlap. A resident does not hunt the hog-sized sleepers, nor does it harvest the stiff, rubbery stalks by cutting them. When hungry, a resident simply sits down on the razor-sharp scree—its broad, calloused underside entirely indifferent to ninety-degree stone—and draws a sleeping herd-beast onto its lap, or settles its forearm along a weeping basalt fissure where the soft, mineral-digesting membranes hang thickest. They rest together for half a day. Through the porous, chamois-like folds at the resident’s belly and inner wrists, warmth and dissolved salts and slow, milky proteins pass inward by osmosis, shared across blurred boundaries until both creatures are equally full, or until the distinction between who was holding whom temporarily ceases to matter. When the resident finally rises, knees creaking with a muffled hydraulic sigh, the animal remains behind, unharmed, merely a pound or two lighter and deeply asleep.

Because distance frays, it is impossible to say how many of them live on the plain. 

Sometimes you will walk for an hour through the crowded dark, stepping carefully over greased lead cables and soft, breathing flanks, and encounter four or five of them spaced twenty paces apart along an invisible seam of ozone, each standing motionless with its head tilted slightly upward, listening to the miles-wide draft carry the click of dripping conduits. If you touch the left wrist of the first one, you might feel a long, puckered scar that feels remarkably like a brazed copper seam. Twenty minutes later, threequarters of a mile down-wind, you brush the left wrist of another—standing in a completely different posture, shoulders slumped, humming a sub-audible bass note into the hull of a freight-car transformer—and feel the exact same brazed seam, warm from your own fingers ten seconds ago.

Whether there are thousands of them spread thin across the vast, sunken pan, or only seven or eight who have forgotten how to stay in one place at one time, makes no practical difference to them. Even two of them standing chest-to-chest in a flooded depression might be the same individual meeting itself at the other end of a bent Tuesday, resting forehead against broad, eyeless forehead in the dark, sharing a slow, warm draft of breath that smells of tallow and wet stone, checking one another’s valves and pulse-rates by touch before parting ways again.

They have habits that look like domesticity, pared down to pure weight and temperature. In depressions where three or four warm iron pipes cross over one another, several residents will gather—or accumulate—piling up smooth, solidified cakes of old axle grease and shed animal cuticles to make raised, dryish bolsters over the sharp rock. There they lie tangled together like walruses or roots, limbs draped over limbs and through pipe-flanges, radiating a bank of heat you can feel on your face from fifty feet away. 

In these resting heaps, they make their only music. One will tap a blunt finger against a hollow, oil-filled conduit—*tok*. Because of the basin’s frayed sequence, the dull, liquid resonance sometimes arrives from the far end of the pipe three seconds *before* the finger strikes the iron. Another resident in the pile feels the premature vibration in its ribs and answers it—*tok*—sending a counter-beat backward up the line. Over hours, they build slow, glacial rhythms that take twenty minutes to complete a single measure, a patient percussion of brass, bone, and water-drops that drifts out across the miles of dark, shallow country and lulls the crowded herds into even deeper torpor.

If you stumble into them in the dark—if you misjudge a step off a razor-edged ledge and pitch forward—you rarely hit the stone. Almost before your foot slips, a wide, heavy arm smelling of warm wool and machine oil is already occupying the space you are falling into. It catches your ribs without a jolt, yielding first like thick gelatin, then holding firm like structural timber. 

For a long minute, the resident will keep a hand on your sternum. Its palm is huge, covering your whole chest, heavy as a heated iron skillet. You will feel its pulse—one slow, oiled stroke—pushing gently through your ribs, checking your tolerances, feeling how fast and anxiously your small, rigid heart is hammering against the dark. It will lean its broad, blind face close to yours, breathing warm, damp air across your cheek, smelling faintly puzzled by how sharply bounded you are, how stubbornly you insist on occupying only one spot at a time, and how hot and hurried your blood runs.

It won't harm you, and it won't try to keep you, though if you stay still in its grip for too long, you may notice when it finally lets you go that the sharp edges of your boot-heels have gone slightly soft, and that for the next half hour, whenever a turbine housing sighs half a mile behind you, you can feel the warm condensation beading on the inside of your own wrists. 

Then the heavy hand peels away from your shirtfront, leaving a smear of clean, warm grease on the fabric, and the resident steps sideways—into the dark, through the iron leg of a derrick, and back into the slow, crowded patience of the flats.
