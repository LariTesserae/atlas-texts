To see what else shares the flats, you have to stop walking and let your eyes adjust to things that move slower than the steam, or things that only move when the steam tells them to. Because the sound is unceasing and the sightlines are absolute, stillness here is not a way of hiding so much as a way of conserving energy. Nothing wastes motion in an eight-mile room with no cover.

Start at the shallow evaporation pans, those wide, lukewarm mirrors of rust-colored water a millimeter deep. If you crouch beside one, ignoring the thudding vibration coming up through your heels, the surface looks entirely dead. Then a faint, geometric crease appears in the liquid film. 

Beneath the water, pressed flat into the smooth silt, are colonies of *shim-worms*—blind, ribbon-thin annelids no longer than a fingernail and the exact dull, oxidized red of the runoff. They have no eyes and no pigment of their own; their translucent skins are packed with ingested iron particles. Thousands of them lie side by side in tight, parallel rows, facing the gentle, almost imperceptible current draining from the nearest vent. Every few seconds, when a heavy *boom* from a nearby travertine cone sends a compression wave through the mudstone, the entire colony flexes in unison. That collective twitch ripples the water just enough to wash a fresh microscopic film of sulfur-rich silt over their gills before settling back into smoothness. They do not wriggle or burrow. They lie like metal filings arranged by a magnet, pulsing faintly to the beat of a stone piston two hundred yards away.

Look closer at the pale gray mounds of blistered pumice sitting out in the open mudstone, the ones that look like heaps of ancient slag. Not all of them are stone. 

If you tap one with the toe of a boot, most will give back the hollow, dry rasp of volcanic glass. Every twentieth or thirtieth mound, however, will yield slightly, like thick, vulcanized rubber, and draw itself down a fraction of an inch tighter against the damp clay. These are what pass for large grazers in the basin, though they have long since given up grazing in favor of becoming plumbing. Biologically, they are something like terrestrial tunicates or shell-less, hypertrophied gastropods, grown to the size of an overturned bucket. Over decades of sitting over microscopic hairline vents in the mudstone, their porous, leathery mantles absorb dissolved silica and calcium from the rising vapor, stiffening into a grey, warty rind indistinguishable from weathered pumice. 

They never move once they settle. Inside that heavy, sound-deadening dome of mineral-impregnated flesh, the creature is little more than a warm, slow-beating heart wrapped around a labyrinth of gill-curtains coated in symbiotic bacteria. It breathes in the faint, hot seepage of hydrogen sulfide coming up through cracks too small to build chimneys, and exhales droplets of clear, distilled water from a narrow, puckered siphon near its base. The glossy black scavenger beetles know the difference between the true pumice and the living mounds; you will often see two or three of those enamel-shelled insects standing motionless around the base of a living mound, drinking the pure, warm water that weeps from its side while the rest of the basin runs thick with iron and chalk.

Where there are grazers, even slow ones like the lead-colored arthropods around the vents, something eventually learns to pry them up. 

You will not see the basin’s hunters out on the open leather-mud at noon, not unless one is crossing from a dying chimney to a louder one—a migration that might happen once in five years. Instead, you have to look directly at the lower terraces of the steam-hammer cones themselves, right where the gray travertine meets the slick hematite apron, bathed in the intermittent coughs of hot mist.

Lying wedged into the fluted, mineral-draped crevices of the cone is a creature about the length of a man's forearm, low-slung, heavy-boned, and broad-headed, built along the lines of a squat, heavily armored salamander or a leg-bearing lungfish that forgot how to swim. Its skin is completely devoid of slime; instead, it is shingled in overlapping, pebble-grained plates of keratin stained yellow and gunmetal by sulfur precipitates. It has no external ears—the foundry roar of the chimney inches above its head would shred a conventional eardrum—and its eyes are tiny, recessed beads covered by thick, smoky nictitating membranes that wipe away warm grit and mineral scale with a slow, greasy blink.

It hunts entirely by phase-cancellation. 

Pressed flat against the stone base of the vent, its broad, pale belly feels every shudder of the plumbing underneath: the building boil, the rising pressure, the exact micro-second shiver in the rock just before the heavy copper or limestone cap blows upward. The lead-shelled arthropods grazing on the black bacterial mats know this rhythm too; as soon as the stone cap drops with its deafening *clang*, they freeze, sealing the edges of their flat shells tight against the slick rock like limpets, impervious to teeth or claws. While the vent builds pressure again, when the vibration is steady and safe, they lift up on their short legs to walk and scrape at the grease-mats.

The shingled hunter waits for thirty, forty, fifty cycles of the chimney, never shifting its weight, letting warm condensation bead and run down its sulfur-stained back. Its metabolism is tuned to the heat of the stone; it needs very little food. When an arthropod grazes within two feet of its crevice, the hunter does not lunge during the quiet hiss of building steam, when its footsteps would send tiny, telltale tremors through the rock to warn its prey. Instead, it waits for the peak of the cycle. In the exact split second that the cap slams back down into its stone seat—when the ground jumps, the air cracks like a rifle shot, and the arthropod reflexively hunkers down—the hunter moves, using the massive acoustic shockwave to mask the scrape of its own heavy claws. 

Before the echo has even flattened out across the basin, it has wedged its broad, chisel-shaped lower jaw—tipped with dense, replaceable plates of iron-hardened tooth—under the lip of the arthropod’s shell, just before the seal finishes locking. With a short, violent twist of its thick neck, it flips the grazer onto its back, exposing the soft, pale gills pulsing underneath. It swallows the meat in two gulps, leaves the empty, lead-gray shell clattering gently against the vibrating stone, and backs slowly into its warm niche in the chimney wall before the valve lifts twice more. Over years, the discarded shells around a hunter's favored crevice don't rot or blow away; they get slowly cemented into the apron by the mineral-heavy mist, turning into smooth, gray bumps in the hematite floor, looking like rows of dropped rivets melted back into the floor of the shop.

Even the air immediately above the chimneys holds something, though it is easy to mistake for frayed mineral fiber. 

On the taller cones—the ones that chug softly rather than hammer, venting a continuous, gentle plume of sulfur-sweet vapor—the top rims are often crusted with delicate, pale yellow needles of pure crystalline sulfur. Mixed among those brittle crystals are clusters of long, stiff, pale threads standing four or five inches straight up into the warm exhaust. They look like spun slag-wool, or tufts of bleached horsehair caught on the rough limestone. 

They are colonial polyps, encased in flexible tubes of chitin and gypsum. When a wet cough of mist rolls out of the throat of the vent, the tips of the threads split open into tiny, translucent fans no wider than a dime, sweeping through the vapor for airborne bacteria torn loose from deep inside the subterranean boilers, and drinking the trace volatiles before the mist vanishes into the glare. The instant the puff ends and the dry, shadowless air closes back in, the fans snap shut with a microscopic click, leaving only dry, stiff bristles gleaming dully against the colorless sky.

As the afternoon wears on and the sun drops toward the low yellow bluffs at the rim of the world, the light shifts from flat white to a thick, tarnished brass, throwing long, soft-edged shadows east from every cone. That is the only change the basin permits. 

Nothing wakes up at dusk, because dusk brings no relief from the heat and no cover from the noise. Out on the leather-dry flats, two enamel-black beetles meet over the shed skin of a third, touch antennae briefly—communicating not by sound or scent, both useless in the sulfur-heavy roar, but by tapping sharp, coded rhythms directly onto each other's wing-cases—and then part, walking steadily away in opposite directions across the bare mudstone. Around the hammering vents, the shingled hunters tuck their broad chins tighter against the warm travertine as the upper air cools by ten degrees. In the shallow rust-pans, the red worms flex once, all together, as a distant boiler shifts a stone deep underground, and then go still again. 

Darkness comes down over the eight miles of clay like a heavy lid, erasing the bluffs, erasing the slick sheen of the pans, leaving only the starlight glinting faintly off wet patches of ironstone and the endless, blind machinery of the floor chugging evenly into the dark, keeping time for things that have never needed to hear silence.
