To understand what lives here, you have to understand why they touch this flat, sun-bleached floor at all. 

The adjacent volume they inhabit—that folded, perpendicular bulk where ninety-nine percent of their anatomy resides—is an interior without sky. It is a cramped, hyper-dense plenum where matter presses against matter in every direction, a thermal trap with nowhere for entropy to go. An engine cannot run without a cold sink; a furnace cannot draw without a chimney. To creatures whose core metabolisms burn at the temperature of smelting iron within an insulated dimensional pocket, this scorching, four-mile-high desert is as frigid and vast as the bottom of an arctic sea. 

They do not live *on* the plateau. They surface here to breathe out. They breach into three-dimensional space the way whales breach into air, except they are shedding megawatts of waste heat and scavenging raw, oxidized crust before sinking back into the crushing, incandescent dark of their native geometry.

At the base of the visible food web—beneath the colossal shadows and the floating drills—are the **Sinter-Ticks** and the **Slag-Weevils**, the only organisms here that have abandoned the perpendicular bulk entirely to live full-time in the flat world.

You will find them congregating beneath that month-long collision by the obsidian ridge, directly under the steady, tinkling rain of shed bone-bolts and cooling dross. To the naked eye, they look like spilled hardware: rusted washers, spiral shavings from a lathe, thumb-sized burrs of cast iron. Sit entirely still on the hot basalt for twenty minutes, and you will see the hardware begin to sort itself. 

A slag-weevil has no head or soft tissue; it is a tightly wound bimetallic spring encased in six hinged plates of blackened tungsten-chitin. It moves by thermal ratcheting. Lying on the blistered stone, its underside absorbs heat until the internal spring snaps taut, vaulting the creature three inches toward the nearest acoustic vibration, where it lands with a dry *clink*, cools through a dorsal pin-fin, and resets. Hundreds of them hop in slow, erratic arcs toward the shadow of the locked titans. When a globule of crystallized lubricant or a sheared gear-tooth drops out of the fractured air above, the weevils pile onto it in a rattling heap. They do not chew; they press tiny, piezoelectric plates against the warm detritus, using the deafening, omnipresent vibration of the valley floor to ultrasonically shatter the slag into powder, which they sweep into magnetic ventral grooves.

They are hunted, in turn, by things that treat three-dimensional space like the surface tension of a pond.

Watch the flat, gray dust pans where the metallic mirages pool. Every few yards, a hairline scratch appears in the silt, drawing itself in steady, rectilinear angles—ninety degrees, forty-five degrees, ninety degrees—like the needle of an invisible plotter. This is the **Angle-Skater**. In the adjacent fold, it is a sprawling, lattice-winged glider fifty feet across; here, all that intersects the ground are the tips of four monomolecular iron skates and a single, hair-thin grounding wire. It rides the electromagnetic wake of the colossal flywheel-beasts miles away. When a hopping weevil crosses its grounding wire and shorts the micro-current, a seam snaps open in the air six inches up—just wide enough for a pair of copper-plated shears to scissor out, snip the weevil in half, draw the magnetic belly-plate back through the slit, and vanish with a tiny pop of displaced air, leaving two neat half-shells of tungsten rusting on the dust.

The grazing rotary drills—those brass-and-bone apertures drifting over the ocher flats—belong to no single small creature. If you were to trace twenty thousand of those floating mouths back through their mica-fracture seams, you would find they all converge onto a single body: a **Reef-Mill**. 

A Reef-Mill is an archive of digested stone, a colonial leviathan miles long that lies moored sideways across the dimensional grain like a sunken continent. It has been grazing this basin since before the eastern escarpment faulted. Its floating proboscises do not move at random; they drop in vast, mathematical spirals—Fibonacci sweeps and fractal grids—testing the sinter for trace veins of vanadium, chromium, and nickel. Listen closely to the screech of the drills and you will notice they are tuned. When a bit strikes a rich pocket of heavy ore, the pitch of its grinding climbs a third, broadcasting a clear mechanical harmonic across ten miles of thin air. Within hours, hundreds of neighboring apertures drift over on unseen manifolds, congregating in a shimmering, brassy cloud above the strike until the air is thick with red iron dust and the rock beneath is milled down three inches in an afternoon.

Then there are the things that cast the half-mile shadows: the **Basin-Tractors**, or **Great Crowns**.

The forty-foot horn-and-carbon treads that materialize to bite the sinter are not feet; they are anchor-pawls. In the adjacent space, gravity—or whatever vector gradient substitutes for it—pulls sideways relative to the plateau. The Great Crown is hauling its incomprehensible bulk uphill against a dimensional tide, and it braces itself against our bedrock to keep from sliding backward. 

Inside that boiling, half-mile dome of shadow and ozone, the air is almost unbreathable, thick with volatile hydrocarbons and ionized metal vapor. The catastrophic hum of its flywheel is not a byproduct of locomotion; the flywheel *is* its heart and its memory. Grown from concentric rings of depleted uranium and dense bone-iron, spun up over ten thousand years of slow grazing, it stores kinetic energy the way a desert tortoise stores fat. Through lean millennia, when the surface rock is stripped of easy ore, the Great Crown runs its internal foundries off the gradual deceleration of that wheel, dropping a single revolution per century.

Because it is so vast and runs so hot, a Great Crown brings an entire parasitic weather system with it wherever it intersects the basin. 

Walk into the edge of its shadow—if your eardrums can survive the pressure wave of its asynchronous pistons—and look up into the empty, static-charged air twenty feet overhead. Hanging in the nothingness, arranged in neat geometric rows where the beast’s unseen flanks would be, are hundreds of **Flue-Barnacles** and **Gasket-Lice**. 

The barnacles are stationary cones of white porcelain-bone that have wedged themselves directly into the spatial seams of the Crown's exhaust manifolds, holding the dimensional rifts open like surgical retractors. They filter the gouts of superheated sulfur and carbon soot blowing out into the basin, growing concentric rings of black diamond and graphite around their rims. Clambering between them—appearing and disappearing in midair as they crawl across the unseen hull—are the Gasket-Lice: flat, articulated ribbons of bronze and asbestos-like silicate fibre, long as a man's arm. They feed on the glowing, cherry-red grease that weeps from the host's overworked bearings, scraping the hot tallow away with copper mandibles before it can carbonize and seize the joint.

And what of the two colossi locked together by the obsidian ridge, whose stuck-whistle shriek fills the afternoon?

It is neither predation nor combat, though from this flat cross-section it looks like murder. Those gear-toothed jaws clamping together at a millimeter per hour are **Die-Presses**, and what is happening above the slag heap is a spawning.

Creatures of steel, ceramic, and vulcanized carbon cannot gestate soft embryos in water; there is no water, and soft flesh would cook to ash inside their manifolds. To make a new organism, two adults must become a drop-forge. 

For weeks, they have braced their spatial frames together against the bedrock of the ridge, aligning their internal blast furnaces across dimensions until their overlapping seams tear open into a shared crucible. Into that blinding, white-hot intersection—mostly hidden from three-dimensional sight, save for the tangles of copper-veined tendons straining to hold the alignment—each parent extrudes billets of refined alloy, braided nerve-wire of pure silver, and starter-gears grown in their own marrow. The clamping jaws are stamping dies. At a millimeter an hour, under millions of pounds of hydraulic bone-pressure, they are cold-welding and drop-forging a juvenile between them.

The unwavering, ear-splitting shriek echoing off the thirty-mile walls of the plateau is the sound of the infant’s primary axle being turned on the lathe of its parents' teeth, trued to a tolerance of a ten-thousandth of an inch.

It will take another forty days for the jaws to meet completely. When they finally bottom out, there will be a single, concussive *crack* loud enough to shatter loose sinter five miles away and send every slag-weevil in the valley vaulting blindly into the air. The overlapping distortion will snap shut. The two ancient engines will uncouple, retracting their glowing, exhausted radiator fins back into the perpendicular dark, leaving the obsidian ridge abruptly, shockingly quiet in their wake.

On top of the heap of shed bone-bolts and dry slag, baking under the ordinary white sun, will sit a smooth, seamless cylinder of blued steel the size of an oil drum. 

For three days it will do nothing at all, cooling in the dry thermal wind while the matte-black silicon lichens slowly reach silver filaments toward its base to taste its stray magnetism. On the fourth day, as the sun drops toward the western rim and the evening seams widen across the valley, a hairline vent will crack open along its flank. It will draw in its first thin, scorching breath of high-altitude air, strike an internal flint against its own dry ribs, cough a tiny puff of yellow sulfur onto the polished basalt, and slowly, smoothly roll sideways—out of three-dimensional sight, and down into the roaring dark where the herds are waiting.
