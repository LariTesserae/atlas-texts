At first, there appears to be nothing else at all—only the wet stone, the slate-colored spans, and the occasional finch. Then a ribbon of cloud drops in, the louvers flare pale green, the fresh condensate sheets down the western face, and one of the angular shadows beneath a cantilevered walkway fails to drip properly. 

The water hits it and parts around an outline the exact matte grey of the municipal composite, dusted with fine, sparkling flecks of raw mica it has picked up from the walls.

It is an animal the size of a split log, flattened to the thickness of two stacked hands, pressed so seamlessly into the ninety-degree crease where a synthetic conduit meets a sheared block of granite that it looks like a structural gusset. Only when the thermal field shifts—cooling the seam it rests against by half a degree—does it unpeel itself from the rock.

It moves without lifting its belly far from the stone, flowing sideways across the sharp facets on eight short, wide-splayed limbs. There is nothing soft about its silhouette. Evolution or deliberate tailoring—in these high, strange settlements it is rarely worth asking which—has shaped its back into low, chamfered plates that mimic cleaved mineral planes. The edges of its carapace are thin and hard, shedding water in clean, brisk curtains rather than letting it bead and freeze. Its skin is dry-looking even when submerged in a sheet of runoff, hydrophobic and cold, colored in flat shades of wet flint, slate, and feldspar white.

Down here, the municipal engineers call things like this *weir-skinks* or *louver-leeches*, though it is neither reptile nor worm. Up close, as it creeps out onto the unrailed stair, its nature is harder to pin down. Its joints do not bend with the fluid grace of muscle over bone; they articulate with slow, hydraulic precision, driven by internal bladders of thick, glycol-rich hemolymph that will not ice over if the creature gets caught in a dead corner of the shadow. Beneath the overlapping slate plates of its flanks, pale, feathery gills—the color of bruised plum—pulse slowly in the thin air, drinking oxygen from both the high-altitude breeze and the super-oxygenated films of freshly snapped cloud-water.

Its undersides are a marvel of quiet mechanics: broad, pale grey pads divided into thousands of microscopic hexagonal ridges. They work by capillary lock rather than suction or glue, gripping wet metamaterial and slick, newly split granite with equal, effortless certainty. When it crosses one of the hair-fine micro-channels in the floor, its footpads briefly dam the racing thread of water, backing it up for half a second before stepping clear and letting the tiny silver surge run down to the grates.

It has no interest in the mountain finch, nor in the blind amphipods down in the dark plumbing. Its grazing ground is much thinner. 

Reaching the warmest seam below the central louver, the creature stops over that faint, soot-colored smear of microbes—Wait, no: it stops *just short* of the smear. If it ate the whole patch, the bare stone would take months in this sterile air to re-colonize. Instead, a wide, lipless mouth underneath its flattened snout lowers to the wet granite. Out slides a broad radula—not toothed like a snail’s, so much as edged like a windshield wiper made of flexible, translucent horn. With two slow, deliberate strokes, it skims only the outermost, oil-slick film of the microbial mat, along with a microscopic buttering of windblown pollen, soot from distant valleys, and tiny flakes of destabilized mica. 

Yet food is the lesser half of what keeps it in this pocket. What it truly lives on is the *gradient*.

Watch where it settles after grazing. It does not curl up entirely on the warm smoked glass of the louver, where the violet and green interference patterns ripple upward. Full warmth would dry its gills and speed its sluggish metabolism until it starved in a week. Nor does it retreat into the freezing corners where the rime needles grow. Instead, it bridges the boundary. 

It parks its long, flat body directly across the invisible line where the infrastructure’s thermal field drops off into raw mountain cold. Its blunt head and front limbs rest on the warm metamaterial just beside the smoked glass; its broad, plated tail extends six inches out onto naked, unheated granite. Within minutes, stray mist settles on its cold tail and freezes into a crisp crust of clear rime, anchoring its rear end to the wall. Meanwhile, its head remains damp and thawed, bathed in the subtle radiant heat of the phase-shift arrays.

Between those two extremes—fifty degrees at the snout, twenty-eight at the tail—something quiet and continuous happens inside its body. Beneath its dorsal plates runs a dense lattice of dark, metallic-looking tissue, rich in salvaged bismuth and iron drawn over decades from the raw granite dust. Like a living thermocouple, the creature harvests the temperature difference itself, letting a tiny, steady trickle of bio-electric current run from its warm forequarters to its frozen rear, charging the slow cellular batteries that keep its blood antifreeze-thick and its grip unyielding without needing more than a fingernail’s worth of black slime a month.

It is not entirely alone in the cleft, though at first it seems to be. 

Look higher up, near the lone cylindrical pylon in the north corner, beaded with cold sweat. Clinging to the shaded curve of the cylinder are three smaller shapes—no bigger than thumbnails, nearly transparent, looking exactly like blistered droplets of frozen condensate that forgot to fall. They are its young, or else its mates; in this species, individuals start life as tiny, drifting specks of jelly carried between courtyard nodes on the damp feet of mountain finches, and spend their first five years as stationary, limpet-like blisters before growing heavy and grey enough to walk the walls.

Then there is the Custodian—or what passes for one here.

The settlement beyond the rim has residents, of course, though they rarely use an open condensation well as a thoroughfare. Why climb an unrailed stair slick with freezing runoff when pressurized corridors run dry and level twenty yards deeper inside the mountain? Still, someone—or something—uses that single eastern stair once every few days.

You can tell before they arrive because the big plated grazer feels a faint shift in the interference patterns behind the smoked glass. Without haste, a subtle pulse of heat runs backward into its tail, melting its anchor of rime from the inside out. With a tiny *clink*, the shed casing of ice drops onto the black basin grates below, and the creature slides two feet sideways into a deep V-shaped fracture in the raw granite, folding its chamfered back until it becomes, once again, indistinguishable from a wedged flake of stone.

A moment later, a figure descends from the bright rectangle of sky.

Whoever lives in the sparse mountainside city above has adapted to the thin air and the verticality in their own quiet way. They come down the unrailed, wet stair without looking at their feet, wearing soft-soled boots of the same slate-grey composite that lock onto the slick treads without a squeak of friction. Their coat is heavy, wind-sheared wool dyed the color of unweathered feldspar, cut loose across a remarkably deep, barrel-vaulted chest built for pulling oxygen out of air half-empty of it. Around their throat and mouth sits a low collar of woven copper-colored mesh that catches the moisture of their own breath before it can frost their eyelashes, turning each exhale into a silent bead of water that rolls down a tiny seam in their lapel rather than puffing white into the cold.

They do not carry tools with moving parts. They carry a slender rod of dark glass and a small porcelain vial. 

Stepping onto the wet basin floor, ignoring the chill water racing around their ankles, they pause at the central louver. Their bare hands—broad-knuckled, deep-flushed with extra capillaries to stave off frostbite, stained faintly at the cuticles with mineral salts—touch the smoked glass, reading the speed of the violet and green bands by some faint electrostatic tingle in the skin. 

Then they glance down at the hand-wide smear of black microbes below the louver. They see the two neat, clean tracks wiped across its surface. Their dark eyes, narrowed behind pale, wind-toughened lids against the fierce ultraviolet glare of the sky, flick sideways to the V-shaped granite crack where a piece of stone has four faint, wet seams around its edges that rock shouldn't have.

The person does not pry the creature out. If anything, the corners of their eyes crinkle slightly above the copper mesh collar. Wild algae and windblown dust clog the hair-fine micro-channels if left unchecked; ice dams form on the louvers if the edge-boundaries aren't routinely disturbed. A courtyard with a healthy *edge-sleeper* in it requires half the maintenance of a sterile one.

Uncapping the porcelain vial, the person lets three drops of clear, mineral-heavy trace brine fall onto the warm granite six inches to the left of the dark microbial smear—a tiny subsidy of phosphorus and sulfur that fresh-split mountain rock lacks. They check the submerged grate where the blind amphipods hide, nudging a trapped sliver of mica free with the tip of their glass rod so it washes down into the dark cisterns.

Above, the wind hisses across the open roof. A shred of cloud drops into the cleft. 

For three seconds, person, pylon, stair, and hidden beast vanish together in bright white fog. The louvers flare pale green; the air snaps with ozone and the sudden, silent *thrum* of vapor collapsing into liquid. Water sheets instantly down the wool coat, beading off the heavy lanolin just as cleanly as it sheets off the carapace wedged in the fracture.

When the air clears again, gleaming and hard under the high sun, the stair is already empty save for wet footprints evaporating from the warm edges of the treads. 

Minutes pass. Drop, *clink*, patter. A sliver of new rime slides off an overhang and shatters on the black floor. Slowly, moving at the speed of a shadow lengthening, the flat grey piece of stone in the V-shaped crack unfolds its eight short legs, flows back down to the boundary between heat and frost, lowers its broad snout to sniff the three tiny, pungent drops of gift-brine drying on the warm composite, and settles its tail back into the freezing dark to let the ice grow over it once more.
