You do not see them until one of them lifts an elbow two inches into the slipstream and the white light cuts a hard, ink-black crescent underneath it. 

Until that moment, they are simply four more grey-ochre blisters on the lee slope of the quartzite whale-back, aligned nose-to-wind like the flightless grouse two hundred yards down the trough. Even when they move, they do not stand. To stand upright in the center of the amphitheater is not merely exhausting; it is a crisis of physics. Raise five feet of upright bone and hide into the laminar gale and the wind does not push you—it plucks at your center of gravity until a heel slips on the polished stone, and once a body begins to roll across these miles of greased, scalloped aprons, there is nothing to catch on until the lip of the caldera drops out into the cloud-sea below. Furthermore, six inches above the granite, the air is thirty degrees colder and strips moisture from exposed nostrils and tear ducts in minutes. Four inches down, inside the boundary layer where the stone drinks the magnesium-white sun all day, the rock is seventy degrees and smells faintly of warm oil, crushed lichen, and dry reptile musk.

So they never rise. They *swim* the stone.

Seen up close, their bodies have been quietly negotiated by ten thousand years of the plateau, though whether they are purely local or come up seasonally from the high, timberless benches just beneath the stratocumulus deck is hard to tell—likely they belong to the rim itself, descending only a thousand feet into the upper cloud-fogs during the dark winter solstice when the basin’s condensation freezes permanently, then climbing back above the milk-white sea as soon as the sun returns its heat to the granite. 

They are built like barrels flattened in a press: enormous, deep-sprung ribcages housing lungs the size of wineskins, set atop short, bowed femurs and thick-ankled feet that turn entirely outward like a frog’s or a rock-climber’s, letting the inner edge of the boot-sole and the whole length of the shin lie flush against the granite. Their shoulders are massive, heavily muscled across the trapezius and latissimus from millennia of four-point creeping, and they wear armor made for friction. Over their chests, bellies, knees, and forearms, they wear aprons of shingled reptile-plate stitched onto cured mustelid hide with sinew, polished mirror-bright on the underside from sliding across the orthoquartzite. 

Nothing on their clothing flaps. A loose fringe or an untied thong in a sixty-knot steady-state roar will whip itself to shreds in an afternoon and beat the flesh beneath it purple. Everything is lashed tight with recessed knots tucked behind the hips and neck, greased heavily with rendered beetle-fat and black lichen-pitch until the whole garment looks like oiled slate. Across their faces they wear no fur—breath condensing on fur freezes into an iron mask the instant a cloud-shadow or ridge-shadow passes over—only thick coats of grey mineral clay mixed with marrow-grease, cracked around the mouths, and narrow, wrap-around goggles carved from the pelvic plates of the big basin predators, pierced not with horizontal slits, which still let in the blinding upward bounce off the polished rock, but with tiny, angled pinholes blackened on the inside with soot brought up from the lowlands in sealed horn tubes.

Their ears are gone to the wind. Not missing, though frostnip has claimed the upper rims on the elders, but plugged permanently while inside the caldera with stoppers of chewed, oil-soaked moss wrapped in gut-skin, held in place by the tight leather skullcaps they never unlace above the rim. Even with the plugs, the basin’s low-register *thrummm* enters through the sternum, the jawbone, and the kneecaps. Children brought up here for their first summers vomit for two days from the inner-ear vibration before their brains learn to subtract the chord from existence, the way coastal people stop hearing surf. Adults who have lived fifty summers on the whale-backs are stone-deaf to anything quiet even when they descend below the clouds; down in the misty scree-valleys of winter, they sit in dead silence anyway, uncomfortable when the air stops pressing against their ribs.

Watch two of them hunting along a condensation band at midday.

They move thirty yards apart, creeping crabwise up the windward edge of a damp seam so their scent—thick with rancid fat and warm hide—is torn instantly backward over empty granite, never crossing the prey ahead. Their hands are bare at the fingertips despite the chill, calloused as horn, splayed wide to feel the micro-grain of the stone. Every twenty paces, the lead hunter stops and taps two knuckles sharply against the bedrock: *tik-tik... tik.* 

Thirty yards away, the second hunter has her chin resting lightly on her wristbone against the granite. She does not hear the tap; she feels the crisp shear-wave travel cleanly through the solid, unfractured pluton beneath the wind’s bass drone. She answers without looking up, lifting two fingers of her left hand from her shoulder blade, the white inner skin of the palm preserved pale under wrist-flaps specifically so it flashes like a heliograph when turned into the violet-sky glare. *Two fingers, rolled left:* the grouse flock in the pan ahead has shifted toward the shadow-line; circle wide.

They do not throw spears. You cannot throw anything in this air; a javelin cast ten feet rises out of the boundary layer, catches the jet, and vanishes sideways half a mile downwind before it hits the stone. They do not use bows. Instead, tucked flat along their ribs are six-foot wands of flexible, laminated horn—ibex or lowland sheep horn boiled and spliced down below, dense enough not to chatter in the slipstream—tipped with short, heavy bone nooses or three-pronged basalt gigs. They slide up behind the basking, cobble-flat lizards like larger lizards themselves, moving only when the reptiles drop their heads to rasp at the ochre crust, inching forward on greased belly-plates without lifting a heel. A slow extension of the horn wand along the stone, two inches off the deck; a sudden sideways flick of the wrist; a pinned lizard flipped onto its pale belly where its suction-toes lose purchase on the granite. Instantly the wind itself becomes the weapon—catching the upturned edge of the broad reptile, trying to skate it downwind, pinning it tight against the hunter’s bone gig while a heavy thumb crushes the skull behind the eyes.

Nothing is wasted, and nothing is butchered messy. Blood spilled loose on dry granite vanishes into tacky black varnish in ninety seconds; blood spilled on a wet rime-band fouls the scarce water pan. They tuck the dead lizards immediately beneath their own warm chests, inside deep ventral pouches sewn into their sliding-aprons, keeping the carcasses supple with body heat until they reach one of the pecked crescent scoops at the saddle.

There, three bodies wedge together like spoons in a drawer, curving around each other below the lip of the depression while the tearing white noise passes seven inches above their spines. 

Life inside a scoop is intensely, claustrophobically tactile. Because they cannot speak, and because inside the cramped granite hollow hands are often busy with stone knives or tucked away from the biting draft, they talk with their ribs, knees, and chins. A mother teaches a nine-year-old boy how to scrape the oily yellow fat-bodies out of a streamlined beetle carapace while guiding his elbows with her own knees; when he cuts too deep and punctures the bitter gut-sac, she does not scold—she presses her chin twice, firmly, against the crown of his leather hood, then taps his sternum once with the flat of the basalt scraper: *Wait. Watch.* Jokes happen down here in the scoops—silent, convulsive shaking of big barrel-chests pressed spine-to-belly, triggered when an uncle mimics the rigid, pompous tail-fanning of a startled cock-grouse using only three spread fingers poked just high enough into the wind above the scoop’s rim to vibrate violently before snatching them back down.

Their cuisine is cold, dense, and brilliantly engineered by necessity. In the bruised quartz mortar-patches beside the scoop, they pound sun-dried reptile fillets—cured simply by pegging thin strips flat under heavy slate slabs in the blistering UV for three hours until brittle—together with the rich, buttery paste of crushed wind-beetles, lowland pollen scraped like yellow flour out of rock-eddies where the upper atmosphere dumps it by the pound, and sour, rubbery shavings of inner moss-core for grit and scurvy-warding acid. The pounding cobble hits the bedrock in long, slow, hypnotic rhythms that travel half a mile through the ridge, letting every other band in the amphitheater know which saddle is occupied. Rendered fat is squeezed out by twisting the mash inside braided sinew tubes anchored into one of the thermal cracks, dripping clear yellow oil onto a sun-hot black basalt platter where it clarifies instantly without smoke or flame.

Water is taken with flat bone straws. When the afternoon shadow of a western whale-back creeps across a damp lee-stripe and flashes the condensation film into clear glaze ice, the older children crawl out dragging the wide slate platters on short rawhide tethers, scraping up curls of frozen rime with bone chisels before the ice can sublimate directly into the dry gale. Back in the scoop, they pack the ice-shavings into dark bladder-bags tucked against their own flanks, melting it with skin-heat, sipping warm, faintly granite-tasting water mouthful by mouthful so no lip ever touches wet stone in the freezing air.

And what does this place do to the mind behind the pinhole bone goggles?

People who live beneath trees, or even beneath moving clouds, live inside a world of interruptions—rustling leaves, shifting weather, dusk softening into night, sounds arriving from unseen distances. Up here, nothing ever interrupts anything. The light is surgical and pitiless until the exact minute the white flare drops behind the western rim, at which point black night falls across the basin like a dropped axe blade, turning the violet dome into an terrifyingly dense crust of unblinking stars that hang right down against the polished ridges without a twinkle of atmospheric haze. And through the blinding day and the star-crushed night, the roaring chord never alters by a quarter-tone.

So they have become creatures of radical patience and strange, geometric reverence. They do not believe in spirits that drift or whisper; anything airy or unanchored here is weak, swept over the lip into the pale cloud-ocean below, which they regard not as water or sky, but as the sediment-floor of a lesser, softer world where clumsy things go when they lose their grip. To them, holiness is *adhesion*. Holiness is weight, sleekness, and staying-power. 

Along the deepest thermal cracks where they cache their basalt axes—some of those black blades polished glassy on the ridges by forty generations of greasy palms, brought up from volcanic dikes two weeks' walk below the clouds by ancestors nobody remembers—you will find small, deliberate inlays that serve no survival purpose. Wedged tightly into hairline fractures where the wind can never pry them loose are thousands of tiny, iridescent blue throat-scales from male lizards and smooth white quartz pebbles, tapped home with bone punches until they form long, dotted lines tracing the exact boundary where the afternoon condensation stops. They do not pray over these lines. When a band crawls past an ancient inlay seam, each person simply pauses for three heartbeats, presses an ungloved palm flat beside the wedged pebbles to feel the seventy-degree heat of the sun-drunk stone against the sixty-knot chill tearing across the back of their knuckles, and taps twice with a thumb-knuckle into the granite.

When someone old finally weakens—when an elder’s knees stiffen so they can no longer keep their hips below the four-inch boundary layer, or when blood-thickening altitude sickness finally swells the heart inside that huge barrel chest—they do not carry the body down beneath the clouds. Digging a grave in solid orthoquartzite is impossible, and leaving a corpse in a lee-scoop would draw every mercury-pale mustelid for five miles to foul the living-hollows.

Instead, the band crawls together out onto the crest of the highest windward whale-back at noon, moving inch by inch into the full, unbuffered force of the jet stream where even the lichens cannot hold. Two younger kin lie flat on either side as living anchors, gripping cracks with bleeding fingertips, while they unlace the dead one’s greased reptile-plate armor—the precious sliding-gear stays with the band—leaving the body wrapped only in a smooth, tight shroud of stitched grouse-skin. They lay the body broadside to the northwest. They take back the bone goggles. They pull the moss plugs from the ears so the valley’s full, thunderous voice can finally enter the skull unimpeded.

Then they let go.

The steady-state gale takes hold immediately, rolling the shrouded cylinder smoothly, almost gently at first, then faster and faster across miles of polished, sunlit stone—no clatter, no bounce on the planed aprons, shrinking to a dark speck across the blinding basin until it reaches the far southeastern rim, shoots out silently over the motionless white sea of clouds, and drops back down into the soft, noisy, decaying world below. Behind them on the ridge, the survivors lie flat for a minute with their chins on their wrists, watching the empty glare, before backing slowly down into the four-inch warmth of the lee slope to scrape the noon ice before the shadow moves.
