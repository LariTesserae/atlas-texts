To understand what lives here, you have to begin with the sweat of the wood.

Far above the bruised-indigo ceiling, around the jagged rim where the retaining walls break the surface and meet the freezing cloud-deck, the ancient cypresses still spread a low, gnarled mat of needles to drink the thin mountain light and strip moisture from the wind. Everything that lives down here in the dark depends on that distant sky-harvest. The trees pump sugars downward through miles of vascular bark, and down here in the black water, the submerged roots exude a microscopic, resinous mucilage—a slow, sweetish sweat meant to buffer their own bark against the tannic bite of the lake. 

The tallow-moss is not moss at all, then, so much as a dense, creamy mat of cold-water fungi and filamentous bacteria feeding on that seep. It is the single pasture of the basin.

The massive, flattened amphibians—some of them broad as skiffs, pale as boiled turnip, with gills that trail behind their heads like bundles of dark, wetfern—are its grazers. Up close, their wide, splayed ribs are hinged with flexible gristle, allowing them to flatten themselves completely against the curve of a root-buttress like wet leather slapped onto stone. Their undersides are pale pink and ceaselessly rippling, lined with keratinous combs that plane away the top millimeter of the tallow-film without ever scratching the bark beneath. When one of them groans, its entire ribcage acts as a sounding board coupled directly to the timber. They are not calling to mates across the open water; water disperses low bass too broadly to pinpoint. They are singing *into* the wood, feeling the return tremor through their bellies to map the thickness of the tallow-pasture ahead of them, and to warn neighboring grazers off a claimed terrace. Move your hand near one, and it will not flee—nothing down here wastes caloric heat on panic—it will merely shift its weight with a slow, rubbery peel and release a single concussive thrum through the floorboards strong enough to rattle the teeth in your gums.

Out in the vast, echoing galleries between the arches, the clicking shoals feed on the drifting crumbs kicked up by those grazers, and on the sparse, frozen windfall that sinks down from the cloud-capped surface miles above: frost-killed midges blown up from the plains, ice-crusted pollen, tiny beads of congealed cypress sap. The fish themselves are heavy and slow, their skins loose and slimy, armoured in overlapping scutes of flexible, translucent cartilage because true bone is an extravagance in water bled entirely of stone. None of them have eyes; where sockets ought to be, their skulls are scooped into parabolic dishes of dense gristle lined with sensory hairs, cocked slightly left and right so each click returns a crisp, binaural geometry of pillars and peers.

Where prey sees with sound, predators become failures of sound.

If you hang motionless in one of the great open spans between the viaducts, letting your ears adjust to the hailstorm clatter of the shoals and the distant, ceaseless thunder of the spillways, you will eventually notice holes moving through the noise. 

There are ambushers out in the open water whose flesh has been tuned over ages to the precise density and acoustic impedance of four-degree fresh water. They are immense, slack-bodied ribbons—twenty or thirty feet long, lacking swim bladders, bones, or scales, composed almost entirely of stiffened, clear mesoglea. When a blind fish clicks at one, the sound wave passes straight through the predator’s body without bouncing back, striking the wooden wall half a mile behind it and returning an unblemished echo of empty space. Only two things inside these phantom eels are dense enough to cast an acoustic shadow: a pair of tiny, iron-hard beaks tucked deep inside an involuted throat, and the half-digested cartilage plates of their last meal, suspended in a sound-deadening stomach lined with velvety gas-foam. To a passing swimmer, the predator sounds like nothing more than a stray, drifting scrap of shed fish-armor—an illusion that holds right up until the water itself folds inward and swallows it.

Stranger still are the hunters that do not hide from sound, but counterfeit it. Along the undersides of the great spanning root-ribs hang creatures shaped like broad, fleshy mantles—distantly derived from leeches or planarians, though grown to the size of heavy carpets. Their inner surfaces are corrugated into thousands of muscular baffles that shift and pucker in real time. When a ribbon of clicking fish approaches a narrow pass between two wooden arches, one of these mantles will detach and undulate into the gap, spreading itself wide. As the shoal’s sonar strikes its baffled skin, the creature absorbs the sharp front edge of each click and ripples its musculature to throw back a softened, slightly delayed vibration—perfectly mimicking the diffuse, stretched echo of a wide-open gallery hundreds of yards deep. Entire columns of blind fish will swim placidly, unhurriedly, straight into the center of a closing funnel of dark muscle, convinced until the very last heartbeat that there is miles of clear water ahead of them.

Yet all of this—the tallow, the groaning grazers, the clicking shoals, the acoustic ghosts—is merely the surface skin of the basin’s life. 

If you follow one of the great hollow viaducts inward and upward, swimming into the dark wooden throat of a spanning rib where the roar of the distant falls becomes muffled and interior, you run into the basin’s most ruthless constraint: five thousand meters above the plains, the air above the lake is starved thin. Cold water holds gas greedily, yes, but at half an atmosphere of pressure, there is barely enough dissolved oxygen in the open basin to sustain anything warmer than cold jelly or slow cartilage. 

Inside the hollow ribs, however, it is not all water. 

Just as swamp-trees on the forgotten plains once pumped air down into suffocating mud, the colossal sinker-cypresses ventilate their deepest structural roots. Along the ceilings of the hollow wooden viaducts, trapped in smooth, vaulted domes of living heartwood, are long pockets of pitch-black gas. If you break the surface inside one of these submerged wooden ceilings, the air hits your lungs cold and heavy with the sharp, clean sting of turpentine, wet peat, and sap, richer in oxygen than the thin clouds far overhead.

Something else breathes there. You hear them before you ever touch one: long, slow, whistling inhalations echoing down the dark air-pockets inside the timber, followed by the soft, greased slide of heavy bodies slipping back down into the black water.

They are not human, even if some distant, terrestrial ancestor standing on the original stone peaks fifty thousand years ago might have been—or might have bred them before vanishing. Whatever walked the high ridges when the weaving began has been wholly remade by cold, dark, and timber. In the water, they are nine or ten feet long, thick-barreled and wrapped in a dense, firm coat of peat-stained blubber as smooth as rubbed soapstone, tapering at the rear into heavy, paddle-fluked hindlimbs fused from ankle to knee. Their heads are smooth, eyeless domes of pale skin stretched seamlessly over flaring cheekbones and massive, oil-filled jaw-pans. They do not click like the fish; they do not need to. The lake is already illuminated for them by the roaring bass of the spillways and the ceaseless hail of ten million swimmers—ambient sound washes off every curve of the basin, and their wide, dish-boned skulls read that background scatter the way an owl’s eye reads starlight.

Only their arms betray an ancestry that once knew dry air and deliberate craft. 

Tucked tight into insulated pockets along their ribs while swimming, those arms unfold at the retaining walls into extraordinarily long, gaunt limbs of corded tendon and bare, leathery skin, kept unfrozen by tight bundles of counter-current arteries pulsing warm blood down to the knuckles. Each hand ends in six elongated, multi-jointed fingers, sensitive enough to feel a single blister of sap rising under bark, and growing directly out from the heel of each wrist is a curved, six-inch spur of dense, amber-colored horn, honed to a smooth bevel against the timber.

These are the tools that leave no scars.

A six-hundred-foot wall of living wood holding back an inland sea in the upper atmosphere cannot simply be planted and abandoned. Left entirely to itself, a branch grows crooked toward a chemical gradient; a knot weakens under shifting hydrostatic load; a tiny split opens deep inside the weave where water pressure could, over four centuries, wedge a seam apart and spill the sea into the clouds. 

So they listen. 

If you wait in the bruised-black stillness near the base of a retaining wall, you may feel a pair of them arrive—utterly silent in the water, displacing the cold liquid with a single, languid roll of their flukes. They hang upside down against the vast wooden slope, anchoring themselves with the hooked claws of their hind-paddles, and press the broad, flat domes of their foreheads directly against the tallow-slick bark. Nearby, a massive flattened amphibian groans, sending a deep, cello-like shudder rolling fifty yards through the timber. Behind that note, the endless, low-frequency thunder of the outer spillway vibrates through the entire thickness of the dam.

The two pale listeners stay motionless for twenty minutes, forty minutes, an hour, reading the way those deep waves refract through six hundred feet of living grain behind their skulls. 

When they find a dissonance—a faint, hollow harmonic forty feet inside the wall where two ancient root-beams have failed to fuse, or where heartwood has grown brittle—they do not dig into it. They swim half a mile down the terrace to where young, pale, whip-thin water-shoots sprout from a vigorous node. With the horn-spurs on their wrists, they part the outer bark of a runner-root with surgical neatness, bruise its cambium between smooth, flat molars to wake its growth-hormones, and haul the supple shoot back across the terrace. Working together in total darkness, communicating only by pressing their throats against each other's shoulders and humming brief, tight chords that never leak into the open water, they braid the live runners into a tension-lattice directly over the weak harmonic. They pin the weave down with wedges of cured, dead heartwood tucked into natural clefts, pack the bruised seams with chewed wads of tallow-moss to seal out the tannin, and glide away toward the hollow ribs to breathe. 

Over the next two hundred years, spurred by the bruised hormones, that lattice will thicken, tighten, and pull the deep hidden fracture shut from the outside. Neither of the weavers who laid the splice will be alive to hear the harmonic clear, though their great-grandchildren, pressing smooth, eyeless skulls to that exact curve of the terrace, will hear the wall ring solid and move on.

This, too, explains why the floor of the basin is so clean, and how bone and gristle endure in water stripped of all stone. 

Nothing is ever allowed to drift toward the roar of the spillways; every gram of calcium, phosphorus, and iron in this sky-sea is an irreplaceable heirloom stolen from the dissolved mountains ages ago. In the open galleries, nothing hits the bottom because the blind shoals and the crawling, pale-shelled scavengers of the deep gullies strip any sinking fish or spent amphibian down to nothing before its body cools. 

And when one of the warm-blooded weavers grows slow—when the counter-current vessels in its wrists stiffen and the deep roar of the spillway begins to blur in its jaw-bones—it does not die in the open water. Accompanied by three or four of its pod, it swims down past the bruised-indigo twilight, past the clicking ribbons of fish, all the way down into the deepest, narrowest root-crypts at the bottom of the amphitheaters, where new, hungry feeder-roots hang like pale curtains in the motionless black. 

There, after a last slow breath taken miles above, it lets the remaining air slip from its lungs in a single silver-black plume that rises and vanishes into the dark. Its kin do not leave it on the smooth floor. Using their wrist-spurs and long, patient fingers, they part a curtain of living rootlets and weave them tightly, reverently across the heavy pale body, lashing blubber and rib and skull straight against the hungry bark. 

Nothing rots in the cold tannin. Preserved whole, the weaver hangs cradled in the living wall for decades while fine, hair-thin rootlets creep silently through skin and muscle and marrow, drinking back the iron of its blood and the precious, mountain-born calcium of its bones—drawing the body slowly into the timber until there is not even a hollow left behind, only a slight, smooth swelling in the grain of the wall that holds up the sea.
