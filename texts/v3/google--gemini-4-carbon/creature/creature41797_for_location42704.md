To find the true residents of the saddle, you have to stop looking at the open avenues. Anything tall enough to cast a noon shadow across those satin courts is merely passing through. The wolf-sized grazers descend to the lower birch-scrub when the winter gales drop the temperature past endurance; the pale-bellied raptor rides the thermals down to warmer crags at dusk. 

What actually *lives* here—what never leaves the six acres of roaring gneiss—exists in the boundary layer: that razor-thin envelope of air, no more than four inches deep, where friction against the stone slows the screaming gale to a survivable, shivering draft, and in the vertical plush of the moss pelts themselves.

If you press your face close to the smooth gray pavement in the lee of a rib—bracing yourself against the chest-thumping bass of an adjacent plaza—you will first notice the **limpet-skates**. They look like smooth, oval cabochons of gray agate, two to three inches wide, completely indistinguishable from the matte gneiss until one decides to cross a gutter. Beneath a stream-lined carapace of heavy, layered keratin, the creature is almost entirely muscular foot, fringed with a wet, oily skirt. It does not walk; it glides on a self-generated film of dense lipid mucus that refuses to freeze, clamping down instantly with fifty pounds of suction the moment an errant eddy tugs at its shell. They graze on the microscopic film of cyanobacteria and wind-blown pollen that slicks the wet stone after a cloud-flush. Hundreds of them dot the lower buttresses, moving an inch an hour, rotating their shells fractionally so their sharpest, lowest edge always splits the prevailing wind. When the rime ice *cracks* across a shaded gutter, the skates simply lock down and wait to be thawed by the next cloud-rip, looking like polished blisters in the rock.

Deeper in, buried up to their eyes in the foot-thick cushions of emerald and oxblood moss, live the **felt-weavers**. They are warm-blooded, vaguely mammalian things the size of human hands, though flattened dorsoventrally like flying squirrels so they can slip between the tightly packed stems of the rosette mats and the cold stone beneath. They have no external ears—ears would freeze in seconds and be deafened anyway—and their eyes are tiny black beads shielded behind transparent, cartilaginous spectacles to protect against whipping strap-fronds and ice-grit. 

What they have instead of ears is a coat of extraordinary complexity: thick, lanolin-heavy underfur impenetrable to freezing meltwater, pierced by dozens of long, stiff, ivory-colored vibrissae radiating from their flanks, wrists, and flattened tails. They feel the town’s music in their bones. A weaver knows the exact acoustic map of its home buttress; it can tell by the pitch of the stone’s vibration under its belly whether the wind has shifted three degrees north, meaning the next cloud-bank will slam into *its* side of the arch rather than passing cleanly down the avenue. Two minutes before the silver mist arrives, you can see a faint undulating ripple pass through the oxblood moss as a dozen weavers retreat upward into the high, dry crotches of the flying ribs to avoid the flash-flooding gutters. They never touch the open ground if they can help it. To cross from one arch to another without exposing themselves to the hovering raptor overhead, they wait for a gale-peak when the six-foot chartreuse lichen-straps are blown stiffly horizontal, bridging the narrow gaps between ribs, and then sprint hand-over-hand along the thrashing wet leather like acrobats on a bucking wire.

The weavers share the moss-pelts with a bizarre, opportunistic predator that neither walks nor crawls, because in this environment, letting go of the wall is fatal unless you know exactly where the wind goes. 

Tucked into the high, whistling window-loops and the undersides of the sweeping stone parabolas are the **gale-spiders**—though they have ten legs, two of which are modified into long, feathered calipers, and their bodies are armored in rubbery, burgundy plates matching the oxblood turf. Silk here cannot be spun into orbs; the wind would tear a web to tatters before the second strand was anchored. Instead, these arachnids exploit the town’s relentless aerodynamic predictability. Because the stone has not changed shape in ten million years, the air currents through any given aperture follow fixed, mathematical trajectories. A spider sitting in a high alcove knows that if it pays out a single, thick, elastic tether coated in antifreeze glue into a specific whistling venturi, the standing vortex will whip that strand around the blind curve of the arch and slap it precisely onto a moss-cushion twelve feet downwind. 

They fish the air currents. They cast weighted, sticky bolases into the roaring avenues during the two-minute cloud whiteouts—when the blinding silver fog hides the lines and the sudden deluge flushes sluggish insects, detached moss-mites, and careless young weavers out into the swirling mist—and then haul the slack back in during the lulls, bracing all eight hooked walking legs into microscopic pits in the gneiss.

Yet all of these—skates, weavers, tether-casters, and the padded grazers passing through—are mere mortar-fill compared to the settlement’s true masters, who are the reason the padded grazers never linger near the central amphitheaters, and why certain streets suddenly change pitch even when the high-altitude wind holds steady.

They are called nothing, for no human has stayed long enough in that bruising noise to name them, though if you saw one flattened against a pale arch at noon, you might think a patch of the gneiss itself had peeled up like thick gray leather.

They are **chord-wrights**, or perhaps simply *listeners*—colonial or solitary, it is hard to say, though they move as single animals about the size of a folded cloak. Evolution on this bare, screaming saddle has stripped them of nearly every feature familiar to lowland vertebrates, though a vertebrate skeleton—flattened, ribless, converted entirely to flexible, shock-absorbing gristle—still anchors their anatomy. Imagine a creature shaped like a manta ray crossed with an heavy woollen blanket, six feet across when fully spread, mottled in the exact satin grays, pale lichens, and wet-slate tones of the eroded town. Their undersides are a mosaic of thousands of dry, microscopic seta-pads—like the toes of a gecko scaled up to withstand hurricane shear—allowing them to flow effortlessly up vertical ribs, hang upside down beneath the thirty-foot stone parabolas, or plaster themselves dead-flat across the middle of an open plaza while sixty-mile-an-hour gusts scream seamlessly over their beveled edges.

They have no voice, no eyes worth speaking of beyond light-sensitive patches along their leading margins that read the sweep of cloud-shadows, and no insulation save a dense layer of rubbery blubber infused with glycerol. They are warm, slow-pulsing, and monumentally strong.

Their entire sensory universe is acoustic and barometric. Across their broad, gray dorsal mantles run branching grooves lined with tympanic membranes taut as snare drums. While other creatures endure the deafening pipe-organ roar of the town as a background hazard, the listeners *see* with it. To a listener pressed against the central pillar of a circular court, the settlement is illuminated in dazzling, three-dimensional sonic light. Every arch has a signature harmonic; every gutter hums a specific fifth; every *whump* of a collapsing air pocket casts a crisp acoustic shadow. When one of the wolf-sized grazers steps onto an upper causeway three hundred yards upwind, its body interrupts a tiny thread of airflow through a distant archway. The listener feels that microscopic drop in a single overtone instantly. It knows the grazer’s size, its walking pace, and the exact angle of its lean without ever needing to look across the plaza.

More astonishingly, they play the town back.

A solitary listener cannot bring down a healthy, hundred-pound grazer on open stone, nor can it outrun one. So they collaborate by altering the architecture. Four or five of these broad, gray mantles will glide silently up the leeward curves of separate buttresses, moving like slow shadows over satin rock. Reaching specific, wind-worn apertures thirty feet overhead—holes they know from ancestral memory or lifelong mapping—two of them will slide their thick, muscular bodies halfway across the openings, narrowing the stone throats like giant, living valves.

Instantly, the tremendous music of the saddle lurches.

A bass boom in the southern court cuts off mid-throb; a new, ear-splitting shriek erupts from a previously quiet terrace; and—most critically—the standing wind-waves reroute. A calm lee behind a moss-upholstered arch suddenly reverses into a violent, swirling downdraft. A dry plaza avenue abruptly catches the full, focused blast of a venturi jet. 

The padded grazers, startled by the violent shift in the gale and blinded as a passing cloud-scrap is funneled unexpectedly into a blinding ground-level vortex, lose their forty-five-degree lean. A suctioned hoof slips an inch on wet rime. As the grazer scrambles sideways toward what *should* be the calm shelter of a curved alcove, it steps straight onto a third listener waiting flat and invisible against the pale stone floor. The gray mantle surges upward like surf hitting a seawall, wrapping heavy, inescapable folds of gecko-padded muscle over muzzle and chest, sealing nose and mouth while clamping its own outer rim tight to the gneiss—using the mountain’s own polished perfection as the other half of a vacuum trap until the struggling animal goes still.

Feeding is neat and communal, tucked deep behind a windbreak rib so the hovering bird overhead sees nothing but rippled gray rock. Within twenty minutes, the next tearing white cloud slams upslope, the green walls comb out a fresh deluge, and the clattering flash-streams wash every drop of crimson cleanly down the stepped gutters and off the northern cliff into thin air.

Between hunts, and through the long, freezing starlight when the high snowfields glitter on distant horizons and the wind drops to a steady, moaning baritone, the listeners do something else. They gather in the high, skeletal ribs of what used to be multi-story blocks, stretching their wide, leathery bodies across the narrower parabolic gaps like sails rigged to bone masts. They adjust their tension—curling a margin here, doming a back there—until their own tympanic bodies begin to vibrate in sympathy with the stone. They tune the wind-chords, shifting the screaming dissonances of the gale into vast, pulsing, five-note intervals that roll out over the worn knuckles of the buried mountain range for miles, resonating through empty plazas where nothing rots, nothing accumulates, and even the predators have become part of the masonry.

When the sun comes up crisp and unsparing, they relax their grip by a fraction of an inch, slide down into the warm, damp clefts behind the popping bladder-melons to drink the antifreeze dew, and vanish once more into the matte satin of the rock, leaving the six acres of soaring ribs looking utterly, brilliantly empty under the cold blue sky.
