You do not see it arrive because it does not cross the basin so much as it accumulates out of the interference patterns. 

Hold your ground behind a buttress of cast-iron housing—somewhere the diagonal gravity pins your boots hard enough to keep the forty-knot cross-currents from taking your legs—and watch the thrashing wall of strap-leaves ten yards out. For a long time there is only the olive-amber murk, the green grease, the strobe of a distant arc-lamp turning the suspended silt into brass filings. Then you notice that a bundle of torn kelp stalks isn't whipping with the vortex anymore. It is being pulled *against* the shear, hand over hand, except there are no hands—just a cluster of pale, articulated calipers the size of bolt-cutters, snapping shut on the rubbery stems not to eat them, not even to cut them, simply using them as rigging to haul a heavy, segmented mass through a pocket of inverted current.

At first you take it for pieces of a wrecked machine that got tangled together in a spinning toroid and refused to come apart. And in a sense, you are right, though there is hot, dark blood inside the seams.

They are called—if there were anyone here with enough uncrushed air in their lungs to waste on naming things—the **Gasket-wrights**, or simply the **Tenders**, though neither word catches the meat of them. There are perhaps four dozen of them scattered across the three-mile pan of fresh basalt, never gathering, rarely acknowledging one another even when two of them end up braced against opposite sides of the same screaming turbine housing.

Close up, through the boiling white cavitation shroud of the locomotive pistons, one of them resolves into a low-slung, terribly dense bulk about the length of a draft horse, built with no regard for streamlining. Streamlining belongs to creatures that negotiate with friction; down here, where friction is uncoupled from wear, trying to slip smoothly through the water will only let the shearing currents spin you until your spine unthreads. Instead, the creature is all flat planes, brutal angles, and recessed hinges, like a cast-iron safe that learned how to crawl. Its outer hide isn't scale or shell, because anything brittle would duplicate its fractures the same way the volcanic glass does, until the animal vanished inside a starburst of its own infinitely multiplying shards. Rather, it is armoured in thick, overlapping aprons of something like vulcanized gristle—dead-black, rubbery, three inches thick, weeping a constant, heavy tallow that smells of boiled linseed and iron filings. When a stray ribbon of stray megavolts leaps from a dynamo tower and turns the surrounding fern-thickets incandescent, the current hits that tallow-slicked rubber, skates across the creature’s back in a web of harmless violet sparks, and grounds straight through its splayed, crampon-tipped limbs into the clinker floor.

It has no eyes. Eyes are fluid-filled bladders, and the first time the stamping mill half a mile off drops its hammer and forces the surrounding brine into dry octahedral lattices, an eyeball would simply turn inside out with a wet *pop*. It has no eardrums, either; the continuous concussion of the basin would pulp any delicate membrane in seconds. 

Instead, the front of the creature is a broad, blind shovel-prow of solid bone—heavy as an anvil, pale grey where the black rubber peels back—threaded with thousands of microscopic canals packed with dense, oily wax. It reads the basin straight through the skull. Every shriek of an unlubricated shaft, every *crack* of freshly extruded basalt, every thunderclap of a collapsing steam-pocket enters that bone prow as a crisp three-dimensional map. When it pauses on a knife-edged ridge of obsidian, tilting its heavy front end slightly left, then slightly right, it isn't sniffing the hot green soup; it is triangulating the pitch of a failing bearing six hundred yards away through twenty million tons of churning mud.

Watch what happens when the razor-sharp stone under its rear limbs suddenly glitches—when a forty-knot downdraft snaps a spire of basalt and the broken edge flickers, duplicates, and re-welds itself three inches sideways, straight through the meat of the creature’s hindquarter.

The beast doesn't flinch. It doesn't bleed out, because its vascular system isn't a single pressurized tree of arteries; it’s a redundant honeycomb of thousands of tiny, self-sealing muscular bladders, each one clamping shut the instant the rock-shard appears inside it. Slowly, methodically, using two mid-body limbs that end in heavy, chisel-beaked pincers, it reaches back, grips the freshly minted slab of volcanic glass jutting through its own flank, and snaps it flush against the hide on both sides. It leaves the stone shard embedded right there in the gristle like a rivet, sealing the wound with its own cause. An older individual is half geology by weight: its rubbery mantle bristling with snapped-off teeth of black glass, sheared iron bolts, and rusted washers it has incorporated into its bulk over decades of crawling through broken physics, until its silhouette looks like a piece of the basin floor tearing itself loose to go somewhere.

And it is always going somewhere, though its errands make no sense to an outside eye.

Wait until one of those abrupt horizontal slots of air slices through the basin at chest height—that two-foot gap of roaring, steaming emptiness between water below and suspended water above, where the tops of the bronze-green ferns are sheared flat. If you look down that deafening slot before it slams shut, you will often see two or three of them haunched half-in, half-out of the lower liquid, their broad bone prows thrust up into the dry gap. 

Exposed to the air, the sides of their thick necks part in four vertical slits—not gills, which would clog instantly with chopped chlorophyll and bacterial grease, but muscular bellows lined with vascularized plates of spongy iron-rich tissue. They don't breathe dissolved oxygen out of the fever-warm brine; there is barely any free oxygen in that rotting, sterile soup anyway, despite the manic flora, because the heat drives the gas straight out into the steam pockets. The creatures are air-breathers, or rather *steam-breathers*, and *electricity-eaters*. Up in the dry slot, their neck-slits flare wide with a wet, hydraulic *hiss*, gulping down pressurized, superheated vapor and black coal-smoke falling sideways from the distant cooling tower. Inside their chests, enormous, slow-beating hearts—plated in cartilage to survive the shockwaves—pump blood nearly at the simmering point, stripping heat, sulfur, and stray chemical energy directly out of the scalding gas before the pressure shifts and ten thousand tons of suspended ceiling slam back down on their heads with a white-foam thunderclap.

They don't duck when the ceiling falls. They just clamp their slits shut, lock their six short, tremendously thick pile-driver legs into the raw fissures of the floor, and take the hit like bridge piers.

Only after the water closes over it again do you see what the creature is actually *doing* all day in the olive-amber dark.

It crawls up the tilted brickwork of the three-story pump house. Gravity near the intake grates is pulling diagonally at forty-five degrees, dragging uprooted oil-drum bladders of kelp into a roaring toroidal blur, so the creature walks up the sheer cast-iron wall as easily as a flat road, hooking its crampons into sheared rivet-holes. It reaches the twin locomotive pistons slamming back and forth in their flooded cylinders, right inside the permanent shroud of boiling white vapor where the heat would strip the skin off a man's bones in three seconds.

Tucked underneath its heavy front apron, protected from the worst of the hydraulic battering, is a nest of smaller, shockingly delicate appendages—pale, segmented, glistening things like peeled shrimp legs or multi-jointed surgical forceps, folded tight against its hot belly. As the beast straddles the screaming, redlining piston rod, those fine pale limbs unfold into the boiling water.

It reaches down into the black muck at the base of the housing, scoops up great double-handfuls of the slick green bacterial grease made of pulverized dead plants, and mixes it against its own belly with thick ropes of waxy tallow squeezed from glands along its flanks. Working in frantic, fractions-of-a-second bursts *between* the strokes of the massive iron crosshead—timing the rhythm through its bone skull with absolute, suicidal precision—its pale forceps pack that dense, rubbery lard directly into the screaming, unlubricated journals and scored cylinder sleeves.

For forty seconds, the metallic shriek of the pump house drops an octave. The white-hot cavitation halo spinning off the piston rod dims to a dull orange. Around the housing, the diagonal pull of gravity slackens by five degrees; a tight, roaring vortex of trapped bushes ten yards away suddenly loses coherence and spills its shredded greenery harmlessly into the current.

The creature doesn't stay to admire the drop in pitch. Before the boiled plant-grease has even begun to wash out of the bearings, it is already backing down the sheared brick wall, turning its blind bone prow toward the strobing smear of the stamping mill half a mile east, where an octahedral pocket of dry space has begun stuttering and hanging open a quarter-second too long between drops of the hammer.

That is the bargain, if something so brainless and compulsive can be called a bargain. There are no snails, no worms, no minnows down here because ordinary biology requires a stable truce between cause and effect—an agreement that water stays wet, that rock wears down when hit, that down remains beneath your feet. That truce is broken across this three-mile pan, or perhaps it is only held together at the absolute ragged edge of failure by these solitary, roaring engines bolted into the floor. Left completely untended, a turbine seizes; when a turbine seizes, the local horizon shears open and stays open, or the basalt stops merely duplicating its edges by three inches and begins multiplying exponentially until a cubic mile of water is replaced in a heartbeat by solid, razor-edged stone.

Nothing built the Tenders to fix the machines, and they certainly didn't build the machines themselves. They have no schematics in those solid bone heads, no comprehension of what a pump house or a dynamo tower was ever meant to power. If a cast-iron pipe bursts, they don't weld it; they cram a living knot of muscular, scaled vine into the breach, pin it down with shards of duplicated obsidian, and plaster it shut with their own tallow until the vine's manic growth welds itself into the rust and holds the pressure. Sometimes, when a wide-clearance gear train is stripping its teeth and threatening to throw a flywheel straight through the fabric of local gravity, an ancient, half-mineralized Tender—too heavy with embedded rock and iron shrapnel to haul itself against the forty-knot floor currents anymore—will crawl straight into the housing, curl its vulcanized, bone-plated bulk into the gap between the spinning shafts, lock its limbs, and let the machinery use its living, rubbery body as a bushing.

If you pry open an inspection hatch on one of the quieter, smoothly humming dynamo towers—one where the blue-white arcs have calmed to a steady corona and the water nearby actually flows in one direction for fifty yards—you will sometimes find one wedged tight inside the dark casing. Its eyesless prow is pressed against the main shaft, its four neck-slits pulsing once an hour in the superheated froth, its flanks deeply grooved where forty-inch steel teeth have been running cleanly over its constantly regenerating gristle for years, keeping the pitch steady, keeping the water wet, holding the floor down in the dark.
