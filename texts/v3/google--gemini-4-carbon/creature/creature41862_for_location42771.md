You do not see it, because there is nothing to see with. If you were strapped to the skid, your hands locked onto the freezing flange of the steel I-beam to keep the wind from peeling you backward off the mountain, you would only know something was sharing the notch with you when the sound of the engine changed.

Not the pitch of the cylinders, and not the shriek of the turbo, which stays pinned at its breathless, altitude-sick whine. What changes is the acoustics of the cage. Something thick moves between the exhaust stack and the nearest wall of fractured andesite, and for four or five seconds, the slap-back echo of the diesel’s roar goes flat and muffled, absorbed by a heavy, sodden mass before the wind tears the sound open again.

To survive in a place where the air is a horizontal river of ice water and stone-dust, and the floor is a shifting bed of knives, a thing cannot have legs that balance, or wings, or ears that rely on quiet. Leave those down in the valleys where the air sits still. Up here, anything that stands upright gets levered over and thrown into the mile-deep dark. Anything soft gets julienned on the scree. 

At first, if your numb fingers brushed against it in the pitch black where the anchor chains bolt into the rock, you would think it was just one of the giant cushion plants—an overgrown, centuries-old hummock of *Azorella* that had somehow crawled off the stone and onto the edge of the steel skid. It has that same dense, unyielding topography: a low, domed mat of tightly packed, fibrous rosettes, slick with condensed cloud and stiff with cold. 

Then the hydraulic pump kicks down a gear, the steel frame shudders, and the hummock *flexes*. It shifts its grip, the whole yard-wide mass of it sliding six inches closer to the heat of the oil pan with a slow, grinding scrape against the diamond-plate steel.

It is not a beast that wandered up from the timberline, and it is not entirely a plant, though the cloud-forest is braided all through it. Better to say it is a knot that the notch tied out of whatever refused to blow away. 

Beneath that heavy pelt of living bryophytes, matted dead bamboo leaves, and black, grease-soaked lichen, there is a body—broad, flat, and radial, built like a giant terrestrial chiton or a rock-clinging crab that gave up shell for gristle. It has no head to face into the gale, because the gale reverses every ten seconds. Instead, it has a perimeter: a thick, calloused skirt of grey, vulcanized hide, toughest where it drags across the razor-sharp flakes of fresh andesite. Underneath that skirt, hidden from the shearing wind, is an arrangement of short, hooked limbs—maybe eight, maybe a dozen, thick as bolt-cutters and surfaced in horn—that do not walk so much as *ratchet*. One hooks a split in the stone, or a braided tree root, or a link of the two-inch anchor chain; the body hauls itself forward an inch against a sixty-mile-an-hour gust; another hook sets; another inch is won. It moves through the thrashing bamboo and over the shifting scree with the patient, lock-jawed certainty of a come-along winch.

Before the rotorcraft dropped the machine out of the sky, there were perhaps two or three of them along the whole ten-mile spine of the massif, eking out a glacial, half-dormant existence in the worst wind-gaps. They lived for decades at the edge of freezing, scraping endolithic algae out of raw fault-planes and chewing the bitter, leathery bark of horizontal *Polylepis* trees, their hearts beating once a minute in the dark, grateful for the wind only because it kept everything with teeth down in the valleys.

Then the sky opened, the steel came down, and the middle of the notch caught fire.

To an animal from the forest below, the diesel unit is a ninety-decibel nightmare of poison and flying iron. To the thing in the notch, the machine is a summer that never ends. 

There is only one here now—the largest, heavy as a soaked bale of peat, having crowded any smaller kin back out into the freezing scree years ago. It has arranged its entire slow, deaf life around the thermodynamics of the skid. It cannot hear the six cylinders hammering away inches from its back; it has no eardrums to rupture, only deep, fluid-filled canals buried in its dense cartilage that read the world entirely through vibration. It *feels* the engine. The uneven, oxygen-starved idle is the baseline pulse of its universe. Through the soles of its hooked feet, it reads the tension of the two-inch haul cable singing over the cast-iron sheaves. When an ore bucket three miles down the mountain catches a crosswind in the gorge below, setting up a wild harmonic oscillation in the wire, the creature feels the tremor coming through the steel frame ten seconds before the cable starts violently slapping its rollers, and it cinches itself flat against the skid, tucking its skirt tight until the fit passes.

It is filthy with the machine’s leavings, and the filth has made it invincible. Years of flung bearing-grease and fine misted hydraulic fluid have worked their way into the living moss and dead thatch on its back, turning its outer coat into a dense, waterproof felt of lithium soap, petroleum, and tough highland turf. Cold spindrift hits that oily dome and beads right off. Whipping bamboo canes lash across it in the dark—*crack, crack, crack*—and do not even register through four inches of greased turf and fibrous hide. 

Watch—or rather, reach out carefully in the roaring black, past the trembling hydraulic hoses—to see how it feeds. 

The radiator grille at the front of the diesel unit is a constant trap. The engine’s cooling fan sucks the horizontal gale inward, pulling torn leaves, shredded fern fronds, and wind-snapped twigs tight against the expanded metal mesh, while the ferns rooted beside the skid beat themselves to green pulp against the vibrating frame. Left alone for a month, the grille would choke solid with wet mulch, the starved engine would overheat even in the freezing wind, seize a piston, and die. 

The maintenance crew twenty miles away at the mine portal has a work-order to fly a mechanic in every six months to clear the intake, though the weather cancels the flight three times out of four. Whenever they finally drop a man down on a hoist cable during five minutes of dead grey calm, he checks the oil reservoir, changes the fuel filters in a frantic hurry, shines his headlamp on the radiator, frowns, and yells into his headset that the wind up here blows too hard for debris to stick to the mesh. 

He never looks ten feet away under the side of the skid, where a low mound of black moss and grease-clotted lichen is locked onto the anchor turnbuckle, utterly indistinguishable from the root-mats choking the boulders around it.

Every few hours in the blind, howling midnight, the mound unhooks from the turnbuckle and ratchets up onto the trembling steel deck. It crawls straight into the blast of hot air pouring off the engine block, pressing its flat underside against the warm, slick steel of the skid until its sluggish blood heats up and begins to move quickly. It hooks two limbs into the frame beside the dull-cherry glow of the exhaust manifold cage—never touching the red-hot iron, stopping a precise two inches short where the radiant heat is sixty degrees instead of four hundred. For an hour it just bakes there in the gale, soaking up the waste calories of burned dinosaur oil, invisible except for the faint, dull gleam of exhaust-light catching the wet grease on its mossy dome.

Then it shifts around to the front of the unit. Using stubby, horn-plated mouthparts set centrally beneath its skirt—built like rock-crushers, evolved to shear frozen bark off stone—it scrapes its way methodically across the expanded-metal radiator grille. *Scritch. Scritch. Crunch.* Over the scream of the turbo and the groan of the ore-cable, the sound is completely lost. It peels away the plastered mats of shredded fern, the crushed cushion-leaves, the pulp of bruised mountain bamboo, all of it warm from the radiator and lightly seasoned with black bearing grease and pulverized andesite dust. It chews the fibrous mash down, swallowing the stone-grit along with the greens to grind the tough cellulose in its muscular gut, licking the condensed cloud-water straight off the cold steel housing of the control box, momentarily blotting out the lone amber LED as its dark bulk slides directly over the glass.

For twenty seconds, the notch is entirely, utterly lightless—just the roar, the wind tearing through the wet trees, the boom of a vacuum collapsing against the cliff walls, and the heavy, greasy *scrape* of horn across painted iron. 

Then it moves an inch to the left, and the little amber light reappears, shining dimly on a retreating edge of scarred, calloused hide and a trail of tiny, stiff black mosses growing directly out of the creature’s back, trembling in the draft. 

Sated, warm to its core, it ratchets down off the vibrating steel, hooks three limbs around the taut iron anchor chain, wedges two more into a fresh-cleaved crack in the stone floor, and pulls its armored skirt down tight over the razor scree. The wind reverses with a deafening report, slamming through the throat from the eastern abyss, flinging ice-cold water horizontally through the thrashing dark. The bamboo clacks like bone; the two-inch cable pops and sings over the sheaves; the six cylinders hammer on at high idle, breathing hard in the thin air, their radiator breathing clear and free. Beneath the chain, warm and heavy and completely deaf, the knot on the stone locks its joints and waits for the grille to foul again.
