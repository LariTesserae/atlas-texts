You spot them first by the stilts they wear—not the stationary bone legs of their sleeping-platforms, which sit vacant during the bright, roaring hours, but walking-stilts strapped from heel to hip. 

To step bare-soled onto the basin floor is to risk punching knee-deep through a half-cooled blister of scalding marl, or slicing an artery open on a razor-thin fin of newly crystallized travertine, to say nothing of the orange-and-slate centipedes that whip out of cooling fissures at the slightest tremor. So the weavers of the bone baskets walk three feet above the living carpet. Their stilts are long, slightly bowed shafts of cured burrower-rib, shod at the bottom not with points, which would pierce the fragile crusts, but with broad, round baskets of woven chitin-splints, like snowshoes made for fresh mud. 

They move across the heaving flats with a wide, rolling, stiff-hipped gait, carrying long balancing-poles tipped with bone hooks. From a distance, through the battering haze of sparrow-sized flyers and rising steam, a foraging party looks like a file of tall, spindly wading birds picking their way across a boiling reef.

Up close, they are human, or were human once, though the bright, shadowless basin has trimmed and cured them to fit its own tolerances. They are small, compact, and dense-boned, scarcely five feet tall off their stilts, with long, heavy-knuckled arms and broad chests built to pump the thick, ammonia-tanged, low-oxygen air that settles in the valley’s depressions. Their skin is pale, the color of unbaked potter’s clay or old tallow, with a faint yellowish cast around the joints and collarbones where their pores excrete excess sulfur. 

Yet you rarely see bare skin. The air here is an abrasive soup of alkaline dust, hot mist, and flying legs. Everyone, down to the smallest child slung in a hide harness between a parent’s shoulder blades, is greased from scalp to ankle in a thick, waterproof paste of rendered amphibian fat mixed with fine grey clay. As they work, this grease dries in the heat and cracks along the lines of their muscles, then softens again in the steam of a vent, giving them a plated, earthen look not unlike the armored burrowers they hunt. They shave their heads and eyebrows clean with flakes of freshly snapped selenite; hair traps calcifying mist, turning within hours into heavy, brittle tangles of limestone that tear at the scalp.

Watch their faces when they turn toward one another, and the strangest adaptation of the white valley shows itself. In a place where the entire ceiling is a mile-wide diffuser of fierce sunlight, and the floor is a wet, pale mirror of fresh gypsum and marl, light comes from everywhere at once. There is no shade beneath a ridge, no relief by looking down. Unprotected eyes go snow-blind in a season. Their brows have grown heavy and low, and beneath them, their eyelids are thick, heavily creased, and lined with a double row of stiff, greasy lashes—not to keep dust out alone, but to narrow their vision to thin, dark slits, like snow-goggles grown of flesh. When they look up toward the milky glare of the roof to read the subtle dark fractures that warn of a falling selenite slab, they tilt their chins high and peer through mere hairline cracks of black pupil. 

They do not speak much down on the flats. Why waste breath? Between the endless, timber-rasping roar of ten million crustacean mandibles, the dry thunder of colliding wings, and the wet *plop-hiss* of the vents, a human shout dies six feet from the lips. 

Instead, their hands and poles never stop talking. A woman testing the crust ahead of a weir-team taps her balancing-pole twice against the hollow bone of her own left stilt—a sharp, resonant *clack-clack* that cuts cleanly through the white noise of scraping shells because its pitch is dryer and higher. Behind her, three teenagers carrying a folded travertine-hod pause instantly, weight shifting on their mud-shoes. She sweeps her free hand across her chest, palm flat, fingers splayed, then drops her fist: *hollow chamber beneath, crust thin, swing wide to the sulfur-vent.* Their hands are enormous for their slight frames, the fingernails thick, yellow, and horn-hard from prying at warm stone, the skin of their palms calloused into tough, grey pads. Even when two of them Climb up onto a sleeping-platform to rest, sitting cross-legged above the steaming tide, they speak in a fast, low murmur accompanied by constant flicks of wrist and knuckle—a half-voiced, half-signed pidgin where the hands carry the nouns and directions, and the raw, sulfur-roughened throat supplies only tone, urgency, and names.

Their lives are governed by a relentless, cheerful impermanence. Nothing can be hoarded that cannot be carried on two shoulders when a platform’s gypsum shelf begins to groan and tilt at midnight. 

Watch two older men harvesting one of the crescent weirs. The artificial pan they built yesterday afternoon has done its work: the diverted mineral water has cooled just enough to bloom thick with rust-red bacterial slime, and the blind, pale crustaceans have piled into it three feet deep, a frantic, clicking mound suffocating its own bottom layers in its greed to feed. The men wade right to the edge of the stone lip on their basket-stilts. They do not bother with nets, which foul and tear on jagged shells. They use broad, long-handled scoops woven from strips of leathery flyer-wing stretched over bone hoops. With rhythmic, unhurried swings, they ladle heavy, rattling heaps of crabs into the tall splint-baskets strapped to their backs, stopping every thirty seconds to whack the rim of a scoop against a boot-stilt, knocking loose an orange-banded centipede that came up with the catch before it can swarm up the handle.

Nothing of the catch is wasted, and nothing is cooked with wood, for no tree has ever grown under the selenite sky. Fire is a rare, sputtering thing fed by cakes of rendered burrower-blubber and yellow sulfur, used mostly for glue-making or signaling through dense steam. For everything else, the earth itself is the hearth. 

Back at a cluster of five cranefly platforms, three hundred yards upslope on a relatively stable bench of three-day-old gypsum, a household is processing the morning’s haul. They have found a "dry-hisser"—a narrow, clean fissure venting steam hot enough to scald, without spitting mud. Over it sits a three-legged frame of heavy chitin beams. Baskets of live crustaceans are slung straight into the steam jet; within four minutes the frantic rattling inside the basket stops, the pale shells blush a dull chalky pink, and the meat within is firm and hot. Children—already nimble on short, wide-bottomed training stilts at age four or five—sit on low stools of porous pumice-like tufa, cracking the steamed carapaces with rounded river-pebbles brought down generations ago from some forgotten upper cave, or more commonly with dense nodules of chert spat up from deeper vents. They pop the sweet, sulfurous tail-meat into clay pots to be sealed with fat, and toss the empty shells onto a broad sheet of hide. 

Shells are timber, tin, and wool here. Small shells are crushed into grit to temper quick-drying vent-clay; large, flat lateral plates are drilled with bone awls and stitched overlapping onto hide vests with mineral-cured sinew, making flexible scale-coats that turn the venomous bite of a centipede and shed gobbets of hot mud when a vent coughs unexpectedly nearby.

The hardest, riskiest work belongs to the crust-breakers—the hunters who go after the blind, mucus-armored amphibians beneath the domes. It takes four people working in lockstep. They look for a fresh clay blister in the act of inflating: a low, grey swelling ten feet across, steaming faintly at its crown, where the mud is still plastic and warm. A burrower will smell the crustacean shoals gathering on the new dome and rise from the warm slurry beneath to punch through and gorge. 

Two hunters stand back on firm travertine, holding taut ropes of braided hide attached to a heavy bone harpoon. The striker walks out onto the trembling flank of the blister itself, sliding her wide mud-shoes rather than lifting them, distributing her tiny weight across the skin of a bubble filled with hot paste. She holds her balancing-pole horizontally, feeling the vibration through the soles of her stilts—distinguishing the deep, rhythmic *blup-blup* of rising gas from the heavy, muscular shove of a four-hundred-pound amphibian swimming upward through the subterranean marl. 

When the crown of the dome suddenly bulges and splits, and a blunt, grit-plated head surges out, jaws gaping to inhale twenty pounds of clicking crabs and hot clay, she drives the harpoon down behind its armored skull-plate and throws herself backward, rolling across the warm mud on her greased scale-vest as the creature thrashes, collapsing the entire dome into a churning pit of grey soup. Her partners haul immediately, bracing their stilts against freshly wedged travertine fins, dragging the beast out onto the rapid-cooling shelf before the mud sucks it down or the scent of torn flesh brings fifty yards of spilled-rope centipedes pouring out of every crack in the floor. Within twenty minutes, right there on the hardening stone, the carcass is stripped: the hollow femurs and long ribs sawed free with serrated flint-and-gypsum blades to become next month’s house-legs; the meat sliced thin and hung on tripods to cure into leathery, salt-crusted jerky; the thick sheets of mucus-armored hide scraped and stretched. By the time the scavengers overwhelm the site—a roaring, snapping vortex of flyers and crabs piling waist-deep over the offal—the hunters are already sixty paces off, scrubbing their greased arms with handfuls of dry chalk.

At what passes for night—when the sun of the surface world drops, and the great mile-wide ceiling fades from blinding pearl to a bruised, milky indigo, then to pitch black lit only by the faint, ghostly blue-green phosphorescence of sulfur-oxidizing bacteria along the seepage rims—the people climb entirely out of the world.

They pull their ladders up behind them. Each cranefly platform stands eight or ten feet above the floor, swaying slightly as the hollow leg-bones flex. Up here, lashed together with rawhide that shrinks drum-tight in the dry updrafts, is a springy deck woven of chitin slats and cushioned with layers of shed flyer-wings, which are oiled and quilted into surprisingly soft, waterproof mats. Low domes of stretched hide pull over the platforms like wagon-tops, keeping out the heavy, erratic night-flyers that blunder through the dark and knock against the poles with dull *thuds*.

Inside the hide tents, five or six people curl together in the humid warmth rising through the floorboards. They unstrap their stilts, rub fresh tallow into the red chafing-rings around their shins and thighs, and eat cold crab-paste mixed with sour, fermented bacterial curd that tastes sharply of iron and vinegar. 

Even asleep, they are tuned to the stone. Every platform has a "plumb-rattle" hanging from the center of its underside: a taut sinew string weighted with a heavy lump of raw sulfur, suspended half an inch inside a dry, hollow carapace. As long as the shelf beneath the stilts holds level, the weight hangs silent. If the gypsum bench begins to subside into an emptying mud-chamber below, or swells with a rising blister, the platform tilts two degrees, the sulfur lump touches the shell, and the dry vibration—amplified by the hollow bone legs of the structure—hums straight into the sleepers' ribs.

No one panics when the rattle hums in the dark. There is no groaning over lost hearthstones. Within three minutes, in total blackness save for the dim, eerie glow of bacterial slime outlining the weirs below, knots are slipped with practiced fingers. Pots are slung in netting; quilts are rolled around tools; stilts are strapped onto bare legs. They climb down into the blind, clicking carpet of night-feeding crabs, pull the four long femur-legs free of the softening marl before the mineral crust can cement them fast, shoulder their entire house in bundles weighing forty pounds apiece, and wade off through the warm, noisy dark toward higher stone—already listening through the soles of their stilts for the solid ring of yesterday's rock.
