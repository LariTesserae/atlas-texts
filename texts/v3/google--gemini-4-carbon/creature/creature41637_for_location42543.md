At first glance, the builders appear to have vanished millennia ago, leaving their miniature metropolis to the birds and the snails. You can stare directly into a three-foot doorway on the fourth tier for twenty minutes and see nothing but bog-wood lintels, velvet liverwort, and the blinding cross-bounce of daylight. 

Then a section of the alabaster wall shifts its weight.

What looked like a corner buttress—pale, tallow-smooth, and faintly veined with grey—detaches itself from the masonry, extends a blunt, muscular forelimb, and resets a tilted plate of selenite by the width of a barleycorn. A beam of white light swings silently across the alley below, striking the heart of a shadowed stonecrop basin. The buttress settles back against the doorframe, draws its knees to its chest, and ceases to move again.

They are still here. There are perhaps forty of them distributed across the seven tiers, though you will never count more than five or six at once unless you spend days learning how to read the slight differences in luster between carved gypsum and living hide.

To survive in a forty-foot pocket of stagnant freezing air without fire—for smoke under a thermal inversion would choke the basin in an hour, and soot would blind the mirrors in a week—whatever first climbed down into this sinkhole had to shed the luxury of burning fuel, both outside the body and within it. Whether they began as stunted hominids marooned on the gravel plain during an ice-sheet’s advance, or something older that diverged before speech took hold, the pit has pared them down to match its own geometry.

Standing upright, the tallest reaches scarcely to a grown man’s hip, built with the dense, low-slung compaction of a barrel or a cairn. Their limbs are short and immensely thick at the joints, ending in broad, spatulate hands and feet shod in seamless pads of grey-white callus as supple as greased rawhide. They have no hair at all—not an eyelash, not a brow—because hair traps frost from breath and sheds into the water-grooves. Instead, they are encased in an inch-thick mantle of firm, subcutaneous blubber, rich in heavy waxes like that of a cold-water whale, over which stretches skin the exact chalky, translucent cream of wet alabaster. In the triple-bounced glare of the selenite plates, light actually penetrates the outer millimeter of their shoulders and cheeks, giving them the exact subsurface glow of the stone they lean against.

If you touched one, they would not feel warm. Their blood runs thick with glycerol and winter-proteins, sluggish and cool, circulating at perhaps forty-eight degrees Fahrenheit—just warm enough to keep nerve and muscle pliable, far too cool to steam in the crisp air. A hot-blooded creature standing on the lowest plaza would pour visible plumes of fog from its nostrils and radiate heat like a stove, throwing the delicate condensation channels nearby into chaos. The residents of the pit do not steam. They inhale once every two or three minutes, drawing the frigid air into broad, baffled nasal cavities that reclaim every drop of moisture before the breath slips back out through pursed, lipless mouths.

Watch one move along a boot-wide cantilevered slate walkway, and you understand why the staircases have no railings and the bridges are no thicker than walking sticks. Speed creates wind; wind disturbs the thermal plug; haste burns sugar that this tiny vertical farm takes months to manufacture. And haste makes noise. So they move like clock-hands, or like chameleons stalking along a bough—a continuous, hydraulic flow devoid of twitch or tremor. A foot lifts, hangs cleanly over a basking moth without casting a sudden shadow, and plants itself on the saddle-worn step beyond. The silky white birds do not even step aside; a keeper simply slides a thick, cool palm beneath a bird’s breastbone, lifts it as gently as an alabaster jug, sets it on the parapet behind him, and continues upward.

Their eyes are extraordinary, and slightly unsettling when two of them turn downward from an upper gallery to regard the pit floor. Enormous, almost completely round, and set beneath heavy epicanthic folds of pale flesh, they lack visible whites; the entire exposed globe is a dark, mirrored slate-amber, overlaid with a second, clear nictitating membrane that slides sideways from the tear duct once every few minutes. In a shadowless box of cross-reflected glare that would snow-blind a lowland traveler by noon, their irises constrict not to pinpricks, but to narrow horizontal slits crossed by tiny vertical combs—natural polarizing louvers that cut the dazzle of the selenite and let them see into the ultraviolet spectrum. To them, the white walls are a map of subtle mineral tints, the waxy farina on the giant rosette plants glows with indicators of sugar-ripeness, and the silent saffron-and-violet flashes of the birds’ throat-pouches are bright semaphore.

They have their own semaphore, quieter still. Having neither voice boxes suited for sound nor ears that protrude beyond tiny, pinhole apertures sealed with waxy down, they speak across the courtyard through faint, deliberate flushes of dark blue capillary blood just beneath the translucent skin of their throats, temples, and broad inner wrists. Two keepers sitting on opposite sides of the pit—thirty paces apart, separated by four tiers of mossy roofs and silent alleys—will hold an hour-long consultation without lifting a finger from their knees. One raises a wrist slightly to catch a mirror-beam; across the pale skin, three slow bands of indigo bloom, sharpen into crisp geometric bars, hold for ten seconds, and fade back to tallow-white. Across the void, the other’s throat darkens in a bruised, stippled diamond—acknowledgment, or perhaps a counter-proposal regarding the angle of an upper cornice crystal as the autumn sun drops lower on the horizon.

For they are not merely inhabitants of the pit; they are its governor-weights, its thermostats, its slow, tireless kidneys. Left to itself for five years, even this miracle of passive engineering would drift out of balance. Frost-heave at the rim would tip a primary mirror; clubmoss would creep half an inch too far over a soapstone cistern; snail-mucus and green algae would dam a hairline herringbone groove until a drop grew heavy enough to fall, splash, and begin the work of erosion.

Every waking hour—though they do not truly wake and sleep so much as cycle through deeper and shallower states of torpor—is given over to microscopic husbandry. 

Look closely at the belt-pouches folded directly into the heavy, loose creases of skin at their hips, and you find their toolkit: burins of black beach-flint hafted in ancient, chert-hard bone; tiny scrapers made from amber snail-shell; tweezers of split bird-quill; and small pads of felted moth-wool. 

A keeper kneels before a sweating alabaster wall, her broad knees sunk into a foot-thick cushion of silver heath that barely dimples under her distributed weight. For half an hour she does not appear to move at all, her slit-pupiled eyes three inches from the stone. With the tip of a quill, she lifts a single translucent bristle-worm out of a condensation flute and sets it on the tongue of a waiting, clear-skinned amphibian beside her heel. Then she draws the flint burin two inches down a groove cut by an ancestor eighty generations dead—not deepening it, only shaving away a micron-thin rind of precipitated gypsum scale that has begun to slow the creeping bead of water above it. She catches the microscopic white dust on a damp pad of moth-wool before it can drift, rolls it into a pellet, and presses it into a tiny mortar-seam under the stair tread.

Nothing is wasted because nothing can be thrown away. There is no *away*. The gravel plain thirty-five feet overhead might as well be the surface of the moon; to climb out into the screaming lowland wind would strip the moisture from their nostrils and chill their un-furred skin to stone in twenty minutes. Everything that dies or is shed inside the geode must immediately become architecture, soil, or flesh again.

In the deepest undercrofts—those crisp, brilliantly lit rooms four layers down where rime-ice permanently fringes the water pots—lie the digestion vaults. They are no larger than bread-ovens, sealed with ground-glass slabs of gypsum greased with bird-fat. Into these go every clipped frond of clubmoss, every shed moth-scale, the droppings of the ptarmigan-birds, the shells of dead snails, and, once or twice in a decade, the cool, curled body of an elder whose heart has finally slowed to a permanent stop between one breath and the next. Inoculated with pale, cold-loving fungi that generate just enough chemical warmth to keep the sealed stone vaults at fifty degrees, everything inside breaks down silently into a rich, odorless black paste and soluble mineral salts. 

Once a month, two keepers unseal a vault, lade out thumb-sized measures of the black paste with bone spoons, and tuck it directly beneath the roots of the millstone-sized rosette plants, sealing the moss back over the incision so not a whiff of nitrogen escapes into the air. Should a body yield new bone, the femurs and ribs are cured in saturated gypsum-brine until mineral replaces marrow, turning them over years into pale chert pins waiting in neat rows inside a cubby for the century when an ancient bog-wood dowel finally shears.

Their diet is as concentrated and bloodless as the light. They never kill the silky white birds, nor the slow amphibians; in a closed box thirty-five paces wide, predation is a fever that ends in starvation. Instead, the animals are living condensers of calories. Every third or fourth day, a flightless hen climbs into a keeper’s lap on a sunny balcony and lays an unfertilized egg disproportionately huge for her body, shelled in soft, chalky membrane and packed with dense orange yolk synthesized from moss-spores and midges. The keeper trades her a spoonful of crushed amber snails and two swollen, sugary leaves plucked from the underside of a stonecrop rosette. They eat the yolk raw, puncturing the membrane with a neat lower incisor and sucking it dry without spilling a smear, then fold the leathery shell and chew it for an hour until the calcium dissolves. They harvest the sweet, antifreeze sap that beads at the wounds of pruned tundra-cushions, licking it from little carved soapstone cups, and scrape high-oil algal films from sunlit plates of slate set specifically in the dampest corners as miniature pastures.

Birth happens as rarely and deliberately as death. When the census drops by one—never before—a single child is conceived in one of the dry, moss-lined inner alcoves where six reflected beams are focused onto a dark slate wall to raise the room’s air to a balmy forty-five degrees. The infant emerges small as a loaf of dark bread, already wrapped in its pale wax-blubber, eyes open and glazed against the glare. It never cries. It does not possess the reflex. If an infant ever drew breath to wail in that acoustic tomb, the sheer shock of airborne vibration might shatter the stillness like a dropped stone; instead, when hungry, its tiny chest flushes bright violet from collarbone to navel, glowing softly against its mother’s alabaster belly until she presses a nipple oozing thick, butter-yellow colostrum to its mouth.

For its first ten years, the child barely leaves the central pillars, creeping on silent palms up and down the tight spiral stairs, learning the angles of the three thousand selenite mirrors the way other children learn grammar. It learns how the summer solstice sends steep, blue-white shafts straight to the bottom plaza and threatens to scorch the liverworts unless seven hundred clay-notched crystals on the upper cornices are tipped three degrees skyward. It learns how a winter gale roaring invisible across the rim overhead sucks slightly at the top inch of the cold-air plug, requiring the broad, thin sheets of cleaved mica stored under the top-tier eaves to be slid halfway out over the highest balconies as wind-baffles.

If you were to lie flat on your belly at the rim of the gravel sinkhole—careful not to dislodge a single frost-bitten pebble over the lip—and peer down into that dazzling, shadowless jewel-box, it might take an hour before your eyes sorted the living stone from the dead.

Then, slowly, the whole thirty-five-pace world resolves into a single, breathing clockwork. Down on the third tier, an elder with skin polished nearly clear at the knuckles sits cross-legged beside a cistern, holding a rough-split plate of selenite in his lap, grinding its edge smooth against wet soapstone at a pace of one stroke per minute, never making a scrape. Two balconies below him, a pair of younger keepers pass a clay pot of rich black humus hand to hand across a shoulder-wide alley, their wrists flashing brief, silent bars of slate-blue across the bright gap. A silky white bird steps over a keeper’s foot; a fat moth drops like wool from a mirror rim and lands squarely on a bald, tallow-pale crown, spreading its wings again to bask there undisturbed.

Not one of them looks up at your dark silhouette against the high, pale sky. Wait long enough, however—let your breath mist in the freezing gale above, let a single stray crystal of frozen spittle drift across the invisible boundary of the thermal plug and fall forty feet down into the windless well—and every slit-amber eye across all seven tiers will pivot upward in the same second, smooth and synchronous as sunflowers, watching the foreign speck of ice fall toward their mirrors until a broad, cool hand reaches silently out from a doorway to catch it before it strikes the stone.
