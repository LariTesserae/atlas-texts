You usually see the lantern first, and it is never an electric one. 

Battery seals fail down here inside a week, and heavy extension cords drag on the sharp travertine until their rubber jackets shred, so the Tenders carry pressurized brass carbide lamps hooked directly to the sternum straps of their harnesses. The open acetylene flame burns with a fierce, hissing white point that punches twice as far through the vapor as the amber cage-bulbs, and it throws off just enough dry, localized heat to keep a small patch of the wearer’s chest from growing slick with bacterial slime. When a Tender moves across the terraces—stepping from the jagged lip of one flooded dam to the next with a flat-footed, rolling gait that never slips on the wet crystal—that white star bobs smoothly through the mist, drawing a comet-tail of caddisflies behind it.

There are perhaps forty of them in the basin at any given time, though you never see more than three together, and they do not live on the surface anymore in any way that counts. Nominally, they are employed by the regional drainage authority that inherited the flooded mine workings forty years ago; a stainless-steel freight cage still drops down the main western shaft every second Tuesday with drums of calcium carbide, buckets of heavy marine grease, brass replacement valves, salt tablets, and crates of canned lard and hardtack. The cage waits twenty minutes at the bottom landing while two figures in dripping rubberized aprons load up empty drums and grease-pencil a single column of pump-head pressures onto a slate board bolted inside the car, and then the bell wire is jerked twice and the cage rises back into the dry world. Nobody from above rides it down. Nobody from below rides it up. Some of the younger ones—twenty or twenty-five years old, broad-chested, slightly bow-legged from climbing steeply pitched dolomite blocks since they could walk—have never seen rain that was cold, or a horizon wider than twenty yards of lit steam.

A Tender’s body adapts to the eighty-five-degree saturation by shedding everything that traps dead water. They wear no cotton, no wool, and no boots. Footwear holds warm, mineral-heavy slush against the skin until the flesh turns white and sloughs off in ribbons; instead, they go bare-legged from the mid-thigh down, their feet and ankles cured over years into colossal pads of yellow-gray callus thick enough to tread directly on raw, coral-sharp tufa without bleeding. Between their toes and across the backs of their calves, they smear a paste rendered from bat fat, axle grease, and crushed sulfur crusts scraped off the hotter vents. It smells sharp and tallow-rank up close, and it sheds water like duck plumage. Above the knee, they wear short kilts and bibs cut from stripped conveyor belting and heavy vulcanized tire-tube, laced together with copper wire—iron rivets rust out in a month, and nylon cord stiffens into brittle rods of stone if you leave it unflexed over a weekend, so everything on their bodies is rubber, brass, copper, or oiled leather kept supple by constant motion.

They shave their heads and eyebrows clean with flat bronze scrapers. Hair is just an anchor for mist and lime; left alone for three days, a beard turns gritty with precipitated calcite and pulls at the skin like drying plaster. Without hair, and coated from crown to collarbone in that dull, glistening film of sulfur-grease, their pale faces look strangely streamlined in the amber dark, like wet river-cobbles or peeled roots. Their eyes stay half-lidded against the perpetual drizzle of condensation, with deep, permanent crow’s-feet at the corners from squinting into acetylene glare, and their ears are plugged flush with softened red beeswax to keep out the spores, the wandering midges, and the physical battering of the hundred-decibel roar.

Because the wax and the noise wall off the air completely, their mouths rarely open except to eat or spit. Speech among the Tenders lives entirely in the bones and the steel. 

Walk along one of the sagging catwalks with a pair of them and you will feel it under your bare soles before you realize anyone is talking: a crisp, syncopated rap of a bronze scaling-hammer against the iron grating—*tink, tink-tink, pause, tink*—that travels four hundred feet down the bolted stringers and right up through the ankle joints of whoever stands at the far end. If they are off the steel, wading knee-deep across the milky turquoise pools where the blind characins bump frantically against their shins, they speak by laying three fingers flat against a partner’s collarbone, jaw, or forearm and tapping out a rapid, fluid shorthand directly onto the skeleton, supplemented by quick, economical flicks of grease-stained thumbs in the white beam of a chest-lamp. Even when two of them lean forehead-to-forehead inside the trembling corrugated shell of a pump-house to share a spoken word—pressing their zygomatic arches hard together so the hum of their vocal cords conducts straight from skull to skull without crossing the air—their voices have gone flat, low, and drone-like, stripped of inflection, closer to the groaning harmonics of the diesel-electric drives than anything spoken in a parlor upstairs.

Their work has no completion state; it is pure subtraction, carried out at the exact speed of the mountain’s addition. Every Tender carries an air-hose slung over one shoulder like a coiled python, coupled into tap-valves welded at hundred-yard intervals along the main compressed-air lines that parallel the discharge pipes. At the end of the hose hangs a short, stubby pneumatic needle-scaler with bronze needles. All day—or whatever eight-hour stretch they measure by the burn-down of two carbide charges—they move along the infrastructure, knocking the stone back off the metal. You come across one crouched straddle-legged atop a four-foot discharge pipe while grey frogs scatter and immediately settle back onto his greased thighs, holding the chattering scaler against a flange joint until a five-inch rind of orange-streaked travertine cracks free in heavy, crystalline slabs and splashes into the terrace below, sending forty crabs scuttling sideways through the boil of fish. They chip the weeping fringes off the undersides of catwalks before the extra tonnage shears the anchor bolts. They scrape the green lamp-moss and caddisfly-silk off the brass cages of the hanging bulbs so the amber halos don’t choke down to blind pinpricks. They climb up into the pump-houses, wipe brown iron-bacteria out of the sight-glasses with wads of pale spleenwort frond, and pack fresh tallow into the weeping gland-seals of the turbines, checking the bearing temperatures not with thermometers—whose glass faces crust opaque in forty-eight hours—but by pressing the sensitive, uncalloused insides of their wrists directly against the trembling cast-iron housings, reading a half-degree rise in friction against the eighty-five-degree baseline of their own skin.

Where they sleep, the cavern looks less like an industrial camp and more like giant swallows' nests wedged high against the dry Lee-walls behind the thickest fluted pillars, forty feet above the terraces where the direct ceiling-sheets miss the rock and the warm drafts off the pump-exhaust pipes rise to create pockets of slightly thinner steam. They build these platforms themselves, lacing scavenged iron grating between columns and letting the dripping carbonate cement the edges into the stone over two or three seasons until steel and limestone fuse into seamless, cantilevered shelves. 

Up on the shelves, out of the main splash, they hang hammocks woven from frayed stainless hoist-cable padded with layers of split rubber hose—anything flat pools water, and anything porous rots, so a sleeping Tender hangs suspended like cargo in a open mesh cradle while warm drops tick steadily through the weave beneath their spine. Above the hammocks, strung on copper wires near the rising heat of the pump exhausts, hang strips of split cave-fish and bundles of peeled crayfish tails curing slowly in the warm draft, alongside upside-down clay crocks smeared inside with sugar-lard to trap midges, which they mash with hardtack crumbs and boiled fern-fiddleheads into dense, salty grey cakes cooked directly on the scorching exhaust manifolds of the diesel-electrics below.

Yet the humans are only the upright, tool-bearing layer of what occupies the basin, and they are neither the oldest residents of the built works nor the largest things moving through the mist between the lamps.

Follow a Tender on a scaling round toward the lower eastern terraces—down where the two distant collapse-chimneys drop their faint, vertical curtains of grey sky-light into the steam, and the rimstone amphitheaters widen out into waist-deep, sulfur-rimmed lagoons—and watch how carefully he tests the milky water ahead of his shins with the butt of a six-foot bronze pry-bar before stepping off a dam lip. He isn't checking the depth. Every Tender knows the contours of those pools blindfolded. He is prodding the bottom silt to wake whatever is lying flattened against the warm travertine so he doesn't tread on its back.

Decades ago, before the upper rock bed dropped and sealed the old horizontal haulage drifts, the surface drainage channels and farm-ponds above the subsidence zone collapsed inward along with forty acres of scrub timber, pulling surface fauna down four hundred feet into the dark. Most of what fell shattered on the dolomite talus or starved when the green leaves rotted. A few things landed in eighty-five-degree water choked with slow, meaty fish and crustacean shells thin enough to crush between effortless jaws, and never had a reason to leave.

The Tenders call them *flatbacks*—a single tap of the flat palm against the top of one’s own forearm—though fifty years ago a county game warden would have recognized their grandparents as common snapping turtles and softshells. Four generations in eighty-five-degree darkness with zero winter dormancy and an infinite trough of protein have warped them out of all surface proportion. Without seasons to slow their growth rings, and bathed continuously in dissolved calcium carbonate and magnesium, the survivors and their brood have ballooned into massive, sluggish plates of living stone, four and five feet across the carapace, weighing three hundred pounds or better. 

You can look straight down into two feet of clouded, steaming water under an amber bulb and never see one until it shifts. Their shells do not stay clean keratin or leathery skin; because they lie motionless for days at a stretch in the supersaturated outfall below the rimstone lips, letting the current wash stunned midges and blind loaches directly past their nostrils, tufa precipitates right onto their backs. Thick, terraced ridges of pale cream and rust-orange calcite grow outward from their costal scutes in jagged, coral-like shelves, hosting their own miniature forests of bacterial fuzz, crawling amber crabs, and grazing snails. A big flatback is a travelling rimstone dam. Only when a heavy, three-clawed foreleg—pale as boiled pork, thick as a man’s thigh, and folded loose with rubbery aprons of sensory skin—pushes lazily against the pool floor does a whole yard-wide slab of orange rock detach from the terrace with a soft, grating crunch and glide away through the boiling characins. Their eyes have shrunk to milky, pinhead beads sunk deep behind fleshy folds that protect them from sharp crystal edges, while the sensory barbels under their chins and along their thick, muscular necks have lengthened into pale, branching fringes six inches long, quivering constantly in the water to read the fine pressure-waves of swimming prey—or the heavy, rhythmic thump of a Tender’s calloused heel coming down a dam-wall twenty yards upstream.

They aren't vicious, not in a place where lifting their sleek, narrow heads three inches yields a mouthful of crab-meat, though a startled five-hundred-pound bull lunging across a shallow terrace will shear an ankle bone clean through just with the jagged mineral plough of its front shell-lip, and its beak can still nip a broomstick—or a rubber air-hose—in half with one hydraulic snap. Between the Tenders and the big reptiles sits an unhurried, physical truce. When a flatback grows so heavily crusted with travertine on one side of its carapace that it begins listing in the deeper pools, straining its neck to bring its tubular nostrils up to the warm mist, it will haul itself with immense, scraping patience onto the low, vibrating concrete apron around a pump-house—drawn, somehow, through forty yards of solid turbulence to the exact place where the bronze hammers ring—and simply park its bulk across the catwalk stairs. The next Tender who comes down the line doesn't curse or pry at it; he hooks his carbide lamp to a stilt-brace, dials down the pressure on his pneumatic scaler, and spends ten minutes chiseling fifty pounds of bulbous white limestone off the turtle’s scutes while the beast lies flat-chinned on the vibrating concrete with its pale eyelids shut, hissing once like a leaky steam-valve when the bronze needles buzz too close to the tender margin of its shell, before dragging itself, newly lightened, back over the orange lip into the gloom.

Look upward from those same lagoons—up past the amber light-cones, past the bundles of dangling surface tree-roots slick with brown bacterial mucilage, into the unlit upper third of the vault where the rising steam thickens against the jagged dolomite bristles—and the stone columns themselves seem to shift in slow, pallid ribbons.

Before the aquifer was breached, the older limestone fissures held small, isolated populations of true troglobitic salamanders—finger-long, translucent pink things that lived fifty years on three crickets a season in cold, sterile drip-pools. When the ceiling collapsed and the warm, sulfur-rich mist turned every vertical wall into an endless pasture of midges, springtails, larvae, and dense bacterial velvet, those starved cave lineages exploded outward onto the wet vertical stone, while surface eels washed down through the fractured sinkholes took over the dark clefts beneath the submerged dolomite blocks. Up on the towering twenty-foot pillars, just beyond the drying heat of the electric bulbs where the emerald moss stops dead, cling thousands of what look at first glance like pale, flattened roots peeled loose from the ceiling bundles: cave-salamanders grown to the length and girth of a man’s forearm, utterly blind, with external gill-plumes splayed behind their wide, shovel-flat heads like wet crimson ferns pulsing slowly in the hundred-percent humidity. They never need to enter the pools at all; the air itself is thick enough with warm water to keep their delicate, vein-traced skins breathing twenty-four hours a day. They hang head-downward on the fluted flowstone on splayed, suction-padded toes, motionless for hours until a cloud of caddisflies drifts out of a bulb-halo or a gray pipe-frog leaps wide onto the wet crystal—then a sudden, whip-crack lunge across the vertical slime, a wet gulp lost entirely inside the thunder of the falls, and stillness again.

High above even the gill-lizards, where the hot diesel exhaust from the boxcar pump-houses rises and pools in dryish, ninety-five-degree pockets among the ceiling fractures alongside the bat roosts, hunt the pale rats and the blind, pigmentless huntsman spiders with leg-spans wide as dinner plates. Everything up there moves by touch and air-current through absolute dark and deafening sound, picking off fallen bat pups, guano beetles, and roast-winged moths that drop from the high-tension cables.

And at the very bottom of it all—in the scalding, iron-choked sumps directly beneath the boxcar stilts, where the six-foot intake bells of the turbine pumps suck eighty-five-degree water down through heavy bronze trash-racks with a force that makes the steel pilings hum like cello strings—lives the thing the basin itself seems to be turning into.

Go down the rungs beneath Station Three with an older Tender when the intake grilles have begun to choke and the manifold needle climbs two pounds past the red chisel-mark on the brass dial. You climb down between the shuddering steel legs into chest-deep churn that smells heavily of rotten eggs, warm iron, and wet chalk, holding fast to a copper guide-chain because the draw of the intake bell ten feet down will pull a grown man’s feet out from under him across the slick tufa. In the piercing white cone of the chest-lamp, the submerged trash-rack isn't fouled with sticks or dead fish. It is woven shut with dense, swaying curtains of pale yellow and rust-red filaments as thick as sheep’s fleeces—colonial sulfur-bacteria and iron-oxidizing sheaths braided together with fresh calcite needles and strands of transparent, rubbery sponge into tough, felt-like leathers that quiver in the suction like breathing gills. Embedded right inside those mineral-bacterial mats live dense clusters of finger-long, eyeless tube-leeches, snow-white flatworms broad as willow leaves, and colonies of tiny, shell-less freshwater polyps pulsing open and shut in synchronized waves, catching the minced rain of dead midges sucked down from three miles of lit pools above, while big translucent shrimp with front pincers stretched thin as sewing needles pick delicately through the living felt without ever getting pulled through the bars.

The Tender hooks one elbow around the vibrating steel stilt, braces a calloused yellow heel against a crusted flange, and goes to work on the rack with a curved bronze hay-knife, slicing away thirty-pound slabs of tough, calcareous bacterial hide that bleed rusty water as they peel free and roll away across the lower terraces, where two hundred crabs and three drifting flatbacks are already converging through the milky warm dark before the torn mat even hits bottom.

Nobody hurries. Fifteen yards overhead, inside the streaming corrugated shed, the governor weights on the diesel-electric spin at their locked, immortal angle; the four-foot riveted pipe thuds softly under its six-inch sleeve of white stone and its solid pavement of chiming gray frogs; the Tender shakes the slime off his bronze blade, taps twice on the steel stilt with the pommel so his partner up in the pump-house knows the gauge needle is dropping back to the mark, and climbs unhurriedly up into the amber steam, already growing a faint, dusty bloom of fresh calcium along the dried edges of the sulfur-grease behind his knees before he reaches the top rung.
