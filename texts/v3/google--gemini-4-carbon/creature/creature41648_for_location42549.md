At first, the green things at the edges appear to be the whole story, and even they look less like residents than survivors clinging to the walls of an engine.

The three ribbons of emerald algae directly under the terracotta spout endure only because nothing else can stand where they stand; they are rooted in the raw bite of the tooth-chisel grooves where the scalding inflow hits hardest, thriving on sheer violence and dissolved sulfur while the torrent whips them sideways day and night without pause. Up in the narrow, booming gap beneath the cornice, the faint haze of protonema on the fresh mortar advances by fractions of a millimeter a week, burned back whenever the plunge shifts and throws a hotter tongue of spray too high. And in the unmortared weep-hole on the shaded south face, the two palm-sized maidenhair ferns live like stowaways in a chimney, flattened and shivering in the wet, percussive blast of air pushed continuously outward by the waterfall. None of these ever cross into the calm, bright volume of the room itself. The algae never colonize the quieter blocks a yard away; the moss never creeps down past the waterline; no loose spore ever takes hold in the mortar joints below.

Look down from the coping at noon—squinting against the white glare bouncing up from twenty feet of fresh limestone—and the reason seems obvious enough: the tank is simply too new, too hot, too clean, too fiercely lit.

Only if you stay on the rim until your eyes adjust to the watery incandescence do small discrepancies begin to show themselves in the geometry.

The shaft has no roof, and its coping stones sit flush with the surrounding ground. Wind crosses the land overhead every hour of the day, carrying grit, chaff, dry grass-seeds, tiny spiders on silk, stray beetles, dust kicked up from paths. Yet the square settling sump at the center of the bottom floor remains surgically bare. Not a grain of blown sand rests in its corners; not a single flake of grey mortar scale or red terracotta crumb lies against its lip; not one scalded midge drifts in the slow eddies behind the stairs. The southward current toward the slotted slate grate is steady, yes, but it is gentle near the floor—nowhere near strong enough on its own to scour heavy grit up out of a recessed stone trap.

Watch one of the mason’s alignment scratches fifteen feet down beside a mortar seam on the east wall. Watch it between the lashing nets of surface glare. Every few minutes, while the waves overhead keep up their brisk, chaotic chop, a six-inch section of that carved scratch bends smoothly sideways—magnifying slightly, doubling its crisp white line as if a thick bevel of clear glass had just slid across the block—and moves steadily north, straight *against* the undertow, before vanishing into the bright stillness beneath the stairs.

Watch the rising sparks of air driven down by the waterfall. Millions of tiny bubbles burst outward from the central pillar of froth and sweep south toward the grate. Most rise freely. Near the lower third of the chamber, however, whole curtains of micro-bubbles abruptly flatten out against an unseen horizontal ceiling ten feet off the floor. They do not pop; they roll sideways in neat, gleaming files, tracing dead-straight lines through ostensibly empty water—turning sharp right angles in mid-volume, outlining invisible rafters and transparent planes stretched taut from wall to wall—before spilling over an unseen edge and shooting upward again.

Nothing swims in the basin. What occupies it does not swim, and it is not an animal.

Before the builders tapped the thermal spring upstream, its substance existed half a mile underground in pitch darkness, dispersed through narrow faults in the hot bedrock: neither plant nor beast, without eyes, gut, nerve, or fixed boundary—only an ancient, blind slick of thermophilic jelly wedded to dissolved silica and carbonate, creeping at geological speed along scalding fissures and feeding on heat gradients and mineral bleed. When the masons intercepted the vein and routed it through fine upstream screens into a fired terracotta pipe, that dispersed substance tore easily into microscopic threads, passed every mesh like clear broth, and poured roaring out of the north wall into this cube.

It should have washed straight across the twenty-foot room and drained away through the slotted slate slabs on the south side. For the first few days after the water was turned in, most of it did.

What stopped the rest were three things the subterranean dark never had: fresh quicklime, standing thunder, and noon light.

Where the raw spring water met the newly smeared lime-and-sand mortar, a sharp alkaline veil bled out of every joint. Whenever wisps of that deep-earth mucilage brushed the mortar seams, the free calcium stung their surfaces into toughness, instantly curing their outer molecules into flexible, semi-permeable skins of clear silicate-lime a thousandth of a millimeter thick—too transparent to cast a shadow, with the exact refractive index of the hot water around them. At the same time, the incessant, drumming roar of the waterfall, trapped between five flat planes of dressed stone, set up a permanent three-dimensional lattice of acoustic standing waves throughout the basin: dead-still pressure nodes alternating with humming walls of vibration. Shreds of clear film and dense gel washed out of the central froth-plume and stalled in those quiet acoustic pockets, touching, fusing edge to edge, and anchoring themselves into the crisp, parallel bite of the iron tooth-chisels on the floor.

Then the noon sun came straight down the unroofed shaft, struck the white mirror-box, and flooded those clear envelopes with raw light from six directions at once.

What lives in the cistern now is a single, continuous, glass-clear body that has been knitting itself together out of the spring’s inflow ever since—a hydraulic architecture of water held slightly denser and more viscous than the water around it, partitioned by self-healing mineral membranes into hundreds of flat chambers, tubes, and taut, trembling sheets.

At midday, under the hammer of the sun, it spreads itself entirely open and flat to drink the heat and radiance. Its broad basal foot lies nested to the exact millimeter inside the shallow square settling sump at the center of the floor, pressed so tightly to the flags that it looks like empty stone. From there, transparent aprons extend across the bottom pavers and climb the western and eastern blocks, hooking microscopic holdfasts into the rough drove-marks while leaving every sharp arris untouched. When a speck of windblown dust or a dead insect drops from the sky into the boiling glare overhead, it never reaches the stone. Caught on one of those stretched, invisible sheets mid-water, anything mineral—a crumb of lime, a grain of blown limestone dust—is folded inward, dissolved by weak organic acids inside a sealed pocket, and built straight into new membrane. Anything organic and foreign—a charred gnat, a dry grass-husk, a torn shred of green algae ripped loose from under the spout—is passed swiftly backward by slow, rippling contractions from sheet to sheet and pushed cleanly through the narrow slots of the south slate grate, keeping the mirror-box spotless so the light will never dim.

Near that south grate, where the silent undertow pulls hardest, the body thickens into tough, braided guys anchored around the masonry frame. One folded lobe stays permanently wrapped over the ends of the swollen oak pegs that pin the slate slabs in place. Those oak pegs are the sole piece of timber in a universe of stone, baked clay, and water; through them, molecule by molecule, it tastes the slow, dark astringency of steeped wood-tannin and holds against the constant suction of the drain.

Because it came out of ragged, lightless cracks in the earth and woke inside a mason’s masterpiece, it knows no shape except the geometry of this room.

Everything it has ever touched here is straight, plumb, square, and modular. The acoustic nodes that cradle it divide the twenty-foot cube into exact mathematical thirds and quarters; the blocks repeat their courses; the floor flags repeat their squares. Over weeks of continuous thunder, the resident has grown fiercely, blindly architectural. Its internal chambers meet at ninety-degree seams. Where it drapes across the lower walls, it presses its sensitive outer skin deep into the mason’s alignment scratches beside the mortar joints—holding onto those deliberate, straight-ruled incisions as if they were the truest things in the world, sometimes depositing an invisible glaze of pure aragonite inside a scratch that keeps the mark gleaming white instead of letting the hot water dull it.

All afternoon it lies still in the glare, discernible only by the stray warp of a chisel-groove or the sudden right-angled detour of rising spark-bubbles.

Only when the sun drops away from the ground overhead and the blazing square of sky turns violet, then black, does it stop being a sheet and become something else.

In the dark, the mirror-box goes blind. Cooler night air meets the steaming surface at the rim, filling the top of the shaft with swirling white vapor, while down below the water remains just as hot and the red terracotta conduit hammers on at the exact same pitch. Freed from the pinning pressure of the sunlight, the clear membranes let go of the western wall and peel smoothly up off the floor flags. By pumping hot, bubble-threaded fluid from chamber to chamber—stringing captured micro-bubbles along its interior ribs like tiny pearl floats—it gathers its scattered acreage inward toward the eastern wall, where the narrow, unbalustraded stone stair drops from the rim.

The stair is the one asymmetrical thing in the cube: a rising sequence of sharp, single-block steps cut for a kind of body that built this room and never came back.

All night, in pitch blackness and deafening thunder, what lives in the basin climbs and re-climbs the eastern stair.

It does not swim up; it steps by hydraulic increments. A heavy, coherent sack of dense water swells onto the lowest tread, braces neatly against the vertical riser, creases itself along the razor-sharp stone edge without tearing, and pours a second chamber up onto the second step, then a third onto the fourth, folding and stacking its transparent bulk into upright, squared-off columns that test what it means to stand vertical against gravity rather than lie flat under light. Rung by rung it mounts through fifteen feet of hot dark water until its uppermost crown breaks the heaving surface in the narrow, handspan gap of booming air beneath the overhanging cornice.

There, inches below the dry ground-level coping stones, it holds itself up in the scalding draft. Its exposed dome—slick, colorless, trembling violently with the concussive slap of the nearby waterfall—presses lightly against the underside of the cornice blocks, sending faint, rhythmic waves of warm mist sideways along the south wall that keep the two tiny maidenhair ferns in their weep-hole glistening through the dry night. For hours it lingers at the top of the stair, pressed to the ceiling of its world, listening through the stone.

Because the drumming roar of the fall saturates every block, the limestone walls are an exquisite sounding board. Even at night, if a fox trots past ten yards away across the turf, or wind rattles dry stalks near the rim, the coping stones shift their harmonic vibration by a fraction of a tone, and the clear column waiting at the top of the submerged stair stills instantly, feeling the weight pass overhead.

By dawn it has already sunk back down the risers, uncreasing its tall folds, letting out its trapped buoyancy bubbles in a single silvery sigh that races to the surface and vanishes in the foam. By the time noon stands directly over the shaft again—or whenever the heavy, muffled thud of boots approaches across the ground and vibrates down through the coping into the water—every vertical chamber has collapsed dead-flat against the flags and the central sump.

Anyone who steps to the edge and peers down squints against a fierce, empty incandescence: roaring hot water, crisp new chisel-marks, millions of sparks racing upward, three green whips thrashing under the terracotta lip, two small ferns trembling in a hole, and fifteen feet down, beside a clean white seam, a mason’s scratch that seems for half a breath to shimmer thicker than an iron point cut it, before the surface glare lashes across the stone and washes the distortion away.
