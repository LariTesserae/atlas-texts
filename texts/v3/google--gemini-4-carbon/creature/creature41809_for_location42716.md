You do not see what lives in the upper basins until you have been standing on the slate platform long enough for the sweat on your neck to match the temperature of the air. When you first climb down through the crooked shale slots, your breathing is too loud, and the tiny wakes you set off by stepping on a submerged rimstone lip travel outward from pan to pan, tapping faintly against the fluted columns, warning the whole room. Everything that can tuck itself away has already done so, not in panic—nothing in this warm soup has the metabolic budget for panic—but with a slow, hydraulic retraction.

Wait twenty minutes. Let the glassy skins of water heal over.

The first thing to resume its business is not the amphipod, nor the salamander, which has likely not moved since Tuesday. It is the crusts themselves. Look closely at the submerged terraces where the ochre bacteria lie thickest, and you will see that some of the pale, waxy nodules you took for cave-pearls are slightly asymmetrical, pinched at one end like drops of tallow cooling on a slant. Every few minutes, one of these nodules tilts by a fraction of a millimeter, lifting its blunt edge from the silt to let a microscopic current of warm, mineral-heavy water pass beneath it. They are *calcite-limpets*—blind, shell-heavy gastropods that have given up making their own armor entirely, instead secreting a sticky protein mucus over their backs that encourages the saturated water to plate them in pure, translucent travertine. They graze on the iron-oxidizing films at a pace that makes the hour hand of a clock look frantic. A single limpet might spend four years crossing a basin the size of a dinner plate, leaving behind a clean, pale track in the rust-colored slime that the bacteria heal over within a week. When one dies, it does not rot; its foot dissolves, its stone dome settles, and the creeping water simply welds it to the floor, turning it into just another bump in the rimstone lip. Half the roughness under your boots is old, dead limpets.

Because the limpets are so slow and so stony, very little bothers to eat them. What does lives wedged into the narrow, submerged clefts between the fallen slabs of older rock, down where the warm seepage pushes up directly from the aquifer fissures. 

If a pale amphipod wanders too close to one of those dark, soap-smoothed wedges, a pair of appendages will slide out into the pewter twilight—not fast, never fast, more like two pale roots feeling their way through water. They belong to a crab no wider than a walnut, though *crab* seems too brisk a word for something so soft-jointed and ghostly. Its carapace is thin, flexible, and unpigmented, the color of boiled cartilage, because calcium here is paradoxically too heavy; if it let the water mineralize its shell the way the limpets do, its joints would lock solid between molts. So it spends its life fastidiously grooming itself. Tucked back under the leaning rock, you can sometimes catch the faintest rhythmic movement: its third pair of maxillipeds, fringed with stiff, brownish bristles, tirelessly sweeping its own eyeless face, its gill chambers, its long, spindly walking legs, brushing away the microscopic crystals of calcite before they can take hold. Its claws are disproportionate—not heavy crushers, which would require too much muscle to feed in this lean room, but long, curved, needle-fine forceps like a surgeon’s tweezers, meant for reaching into tight crevices to pluck out soft cup-fungi, blind isopods, or the rubbery egg-sacs the salamanders glue to the undersides of drowned ledges.

And then there is the reason for the clay jars, and the copper pins, and the black burl scoop.

Nobody lives down here on the slate platform—a human body kept in this perpetual, hundred-percent-humidity bath would develop skin rot within ten days, lungs heavy with warm vapor, joints aching from the constant sideways wedging between pillars. The keeper does not live in the hollow. Yet the hollow is tended, and the tending is a life of its own, so deeply fitted to the sinkhole’s clock that it looks like abandonment to anyone used to the surface.

If you stay until the wet-ash light begins to thicken toward evening gray, you might hear a sound from above that does not belong to the water: the dry, gritty *scritch* of heavy hemp rope Paying out over a lip of slumped shale at the western rim, where one of the crooked sky-slots widens enough to pass a basket, or a small, compact person.

She comes down barefoot. Boots are useless on the greased-soap curves of the flowstone; you need toes that can splay and grip the micro-terraces of the travertine, calloused soles tough enough not to mind the occasional sharp crystal druse, skin sensitive enough to feel the slickness of an ochre bacterial mat before you commit your weight to it. She wears almost nothing—a wrap of heavily oiled linen around her hips, her hair bound tight to her skull with a leather thong so it won't snag on the blunt stalactites or trail in the brines. Before she even drops through the slot, she has smeared her shins, forearms, and shoulders with rendered mutton tallow; in the pewter dimness, her skin has the exact dull, waxy gleam of the wet columns she slips between, shedding the warm condensation in round, distinct beads just like the green copper pins.

She moves sideways through the stone crowd without looking for handholds. Her hands already know every swollen fold of drapery, every gap between the leaning boulders. She doesn't splash. When she steps from one submerged rimstone lip to the next, she places her foot slowly, sliding it edge-first through the surface tension so the glassy skins barely ripple. She has been coming down into this pocket four times a year for forty years, and her mother before her, and someone with a cold-hammered copper chisel five hundred years before that.

You realize, watching her crouch on the stacked slate platform—her knees drawn up to her chest, taking up no more room than the stone allows—that she is not collecting the water. You don't need to chisel channels and set heavy clay jars on dry ledges just to haul up warm mineral water.

She reaches out and lifts the waterlogged burl scoop from its two inches of brine. Turning sideways, she reaches into the deeper, chiseled trough wedged between the two leaning pillars, where the three upper basins feed their warm seepage together. Hanging submerged in that trough—invisible until she hooks a loop of tarred horsehair off a submerged knob of calcite—is something that looks like a drowned fleece, or a heavy mat of pale, translucent felt.

It is neither animal nor plant, though the surface villagers call it *stone-caul* or *mother-of-brine*. It is a symbiotic scoby—a thick, rubbery, layered consortium of thermophilic bacteria and aquatic fungi that grows nowhere else on the coastal shelf except in the exact mineral-and-gas equilibrium of this single warm sinkhole. Left to itself in the natural shallow pans, it only forms thin, ragged skins no bigger than a saucer before the limpets graze it tattered. Concentration is what makes it thicken: the ancient pecked gutters bring just enough steady, warm sulfur-and-calcium seepage into the deep trough to feed a mat three fingers thick, heavy as wet leather, pale ivory on top and stained faint rust underneath.

She does not take the whole thing. If you take the whole mother, it takes twelve years for floating spores and stray bacteria to weave a new one from scratch. Moving with the unhurried, economical precision of the cave-crabs, she lifts one corner of the slick mat onto her bare knee, draws a small, leaf-shaped blade of bronze from her hip-wrap—iron rusts to red mush in a single season if you accidentally drop it down here—and slices off a strip four inches wide. The cut makes no sound at all, like slicing firm custard. She lets the parent mat sink slowly back into its warm bath, looping the horsehair tether back over its calcified knob.

Now the copper pins make sense. She rises carefully, turning her greased shoulders between the damp pillars, and hangs the severed strip of pale caul across the three green pins wedged into the vertical seam. Over the next three months, suspended in the motionless, saturated air out of reach of the crabs and limpets, the strip will slowly weep out its bitterest heavy brines, curing in the perpetual warm sweat of the stone while absorbing the airborne spores of the thumbnail cup-fungi. 

From one of the heavy, unglazed clay jars—not one of the ones cemented fast by a drip; those were abandoned decades ago when the ceiling water shifted its path by two inches, and nobody here wastes effort fighting the stone—she lifts a flat slate lid. Inside, the strip she hung on the pins at the autumn equinox has finished its slow slump: it has liquefied at the edges and firmed at the core into a dense, amber-colored paste that smells faintly of hot wet flint, iodine, and turned earth. Using the heavy black burl scoop, she transfers this cured paste into a smaller leather flask slung at her neck, scrapes the inside of the clay jar clean, and transfers the strip hanging on the pins—Wait: she checks the pins first, running a thumb over their green patina. The dissolved copper salts that bead on those pins drip onto the curing strip in trace amounts, keeping the surface molds from spoiling it while the deeper cave-ferment works. A strip from the autumn pins goes into the jar; the fresh winter strip from the trough goes onto the pins. 

Everything is done in silence. Her breathing is shallow, accustomed to the heavy carbon-dioxide pocket that pools near the floor on windless days; she knows that if the tiny, hollow *blup* of gas in the corner basin starts coming faster than once every ten heartbeats, or if the cave-salamanders have all climbed near the ceiling slots instead of hanging midway down the pillars, she must leave the scoop, climb the rope at once, and wait for a coastal gale outside to scour the upper ledges and draw the bad air out. Today, a mottled salamander hangs upside down four feet from her left ear, its throat pulsing once a minute, completely indifferent to her tallow-greased warmth. Its blind pale eyes—little more than dark spots under smooth skin—don't register her at all. To the salamander, she is just a warm pillar that occasionally shifts its weight, infinitely less interesting than the fat, sluggish crane-fly larva working its way through the wet clay behind a cluster of cup-fungi.

Before she climbs back up, she performs one small act of maintenance. Taking the back of her bronze knife, she leans down from the slate platform and gently scrapes the newly formed film of white travertine crystals out of the three pecked gutters, thumbnail-width by thumbnail-width, keeping the ancient channels open for another season so the stone won't seal the trough shut. The pale grit drifts down into the lower basin, settling softly over the backs of three calcite-limpets, who simply incorporate the dust into their shells and keep inching toward Tuesday.

She sets the ironwood-dense scoop back down in its two inches of clear brine, in the exact indentation its rounded bottom has worn into the silt. She slips sideways between the crowding columns, finds the dangling end of her hemp rope, and pulls herself hand-over-hand up into the crooked slot of pewter sky, her oiled ankles vanishing between the slumped shale ledges with hardly a scrape of grit.

Above, on the windy coastal shelf, that amber paste in her flask will be traded grain by weight-of-silver grain—smeared into deep puncture wounds made by fish-spines and rusted hooks, where nothing else stops the grey mortification from climbing an arm, or dissolved a fingernail's-breadth at a time into warm goat's milk to break the winter lung-fevers. Up there, it is a miracle, a fiercely guarded secret of one quiet household whose women always smell faintly of mutton fat and wet limestone.

Down here, the tiny ripples from her last step tap softly against the base of the pillars, cross the tiered pans, and die out against the weeping walls. Five minutes after the rope is hauled clear of the shale slot, a ghostly, soft-shelled crab eases half an inch out from beneath a rounded boulder, reaches its long, needle-fine tweezers into the chiseled gutter, and delicately picks up a tiny curl of pale scoby trimmed loose by her bronze blade. Higher up, a heavy drop gathers at the blunt tip of a brown curtain, swells until gravity wins, and falls—*plink*—into the exact center of a three-inch pool, and for the next ninety days, nothing else happens at all.
