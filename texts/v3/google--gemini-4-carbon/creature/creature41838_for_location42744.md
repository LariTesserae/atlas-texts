To see them clearly, you must first distinguish them from the wool and the stone. A figure paused on a terrace landing—resting a load of dung-cakes against a chest-high cairn while waiting for a string of beasts to clear the kinked trench below—looks like an outcrop furred with frost. Only when a breath slips past the felt muffler, blossoming briefly into white rime before the dry air swallows it, do you realize the mass is breathing.

Unbundled indoors, down in the close, lanolin-heavy dark of the sleeping vaults, their bodies tell the history of the altitude. They are short-limbed and immense of chest, their ribcages sprung wide like barrels to house lungs that take in a third more air with every draw than any lowlander’s. Their hands are broad, thick-padded, and perpetually warm, fed by wide capillaries that refuse to constrict even when handling frost-rimmed slate; a mason can set dry stone bare-handed in a biting breeze for half an hour before tucking their fingers back into the greasy armpits of their tunic. Their blood runs thick and dark as plum-butter, heavy with red cells. Under the layers of smoked grease and peat-soot that seal their skin against the scouring dryness, their faces are wind-burnished to the color of old copper, spiderwebbed across the cheekbones and nose-bridges with fine purple bursts of broken vessels. Around their dark eyes, the skin is gathered into permanent, deep-creased squints, and many of the elders carry milky crescents of frost-scar and snow-blindness across their corneas—hence the universal slits of blackened horn or flattened bone worn tied across the brow whenever they walk the upper roofs at midday.

They smell of what keeps them alive: old tallow, sour fermented mare’s or grazer’s milk, woodless dung-ash, and raw wool. Because liquid water is far too precious to waste on skin—every drop scraped as rime from the black basalt walls or chipped as yellow ice from the cisterns must go into the soup-cauldrons or the beasts’ troughs—nobody washes in the lowland sense. To put water on the body in this air is to invite the skin to crack and bleed before nightfall. Instead, in the stifling, sixty-degree warmth of the chambers directly above the livestock pens, families scrape one another clean once a ten-day. They smear the skin with warm marrow-fat mixed with finely powdered grey clay, rubbing it in until it lifts the grime and dead scale, then slick it off with the curved, polished rib-scraper every adult wears at their belt alongside their flint-knife and copper awl. The scrapings are rolled into little grey pellets and tossed down through the floor-chute into the night-soil bins; nothing organic is ever lost to the wind if it can feed the thumbnail tubers on the roof.

Movement among them is an art of absolute economy. A child learns before speaking that to run is to gasp, and to sweat inside one’s sheepskins is to freeze an hour later when the damp fleece turns to armor-plate. So they walk with the *step-and-settle*: a slow, rolling, flat-footed gait from the hip, placing the whole soft sole down at once on the glaze-ice, pausing a fraction of a heartbeat on the locked knee before lifting the trailing foot. Watch a line of porters ascending a four-hundred-foot stepped ramp from an ice-gully, each carrying two hundred pounds of river cobbles or frozen meat slung from a woven forehead-strap. They do not heave. They sway upward like pendulums in slow water, sixty paces to the minute, breathing through the nose in long, silent, four-beat draughts. When two lines meet in a trench barely five feet wide, there is no shouting for right-of-way. Those descending step onto the narrow stone ledges built at shin-height into the wall batters, pressing their backs flat into the dry-laid schist while the laden climbers brush past, sheepskin hissing softly against sheepskin, foreheads dipping an inch in silent acknowledgment.

Speech is shaped by the same thrift. You do not open your mouth wide to the freezing air, and you do not waste lungful after lungful shouting across a windy basin that will tear the sound apart in twenty yards. In the alleys and roofs, they communicate across distances with short, piercing bone-whistles—two notes for a loose herd, three sharp pips for a rock-slip, a long falling trill to call a child down before the shadow hits the terrace—and with broad, unmistakable gestures of the heavy-mitted arms. Up close, spoken language is tucked back behind the teeth and deep in the throat, rich in dry clicks, nasal hums, and soft uvular stops that require scarcely parting the greased lips. Inside the vaults at night, packed shoulder to shoulder under mounds of cured hides while a single tallow-wick gutters in a soapstone dish, their voices drop to a continuous, chest-vibrating murmur. Stories there last for weeks. There is no hurry to reach the end of a tale when winter is ten months long and the ancestors whose bones hold up the ceiling lived in this exact room four hundred years ago.

Their lives are bound to the great wool-skirted grazers in a knot so tight neither species could survive a single season on the plateau alone. The beasts are not property so much as huge, four-legged hearths that walk. A household’s wealth is measured not in land or coin, but in *stall-heat*—how many broad, steaming backs pack the sunken cellar beneath their floor. A family with twelve beasts sleeps in woolen shifts, kneading dough in comfort while frost feathers the inside of their smoke-hole; a family down to three beasts sleeps fully dressed in a tight, shivering knot, listening to the permafrost creep up through the flagstones. 

Consequently, the city’s great seasonal pulse is the slow, tidal breathing of the herds. Half a household—usually the younger aunts, uncles, and sturdy adolescents whose lungs are clearest—spends four to six months out beyond the fifty-mile labyrinth, drifting across the bleak, gravel-floored steppe basins where sparse tussock grass cures on the stalk. The other half—the elders, the nursing mothers, the masons, the water-scrapers, the blind—stays behind to tend the stone. Every autumn, for sixty days without pause, the inward tide thickens the sunken trenches: millions of saucer-hooves clicking softly over polished granite, filling the city with a dense, musky fog of breath and fresh droppings. Children scramble alongside the flanks of the moving beasts with leather baskets, catching the dung before it even strikes the ice, slapping it onto the sunny south-facing walls to freeze-dry into fuel bricks.

Because wood does not exist—most citizens go from birth to sky-burial having seen no timber larger than a thumb-thick willow wand traded up from the distant gorges for salt and wool—their relationship with fire is surgical. Nobody builds a blaze for cheer. Fire is kept alive in deep, sunken clay-lined pots buried in the center of the floor, fed one dry dung-pat at a time and choked down with flat slate lids so it smolders without flame, radiating slow heat into the surrounding stone benches. To boil water at this elevation is easy enough—it bubbles well before it is truly scalding—which means raw meat or hard grain boiled in an open pot stays tough forever. Instead, they cook by weight and patience: chunks of tough, dark grazer-mutton, rancid tallow, crushed tubers, and bitter wild sedge-seeds are packed into heavy steatite vessels whose lids are lashed down with wet rawhide that shrinks tight as it dries, sealing the steam inside until the pressure softens even gristle into rich jelly over a day’s smolder. Between meals, everyone carries a leather pouch of toasted, stone-ground barley or sedge meal mixed with hard tallow; you pinch off a walnut-sized lump with warm fingers and let it melt slowly against the roof of your mouth as you climb.

Above all else, these are people of the dry joint and the plumb-by-eye. Everyone in the fifty-mile rookery is at least a passable mason, because to neglect a wall is to murder your neighbors downslope. Frost-heave is the city’s slow, sleepless enemy; ice lenses form beneath foundations, swelling by fractions of an inch over decades until a ten-foot-thick retaining wall develops a subtle, pregnant belly. 

Walk through any district at midday and you will see the Wall-Readers at work. Often they are very old women or men whose joints have stiffened too much for the steppe drives, wrapped in triple-layered white hides, sitting motionless on folding bone stools across the alley from a suspect tier. They watch the shadow-lines as the razor-sharp sunlight slides across the granite courses. Where a slab has tilted a hair’s breadth out of true, its shadow catches differently. They press an ear—or a hollow grazer’s horn—against the base of a wall, listening to the microscopic *tick-tick-grate* of stone settling under millions of pounds of surcharge. When a bulge is condemned, the whole neighborhood turns out. There is no mortar to chip away; the city comes apart like a puzzle. Roof cobbles are stacked aside, slate shingles unpeeled, ancient whale-sized rib rafters untied and laid reverently on felt pads, and course after course of angular schist handed down warm, bare-fingered chains until they reach the heaved ice-lens thirty feet down in the dark. They dig out the rotten permafrost with horn picks, pack the pit with clean, dry gravel that drains seepage without swelling, and lay the exact same stones back into the exact same notches their great-grandparents knapped for them, locking each slab by friction and gravity alone. A single repair takes from first thaw-trickle to deep winter, conducted in near total silence save for the rhythmic *huff* of breath and the dry clink of diorite mauls trimming a fresh cleavage plane.

Even the animals wrapped into the masonry have been half-domesticated by this patient stillness. No citizen ever harms a pika or a rock-vole unless famine is chewing the leather off the sledges; instead, households leave narrow, deliberate lintel-gaps in their sunny outer walls—*hay-pockets*—knowing the little round-eared beasts will pack them solid with cured sedge grass all summer, insulating the stone six feet deep better than felt ever could, and providing an emergency fodder cache if an early blizzard locks the herds out on the steppe. On the roofs, the thousands of pale cliff-pigeons are farmed without cages. Warm smoke-vents are built with labyrinthine stone collars lined with nesting ledges; the birds crowd inside for the heat, and once a week a child reaches quietly into the warm, cooing dark to gather squabs and scrape up the rich white guano—essential both for the tuber beds and for tanning hides supple enough to bend at forty below.

And at the top of the city, on the highest, rawest windward spurs where even the condensation walls yield only dry rime and the wind blows hard enough to bruise exposed flesh, wait the butcher-towers and the broad, flat plinths of pale granite.

When someone’s lungs finally give out—often quietly in the dark before dawn, choked by decades of dung-smoke and thin air until the thick blood simply stops pushing—there is no digging of graves. You cannot dig permafrost with horn and slate, and you cannot burn a body where fuel is measured by the handful. Death is simply the final dismantling of a structure so its pieces can stay in circulation. Before the body stiffens into stone-hard ice—which happens in an hour once carried out of the vault—the family bears it up the stepped ramps on a bone-runner sledge, wrapped in a single worn felt sheet. 

The Cutters live near the plinths: calm, grease-shiny specialists with small, wickedly sharp blades of cold-hammered red copper and heavy mauls of banded gneiss. There is no weeping aloud up on the spurs—tears freeze eyelashes shut in seconds—only a circle of bundled kin standing back to the gale, humming a single low, chest-deep chord that vibrates in their ribs. Working quickly before the flesh freezes too solid for copper to part, the Cutters return the soft warmth to the sky, slicing and pounding flesh and organs and marrow-filled long bones into coarse paste mixed with barley meal. The moment they step back, the air darkens with ten-foot shadows. Dozens of bearded vultures drop from the dark-blue zenith with a stiff, papery roar of primary feathers—the loudest sound ever heard on the plateau—and in twenty minutes the granite slab is scraped cleaner than clay and grease ever scraped the living skin.

Nothing is wasted, though, if the city still needs a span. If the deceased was tall and strong-boned, sometimes a thighbone or a clean, heavy collarbone is held back from the mauls, cured in the dry gale for three winters until it rings like porcelain, and brought back down into the trenches. Years later, wedged tight as a shim beneath a sagging rib-rafter or carved into a slick runner for a water-sledge, the ancestor goes right on bearing weight, polished dark and satin-smooth over the centuries by the wool-padded shoulders brushing silently past in the cold.
