At first, staring down through that dazzling, freezing column of light, you would swear you have already seen every living thing in the shaft. The siphon-barrels, the tiered iron crawlers, the clockwork snails, the clanking shoals of piston-jellies, the little wire-cutter crabs snipping at loose slag—together they pack the narrow chasm so tightly that there appears to be no ecological gap left to squeeze a fingernail into, let alone another way of life.

Stay at the edge long enough for the hammering volume to stop numbing your thoughts, though, and your eyes begin to separate the outer crust of the foundry from what lies behind it.

Because nothing here roams and every generation anchors itself directly atop the shoulders of the last, the walls are not a single layer of animals clinging to fresh basalt. They are three and four beasts deep. Beneath the sunlit outer tier of booming copper barrels and hissing black-iron crawlers lies a buried under-story of their dead predecessors—ancestors that were overgrown, pinned shut, and slowly starved of mineral water decades ago. Their gutted copper drums and empty iron carapaces remain fused to the sharp rock steps by precipitated sulfide, forming a dark, flooded labyrinth of seized valves, hollow boilers, and slack, rotting gaskets sandwiched between the living machinery and the raw obsidian seams of the mountain.

It is back in that muffled plumbing—and down along the bright scrap-talus at the floor—that the cistern’s unarmored residents keep house.

You notice their work before you ever spot a body. Down near the bottom of the trench, where the rusty mineral stain weeps out of the fault line into the crisp daylight, the fallen litter of shed gears and snapped bone torsion-rods does not lie entirely at a natural angle of repose. Around a few dark gaps beneath overhanging steps of fractured basalt, broad, curved plates of shed copper hull have been dragged together and wedged overlapping like crooked roof-slates, glued at the seams with stiff gray gobs of secreted wax. They are not shelters against predators—nothing in this shaft hunts. They are acoustic baffles. In a narrow stone box where overlapping forty-second shockwaves from a hundred drop-forge valves create standing waves strong enough to rupture an unprotected egg membrane, soft tissue can only develop where the sound is deflected. Peek inside one of the dead, gutted siphon-barrels behind those copper eaves and you will find strings of amber eggs the size of buckshot, each armored in thin flakes of pyrite, hanging dead-still in water that barely trembles while the bright chasm inches away rings like the inside of a struck anvil.

Wait through twenty or thirty cycles of the big barrels, watching one of those narrow four-inch gaps between raw rock and rusted brass, and the builder will eventually ease itself out into the glare.

At first glance it looks like a bundle of stripped anatomical rigging that lost its shell and tried to mend the loss with floor sweepings. Evolved from the same six-limbed stock as the blind iron crawlers stacked up the walls, it took the opposite evolutionary wager: in water just above freezing, smelting a heavy suit of metal armor locks an animal in place for life, so this lineage abandoned the furnace. Its central body—broad and flat as a leather bellows so it can slip sideways behind jammed pipework—is encased only in pliable, overlapping aprons of tallow-colored chitin, padded thickly underneath with firm yellow blubber against the cold. Across its back, cemented down with tarry dabs of oxidized joint-grease, it wears a lopsided, asymmetrical roof of scavenged trash: a crescent of tarnished siphon-lip, two mismatched iron plates from dead crawlers, a sharp chip of black volcanic glass. None of the pieces match or sit straight; they shift and grate softly against one another like loose slates whenever its flanks expand.

Nothing about its six limbs obeys symmetry either. The rear four have stretched into long, double-hinged stilt-hooks no thicker than wrought-iron pokers, tipped not with claws but with split pads of biological lodestone that lock onto iron shells with a faint, dry *tick* so passing shockwaves cannot shake it loose. Its right forelimb has swollen into a crooked compound lever—pale, calcified bone struts rigged externally with thick tendon-cables and a heavy wedge of solid iron sulfide, ratcheted at the elbow so it can lean hours of slow, stored tension into a rusted seam without burning fast muscle. Its left forelimb does no heavy work at all. At the wrist it splits into a delicate, uneven fan of thirty or forty stiff filaments made of precipitated brass and fool’s gold, staggered in length from stubby pins to horsehair-fine needles, held splayed out ahead of it in the bright water like the comb of a broken music box.

Watch that brass comb when a siphon-beast thirty feet overhead slams its intake valve shut. As the percussive *thud* ricochets down the shaft, every needle in the fan shivers at its own harmonic pitch. By touching the base of the trembling comb against the copper barrel beside it, the creature reads the echo passing through the neighbor’s shell the way a quarryman taps stone to listen for a hidden crack—sorting healthy, water-full beasts from barrels whose valves are beginning to stick, silt up, or rot inside.

And unlike the blind crawlers, it is looking back at the light. Along the front rim of its tallow-white hood, the centuries since the mountain split open to the sky have coaxed old, dormant larval eye-spots back into service—not clear lenses, which the fierce high-altitude ultraviolet would cloud and burn, but a crooked row of tiny pinhole slits shaded beneath a jutting visor of corroded brass and lined inside with microscopic mirrors of iron pyrite. It sees its vertical world in overlapping strips of blinding white glare, ink-black crevice shadow, and glinting metallic edges.

Even so, it does not dart or swim. Nothing in one-degree water can afford to thrash. Yet where the millstone snails measure their journeys in feet per week, this creature creeps up the crowded cliffside at the deliberate, uncanny pace of a clock’s minute hand—gliding two inches, locking its magnetic stilts, waiting motionless through an entire forty-second *thud*, then reaching smoothly across a gap to hook the next rung. To an observer peering down from the dry rock above, it looks patient to the point of paralysis; to the snails and siphon-beasts it walks across, it moves like wind.

Follow one as it climbs the shadowed wall during the middle hours of the day, and you see how it pays for even that modest extravagance of speed.

Every few yards, it pauses beside a dense tier of black-iron crawlers, uncoils a flexible, copper-ringed proboscis from beneath its hood, and presses the flared tip directly over one of a crawler’s pale cartilaginous gaskets—sipping the thin silver ribbon of waste hydrogen bubbles straight into an elastic gas-bladder under its ribs instead of letting them escape toward the surface. The blind crawler beneath it never flinches; the gentle suction clears flaking sulfur crust from its pressure seam, leaving the gasket cleaner than it found it. When the sheer wall breaks off at an overhang, the climber does not waste days detouring down to the floor. It waits on a ledge until a weak thermal current rising off sun-warmed black iron nudges the central shoal of piston-jellies against the wall, hooks two lodestone stilts onto a jelly’s rigid crank-arm, tucks its brass comb-hand safely under its scrap-shingled belly to shield it from scalding puffs of yellow steam, and rides the clanking, jerking shoal upward through the crisp shaft of daylight like a clot of fouled chain caught in the rigging.

Its destination is the ceiling.

Climbing onto the highest encrusted ledge, just feet below that blinding white mirror where mountain wind whips silent ripples across the sky, it braces all four stilts into bare fractures of young basalt. Directly overhead swirls the rainbow-tinted film of coughed-up biological lubricant, trapping flat, quicksilver blisters of escaped hydrogen against the underside of the surface tension. From behind the creature’s scrapped hood, a narrow, heavily greased chimney-tube of tarnished brass telescopes slowly upward until its tip pokes three inches *through* the oil slick into the freezing, arid mountain air—the single piece of anatomy in the entire flooded shaft that ever touches the dry world outside.

If an ibex picking through the pass ever looked down into the booming cleft of water, that is all it would see break the rainbow sheen: a dull brass reed no thicker than a thumb, rimed with dry salt and stiff tallow, opening a three-lipped valve with a tiny wet *pop* to draw a slow, three-minute draught of thin alpine sky before sliding silently back beneath the mirror.

Down under the glare, as that cold sky-oxygen diffuses across damped internal membranes to meet its hoarded bladder of crawler-hydrogen, a dense mat of *Knallgas* bacterial symbiotes packed around its central nerve-cord reacts the two gases together in a slow, flameless burn. In water as clear as optical glass, you can actually see the result: a faint, wavering shimmer of heat-haze leaking from the seams of its pale belly, warming its core ten or twelve degrees above the surrounding icebox—just enough to keep its joint-grease runny and its oversized knot of ganglia sharp while everything else in the cathedral remains locked in near-freezing stupor.

It spends that hoarded warmth as the foundry’s mortician.

In a vertical cleft packed wall-to-wall without predators, death is a structural crisis. When a barrel-sized siphon-beast dies wedged halfway up the wall, its copper casing stays bolted to the basalt; if nothing ever dismantled the dead, sterile tombs would eventually choke every active ledge. Worse still are the millstone gastropods. When an old snail’s heart stops, or its shell cracks against an obsidian step, weeks of wound mechanical energy remain locked inside its coiled internal ratchets. Left to corrode on their own, dead snails eventually fail all at once—popping open with the violence of a snapped winch cable, flinging jagged iron shards across the room and raining fresh bone splinters onto the talus below.

Listening constantly with its splayed brass comb, the climber locates a snail whose rhythmic *clack* has gone silent while its shell still hums taut with trapped torque. Anchoring all four lodestone stilts onto the dead snail’s upper curve, it slides a single stiff feeler-needle through the slack, acid-coated foot-slit to find the internal sear-tooth, wedges its crooked, ratcheted pry-arm against the rock face for leverage, and trips the dead clockwork *one pawl at a time*. Across an afternoon, you can hear it working over the deeper thuds of the room—a measured, metallic *clack-whir... clack-whir... clack-whir*—bleeding months of lethal spring-tension harmlessly out into the freezing water until the coil goes slack. Only then does it lever up the casing to feed for days on the rich, copper-laced viscera and dense mineral fat inside, surrounded at the fringes by a patient ring of little wire-cutter crabs waiting to snip up whatever scraps drift loose.

There is neither hydrogen budget nor deadfall enough in so narrow a cleft for many of them. Four, perhaps five adults divide the entire flooded nave among themselves, while half a dozen palm-sized juveniles—too small yet to reach the sky-mirror, pale as tallow drops and wearing tiny glued hats of mica and chipped snail-tooth—skulk behind the dead copper barrels, scraping bacterial sulfur-felt out of warm joints. When two adults cross paths on a sunlit tier of crawlers, they never waste precious heat or risk tearing their insulation in a fight; a punctured hide in near-freezing water kills long before a wound can clot. Instead, they press their brass comb-hands onto the same iron carapace and strike rapid, dry counter-rhythms into the metal, slipping crisp bursts of *tik-tik-tak* precisely into the brief acoustic troughs between the forty-second drop-forges, taking stock of each other’s weight, warmth, and claim to a dying barrel before the smaller one unhooks its stilts and yields the seam.

Only the oldest one in the basin no longer climbs toward the blinding ceiling.

Down at the very bottom, wedged beneath the overlapping copper eaves right where the rusty mineral stain weeps out of the young fault line, sits an individual grown too broad and heavy with decades of accreted sulfide crust to trust its stilts to a piston-jelly’s crank. Its brass sky-reed fused shut with mineral scale years ago. Slipping slowly back down into the cold, anaerobic metabolism of its subterranean ancestors, it moves now only inches a day, warmed faintly by the iron-rich seep breathing up from the dark roots of the mountain. In the bright, cold noon sun that plunges all the way to the talus floor, you can make out just the front fan of its tarnished brass comb protruding from under the scrap-slate overhang—resting lightly against a cluster of muffled dead barrels packed with amber eggs, tapping a slow, steady pulse against the copper hulls so the unhatched brood inside can tune their growing filaments to the exact deafening pitch of the room before they ever crawl out into the light.
