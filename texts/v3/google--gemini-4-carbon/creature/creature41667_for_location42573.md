You can stand ten feet from one of them for twenty minutes and look directly at them three times before your eye separates their outline from the tangle.

It is not deliberate mimicry, not the theatrical disguise of an insect shaped like a twig. It is simply what happens to flesh over hundreds of generations when there is no UV radiation to darken it, no cold to demand fat, and no shadow anywhere to hide a sharp silhouette. Their skin has the dense, faintly waxy translucence of candle tallow or peeled lychee fruit—pale ivory over the thicker muscle of the thighs and shoulders, warming to a muted, milky rose where blood vessels run close to the surface of the throat, the inner wrists, and the ears. They are completely hairless, even at the crown and the brow. Against a backdrop of bleached amber leaves and bone-colored boughs, with white light pouring equally from above and bouncing back up through the translucent floor below, a resting body casts no grounding shadow. They simply dissolve into the brightness.

Only their eyes give them away, though rarely, because they seldom open them more than a crack. Beneath heavy, thickened lids that slant slightly downward to visor the glare, the iris is so darkly pigmented—a dense, light-drinking sepia, almost black—that it swallows the white reflection of the ceiling. They look at the world through horizontal slits, the way someone on a snowfield squints against noon, except their faces are entirely relaxed. There are no crow’s-feet of strain around their temples; the narrowing of the eye is structural, calm, permanent.

When one moves, it is with the unhurried, deliberate economy of an arboreal lizard. 

In an atmosphere hovering perpetually just above blood heat, saturated with dampness, speed is a poison. A sprint of fifty yards would raise a core temperature that the wet air refuses to cool; sweat here does not evaporate, it merely coats. And so urgency has been bred—or taught—entirely out of them. Watch a woman descend from a middle-tier avenue toward a stone-ringed cistern: she does not swing, and she never drops the last two feet. One long, lean arm reaches out, pale fingers hooking over a fused wooden strut; her weight transfers smoothly, silently, before the rear foot peels away from the pale bark with a faint, damp *kiss* of suction. Her feet are broad, flat, and heavily calloused along the outer edge and the ball, the big toe set slightly wider from the others than an outsider’s—not quite opposable, just splayed enough to pinch a thumb-thick runner or wedge securely into the crotch of a graft.

Around her hips, or slung diagonally across one bare shoulder, she wears the only textile the basin affords: flat, supple strips peeled from the inner bark of *water-wood* stolons, cured in sap until they feel like oiled chamois. These bands are not worn for warmth or modesty—concepts that require cold or strangers—but as tool-loops. Slipped snugly into woven pockets sit three or four pristine, teardrop-shaped blades of black obsidian and smoky chert, wrapped at the butt in dried leaf-parchment to spare the palm.

Everything here is an edge or a graft. Nothing is struck.

Watch one of the elders—a man whose lean, cord-muscled frame has dried slightly with age, his pale skin taking on the matte, clean texture of the dead parchment-leaves that hang forever in the canopy—working at a basalt dike near the perimeter. He does not hammer the rock. In this sealed silence, the sharp crack of stone-on-stone would travel like a gunshot, jarring the ears and sending razor-splinters flying where any deep cut invites slow, hot sepsis in the humid air, and where every spilled drop of blood is an expensive loss of salt and water. Instead, he slides the tip of a seasoned, iron-hard wooden wedge into a microscopic hairline seam in the unweathered black rock. He ties it tight with green fibrous cord, then unstoppers a small gourd-like nut shell and drips warm water onto the dry wood.

Then he leaves.

He will come back—not tomorrow, since there is no tomorrow, only *after sleeping twice* or *after the cistern fills*—and the swollen wood will have quietly pushed off a clean, ringing plate of black glass three feet long, resting safely in a cradle of woven stems he grew beneath the seam a year ago to catch it.

Time, for the basin-dwellers, is spatial and botanical rather than celestial. Without sunrises, moons, or seasons, their language—when they use it—has no words for *yesterday*, *winter*, or *hurry*. To speak of the past is to speak of wood thickness. A marriage is *two finger-widths of cambium old*. A remembered rockfall happened *when the great archway at Clear-Pool was still two separate branches*. 

Because there is no night to gather everyone simultaneously into unconsciousness, the basin never sleeps all at once, and is never wholly awake. Perhaps sixty or seventy people live across the entire four-mile expanse—the strict, steady number that the closed loop of moisture and slow-growing starch can carry without straining the root-mat—distributed in clusters of three or four pavilions within easy climbing distance. At any given moment in the motionless white blaze, a third of them are curled inside the pearly, mica-shaded cages. They sleep on bare, smooth-scraped living wood, which holds a steady, coolish sap-temperature slightly lower than the air—the closest thing to a cool sheet in their world. To make darkness, a sleeper simply pulls a broad, cured curl of opaque white dead-leaf loosely across their brow, turning their face into the crook of an elbow. 

Another might sit ten yards away on an open bough, wide awake in what an outsider’s body would scream is high noon, methodically scraping the glassy inner pith from a split tuber with a thumb-sized flake of quartz. 

Their diet is cool, wet, and entirely raw. Fire has never existed here, and could not be permitted if it were discovered; in a sealed chamber filled with tons of dry, attached parchment-foliage and resinous wood, a single runaway flame would turn the hothouse into a suffocating kiln within an hour. They eat the dense, faintly sweet starch cut cleanly from swollen stolons—always sealing the incision afterward with a smear of leaf-wax so the root does not bleed sap and scar poorly. They harvest the blind, heavy nuts before they fuse into the floor, cracking them gently between two flat stones wrapped in bark-cloth, mashing the oily white meat into a paste flavored with the sour, mineral-sharp jelly scraped from the undersides of juvenile ferns. Twice in a waking cycle, they visit a rosette pool or a stone cairn, parting the iridescent wax skin on the water’s surface with a clean finger, drinking from small, translucent cups carved out of milky selenite fallen from the high roof, then blowing softly across the pool so the wax film closes back over the water without a ripple. Nothing is wasted. Even their bodily wastes are never dropped carelessly onto the upper boughs; they descend through vertical shafts in the root-mat to deep, designated hollows twenty feet down near the bedrock, packing their leavings into slits cut in old feeder-roots, returning nitrogen and salt directly into the timber underfoot.

When two of them meet along one of the broad, crooked avenues—two pale figures pausing on bone-white wood amid the luminous yellow-green ice of the leaves—they rarely speak aloud at first. In the thick, warm air, baffled by millions of fleshy panes, a raised voice feels coarse, effortful, almost violent. Instead, they communicate across short distances through *wood-touch* and hand-cant. A knuckle tapped twice against a fused rail sends a crisp, dry vibration forty feet through the rigid, grafted lattice into the soles of the other person’s feet: *I am coming up behind you; do not startle onto a sharp edge.* 

Up close, their spoken tongue is astonishingly quiet, built almost entirely of voiceless fricatives, soft bilabial clicks, breathy whispers, and long, murmured vowels that require barely parting the lips. Standing four yards off, a conversation between three elders sounds like nothing more than condensation sliding down stone and the occasional dry *tick* of cooling bark. Their hands do half the work—slow, fluid gestures against their pale chests where the dark skin of the palms doesn't get lost in the surrounding glare.

What do you do with a mind that has too much light, enough food, no predators, and seventy years of motionless warmth?

You pay ferocious, microscopic attention to small things.

Now you see what the five melon-sized quartz crystals inside that empty pavilion are for. If you wait long enough—an hour, six hours—a young man climbs silently up through the floor-opening, his bare skin slick with the ambient sheen of the thicket. He sits cross-legged on the smooth cambium, lifts the third crystal into his lap, and picks up a palm-pad of tough, fibrous stolon-bark smeared with fine white paste: powdered selenite mixed with tree-gum. For the next two hours, breathing slow and shallow, he rubs a single natural facet of the quartz in tiny, circular strokes. 

Scattered across the middle tiers are stones that have been hand-polished by four successive generations until they are clearer than water, cut with flat, precise optical planes that catch the shadowless white glare from overhead and split it—just inside the dimness of a mica shelter—into a sharp, trembling band of violet, indigo, crimson, and deep forest green thrown against the pale wood wall. Deep, saturated color is the rarest substance in this bleached world. They farm rainbows out of rock the way surface peoples smelt gold, sitting together in absolute silence to watch a three-inch smear of pure blue rest on the back of a child’s pale hand.

And they are arborist-architects on a scale of centuries. Walk the basin with their eyes, and you realize that the entire four-mile thicket is not wilderness at all. Every broad avenue, every convenient step-branch, every living collar of sponge-tissue wrapped neatly around a sweating basalt spire was guided there. Each person carries in memory a map of several hundred active grafts begun by grandparents or intended for grandchildren. A woman pausing on her way to harvest tubers will spend ten minutes gently binding a pale young shoot against a neighbor’s limb with biodegradable leaf-twine, adjusting its angle by five degrees so that, forty sleep-cycles from now, the fused wood will form a buttress over a sharp talus fissure, or tilt a giant water-rosette two inches eastward to catch a richer drip-line from the quartz ceiling.

Even in death, they do not break the closed loop or leave debris behind.

Far toward the western wall, where a cluster of black dikes creates a quiet labyrinth of razor-edged corridors and the root-mat swells thirty feet deep over a dry tectonic cleft, the horizontal trunks grow unusually thick and smooth, swollen into long, gentle mounds. There are no cairns here, no carved markers. When someone’s breath finally thins and stops, their kin wash the body with a damp cloth—reclaiming the rinse-water for a nearby fern-bed—fold the limbs neatly against the chest, and carry them down into the lower root-vaults. There, using their sharpest obsidian blades, they part the living bark of a massive foundational stolon along a clean six-foot seam, hollow out the soft, watery pith within, lay the pale body inside the damp wooden cradle, and bind the bark tightly shut again over them.

Within a few hundred sleeps, the seam heals into a smooth, faint ridge the color of wet ash. The moisture returns to the sap; the calcium of the bones goes up into the waxy cuticles and glassy, translucent leaves overhead. Above them, the white mineral sky blazes on without flickering, pouring straight through the canopy, through the pale amber veins, lighting the living and the wood that holds the dead in the exact same calm, shadowless glow.
