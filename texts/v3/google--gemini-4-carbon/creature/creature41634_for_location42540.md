To find what actually *rules* the basin—or at least, what passes through it with something resembling intention—you have to stop looking at the carpet of bone-pistons and cast-iron backs, and start watching the gaps where the noise drops out.

Every mile or two, moving against the grain of the salt-squalls, there is a traveling pocket of muffled sound. Inside it, the deafening wet roar of ten million valves drops to a dull, underwater thudding, as if someone had thrown a heavy lead blanket over half an acre of the plain. Walking in the center of that deadened zone is a thing that looks, from a hundred yards off in the pewter mist, like a stooped, staggeringly burdened tinker wrapped in wet layers of black felt and tarnished brass chain.

Closer up, the silhouette refuses to resolve into a single anatomy. 

They are called—if there were anyone here with a throat left to name them—the Gauge-Walkers, though they are neither fully singular nor entirely distinct from the machinery they wade through. One stands roughly nine feet high at the hump, bowed forward under a towering, asymmetric shelf of living condensers, coiled gut-tubing, and whistling pressure-bladders that have grown directly out of its own scapulae. It has four legs, or rather two thick, pillar-like hind limbs shod in flared bell-bottoms of horn and bog-iron that distribute its immense weight across six square feet of writhing fauna at a time, and two narrower fore-crutches tipped with vulcanized, rubbery pads that taste voltage and test the local reality before the heavy hooves come down.

It has no face. Where a head ought to hang beneath the shelf of its shoulders, there is a swinging brass-and-cartilage turret, heavily baffled with flaps of greasy grey hide against the freezing brine-rain. Set into this turret are seven or eight apertures of varying bores, none of them eyes. Vision in the pewter gloom, where light smears and duplicates herds of striders, is worse than useless; it is a trap. Instead, the Walker reads the basin through pressure differentials, acoustic return, and stray induction. Two of its forward apertures are flared acoustic horns lined with stiff, copper-sheathed hairs that twitch constantly, mapping the exact pitch of every chuffing exhaust and spinning flywheel within three hundred yards. Beneath them hangs a prehensile bundle of wet, pale nerve-cables wrapped in flexible lead-mesh sleeves, constantly uncoiling to brush the shells underfoot, taking the temperature and temporal coherence of the pavement.

Everything about a Walker is built to survive a world that has forgotten which way time and torque are supposed to point. 

Watch how it crosses a patch of lathe-water. A lesser crawler-engine blunders into the flat, dark pool and is instantly spun into a screaming knot of snapped tendons and sheared bolts by the disembodied rotational force. The Walker stops at the rim. Its acoustic horns swivel down; a secondary bladder high on its back gives three sharp, percussive *clacks* like a heavy circuit breaker tripping. From its underbelly, it lowers a spinning, football-sized flywheel of dense magnetite on a muscular, tendon-wrapped shaft. It spins the wheel counter-clockwise at four thousand revolutions per minute and dips just the rim into the dead-flat pool. The invisible torque of the water bites into the counter-spinning wheel; there is a shriek of tortured physics, a spray of sparks that smell of cold garlic and hot pennies, and for six seconds the disembodied force in the puddle is wound backward like an overwound watchspring until it snaps and goes slack. The water ripples once, remembering it is just water, and the Walker steps heavily across, its broad iron-rimmed hooves crushing three gear-parasites into black tallow without breaking stride.

They are cold-blooded in a way terrestrial biology cannot comprehend. Ordinary flesh freezes here; ordinary engines seize unless they burn furious fuel. A Walker’s blood is a eutectic brine laced with heavy metallic salts and natural glycols, circulating at twelve degrees below zero, pumped not by a beating heart—which would burst under the erratic pressure-spikes of the plain—but by three continuous Archimedes screws of polished bone turning smoothly inside its chest cavity. It does not breathe air for oxygen. It inhales the bruised-pewter mist to condense the suspended mineral oils and sulfur compounds across gill-racks of folded zinc-flesh inside its flanks, and exhales a heavy, syrupy sludge that drips down its legs, lubricating its own joints against the abrasive metallic silt.

What makes the Walkers terrifying—more than their bulk, more than the dull, cherry-red glow visible deep inside the maintenance slits of their ribs—is that they are *curators* of a blind, wild thing, without having a drop of mercy or design in them. They do not build, yet they cannot stop adjusting.

A Walker will trudge for six hours through the slush-drizzle, ignoring the blinding blue arc-flashes of the welding scavengers, ignoring the twelve-fold ghost-herds clanging past its flanks, listening for a specific kind of wrongness in the underlying thrum of the gneiss. When it finds it—say, an ancient turbine-reef where five hundred sessile drum-beasts have accidentally synchronized their exhaust strokes, setting up a resonant harmonic that is turning fifty yards of solid granite into tarry quicksand and threatening to swallow the whole reef into the earth—the Walker stops.

It lowers its heavy fore-crutches into the boiling slush. Out from beneath the wet felt-like mantles of its belly unfold its true hands: four terrifyingly delicate, six-jointed manipulators of bare white bone, vanadium steel, and cold red muscle, ending not in fingers, but in adjustable jaws, calipers, sodering-spines, and valve-keys. 

With the slow, trembling precision of a surgeon working in the dark, the Walker reaches down into the packed, roaring mat of bodies. The sessile drum-beasts do not fight it; nothing without eyes knows it is there until the bone-wrenches bite. The Walker does not kill the reef. It selects every third organism, clamps its vanadium jaws onto the flanged keratin exhaust-horn, and *twists*—crimping the aperture by four millimeters, altering the back-pressure, retuning the beast’s deafening *chuff* down by half a flat. If a parasitic gear-train gets in the way, the Walker plucks it off like a tick, cracks its shell against its own iron knee, and drops the shivering rotor into a dorsal intake-funnel between its shoulders, where internal grinders reduce it to raw iron and grease in seconds. 

Sometimes it grafts. That is the strangest thing to witness in the grease-gray dusk.

A Walker will carry things tucked into the warm, oily folds beneath its condenser-shelf—things it pried loose three miles back. A severed boiler-worm still venting Orphaned steam four hundred yards away; a basketball-sized bladder of pressurized brine; a cluster of those clicking, copper-tongued hover-flies pinned alive through their ganglions onto a strip of gristle. Kneeling in the freezing slush while syrupy rain hisses off its hot dorsal vents, the Walker will pry open the iron casing of a dying, half-smelted crawler, shove the living brine-bladder directly into the ruined gearbox, and strike a controlled, low-amperage arc from its own lead-sheathed nerve-cables to fuse the two incompatible anatomies together. 

Why? Not out of kindness, and not to build a tool it will ever return to use. The spliced thing usually staggers off sideways, leaking steam and clicking erratically, a miserable, lopsided chimera that will last forty-eight hours before the bowling-ball scavengers smelt it down. The Walker does it the way a beaver piles mud on a stick when it hears running water. The sound of a leaking, inefficient gradient—heat going to waste without doing work, torque spinning loose in a puddle without turning a shaft, cause drifting three seconds ahead of effect—itches inside the Walker’s acoustic horns like a splinter in an ear canal. It *must* close the circuit. It must put a load on the loose force.

In a very real sense, they are the immune system of the basin’s broken physics, stitching torn causality back onto meat and iron so the plain doesn't unravel altogether.

They are solitary until the salt-squalls freeze low enough to drop the ambient voltage, and then two or three will sometimes converge over a deep thermal fissure where the bedrock runs hot and soft like warm asphalt. Their meetings look like slow-motion train wrecks conducted in polite silence. They press their baffled brass turrets together, sealing the wet leather flaps edge-to-edge to keep the brine out, and open internal vents. For three hours they stand locked in a tripod of twelve iron-shod legs while the gale whips vertical panes of razor-ice around their ankles, trading internal pressures—equalizing their bone-screws, pumping gallons of warm, memory-rich electrolyte sludge from one hull into the next, swapping half-digested schematics of where the lathe-pools have migrated and which miles of the plain have gone temporally sour.

And clinging to the Walkers themselves—because nothing in this twenty-mile saucer goes uncolonized—is an entire vertical slum of tiny specialists that exist nowhere else.

Tucked into the warm, vibrating creases behind the Walker’s knees live pale, blind, thumb-sized crabs made of porcelain and copper wire. They scurry constantly up and down the nine-foot expanse of the creature's hide, scraping away the jagged, misfiring panes of vertical frost before the anomalous ice can slice into a pressurized hose, eating the black sulfur-slime out of its hinge-joints, and tapping tiny, hair-thin needles into its dorsal condensers to sip a drop of distilled oil. Higher up, nested right among the whistling brass pipes of the Walker's shoulder-hump, sit fist-sized lumps of grey sponge threaded with silver filaments. They have no mouths, no legs, no guts at all; they are pure biological capacitors. Whenever the Walker steps on an arc-scavenger or takes a stray bolt of pre-echoed lightning across its back, these silver sponges drink the lethal surge before it can boil the Walker’s nerve-cables, glowing a sudden, fierce violet under the mist and humming like struck tuning forks for twenty minutes afterward while they bleed the stolen charge slowly down into the Walker’s chest-screws.

If a Walker ever dies, nobody has seen the corpse. 

The theory—if you watched one old, listing giant whose hind-hoof had cracked and whose muffler-field was stuttering, letting blasts of raw industrial roar leak into its private quiet—is that they don't fall down. When the bone-screws finally wear thin and the internal brine cools toward seizure, the Walker simply stops lifting its feet. It settles its massive, flared hooves into a gap between two turbine-reefs. Its black iron-sulfide hairs grow down out of its fetlocks and root into the vibrating gneiss. Its delicate vanadium hands fold away forever behind plates of thickening horn; its acoustic turret fuses shut against the rain; its dorsal condensers flare wider, catching the syrupy mist. Within a year, fifty flattened gear-parasites have pressed their toothed rims into its trembling flanks to steal the turn of its chest-screws, and a flock of blade-winged hoverers hangs suspended in the broken inertia above its back, dipping copper tongues into its vents.

It doesn't die at all. It just gives up walking, and joins the pavement.
