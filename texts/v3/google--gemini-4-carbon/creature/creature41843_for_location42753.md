You notice their hands before their faces, and their posture before their hands. 

A person raised on the flat prairie above walks by throwing their weight forward and letting the ground catch it, heels striking first. Down here, nobody strikes the earth. Even the heaviest stone-porters move with a slight, habitual bend at the knee, setting the ball of the foot down first inside thick soles of coiled rush or Boiled-and-beaten felt, testing the slate before committing their center of gravity. They move up and down the stepped alleys and roof-ladders with the fluid, unhurried economy of mountain goats, though their bodies are built less for leaping than for leverage: broad across the collarbones, thick in the wrists and calves, with the slightly rounded shoulders of people who spend half their waking lives looking precisely where their feet are going and the other half leaning into counterweights.

Their skin carries the particular, matte pallor of the basin. Even on clear days when the upland sun pours straight into the trough, the air is full of drifting stone-flour—a fine, impalpable dust of ground greywacke and unslaked lime that settles into the creases of knuckles, dusts eyelashes pale grey, and gives dark hair the muted sheen of pewter. Around their wrists, knees, and throats, their clothing is invariably slick and dark with mutton-tallow. Tallow is the grease that keeps the city silent, and everyone carries a horn or wooden pot of it hooked to their belt; a crane-tender or water-warden will pause mid-sentence to thumb a smear of fat onto a dry hinge or a rubbing rope-guide without even looking at what their fingers are doing. Their palms are mapped with blue-black splinters of slate healed cleanly under tough calluses, and the skin of the lime-burners and gutter-masons is cracked and cured like alum-tanned hide, constantly treated with rendered fat and vinegar to neutralize the caustic bite of the mortar.

Clothing is built for friction and damp cold. Because the air pooled at the bottom of the graben never truly warms, people dress in dense, layered woolens—unbleached greys, lanolin-yellows, and dark sheep-browns brought down in bales from the rim-herders—cut close to the ribs so nothing snags on a protruding stone prism or gets drawn into a pulley block. Over the wool, nearly everyone wears a leather *scarp-apron*, worn either down the front by sawyers and kiln-tenders, or strapped backward over the shoulders and hips by the stair-porters who carry sixty-pound slate slabs cinched to wooden pack-frames. Jewelry does not dangle or clink; there are no chains of coins, no glass beads to shatter on a stone stair. Ornaments are single, heavy things: thumb-rings carved of green serpentine, or flat pins of polished iron pyrite—fool’s gold pried straight out of the fresh cliff seams—worn at the collarbone to catch the steep midday light before the mineral has had years enough to tarnish.

To listen to them speak is to hear a language actively losing its sharp edges. 

Only the graybeards—the first generation who stood on the prairie grass as children, heard the deep, sickening *thrum* in their teeth when the crust dropped away, and climbed down into the raw pit while the dust was still hanging in the air—still possess the flat, carrying voices of plains-folk, and even they keep them damped down as if speaking in a sickroom. Among the younger generations born inside the honeycomb, shouting isn't merely bad manners; it is felt in the gut as a physical obscenity, like throwing a rock at a brother’s head. Their speech has dropped into the chest, rich in sibilants, hums, and soft labial stops that ride the heavy, damp air across two rooftops and die cleanly before hitting the cliff wall. 

Across longer distances—from a crane-platform high on the western greywacke down to a stone-yard three hundred feet below—they do not use their voices at all. If you watch the rooftops, you see conversations flashing silently back and forth: two fingers tapped against the collarbone, a flat palm tilted to show the angle of a bedding plane, a twist of the wrist indicating a slackening stay-rope. Every child of six knows two hundred crane-and-mason signs. When a fresh pocket of loose shale shifts high on the boundary scarp, no alarm bell rings—there is not a single bell or gong in the entire two-mile basin. Instead, a wave of silent posture sweeps across the terraces: thousands of pairs of hands rising simultaneously, palms flat and stillness absolute, while every sawyer stops drawing copper through limestone and every wooden axle comes to rest, leaving five thousand people listening together in dead quiet to hear whether the rock above has finished speaking.

Their whole civic order hangs on that listening. There are no mayors, landlords, or hereditary chiefs down here; nobody owns a plot of ground when the ground itself is still negotiating its final angle of repose, and nobody owns a wall when your neighbor’s kitchen hearth is the buttress keeping your bedroom from sliding into a cistern. Authority belongs instead to the *Plumb-Wardens* and the *Seam-Readers*.

You see them making their rounds at dawn, while the fog-frames are still dripping hard into the rooftop vats. They are quiet, squint-eyed men and women who wear soft rush sandals thin enough to feel the tilt of a floor-slab through their soles. In leather cases at their hips they carry the city’s most sacred instruments: lead-weighted silk plumb-lines, brass calipers, and shallow wooden bowls brimming with dark lamp-oil, which they set on marked ledges to read the microscopic tremble of the bedrock in the oil’s reflection. Throughout the day they inspect the *telltales*—thousands of tiny, bone-dry splinters of pine cedar wedged tightly across hairline fractures in the cliff faces and cellar boulders, sometimes cemented at each end with a dab of brittle rosin. If the fault creeps by the thickness of a fingernail during the night, the rosin snaps or the splinter bows; if the gap widens, the splinter drops silently onto a square of white wool laid beneath it. 

When a Seam-Reader finds three fallen splinters along the same fault-trace, they simply chalk a slant-mark on the lintels of the houses above. Nobody argues. Within an hour, neighbors arrive with tallowed pry-bars and greased timber jacks. Families lift their plank-beds and leek-troughs onto the next roof over, unstack half a dry-stone parlor wall, re-wedge the foundation prisms to match the earth’s new mood, and rebuild the room four inches narrower before supper. Architecture here is treated less like dead monument and more like rigging on a ship under sail—constantly slacked, hauled taut, and re-lashed as the hull works.

Because they have no soil to bury their dead and cannot spare the charcoal required for true cremation—every stick of pine and birch must come down the switchbacks on a human back, reserved strictly for smelting copper, firing water-pipe clay, and burning limestone into quicklime—even death is folded back into the stone-work. Wait long enough in one of the deep, cold under-vaults where the house foundations straddle raw angular talus, and you will pass a dry-walled niche sealed with pale, airtight lime plaster. Inside, packed in spent, dry rock-flour and quicklime dust that drinks away all moisture without smell or rot, the dead desiccate swiftly in the cool air until they are light as dry reeds, becoming permanent structural fill inside the honeycomb their hands stacked.

Even the animals have been shaped by the rule of quiet. The roof-dogs—wiry, low-slung terriers with oversized ears and thick ruff-fur around their necks—have been bred ruthlessly over forty years for muteness. A pup that yips at a shadow is carried up the cliff-stairs in a basket and traded to the prairie shepherds before it is weaned. Those that stay hunt rock-mice along the slate gutters in eerie silence, communicating with their handlers entirely through ear-twitches and the tap of a tail against a bootleg, leaping eight-foot gaps between alley-eaves with a soft *whuff* of breath and four padded feet landing like dropped mittens. Beneath the floorboards, nobody sweeps away the wide, horizontal sheet-webs of the cellar spiders; a torn web between two foundation boulders is another kind of telltale, showing that the stones shifted in the night, and the spiders keep down the cistern-midges besides.

At dusk, the sun vanishes abruptly behind the sharp western rim—no lingering twilight on a horizon, just a clean shadow-line racing across the roofs in minutes and plunging the whole trough into cool, blue-grey shade while the sky overhead is still bright gold. Work stops before lamps are needed, to save tallow. People climb up out of the damp lower tiers onto the highest slate terraces, pulling thick felted shawls over their shoulders. They sit along the edges of the barley-boxes and fog-frames, eating bowls of warm barley-mash thickened with mutton fat and sharp green leek-tops, passing small clay cups of pine-needle tea brewed with mineral-heavy aquifer water that tastes faintly of iron and cold chalk. 

Up there, with the faint warmth of five hundred banked lime-kilns rising through the soles of their rush shoes and thin ribbons of blue smoke standing straight up around them like pillars holding up the evening sky, a low murmur rises—thousands of soft voices talking two yards apart, interspersed with the rhythmic *click-slide* of bone knitting-needles working raw prairie wool, the steady *drip-drop* of weeping aquifers into slate troughs, and, very occasionally, deep under everyone’s heels, a single, muffled, subterranean *thump* as the young earth settles another hair’s breadth into its bed, followed by a brief pause in the talk, a glance at a dish of lamp-oil sitting level on a coping stone, a nod, and the quiet resumption of supper.
