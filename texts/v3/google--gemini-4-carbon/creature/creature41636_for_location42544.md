At first glance, the gray paste wedged between the shards of traprock looks merely damp, a dull mortar of pulverized basalt and mineral-heavy condensation. If you held a light to it and waited—though no one ever has—you still wouldn’t see movement. Yet the paste is terraced. Across a span of four inches, in the angular trough between two blast-fragments, the wet rock flour is arranged in microscopic stepped basins, like travertine pools at the lip of a hot spring, though nothing here flows fast enough to carve them.

What lives in the paste has no discrete body to count. It is a skin of lithotrophic slime, dense and heavy as cold tallow, bridging the gap between iron-eating bacteria and something with the slow, hydraulic ambition of a plasmodium. It came in, perhaps, as a dormant spore packed into the bearing grease of the overhead trolley, or rode down on the drill steel before the final charge was set, and woke in the forty-eight-degree dark to find a world of raw, unoxidized stone surfaces and weeping metal.

It feeds on the crispness of the blast. Freshly fractured basalt is chemically restless; its cleaved crystal lattices expose reduced iron and magnesium that have never met oxygen or water. The gray film spreads over a razor-sharp stone edge, transparent at its margins and dull pewter where it thickens, and digests the raw mineral face atom by atom. Where it has lingered for a year, the glassy fracture of the traprock loses its sheen, turning matte and slightly greasy to the touch, the knife-edge dulled by the thickness of a human hair.

For a long time, that is all there is: the blind, giant flora drinking the infrared above, and this gray, mineral-licking film creeping through the wet dust below. They do not compete. When a three-meter frond finally drops from the gantry and cures into black parchment across the talus, the gray film does not eat it—it has no enzymes for cellulose or waxy cuticles. Instead, it uses the fallen leaf as a roof. Protected from evaporation beneath the heavy, leathery tent, the film swells.

Here, under the black arches of cured leaves, the inhabitant gathers enough mass to change its mind about what it is.

Lift one of those stiff, dead fronds—it would crack like dry veneer in your hands—and you would expose a knot of things that look like peeled shallots or pale, blind thumbs, clustered in the wet hollow beneath. They are turgid sacs of translucent, rubbery membrane, each the size of a plum, anchored into the gray paste by tough, hair-thin black rhizoids that pry into the micro-fractures of the basalt. Inside each sac, suspended in a clear, viscous brine, sits a single dark grain of precipitated magnetite or copper salt, scavenged from the sweat of the busbars and exchangers.

They are thermal blisters, organs of storage and rudimentary sense, though they belong to no single nervous system. If the load in the distant grid shifts and the nearest olive-drab exchanger rises half a degree in temperature, the fluid inside the sacs expands microscopically; the dark metallic grain within settles slightly lower in its bath, pressing against the bottom membrane. Over weeks, the gray sheet responds to that pressure by withdrawing its substance from the scorching base of the exchanger and pumping it—through flattened, vein-like tubes no wider than pencil lead that run along the shadowed undersides of the rubble—toward cooler puddles three meters away.

Where the golden, greasy threads of pollen hang down from the cabbage-sized flowers above and smear across the rocks, a stranger negotiation takes place. The plant’s pollen is rich in lipids and heavy sugars forged from heat-chemistry, meant to germinate on wet stone. When a thread finally touches down into a patch of the pewter slime, it does not rot, and it is not digested. Instead, the slime sleeves it. A translucent cuff creeps upward along the hanging grease-thread, sometimes climbing half a meter into the black air before gravity and dryness halt it, turning the slack golden strand into a stiff, beaded stalagmite. Inside that sleeve, the pollen grains still try to germinate, pushing out pale, blind root-hairs that never reach the stone; the slime drinks the sugars leaking from their effort and, in trade, pipes up droplets of mineral-rich water from the dinner-plate puddles below, keeping the suspended pollen alive in midair for months as a captive sugar-gland.

And higher up, there is the second phase of its life—or perhaps a separate creature entirely that has merely learned to farm the first.

Follow one of the stout, hose-thick vines up a steel I-beam toward the vault’s crown, past the bruised-plum leaves, to the place where the braided power cable of the inspection trolley hangs in its deep catenary loop. The trolley moves too slowly to shake anything loose; a quarter-turn of its grease-packed wheel takes an hour. Built directly onto the rusted cheek of the trolley’s housing, riding its glacial transit from north to south across the cathedral dark, is a structure that looks like a wasp’s nest made of soldered lead and black wax.

It is the size of a loaf of bread, asymmetrically fluted, its flutes pointing downward toward the distant heat-exchangers. Nothing flies in or out of it. There are no holes. If you pressed a thumb against its flank, it would give slightly, warm and firm as vulcanized rubber, and leave a faint smear of graphite-colored oil on your skin.

Inside that dark sponge, compartmentalized into thousands of hexagonal cells no larger than pinheads, lives the chamber’s only true motile thing—though "motile" here must be measured against the clock of the inspection trolley. They are flat, eyeless lozenges of pale cartilage-like tissue, thin as fingernail clippings and destitute of legs, jaws, or shells. They move by warping their own edges in slow, peristaltic ripples, gliding over a slick internal film of condensed machine oil and water.

They are thermal-gradient grazers, creatures that have decoupled themselves entirely from the speed of muscle and nerve. A single ripple along a lozenge’s margin takes four minutes to complete. They emerge from tiny slits at the base of the lead-gray nest only when the trolley passes directly above a curtain of gantry-vines—an intersection that happens for six days at a time, twice a year, before the geared wheels inch the housing back out over the twenty-meter abyss.

During those six days, the pale lozenges slide down the trolley’s suspension hanger and out onto the braided power cable, riding the warm belly of the cord where the condensation gathers. They do not chew the immaculate leaves below; they have nothing to chew with. Instead, five or six of them will gather at the lowest curve of the cable, arranging their flat, fingernail bodies into a shallow cup around a swelling bead of water. They drink the dissolved iron from the rusted rail, the microscopic aerosols of hydrocarbon breathed off by the blistered olive-drab paint forty-eight degrees below, and the faint stray mist of organic acids exhaled by the giant flowers.

When the drop grows too heavy even for their cupped bodies to hold, they part—taking twenty minutes to peel their edges back—and let it fall.

Sometimes one fails to let go in time. It rides the detaching bead twenty meters down through the breathless black, tumbling without panic because it has nothing fast enough to register falling, and strikes a broad, plum-colored leaf below with that solitary, cavern-filling *thock*.

The splash scatters the drop into smaller beads across the lacquered surface of the frond. The pale lozenge lies flat where it landed, stunned not at all, adhering instantly by wet tension to the warm leather of the plant. Over the next week, imperceptibly, it will glide down the three-meter pleat of the leaf, avoiding the scorching copper busbars by the heat-ache in its blind cells, until it reaches the sharp traprock at the base.

There, in the velvet black, only inches from an amber pilot bulb that has burned without blinking since the chamber was sealed, it slides off the plant and onto the gritty gray paste. It does not crawl away. Over forty days, its pale, fingernail edges grow indistinct, softening into pewter slime, trading its slow travel back into the stationary memory of the floor, while twenty meters overhead, the trolley wheel finishes another revolution in the dark.
