Because sound dies at twelve feet, everything here lives inside a tiny, mobile sphere of intimacy surrounded by an ocean of dead cinema. 

Watch a fern-choked ravine from twenty paces off and you see only pantomime: dark maroon fronds thrashing in violent, silent hysteria, a pack of low, wide-shouldered predators tearing into a segmented carcass with all the acoustic weight of smoke, six-ton basalt shafts slamming home with no more report than a turned page. Take four steps closer—cross that invisible threshold where the air briefly remembers how to carry a wave—and the noise hits your skull like a swung shovel. Suddenly the air is thick with wet, bronchial roaring, the sharp *snick-snick-snick* of stamped brass mandibles, the high hydraulic hiss of vented lung-steam, and the heavy, rhythmic *clack* of bone ratchets locking into place. Step back two paces, and the lid slams shut again.

Life on the plateau has organized itself entirely around that suffocating horizon, and around a single metabolic truth: the sun is not above them; it is beneath their feet.

Ten percent of daylight filtering through graphite cloud could never feed biomass at this feverish, suffocating density. The black and oiled-bronze canopies drink what dimness they can, yes, their leaves broad and greedy, spread like slick solar collectors—but their real hunger is downward. Dig a blade into one of the hydraulic root-clamps gripping a reciprocating shaft of granite and you find tissues packed with stacked crystalline plates of biological quartz and tourmaline. They are piezoelectric. Every time the planet shudders, every time a basalt column strokes upward or a fault line grinds sideways, the roots convert raw crushing mechanical stress directly into chemical energy. The whole forest eats friction.

And underneath the roots, wedged directly into the pinch-points where rock shears against rock, lives a basal ecology of things that *want* to be crushed.

Look into the weeping, grease-blackened tolerances between pumping stone columns and you will find flat, leathery, unsegmented pads of flesh the size of dinner plates, gray-pink and slick with self-secreted molybdenum slime. They have no eyes, no mouths, no guts. They slide themselves deliberately under descending six-ton blocks of stone, take the full catastrophic удар of the stroke across rubberized, fluid-filled lattices of gristle, and shudder with metabolic ecstasy as the shockwave drives ATP synthesis across every cell in their bodies, before oozing two inches left to catch the adjacent piston’s drop. Around their margins swarm finger-length, copper-toothed scrapers that graze on the dead, vulcanized rind sheared off the pads, and those scrapers in turn are vacuumed up by whip-tailed amphibians whose skin glistens with natural insulating varnish, allowing them to leap straight through sheets of violet dynamo-static without stopping their hearts.

Where physics stutters, appetite adapts with terrifying casualness.

Approach one of the looping acres—where the crust heaves up, shatters into razor plates, vanishes with a soft atmospheric *pop*, and resets every four seconds—and you find its perimeter lined like a watering hole. Nothing enters the boundary entirely; anything wholly inside when the reset hits is simply un-written back to its state four seconds prior. Instead, heavy, crab-bodied browsers squat just outside the fault line on splayed legs shod in soft ironstone grit. Every time the acre re-extrudes, a stand of swollen, castor-scented fern shoots appears three feet inside the zone. Quick as piston-strikes, twenty muscular, yard-long tongues shoot across the boundary, rip the fronds free, and haul them back into continuous space. Matter dragged out of a stutter doesn’t stay solid—four seconds later it still vanishes from the browser’s stomach with a muffled, wet *thump* that makes the animal’s flanks cave inward—so their digestive tracts have become blast-furnaces of concentrated acid and symbiotic sparks, stripping every spare calorie and free electron out of the phantom pulp in three point eight seconds flat before the meal ceases to have ever been eaten. They live in a state of roaring, perpetual starvation and gluttony at once, metabolizing ghosts, venting plumes of sour white steam from spiracles along their backs.

Predators use the loose seams of space the way spiders elsewhere use trapdoors. What looks like a harmless, fist-wide crease in the air beside a warm puddle of condensation will suddenly extrude a single, yard-long forelimb tipped with hooked hornblende claws—the rest of the animal crouching patiently twelve yards upslope behind an outcropping of zinc fins, poking only its strike-arm and a stalked, heat-sensing nostril through a local tear in geometry. Even the small brass-shelled flyers lay their clutches in spatial pockets that only open when the magnetic reefs hit a specific rotational harmonic, hiding their soft larvae inside creases of nowhere until the shells harden.

Out on the vast terraced tablelands, where whole miles of crust ripple steadily toward subterranean furnaces like conveyor belts, stillness equals incineration. To sleep on the rock is to be carried into a white-hot trench by morning. 

Here live the largest solitary bodies on the continent: the counter-walkers. From a ridge, you might mistake one for a stable knoll of weathered slate—the *only* weathered stone in sight—draped in luxuriant, undisturbed gardens of bronze moss and flowering static-ferns. Look lower, beneath the overhanging eaves of its carapace, and you see forty pairs of pillar-thick legs arranged in staggered centipede waves, stepping endlessly, unhurriedly forward at the exact inverse speed of the sliding rock beneath them. Some are two hundred feet long, flat as river barges, built of laminated bone, dense tallow-cushioning, and overlapping plates of precipitated zinc that shed heat in shimmering curtains. They never stop walking. From the hour they drop wet and soft from their mothers’ undersides onto the moving stone, their nervous systems lock onto the magnetic north of the deep dynamos and pace against the conveyor. They graze the fast-uncoiling canopies that erupt ahead of them, digesting iron-sulfide bark in guts that rumble like ball mills, while entire passenger ecosystems—flightless brass beetles, nesting colonies of six-limbed climbers, miniature velvet-skinned ambush cats—live fifty-year lifespans on the broad plateaus of their backs, the one place in three thousand miles where an egg can be laid down and still be there an hour later. When an old counter-walker finally throws a joint or strokes out after a century of marching, the plateau claims it within twenty minutes: the sliding rock carries the mountain of meat and metal backward to the lip of a furnace fissure, tips it slowly over the edge in dead silence, and flashes ten tons of tallow into an updraft of greasy golden cloud that feeds the low ceiling overhead.

And threading through all of this—clambering along the mooring-thick vines, chimnying up warm basalt flues, crossing the backs of the counter-walkers like fleeting maintenance crews—are the ones who remember.

You will never find a hut of theirs, nor a chipped flint—why knap stone when every fresh talus slope deals out ten thousand surgical obsidian razors a minute, ready to be pinched up, used for six cuts, and dropped back into the churning scree? You will not find a hearth, a cairn, a blazed tree, or a buried bone. Yet if you wait near a spinning copper reef where the violet static sheds steady warmth and keeps the larger flickering wolves wary, you may see what looks like a knot of dark, oil-slicked burls detach itself from the underside of an ironstone arch and drop twenty feet to the wet slate.

They land in absolute silence outside the twelve-foot mark, absorbing the drop across four heavy lower limbs before rising onto their hind pairs. 

Evolutionarily, they belong to the same deep six-limbed stock as the pale, eyeless climbers racing over the copper veins above, though these are neither pale nor eyeless. Fully stretched, one stands slightly taller than a tall man, slung low in the pelvis, covered in tight, matte-charcoal hide pebbled with grit-resistant keratin. Their lowest pair of limbs are thick, digitigrade pistons ending in wide, split hooves of self-vulcanizing black horn, cross-hatched underneath like wet-weather tires to bite slick obsidian without sliding; when they walk across friction-hot rock, their soles leave faint, acrid wisps of blue rubber-smoke. The middle limbs are heavy bracers—longer, muscled like anchor chains, used for knuckle-vaulting across opening chasms or wedging into rock chimneys when an earthquake hits, fitted at the elbows and wrists with biological cam-joints that can mechanically lock into place without burning muscle energy, letting them hang asleep over a bottomless fissure all night. Tucked above those, emerging beneath wide collarbones, are a pair of slender, startlingly delicate true arms with two opposing thumbs on each seven-fingered hand, the fingertips calloused in conductive native copper precipitated straight out of their sweat.

Because the boiler-room air would drown an ordinary lung in minutes, their ribcages are fluted with vertical rows of muscular, gill-like condenser baffles. They inhale through wide slits behind the jaw; the baffles wring the hot vapor into liquid water before the air ever reaches their lungs, spilling a continuous, warm rivulet down channels in their sternums to flush grit from their skin and cool their blood. Their faces are broad, flat, and mobile, dominated by four eyes arranged in a shallow arc: a small outer pair filmed with dark oil to cut the blinding glare of close-range electrical arcs, and a large, liquid, amber-backed inner pair tuned deep into the thermal infrared. They do not wait for rock to shatter to know where a fault lies. To their inner eyes, a cliffside three seconds away from shearing upward blooms a bright, warning fever-orange from friction deep inside the stone, giving them time to kick off into the vines before the crag turns into a stamp mill.

Step inside twelve feet of them—if the perimeter lookouts let you—and you realize why they travel packed shoulder to hip in tangles of eight to fifteen individuals, forever brushing knuckles, leaning ribs together, draping middle arms across one another’s backs.

Outside that twelve-foot sphere, a single individual is amputated from mind itself. Out there in the hush, there is no warning cry, no name, no shared past. Inside the huddle, they are deafening.

To step into the acoustic bubble of a band is to step into a continuous, polyphonic engine-room of voice. They never shut up. Even while sleeping hung from iron-sulfide cables, three or four of the huddle keep up a low, rhythmic, chest-resonating *thrum-clack-hiss*, passing cadences back and forth from throat to throat. Because nothing can be written down—no mark scratched on slate survives the afternoon, no notched stick outlasts the week—their entire material culture, their maps of shifting fault-harmonics, their genealogies, their taxonomies of poisonous tallow-seams, and whatever strange philosophies grow in a world without permanence exist solely as standing waves of sound bounced continuously inside that tiny pocket of functional air. A memory that stops being vocalized by the band for ten minutes is as gone as a forest tipped into a furnace trench. And so a band is less a tribe than a single circulating lung of memory: elders with scarred condenser-flutes rasp out long, percussive baselines of regional rhythm—*how many strokes the hexagonal shafts make before the shale vents steam, how blue the static turns before an acre loops*—while juveniles weave rapid, clicking improvisations over the top with plated teeth, testing new terrain against the old song.

When they must scatter—to hunt tallow-worms in narrow clefts, or cross a brittle hornblende bridge one body at a time—they do not willingly go mute. From muscular spools tucked behind their collarbones, they draw out thin, wet, tendon-tough cords threaded with living copper-sheathed nerves, hooking thebone-barbed tips into matching sockets on their companions' shoulders. Strung out up to sixty yards through the thrashing dark ferns like mountaineers on dark wire, they go silent to the air, yet talk frantically through the lines via crisp electrical twitches and muscular tugs—a tactile telegraphy of *jump now, rock hot beneath left hoof, flicker-wolf three yards upslope, hold fast*. If a shearing fault snaps a cord, the severed individual freezes for half a heartbeat in the sudden, terrible hush, reels the bleeding filament back into its chest-spool before static from the ground can fry its nervous system, and sprints blindly back toward the thermal glow of its kin, starving to hear the song before it forgets its own place in the measure.

They build nothing, tame nothing, clear nothing. Yet they are masters of the *temporary coupling*.

Watch a band come upon a freshly extruded seam of sulfur-rich tallow-clay trapped beneath a jammed, straining shelf of slate that hasn't quite sheared free. They do not pry at it with levers. Instead, two of the heaviest adults wedge their cam-locked middle limbs between the jammed shelf and an adjacent, rhythmically pumping basalt piston, bracing their vulcanized hooves against the stone, using their own dense, grease-cushioned skeletons as temporary connecting rods. On the next up-stroke of the basalt shaft, six tons of blind planetary force route straight through their locked joints—their quartz-laced bones briefly glowing dull violet under the skin with piezoelectric discharge—and pop the slate shelf cleanly off its hang-up. Before the cleft can snap shut, the younger ones dive in, scooping hot, buttery gobs of mineral tallow into foldable, oil-proof pouches of shed climber-skin slung under their armpits, slapping gobs directly into one another's mouths, before a thermal flare in the stone beneath warns them out. Two seconds later the cleft slams together hard enough to powder granite, and the band is already twenty feet up a trembling zinc spire, licking grease from their copper-plated thumbs, singing the cadence of that particular seam into the group-voice in case the crust repeats it ten miles east next month.

Farther in toward the sunken basins—where that miles-wide cyclone of warm mist and airborne iron filings turns lazily over tearing, muscular crust—even the huddle-bands rarely venture, though other things live in the funnel itself. Look up into the rotating wall of suspended iron dust when sheet lightning illuminates it from within, and you can make out vast, translucent, ray-like membranes half a mile across, stitched together by branching ribs of magnetized cartilage. They never touch the jagged plateau at all. Riding the perpetual thermal updrafts of the torn crust, they flex their electromagnetic skeletons to herd clouds of airborne iron filings across their wet dorsal skins, harvesting the induced electrical current of the spinning metal storm, drifting in slow, luminous spirals just beneath the graphite cloud-deck. When they die, they tear into fluttering ribbons of tough, rubbery parchment that snow down miles away onto the black canopies—immediately swarmed by brass beetles, dissolved by castor-sap vines, or snatched up by a passing huddle-band to wrap a newborn tightly against a mother’s warm sternum-rivulet until its hooves vulcanize.

Stand on that razor-crest in the murky dusk and let your eyes adjust to the orange rock-blush and snapping blue static. Nowhere in the thousands of miles before you is there a straight line, a laid stone, a wheel on an axle, or a thought made permanent in matter. The mountain beneath your boots will be talus by midnight and molten slurry by noon tomorrow. Yet every wet cleft, every greased shaft, every four-second misfire of time, and every ten-foot bubble of air moving through the dark leaves is packed to bursting with hot, flexing, watchful vitality—greasing the strokes, riding the pistons, singing furiously to itself in the tiny spaces where voices work, wholly at home inside an engine that manufactures nothing at all except the next violent, glistening second.
