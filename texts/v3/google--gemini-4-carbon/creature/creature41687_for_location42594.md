You think at first that the chamber is entirely automated, and for the first twenty minutes, standing on the grated landing of Tier Three with your shirt plastered to your ribs, you are right. Nothing moves except the counterweights and the trembling needles. 

Then you notice a brass sight-glass four yards away. Half a minute ago, its thick crystal tube was clouded on the outside with pale mineral scale and grease-film. Now it is clean—wiped clear in a single, thumb-wide vertical stroke—and there is a faint smear of fresh tallow gleaming on the packing nut just below it. You did not hear footsteps on the checkered plate. The iron stairways did not ring. 

If you look down through the woven gloom of the grating rather than along the catwalks, and wait until the dynamo shifts load and the bulbs dip to red embers, you will see him by the green glow of the lubricators. Or rather, you will see a curvature of wet, blackened leather wedged into a fourteen-inch gap between a brick-jacketed retort and a copper return-main—a space you would have sworn could not admit a human ribcage.

There are three of them on shift at a time, though you will almost never see two together. On the payroll ledgers kept four hundred feet above in the cool surface offices, they are listed as *Gland-Packers and Sump-Tenders*, which tells you nothing about what seven years in the deep condenser slots actually makes of a person. 

They do not use the catwalks if they can help it. Catwalks are built for upright men with boots and toolboxes, and upright men faint in this air within the hour; the heat pools under the gratings and hammers at the crown of a standing skull. Instead, the tenders move through the interstitial plumbing like slow, heavy arboreal creatures. They go barefoot, or shod in split-soled moccasins stitched from scrap conveyor-belting, laced tight around ankles corded like braided wire. Iron at sixty degrees Celsius will blister soft skin, so their soles and palms have cured over the years into thick, horn-colored pads, cracked at the creases and permanently stained plum-black with graphite grease and sulfur-ash. When one of them climbs from Tier Two to Tier Five, he doesn't take the spiral stair; he hooks a heel over a cooled intake flange, catches a counterweight guide-rail in the crook of an elbow, and draws himself upward through a vertical chimney of warm pipes with a smooth, boneless economy of effort that wastes not a single calorie in excess heat.

Up close—if one emerges onto a landing to bleed a recalcitrant pressure-trap—the shock is how slick and hairless he is. Every scrap of hair on scalp, brow, and forearm has long since been singed off by flash-vents or sloughed away by the tallow-mist, leaving skin the color of old, Boiled parchment, shining incessantly with a mixture of sweat and cylinder oil. They grease themselves deliberately before descending. Dry skin splits in the radiant bake of the basalt walls; oiled skin beads the scalding indoor rain and lets it roll off before it can scald. Across their shoulders and the ridges of their spines, they wear constellations of small, pale, circular scars—white stars where sudden drips of ninety-degree condensate hit them while they were wedged somewhere too tight to flinch.

They are small—selected for it originally, or whittled down to it until every ounce of tallow on their own bones has rendered away, leaving long, flat muscles lying directly over rib and collarbone like rawhide lashed to a frame. Their chests are slightly barrelled from drawing shallow, measured breaths of heavy, low-oxygen vapor, and they never breathe through the nose; the dry radiant heat near the retorts sears the sinus membranes. They breathe through mouths held a quarter-inch open, tongues resting against a pebble of smooth quartz or a brass ball-bearing kept under the cheek to keep the saliva flowing.

Because speaking aloud wastes moisture and carries poorly through the sub-audible thrumming of the basalt, they have dropped language down here almost entirely. If two of them are working opposite ends of a long manifold—one on Tier Two, one thirty feet above him in the amber fog of Tier Five—they talk through the iron. One takes the brass-headed spanner slung from his waist-cord and gives two sharp, fingernail-light *tinks* against a dry steam-riser. Forty feet up, the other rests two fingers against the pipe's coupling, feels the crisp ring come cleanly through the metal above the bass hum of the boilers, and taps back once with a knuckle. *Open a quarter-turn.* *Hold.* *Clear.* To an outsider, it sounds no different from the cooling-ticks of the expanding girders.

Watch one work for an hour and you begin to understand that he does not view the chamber as a machine. A machine has an outside you stand beside and levers you pull. This place is an anatomy, and he is an internal corpuscle. 

He carries no heavy kit—only a looped rawhide belt hung with twists of tallow-soaked hemp packing, a flat bronze scraper (steel sparks against the basalt if dropped, and there are pockets of sour gas under the sump), a tin pot of graphite paste, and a short, curved pruning knife. 

The knife is for the flora. Half the tender’s job is mechanical; the other half is horticultural, and the two cannot be separated. The pale, vulcanized vines that drink from the weeping flanges are the chamber’s best leak-detectors. A tender crawling through a dark pipe-chase doesn't squint at every rivet in the gloom; he runs a hand along the insulation until his fingers meet the sudden, frantic tangle of blind white shoots. Wherever the vine is lush, a gasket is weeping. He slices the tough, rubbery stems back with two deft strokes—they bleed a thick, milky latex that smells of hot slate and hisses faintly if it drops onto a steam-jacket—scrapes the bright orange velvet of young rust from the bolt-heads, packs the seam with tallow-hemp, tightens the gland a fraction of a turn until the weeping slows to a tolerable bead, and then—curiously—leaves one small root-runner intact, tucked neatly back against the cooler side of the bracket. 

They cultivate the pit. Management on the surface probably does not know this. Management certainly does not know about the alcoves behind the brick retorts on Tier Four, where the basalt wall curves inward along an old blast-fracture and a cold-water intake main sweats sweet, un-sulfured mountain water directly above a warm exhaust duct. 

There, out of sight of the main catwalks, the tenders have wedged flat slabs of split basalt between the girders to make hanging shelves. They have transplanted thick, cushioned mattresses of the pale, heat-loving moss onto the stone, creating hot, damp beds perched sixty feet above the sump. When the pit is balanced—when every counterweight is cycling cleanly on its greased cable and the canvas mufflers are sighing on their precise thirty-second rhythm—a tender does not climb out. Climbing out into cold surface air makes their joints lock and sets them coughing up gray phlegm for hours. Instead, he curls on his side on a moss-shelf behind a retort, tucked into the ninety-eight-degree pocket where the cold pipe drips three inches from his face. He catches the clean condensation in a tin cup, wipes the sulfur-crust from the corners of his eyes with a rag dipped in warm oil, and sleeps for twenty minutes at a stretch—waking instantly, before his eyes are even open, if the pitch of the floor-thrum drops by half a hertz or a pneumatic louver sticks on Tier Six.

Around their resting ledges, they encourage the tin-stiff ferns, training them around the wire cages of the carbon-filament bulbs to shade their eyes from the amber glare. In tiny, warm terracotta saucers salvaged from broken electrical insulators, they even grow something they brought down themselves years ago: small, colorless, blind caps of a tough subterranean fungus fed on shredded hemp-packing and bone-meal grease, which they chew slowly like salted gristle while watching the sight-glasses bubble.

And deep down at the very bottom—below Tier One, where the ladders end in warm, mineral-thick slurry and the sharp, unweathered basalt shards lie piled as the blasters left them—there is something else beginning. 

The tenders know about it, though they haven't reported it yet. Down in the sump-corners where the indoor rain washes the tallow-film, the scorched flint-dust, the iron-oxide, and the latex sap of cut vines together into shallow, ninety-degree puddles, a skin has formed over the water. It is neither quite plant nor quite oil-slick. It lies across the warm brine in leathery, iridescent sheets the color of tarnished copper, rippling slightly with the sub-audible vibration of the rock. When a drop of scalding water falls from fifty feet up and pierces it, the tear pulls itself slowly shut again over five or six minutes, drawing the orange rust-silt inward toward thick, gelatinous nodes anchored to the raw drill-flutes of the stone. 

When the youngest of the tenders descends to clear the suction-strainers at the bottom of the pit, wading calf-deep through the hot, dark broth in the amber twilight, he uses his bronze scraper to peel these leathery mats gently away from the intake grilles rather than tearing them, folding them back onto the warm basalt ledges the way a gardener lifts a creeping vine out of a footpath. It keeps the sulfur down, the older men have signaled to him—two taps on the brass housing, a flat palm tilted in the yellow vapor—and besides, down here in the raw slot where nothing else from the upper world can draw a breath, they are inclined to be neighborly with anything else that has figured out how to drink the sweat of the iron.
