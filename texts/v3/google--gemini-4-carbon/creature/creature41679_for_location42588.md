At first, looking down into a vertical cleft between two sweating heat-exchangers, you might mistake them for bags of ballast left snagged on a tension-cable, or clusters of large, pale molluscs riding a descending counterweight. They move with the architecture’s own rhythm—a patient, swaying inertia—until a magnetic slip-plane hisses below them, and three or four detach at once, dropping twenty feet through sheets of hot condensate to catch a wet basalt ledge with a soft, synchronized *slap* of heavy flesh.

They are human, or began there long enough ago that the skeletal plan remains legible beneath the alterations of pressure, heat, and noise, though they no longer look built for flat ground. Standing upright—which they rarely do, preferring a low, four-point crouch with their hips dropped below their knees—an adult is short and tremendously dense, packed with slow-twitch, hydraulic muscle wrapped in a thick, insulating sub-layer of slick, grey-white fat that absorbs shock when a shifting wall throws them sideways. Their skin is completely hairless, tough as boiled hide, and permanently moist, secreting a faint, waxy tallow that beads the constant, mineral-heavy rain so they do not waterlog or macerate over decades of unbroken dampness. Across their shoulders, outer thighs, and the backs of their forearms, this hide thickens into pale, overlapping callous-plates the colour of boiled cartilage, scarred and polished smooth from sliding down rough ceramic spillways and bracing between grinding housings.

Look at their hands and feet, and you see how completely the concept of the floor has left them. Their fingers and toes are long, wide-spaced, and flattened like spatulas, tipped not with nails—which would tear off in an instant on moving masonry—but with dense, bruised-looking pads of dark keratin. The palms and soles are deeply corrugated with soft, muscular ridges that work like tire treads and suction cups combined, capable of weeping a tacky, quick-releasing mucus when gripping wet memory-ceramic or slick, oiled phase-membranes. Their ankles and wrists have an alarming, loose-jointed rotational range; a woman clinging to a vertical conduit can have her feet pointed straight down, her knees bent sideways flat against the pipe, and one arm twisted backward overhead to hook a frayed carbon tendon, looking entirely relaxed while five hundred tons of basalt rumbles past six inches behind her spine.

Because the air is permanently roaring, their ears have shrunk to tight, muscular slits tucked behind heavy mastoid ridges, sealing shut underwater or near high-pressure steam vents. They barely use airborne sound at all. Watch two of them sharing a momentary niche while a bridge granulates overhead: they press a bare shoulder, a knee, or the flat of a palm against the sweating stone between them, or directly against each other’s collarbones. Their throats are thick and barrelled, housing heavy, cartilaginous resonating chambers that do not form words in the air, but send low, complexly modulated thrums—sharp clicks, harmonic buzzes, and sudden, chest-dropping *thumps*—directly into whatever solid matter they are touching. Two people fifty feet apart on the same load-bearing manifold can carry on an intricate, rapid-fire conversation through their feet while a million gallons of greywater crashes between them, reading the subtle, bone-conducted interference patterns against the machine’s own singing hum.

Their eyes are dark, set deep under protruding, rubbery brow-ridges that act as rain-gutters, channeling streaming runoff away from their vision, and veiled by translucent secondary lids that flick sideways against flying grit, chemical mist, and the sudden, blinding refraction of friction-nulling fields. Yet they rarely look far ahead. In the lower tiers, sight is good for thirty feet at best; their true spatial sense lives in the skin and the inner ear. Fine, stiff, whisker-like dermal papillae sprout along their jawlines, ribs, and shins—sensitive to micro-shifts in barometric pressure. Before a vacuum seal blows two levels down, before an overhead manifold drops to plug a gap, the air inside the corridor compresses by a fraction of a percent. You can see an entire family group react at once without looking up: a sudden, collective stillness, chins lifting slightly to taste the draft, followed by a fluid, unhurried scatter up the nearest cabling four seconds before their previous perch is sheared cleanly out of existence.

To live where no two rooms stay adjacent for an hour requires abandoning the idea of a home, a hoard, or a territory. They call themselves nothing at all in the air, though the vibration-name they tap into the stone translates roughly as *the silt that rises*—as opposed to the dead mineral broth that washes down to the unseen floor.

They travel in cohesive, multi-generational bands of twelve to thirty, bound together not by walls, but by braided harnesses of scavenged, stiff-velvet carbon fiber worn permanently across their chests and hips. Everything a band owns is strapped tightly to their bodies, streamlined so nothing snags in a closing gap. They carry short, curved pry-bars of dense, salvaged memory-metal that they use to test the charge of phase-membranes or shuck giant grey crustaceans off the walls; collapsed bladders of treated scavenger-leather for catching clean rainwater; and small, waterproofed pouches of bone needles, rendered bird-fat, and dried lithotrophic pastes. 

Sleep is a communal engineering problem. Nothing stays safe for eight hours, so they sleep in four-hour shifts, and they sleep suspended. When a band settles for a rest cycle, scouts climb into the upper interstices to read the "tide" of the local machinery—feeling the heat of the conduits and the pitch of the field-hum in their teeth to find a structural cluster that has just completed a major rearrangement and is likely to hold its configuration for a few hours. There, they anchor quick-release friction-hooks into porous cooling walls and string up tight, cocoons of braided carbon mesh directly over an open drop or a water-chute. Three or four bodies pack into each web for shared regulation in the drafty damp, slick skin pressed to slick skin, while two waking sentries crouch on the anchor-points above, palms flat against the stone. If the pitch of the singing hum rises—meaning the local fields are powering up to drop load—one sharp jerk on a master cord drops the entire sleeping-web cleanly into the chute below, dumping the band wide-awake into a known runoff channel before the walls can close on them.

Infants are born small, rubbery, and fiercely gripping, able to lock their spatulate fingers into the loops of a parent’s chest-harness within minutes of birth. For the first two years, a child never touches the architecture directly; the stone is too hot in places, too abrasive, too prone to sudden, skin-tearing shifts. They live tucked inside forward-facing slings made from de-powered, scavenged phase-membrane—material soft as wet silk—drinking milk rich as whale-fat while their inner ears calibrate to the chaotic, three-dimensional drop-and-heave of their parents' leaps. By three, they are clipped to five-foot tether-lines, scrambling alongside the band over safe, broad domes, learning the fundamental grammar of survival: *warmth rising means a vent is clearing; cold stone means a block is dead-weight and about to drop; when the grey crabs let go, let go with them.*

Their diet is hot, briny, and overwhelmingly rich, harvested on the move without fire—since open flame is impossible in the wet gale, and unnecessary where thousands of surfaces run near boiling. To cook, they slap gutted, six-limbed aqueduct-predators or handfuls of shelled wall-grazers into mesh bags and wedge them into the exhaust-flues of working heat-exchangers, pulling them out twenty minutes later steamed tender in the machine's own mineral breath. They scrape the rust-orange lithotrophic slimes—only the bright, tangy, voltage-fed blooms, avoiding the bitter black strains heavy with leached metals—rolling them into chewy, sour cakes mixed with crushed midges and the rich, salty yolks of leather-wing eggs raided from steam-plume updrafts. 

They do not worship the basin, nor do they try to pilot it, fix it, or understand why it runs. To them, the endless, tearing rearrangement of the world is simply weather made of rock. Yet they possess an astonishing, embodied literacy of its moods. An elder—recognizable by the thick, milky callouses over their knuckles, a spine curved permanently into a shock-absorbing C, and skin marbled with faint, branching Lichtenberg scars from decades of brushing leaky field-membranes—can press their cheek to a sweating basalt bulkhead, close their eyes, and feel the macro-rhythm of five square miles. They know when a subterranean reservoir is about to drain miles away by the minute change in the viscosity of the wall-sweat; they know which pulsing trunk-conduits carry scalding chemical slurry and which carry warm, clean greywater you can ride like a flume for three districts to escape a sinking whirlpool.

Indeed, riding the basin is how they cross great distances, and how bands meet. Every few weeks, when a massive squall line forms and thousands of roofs tilt open like stone petals to swallow the deluge, the resulting subterranean surge flushes the major arterial aqueducts at predictable pressures. Bands converge on the lip of these roaring spillways from miles around. For forty minutes—while a temporary plateau of locked cargo-housings holds steady under the grey sky—two or three hundred people might gather in the warm, hammering rain.

It is a bizarre, silent carnival beneath the deafening roar of the sky and the sink. Because they cannot shout across the crowd, they link up—dozens of slick, heavy-shouldered figures sitting in dense, tangled rings on the wet slate, ankles hooked over ankles, palms pressed to backs and knees, turning the entire gathering into a single, continuous acoustic circuit. Jokes, warnings about erratic districts, genealogies, and courtships ripple through fifty bodies at once as rolling waves of chest-thumps and finger-tapped trills. Adolescents show off by sprinting across active friction-nulling gaps, letting the mirage-fields catch their oiled heels so they hydroplane on pure refraction across forty-foot drops, twisting in mid-air to slap safe stone on the far side while the elders thump heavy, approving bass-notes into the roof. Tools are swapped; young adults unclip their harness-tethers from their birth-bands and clip into new ones; bodies slick with tallow pair off in the warm, curtained alcoves beneath tilted rainwater-petals.

And they sing—though an outsider standing ten feet away on dry boots would hear nothing over the cataract-roar of the basin. Pressed into the ring, however, you would feel it: two hundred barrelled throats dropping into resonance with the deep, tooth-aching hum of the gravity-nulling fields below, layering counter-rhythms over the tectonic groans of shifting mega-blocks, turning the blind, headless labor of the continental engine into music inside their own ribs.

Then the rain slacks. Deep underneath them, vacuum seals boom open like distant artillery; the stone petals begin to groan downward; a tremor of pre-shift tension runs through the slate. Without panic, without a farewell wave, the ring breaks. Clips snap onto harnesses, children are cinched tight against chests, and before the temporary plateau has separated into twenty diverging warehouses, the people have already poured over the edges like water—dropping into chutes, swinging along wet carbon tendons, vanishing back into the warm, grinding dark between the gears.
