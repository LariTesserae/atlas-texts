At first you miss them because you are looking for things that displace the air around them, or cast a shadow beneath a brow, or shrink into the distance the way distant bodies ought to.

When you finally notice one standing beneath a woven-reed awning, you take them to be thirty paces off. You can see the beads of warm condensation clinging to the collarbone, the faint bluish branching of veins beneath the skin of the inner wrist, the exact, wet seal where two toes press together against the red brick. Only when you start walking toward them—crossing one flooded court, stepping over a thigh-thick vine, ascending three crisp terracotta risers onto a causeway that leaves you no higher than before—do you understand that they are more than a mile away. As you walk for twenty minutes through the steam-thick glare, they do not grow larger in your sight. They simply slide slowly downward from the upper register of your vision toward the bottom, like a figure painted near the top of a scroll being lowered to meet your eyes, every pore as sharp from a league away as it is when you finally stop within reach of their hand.

Once your eye learns how the basin stacks its distances, you see them everywhere.

There are hundreds of them, spaced out across the miles of ochre floor with the same breathable, uncrowded regularity as the groves and the ash-colored birds. None of them shelter under the awnings for shade—there is no shade beneath the reeds, where the undersides of the woven mats blaze just as whitely as the open sky—but they stand there all the same, singly or in pairs, letting the lukewarm drops that gather on the cordage fall onto their scalps and shoulders. Others sit hip-deep in the wide clay saucers among the soft-shelled turtles, legs stretched straight out before them on the submerged tile. A few lie flat on their backs along the narrow ridges between flooded courts, eyes wide open to the white dome overhead.

They are roughly human in plan, though heavier in the limb and fuller in the trunk, built with the same turgid, water-swollen completeness as the twenty-foot stalks that rise from the floor slots. None of them have hair—neither on their heads, nor at their groins, nor along the smooth rims of their eyelids, which need no lashes where sweat has no salt to sting and light comes equally from the floor and the sky. Their skins vary across the exact palette of a potter’s yard before and after the kiln: some are the pale, slick grey-white of river marl; some the warm buff of dried reed-pith; others a deep, iron-rich terracotta or the dark plum-brown of steeped bark. Up close, their flesh is intensely warm and yielding, plumped with slow fluid rather than taut muscle. Yet if you look behind an ear, or along the hollow beside a collarbone, or down the long curve where the flank meets the hip, you can find faint, unweathered marks that belong less to birth than to finishing—a sweeping, delicate grain in the skin as if smoothed with wet grass, or a tender, raised seam running down the inner forearm like slip pressed up between the halves of a mold, pulsing softly with life.

Nothing in them hungers, and nothing hurries. Their mouths are small, smooth-lipped, and mostly kept closed; their ribs expand once every two or three minutes in a long, unforced draw, taking the saturated heat into their chests and holding it there before letting it slide back out. They have no teeth behind their gums—only smooth, hard ridges pale as bone, like the bills of the wading birds—and their bellies neither churn nor hollow. Instead, water moves through them the way it moves through the floor. One will kneel beside a brimming wicker basket, dip their chin through that stubborn, invisible meniscus that keeps the water from spilling between the gaps in the reeds, and drink deeply without making a ripple. Half an hour later, sitting upright on a dry terrace, clear lukewarm moisture weeps gently from the palms of their hands and the arches of their feet, sliding down onto the clay without leaving a dark mark. When they walk, they ride upon that microscopic film of water between sole and tile. They leave no prints behind them to dry because their feet never truly break contact with the basin’s continuous wet skin.

Like the cobble wall that turns seamlessly into the stem of an arum lily, many of them share quiet, borderless continuities with the materials around them. These are not wounds or deformities; there is no scar tissue where one nature yields to the next. A broad-hipped figure with skin like tallow sits on a stepped ledge, and from their left knee downward the flesh clarifies and hardens into unglazed red terracotta, ending in a square, flat-bottomed architectural plinth instead of a foot; when they rise to walk across the court, the clay base parts cleanly from the floor and steps forward in rhythm with their fleshed right leg, striking the wet brick without a sound. Another carries a thick, waxy green philodendron petiole rooting smoothly out from the meat of their trapezius, its single yard-wide leaf arching above their head like a parasol that casts no shadow, beading water down their spine. In the deeper hand-coiled cisterns—vessels five feet high whose mouths are barely wide enough to admit a human thigh—smaller, broader-ribbed individuals live entirely submerged. If you peer over the thumb-impressed lip of a cistern, the water at the bottom is just as blindingly bright as the plain outside, and curled comfortably against the curved clay wall four feet down is a pale, wide-eyed sleeper who never sleeps,copper frogs clinging to their shins, looking steadily back up through the glassy lens of the water while thin, translucent gill-frills stir lazily behind their jawbones—though there is no current in the jar to stir them.

Even among those whose bodies are wholly flesh, boundaries remain loose if they linger too long against the world or each other. Two figures sitting shoulder-to-shoulder beneath a timber post will, over the course of several hours, begin to share an inch of skin where their upper arms press together—tallow shading effortlessly into dark ochre without a crease. When at last one decides to rise, they draw apart slowly, without pain or tearing; for a second the flesh stretches like warm wax, parts cleanly, and vanishes back into two separate, unblemished contours, as instant and final as a water-ring hitting the lip of a saucer.

When they move across the open flats, the basin’s broken sequence catches on them just as it does on the lizards and birds. A tall, marl-skinned figure will walk down a causeway, step cleanly through the solid chest of a rough-hewn timber post without breaking stride or disturbing the cordage lashed around it, and emerge dry-shouldered on the far side. Another will rise from the rim of a saucer and walk fifty paces toward a grove, yet leave themselves sitting on the rim behind—and leave a third version paused mid-step halfway between. For a minute or an hour, all three exist in full, warm solidity: the sitting self watching a snail cross their instep, the mid-stride self balanced on one foot with heel raised, the farthest self touching the waxy underside of a giant leaf. Sometimes a turtle crawls around the ankle of the trailing double moments before that body ceases to be there, leaving the space above the wet brick empty without any rush of displaced air.

They make no sound at all. Even if one opens their mouth and breathes hard against the back of their hand, the heavy air swallows the rasp before it clears their lips. Yet they are intensely, perpetually occupied with one another across the miles.

Because distance does not diminish anything to the eye here, privacy does not exist, nor does isolation. A person standing near the western rim of the basin sees the twitch of an eyelid on someone two miles east at the exact visual scale of a lover standing nose-to-nose, layered flatly behind fifty intervening groves, awnings, and wading birds as if pressed onto one crowded pane of white fire. Their entire shared life takes place inside this flattened geometry. Watch them long enough and you begin to realize why they stand motionless for hours in seemingly arbitrary spots, or walk half a mile across glaring brick only to move a wicker basket two finger-breadths to the left, or lift one rounded arm at an odd angle and hold it there while frogs climb their calf.

From where *you* stand, the gesture means nothing. Walk a mile north and turn around, however, or step into the exact tile just vacated by another observer, and the flattened sightline snaps together: the curve of that raised forearm half a mile off aligns seamlessly with the lip of a coiled cistern a mile behind them and the drooping tip of a palm frond two hundred yards in front, completing a continuous, unbroken arc across three separate depths of space. They spend eternity tuning the flat visual field for eyes miles away—nudging a dinner-plate turtle three inches across a puddle so its pale shell plugs a gap between two distant timber posts, tilting their chins so the curve of a cheek rhymes with a stepped terrace on the far horizon, holding a pose until someone leagues off shifts weight from left foot to right in silent acknowledgment, releasing the line to dissolve into a new one.

Only one place in the basin disrupts their calm choreography.

Near the center, around the half-laid course of wet, unfired grey clay bricks where the wooden mallet and wooden-bladed trowel lie abandoned, none of them walk. A ring of empty, glistening red tile thirty paces across surrounds the unfinished work, kept clear even of turtles and birds, though no wall marks its edge. There is always a small handful of the inhabitants gathered just outside that invisible perimeter—five or six of them crouching on their heels or sitting cross-legged on the warm terracotta, facing inward.

They never reach for the mallet. They never touch the grey bricks. Even in their flattened sightlines, they carefully avoid lining anything up behind or across that low, glistening row of sliced clay. They simply sit and look at the wet curl left by the trowel’s edge—the one surface in all the blinding miles that has not yet been brought to its permanent state, slick and plastic and dark against the fired red floor. Occasionally, one of the watchers will lift a slow hand to the faint, grass-brushed seam behind their own ear, or press a thumb gently into the soft flesh of their own forearm until a shallow pale dimple forms and slowly swells back out again, looking from their skin to the wet grey brick and back with wide, shadowless eyes—as if trying to recall which side of the kiln they belong to, in a bright, hot afternoon that has no way of moving toward evening.
