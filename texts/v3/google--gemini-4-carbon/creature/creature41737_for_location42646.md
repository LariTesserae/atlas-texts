Begin with the worms, because they hold the basin’s plumbing together, and everything larger walks on their roofs.

Where the steel pipelines sweat at their riveted seams, and along the lip of every travertine saucer where heavy fluid spills over into the next dish down, there are grey, convoluted ridges that look like braided mortar. Break a piece off—it takes a hammer or a heavy stone to crack it—and the inside is honeycombed with glossy, porcelain-smooth tunnels the diameter of knitting needles. Each tunnel houses a single *ferric-worm*, four inches of dense, maroon muscle saturated with so much oxygen-binding myoglobin that a crushed specimen stains the rock like old paint. They never emerge into the sun. They sit anchored in their self-built masonry, extending only a tiny, stiffened crown of bronze-colored radioles into the warm, creeping film of brine. Unlike sea-worms, whose plumes wave softly in a current, these crowns are rigid, comb-like, flicking back and forth with a sharp, mechanical regularity to rake microscopic sulfur-threads and bacterial floc out of the syrupy runoff. When the afternoon sun thickens the brine to paste, the worms draw back an inch, pull a plug of pure, crystallized potash down over their doorways like a manhole cover, and wait in the dark, stewing safely in their own retained moisture until the night-melt from the rim dilutes the pans again.

Over these worm-reefs flows the slow, continuous paving of the *pan-lice*.

Up close, a pan-louse looks less like a crab or a woodlouse and more like a dropped fingernail made of tarnished pewter. Their carapaces are extraordinarily flat and wide, beveled down to a knife-edge around the entire circumference. When one settles onto a slick of ochre mat, it presses that rim flat against the wet silica and arches its central body slightly upward, creating a suction seal so tight that neither the mountain gales near the rim nor the prying beak of a scavenger can flick it loose without shattering its back. Underneath that dome, protected from the drying glare, four pairs of delicate, blood-red gill-curtains trail directly in the brine film, drinking oxygen out of liquid too salty and thin to drown them, yet wet enough to keep their membranes from crisping.

They do not walk individually so much as shift in vast, crowded pavements. Because there is no room between domes, five thousand of them might occupy the flank of a single sulfur blister, overlapping like roof slates. Their mouths are Underneath—rotary rasps hardened with iron sulfide—scraping microscopic lanes through the purple and green mats. You can watch a herd of ten thousand pan-lice grazing three feet away and hear nothing at all through the thin air, yet if you lay a bare knuckle against the warm sinter beside them, you feel a faint, continuous vibration in the bone: a gritty *zzzzz* of ten thousand iron teeth filing stone. When a geothermal rig thirty yards away drops its counterweight and sends a heavy seismic *thump* rolling through the crust, every louse within fifty paces clamps down simultaneously. For three seconds, a patch of hillside turns from shifting, glistening pewter to dull, rigid stone—a sudden visual stutter across the landscape—before they all lift a millimeter and resume creeping toward the seep-lines.

The brine-flies live in the half-inch of dead air trapped directly against the grease of the pans. Above that boundary layer, the forty-five-degree heat and five-thousand-meter altitude thin the atmosphere until tiny wings simply beat at nothing; an updraft catching a fly two inches off the stone flings it tumbling skyward like dust, doomed to freeze in the blue dark above. So the flies have largely given up real flight. Their wings are narrow, paddle-shaped stubs fringed with oily hairs. They use them like oars and skids, rowing furiously across the surface tension of the heavy lithium-brine, skate-jumping from crustacean back to crustacean back in blurred, frantic zigzags. Their larvae are blind, transparent bladders packed with salt-pumping glands, rolling in the warm, rust-pink sludge at the bottom of boot-heel dishes, eating the molted skins of pan-lice and the dead bacterial paste that cooks loose at noon.

Hunting this ankle-high commotion are the spindle-lizards—though "lizard" barely suits what high-altitude hypoxia has made of them.

A full-grown adult is a foot long from snout to tail-tip, yet weighs less than a sparrow. Beneath skin the color of bleached bone and rusted iron, its torso is almost entirely lung: two immense, chambered bellows armored in wide, curving ribs that swing outward and inward ninety times a minute, even at dead rest. Watching one perched on the black iron flange of a wellhead is unsettling; it looks less like a basking reptile and more like an animal in the final, panting throes of heatstroke. Yet its eyes—sunken behind overhanging eaves of yellow horn to block the fierce ultraviolet glare, and wiped constantly by a translucent, salt-shedding third eyelid—are bright, black, and calculating.

They stand high on four stilt-thin legs, holding their bellies well clear of the sixty-degree rock. Their toes end not in claws, which would skate uselessly on waxy silica, but in broad, spatulate pads covered in stiff, heat-deadened bristles, allowing them to sprint across boiling travertine or vertical copper piping without blistering. Because the thin air starves their muscles of endurance, they cannot chase anything far. A spindle-lizard hunts in three-second explosions. It waits in the ink-black shadow under an insulated conduit, ribs pumping furiously to hyper-oxygenate its dark, thick blood. Then it darts into the white glare—*tic-tic-tic* across the crustacean backs—snaps six brine-flies or a soft, freshly molted pan-louse into a long, tweezer-like jaw, and vanishes back under the pipe before its muscles choke on lactic acid and the sun boils its brain. Every twenty minutes, it pauses at a weeping iron rivet or its own nostrils sneeze out two tiny, dry pellets of pure white salt, clearing its blood of the brine it swallows with every meal.

Where the lizards are built for frantic economy, the *gasket-crabs* are built like pieces of the machinery itself.

You almost never see them at midday. They belong to the architecture of the extraction rigs and the older, slumped fumaroles. Imagine a terrestrial crab the size of a dinner plate, heavy-shelled and slow, whose carapace has become encrusted with the exact same yellow-white mineral rime that welds the cast-iron pipe cradles to the stone. In fact, they cultivate it: short, hooked hairs on their backs trap splashing brine until travertine grows right over their armor, turning a motionless crab into just another bulbous lump at the base of a condenser tower.

Their left claw is tiny, shaped like a curved spoon for scooping bacterial jelly and scraping crystallized sulfur off cooling fins. Their right claw is grotesque—a massive, blunt-toothed vise with leverage enough to crack a ferric-worm’s masonry tube like a peanut shell, or shear right through the pewter armor of an adult pan-louse. Because carrying that much shell and mineral crust through thin, hot air is metabolically ruinous, gasket-crabs move only when shadows lengthen, or inside the damp, vibrating labyrinths beneath the wellheads. They love the machines. A crab will wedge itself directly between two warm copper fractionating pipes, letting the steady, dripping condensation from a leaky valve wash over its chambered, lung-like gill vaults. Maintenance crews—on the rare, miserable mornings they hike in with oxygen bottles before dawn—curse them endlessly, because a gasket-crab treats hardened rubber seals, lead packing, and grease fittings as extraordinarily rich, chewable varieties of bacterial mat. Every pipe joint in the basin is wrapped in heavy galvanized wire mesh specifically to keep those right claws from peeling the gaskets out of live steam lines.

Not everything crawls in the open. Beneath the polished crust, the sinter domes are not solid rock; they are old, overgrown gardens of previous centuries, riddled with cooling cracks, abandoned steam vents, and collapsed cavities filled with breathless, wet heat.

Down in that dark, sweating sponge lives the *sinter-blind*—an arachnid, or the cousin of a whip-scorpion, gone pale and translucent as boiled gristle. Its body is no bigger than an almond, flattened like paper so it can slip through millimeter-wide fractures between mineral layers, while its front pair of legs have stretched out into hair-thin sensory whips ten inches long. In the subterranean quiet—where the surface wind does not exist, and the only sound is the constant, deep *thud... thud...* of the flywheel pumps half a mile away—those whips tap lightly along the wet ceilings of pockets, tasting the chemistry of the sweat and feeling for the tiny, rasping vibrations of soft-bodied mites, springtails, and juvenile crustaceans sheltering from the noon glare. When the magma sill shifts slightly below, or a blocked fumarole redirects scalding fluid into an old gallery, a whole seam of stone will suddenly bleed dozens of pale, frantic threads as the sinter-blinds pour out of a hairline crack, scramble blindly across three feet of blinding sunlight—curling instantly if they take five seconds too long—and vanish into the cool dark beneath a pipe cradle.

And overhead, stacked in silent, revolving rings from five hundred feet up to two thousand, hang the *kettle-vultures*.

From the basin floor, they are only black, rectangular slots cut out of the steel-blue sky. Up in the thermal chimney, where the forty-five-degree breath of the three-mile shelf punches straight up into the freezing mountain air, the lift is tremendous and turbulent. A kettle-vulture has wings seven feet across, plank-straight and slotted deeply at the tips like splayed fingers, covered in coarse, matte-black plumage that absorbs solar heat even when the bird drifts out into sub-zero gales at the edge of the column. They have ratchet-like tendons in their shoulders that click into place, allowing them to sleep on the wing for hours without spending a calorie, turning and turning above the silent cauldron below.

Their heads and necks are entirely bare, covered not in soft skin like lowland scavengers, but in dry, overlapping scales of pale horn—protection against the stinging, caustic grease of the pans when they come down to feed. Their eyes are telescopic. From a quarter-mile up, a kettle-vulture watches the rhythm of the spindle-lizards darting between pipe shadows. It knows what healthy speed looks like, and it spots the subtle hesitation when an old lizard’s overloaded heart finally falters in the midday hypoxia, or when a gasket-crab misjudges the afternoon thickening of a pan and gets cemented by its own belly-hairs into drying lithium paste, cooking slowly in the sun.

When that happens, the bird simply pulls its wings into a tight arrowhead and steps out of the sky.

Because of the starved air, there is no roaring whistle of feathers until the very last split second—just a sudden, hurtling black shadow expanding across the white crust, a dry *whuff* as seven feet of wing snap open six inches above the travertine to brake, and the sharp *clack* of horn feet hitting sinter. It does not linger. Even a kettle-vulture, with hollow bones ventilated by nine auxiliary air sacs, cannot endure the wet-bulb furnace on the floor for long. Its open beak drips stringy fluid onto the hot stone; its throat pouch flutters furiously to dump heat. In forty seconds it has bolted the lizard whole, or wrenched the cooked meat out of a cracked crab shell, and then it runs—awkward, bounding hops over worm-reefs and scalding dishes, avoiding the hiss of steam louvers—always heading toward the nearest active rig or big fumarole. It leaps straight into the rising white plume of a thermal vent or a blowing exhaust stack, lets the violent blast of hot vapor catch its broad wings like an elevator, and rides the column effortlessly back up into the cold, breathable blue.

There is one last layer to the basin’s life, active only in the violent transition of dusk and dawn, along the jagged basalt rim where the plateau ends and the thousand-meter drop into the clouds begins.

Here, the forty-five-degree air pouring off the shelf slams into sub-zero mountain wind. All night, rime ice builds in booming, crystalline beards along the outer boulders and the rusted perimeter fence posts. Tucked deep into the frost-shattered crevices right on this knife-edge—where their hindquarters sit in freezing shadow and their noses point toward the warm, salt-smelling breath of the basin—live small, round-eared mammals no larger than clenched fists: *rime-pikas*, with fur as dense as chinchilla wool on their backs and bare, leathery, heat-radiating bellies.

They cannot survive twenty yards inside the basin at noon; they would boil inside their coats. Nor can they survive on the sterile, glaciated cliffs below, where nothing grows. Instead, they live strictly on the three-yard-wide collision line. At first light, before the sun clears the eastern lip and while the outer pans are still sluggish and cool, the rime-pikas slip down into the weep-zones. Moving through the shredding rags of morning fog, their little jaws work silently at the sparse tufts of wire-grass and salt-crusted halophytes, clipping leaves, licking fresh, pure meltwater directly off the melting ice-collars before it hits the salty rock and flashes away or turns to brine. Sometimes they crunch up cold-stunned spindle-lizards that strayed too close to the rim at dusk and stiffened in the sudden night chill, getting calcium and salt in three quick bites before bounding back to the breezy, freezing safety of the ridge.

And once every four or five months, the *turnkey* comes through the southern notch.

You cannot rightly say the turnkey lives here—no human does—yet to the creatures of the shelf, this slow, silver-clad biped is simply a seasonal animal, rarer than a vulture, predictable as a valve. They arrive thirty minutes before sunrise, climbing up out of the blinding sea of mountain clouds in a bulky, reflective suit veined with chilled water-tubes, carrying a heavy oxygen rebreather on their back and a long brass wrench in one gloved hand. Through their polycarbonate visor, swiftly smearing with salty grease, they walk the narrow paths from dome to pipe-casing, boots crunching through old sinter crusts.

Nothing flees them. In a place where fleeing costs oxygen you cannot spare, fear is an unaffordable luxury. As the turnkey kneels beside a black iron wellhead—hearing nothing inside their helmet except the hiss of their own breathing rig and the deep thump of the flywheel coming up through their kneepads—spindle-lizards immediately crowd into the cool, sharp-edged shadow cast by their crouched body, ribs pumping fast, peering up at the mirrored visor. Pan-lice creep right onto the toe-caps of their heavy boots to rasp away a splash of fresh bacterial slime. When the turnkey swings the brass wrench to crack a fouled drain cock, venting a gallon of hot, rust-colored sludge across the travertine, they do not scatter life; they ring a dinner bell. Before they have even stood up and shouldered their wrench to trudge toward the next rig half a mile away through the silent, shimmering heat, five hundred pewter shells are already pivoting toward the spill, brine-flies are rowing across its steaming surface, and high overhead in the flat steel sky, three black rectangles tilt slightly on locked wings to watch the silver thing walk.
