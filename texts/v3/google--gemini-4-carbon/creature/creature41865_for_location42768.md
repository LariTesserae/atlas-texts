For sixteen hours out of twenty-four, and through all the long months when snow packs ten feet deep along the surrounding ridges, the stone court keeps solely to its smaller, slower tenants.

Most of what truly abides here never comes up into the light at all. Ten feet beneath the pale limestone floor, inside the flooded karst chambers that prime the half-hour siphon, the dark water is warm as blood and hung with creamy curtains of submerged travertine. Thousands of the flat, thumb-sized crabs cling upside down to those subterranean ceilings, far denser in the dark than the solitary stragglers that occasionally wander out into the blinding day. Sharing the chambers with them are creatures without legs to count: ribbon-thin, eyeless cave eels no longer than a human finger and pale as peeled willow twigs, which nose through the bacterial silk on the rock walls and swallow crab-molts whole. Every twenty-eight minutes, when the stone bend fills to its crest and the whole hidden plumbing sucks itself dry in one violent heave, the eels whip sideways into tiny solution pockets to keep from being spat up through the fist-sized blowholes onto the sunlit floor. Now and then a small one misses its hold, shoots up in the warm gush, thrashes blindly in two inches of sunlit mineral water while the giant leaves boom overhead, and slips back down a drainage seam before the stone drains bare.

Above ground, life hides where the noise dies. Around the bases of the haystack-sized liverwort mounds, where the velvety emerald dome curves inward to meet the wet limestone in a dark, breathless crease, live flat, shell-less slugs the exact grey-green of wet marl. They are wide as coat buttons and thin as knife blades, breathing directly through their slick backs. Moving an inch an hour, they graze away every yellowing cell at the bottom margin of the liverwort as fast as it ages, keeping the green domes shaved to that eerie, unbroken perfection while leaving nothing behind to rot. In deep winter, when snow drops out of the close sky and melts before it reaches the basin floor—turning the whole court into a tall, boiling chimney of white steam that smells of wet chalk—bighorn ewes come to stand along the dry, frozen upper rim. They never venture down the slick curves where hooves have no purchase, only crane their necks over the lip, lick the warm mineral rime at the edge, and listen with twitching ears to the unseen *thwack-boom* hammering away inside the fog.

The people who bruisings and cobbles mark the floor do not live inside the bowl, and do not try to. Their summer camp sits twenty minutes down the leeward shoulder of the mountain in a dry, silent hollow among stunted subalpine larches—a low lean-to wedged out of the wind where a small fire of resinous twigs can actually catch and hold. Down there, the air smells of pine pitch and cold slate, and you only know the basin exists if you lay your ear flat against an outcrop of bedrock and feel the faint, irregular tremor in your jawbone.

Yet from the week the high passes clear in late spring until the autumn gales drive everyone back down to the timbered river gorges miles below, the court is inhabited all day long.

They come over the northwestern lip an hour after full daybreak, when the sun climbs clear of the crags and pours straight down into the nave. Before stepping over the rim, they strip almost bare. Under a dry, overhanging slab of caprock—the same warm, sunny ledge where the solitary black wasps land to drink—they tuck away their greased hide boots, their woven rabbit-skin vests, and their pouches of dry tinder. Going into the basin dressed in cured leather would ruin good skins in a morning, turning them slimy with warm lime-sweat. They climb down into the court wearing only short, soft-beaten aprons of old bark fiber, bare-chested and barefoot, descending sideways along a single diagonal seam where embedded nodules of ancient horn-coral roughen the slick limestone just enough to catch the side of a calloused big toe.

There are almost always two of them, and for the past several summers it has been the same pair: a broad-backed woman in her fifties with cropped, iron-grey hair and thumbnails thickened like horn, and her sister’s son, a quiet man near thirty whose left ankle was crushed under shifting scree four winters ago and knit back together crooked and thick. Down in the cold valley camps, where meltwater streams run green with glacier flour and numb a wader’s shins in thirty seconds, his bad joint locks up by noon and her knuckles swell tight around an awl. Stepping down into the basin is like wading into the warm breath of a barn. Within five minutes their wind-roughened skin is shining with fine condensation and seep-mist, their toes spread flat and easy on the tepid stone, and the stiffness goes out of them.

Back down at the autumn gatherings in the gorge, anyone can tell the people who work the high vat before they open their mouths. All summer long, they wear a faint, chalky tide-mark of dried white travertine from the soles of their feet to two inches above their anklebones, left there by the half-hour floods drying in the crisp mountain air on the walk back to camp. Their heels and palms, instead of cracking black with pine soot and cold dirt, are pumiced smooth and pale by warm alkaline water.

They treat the basin less like a wild place than like an enormous, moody piece of living machinery whose rhythms must not be crossed.

The river cobbles pinning the bark bundles in the shallow troughs were not carried up by either of them. Those nine heavy, melon-sized stones of dark valley basalt and speckled granite were lugged up the mountain in rawhide slings three generations back, because native limestone crumbles gritty in the seep and leaves nothing loose to weight a trough. The stones are heirlooms; each has a faint collar of crystalline lime grown around its belly that knocks off with a wooden click whenever a fresh bundle of stripped larch or mountain-willow bast is tucked underneath.

Settled on their heels in that ten-foot eastern pocket where the pecked drip-lip turns the overhead *crack* into a soft, wet *tup*, the two of them work with their backs nearly brushing the warm, sweating wall and one flank sheltered by the nearest of the giant liverwort domes, which drinks up the sharpest slap of the echoes behind them. Across her bare thigh, or over a smooth granite cobble dragged briefly out of an empty trough, the woman lays a sodden, caramel-colored ribbon of retted bark and draws a dull-backed flake of dark chert down its length. *Shhhk. Shhhk.* The loosened outer rind peels away in translucent brown jelly, leaving behind skeins of inner fiber pale as boiled sinew and tough enough, once dried and twisted, to snare a mountain ram or hold a fish-weir together in winter rapids. Beside her, the nephew twists yesterday’s dried strands double against his good shin, drawing each finished yard through a smear of clear, glassy parasol-mucilage dipped from the folded leaf-cup. Dried in the mountain sun, that plant-gum turns fiber impervious to ice-fray and water-rot; down in the river valleys, hunters trade prime beaver pelts and blocks of black chert just for ten fathoms of high-basin cord.

Nothing of their mess stays on the floor. They time their scraping to the subterranean clock under their heels.

Long before a visitor’s ear would catch anything over the racket of the leaves, the two of them feel a subtle change through the soles of their feet—a faint, hollow tightening in the rock as the hidden siphon primes itself. Without looking up or breaking sentence, the nephew reaches down, gathers their loose chert blades and dry fiber skeins, and sets them on a narrow bedding ledge ten inches up the wall. Ten seconds later, the blowholes clear their throats with that deep, wet *chug-roar*, and warm water surges across the court, swirling up around their ankles and calves. While the two-minute flood stands full, both of them swish their freshly scraped fiber briskly through the warm water around their shins, washing away every shred of brown bark-slime and sour pulp. When the siphon breaks and the floor drinks the water silently back down its hairline cracks, all the fine chaff gets sucked straight down into the dark karst below—where the blind eels and chamber-crabs make short work of it—leaving the pale limestone between the giant plants as spotless as before they came.

Now and then, between flushes, one of the little translucent crabs edges out of a blowhole near the eastern wall, drawn by microscopic specks of sweet cambium left in the millimeter glaze. It takes twenty minutes to sidle up to the nephew’s crooked ankle, scraping steadily with tiny spoon-claws right against his wet skin. Neither of them ever brushes one away.

What the basin alters most in them is how they speak, and what they speak about.

Ten paces out toward the center of the court, where plum-sized drops fall thirty feet onto taut hollow ribs, two people standing chest to chest would have to scream themselves raw to trade three words. Even tucked inside their pecked eastern niche, shouting is useless; a raised voice hits the curved grey stone and smears into the booming of the leaves. Instead, they talk almost without moving their ribs, pitching their voices in a flat, hummed chest-register that slips neatly underneath the bright *thwack* of the high canopies and above the deep resonance of the karst. They sit close enough that their shoulders touch when they lean forward to rinse a skein, watching the corners of each other’s mouths as much as listening.

Because no one down in the cramped bark-shelters of the winter gorge ever has a room with solid walls—and because nobody climbing over the rim could ever sneak up on them down that slick limestone curve or hear a syllable from twenty feet away—that ten-foot corner inside the thunder is the most private place on the mountain. For an hour at a stretch they will say nothing at all, letting the *tup-tup-tup* on the curled leaf-margin and the overlapping battery of great drum-strokes fill their skulls until thought goes pleasantly blank and only their thumbs think. Then, between one siphon-roar and the next, one of them will speak with sudden, unguarded plainness—picking up a thread dropped two days ago, naming an old grudge kept carefully swallowed down by the hearths, laughing silently with shaking shoulders over some pompous cousin down in the valley, or recounting word for word how the woman’s dead uncle knelt on a slung plank forty summers back with a greenstone maul, bruising that overhead lip flake by stubborn flake while water hammered the crown of his head.

They guard the slow plants with a ferocious, unsentimental thrift. A single three-yard parasol leaf, thick as saddle leather, would roof a whole lean-to against hail, yet neither of them would ever slice a living stem. Along the thickest of the wrist-wide stalks, you can trace a faint spiral of pale, puckered dimples—thirty or forty tiny healed slits from past summers’ mucilage taps, never more than one shallow nick a year on the biggest cluster, moved faithfully before frost so the wound seals cleanly in its own gum. Once every three or four years, when millions of impacts finally fatigue a woody joint beneath a parasol and one of the heavy green seed-bladders splits open on the stone, they watch where the half-hour surges roll it. Left to the blind tilt of the floor, four pods out of five eventually wash against a narrow drainage seam too tight to root in and slowly dissolve in the alkaline glaze. If the woman spots a split pod wedged over a dead crack on her walk to the troughs, she will hook it gently with the side of her bare foot and nudge it two spans sideways into the path of the next siphon-wash, aiming it toward a deep, empty solution pit near the southern wall—an investment in shade and gum for people not yet born, who probably will not know who steered it there.

When the close bright square of sky overhead turns cold pewter in late afternoon and shadows climb the sweating limestone walls, the air at rim-level sharpens fast. They pull the dark chert tap from the stalk—leaving the leaf-cup pinned shut with its bone splinters so night-drips won’t dilute the gum—wash their scrapers in the day’s last warm flood, sling their heavy wet hanks of clean fiber across their shoulders, and climb barefoot up the coral-seam out of the bowl.

Stepping back over the stone lip into the thin, dry wind near the treeline hits their ears like going suddenly underwater. For the whole twenty-minute walk down through the crooked larches, neither of them speaks; the mountain silence rings too loudly in their heads, and their wet ankles dry white and tight in the cold grass. Behind them, thirty feet down inside the smoothed grey rock, the empty court goes right on booming into the dusk, washing its own stone clean every half hour under a darkening sky.
