Look closer at the pale, shingle-stacked creatures—past the frantic, feathered blur of their dorsal gills—and the reason for their arrival in a brand-new iron tower two miles in the sky begins to resolve. They are not cave-dwellers dragged upward on construction boots, nor oceanic strays. They are creatures of the upper cloud- deck itself, things that spent ten thousand generations drifting as microscopic, desiccated cysts in the thin air above the mountain ranges, waiting to strike the rare, roaring thermal updraft of an active fumarole or high-altitude ice-geyser. When the condenser turbines began drawing millions of cubic feet of raw stratosphere through the cooling matrices, it acted as a sieve for half a hemisphere of sky. Everything that was sleeping in the high wind was caught, wetted, warmed by half a degree, and sluiced into this iron rib-pocket. 

In the wild, their lives were brief, frantic sprints before a volcanic vent choked off or a summer melt refroze. Here, inside a governed machine, they have stumbled into eternity, and their bodies have begun to warp under the weight of a spring that neither warms into summer nor hardens into ice.

The pale grazers—if you pry one loose from the blue-grey mill-scale with a putty knife, which takes forty pounds of pull and leaves a crisp, wet *pop* you feel in your knuckles rather than hear—are barely individuals anymore. Through their waxy, semi-translucent backs, you can see no eyes and very little gut, only a broad, pulsing vascular ring dark with copper-based blood, and beneath it, a pale, branching lattice of cartilage that mimics the exact geometry of the stamped steel beneath them. Those clinging over rivet-heads have grown domed, dimpled undersides that fit the factory-hammered steel like custom prosthetic sockets. Those wedged along the sheared edges of the sheet-metal plates have developed calloused, grooved lips along their mantles that ride the razor-bright steel without ever being cut, using the sharp edge as a rail to guide their slow, millimeter-by-millimeter lateral shuffles.

They do not pump their own blood. They don't need to. If you watch the faint turquoise flush beneath their skins under the amber glow of the gauge lamps, you realize their hearts are completely Slack. The colossal, locked-pitch roar of the reduction gear four feet behind the eastern bulkhead—vibrating the steel walls at an unchanging mechanical frequency—shakes their bodies so thoroughly that their circulatory fluid is driven entirely by acoustic resonance. Tiny one-way valves in their veins simply let the vibration of the tower hammer the blood forward. If you carried one down the service ladders into the quiet rooms below, it would suffocate within minutes, its blood pooling stagnant in a body that has forgotten how to beat for itself.

Their mucus is the mortar of the room. Up near the overhead drive-shaft collars, where the fresh whale-oil and tallow grease weeps microscopic droplets into the condensing spray, the largest of the dinner-plate grazers crowd in heavy, trembling heaps. They filter the rendered animal fats out of the freezing mist and metabolize them into a clear, dense glycerol slime that acts as biological antifreeze. It coats their flanks and sheets down the walls, thick as egg-white, glazing the bright steel and braided copper straps. That is why the sheared metal stays bright as a newly minted coin: oxygen cannot touch the iron. The whole chamber is pickled in living grease-mucus.

Within that slick, sub-zero glaze live the brittle-worms, though calling them worms misses the metallic strangeness of their build. They are segmented like centipedes, flat-bodied so they can slip between the overlapping, mica-like margins of the grazers without breaking the larger creatures' suction. Their jaws—that dull, tarnished copper color—are actually mineralized with raw copper leached from the braided grounding straps along the floor-beams. Where the straps pass beneath the flooded diamond-plate troughs, the outer strands of wire are subtly frayed and thinned, chewed away atom by atom by worm-radulae to harden their mouthparts. 

The worms move with a stiff, clockwork jerkiness, never curling smoothly, always snapping from angle to acute angle as they harvest the dirty outer skin of mucus where airborne rock-dust and soot collect. They have no eyes either, only long, stiff sensory bristles along their flanks tuned to the vibration of the pipes. When a grazer’s grip finally fails—usually not from age, because nothing seems to age normally here, but simply because three neighbors wedged beneath a brass pressure-valve grew half a millimeter too wide and popped the middle one loose like a watermelon seed—the worms know before the body hits the flooded floor. The falling shelf of flesh interrupts the standing acoustic wave in the water trough by a fraction of an inch. Instantly, from beneath the drainage grates and behind the flange-bolts, dark knots of worms unspool along the diamond-plate grooves, following the disrupted ripple-grid straight to the pale, upturned belly.

They take it apart with surgical neatness, shearing the soft cartilage along the grain, leaving nothing to clog the narrow slots of the drain grates—because if the drains ever clogged, the water would rise two inches, submerge the lower manifold, alter the thermal bleed of the pipes, and shatter the room's thirty-two-point-five-degree truce. Whether by blind instinct or sheer mechanical geometry, the worms keep the gutters immaculate.

Above them, suspended in the mist, the clots of heavy-winged midges aren't flying in any way an outside insect would recognize. In the violent, buffeting sound-field between the vertical brass conduits, the air itself is divided into invisible cells of high and low pressure—the acoustic nodes and antinodes of the reduction gear’s drone. A midge cannot cross the wall of an antinode; the sheer kinetic punch of the sound wave bounces it back like rubber off iron. So each soot-black sphere of five thousand insects is trapped inside a dead-calm pocket the size of a grapefruit, four or five inches across, suspended in midair between two sweating pipes.

Look right into one of those dark spheres—bringing your goggles an inch from its shivering perimeter—and you see that the midges on the outside aren't even facing inward. They hang tail-down, stubby, bristle-fringed wings blurred not for lift, just flicking condensate out of their spiracles while the acoustic pressure holds their heavy, oil-fat bodies aloft like ping-pong balls in a fountain jet. At the exact center of each suspended ball is a single, walnut-sized mass of froth—midge eggs whipped together with aerosolized tallow-mist—where pale, legless grubs hatch, feed on the unfertilized trophic eggs of their sisters, pupate, and join the hovering wall without ever touching metal or water from birth to death. When an old midge dies, its wings stop clearing water; it instantly grows heavy with beaded mist, slips through the bottom of the acoustic pocket like a stone dropping through a surface film, and lands in the grazing-trough below before it has fallen two feet.

And yet there *is* one thing in the cistern-pocket that moves across all of it, something you do not notice for the first twenty minutes because your eyes interpret it as part of the plumbing.

Threaded behind the manifold pipe, coiled tightly around the warmish brass collars of the three amber gauge lamps and extending flattened, strap-like branches along the underside of the structural tie-rods, is something the thickness of a man’s wrist and the exact pewter-grey of wet mill-scale. At first glance it looks like heavy lead caulking extruded from a joint, or an insulated cable harness wrapped in oiled canvas. Only when the dull amber filament light catches its margin do you see the slow, peristaltic ripple traveling down its length—one single wave every four or five minutes—and realize it is a single, contiguous animal, or a fused colony acting as one.

It is a creature built entirely around thermal theft and vibration dampening. Its skin is leathery, Tough, embedded with grit filtered from the cloud-intake: microscopic shards of high-altitude volcanic glass, meteoric dust, and iron filings left behind when the tower’s bolt-holes were drilled last month. Beneath its broad, ribbon-like belly, it hugs the hairline seams where the black-green iron-moss tries to grow, keeping the tiny warmth of the three carbon-filament lamps sealed beneath itself like a hand cupped over an ember.

Unlike everything else in the room, it hates the noise, or rather, it feeds on the boundary between the noise and stillness. Where its rubbery grey limbs bridge the gap between a vibrating intake pipe and a rigid structural bracket, its dense, gristled flesh acts as a living shock-absorber, soaking up the violent mechanical shear and turning that kinetic battering straight into metabolic heat. Touch it with a bare finger—if you dare slip a glove off in that freezing mist—and while the steel beside it burns with wet cold, the grey strap-flesh feels faintly, shockingly feverish, forty-five or fifty degrees, slick with warm tallow-sweat.

Because it is warm, the pale gill-grazers crowd recklessly onto its back, stacking four-deep along its ridges to bask, their feathered gills beating slower and lazier there than anywhere else in the chamber. The grey ribbon-thing lets them. Every quarter-hour or so, without haste, a fold of its pewter mantle simply rolls inward like a slow lip, engulfing three or four of the basking dinner-plate creatures whole, sealing over them without disturbing the neighbors an inch away, who immediately slide sideways to claim the newly vacated warm spot. Inside the grey strap, digested solely by heat and the grinding action of the tower’s own vibration against its swallowed grit, the grazers are dissolved into rich slurry to extend another flat, gray tendril half an inch further along a tie-rod.

It has already found the bullseye lens in the ceiling; a thin, exploratory finger of pewter flesh is currently pressed against the slushy wire-glass, testing its coldness and turning back. Another, thicker lobe has reached the eastern bulkhead—the plate directly in front of the roaring locomotive-sized reduction gear—and flattened itself there into a broad, calloused ear three feet wide, drinking the pure, punishing thunder straight through quarter-inch steel. And one very thin, pale tip has crept right up to the inner rubber gasket of the doorway hatch where you stand. It rests against the inside of the latches, completely still, waiting neither for escape nor for prey, merely measuring whether your boots on the diamond-plate have changed the hum of the iron by enough of a fraction to make you part of the machine, or debris for the grates.
