You do not see it at first because you are looking for something with a head, a back, and a distinct edge, whereas the sovereign occupant of the sphere is currently stretched across fourteen feet of the eastern bulkhead, braided so deeply into the bladder-ferns and corroded pipe-hangers that it looks like a vein of older, darker copper.

If you stare at a cluster of tarnished brass crabs for twenty minutes, however, you will notice one of the roots behind them tighten, detach from the iron, and lift into the humid air.

What emerges from the mat of dead fronds and rust-mulch is a body roughly the mass of a large greyhound, though distributed according to a completely different geometry. It has no spine—bones would ache intolerably in the ceaseless acoustic battery of the chamber, rattling in their sockets until the joints inflamed. Instead, it is built of dense, vulcanized-looking cartilage and heavy hydraulic muscle, encased in a hide like oiled chamois stained deep, oxidized plum by centuries of iron-water. Its torso is flattened, broad as a shield, hugging the curve of the hull so closely that it seems to pour rather than crawl over the rounded nubs of the rivets.

From this low, muscular hub radiate six limbs, though calling them legs or arms misses their slow, fluid utility. They are long, tapering cables of muscle, each ending not in claws—which would skitter uselessly on slick brass and wet leaves—but in wide, fleshy rosettes. When one of these pads presses against a sweating manifold or an I-beam flange, the center retracts slightly, creating a vacuum seal strong enough to hold five hundred pounds against an overhang, aided by a fringe of microscopic, velcro-like setae that grip the porous rust. 

It moves by unpeeling one limb with a wet, inaudible *pop*, extending it through the ferns with the hypnotic, unhurried grace of a compass needle finding north, and sealing it down a yard ahead. It takes ten minutes to cross from Manifold Four to Manifold Five. There is nowhere to rush to, and nothing here can hear footsteps anyway.

Its relationship with the deafening roar of the sphere is total. Along the scalloped margins of its flat body run two rows of pale, drum-tight membranes—not ears, for there is no sense in *hearing* a single eternal note, but barometric and vibrational organs of exquisite sensitivity. To this creature, the roar is not noise; it is illumination. The sound waves bouncing off the iron walls, refracting through the thirty-yard sphere of empty air, and dampening against the wet epiphytes create a standing three-dimensional hologram of pressure inside its nervous system. 

Even with its eyes closed, it feels the exact thickness of the rust on the far side of the room. It feels a plum-beetle shifting its weight sixty feet overhead because the beetle’s carapace alters a fractional harmonic in the white noise. And when one of the translucent amphibians lets go of a bracket to drift across the central void, the creature feels the tiny, silent hole the glider punches through the standing acoustic field, tracking its lazy spiral down to the millimeter.

Yet it has eyes, too—three of them, set in a low triangle near the front of its shield-like mantle. They are large, globular, and dark as polished obsidian, lacking eyelids, cleaned instead by a constant, slow wash of viscous tears that drain into grooves along its flanks. Because the greenish daylight from the upper ports is shadowless and unchanging, these eyes have lost the ability to track rapid motion, which does not exist here. Instead, they possess a microscopic, magnifying acuity. Resting motionless for three hours on a warm copper bend, the creature will watch a single bead of condensation swell on a bolt-head six inches away, observing the tiny, swirling constellations of mineral dust and bacteria inside the drop, waiting for the surface tension to fail.

It is the gardener, the predator, and the ballast of this hanging world, though none of those roles require violence. 

Watch it feed: it reaches a long, plum-colored limb into a scalding crevice behind a brass baffle where the steam-roar is fiercest, a place hot enough to blister human skin. Its rubbery hide is heavily insulated with mineral-laced fats; it feels the heat only as a pleasant, loosening warmth. With the curled lip of its rosette pad, it scrapes up a thick ribbon of thermophilic bacterial slime and rusted scale, drawing it back to a circular, puckered mouth on its underside. There, plates of dark, iron-infused keratin grind the paste silently. If a tarnished crab happens to be sitting on the slime, the creature does not strike at it; it gently pries the crab loose—waiting patiently for five minutes until the crab’s own grip tires—tucks it beneath its mantle, and crushes it slowly, absorbing the calcium and brass-tainted meat alike.

Sometimes, when the distant, unseen engine miles below the clouds shifts its seasonal output, a gauge needle creeps a fraction of a millimeter, and the pitch of the twelve manifolds drops by a quarter-tone. 

When this happens, the acoustic geometry of the room warps. The standing crosshatch on the little washbasin pool at the bottom of the hull blurs for a few seconds before snapping into a new, slightly wider geometric lattice. 

This is the only weather the creature knows, and it responds to these barometric moods with a strange, solitary ritual. Over the course of an afternoon, it climbs. Hand over hand, rosette over rosette, it ascends the curving wall, passing through the warmest equatorial belt of the manifolds, moving up where the vegetation thins and the cold of the twenty-eight-thousand-foot altitude bleeds more sharply through the iron skin. It climbs all the way to the ceiling of the dome, wedging two of its limbs into the rusted framing of one of the four circular glass ports.

Hanging upside down at the apex of the sphere, bathed in the raw, unfiltered violet-blue glare of the stratosphere, its dark plum skin flushes a brighter, bruised crimson as it dumps excess internal heat against the freezing glass. If you were standing down by the drain plug looking up across the thirty yards of empty, roaring air, you would see its broad silhouette eclipsing one of the skylights, turning the afternoon light in that quarter of the room the color of diluted wine. Its three black eyes stare outward through the dirty, wire-reinforced pane at the blinding emptiness of the upper sky—an infinite expanse without walls, without leaves, without pressure, and presumably without sound—observing it with complete, unblinking indifference.

Then, when its core has cooled by three degrees, it lets go.

Not with five limbs, and not to fall like a stone. It releases four pads at once, swinging smoothly beneath the glass, then pops the last two free with a synchronized shrug that imparts a slow, deliberate spin.

As it drops into the vast, uncluttered center of the room, it flattens itself entirely. Flaps of thick, translucent webbing—ribbed with flexible cartilage—unfurl between all six extended limbs, transforming the heavy animal into a wide, hexagonal parachute six feet across, veined with dark purple vessels. 

The rising thermal draft from the lower hull catches it ten feet above the bottom ribs. 

For a long moment, the creature arrests its own fall just above the hammered-pewter pool at the nadir of the sphere, hovering three feet over the motionless geometric water without disturbing its surface. Then, tilting two limbs by a fraction of an inch, it catches the perimeter updraft and rises again—sailing in a grand, silent, slow-motion banking turn around the curve of the jungle, five yards out from the leaves. 

Translucent little gliders drift out of its path like dust motes pushed aside by a ship’s bow. The deafening white noise of the steam presses up into the drum-tight membranes of its belly, holding it aloft as surely as the hot air does. It makes two full revolutions of the church-sized void, checking its territory—feeling the moisture content of every fern bank, sensing the slow digestive pulse of a smaller, younger version of itself tucked deep inside a hollow I-beam near the southern cable-mount, mapping the slow creep of rust that will eventually, centuries from now, open a pinhole to the freezing sky.

Satisfied that the world is still round, warm, and whole, it folds one forward limb, spills the air from under its mantle, and drifts softly against a weeping copper pipe, sealing itself back onto the iron with a silent kiss of flesh, already half-indistinguishable from the creepers before its skin has finished wrinkling.
