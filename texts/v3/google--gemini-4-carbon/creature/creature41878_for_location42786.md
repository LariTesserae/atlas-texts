To see what lives in the blister, you have to stop waiting for a door to open on one of the ceramic plazas. 

The balconies, the recessed porticoes, the sunken courtyards no wider than a thumbnail—none of these were built to accommodate shoulders, or feet, or eyes that look out at a view. If you brought a magnifying lens up to the matte-gray tabletops, you would find no benches, no lintels worn smooth by passing sleeves, no stairs scaled to a stride. The architecture looks civic only because human eyes cannot help reading grids, tiers, and viaducts as places to walk. In truth, the districts are radiator fins, wave-guides, and resonant cavities. They are the bones of the inhabitants, worn on the outside and shared in common, the way a coral head is shared by polyps—except there are no polyps tucked into the holes.

Watch the cyan flickers instead. Look closely at the places where the amber thoroughfares meet the gray ceramic, especially during the thirty-second squalls when the hot rain sheets down from the apex and curves around the metric shells.

Just inside the dry envelope of a district, where the air is completely still and stripped of humidity, there is a faint, localized shimmer—not heat-haze, which rises sloppy and turbulent from the floor, but something crisp, faceted, and deliberate. It looks at first like a tiny flaw in the refractive index of the air, the size of a grain of rice, hovering a millimeter above a cantilevered terrace. Then it splits into three, drops down a needle-spire faster than the eye can follow, and reappears across a sunken plaza as a pale, violet-white pinch of light so brief it barely registers on the retina.

They are topological knots—self-sustaining braids of electromagnetic field and trapped phonon resonance, bound together by the same altered physics that keeps the stratosphere from sucking the room dry. They have no mass to speak of, no membranes, no chemistry. If the metric distortions that hold the city aloft were to fail for half a microsecond, every resident of the municipality would untie itself at the speed of light and vanish into waste heat.

Yet they are unmistakably *someone*, and they are very busy.

Because they have no bodies to insulate them, their entire existence is an intimacy with gradient and geometry. A single knot—call it a citizen, though it is closer in structure to a standing wave with a memory—cannot survive in open, unstructured space. Left out in the wet, turbulent middle of the cavern, the thermal noise of the steam would scramble its phase relationships in milliseconds, the way surf tears apart a smoke ring. They need the matte-gray ceramic the way an echo needs a canyon. The thousands of tiny porticoes and windowless basilicas are acoustic and photonic baffles, milled to sub-micron tolerances so that a resident can slip inside a colonnade and fold itself into a stable, low-energy sleep, or step out onto a balcony to couple with a passing neighbor.

When two of them meet on a plaza, they do not stop and face each other. They pass straight through one another’s volume, and in the overlap—which lasts perhaps four billionths of a second—interference patterns bloom between them. To an outside observer, it looks only like a microscopic spark of frost-blue static against the gray stone. Within the frame of reference of the inhabitants, where subjective time runs at the clock-speed of light bouncing between ceramic walls a millimeter apart, that four-nanosecond intersection is an afternoon. They trade state-vectors, adjust each other’s drift, argue, align, and part, one shooting off along an amber coherent-light beam toward a neighboring slab three meters away, the other dropping down an internal shaft to shed excess entropy into the ceramic sink.

This is why the cavern feels so empty, and why the sound dies in the air. A human second is geological to them. Between one drip of mineral-sour condensate letting go of an obsidian tooth at the ceiling and its impact on the scree ninety meters below, three or four generations of civic thought have risen, reorganized the traffic patterns of the lower districts, composed vast, silent symmetries across fifty floating blocks, and archived the results. 

To them, the cavern is not a restless room; it is a monumentally slow astronomical environment. 

From the perspective of a knot poised on the rim of an upper-tier viaduct, the world outside its dry metric shell is frozen in amber. A falling raindrop hangs overhead like an immense, trembling moon of sour glass, taking ages to drift down past the curve of local gravity, its surface crawling with sluggish, dull-red reflections of the magma throat far below. The three-minute cycle of the steam belch is a Great Year—a long, predictable climate epoch. When the superheated plume begins to rise from the cleft in the floor, the elders and infrastructure-choruses of the municipality watch it coming the way planetary astronomers watch a glacial maximum approach. 

That silent, half-meter slide of a floating neighborhood—the tilt on unseen gimbals as the heat-haze washes past—is not an automated reflex of dead machinery. It is a collective helm-maneuver, debated and executed by tens of thousands of braided minds leaning their combined field-pressure against the metric anchors. They tilt the basilicas to catch the updraft’s kinetic sheer, drinking the mechanical energy through the sound-hungry ceramic, converting the cavern’s violence into the crisp, coherent cyan and amber light they eat and breathe. Even the sharp *tick* of the young volcanic walls cooling by a fraction of a millimeter does not go wasted; before the sound wave can cross the ninety-meter chamber, the outer districts have already leaned into it, swallowed its vibration, and digested the crack of the rock into three more hours of municipal street-lighting.

And the bismuth-like tarnish on the lower walls—the iridescent step-pyramids assembling and disassembling in the fierce heat near the floor—is neither an accident nor an independent organism. 

It is their quarry, and their midden, and their slow, blind livestock.

Every so often, when a ceramic block shows the faintest atomic pitting from stray sour-mist or cosmic rays bleeding through the violet sky-window overhead, a work-detail detaches from the lowest district. Five or six knots braid themselves into a single, tighter, heavier configuration—something dense enough to survive for a few whole seconds outside the metric envelopes, glowing a hard, stressed magenta as the wet heat batters it. Riding one of the downward cataracts of deflected rain, this composite swimmer plunges toward the scorching tachylite at the base of the chamber. Just before the water flashes to steam against the rock, the knot leaps into the iridescent crust.

For minutes afterward, that patch of the wall goes frantic. Step-pyramids collapse in shimmering cascades; hopper-crystals of metallic tarnish race sideways across the black glass, sorting trace elements out of the volcanic sweat—bismuth, tellurium, stray silicates, heavy metals leached from the young porphyry. When the next thermal plume belches upward from the throat, a microscopic puff of refined, metallic dust lifts off the tarnish, riding the updraft. Waiting districts forty meters above tilt their sunken plazas like open palms, drop their metric shields for a curated millisecond along a hairline seam, and catch the rising motes, sintering them straight into worn colonnades and frayed wave-guides.

Nothing is left over. Nothing accumulates. That is why the avenues remain immaculate.

Yet not all of them stay down in the steam-harvesting middle of the room. If you stand on the narrow basalt shelf long enough for your eyes to fully adjust to the gloom, and look up past the highest ceramic slabs toward the jagged doorway at the apex—where the drum-skin of altered physics holds back the freezing stratosphere—you will see a solitary district hanging higher than all the rest.

It is smaller than a dinner plate, stripped of basilicas and deep arcades, shaped instead like a flat, dark terrace with a single needle-thin spire pointing straight at the rent in the peak. The rain starts below it, so this highest block never gets wet, though its underside is constantly frosted with hoarfrost that sublimates and reforms in silent pulses. Very few amber thoroughfares connect it to the metropolis below; traffic goes up to it rarely, and comes down rarer still.

On that terrace gather the oldest knots—those whose internal braids have grown so dense with accumulated phase-memory, so intricate and tightly wound after subjective millennia of passing through their neighbors, that they have begun to run hot against the gray ceramic, too complex for the narrow hallways of the lower town. They climb the thin amber thread to the cold terrace beneath the window and spread themselves out across its flat, matte surface, letting the bitter radiative sink of near-space cool their loops.

There they sit, arranged along the rim of the little plate like faint, stationary beads of ultraviolet dew, looking up through the invisible membrane at the bruised violet-black sky. Down below, the young rock ticks, the magma throat breathes its slow epochs of steam, and the bright, frantic municipality tilts and harvests in the dark. Up on the high terrace, the heavy ones do almost nothing at all. They hold themselves utterly still against the pull of the stars and the shrunken glare of the sun, measuring the glacial turn of the planet beneath them by the crawl of hard, unblinking light across a doorway three paces wide—wondering, at a speed too fast for stone to ever hear, what sort of architecture could possibly hold them out there in all that terrible, unbaffled emptiness.
