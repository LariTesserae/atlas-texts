At first you miss her because you are looking for an animal, and everything in the basin has given up the habits of animals in favor of the habits of hardware. 

Where the curved wall meets the rolled lip overhead—just beneath the overhang, out of the direct fall of the plain sky—there is a heavier thickening in the wood, a smooth, horizontal shelf no wider than a butcher’s block. Stretched along it, curved to match the arc of the chamber, lies what looks like a thick, copper-sheathed cable or a secondary root-burl, four feet long and broad as a man’s thigh. 

Then a heavy drop of condensation detaches from the rim above, strikes the middle of that dark cylinder, and instead of splashing, rolls off a coat of dense, greased pelage. Near the forward end, a flat, broad head lifts half an inch, breaks contact with the wood with a faint, wet *pop* of suction, and settles back down two millimeters to the left.

If her ancestors were river otters or giant marsh-minks out on the sodden flats, the pocket has spent fifty generations planing the frantic mammal out of her, just as the warm seepage has planed the knots from the rhizomes. She has no neck to speak of; head, barrel-chest, and heavy, rudder-like tail form one continuous, hydrodynamic taper, flattened on the underside like a smoothing iron. Her fur is very short, stiff as wire brushes, and permanently slick with a heavy sebaceous oil dark with metallic salts. Where the plain afternoon light catches her flank, she does not gleam like wet fur; she has the dull, pewter sheen of graphited cast iron.

For forty minutes at a stretch, she does not breathe—or rather, she breathes so shallowly, timed so precisely to the fifty-hertz shudder of the timber beneath her ribs, that her sides never visibly heave. Her metabolism has outsourced its furnace. Mammals spend their lives frantically burning meat to keep their blood near a hundred degrees; she simply lies flat against the conductive vein of the shelf, letting the waste heat of the deep turbines and the low, steady trickle of stray earth-current keep her core warm. She has the slow, unbothered heart rate of a crocodile, though her blood runs thick and dark red.

Watch her face, and you see how the noise lives inside her. She has no external ears—only tight, valved slits flush with the skull, sealed against the boiler-room roar that would deafen anything born outside the lip. Her eyes are small, set wide, and filmed over by tough,amber nictitating membranes that never fully retract; in this plain, shadowless chamber where nothing ever lunges from cover, sharp sight is an expensive luxury she no longer pays for. Instead, her broad muzzle is bristled with forty or fifty remarkably thick vibrissae, not white hair, but stiff, flexible filaments plated in the same dull zinc-copper amalgam as the beetle shells. 

When she finally moves, she does not scramble or slink. She slides. 

She eases down the vertical curve of the wall headfirst, belly pressed flat to the weeping wood, descending on the film of warm runoff like a well-oiled piston in a sleeve. Her legs are short, set almost laterally like a lizard’s, ending in wide, calloused pads completely devoid of claws—claws would gouge the metallic plating of the bark, break the circuits, invite rot. Instead, the soles of her feet are ridged like plumber’s wrenches, gripping the slick, buzzing timber by sheer friction and micro-suction. 

As she passes a vertical seam of gunmetal beetles, not one of them breaks formation or snaps its wing-cases shut. Her body carries the exact ambient charge of the wall; to the beetles, she is not an intruder, only a moving fluctuation in the ground-state. As her shoulder brushes within a millimeter of the line, three blue sparks jump in neat succession from the beetles' antennae to the stiff metallic whiskers along her jaw. Her upper lip twitches—not in pain, not even in startlement, just the crisp, involuntary *tick* of a voltmeter needle registering a healthy load.

She reaches the bowled floor and moves among the saucer-sized frogs. Here, you realize what she is for—or at least, what role the pocket has smoothed her into playing. 

No engine runs forever without throwing slag. The warm groundwater constantly brings up dissolved minerals, and while the fused roots plate themselves in it, the intake bulbs have a harder time. Over days, the calloused rims of those fist-sized valves accumulate a pale, crusty scale of precipitated lime, iron oxide, and dead biofilm. Left alone, a valve’s snap would grow sluggish; its seal would weep; the water level in the dish would rise half an inch, drowning the lower tiers of beetles, shorting the copper veins, throwing the whole gridded geometry of the parlor into chaos.

She stops beside the nearest intake bulb just as it hits full capacity. *Clack-hiss.* Warm vapor jets directly into her chest; she doesn't blink. Before the valve can begin its next slow yawn open, she lowers her heavy jaw to its rim. 

Her incisors are gone, replaced front and back by a single, continuous bevel of dark, iron-hardened enamel—orange-black, like a beaver’s, but wide and flat as a machinist’s scraper. With three short, deliberate strokes, timed precisely between the fifty-hertz pulses of the floor so the vibration acts like an ultrasonic scaler, she planes the crusty mineral buildup clean off the living lip of the valve. She swallows the calcareous grit alongside a mouthful of warm water. It will sit in her muscular, gizzard-like stomach, grinding down the heavy shells of what she eats next.

She does not hunt the frogs, and she does not sweep the walls of beetles. That would leave gaps. Instead, she slides slowly across the center of the dish, sweeping her copper-whiskered chin a fraction of an inch above the water-sheen, reading the electrical grid of the room. 

Every living thing here acts as a resistor, drawing current and shedding heat. When every frog and every beetle is healthy, the voltage across the floor is a flat, symmetrical hum. Wait long enough, however, and age catches up to one of the components. 

Halfway up the eastern curve, four feet off the floor, a single gunmetal beetle has begun to fail. Its wing-fanning has dropped out of the dynamo whine by half a kilohertz; its internal resistance is spiking, turning it into a tiny hot-spot on the bark vein, drawing slightly too much current from its neighbors a thumb’s-width away. Or down on the floor, an old amphibian’s skin has begun to lose its mucous conductivity, its heart fluttering out of phase with the turbine tremor below, creating a faint eddy in the electromagnetic field no larger than a teacup.

Today, it is a frog near the northern curve. To your eye, in the plain afternoon light, it looks identical to the thirty others: flat, gray-green, motionless, two and a half feet from its neighbors. 

She does not stalk it. She glides straight down the shallow grade of the dish, parting the warm sheet of water smoothly around her broad chest, and stops nose-to-nose with the failing animal. Her metallic whiskers fan out, framing its saucer-wide body without touching it, tasting the sour, erratic sputter of its fading bio-electric field. The frog does not leap. Nothing in this hollow has leapt in three hundred years; there is nowhere to leap *to*. 

Her jaws open—surprisingly wide, hinged far back like a trapdoor—slide under the frog's flat belly, and close with the exact, unhurried *clack* of the root-valves. One heavy crunch of her molar plates, muffled entirely by the boiler-room roar of the floor, and she swallows it whole. 

She does not lick her chops. She does not look around for another. Instead, she settles her flat, heavy underside directly onto the bare circle of humming wood the frog just vacated. 

For ten minutes, twenty minutes, an hour, she stays right there, two and a half feet from the nearest amphibian on the left, two and a half feet from the nearest on the right, holding the spacing of the grid intact with her own body so the stray current across the floor doesn't arc or pool unevenly. Deep in the wet dark beneath the root-pan, where the juvenile stages wait in flooded, vibrating galleries, a new flat-bodied frog—freshly metamorphosed, skin bright with new copper—is already squeezing up through a wide lateral pore near the wall's base, drawn automatically toward the only cool, unoccupied coordinate in the dish.

When her whiskers register the juvenile sliding into the edge of the territory, she breaks suction with the floor, turns with the slow, oiled radius of a barge in a canal, and climbs the vertical timber back toward her shelf beneath the lip. 

Halfway up, she pauses along a bare stretch of green-black wood between two columns of whining beetles, opens her mouth, and coughs up a single, compact pellet no bigger than a walnut—every indigestible trace of copper scale, chitin, and bone from the past week, bound tight in insoluble grease. It drops cleanly into an open intake bulb just before the calloused valve snaps shut—*clack-hiss*—shooting the pellet straight down into the pressurized, subterranean dark to be carried miles away by the deep industrial raceways. Nothing rots here.

Back on her narrow shelf beneath the overhang, she tucks her broad muzzle over her tail, completing her own small, closed circuit. A bead of clear water gathers on the heavy wooden lip above, swells until its weight overcomes its tension, and drops squarely onto the center of her pewter-dark back, splitting evenly down both sides to rejoin the warm, sheeting floor below. She does not twitch. In the clear, plain light, she is once again indistinguishable from a thickened root-vein—warm, deaf, heavy with metal, holding the tolerance of the room to within a thousandth of an inch.
