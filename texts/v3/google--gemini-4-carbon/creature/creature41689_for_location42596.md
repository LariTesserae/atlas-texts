You hear them before you separate them from the foliage, and you smell them—woodsmoke, tallow, crushed cedar, and hot wet wool—before you realize the bundled shape crouching on an overhead flume is a person and not a burl on the alder trunk.

They are built like river-stones: low-centered, barrel-chested, thick through the calf and wrist, with a heavy, rolling gait adapted to surfaces that tilt, bounce, and weep beneath their weight. Nobody walks with locked knees here. From the time a toddler is unstrapped from a cradle-board and let loose into the wicker crawl-tunnels alongside the rock-cavies, they learn to move with a primate’s four-handedness. An adult carrying a sloshing tub of offal up a notched log-stair does not climb stiffly; they flow upward, free hand slapping wet wood, bare or soft-shod toes curling around the mossy notches, hips swinging wide to counter the sway of a willow-cable bridge when a gust hits the hollow.

Look at their hands and feet first, because the damp writes its plainest history there. Their knuckles are wide, permanently flushed rose-red or plum-purple by the chill, crosshatched with fine white scars from flint edges, fish-spines, briar-thorns, and otter nips. Beneath thick, horn-hard nails, there is a permanent crescent stain of dark green and brown—alder tannin, bog-peat, and bruised liverwort that no amount of snowmelt will ever scrub out. Down in the bog-tiers, the goat-herders and char-trappers often go barefoot from spring thaw until the hard rim-freezes, claiming boots only hold the cold water against the skin to rot it; their soles are thick as cured hide, splayed wide like paddles, and greased every morning before the hearth with rancid mutton-tallow mixed with pine pitch and wood-ash until the skin shines dull black and sheds water like a duck’s back. Higher up in the windy canopy, people wear short, calf-high moccasins of smoked char-skin or oiled goat-hide, lashed tight around the ankle with braided sedge-cord, studded under the arch with rough copper hobnails or sharp chips of flint to bite into slick liverwort.

Their clothing makes them look twice as broad as they are, turning every person on the catwalks into a rustling, dripping haystack. Everything is layered to shed mist and trap body heat without rotting in a week. Next to the skin goes soft, greasy under-fleece sheared from the bellies of water-goats and spun with the shed down of rock-cavies—never washed with lye, so it keeps all its natural lanolin. Over that go tunics of pounded cedar-bark fiber and felted wool, dyed in muted, boggy colors that bleed into one another: rust-orange from alder bark, mustard-yellow from roof-lichens, slate-black from iron-rich peat mud. And over everything goes the rain-gear: tiered, overlapping capes of tufted sedge-grass that rustle loudly when they turn their shoulders, or short, stiff ponchos of bladder-skin and oiled leather smoked over green juniper until they smell like cured ham and turn brown and translucent as amber. 

Because a hood blocks your peripheral vision—and losing sight of a swinging boom, a diving hawk, or a slick gap in the planking means a thirty-foot plunge into the bog-pools—almost nobody wears hoods. Instead, they wear broad, conical hats woven from split spruce-root and waterproofed with spruce-gum, strapped firmly under the chin. The brims are wide enough to shoot cascade-water clear of their collars, and the crowns are Inevitably colonized by stray moss spores or tucked full of useful clutter: bone awls, copper fish-hooks, spare rawhide thongs, bright jay-feathers, and sprigs of wild mountain mint crushed periodically under the nose when the wind blows a ribbon of sour goat-muck up from the lower hurdles.

Wait until the cloud-rack parts and that pale-gold sideways light hits a crowded deck, and their faces leap out in sharp relief. They have wind-watered eyes—narrowed at the outer corners in a permanent squint against blowing spray, quick-darting, missing nothing—and skin weathered to a tight, ruddy sheen across high cheekbones. Hair is universally worn greased and tightly bound; loose hair snags in briar-parapets and whips across the eyes in a gale. Men and women alike plait it with strips of dyed eel-skin into thick clubs or coil it tight against the nape with carved bone pins. 

You will notice almost immediately that nobody speaks at a conversational murmur. The hollow eats soft sounds. Between the hydraulic roar of the flumes and the clatter of tipping-buckets, a quiet voice vanishes two paces from the lips. Yet they do not scream themselves hoarse; over generations, they have developed a sharp, percussive register pitched precisely to cut *over* the low rumble of water and *under* the shrill hysteria of the jays and cavies. Their speech is clipped, rhythmic, packed with hard consonants and sudden glottal stops, thrown forward from the diaphragm like a stone skipped across a pond. 

More striking still is their whistling. Half the civic governance of the slump happens without words at all. Two knuckles jammed between the teeth, or just a curled wet lip, produces a piercing, two-note *wheep-wheep!* that slices straight through forty yards of blowing mist. It is a full grammar: a rising trill warns the lower tiers that a jammed flume is about to be kicked clear and forty gallons of dirty water are coming down; a staccato three-beat rattle tells a herding dog to pin a stray buck on the third ramp; a long, dropping slide calls the children home from the rim-crags before a squall hits. Because hands are nearly always full of wet rope, slippery fish, or peeled hazel poles, they accompany these whistles with big, angular arm-signals—elbows out, palms flashing white—so a conversation between an arbor-grafter high in the cloud-cedars and a copper-smith down by the bellows-wheel looks like a brisk, semaphore dance accompanied by bird-calls.

They have no concept of personal space, and equally little concept of species boundaries. Privacy does not exist when your bedroom floor is a woven living-root cradle slung six feet above your neighbor’s smoky hearth-niche, and when a drafty wall of turf and pleated alder-boughs is all that separates your stew-pot from a nest of muttering mountainfowl. To live here without going mad requires a particular kind of porous, unbothered temperament. If someone bumps you hard on a narrow catwalk to let a laden water-goat splash past, nobody apologizes; they grab your belt-strap so neither of you goes over the slick edge, slap your oiled shoulder to show they’ve got your weight, and keep moving. 

That same rough, unsentimental intimacy extends to the animals. They do not keep pets, and they do not view wildlife as pests; everything with warm blood is simply another noisy, thieving, useful citizen of the tangle. Watch an old woman sitting cross-legged under a hide awning, knapping flint scrapers over a leather apron. A semi-tame otter slithers up onto the wet planks beside her, hissing for a scrap of dried char; without looking up from her pressure-flaker, she shoves its wet snout firmly aside with her bare heel—the exact same gesture she uses ten seconds later to keep a crawling grandchild from grabbing a sharp core—then tosses a strip of fish-skin over her shoulder for the beast to catch. Men walk the upper rigging with lean, yellow-eyed fishing-weasels draped around their necks inside their sedge-capes for warmth, letting the creatures dart down their sleeves to snap up cavern-spiders or chase mice out of the berry-racks. Even the thieving corvids know individuals by sight; a notorious nest-robber will draw rocks from every child in the basin, while an old arbor-warden who tosses bruised trout-guts onto the roof-turf will walk everywhere under a noisy, protective halo of black wings.

Their work is never finished, because the town itself is trying to digest and drown itself every hour of the day. There are no idle specialists here, though there are deep, fiercely guarded crafts. 

The quietest—relatively speaking—are the **bough-pleaters**, usually older men and women whose knees have stiffened too much for chasing goats in the lower bog, though their grip remains iron-strong. They move slowly through the structural bones of the town with pouches of grafting-clay, wet sphagnum moss, and rolls of rawhide binding. They think in decades. When a family grows, they don't haul lumber; a pleater bends three young alder shoots down from an upper terrace, scrapes the green cambium with a tiny copper knife, binds them tight into the root-arch of a lower cloud-cedar, and packs the wound in peat. Ten years later, that living joint will hold the weight of a hearth-platform and six sleeping bodies. They walk the catwalks tapping trunks with horn mallets, listening through the soles of their feet for the hollow thud that means core-rot has crept up from the drowned floor ten feet below, deciding which ancient branches must be culled for firewood and which new sprouts must be coaxed sideways to take the strain.

Opposite them in temperament are the **flume-runners**—mostly lean, reckless teenagers and sharp-eyed twenty-somethings who treat the whole three-hundred-pace amphitheater as a vertical rigging-course. Armed with long, hook-tipped hazel poles and wooden mallets slung from their belts, they are the town’s circulatory surgeons. Water weighs too much to ignore; let an overhead chute clog with shed feathers and wind-snapped twigs during a heavy cloudburst, and within four minutes the backed-up torrent will tear a whole family’s roof-terrace out of the branches and drop it onto three houses below. So the runners live on the slick outsides of the troughs, sliding down braided willow-cables on Thick leather palm-guards, kicking jams loose in explosions of cold spray, wedging cedar shims into leaking joints, and shifting wooden diverter-gates with a heavy *clack* whenever a lower grindstone needs power or a smoky forge-bellows needs its tipping-bucket sped up.

Inside the cramped, low-ceilinged hearth-niches—where floors are paved with flat river-slates laid thick over beds of clay and wet sand so the living tree beneath won't catch fire—people crouch out of the wind in dim, amber-lit warmth that smells fiercely of damp alder smoke, bubbling fish-broth, and drying socks. Nobody stands fully upright indoors; ceilings are low to hold the heat, hung so thick with curing sausages, bundles of willow-withes, bladder-bags of rendered fat, wicker cavy-cages, and drying nets that moving across a room means ducking and parting a curtain of provisions. 

Here they eat constantly, burning calories against the wet mountain chill: wooden bowls of thick, peppery char-and-cavy stew thickened with pounded fern-rhizomes and sour bog-cress; flat cakes of traded lowland barley toasted on hot stones and smeared thick with rank, salty water-goat butter; handfuls of tart cloudberries preserved in jars of cold spring-water. Tools are small, personal, and endlessly mended. Copper is precious—traded up from the valleys far below for bundles of prime otter-pelts, smoked mountainfowl, and rare cloud-cedar burl—so every scrap of green-patinated metal is cold-hammered, riveted, and re-forged on stone anvils driven by the rhythmic *thump-slosh* of the water-hammers outside until a knife blade is worn down to a sliver no bigger than a willow leaf.

Nothing about their lives is clean, dry, monumental, or permanent. When someone dies, they are not buried in the flooded root-mat below—nothing stays down there except waterlogged rot—nor burned, since dry fuel is far too dear to waste on a corpse. Instead, wrapped in a tight shroud of woven sedge and greased wool, they are carried up out of the horseshoe entirely, hoisted over the upper rocky rim onto the wind-scoured crags above the cloud-snag, and left to the clean air and the broad-winged hawks they spent their lives shouting at. 

Down inside the basin, meanwhile, before the mourners have even climbed back down the slippery rim-ladders into the roar of the water, someone is already re-tying the willow-cables on the dead person’s terrace, scraping fresh liverwort off the door-sill, shooing three slate-gray hens off the cold hearth-slates, and turning the wooden diverter-paddle back into the stream so the tipping-bucket fills, groans, tips, and starts beating the town’s wet pulse all over again.
