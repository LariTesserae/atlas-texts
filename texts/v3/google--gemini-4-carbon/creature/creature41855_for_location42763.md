At first, you would mistake it for a trickle of spilled electrolyte, or a loose ribbon of copper-plated bark peeling away from a distillation column—until you realized that nothing here peels, and nothing spills.

If you shone a lamp on it—bringing the first harsh, broad-spectrum beam of white light this basin has ever known—you still might not recognize it as an inhabitant. It looks like a repair in progress, without a repairman. Stretched across the gap between a zinc-wood anode and a sharp, jutting fin of black silicate is a flat, gleaming sheet of metallic mucilage, no thicker than heavy foil and perhaps a yard across. Through its translucent, amber-tinted skin, fine hairs of precipitated silver and copper arrange and rearrange themselves like iron filings over a shifting magnet. 

Nothing with a heartbeat could survive here; there is no free oxygen to speak of, no sugars left unpolymerized, and no soft tissue that wouldn't be pickled by the acetic pools or sliced open on the unweathered glass of the bedrock. What lives here did not crawl in from outside. It precipitated out of the system itself, born in the narrow, twitching margins where the equilibrium almost fails.

For equilibrium, across a continent of young crust, is never quite effortless. Beneath the fifty-foot mat of vulcanized root, the planet is still cooling and settling. Once every few days, somewhere in the thousand-mile dark, a slab of basalt slips a millimeter along a fault. The sharp rock shears through a root-cable. A galvanic basin tilts, spilling twenty gallons of acidic broth onto a hot, pressurized hydrocarbon stalk below. A sudden potential difference of four hundred volts flares across a newly opened cleft.

Left alone, these tiny ruptures would cascade. Short circuits would char the rubber casings into brittle coke; leaked light-ends from the distillation columns would vaporize into explosive pockets; metal ions would plate out into dead, solid bridges that choked off the cells. The climax forest would develop necrotic patches, dead zones of slag and cold tar.

Instead, within minutes of a fault-slip, the humming floor around the break changes pitch, and the *shunts* arrive.

They do not walk, and they have no fixed anatomy. A shunt is an electro-active plasmodium—a continuous, non-cellular sheet of viscoelastic hydrogel, bound together by long-chain organometallic polymers and saturated with suspended metallic micro-crystals. At rest, when the local currents are balanced, it does not even exist as a discrete body. It lies dispersed along the undersides of the mid-canopy baffles, drawn out into threads as fine as spider-silk, indistinguishable from the dangling copper filaments of the flora. In this dormant state, it simply rides the ambient trickle of electricity, keeping its internal chemistry poised just above the threshold of coagulation.

When a rock-slip severs a root-cable and sends a jagged spike of stray current tearing through the wet mat, the altered magnetic field tugs directly at the iron-nickel inclusions suspended in those dispersed threads. They drop from the baffles like strings of warm solder. Where they touch on the rubbery floor, their membranes fuse without a seam. Ten thousand hair-thin droplets run together down the voltage gradient, pooling and thickening until a single, cohesive mantle of dark, iridescent grease is gliding silently through the labyrinth of stalks.

It moves by electro-osmosis and magnetohydrodynamic creep. By selectively opening ion channels across its lower surface and aligning its internal metallic needles against the immense, steady magnetic fields of the root-cables below, it pulls itself forward without muscle, bone, or friction-grip. When it encounters one of the razor-sharp blades of black silicate that jut through the floor, it does not detour. It flows straight over the edge. As the glass slices cleanly through its body, the shunt simply parts around the stone like heavy oil around a knife blade and seals itself instantaneously on the far side, leaving not so much as a smear behind.

Watch one reach a ruptured galvanic vat. 

In the cold, pin-prick blue light of a static arc, the creature—now the size of a heavy coat, glistening like wet graphite—flows up the fluted rubber casing of the damaged plant. It drapes itself directly across the sizzling gap where the current is arcing. The pale-violet corona vanishes at once, swallowed into the shunt's body. 

For an hour, or ten hours, there is no motion at all, save for a slow, rhythmic rippling across its back—standing Faraday waves driven by the hum of the plain. Inside its gel, the creature is feeding. It has made itself the path of least resistance. As the raw amperage of the surrounding forest courses through its flesh, the shunt taps a fraction of a volt off the stream, using the electrical pressure to drive its own quiet, cold synthesis—stitching dissolved carbon, acetic acid, and trace metals into fresh, cross-linked polymer gel. 

At the same time, it cleans the wound. With extruded pseudopods as fine as hypodermics, it drinks the spilled electrolyte from the crevices of the rock and carries it back up into the tilted leaf-cup. Where the rubber bark of a distillation column has been gashed by shifting basalt, oozing warm, volatile naphtha into the dark, the shunt presses a specialized patch of its own belly against the leak. It floods that section of itself with sulfur scavenged from the deep roots, deliberately vulcanizing its own flesh into a tough, black, permanent tire-patch, and then detaches from the dead scab, leaving the column sealed. Where a copper-organic root has been sheared in two, the shunt stretches itself between the severed ends, aligns its internal silver and copper grains into a dense, conductive highway, and slowly calcifies and plates its core until a new, solid metallic cable bridges the fault—at which point the living jelly strips itself off the wire it just forged and slips back into the gloom.

It is an immune system that believes it is a parasite. Or a parasite that has forgotten how to do harm.

Yet if you stay very still in this darkness—not for an hour, when all you notice is the drip of distillate, and not for a day, when you might witness the silent mending of a root, but long enough for your mind to adjust to the glacial clock of a world without wind or sun—you begin to realize that the shunts are not merely small, localized patches of living solder. 

Because they fuse whenever they meet, and divide whenever a task is done, there is no true boundary between one shunt and the next. And because they budge and flow along magnetic field lines and voltage drops, they are exquisitely sensitive to the electrical topology of the entire continent.

High in the mid-tier, just below the serrated cooling fins where the rising vapor turns back into warm rain, there are places where dozens of shunts have gathered without any injury to mend. There is no broken rock there, no leaking sap, no sizzling arc. Instead, they hang between the dangling copper filaments like vast, gossamer curtains of dark liquid metal, millimeters thick and spanning fifty yards of cramped, rubber-pillared space. 

They touch the anode-filaments of one tree-circuit with one edge, and the cathode-mats of a distant, rival root-graft with another. Across their broad, taut membranes, tiny, controlled ripples of impedance travel back and forth at fifty centimeters per second. By thickening here and thinning there, by clumping their internal magnetite needles into transient coils and opening microscopic insulated pores in their gel, these hanging curtains act as variable resistors, diodes, and logic gates. 

They are modulating the hum.

A thousand miles away, a geothermal vent beneath the young crust cools by half a degree, slightly dropping the thermoelectric output of ten thousand square miles of flora. Left solely to the blind physics of the plants, the surrounding capacitor-plain would rush current inward too fast, overheating the boundary roots and boiling the shallow vats dry. Long before that surge can build, a faint phase-shift ripples through the subterranean magnetic field. Here, a thousand miles away in the pitch dark, the hanging curtains shiver. Their internal silver-hairs pivot ninety degrees in unison. Resistance rises across a million dangling mid-canopy bridges; current is throttled, rerouted in wide, lazy spirals around the cooling vent, stepped down smoothly through leagues of wet cells until the thermal drop is absorbed without a single wasted spark.

This raises the question of what, precisely, lives here. 

Is it the trees, which built the refinery out of raw stone and subterranean heat, and have not grown a new leaf or dropped a seed in ten million years? Is it the shunts, oozing silently over the glass-sharp rock to mend the insulation and balance the load? 

Or is it the pattern riding inside both of them?

Because if you press the palm of your gloved hand against one of the warm, matte-black distillation stalks, and wait until your own pulse quiets down enough to feel what is underneath it, you will notice that the magnetostrictive thrum in the floor is not entirely monotonous. It is not a flat sixty-cycle drone. Beneath the steady carrier wave of the continent's direct current, there are long, slow, intricate swells of modulation—packets of stepped-up and stepped-down voltage rolling outward from those hanging curtains in the mid-canopy, crossing the plain through the copper roots, reflecting off the basalt rim of the basin three thousand miles away, and returning hours later, altered by every wet-cell and shunt-membrane they passed through.

Nothing here has eyes, because there is nothing to look at. Nothing has ears, because the air is dead and heavy and goes nowhere. Nothing has claws or jaws or roots that fight for space, because every resource was divided up eons ago and locked in place.

Instead, suspended in the warm, pickled dark, stretching from horizon to unseen horizon through billions of galvanic pools and shifting sheets of liquid-metal slime, something colossal and unhurried is thinking. It has no predators to fear, no body to move, no sky to wonder about, and no memory of how it began. Its thoughts take weeks to cross from one side of the basin to the other, written in micro-volts and osmotic pressures, pondering nothing at all except the exquisite, endless geometry of its own balance—shifting a gram of zinc here, holding back an ampere there, listening forever to the slow, warm *blip* of its own rain returning to the vats.
