To see what lives here, you have to stop looking across the basin and look down through it.

The bright green surface is a lie of omission. Only the top quarter-inch of the cushion-mat is alive in the light—millions of tiny, waxy leaf-rosettes wedged together as tightly as masonry to keep out the wind and survive the ultraviolet scour. Directly beneath that thin photosynthetic rind, all light dies. For the next three or four feet down, there is no soil, only a flooded, pitch-black catacomb of woody stems, Paleolithic root-tangles, and unrotted plant fiber too cold to decay. 

Down at the bottom of that wet sponge, resting directly on the raw, knife-edged basalt, the air does not matter. The thin atmosphere above the plateau is starved of oxygen, and the sun burns unprotected tissue in minutes, so life here has largely abandoned the surface. Meanwhile, the endless sheet of snowmelt flushing through the dark root-maze is near-freezing and saturated with dissolved gas. Down in the black plumbing of the bog, breathing water is far easier than breathing sky.

Where the concrete pilings of the pumping stations punch through the mat into the bedrock, the steel bolt-heads and rebar sweat iron into the current. Around every piling, thriving in the dark, cold flow, hang thick, undulating curtains of iron-oxidizing bacteria—slippery, rust-orange aprons that feed on the slow corrosion of the station’s foundations. Blind, translucent amphipods no bigger than rice grains graze these ochre slimes in the tens of thousands. They are eaten in turn by the pale, hook-jawed grubs of the surface beetles, which spend nine or ten winters crawling through the submerged stem-forest before climbing into the glare. That oily, iridescent flash on an adult beetle’s back is not pigment; its carapace is literally mineralized with scavenged iron and manganese leached from the hydro-pilings, making its shell heavy enough to anchor it against the mountain wind and hard enough to survive the snap of a bird’s beak.

Deeper still, pressed flat against the unweathered volcanic stone itself, live the basin’s true natives. 

Because the mountains have not had time to round off their edges, the bedrock beneath the moss is a treacherous geometry of ninety-degree steps, glassy splinters, and razor-sharp fault fractures. Moving across it requires armor. The amphibians that inhabit this dark boundary layer are the size of a splayed human hand and nearly as flat as slate. They have no lungs at all—only vestigial slits where gills once were, and across their backs and flanks, extravagant, ruffled cloaks of blood-purple skin that billow gently in the under-moss current, drinking oxygen straight from the meltwater. Their undersides are the exact opposite: chin, belly, and stubby, clawless toes are paved in overlapping plates of black keratin, hard as horn, allowing them to haul themselves over raw basaltic glass without tearing open.

Elsewhere in the mountains, their distant cousins sing to find one another. Here, under the incessant, multi-mile hiss of sheeting cascades and the tooth-loosening bass of the penstocks, airborne and waterborne calls are useless. Instead, they use the stations.

A concrete piling driven into solid basalt acts as an acoustic beacon, broadcasting the steady sixty-hertz hum of the pumping machinery hundreds of yards outward through the raw stone. In the pitch dark beneath the sedges, the horn-bellied amphibians follow that vibration up the sharp rock terraces until they reach the buried concrete. Dozens of them cling vertically to a single piling like wet, ruffled shingles, arranged in loose rings. They have no voices, and their eyes are shrunk to skin-covered pinpricks that register only the faintest difference between surface disaster and basement safety. They find mates by reading the shadows in the machine’s vibration: when another heavy body hauls itself onto the concrete three feet away, it subtly dampens the standing wave humming through the piling. A newcomer announces itself by lifting its armored throat and striking its chin sharply against the concrete—*tick, tick, tick*—cutting crisp, solid-state clicks cleanly beneath the continuous roar of the water.

Above them, out in the blinding open, the transmission towers run their own quiet harvest. 

Updrafts rising off the unseen gorges miles below the plateau constantly carry lowland drift into the high sky—tiny midges, winged aphids, and ballooning dwarf spiders riding threads of gossamer up into the freezing air. Most are swept straight over the ridge to die on snowfields, or drop onto the sheeting water of the bog and wash over the ledges. Along the sparse line of galvanized steel towers, however, the air is heavily ionized. The humming, crackling ceramic insulators throw wide coronas of static charge into the damp breeze. 

When a charged strand of spider silk drifts within twenty yards of a tower, electrostatic attraction yanks it straight out of the wind and snaps it onto the grounded steel lattice. The undersides of the steel cross-arms are fuzzy with gray, wind-tattered silk and desiccated lowland gnats. Tucked inside the dry bolt-sleeves and the inverted cups of the dead-end brackets—never once descending to the flooded green world thirty feet below—live colonies of pale, flat-bodied harvestmen with legs like bent wire. Kept a fraction of a degree warmer than the air by the faint inductive eddy-currents humming through the galvanized steel, they creep out along the vibrating angles between gusts, picking frozen lowland insects off the metal before the ultraviolet glare bleaches them white.

And then there are the sheds.

From the outside, the corrugated iron structures look hermetically sealed against the world, shuddering on their pilings like dropped engines. Inside, there are no windows and no bulbs, yet they are not entirely dark. Each shed sits directly over a deep concrete intake sump that plunges down through the bog-mat to draw in the runoff. Outside sunlight, fierce enough to penetrate feet of clear mountain water, refracts down beneath the submerged steel skirts of the building and shines up through the intake well, filling the windowless iron room with a dim, wavering, underwater emerald glow.

The air inside is not merely loud; it is a physical architecture of pressure. The deep cavitation roar of the main penstock presses against the chest like a heavy hand, while the rotary trash-screens turn with a slow, wet, metallic scrape, raking continuous mats of torn bryophytes, drowned iron-beetles, and careless, ruffled amphibians out of the intake current and dumping them into steel drainage troughs. Every few minutes, when a hydraulic governor groans and the bypass valve fires outside, the air pressure inside the shed drops and slams back against the corrugated walls, shaking warm droplets of lithium grease and condensation off the valve stems.

No mammal or bird crossing the plateau will come within four hundred yards of these buildings; the acoustic violence drives them back long before they reach the pilings. 

Yet if you stood on the vibrating steel grating in that dim green under-light, once your eyes adjusted, you would see movement beside the warm casing of the hydraulic accumulator.

They are stocky, low-slung beasts, roughly the size and shape of a broad-skulled badger, descended from high-talus crevice-dwellers that were slowly squeezed toward the ridges centuries ago as the cushion-bog swallowed the bare rock. Sometime in the decades since the steel was bolted down, a few squeezed up through the concrete weep-drains into the sheds and never went back to the cold. Their coats are extraordinarily dense—not loose fur, which would ice up in the valve-mist, but stiff, oily plates of pale wool stained rust-orange along their bellies from the wet iron grating and smeared slick black along their flanks from brushing past greased bearings. Their tails are stubby, hairless nubs that cannot freeze; their eyes are pale, milky amber split by horizontal, slit-like pupils built to squint against high-altitude glare.

What allows them to live here is not evolution—the iron has not been on the mountain nearly long enough for that—but routine infancy trauma. 

Every litter born in the dry corner behind the warm oil-pumps loses its hearing before its eyes open. The first dozen times the bypass vent shrieks, the delicate structures of their inner ears shear flat and calcify over, their small, crumpled ear-flaps sealing shut with thick wax and scar tissue. They grow to adulthood inside a world of absolute, velvety quiet. The 120-decibel shriek that would send a mountain fox into convulsions does not even make them twitch a whisker. 

Instead, they listen entirely through the broad, leathery pads of their feet. Resting a forepaw against the steel floor-plate, an old female can feel the slight change in turbine load miles down the mountain; she feels the rising pressure in the governor housing five seconds before the relief valve cracks open, lazily shifting her weight away from the cold draft of the vent pipe before it fires. Hunger requires almost no effort. Four or five times a day, she waddles across the shuddering grating to the rotary screen’s discharge trough, delicately hooking stunned, horn-bellied amphibians and heavy, iron-shelled beetles out of the dripping steel teeth, crunching through mineralized carapaces with broad, flat-crowned molars, and licking warm, salty grease off the piston collars on her way back to sleep.

Each iron shed is too small to hold more than a solitary adult or a mother with a single slow-growing kit, which means that eventually, across the empty miles of the basin, they have to visit one another.

They never cross by day, when the harrier glides low over the prisms and the sun scours the open benches. They wait until the sun drops behind the sharp green rim of the ridge. At this altitude, night falls like an axe. Within twenty minutes of dusk, the thin air plummets well below zero. Across all five miles of the basin, the shallow curtains of sheeting water stiffen at their edges, and the millions of droplets hanging on the cushion-plants freeze into a continuous crust of black glass, while the deeper water keeps hissing invisibly underneath. 

Only the thick steel penstock pipes remain bare. Driven by the friction and thermal mass of thousands of gallons of rushing water plunging inside them, the dull grey pipes stay just above freezing, shedding frost as fast as it settles. 

In the sub-zero, star-blasted dark, while the ceramic insulators overhead spit faint violet threads of corona fire into the thin air, small, grease-darkened shapes slip out from beneath the corrugated skirts of the sheds. They do not step onto the frozen crust of the bog. One by one, they climb onto the broad, rounded backs of the steel pipes emerging from the moss. Guided entirely by the deep, steady thrum rising into the soles of their feet, wholly indifferent to the twin roars of ice-rimmed cascades and vibrating steel, they trot single-file along the warm iron spines across the pitch-black basin—moving unhurriedly from roaring island to roaring island, passing one another in the dark by touch alone.
