Behind the unreflective glass of a third-tier spire, two hundred feet above a basin of sheet-water, a person stands barefoot on warm, dark stone that looks almost identical to the wet basalt outside, though this floor has been polished to the texture of dry silk. 

They are tall, with the slightly elongated ribcage and barrel-chested build of people whose ancestors spent generations adapting to thin mountain air before the sealed spires were ever raised, even though the air inside the tower is maintained at a rich, sea-level density. They keep the physiological habit anyway, out of vanity or reverence. Their skin has the pale, faintly bluish undertone of glacial milk, save for the backs of their hands and the bridge of their nose, which are wind-burned to a permanent, dull russet from hours spent outside without an atmospheric veil. Their hair is dark, coarse, and cropped close to the skull so it doesn’t tangle in the electrostatic eddies near the thermal rings.

If you watch them for an hour, the most striking thing is their stillness. They do not pace. They do not fidget. Humans in older, denser cities move with a constant micro-agitation—checking surfaces, shifting weight, filtering out a thousand competing hums and sirens. The people of the plateau have absorbed the cadence of the raptors on their causeways and the stone beneath their foundations. This one stands at the glass for forty minutes without shifting their heels, watching the pale canids three miles off work the lagging calf into a boulder trap. They aren't using optical enhancers, though the glass could provide them with a blink. They are simply looking, letting their eyes water slightly in sympathy with the cold they cannot currently feel.

They call themselves something that translates roughly to *the Ballast*, or *the Counterweights*, though there is no pomposity in the word as they use it; it is a technical term from structural engineering. The plateau is rising four millimeters a year, fracturing, shearing, groaning under monstrous horizontal compression. Their city was not built here *despite* the violent youth of the rock, nor merely to harvest the colossal geothermal and piezoelectric potentials bleeding out of the fresh faults. The city is an instrument of calibration. Its deep pilings—which are not pilings of steel, but localized fields of locked molecular tension reaching miles down into the granodiorite—drink the seismic violence that would otherwise shatter the tableland in catastrophic, continent-shaking ruptures, bleeding the energy off slowly as heat, transit-momentum, and silent light.

To live here is to be a caretaker of stresses.

When the tremor hits the western horizon—the one that lifts the ten thousand waders into the air—the person behind the glass does not look toward the fault. Instead, their eyes drop slightly, losing focus on the outside world. Beneath the skin behind their left ear, a tiny, subcutaneous node warms by half a degree, translating the spire’s counter-harmonic adjustment into a brief, phantom chord in their inner ear: a low, resonant *C-sharp* sliding smoothly down to *C-natural*, the sound of forty billion tons of rock finding a safe, new purchase. Their shoulders drop a fraction of an inch in unconscious mimicry of the crust. It was a clean slip. No shear-lock in Sector Nine. They return their gaze to the wolves.

There are perhaps four hundred thousand of them spread across sixty thousand square miles of stone. In a tower five hundred feet tall and ninety feet wide, there might live twelve people, or two, or a single extended family of seven occupying forty vertical levels of terraced, open-air rooms where indoor mist-gardens of high-altitude cedar and tree-ferns grow in stepped troughs of crushed basalt. They have immense space, and so they keep very few things. A room might contain a low, wide table of sintered stone, three heavy woolen throws woven from the combed-out winter coats of the wild ungulates below, a ceramic bowl holding fragments of smoky quartz picked up on a morning walk, and nothing else. Clutter feels, to them, like blockage—like stress accumulating on a locked fault.

When they go down to the rock, they dress with extreme, ritualistic care. There are no vehicles on the plateau surface; anything with wheels or tracks would score the unweathered stone and churn the pristine algal mats into grey slime, and low-altitude flyers would scatter the herds and clear the causeways of roosting hawks. If you want to leave the transit-grid, you walk.

At the base of a monolith, an invisible membrane parts without a hiss, letting a pair of walkers step out onto a dry sill just inches above the seep-water. The cold hits them like an iron bar across the forehead. They wear articulated boots with soles made of a dense, sacrificial polymer that mimics the tough, gripping pad of an ungulate’s hoof—soft enough to be sliced to ribbons by the razor-edged basalt over twenty miles, gripping the wet, slick facets without scarring them, disintegrating cleanly into inert silica dust when shed. Over lean, heated base-layers, they wear long, hooded coats of matte-grey oiled canvas, cut heavy so the wind doesn’t snap the fabric and startle the birds.

Out on the flats, they are nearly mute. Speaking aloud in air that thin and quiet feels obscene, like shouting in a cathedral, and the cold dries the throat in minutes. Instead, they communicate through a combination of spare, centuries-old hand-signs—originally developed by high-ridge surveyors—and sub-vocalized pulses sent from throat-skin to inner ear, brief flashes of shared inclination rather than words: *look northeast; slick ice under that sheet; bull watching us; wait.*

They do not manage the wildlife, and they certainly do not tame it, yet an intricate, unsentimental intimacy exists between the towers and the herds. The ungulates—whom the residents call *greys* or *shale-backs*—know that the white alloy rings resting over the thermal seeps leak a faint, steady halo of warmth into the surrounding shallow water, keeping the mineral-rich algae blooming thick and green even during a mid-winter freeze. In deep sleet, hundreds of the heavy, greasy-wooled beasts will press right up against the base of a dark monolith to use it as a windbreak, rubbing their itching flanks against the seamless metamaterial. The metamaterial sheds the lanolin and grit effortlessly; by noon, a thin grey ribbon of shed wool and tallow floats away on the overflow sheet, immediately descended upon by flocks of slate-backed waterfowl who line their shallow gravel nests with the oily fleece.

The humans harvest from this abundance, though only by hand, and only within rules so strict they feel like superstition. Once a season, small parties walk out miles from the nearest causeway carrying long, silent-bolt rifles that accelerate needles of frozen bismuth—leaving no lead in the water, making no report louder than the snap of a dry willow twig. When they drop a grey, they butcher it right there on the wet stone with ceramic blades that never dull against the grit, working quickly with bloodied, freezing bare hands because gloves ruin the dexterity needed for clean joint-work. They pack out the heavy quarters, the tongue, the heart, and the thick, sour-smelling tallow on rigid carbon-frames strapped to their own backs. Everything else—the paunch, the ribs, the hide—is left spread neatly on a high basalt table. Before the walkers are half a mile back toward their spire, the cream-feathered raptors have dropped from the translucent causeways like stones, fifty of them mantling over the red feast in total silence, while the pale canids wait twenty yards off in the fog, licking their chops, waiting their turn.

Inside the towers at night, when the plateau vanishes into an ink-black void broken only by the blazing, unblinking wheel of high-altitude stars—for the city sheds not a single stray lumen of light outward, lest a migrating flock smash itself against a spire—the interior core of the architecture glows softly, insulated behind polarized walls.

Here is where their warmth hides. Groups of twenty or thirty gather in the deep root-chambers of the central monoliths, spaces carved directly into the living granodiorite two hundred feet beneath the surface. The rock walls are left rough, dark, and weeping cold mineral water down into narrow perimeter channels, while the center of the room is warm, lit by amber induction braziers. They roast slabs of dark, mineral-tasting venison over hot stones, drink hot, salted infusions of dwarf-willow bark and fermented lichen-sugars imported from lower, forested valleys a thousand miles away, and talk in low, gravelly voices that rarely rise above a murmur.

What do they do with minds freed from material scarcity, sitting inside a machine the size of a country? 

They practice forms of patience that lowland humans find borderline incomprehensible. There are visual artists among them who do not paint or sculpt, but instead map the transient geometries of the water-sheets—tracking how a three-inch shift in barometric pressure over the northern ridges alters the refraction angle across forty square miles of wet basalt, recording compositions of cloud-shadow, flock-wheel, and sleet-curtain that last eleven seconds and will never repeat before erosion finally carves a riverbed ten thousand years from now. There are deep-crust listeners who spend forty years learning to read the micro-fractures in a single cubic mile of stone beneath Spire Twelve, knowing its subterranean knots and fluid pressures better than they know the veins in their own wrists.

And there are the watchers of the causeways, mostly children and the very old. 

A child of seven lies flat on her stomach on the interior mezzanine just where a translucent causeway meets the spire wall. Separated from the outside air by an inch of force-locked glass, her nose is three inches from the talons of an old female raptor roosting on the span. The bird stands two feet tall, smelling—she imagines—of wet feathers, cold grease, and bone. Beneath the bird’s scaled, yellow toes, the causeway shimmers faintly violet as a freight capsule carrying two tons of refined lithium-brine shoots past beneath the surface at nine hundred miles an hour, completely silent, completely unfelt. 

The hawk ruffles her throat feathers against a sudden gust of sleet, turns her fierce, pale-gold eye sideways, and looks directly through the glass at the child. Neither blinks. The hawk sees a shadow in a warm cliff-crack; the child sees the true owner of the sky. 

Down below, in the grey twilight, the wet stone clicks with a million hooves moving slowly east toward the morning sun, and miles beneath them both, the young rock groans softly into the city's waiting hands, and goes back to sleep.
