If you follow one of the walking cairns indoors—ducking through a low, U-shaped threshold worn smooth as whale-tallow, pushing past triple-hung curtains of stiffened seal-gut weighted at the hems with smooth basalt cobbles—the sudden absence of light hits like a blow to the sternum. 

Inside the dry-stacked towers, the air is thick, fiercely warm, and startlingly dark. It smells of rendered bird-fat, old salt, damp wool-felt, fermented shrimp-paste, and the dense, milky musk of twenty human bodies packed into a room ten feet across. Here, the layers finally come off, and you see what centuries in the basin have made of the people.

They are built like barrels—short-limbed, immensely broad across the ribcage to house lungs oversized for the thin air, with thick pads of subcutaneous fat over their cheekbones, necks, and knuckles that give even the oldest among them a smooth, taut, moon-faced look. Their skin, where the sun never touches it, is the pale, translucent amber of dried sinew; around the narrow horizontal band of their eyes, however, where light leaks through the burned slits of their bone goggles, a permanent mask of purple-black hyperpigmentation is etched across the bridge of the nose, looking like a smear of soot that cannot be washed off. 

When they strip off their five-fingered gut-mittens, their hands are astonishing: stubby, heavily calloused, yet jittering with a constant, unconscious micro-dexterity, the fingers twitching in the half-dark as if playing invisible flutes. They have lived inside a sign language of flashed light and bone-taps for so many generations that thought itself has migrated into their wrists. Even here in the warm dark, they rarely speak aloud. Their vocal cords have gone lazy and soft from disuse. When they do make noise—usually in the pitch-black sleeping lofts where hands cannot be seen—it is a low, closed-mouth *humming* felt directly through the ribs of the person pressed against them, modulated by light taps of the fingernails against a collarbone or jaw. A baby does not cry here; an infant born in the basin learns in its first dry-throated week that an open-mouthed wail brings an agonizing stab of cold even near a drafty door, and instead communicates hunger by furiously flexing its toes against its mother’s belly and clicking its gums.

Watch an old woman tending a rendering-pot in the center of the floor. The heat source is not fire—smoke would choke a sealed stone tower in minutes, and there is nothing woody to burn—but a shallow trough of dark soapstone fed by a trickle of oily grease pressed from the forearm-thick wall-worms, lit along a tiny wick of silver moss that burns with a clear, almost invisible blue lick of flame. Around the pot, four of the wingless, glass-plumed birds sit tucked among the humans' thighs like fat hot-water bottles. 

Down here, you realize the birds—the *shiver-hens*, if their slate-flash name could be translated—are not merely scavengers outside. They are living masonry and portable furnaces. Their core body temperature runs dangerously high, fueled by the frantic calories of the deep-mountain brine-shrimp, and their hollow, spun-glass filaments trap heat with terrifying efficiency. When a child has to go out onto an upper catwalk, two birds are shoved directly inside the front of their gut-parka, heads tucked peacefully under the child’s chin, radiating dry, musky warmth against the human heart while the child works. In return, the humans preen the freezing brine-crusts from the birds’ ear-holes and feed them scraps of worm-tallow. The birds are utterly silent, lacking syrinxes entirely; when content, they vibrate like low-speed drills, a sub-audible purr that travels up through the slate floorboards and settles into the teeth of everyone in the room.

Beneath the floorboards—for the lowest story of every tower is not lived in by humans, but serves as a shifting, slush-floored stall—live the six-legged ungulates. Up close, in the gloom, they are stranger and softer than they look in the glare. Their grey felt coats are not true hair, but a dense, continuous mat of interlocking wool and fungal threads that grows in heavy, overlapping aprons down to their fetlocks, shedding freezing brine like oiled canvas. Their middle pair of legs is shorter and thicker than the fore and hind pairs, positioned directly under their center of mass and tipped with split, splayed hooves lined with rough cartilaginous pads that grip wet glaze-ice like suction cups. 

They do not sleep standing up; they fold all six legs beneath them like gigantic, woolly sowbugs and press their broad flanks directly against the dry-stacked foundation stones so their body heat keeps the mortarless rock from cementing itself rigidly with frost—because a rigid wall in this basin is a dead wall. Everything must give. Everything must slide an inch left when the cliff-seams belch, and an inch right when the noon sun melts a pillar. The beasts feel the hydraulic build-up in the basalt cliff minutes before the seams actually blow. Their flat, shovel-horned snouts are packed with baroreceptive nerve endings that read the micro-tremors and pressure drops in the rock. Long before a human eye sees an ice-blister swelling under an alley, three or four of the beasts down in the dark stalls will suddenly shift their weight in unison, knocking their horn-plates softly—*tok, tok*—against the ceiling beams. Upstairs, a human ankle feels the tap through three layers of cured hide; out goes an arm through a window-slit; *clack* goes the black slate baton in the blinding sun; and twenty yards away, six crews drop their ice-lenses and grab their bone pry-bars before the water has even breached the seam.

And what is coming out of that seam? Where do the translucent shrimp come from, gushing endlessly out of solid rock three miles up in the violet-black sky?

Ask one of the silent scaffold-climbers—the leanest subset of the population, adolescents who haven't yet thickened into adult barrels, who spend their days lashed by kelp-ropes to the sheer basalt cliff-faces, chiseling new gutter-grooves around shifting fissures. They know the mountain is hollow, and they know it is warm deep inside, heated by some drowned volcanic root steeped in an ancient, uplifted subterranean sea. Because sometimes, when a high seam blows unusually hard, it doesn’t just spit slush and blind white shrimp. 

Sometimes it spits *pieces* of larger things, shattered by the furious hydraulic squeeze through narrow basalt cracks on their way out of the dark. A pale,segmented raptorial claw the length of a man's leg, twitching weakly in the freezing foam before the shiver-hens strip it bare. A sheet of tough, rubbery, bioluminescent skin that glows a faint, sickly green inside a dark tower for three days before drying into stiff parchment used for lantern-windows. Soft, eyeless, melon-sized polyps that freeze solid mid-air and shatter like porcelain on the alley stones, smelling overwhelmingly of iodine and sulfur. The inhabitants harvest it all without superstition or pause. To them, the mountain is simply a whale that never dies, and they are the lice wedged securely in the crease of its blowhole.

Then there is the fourth side of the square: the drop.

For all their dense, claustrophobic interdependence, the basin cannot make everything. There is no bladder-kelp in the mountain’s gut; there are no whale-ribs in the rock; there is no flint or new slate once the old stones grind down to pebbles. Look closely at the weathered parapet along the sheer southern lip, where the slush-carpet ends abruptly at a dizzying fall into the blinding sea of clouds below. Anchored deep into the basalt there are massive capstans made of stacked vertebral disks turning on greased bone axles, wound with hundreds of fathoms of braided kelp-cable thick as a man's thigh.

Not everyone who lives here stays in the box. There is a sister-caste—or perhaps the same families on a generational rotation—who live down *under* the cloud-deck, thousands of feet below on whatever cold, wave-battered ocean shore produces the giant kelp and the drift-bone. Nobody from the coast can climb up here; their lungs would burst like bladders in the thin, burning inversion layer, and their voices would freeze in their throats. And nobody born in this high basin can go all the way down into the crushing, wet, deafeningly *loud* soup of sea-level air without going mad from the roar of surf and wind.

So they meet halfway, inside the dead white belly of the clouds, on narrow, ice-glazed ledges where neither side can breathe comfortably. Once every few moons, you will see six of the six-legged beasts harnessed to the great bone capstans at the cliff's edge, walking silently in a tight circle through the rime-slush, hauling up a massive sling of stiffened hides dripping with cloud-damp: bundles of fresh, salt-cured kelp, unworked ribs of great baleen leviathans, baskets of coarse coastal sand for scouring bone. And into the emptied sling go sealed gut-bladders of pure, clarified worm-oil—worth its weight in blood down below, for it will not congeal in any earthly cold—along with bricks of compressed, sun-dried brine-shrimp and panes of crystal-dense pressure-ice cut from the mountain’s throat.

No words are exchanged at the cliff-edge, of course. Only a three-foot mirror of polished black slate mounted on a swivel at the parapet, angled downward to punch sharp, rhythmic needles of stolen high-altitude sunlight straight down into the cloud-glare below, waiting for a dimmer, diffused flash to blink back up through the wool.

Stay until dusk, and the whole manic machinery slams into reverse within ten minutes. 

Because of the enclosing cliffs and the razor-thin air, twilight does not linger; when the sun drops behind the western basalt wall, the scalpel-light vanishes as if a shutter has slammed down. Instantly, the violet-black sky swallows the square, stars snapping out hard and steady and enormous, unblinking in the still inversion. Without solar radiation striking the dark slate, the temperature plummets twenty degrees in the breath between one heartbeat and the next. 

The phase-war ends in an immediate rout. Every weeping seam along the cliffs freezes shut with deep, internal hydraulic *booms* that you feel purely in the soles of your boots—the mountain holding its breath until morning. Mid-air arcs of brine turn into solid glass bridges halfway across an alley. Out in the lanes, nothing can survive exposed. Within seconds of the shadow creeping up the eastern wall, the alleys empty completely. The walking cairns vanish through the U-shaped doors; the last of the pewter-shelled crustaceans drop into deep foundation crevices; the stray shiver-hens dive in white avalanches down roof-chimneys straight into the waiting laps of the households below, plugging the flues with their own hot, greasy bodies to seal the warmth inside.

Within five minutes, the entire vertical city is entombed under a fresh, tightening glaze of black ice. Nothing moves from ledge to ledge. Nothing breathes the outside air. Yet if you pressed your gloved hand flat against the freezing slate of any tower wall in that star-pierced dark, you would feel it right away: the faint, continuous, sub-audible *thrumming* of hundreds of hot-blooded birds vibrating in the dark, the slow shifting of heavy six-legged beasts leaning into the stones below, and the soft, rhythmic *tap-tap-tap* of calloused human fingers gossiping silently against bone rafters, waiting in the stifling, musky dark for the sun’s blade to cut them loose again at dawn.
