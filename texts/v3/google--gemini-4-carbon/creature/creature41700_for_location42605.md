To find what actually rules this drowned valley, you have to stop looking in the sunlit shafts of open water and start looking *at* the walls of green—and then keep looking until your eyes adjust to the fact that some of the foliage is breathing.

Life in a geologically fresh catastrophe is always a strange assortment of stranded locals that adapted violently fast, wind-blown opportunists, and ancient specialists woken up from subterranean cracks. Because the basin has no predators with jaws and teeth, and because the water is steeped in lithium—a natural, omnipresent sedative—evolution here has discarded panic entirely. Everything that lives in the warm labyrinth shares a dreamy, drugged-out patience and a shocking fragility of form. With no currents to batter them and no mouths to crush them, bodies have grown extravagant, lacy, and thin as wet tissue.

### The Glass-Boned Architects

The snails grazing the underside of the surface tension are only the juveniles. When they reach the size of a teacup, their shells—made not of chalky white calcium, but of clear, flexible protein mineralized with silica and pale carbonate—become too heavy for the surface film to hold. Rather than sink into the dark scree sixty feet down, they move into the mid-water canopies. 

Down in the vaulted galleries of rust-red macrophyte, an elder snail can grow to the breadth of a dinner plate, though its shell is so thin you can read the slow, rhythmic squeeze of its pale heart right through the whorls. They do not merely graze the plants; they tailor them. Using a radula covered in microscopic combs rather than rasping teeth, a giant glass-snail pulls five or six swaying stems together and coats them in a thread of mucilage that hardens instantly on contact with the iron-rich water. Over weeks, a single snail weaves a shaded, stagnant alcove out of living leaves. 

Inside this chamber, the snail hangs suspended by a single thread of slime, cultivating gardens of rust-colored iron bacteria and sweet, gelatinous algae on its own shell, turning slowly in the diffused green light like a planet so its garden gets even sun, reaching back with an extraordinarily long, Divided lip to harvest from its own back once a day.

### The Bubble-Thieves

Because the water holds almost no dissolved nitrogen, the plants are starved for it, and some have grown quietly carnivorous—like the vast mats of bladder-wort. Yet this has given rise to a tiny, jewel-like specialist that treats the plants not as food, but as lungs and larders.

Look closely at the silvery frost of oxygen beads clinging to a serrated olive leaf, and you might see one of the beads sprout six jointed legs of spun amber and walk four inches up the stem. 

They are descendents of terrestrial alpine mites or bark-beetles that tumbled from the crags when the valley first flooded. Small as apple seeds, their carapaces are coated in a dense velvet of hydrophobic hairs. They cannot breathe water, and they never visit the surface—the steam blanket is too disorienting, and the cold mountain wind inches above it is fatal. Instead, they live thirty feet down, grazing on the tiny pearls of pure oxygen exhaled by the sun-struck leaves. When a mite’s air-cloak grows stale, it simply walks over to a fresh, fat bubble clinging to a frond, embraces it, and lets the fresh gas snap into its velvet coat with a microscopic *tic*. 

The mites lay their eggs directly inside the gas-bladders of the floating ferns, piercing the plant’s translucent skin with a needle-sharp ovipositor so their pale larvae hatch in tiny, sunlit greenhouses of warm, pure oxygen, suspended safe in the middle of the lake.

### The Talus-Weavers

Drop down past the sunlit colonnades, down through the narrowing shafts of amber light where the warmth grows slightly sharper and smells faintly of sulfur, and you reach the raw, knife-edged rubble of the drowned mountainsides. Here, the light is a dim, olive twilight, and a continuous, silent snow of dead leaf-flakes, shed snail-slime, and carbonate crystals drifts down onto the brutal angles of the fractured porphyry.

This is the domain of the blind crustaceans—though the solitary one wedged in a boulder crack is merely a scout at the edge of its territory. Deeper in the talus, where huge slabs of fallen cliff lean against one another like a house of cards, thousands of them live in silent, tactile communes.

Their bodies are no larger than a human thumb, flat as wafers and translucent white, with pale blue blood visible in their gills. Their true size is in their reach: six pairs of legs and four antennae drawn out into filaments twenty inches long and finer than spider silk. In a world with zero current, water does not bring scent or vibration to a waiting hunter; the hunter must become a stationary net. 

In the dark spaces between the razor-sharp stones, hundreds of these creatures anchor themselves to the raw rock and extend their hair-fine limbs until they overlap, filling cubic yards of water with a living, three-dimensional cobweb of nerve endings. They do not weave silk; they *are* the web. When a flake of rotting bladder-fern or a dead oxygen-mite drifts down into the abyss and brushes a single filament, three or four crustaceans register the touch simultaneously. There is no fight. Lithium-slow, infinitely polite, the nearest individual reels the prize in with two secondary feelers, while its neighbors slowly part their filaments to let the meal pass without tangling, then drift their sensory hairs back into place over the course of twenty minutes. 

If a fresh stone splits off the dry peaks seven thousand feet up and comes crashing down through the canopy—*clack, boom*—sending a rare, violent shockwave through the deep, the entire white web snaps inward in a fraction of a second, vanishing into the microscopic fractures of the rock, leaving only bare, dead stone until the siltless water goes completely still again.

### The Ribbon-Gardeners

And then there is the coppery-green swimmer—halfway between leech and eel—that crosses the sunlit corridors like a ghost. If you follow it into the dense, three-dimensional wall of greenery, you discover it is neither fish nor worm, though its distant ancestors may have been high-mountain salamanders trapped by the porphyry heave, forced by the warm, strange chemistry to abandon metamorphosis entirely and stretch out into something wholly aquatic and bizarre.

Locals, if any ever climbed the dry granite to peer through the fleece of steam, would never believe how large the oldest ones get, because you almost never see an entire one at once. 

They have abandoned bone for flexible cartilage and lost their hind limbs completely; their forelimbs have flattened into delicate, fern-shaped pectoral fans behind small, recessed eyes the color of tarnished brass. Their external gills do not look like animal parts at all—they are extravagant, branching plumes of rust-red and chartreuse membrane sprouting from the neck, virtually indistinguishable from the ruffled macrophyte leaves around them. An adult can be six feet long and no thicker than a man’s wrist, winding horizontally through the packed foliage like a stray vine.

They are the apex animals of the basin, yet they scarcely hunt. Instead, they are creatures of pure sensation and subtle husbandry in a crowded world. Because the vegetation grows so aggressively inward, left entirely to itself, the submerged jungle would eventually choke its own light, stagnate in pockets, and rot. The great ribbons prevent this. 

Moving with hypnotic, undulating slowness, an elder ribbon slips through the claustrophobic thickets, tasting the water’s mineral gradients with its skin. Where the greenery has packed too tightly and the water has gone sour and dark, the creature uses a pair of smooth, fused beak-plates in its mouth—originally meant for crushing tiny snails—to neatly snip the pale, anchoring stems near the base. It doesn't eat the stems. It watches with brassy, unblinking eyes as the severed tower of foliage slowly tilts and floats upward toward the steam-blanketed surface, opening a brand-new vertical shaft of fierce alpine sunlight straight down to the rock.

Into this new shaft of warm gold, fresh bubbles immediately begin to bead—*blup, tic, blup*. The mites come walking down the leaves to harvest the gas; the giant glass-snails glide to the edges of the new clearing to sun their shell-gardens; the dislodged flakes of leaf snow down into the dark scree to feed the waiting webs of the blind white crabs. And the long, coppery-green swimmer hangs weightless right in the center of the new sunbeam it has made, spreading its ruffled gill-plumes wide to drink the warm oxygen, completely motionless for four hours at a stretch, suspended between the sharp, drowned earth and the quiet steam.

### The Rim-Walkers

Only one creature crosses the border, living right on that knife-edge waterline where eighty-five-degree mineral water meets the freezing, thin mountain air. 

In the two-inch band of lime-encrusted, steam-drinking moss that rings the entire basin like a bathtub rim, live small, charcoal-black spiders with legs spanned wide like water-striders, dusted white with mineral crust from the evaporating mist. Above them, three inches up the bare granite, is a howling, dry desert of wind and ultraviolet glare; below them, three inches down, is a tropical jungle. 

They hunt the tiny midges that breed in the wet moss, and when the afternoon wind drops a frozen grass-seed or a blown alpine moth from the high peaks onto the mirror-flat surface of the lake, these spiders step out onto the warm skin of the water, shielded from the sky by the low fleece of vapor. They skate out through the faint smell of wet slate and iron, dodging the tiny concentric rings of popping oxygen bubbles, claim what the cold mountain has dropped, and carry it back to their narrow green ledge—the only things in the valley that ever hurry, running along the ceiling of a world that has forgotten time.
