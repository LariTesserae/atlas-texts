Nothing that lives in the gulf holds a drop of liquid inside it. Anything wet—an eye, a tongue, a vein of blood—would freeze and sublimate into the thin air before a breath could be drawn, leaving behind a shriveled pinch of salt. Life here is entirely dry. It is built of talc, woven keratin, piezoelectric bone, and spun slate-glass, oiled by fine, slick powders of graphite and chalk. 

Because there is no water to carry nutrients, the metabolism of the entire abyss runs on static charge and friction. The black crust that flakes along the undersides of the arches—what looks like burnt paint—is the root of everything. It feeds on the constant, dry rubbing of stone against stone, drinking the faint, hair-raising voltage generated whenever a million talus pebbles unseat themselves and slide past one another in the thin wind. Everything else eats the crust, or eats what eats the crust, passing that hoarded spark up a silent, brittle food chain.

If you watch the six-limbed husks—the grey, felted grazers that ride the migrating streets—for long enough, you realize they do not have heads. They have no front or back, because front and back belong to creatures with a reliable floor. Instead, they are radial and flat as pressed flowers, about the breadth of a cartwheel when fully splayed, with a ring of dull, mica-plated eyes circling their upper and lower rims alike. Their pelts are thick mats of dead, split filaments that frizz outward whenever an electrostatic front approaches, turning the creature into a pale dandelion puff a full minute before the stones beneath it begin to loosen. This is how they read the weather. When the filaments on their eastern edge lift and stiffen, they know weight is about to tilt east; they simply unhook three of their dry, calloused claws, let their flat bodies catch the new angle of *down*, and slide onto the departing masonry as smoothly as a coin pushed across a table. 

When one reaches a fresh seam of black static-crust, it flattens itself tightly against the latticework. Underneath, in the center of its belly, is a circular rasp made of flinty teeth that turns slowly, like a millstone, grinding the brittle black crust into soot and inhaling it into electrostatic sacs where the charge is stripped away and the dead carbon is vented out its flanks in neat, rhythmic puffs, like smoke from a tiny bellows. When two of them meet on a narrow ribbon of basalt, they do not fight; they slide over one another like two sheets of stiff parchment, trading a brief, silent crackle of blue static in the twilight before continuing on.

Below them, in the tangled perspectives of the middle gulf, stalk the stilt-walkers—the things with limbs like bundled slate needles. Seen up close, should one ever step across an invisible gravity-seam onto your terrace, they are eight or nine feet of terrifyingly delicate truss-work. Their bodies are open lattices, much like the walls they walk among, so that the pewter light shines right through their thoraxes. Wind passes through their ribs without pushing them off balance. Their legs are composed of three or four slender rods of mineralized chitin held parallel by taut, dry tendons that twang almost inaudibly when they shift their weight, tuning themselves to the pull of the stone beneath them. 

They have no muscles as a mammal understands them—no wet, heavy bellies of meat to haul across shifting vectors. Instead, their joints operate like dry, electrostatic ratchets. Stored charge from their prey pulls smooth plates of horn past one another in crisp, microscopic clicks. Consequently, they move with the eerie, stuttering grace of clockwork hands: a long pause, a sudden, frictionless glide across three yards of vertical wall, another motionless pause while two long, feathery antennae taste the chalk-dust for the tang of ozone that betrays a hiding grazer. When one catches a smaller glider—one of the blind, four-winged kites of stretched skin that drift endlessly in the updrafts, filtering airborne soot—it does not chew. It folds its needle-legs around the prize like a closing umbrella, pierces the brittle shell with a hollow proboscis of grey flint, and drains the creature’s charge in a single, sharp *tick* that briefly illuminates its own translucent ribcage from within, like a faint purple flash inside a smoky lantern. What drops away into the five-mile dark afterward is uncharged husk, already halfway to dust before it vanishes.

Yet neither the felted husks nor the stilt-walkers account for the city. Neither of them stacks stones.

If you stay on that rim-side terrace until your eyes adjust completely to the plum-colored gloom—past the point where you see the tenements, past the point where you watch the staircases fold and unfold—you begin to notice a third kind of movement. It is much slower, and it does not fight the rearranging architecture or merely ride it. It *guides* it.

Look closely at the underside of a cantilevered plaza just before a gravitational tide sweeps through. Clinging inside the open bone-marrow holes of the dry-laid slate are things that look, at first, like bundles of pale roots or tangled skeins of dirty cobweb stuffed into the masonry. 

They are colonial—or perhaps they are single, diffuse organisms spread across whole neighborhoods, nobody could ever untangle one without collapsing an avenue. Each unit is no larger than a human hand: a dry, papery bladder of translucent skin, weighted at the bottom with a smooth gizzard-stone it has swallowed, and trailing dozens of yard-long, hair-thin tendrils coated in micro-hooked silica, like dry burrs. 

Individually, they are witless. They hang inside the hollow walls, sipping stray static from the black crust and feeling the strain of the stones pressing around them. Every dry-stacked wall in the canyon hums with microscopic pressure—the sheer, silent groan of counterbalance holding tons of talus suspended over nothing. These burrow-weavers feel that tension through their burr-coated threads. To them, an unbalanced arch or a sagging ribbon of basalt is an irritation, an unpleasant pinch in the dark.

And they have learned—over ages of erosion that pared the mountains down to blades—how to shed stones the way a tree sheds leaves.

Minutes before a gravity front arrives from the unseen floor of the gulf, the pressure in the air shifts. Throughout a tenement block, ten thousand tiny papery bladders contract at once, shifting their swallowed gizzard-stones a fraction of an inch to the left. At the same time, they pull back fifty thousand hair-thin threads from one specific seam of polished river-flat slate, while anchoring fiercely onto the seam beside it. 

They do not lift the stones. Nothing alive could lift a courtyard. They simply *unlock* it.

When the tilted gravity-wind hits, the unlocked stones peel cleanly away into the current, leaving the anchored frame intact. And half a mile away, along the flank of a hollow watchtower where another net of threads is waiting, hungry for fresh surfaces to farm the black static-crust upon, millions of microscopic silica hooks reach out into the dusty air like velveteen grass. As the drifting ribbon of talus brushes past, the threads snag the leading pebbles, bleed off their momentum with a soft, papery *shhh*, and guide them—by the merest ounce of nudge and counterbalance—into the notches of a brand-new balcony. Once three stones lock under the local downward, the weavers let go, pull back into the fresh marrow-holes they have just created, and wait for the black crust to seed itself in the new joints.

The city is not a ruin, and nobody abandoned it. It is a reef. 

Every stepped amphitheater, every blind watchtower, every switchback staircase leading nowhere is a fluke of load-bearing geometry—the shapes that dry, flat slate naturally falls into when billions of tiny, blind masons endlessly trim and catch it to maximize surface area against the electrostatic wind. An amphitheater is not for watching plays; its stepped curve is a parabolic dish that funnels rising soot and charge down into the densely packed nurseries at its base. A narrow alley is a venturi chute to speed up the thin air so the static-crust grows thicker along the eaves. Two parallel staircases descending in opposite directions are simply two colonies growing past one another along twin seams of opposing weight, indifferent to the fact that a creature with two legs would lose its mind trying to walk between them.

And yet, there is one last thing out there, deeper in the pewter light, born of the reef’s sheer age.

Sometimes, down where the pewter glow is brightest in the troughs, you will see a human-sized shape walking slowly up an inverted stair, wearing a ragged cloak of grey talus shards. 

It is not a traveler. If it turned toward you, there would be no face beneath the hood of flat stones—only a dense, dry knot of golden-grey felt and braided silica cord, studded with stolen mica eyes scavenged from dead grazers, housing a fist-sized core of crystalized flint that clicks softly to itself like a cooling stove. 

The reef has been building and unbuilding itself for so long that some of the weaver-colonies have detached from the walls altogether. Rather than wait for the wind to bring them stone, a tight braid of thousands of threads will stitch together its own mobile cairn—a hollow, bipedal or quadrupedal basket of thirty or forty small, polished slate pebbles held in tension against one another like the bones of a foot. Inside this rattling, open-work shell of floating masonry, the colony walks the causeways. 

They are the masons-errant, the curators of stresses too subtle for the wall-bound colonies to feel. One will shuffle silently out onto the spine of a mile-long, torso-thin bridge that has begun to bow too sharply upward. It stops in the exact center of the span, five miles above the unseen floor, with the purple cloud-ceiling looming overhead and chalk dust drifting horizontally past its knees. For an hour, it stands completely still, listening through its stone feet to the dry, tectonic whisper of the arch. 

Then, very deliberately, it reaches into its own chest, unslots a single, palm-sized river-smooth piece of basalt from its own ribcage, leans down, and wedges it into a gap in the bridge underfoot. 

The whole mile-long causeway shivers once, settles half an inch, and locks tight for another century. 

Now slightly lopsided, a little lighter than it was, the hollow thing stands back up. It plucks a loose, unanchored shard from the parapet to mend the hole in its own side, tests the fit with a dry *tck*, and walks calmly off the edge of the bridge—dropping twenty feet through the pewter twilight before catching a sideways current of migrating courtyard tiles and strolling away toward the opposite mountain, upright on a street that is still assembling itself beneath its heels.
