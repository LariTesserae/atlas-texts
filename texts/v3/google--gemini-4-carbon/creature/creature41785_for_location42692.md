At first glance, the hollow contains nothing that was not already listed in its inventory of stone, dust, and stray geometry. A watcher standing at the lip of the fissure—assuming they had brought air enough to stand there, and warmth enough to keep their eyes from freezing in their sockets—could stare down into the pewter gloom for an hour and swear the basin was entirely vacant. 

Only when the watcher turned their head slightly, or shifted their weight from one boot to the other, would they notice that one patch of the slate-gray satin floor did not shift its perspective quite right. 

It rests near the western wall, half-draped over a smoothed rib of hornfels just below the tarnished silver calibration marks. It looks, initially, like a mirage caused by cold air layering over colder stone—a slight thickening of the bruised twilight, roughly the size of a folded cloak, where the underlying talc ripples appear fractionally displaced, as though seen through a thick, flawed lens of smoky quartz. 

If you wait, and know how to look at things that do not hurry, its margins begin to declare themselves.

It has no skin, no shell, no membrane to hold the near-vacuum out, because there is nothing wet inside it to boil away. What lives here is an assemblage of dry, articulate plates and filaments—a low, multi-limbed architecture built from the very hornfels and heavy-isotope crusts of the basin, held together not by tendon or joint-grease, but by small, captive knots of the pocket’s own broken metric. 

Its body—if a loose federation of mineral slats and spatial tension can be called a body—consists of perhaps forty flat, slate-gray ossicles, each worn to the same matte satin as the bedrock. They do not touch one another. Between each overlapping shard of stone hangs a millimeter of absolute, dead-black emptiness, identical in texture to the needle-thin legs of the tripod. When the creature moves, one shard tilts, and the adjacent shard answers a fraction of a second too late, or occasionally a fraction of a second too early, the motion rippling down its length like a stutter in a film strip.

The surveyors who left the tripod and scored the western wall did not bring this thing with them, nor did it climb the massif from some lower, softer valley. It is what happened to their leftovers—or perhaps to one of their instruments—after ten thousand years of sitting unattended in a place where cause and effect had come unglued. It is a feral calibration. A piece of stray mechanics that forgot its equation and learned appetite instead.

Watch it during the long, slow hours when the indigo strip overhead darkens toward night, and the colorless glaze of condensed gas begins to mist the rear crease of the wall. 

A single limb extends from the clustered plates. It is long, fragile as a grass stem, composed of hair-thin needles of tarnished silver—the very same alloy inlaid into the wall's ruler, pried loose grain by grain over centuries and reassembled. The limb reaches out toward the bare bulge of rock near the entrance where the boulder’s unattached shadow lies draped. 

When the silver tip touches the stray shadow, it does not pass through it as through mere darkness. It *hooks* into the edge of the shade as though lifting a dried pellicle of paint. With an agonizingly slow, ratcheting retraction of its non-contiguous joints, the creature drags the shadow three millimeters out of its natural drift, pulling it downslope toward the shallow basin where the dull ochre light lies pooled. 

It is grazing. 

Nothing can live on bare silicate and dry cold alone; life requires a gradient, a downhill slope down which energy can fall. Elsewhere, creatures use oxygen and sugar, or sulfur and volcanic heat. This thing uses discrepancy. Just as the lead-colored crystal crust beside the tripod feeds passively on temporal shear, the creature actively harvests the unresolved tensions of the pocket. It herds stray shadows toward orphan light. When the dragged edge of the boulder's dark meets the meniscus of the pooled, ancient noon at the bottom of the bowl, the two anomalies do not cancel out cleanly; they grate against each other. Along their line of contact, the air tightens, and a faint, microscopic static of reconciled geometry bleeds off. 

The creature lowers its central mass—a shallow, upside-down dish of worn stone lined underneath with thousands of bristling, dendritic cilia—directly over that seam of friction. It settles there, motionless, absorbing the bleed. Beneath its shell, faint, exhausted flashes of ochre and indigo pass through the gaps between its floating plates, like slow lightning seen through closed shutters, as the stored afternoon of another eon is digested one reluctant photon at a time.

It has senses suited entirely to this quiet. It has no eyes—what would it look for? Nothing enters from above except the flour-hiss of wind and the occasional speck of meteoritic dust. Instead, across its dorsal plates run fine, parallel grooves filled with gray talc. As the creature shifts through the alcove’s warped grid, the talc grains roll and sort themselves into tiny, shifting interference patterns, mapping the invisible contours of thickness and thinness in the local space. It feels the three suspended scree chips in their tar-thick pocket of duration the way a blind cave- spider feels a knot in its web: as a dense, heavy mass of *not-yet* hanging four inches off the floor. 

It leaves those falling stones strictly alone, though once every few years it approaches the stone basin beneath them to groom the dust caps off their upper facets. It does this with delicate, multi-jointed palps that move at two speeds at once: at the shoulder, the limb twitches in normal, brisk mountain time; by the time the slender tip reaches into the slow-zone around the pebbles, the motion has cooled and thickened until the palp takes weeks to sweep three grains of silicate clear, emerging again months later while the rest of the body has stood patiently waiting for its own hand to return from Tuesday.

Why it tends the falling scree is hard to say. Perhaps if the dust piled too heavy on the chips, the added mass would alter their fall and spoil the pocket’s tension; or perhaps the creature simply retains, deep in its borrowed silver filaments, a vestigial compulsion toward neatness inherited from whatever meticulous mind first drove the tripod into the hornfels.

Indeed, its whole existence has the character of a caretaker who has outlived the estate, the heirs, and the language in which the deed was written, until the upkeep itself has become biological. 

When the deepest dusk arrives and the film of frozen atmospheric gas glazes the rear crease of the rock, the creature retreats there. It does not drink the condensate—wetness is alien to it—but uses the brief, frictionless slick of liquefied gas to realign its floating ossicles, sliding its plates against the cold wall to shed accumulated static. 

If you are still watching from the fissure's lip when the hours-long silence is broken by that tiny, glass-dry *tick* deep inside the rock, look closely at the western wall. 

Just before the sound occurs, the creature climbs the vertical stone—not clinging with claws, simply leaning its mass into a local tilt of gravity until the wall becomes, for its three square feet of occupancy, a floor. It positions itself beside the four-foot calibration ruler, right where the middle five inches stretch wider than the surrounding cliff. With two needle-fine silver limbs, it grips two adjacent hash marks that have drifted too far apart in the alcove's slow, structural sag. 

It pulls. Its forty floating stone plates shudder, drawing closer together until their dead-black gaps nearly close, spending the energy it grazed from the shadow and the ochre pool. Slowly, invisibly, a hairline seam of strained space is drawn taut, dragged back a fraction of a millimeter from total collapse—and let go.

*Tick.*

A tiny flake of gray talc shivers down from its back. The widened five inches on the ruler narrow imperceptibly, easing back from the brink of tearing open altogether. Without that periodic nip and tuck, the pocket’s broken metric might have unraveled centuries ago—spilling the pooled noon, dropping the three scree chips with a sharp clatter, letting the third leg of the tripod fall out of the rock, reducing the whole strange basin to ordinary, dead mountain stone. And if the basin became ordinary, the thing that lives in it would starve within a year.

So it keeps the wound open, and keeps it from fatal bleeding. After the *tick* fades into the thin, padded air, the creature loosens its grip on the wall. It slides smoothly down the satin curve of a blurred rock seam, settles into a shallow concavity beside the sunken tripod leg, and lets its plates drift slightly apart until its outline dissolves once more into a mere flaw in the twilight—a low, patient curvature in the dust, waiting for the boulder's shadow to creep another millimeter upslope.
