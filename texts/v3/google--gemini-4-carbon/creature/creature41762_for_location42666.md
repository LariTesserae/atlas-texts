If you stand very still on one of the middle catwalks—say, wedged between the sweating flank of a vulcanized cistern and a curtain of plum-dark velvet leaves—and you wait long enough for your breathing to slow to the pace of the room, you will eventually realize that the rusted tin tenements are not houses at all. 

If you were to pry open one of the louvered shutters, you would find no cots, no tables, no rusted stoves. The corrugated walls are merely shells built to enclose nests of dry, shredded asbestos and the shed, papery husks of dead vines. The doors are bolted from the inside, and the crawlspaces between the stacked boxes do not connect via hallways, but via narrow, greased chutes and vertical chimneys no wider than a keg. 

What lives here does not walk the catwalks during the day, because there is no day, and it does not walk them at night, because walking upright on stamped steel requires an expenditure of furious, mammalian energy that this place punishes. Rapid movement in wet, hundred-and-ten-degree air cooks a creature from the inside out. To survive in the tear, blood must run closer to sap. 

You will smell them before you see them: a faint, sudden drift of camphor, hot lard, and crushed copper-penny scent cutting through the stagnant broth of wet iron. Then, three feet from your elbow, what you took to be a sagging canvas insulation-wrap slung over a warm brine-pipe will slowly blink.

The ward-tenders are human, or their grandparents were, or perhaps they are what happens to a specialized guild of deep-earth engineers after six generations of refusing to come up the shaft. Whatever contract originally bound them to the basalt tear has long since softened and dissolved into pure, biological habit. There are perhaps thirty of them dispersed throughout the vertical maze at any given hour, though you will never see more than two at once, and rarely all of one.

They are built for the gaps. Their collarbones are hinged forward, narrow and malleable, allowing their shoulders to fold inward so they can slip sideways through the nine-inch clearance between a hot brick boiler and a sheer flint blade without scraping skin. Their limbs are long, heavy-boned, and strangely smooth, lacking the sharp definition of fast-twitch muscle; instead, their flesh has a dense, doughy, hydraulic quality, made for slow, inexorable leverage rather than lifting or striking. A tender cannot throw a punch, and if one fell from a catwalk it could not catch itself in time—its reflexes are submerged in a permanent, waking torpor—but it can wrap its broad, flat-knuckled hands around a seized four-inch brass valve and apply steady, unyielding torque for forty-five minutes without trembling, until the rusted threads finally groan and give way.

Their skin is the color of tallow candles kept in a smoky room—a pale, translucent waxy yellow, wholly hairless, not a lash or an eyebrow left to trap sweat and blind the eyes. Because evaporation does not work in air already saturated to its limit, they scarcely sweat at all. Instead, they regulate their temperature like reptiles, by conduction. When a tender grows too sluggish, it presses its belly and thighs against a seeping copper Sweat-pipe until the deep, hot pulse of the earth brings its blood up to working speed; when its head begins to swim with heat-stroke, it wedges its spine tightly into a weeping seam of raw basalt, letting the cold groundwater trickle over its ribs and carry the fever down into the sumps.

Consequently, they wear almost nothing. Woven cloth rots in a week down here, blooming with black mold and dissolving into slime. A tender wears only an apron or a cross-harness of cured, vulcanized rubber—cut from retired cistern bladders—slung low over the hips to hold tools. The rubber slaps softly against their slick thighs when they shift weight. Their knees, elbows, and the heels of their palms are plated in thick, gray-yellow calluses as hard as horn, grown from decades of bracing against hot rivets and razor-sharp basalt planes. 

Watch one work, if your eyes can hold the focus without sliding into heat-sleep. 

A tender emerges from a tin crawlspace feet-first, sliding downward along a greased vertical stanchion like a drop of heavy oil. It does not look down; its bare feet, broad and prehensile at the big toe, know every bolt-head and rusted weld in the dark. Its eyes are large, slightly protuberant, the irises widened almost to the rims so they appear entirely black in the amber gloom, catching the dull-cherry reflection of a heating element like twin beads of tar. 

It reaches a cluster of barrel-cycads strapped beside a turbine housing. Moving with the underwater deliberation of a sloth, the tender unhooks a brass grease-gun from its rubber harness. It does not pump the handle rapidly; it leans the entirety of its pale, heavy torso against the plunger, letting gravity drive the dark tallow into the bearing of the ten-foot flywheel. As the wheel turns—an inch every five minutes—the tender lets itself ride the rim upward for a yard, using the engine as an elevator, pruning a bruised-plum leaf that threatens to foul a pressure gauge. It pinches the thick stem off with thumbnails thickened like horn chisels. Immediately, before the plant can bleed its precious moisture into the air, the tender dabs a smear of black pipe-dope onto the wound from a tin pot hung around its neck.

To the tenders, there is no boundary between the metallurgy and the botany. Both are simply plumbing. 

They are the ones who graft the surgical tubing into the cycads. The deep geothermal brine drawn up by the silent rams is rich in heavy salts, lithium, and sulfur—compounds that would rapidly scale and choke the delicate brass valves of the upper conduits if sent straight to the surface. So the ward itself is a filter, a living kidney wedged into the rock. The machines pump raw, hot, toxic mineral-broth into the soil-boxes and chemical baths; the bloated, heat-drinking flora draw the poisons up through their roots, bind the heavy metals into their dark, velvety leaves, and distill a clear, viscous, ester-rich sap into their stems. The tenders tap this sap into the glass carboys. Half of it is fed into the return-pipes ascending through the ceiling plug—whatever high, cold city sits forty yards above runs its delicate clocks, or mixes its medicines, or burns its lamps on the clear fluid refined in this pit. The other half of the sap the tenders boil down in small copper kettles over the banked heating elements, skimming the scum to make the slick grease that keeps the flywheels turning, and drinking the warm, bitter, lithium-laced dregs themselves. 

That broth is half the reason their hearts beat twenty-two times a minute, and why their faces carry such a smooth, blank, terrifyingly peaceful expression. 

They do not speak. In a space that swallows sound, where every cubic foot is packed with baffling leaves and iron plates, developing a loud voice is useless, and whispering takes breath better saved for climbing. Instead, they communicate through the armature of the ward itself. 

If you rest your fingertips lightly on a steel handrail—not a hot pipe, just a structural strut—you will occasionally feel a tiny, rhythmic *tink... tink-tink* shivering through the metal, distinct from the deep hum of the earth. Thirty yards below, down near the duckweed sumps, a tender has tapped the flat of a bronze wrench against a girder. Up near the basalt ceiling plug, wrapped around a bundle of copper conduit like a pale gourd, another tender feels the vibration enter its jawbone where its cheek rests against the iron. A minute later, a valve turns a quarter-inch; pressure shifts silently through three hundred feet of tubing; down in the sump, a gauge-needle trembles infinitesimally to the right, answering the question. 

When two of them meet on a narrow spiral stair, they do not step aside—there is no room—nor do they nod. They simply flow over and past one another with slow, blind intimacy, skin slick with condensation sliding frictionlessly against skin, rubber harnesses creaking faintly, trading body-heat for a moment as one goes up to tend the amber bulbs and the other goes down to harvest the fist-sized, shell-less snails from the mineral crusts. 

They eat those snails raw, cracking the gritty mineral mantle away with brass pliers, and they eat the pale, blistered pith of the heat-vines, and the translucent orange bladders of the crevice-ferns, which burst in the mouth with warm, slightly sour distilled water. Nothing goes into the sumps that does not come back up through a root or a stomach. Even the tenders themselves stay in the closed loop. No dead have ever been hauled up through the ceiling plug—there isn't a hoist wide enough anyway. When an old tender's heart finally slows from twenty beats a minute to ten, and then to none, the others carry the limp, heavy body down to the lowest brick-lined boiler housings, open the heavy iron doors to the chemical stew-tanks where the root-mats of the giant gourds drink directly from the warm sludge, and slide them gently in. 

If an outsider were somehow lowered into this pit—a surface inspector in heavy wool and brass-buttoned canvas, gasping immediately as the wet heat hits his lungs like a wet rag—the ward would not attack him. Nothing here has the spare metabolic fury for violence. 

At first, he would think the place entirely abandoned, just as you did. He would clamber up the spiral stairs, his boots clanging flatly against the steel, sweating rivers, knocking dark leaves aside, panicking slowly as the sheer claustrophobia of the packed iron pressed in on his ribs. He might not notice for an hour that the amber filament bulbs fifty feet above him were dimming, one by one, turned down in their sockets by unseen hands to spare his frantic, dilated eyes—or perhaps just to see if he glows. 

Only when the heat finally felled him, when he slumped panting on a grate beside a rubber cistern with his coat torn open and his heart hammering like a trapped bird, would the background begin to detach itself from the machinery. 

A pale, hairless shape would ease out from behind a brick chimney. Another would uncoil from the warm curve of an asbestos-wrapped tank, sliding down a mooring-thick vine without rustling a leaf. They would gather on the catwalks half a level above and below him, leaning their calloused elbows on the rails, resting their chins on their forearms, blinking with slow, tar-black eyes. 

They would wait, with the infinite patience of hydraulic rams, for him to stop thrashing. And once he lay quiet enough not to hurt himself, a tender would reach down with a broad, cool, damp hand—chilled deliberately against a weeping basalt seam just for him—and press a translucent orange fern-bladder against his cracked lips, wondering dimly what sort of strange, fast-burning valve had broken loose from the ceiling, and where among the pipes he ought to be wired in.
