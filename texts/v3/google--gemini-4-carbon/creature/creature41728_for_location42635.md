Before this pocket dropped into the light, everything in it belonged to the horizontal dark. 

For miles beneath the scrub-plain, the abandoned thermal network stretches out like a dry root system—brick-lined tunnels, expansion vaults, service crawls, and iron mains cooling slowly over decades. The creatures currently packed into the collapse were bred for that sealed, lightless architecture, migrating endlessly along hairline fractures to chase the dying remnants of industrial heat. When the ceiling gave way six weeks ago, they did not flee the sudden, blinding column of afternoon sun. They crowded into it. The collapse severed a main artery, creating a chimney that draws the faint, dispersed breath of ten miles of dead pipework and vents it all through one ragged throat. What was meant to be thinly spread across a subterranean county is now jammed into the volume of a single room.

You have to look past the copper-plated grazers and the suede-skinned hunters—they are merely the surface traffic, the visible skin of the pit—to see how deeply the meat is wedged into the machinery.

Take the salt prisms. Near the bottom, where the mineral crusts grow thickest in the oily exhaust, several of the angular, milky crystals are slightly warmer than the rest, and their geometry is fractionally off. If a suede-skinned hunter steps on one, putting its heavy, clockwork weight onto the ridge, the prism does not fracture; it yields a millimeter, sweating a sudden bead of acrid, rust-colored fluid through microscopic pores. These are sessile, shell-secreting worms that drifted in as airborne spores or crawled up the pipes as larvae, anchoring themselves directly over warm rivets. They drink the mineral-heavy vapor and excrete dissolved salts through their mantles, building tall, geometric chimneys around their own soft, pulsing bodies. Only at the razor-sharp apex of each false crystal is there an aperture no wider than a pinhole, from which a single, transparent filament drifts upward on the updrafts, fishing for grease-midges and lichen spores.

Around the sheared ends of the iron pipes—the very mouths of the breath—nothing moves openly, because the air there is too hot and thick with the tang of old machine oil. Yet the inside rims of those dark pipes are lined with something that looks like blistered black paint or heavy, congealed axle grease. When the pressure equalizes every few minutes and the deep, humid exhalation swells upward, this black lining ripples. It stretches outward into thin, iridescent bubbles that catch the sunlight, film-thin and shimmering with rainbows of petroleum, trapping the microscopic midges that get tumbled in the draft before snapping silently back into the dark iron throat to digest them.

Then there are the things that live off the hunters.

The three blind, knuckle-walking beasts perched on the flywheel shards look entirely dominant, methodically levering copper crawlers off the basalt with their chisel-fingers. But watch the hollows behind their collarbones, or the deep creases where their loose, wet-suede hides fold over their joints. Tucked into those warm, flexing crevices are flat, pale yellow mites the size and shape of brass washers. They do not bite the hunters. Instead, when a hunter crushes a copper-shelled crawler between its flat molars, fine flecks of shell-dust and rich, clear hemolymph dribble down its chin. Instantly, four or five of the yellow washers slide smoothly out of the skin-folds, skate down the damp hide without triggering a single twitch of the hunter’s vibration-sensitive snout, mop up the spilled fluid, and glide back into the dark creases before the hunter swallows.

Higher up, beneath the jutting forty-inch stub of narrow-gauge rail, the clusters of greased-ball-bearing egg sacs are tended by something nobody sees unless they watch the shadows inside the concrete cracks. The sacs pulse because they are thin-walled and drinking moisture, swelling taut during the ten-second fogs. Between them, looking entirely like a bent, oxidized cotter pin left behind by a track-layer, hangs an angular, six-legged mother. Her body is no thicker than bailing wire, forged in sharp ninety-degree bends so she can lock herself into the square keyways and bolt-holes of industrial ironwork. Every twenty minutes, she unhinges one long, rusted-looking foreleg to gently rotate an egg sac, ensuring the bright, unaccustomed ultraviolet light pouring down from the sky doesn’t cook one side of her brood faster than the other. When a revolving fleck of gold light from the brass governor sweeps across her, she freezes instantly, registering the sudden warmth of the reflection as a potential shift in the stone.

Even the shallow teacup of warm condensation sitting in the crook of the bent steel girder is an entire province. The water trembles constantly from the micro-tremors of the deep conduits, standing in a permanent, tight crosshatch of standing waves. Floating on the meniscus is a scum of iron oxide, and skating beneath that film—upside down, clinging to the underside of the surface tension—are dozens of translucent, comma-shaped shrimp no bigger than eyelash clippings. They graze on the rust-film and the drowning spores of tallow-lichen. Whenever a heavy drop of fresh sweat falls six inches from an overhead bolt and smashes into the teacup, the tiny basin detonates into spray; for three seconds the shrimp are tumbled violently through their whole world, avoiding the razor-edges of submerged mica flakes, before floating calmly back up to re-attach their feet to the ceiling of the water.
