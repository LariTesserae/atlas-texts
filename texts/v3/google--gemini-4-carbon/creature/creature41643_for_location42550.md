To survive on greased bone in a silent landslide, a creature must give up the idea of standing against the water and become, instead, an interruption in its pressure. 

If you were to touch one in the dark—which is the only way you would ever find it before it found you—you would mistake it at first for a thick sheet of heavy hide torn from a drowned walrus, or a broad, rubbery apron of dead kelp pinned momentarily against the gneiss. It has no silhouette. Lying at rest, the *skrid*—if the people above had a name for the thing that steals their stone weights, though they have never hauled one ten feet off the bottom before it let go—is a circular, muscular mantle four feet across and scarcely two inches thick at its domed center, tapering at its margins to a ruffled skirt as thin as wet parchment. 

Its upper skin is not smooth, because smooth things slip, and it is not rough, because rough things catch the drag. Instead, it is dimpled like the surface of an eroded glacial cobble and tough as vulcanized gristle, colored the exact non-color of wet slate dusted with mica. Embedded in this hide are thousands of microscopic, backward-slanting denticles that shed the scouring rock flour so the animal is not sanded raw while it waits. It has no eyes at all; where eyes might once have been in some distant, sunlit ancestor, there are only two shallow, skin-covered troughs packed with clear, stiff jelly that register the faint, iron-colored bruise of midday not as light, but as a slight change in water density, telling the creature when the seals are likely to come down.

Its true face is pressed against the planet. Beneath the broad skirt, there is no skeleton—only a radial lattice of dense, flexible cartilage like woven willow, allowing the body to pour itself over round cobbles or flatten completely onto bare bedrock. The entire underside is a pale, cold-blooded muscle divided into seven concentric rings of suction chambers, lubricated by a thick, tacky mucus that does not wash away in fresh meltwater or curdle in salt. When a heavy bore comes down the sunken river mouths and the water hauls suddenly north, the creature does not fight it; it simply flexes its center upward by half an inch. The drop in internal pressure locks its rim to the polished gneiss with the force of a horse’s pull. The current hits the sloped edge, accelerates over the low dome, and actually presses the animal harder into the stone. A rolling skull-sized cobble can strike its flank, ride straight up over its back with a muffled *thump*, and roll off the other side without dislodging it; the cartilage ribs simply give beneath the stone’s weight and spring slowly back when it passes.

Movement across the plain is a matter of peeling and pouring. It lifts its downstream edge a fraction of a millimeter, lets the push of the water slide it forward six inches on a slick of its own grease, and clamps down again before the cross-current can get under its belly and flip it like a dead leaf. For longer journeys, when a pan of cobbles begins its slow, *shuck-shuck-shuck* migration across a basin, the animal settles onto the shifting stones, grips six or eight of them at once to spread its weight, and rides the sliding floor for a hundred yards in the pitch dark, letting the geology do the walking.

In a world where clay swallows sound and silt swallows sight, the *skrid* hunts by reading the shape of the shove. Running all around the thin, scalloped margin of its skirt is a fringe of pale, fleshy filaments no longer than eyelashes. These are exquisitely tuned to shear and pressure. The creature lies motionless for days at a time, feeling the vast, silent architecture of the eddies passing over its back. It knows the difference between the dead, tumbling tumbleweed of torn red wrack—which rolls with the exact speed of the water—and the slight, stubborn lag of a flatfish holding station, or the heavy, rhythmic displacement of a sleeper shark passing thirty feet overhead. 

It also knows the faint, unnatural hum of a taut spruce-root line vibrating in the current.

To the people sitting in skin boats eighty feet above in the slush and grey chop, the bottom is simply a thief. They feel a sudden, heavy *tug-tug* on the braided rawhide—not the sharp jerk of a cod or halibut, not the violent rolling of a hooked shark, just a dead, snagging weight, as if the soapstone sinker has wedged itself between two boulders. They haul hard; the line stretches, hums, and does not give an inch. Then, just before the spruce root parts, the tension vanishes entirely. When they pull the line up through the sixty feet of freezing soup, the bone gorge is clean of fat, and the grooved basalt sinker is gone, the rawhide binding neatly sheared through as if by dull shears.

Down in the grain-filled dark, what has happened is very quiet. Following the low-frequency thrum of the line across a quarter-mile of bare stone, the *skrid* flows up over the twitching bait and the dancing stone weight, enveloping both under the bell of its body. At the center of its pale underside sits a circular, collar-like mouth armed not with teeth, which would snap on stone, but with four overlapping plates of dark, horn-like keratin, thick as horse-hooves and sharpened continuously against one another. It snips the leached white fat from the bone gorge first, swallowing it whole without letting the sharp antler-tip touch its gullet. 

Then it addresses the sinker.

Nothing in this basin can afford to ignore weight, and nothing can afford to ignore salt and calcium, which are scarce where the freezing meltwater dilutes the sea. Moreover, an animal with no bones and a flat body lives in constant terror of buoyancy; a single bubble of digestive gas would lift it off the slick gneiss and send it tumbling helplessly away into the upper murk like a crushed willow trap. So the *skrid* eats stone for ballast. Usually it swallows smooth glacial pebbles the size of plums, holding thirty or forty pounds of them in a tough, muscular gizzard ring encircling its gut, letting them grind up whole whelk shells before regurgitating them once they are polished too small to be useful. 

A fist-sized piece of pecked soapstone, soft and greasy to the touch and smelling faintly of woodsmoke and seal-oil from the warm hands that grooved it, is a rare prize. The horny beak nips through the frayed rawhide lashing with a single, slow contraction. If the stone is too large to swallow whole, the animal clamps it against its chest-plate, tucks its rim tight against the bedrock, and spends six hours in the roaring, silent dark scraping curls of grey talc from the sinker with its beak, swallowing the heavy paste to weight its belly while the forgotten bone gorge dances harmlessly six inches above its back.

Smaller lives exist only by borrowing the *skrid’s* stillness. Because there are no crevices in the planed gneiss, the animal itself becomes the only topography for half a mile around. Tucked into the warmish, mucus-lined folds just behind its gill-slits live pale, blind amphipods shaped like flattened lice, grazing on the rock flour and organic peat-slime trapped by its denticles. Underneath its broad skirt, riding safely inside the calm ring between its suction pads and its mouth, you will almost always find two or three juvenile eelpouts, coiled like pale watch-springs. The *skrid* could crush them or swallow them at any moment, and occasionally, in lean winter months when the surface freezes solid and no fat comes down on strings, it takes one. Mostly it ignores them; in return, the little fish Nose into the creases of its suction rings and pick away the parasitic worms and sharp bits of broken shell that might break its vacuum seal against the rock.

They are solitary almost all their lives, spaced out across the five-mile valley floor like lonely iron rivets. When they must find one another, scent is useless—the boiling horizontal vortices tear a chemical trail into unreadable ribbons within ten yards. Instead, a heavy female ready to spawn finds a wide, shallow pan packed with millions of slick cobbles. She settles onto the edge of the bed, locks half her body to the solid gneiss rim, and reaches the thick, muscular margin of her skirt out over the loose stones. Gathering a dozen fist-sized ovoids in her underside grip, she lifts them three inches and slams them down against the bedrock. 

*Thud.* Five seconds of surging dark. *Thud. Thud.*

High frequencies die instantly in the suspended clay, swallowed within paces, so there is no crack or clatter. What travels out through the gneiss itself, and through the dense, cold water hugging the floor, is a pure, sub-audible pressure wave—a heartbeat in the stone that passes clean through the rolling mud-knots for two miles in every direction. 

Hours later, smaller males come skidding and pouring out of the slate-colored fog, riding the sheer-walls downgrade toward the tremor. When two meet in the blind dark over the cobble pan, they do not fight by biting; they try to peel one another off the rock. Sliding edge-to-edge, they shove thin wedges of muscular skirt underneath a rival’s rim, trying to break his seal and let the violent east-bound haul catch his pale belly and cartwheel him away into the miles of shifting gravel.

The winner slides wholly over the female’s back, clamping his suction rings directly onto her rough, mica-colored skin so the two become a single, double-thick mound against the current. Beneath the shelter of both their bodies, entirely walled off from the tearing water, she extrudes not free-floating eggs—which would be swept over the seaward sill and lost in an hour—but a slow, braiding rope of clear, leathery capsules cemented together with a glue that cures instantly in near-freezing brine. Working blindly with the agile center of her belly, she forces this sticky mass deep down into the interstices between seven or eight heavy cobbles, binding the loose stones together into a single, thirty-pound raft that will not roll as easily when the next bore passes. 

Even if the pan mobilizes and the whole sheet of stones slides fifty yards north with its muffled *shuck-shuck-shuck*, the bound cluster moves as one heavy unit, sheltering the tough little purses at its core. Months later, when the capsules split, what crawls out is already flat as a thumbnail, blind, heavy-bellied with yolk mixed with fine grit its mother packed into the egg-case, clamping down onto the slick stone before the water even knows it is there.

Sometimes, one of the tumbled willow traps comes rolling end-over-end across the plain and strikes an old *skrid* that has grown too vast—six feet across, weighed down with fifty pounds of swallowed basalt and half-erased net-sinkers. Rather than let the rolling cage pass, the creature flows up and over the crushed withes, flattening the rotten willow entirely beneath its bulk simply because there is still a smell of rotten fish-heads clinging to the bark lashings, or because a trap holds eelpouts. For half a day the great, grey blister sits motionless in the rushing dark, digesting what lives inside the basket, while the bark strips part one by one under its grinding belly and the flat ballast-stones lashed to the willow frame drop loose, sliding quietly into its gizzard or rolling away down the long, polished grade to join the rest of the round, nameless rock.
