To find anything here that moves on its own terms, you have to stop looking for spaces between the stems. There are no spaces, so there are no limbs built for striding, no wings built for air, no shells meant to push aside gravel that hasn’t been ground yet. If a creature had legs or claws, the thatch would simply pin it between two expanding cables of silica-wood and vitrify it into the scaffolding along with the shaded-out branches.

To live inside a solid, pressurized block of machinery, you have to belong to its plumbing.

Cut open one of the great vertical vascular trunks—the ones three meters across that thump like marine engines as their internal valves cycle—and before the clear, cold sap can congeal to seal the wound, something may spill out with the flood. At first glance it looks like a torn gasket, or a shed lining of the pipe itself: a ribbon of translucent, rubbery cartilage as long as a man’s forearm, utterly flat, edged with faint iridescent ripples. Left on the raw black silicate of a fault-ridge, it dries and stiffens in minutes into a brittle curl of horn. It has no eyes, because inside the vascular column there is nothing to see, and no mouthparts capable of chewing a single cell of that aircraft-grade cellulose.

Inside the trunk, however, riding the roaring upward surge between valve-strokes, it is quick as an eel.

They might be called *shunts*, or *valve-slips*, though they have no name here. Their entire anatomy is an accommodation to high-pressure fluid dynamics and acoustic shock. A slip’s body is a muscular hydrostat reinforced with flexible lattices of the same spun silica found in the leaves—stolen, molecule by molecule, from the dissolved mineral freight of the sap before the plant can deposit it. Along both flanks run microscopic combs of stiff cilia tuned to the exact mechanical frequencies of the canopy. Long before a massive pneumatic bladder vents overhead, or a lower valve snaps shut with enough hydraulic hammer to crush an ordinary organism to paste, the slip feels the preparatory electrical spike flashing down the iridescent nerve-grains of the wood. In a fraction of a second, it flattens itself flush against the smooth interior wall of the xylem, extruding a molecular Suction-film along its belly. The column of cold water thunders past at forty knots; the valve slams; the shockwave rings through the timber; and the slip peels its leading edge free and rides the rebound upward into the next chamber.

It does not eat the plant. Anything that ruptured a conduit wall or scraped at the meristem would trigger an immediate localized coagulation—a pressurized flood of fast-setting resin that turns an intruder into an amber inclusion within seconds. Instead, the slips are skimmers. They hang suspended in the upward-rushing sap like living filters, combing out stray, unpolymerized sugars, rogue trace-ions leached fresh from the fractured bedrock below, and tiny globules of lipid lubricant shed by the cycling valves themselves. They take so little from any single trunk—a fractional drop of efficiency lost to friction—that the vast continental ledger of the root-network registers their presence merely as normal hydraulic drag, indistinguishable from a slight bend in a stem.

Wait longer by that freshly jacked fault-scarp, though—or better yet, listen through the soles of your boots to the raw stone—and it becomes clear that the slips inside the pipes are only the smallest, simplest compromise with this place.

The canopy is an engineered system, and engineered systems of sufficient scale develop ghosts in their maintenance routines.

Down at the bottom of the thatch, fifty or eighty meters beneath the slate-gray sky, there is no soil, only the *vitreum*: the dead, drained, carbon-silicate lattice of older growth that has been fused into rigid architectural trusswork atop the raw crust. It is pitch-black down there, cold as a cistern, and deafening beyond human tolerance. Deluges of water pouring down from the upper tiers crash continually through this glassed-in labyrinth, swirling around razor-edged thrusts of basalt that are still cracking and shifting as the plateau heaves upward. And here, where tectonic violence constantly shears through the rigid silicate scaffolding faster than soft green root-tips from above can reach down to repair it, lives something much larger.

You never see the whole of a *loom-worm*—not because it hides, per se, but because at any given moment eighty percent of its mass is threaded through forty cubic meters of packed, vitrified lattice like mercury squeezed into a sponge.

It has no fixed shape. In essence, it is a mobile, non-botanical root-system: a braided bolus of pale, glistening, hyper-dense muscular cables sheathed in a slick, graphitic cuticle that sheds razor-sharp rock splinters the way greased Kevlar turns a blade. Where a fresh crustal fracture splits the bedrock with a rifle-shot crack, shearing a dozen vitrified trunks and dumping a pressurized cataract of nutrient-sap into the dark, the loom-worm arrives minutes ahead of the plant’s own repair tendrils. It hunts by seismology. Its entire surface is an ear; it reads the rising tension in the unoxidized stone, triangulating the micro-tremors of a coming fault-slip miles away through the rigid, ringing frame of the dead lower forest, already pulling its hundred braided tails through narrow gaps in the trusswork to converge on the point of rupture.

When the rock splits and the severed root-cables gush cold, clear fluid into the fresh fissure, the worm pours itself directly over the ragged stumps.

What happens next looks less like feeding than like surgery—or highway robbery disguised as first aid. The worm clamps its graphitic sleeves over both ends of a sheared vascular trunk, sealing the leak instantly and Becoming, for a few hours or days, the missing section of pipe. The great continental network above registers that pressure has been restored; the emergency resin-flood is aborted; the thermal and electrical balancing currents resume their flow right through the conductive dorsal nerves of the animal itself, which faithfully relays every signal without dropping a cycle. All the while, inside its muscular sleeve, the worm is bleeding off five, ten, fifteen percent of the rich, pressurized mineral-and-carbohydrate slurry surging across the gap, warming its cold tissues on the stolen electrical load while the plant’s own pale root-wedges slowly grow inward around it to permanently seal the fault. Just before the fusing wood can lock it in place forever, the worm uncouples with a sharp hydraulic *pop*, slicks itself thin, and withdraws through a narrowing keyhole in the growing wall, already flowing off along the dark, dripping silicate struts toward the next stressed seam in the crust.

And high overhead, where the slate-black and oil-violet fronds hum against the cloud-deck?

Stand on that newly thrust spine of black glass for an hour, watching the endless, shivering ocean of metal-stiff leaves, and your eyes begin to ache from the uniformity of the tremor. Billions of fronds vibrating at precisely the same piezoelectric pitch, snapping grey mist into sheets of falling water. Everything moves, yet nothing goes anywhere.

Until a patch of canopy ten yards square suddenly drops half an octave—just for a heartbeat—and a flat shadow slides *across* the grain of the wind without disturbing a single stalk.

Look directly at the spot and there is only foliage: matte black under thin cloud, shifting smoothly to slick violet as a darker squall rolls overhead. Look away, catching it at the edge of vision where motion registers faster than form, and you realize that three or four broad, overlapping "fronds" in that cluster are not rooted to any stem below.

They are a single, vast sheet of leathery, hexagonal-ribbed membrane, two meters wide and no thicker than heavy canvas, draped directly across the trembling surface of the upper deck. Its dorsal skin mimics the optical phase-change of the leaves beneath it with terrifying fidelity—not through pigment cells, which would be too slow, but through microscopic liquid-crystal blisters pumped tight or slack in real time as it reads the electrical state of the leaf-surfaces under its belly.

Nothing flies in the thin, roaring soup of mist above the plateau; the wind-shear and the sudden, localized downdrafts caused by millions of acres of synchronized exhalation would slam a bird straight down into the sheet-metal edges of the bracts. The *mantles*—or *skates*, if you watch how they move—never leave contact with the surface. Beneath its broad, mimicking veil, a skate’s underside is carpeted with thousands of tiny, frictionless pads of slick silica-cartilage, lubricated by the constant sheet of condensing cloud-water. It does not crawl. By stiffening and relaxing the hexagonal trusses in its own flat body at a slight harmonic offset to the high-speed piezoelectric shudder of the canopy beneath it, it surfs the vibration itself. Like a coin skittering across a ringing anvil, it glides effortlessly across the top of the continent at twenty miles an hour, completely silent inside the roar, riding the mechanical hum of the world from horizon to horizon.

What does a skate want, sliding forever across an unbroken roof of pristine, inedible leaves?

Watch one stop over a massive pneumatic vent just before the rhythmic hiss of surplus oxygen blasts upward into the fog. The skate anchors its leading rim beneath the lip of two overlapping cup-bracts, arches the center of its two-meter span slightly into a shallow dome, and takes the full force of the warm, oxygen-saturated exhaust underneath its belly. At the same time, thin, needle-sharp grounding filaments along its trailing edge slip deftly between the guard-cells of three neighboring stomata—not piercing tissue, never drawing a drop of sap that might trigger an alarm, simply bridging an electrical potential gap between two slightly out-of-sync vascular columns below. A tiny, silent spark of stolen current jumps across the skate’s metabolic core. Saturated with pure vented oxygen, humming with induction-thieved wattage from the plant’s own power grid, it fixes atmospheric carbon directly in its dark tissues without ever casting a shadow sharp enough for the leaf underneath to register itself shaded and self-destruct.

When two skates meet on the vast, churning slate table, they do not fight; there is no shortage of roof, no shortage of cloud, no shortage of hum. They simply overlap like two slick violet leaves shuffled together in a deck, exchanging tiny packets of genetic code and trace-silica through the film of cold rainwater between them, before altering their harmonic pitch and skating apart along the crest of a rising vapor-boil.

Nothing here is soft. Nothing sings, nothing digs burrows, nothing leaves dung or bone or bruised petals behind, because anything messy or loud or hungry in the old evolutionary way would be diagnosed as mechanical damage by a three-thousand-mile-wide engine and sealed over before nightfall. Whatever lives here—whether shooting the dark cataracts inside the trunks, splicing broken pipes down on the knife-edged basalt, or surfing the piezoelectric thunder up under the clouds—lives the way a flicker of static lives in a transformer, or water-hammer lives in a main. They are creatures that have learned to hold completely still inside the fury of someone else’s design, disguising their breath as weather and their hunger as friction, waiting out an eternity in which the rock has not yet had time to turn to dust.
