You have to wait for your eyes to stop trying to separate the plumbing from the stems.

If you stand on the grated landing with your hand on the wet handrail—feeling the sixty-cycle hum climb into your wrist bones until your own pulse seems to lock onto the generator’s phase—the green mass stays entirely still for five, ten, fifteen minutes at a time. The flywheel turns: *one, two, three, four*. The relief valve coughs warm oil-mist into the ginger leaves, making them nod once and drip. A bead of condensate runs along the belly of an overhead return pipe, fattens at a flange bolt, and drops into the duckweed with a sound nobody could ever hear.

Then you notice a length of reddish aerial root hanging beside the transformer housing that isn't hanging quite plumb.

The air in the basin is dead calm, heavy as wet flannel, so nothing swings. Yet this particular strand, thick as a man’s thumb and furred with pale root-hairs, has a slow, deliberate hook at its tip. While you watch, the hook opens, reaches three inches sideways across the gap between a brick pilaster and a high-voltage conduit, touches the warm rubber jacket, and holds. Higher up, hidden under the broad, waxy umbrella of an elephant ear, the parent mass shifts its weight.

At first you would swear it is just a slipped knot of *Philodendron* root-stock, a burl of fibrous bark and peat-dark humus that has torn loose from a mortar joint and is settling into the fork of a cast-iron bracket. It is the size of a bundled winter coat, shaggy with liverwort and dripping rootlets. Wait another ten minutes, though, and you see that it is not wedged in the bracket; it is *holding* the bracket. Five thick, woody appendages—neither quite root nor quite limb, jointed like bamboo or the legs of a stick-insect, but covered in flaky, lenticel-spotted periderm—are wrapped around the warm return pipe, drinking the heat straight through their bark.

There are perhaps a dozen of them in the basin, though you will never count more than two in an afternoon, because they do not travel so much as *relocate* at the speed of a clock’s hour hand.

They have no eyes—eyes would only foul with oil-film and mineral scale in here—and nothing you could call a face. What they have instead is a dense, sensitive crown of pale, blunted tendrils tucked underneath the main bole, protected from the bright overhead sky like the gills of a mushroom. When one of them decides to move down toward the sumps, those pale feelers uncurl first, tasting the stone and metal ahead. They read the basin entirely through heat, chemical film, and the precise geometry of vibration. To a creature whose whole body is a bundle of fluid-filled vessels and taut wooden fibers, the sixty-cycle hum of the turbine house isn't deafening noise; it is daylight. Every bolted flange, every hollow conduit, every hairline crack in the settling brick sings at a slightly different harmonic pitch under the generator's drive. Where a transformer bushing carries live current, the sixty-hertz buzz sharpens into an electric prickle that the pale crown-tendrils flinch away from long before they cross the six-inch pruned halo left by the maintenance crew’s shears.

Watch the one on the return pipe as the afternoon light shifts off the upper brickwork. Slowly, one of its woody limbs unclasps from the iron collar. There is no muscle under that bark—it moves by hydraulic turgor, pumping mineral-heavy sap from chamber to chamber inside its joints with a faint, internal creak you couldn’t hear even if your ear were pressed against it. It lowers a curtain of fine, rusty-red feeding threads down into the quarter-inch sheet of warm water sliding across the buried flagstones.

Where the threads touch the runoff, they fan out into a delicate, lacy weir, six feet wide, trapping the microscopic drift that nothing else in the yard wants: iron-oxidizing bacteria, sloughed velvet algae, soot washed down from the corrugated eaves, and the faint, iridescent slick of petroleum weep from the flywheel sump. Over decades in this sunken pocket, whatever ancestral root-crawler or colonial bryozoan washed in through the river intakes has married its metabolism to the utility yard. Within the spongy, peat-like core of its body live dense colonies of sulfur- and hydrocarbon-eating microbes, stewing at eighty-five degrees. It skims the rainbow sheen off the grease-trap overflow and digests it into slow, dense lignins and dark amber resin.

That is why the municipal maintenance man—coming down the iron ladder once every ten weeks with his grease gun, his brass padlock keys, and his insulated bypass loppers—has never filed a report on them, and maybe has never quite realized they are there.

When the heavy steel door in the upper tenement clangs open fifty feet above, and boot-heels ring on the top rung of the catwalk ladder, the foreign vibration drops instantly down the steel stanchions into the masonry. Every one of the boles feels the irregular, heavy thudding cut across the smooth four-second rhythm of the flywheel. Instantly, they vent water from their joint-bladders and lock. Feeding fans retract into indistinguishable hanks of aerial root; jointed limbs flatten along the mortar seams or braid themselves tightly into the existing curtains of *Cissus* and strangler fig. By the time the workman reaches the grated landing at the bottom, wiping his neck with a rag in the soup-warm mist, the creature beside the wheel valve is just another mossy knee of root-stock wedged behind a pipe hanger, wearing the same ten-year-old splatter of municipal green paint on its oldest dorsal plates that it caught the last time a painter came through with a spray rig.

In fact, the maintenance man’s work is half done before he arrives.

Look closely at the brass pressure gauges with their convex glass domes. Around the verdigris-ringed bezels, the creeping fig and star moss stop abruptly, rubbed clean away, and the glass itself bears faint, concentric smears, as if wiped by a wet chamois. At night, when the basin is pitch-black and just as warm as noon, the smaller individuals—no bigger than two cupped hands, still soft-shelled and olive-green like oversized fern crosiers—haul themselves up the valve stems. They are drawn to the tiny, rhythmic tick of the Bourdon tubes trembling inside the brass cases. They press their flat, velvety undersides right over the convex glass to soak up the crisp mechanical pulse of the needle, grazing off the green algal film and mineral crust in the process, leaving the dials clear enough to read at six paces.

Lower down, in the flooded, olive-lit gloom beneath the cable trays and tree ferns, lives the rest of the basin’s silent traffic, almost all of it clinging to the boundary layer where water is only a millimeter deep.

Lift a corner of the root-felt on the iron grating—it peels back reluctantly, tough as vulcanized rubber—and the warm iron underneath is tiled with flat, blind isopods the color of tarnished pewter. Their shells are flanged out wide around the margins like tiny limpets so the constant sheet-flow presses them down instead of washing them into the sumps. They have heavy, lead-dense carapaces compounded from the dissolved iron and lime in the mortar seep; if you drop one into a puddle, it sinks like a buckshot pellet. They graze in tight, overlapping rows along the weeping rivets, chewing away the soft orange blooms of fresh rust before it can pit the structural steel, leaving behind microscopic trails of black magnetite grit that wash neatly into the drains.

And in the settling tanks themselves, under that tight, lawn-like carpet of duckweed and water lettuce, something else holds station right at the edge of the submerged discharge pipe’s lazy boil.

It never breaks the surface. If you parted the water lettuce with a stick, the green mat would simply close back over your wrist like warm porridge, and you would see only murky brown river-silt suspended in current. But if you pushed a bare broom-handle straight down four feet to the riveted steel floor of the tank, you would strike what feels like heavy submerged tarpaulin—leathery, ribbed, and warm—lying flat across the bottom plates from corner to corner.

It isn't an animal with fins or bones; the low-frequency thrum of the penstocks thirty yards north would shatter a swim bladder in an hour and drive anything with a lateral line frantic. Instead, it is a single, broad mat of colonial freshwater sponge and bryozoan tissue, four inches thick and tough as conveyor belting, reinforced with silica spicules drawn from the alluvial clay. It covers the entire floor of the tank like a living gasket. Thousands of tiny, muscular oscula—chimney-like pores the diameter of a pencil—stand up from its surface in neat, geometric rows aligned to the slow hydraulic eddy of the discharge pipe. They open and shut in long, lazy waves that match the four-second *chuff* of the pneumatic relief valve outside: drawing in turbid river water, stripping out every grain of suspended clay and decaying leaf-meal from the canopy above, and exhaling water so clear it emerges over the mossy concrete spill-lip looking like melted glass. Once a year or so, when its lower layers become choked with compacted river mud, it splits off dense, heavy bricks of bound silt from its underside and nudges them slowly, millimeter by millimeter via cilia and hydraulic heave, over the sludge-weir so the tank never fills in.

Even the banded snail on the dead porcelain insulator is not quite an ordinary garden stray. Its shell is extraordinarily thick, banded not in yellow and brown, but in dull copper and slate-black, built from metal salts licked off the weeping brickwork. Its muscular foot is broad and deeply grooved to grip wet glaze while the steel rack trembles beneath it. Every few hours, it reaches the bottom skirt of the insulator, turns deliberately before its tentacles touch the bare galvanized pin where stray induced voltage tingles in the wet rust, and climbs back up into the warm drip-line under the canopy creepers.

Nothing here fights the machinery, and nothing is crushed by it. Around the exposed four-second flywheel beside the grease-trap sump, a massive, ancient bole—the largest of the root-crawlers, thick as an oil drum and plated in bark that looks almost indistinguishable from cast iron—has grown or settled into the narrow brick alcove behind the axle bearing. It has been there so long that two strangler-fig buttresses have fused across its back, pinning it willingly in place, though its lower limbs still shift fractionally when the seasons change the river’s temperature.

One of its blunt, calloused wooden limbs rests permanently against the cast-iron bearing cap, soaking up the steady one-hundred-and-ten-degree friction heat of the turning shaft. Another limb hangs down within a quarter-inch of the flywheel’s heavy iron rim. As the wheel comes around—*one, two, three, four*—carrying tiny clots of flung bearing grease and stray wind-dropped leaflets stuck to its slick metal edge, a stiff, brush-like pad of dry root-fibers at the tip of that hanging limb skims the iron with the lightest possible whisper of contact.

It cleans the rim on every single revolution, licking off the warm tallow-and-mineral grease before it can fling onto the walkways, drawing the rich, heavy carbon slowly back up into its dark interior, while fifty feet overhead the flat afternoon sky passes unseen across the pipe-bridges, and the turbines roar on without a break in their stride.
