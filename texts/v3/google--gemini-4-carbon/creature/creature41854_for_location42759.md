At first, you would swear you are entirely alone, and for hours of wading along the satin-smooth cobbles, you would be nearly right. The city does not teem. Anything with lungs like yours, or blood that needs cooling, would drown standing up in that broth of air before a day was out. Whatever belongs here does not fight the heat or try to seal itself off from the brine; it shares the same temperature, the same osmotic pressure, the same slow, boundaryless patience.

You find the signs of them before you ever catch one in the act of existing.

Along the low clay dikes of an interior courtyard, there will be a smear of fresher, darker silt laid neatly over a crack where the water had begun to seep the wrong way—pressed flat not by a trowel, and not quite by fingers, but by something broad, soft, and faintly ridged, like the heel of a giant palm or the muscular foot of a snail. Near the base of the pale, waxy stalks, you might notice three or four river cobbles stacked in a precarious, off-center cairn that holds together despite having no right to balance. Wait beside it for twenty minutes while the upside-down pools gather on the ceiling overhead, and when a fat, feather-slow drop finally lands on the top stone, the whole cairn isn't knocked over; instead, it has already been disassembled and laid out in a neat row six feet to the left, though nothing crossed the tarnished-copper sheen of the water between you and it.

What lives here are the **Sluice-Keepers**, though *keeper* implies a vigilance too sharp for what they do, and a plural implies it is always easy to tell where one ends and the next begins.

You first see one by mistaking it for an architectural correction.

Across an acre of open flat, where a sudden square of warm rain is hissing down into the ankle-deep brine, a heap of wet, terracotta-dark clay appears to slump out from the base of a colonnade. It does not splash. It eases into the water like heavy batter, gathering mass that wasn’t visible against the pillar a moment before. As it moves out onto the causeway, it draws itself up—not onto bones, for there isn't a lever or hinge anywhere inside it, but by hydraulic pressure, inflating internal chambers with the warm brine until it stands five or six feet high.

Up close—and you may find yourself up close without having walked the intervening distance, turning around from an empty archway to discover one occupying the step just below yours—its body has the bruised, semi-translucent luster of salt-cured kelp or thick tallow, filmed over with fine black silt that shifts in slow, marbled currents across its skin. It has no face, no eyes needed for the dim copper under-light, and no mouth for eating, because nothing here eats in the way surface things do. Instead, its upper mass folds over itself in heavy, cowled pleats, like damp linen draped over a bust, or the layered mantle of a tremendous, shell-less mollusk. Beneath those folds open and close slow, puckered siphons that draw in the saturated air and express thin trickles of mineral-heavy water down its flanks.

When it walks, it doesn't step so much as pour its weight forward onto two thick, pillar-like pedestals that flatten into broad suction-pads against the cobbles, then peel up again without a sound—unless you are standing half a mile away on the limestone terraces, in which case that soft, wet detachment might sound right inside the shell of your ear like someone turning a damp page.

They are the ones who hollowed the staircases. Not ten thousand bare feet long vanished, though there were more of them once, ages ago when the basin’s heat was younger; rather, a few hundred tireless, heavy-bottomed bodies ascending and descending the same drowned steps over epochs, carrying loads of dissolved travertine inside themselves and sweating it back out onto the lintels to keep the arches from giving way entirely.

Watch one at work inside a vast, impossible courtyard, and you begin to understand the city’s broken geometry. The space isn't malfunctioning; it is being tended by creatures whose own anatomy has come unmoored from ordinary distance.

A Keeper kneels—or rather, settles into a broad, bell-shaped mound—beside one of the low clay partitions. Two long, boneless limbs, thick at the shoulder and tapering to broad, spatulate paddles, unroll from beneath its cowl. It slides both paddles down into two inches of glowing brine, sinking them to the elbow, then to the shoulder, right through what ought to be solid packed earth. Thirty yards away, at the far end of the courtyard where steam hangs thick beneath an arcade, those same two pale-dark paddles emerge *upward* out of a dry stone bench, lifting a block of porous limestone that has grown too heavy with salt, drawing it down into the bench until it surfaces between the Keeper’s knees in a gentle eddy of copper light.

They use the warped seams of the basin the way a weaver uses a loom, or a canal-man uses locks. They know why a stone gutter runs uphill; they built the incline three centuries ago to catch a stray vein of gravity that drifted down from the upper terraces, banking the clay carefully so the brisk, warm current wouldn't spill out and scour away a neighboring plaza. When a curtain of rain starts falling twenty feet up and stops in midair, you will sometimes see two or three Keepers gathered beneath that invisible roof, lifting their pleated cowls toward the dry boundary. They reach up into the empty air until their forearms vanish at the wrist, groping gently inside the unseen cistern overhead, clearing away a blockage of mineral scale that you cannot see until a fistful of white, crusty travertine drops out of nothingness into their waiting cradles.

Are they people? Were they ever?

That is the hardest thing to settle as you watch them. There is something hauntingly civic in their habits, a leftover muscle memory of municipal life that outlasted eyes, speech, and individual names. Sometimes, on the wide benches of those flooded amphitheaters, four or five of them will sit side by side for hours, submerged to their waists, doing no repair work at all. The clay benches stay warm beneath them; when they finally slide off into the dark water, they leave behind those beaded outlines of condensation that look so painfully like the trace of a human sitter. While they sit together, their mantles touch at the edges and actually fuse for a while—silt and clear lymph circulating freely from one body into the next, equalizing heat, trading dissolved salts and slow chemical memories of which dome shifted east since the last steam-breath, which mud-wall finally let go in the outer flats, which colony of pale stalks needs replanting at a corner where the space has stretched too thin.

When they separate again, pulling apart like warm wax, there are not always the same number of them as sat down. Four may arrive at the bench, pool their sluggish, ancient thoughts into a single wide mound for half a day while water climbs the columns behind them, and then divide into three larger ones—or six smaller, nimble ones shaped almost like crouched youths—each gliding off down a different radial cobble-fan on its own errand.

They are not the only inhabitants, though the others are little more than specialized thoughts the city—or the Keepers themselves—shed into independent flesh.

Down inside the deep, submerged stairwells that drop past the basin’s true floor into the source of that tarnished-copper glow, things move that never break the surface. If you crouch at the edge of an interior pool and stare down past the seventh or eighth step, where the water grows thick and syrupy with dissolved ore, you will see the **Lantern-Skins**: broad, paper-thin sheets of living membrane, wide as bedsheets and veined with dull metallic copper, undulating with agonizing slowness from step to step. They graze on the heat rising from the deep faults below the silt, drinking the raw geothermal dark and turning it into that bruised, sourceless under-light. Now and then a Keeper wades down into the stairwell until the brine closes over its cowl, disappearing for an hour, and comes back up draped in one of these glowing skins like a shawl—carrying light into a windowless aqueduct that has forgotten how to touch the ground, pasting the living membrane against an interior vault where it clings and dimly shines for months before dissolving back into the drip.

And those waxy, wrist-thick stalks at the corners of foundations are not quite plants. If you lay a bare hand on one—it feels neither cold nor slimy, just smooth and firm as warm candle-tallow, pulsing once a minute—it does not flinch from you so much as reconsider its placement. They are **Plumb-Stems**, blind boundary-markers rooted into the shifting substrata where the basin's geometry slips most fiercely. Their roots don't seek water; water is everywhere. They seek *adjacency*. When two avenues quietly trade places in the dark, the stalks feel the shear in the silt below, draw themselves down through a shortcut in the mud, and push back up wherever the corner went, anchoring the foundation just enough that the bathhouse doesn't drift clean out of reach of its own causeway. The Keepers tend them almost affectionately, wiping salt-crust from their pale skins with soft, silt-slicked paddles, sometimes leaning a heavy, eyeless cowl against a cluster of stems to feel the deep structural hum of the basin through their roots.

As for how they regard an intruder—something small, hot-blooded, upright, gasping the wet air and splashing loudly with every step—they do not treat you as an enemy, or even quite as an animal.

To them, you are a leak. You are a small, erratic knot of high pressure wandering unchanneled across the flats, shedding sharp little noises into the pressurized hush and displacing water in messy, concentric rings that shouldn't be there.

If you stay long enough, two or three of them will eventually begin to work around you. You won't hear them approach; you will simply look back down the causeway and find a broad, clay-dark figure standing ten paces behind you, its pleated cowl tilted slightly as its siphons taste the strange, salt-sharp sweat rolling off your skin. Look ahead, and another is already kneeling at the next non-existent intersection, patting wet adobe into a low lip across the cobbles.

They won't strike you or seize you. They move with the gentle, implacable courtesy of caretakers redirecting an overflow. By banking a curve of mud here, or shifting three cobbles there, they alter which way the path leads. You step around one of them to keep walking straight toward the limestone ridges, wade twenty yards through the bath-warm shallows, stoop through what looks like a ruined garden wall to get clear of them—and emerge back on the central plaza, half a mile behind where you were, with the rain stopping in midair beside you and your footsteps already smoothed out of the black silt.

If you were to collapse at last from the heat, folding down onto one of the smooth clay benches where the air feels swallowed rather than breathed, they would come and sit beside you. Slowly, without malice or hurry, they would drape cool-warm, wet pleats over your shoulders to stop your terrible, wasteful evaporation, letting the brine sheet up over your ankles and knees, trying patiently to equalize your pressure with the room’s—trying to help you settle in and learn how to hold up an arch, or carry a gutter uphill, like everything else that endures at the bottom of the dark.
