To find who tends the basin, you have to stop looking at the ground. Nobody walks in the mire if they can help it; a human boot punches straight through the floating root-skin, breaking a seal that took forty years of tangled sedge and bacterial glue to weave, and the scalding mud underneath will blister an ankle through thick rubber in ten minutes. 

Instead, look to the sprawling, chest-high canopy where the woody shrubs lock elbows over the water, and look to the undersides of the sixty-ton harvesters themselves.

Suspended beneath the forward chassis of the oldest rig—Number Four, whose copper heat-exchangers have long since gone the bruised, blackish green of pond-weed—hangs a wide sling of braided polymer mesh. Inside it lies a man in his fifties, naked to the waist, gleaming with the same mineral-sweet slick that coats the leaves. His chest is noticeably deep, barrel-sprung by generations of fourteen-thousand-foot ancestry and his own decades in the bowl, his ribs shifting in slow, practiced bellows-strokes that pull thin air all the way down to the bottom of his lungs without the frantic panting of a lowlander. His skin is pale from living under steam and broad leaves, except across his shoulders and the bridge of his nose, which are burned the color of cured mahogany by the fierce, unfiltered ultraviolet that stabs through whenever the mist parts.

He is asleep, or nearly so, rocked by the glacial, six-inch-per-minute creep of the harvester. Ten inches beneath his shoulder blades, knee-deep bathwater slides past over velvety ochre silt. Just above his nose, the warm steel belly of the machine ticks softly as its internal condensers cycle. When Number Four lets out its rhythmic pneumatic sigh, the man’s breathing unconsciously matches it.

There are eleven of them in the basin at any given time—the Sump-tenders, the low-valley dispatchers call them on the ledgers, though among themselves they rarely use titles at all. They do not live here permanently, because the human body eventually rebels against perpetual saturation; leave a person in ninety-eight-degree, hundred-percent humidity for six months straight and the skin between their toes and under their arms begins to slough away, colonized by the very same velvety green thermophiles that glaze the basalt bolsters. Yet they cannot stay away long, either. Down in the dry, freezing mining towns at nine thousand feet, their enlarged, high-pressure hearts beat too hard, the dry air cracks their sinuses until they bleed, and the roar of diesel trucks and gravel crushers makes them flinch and cover their ears. So they work four weeks in, two weeks out, riding down and up in a pressurized personnel basket slung beneath the Tuesday supply aerostat.

Right now, three hundred yards upslope from Number Four, a younger woman named Maren is moving across a flooded terrace without touching the mud. 

She wears wide, oval frames strapped to her calves and forearms—not snowshoes, though they look like them, weight-distributing hoops strung with taut, hydrophobic webbing. Moving on all fours like a slow, deliberate spider across the top of the chest-high bladder-fern thicket, she spreads her weight across four square feet of rubbery foliage at once. The muscular, stunted branches bow six inches under her, pooling warm rainwater around her wrists and knees, then spring slowly back as she shifts forward. Around her waist rides a belt heavy with brass spanners, silicone grease-tubes, and ceramic scraper-blades—no iron or cheap steel, which the lithium brines and hydrogen sulfide traces would turn to orange paste in a week. 

At the edge of a brimming, turquoise-tinted pan, she stops and lowers her head toward a cluster of wheelbarrow-sized bromeliads. 

A young browser—three hundred pounds of dark, coarse-haired muscle, slick as an oiled seal, with flared, valvular nostrils and feet splayed wide like dinner plates—is wedged sideways between two thick shrub-trunks, up to its hocks in hot silt. It isn't stuck; it is scraping its flank against a crust of sulfur-yellow mineral scale on an exposed stone bolster, eyes half-shut in idiot bliss. As it heaves backward, its heavy rump bumps the lowered, segmented proboscis-pipe of Harvester Two, which is currently sunk forty feet into a hairline vent just beyond the sedge.

The harvester’s proximity sensors register the lateral pressure against its pipe. Instantly, with a soft hydraulic *shhh*, the sixty-ton machine freezes its forward creep and bleeds pressure from its boom so the rigid metal collar won't shear off the delicate sinter lip of the geological vent.

Maren clicks her tongue twice against the roof of her mouth—a sharp, wet sound that carries surprisingly well where shouting only dies in the soggy leaves. The browser turns its heavy, tapir-snouted head, ears flicking droplets, and regards her with small, unhurried brown eyes. Neither of them is afraid of the other. Nothing with teeth larger than a rail’s beak has ever climbed the freezing, mile-wide scree outside the rim. To the browsers, the machines are simply enormous, slow-moving boulders that smell pleasantly of warm soybean hydraulic fluid and always know where the best, saltiest mud-wallows are; the humans are merely large, pale monkeys that occasionally pry ticks from behind an animal's ears or shoo it away from a valve-stem.

Maren reaches down with a long, hooked fiberglass prod—the kind they keep clipped to the dorsal railings of every rig—and gives the browser a firm, familiar poke right behind the hipbone, where the skin is ticklish. The animal grunts, a deep, resonant *whuff* that ripples the surface of the pan, hauls its broad hindquarters out of the silt with a wet sucking sound, and lumbers ten paces away to rip up a dripping mat of water-weed. Within seconds, Harvester Two’s internal logic registers the cleared strain gauge; a solenoid clicks, and the bladder-wheels resume their imperceptible roll.

That is half the work here: peeling life gently off the machinery before one damages the other.

Left entirely to automated programming for three weeks, a harvester does not break down in any dramatic, smoking fashion. Instead, the basin simply swallows it. Pale, leafless orchids find the ninety-degree copper radiator fins vastly superior to tree bark and curtain them so thickly in spongy aerial roots that the heat-exchangers choke and stall. Platter-sized snails rasp away at the vulcanized rubber seals around the optical sensors, drawn by trace calcium in the curing compound, blinding the rig until it sits helplessly in a bog-meadow waiting for a satellite ping that can’t cleanly penetrate a dense ground-steam bank. Translucent amphipods swarm up the condensate drain-tubes and pack them solid. Even the pink marsh-frogs lay strings of glistening, heat-tolerant eggs inside the recessed coupling collars on the dorsal tanks; if a tender doesn’t climb up with a wash-bulb and gently flush the spawn into a warm puddle before the silvered aerostat drops out of the cobalt sky, the descending umbilical clacks shut on a mash of jelly, fouling the check-valves.

By midday, when the sun stands straight overhead and the light turns brutally bright—etching every water-beaded fern frond in hard, shadowless glare while boiling thin white vapor off the stone ledges—nobody works at all. Even the browsers sink up to their eyes in the cooler, shaded runnels at the backs of the terraces, only their dark, wet nostrils breaking the water.

This is when the tenders gather, usually in twos or threes, on the broad, rounded hull of whichever rig happens to be parked nearest the central vents. 

They rig a tarpaulin of silvered mylar—salvaged from retired aerostat skins—between two hydraulic booms to block the searing high-altitude sun, creating a patch of deep, luminous shade. There are no campfires here; nothing in the basin has been dry enough to burn since the Pleistocene, and open flames are forbidden anyway under the charter that keeps the shelf unscarred. To cook, they lower sealed titanium canisters on braided lines straight down into an unharvested fissure thirty feet away, where clear, superheated water pulses at just below the altitude-lowered boiling point. Twenty minutes later they haul up poached tubers, dense flatbreads steamed heavy and chewy, and tins of salted mutton sent up from the cold valleys—rich, fatty food, because you lose salt so fast through your skin in the conservatory that without constant brine-tablets and grease your calves knot up with cramps by dusk.

They eat sitting cross-legged on the warm brushed alloy, passing a canteen of cooled condensate laced with lime and potassium, speaking in the low, flat, uninflected cadence the basin teaches everyone after their first year. Raising your voice in thin air wastes breath, and the wet greenery eats high pitches within twenty yards anyway, so their talk consists mostly of murmured shorthand, long comfortable silences, and small hand-signs flashed across the mist. 

Every one of them carries the subtle physical hallmarks of the shelf. Their fingernails and toenails are thick and stained a faint, permanent ochre at the cuticles from mineral water. Their hair is kept cropped close to the skull to prevent mildew, and they smell—not unpleasantly—of tea-tree salve, lanolin, wet stone, and crushed wild ginger, which they rub onto their ankles and wrists twice a day to keep fungal rashes at bay and discourage the tiny steam-flies from drinking at the corners of their eyes. Most of them are older. Twenty-year-olds brought up by the company rarely last two rotations; the crushing stillness, the lack of a horizon beyond the jagged grey teeth of the rim, the absence of screens or engines roaring or anywhere to *walk* fast, drives restless minds frantic. Those who stay are people with slow pulses and meticulous hands—former watchmakers, deep-sea saturation divers retired from cold oceans, high-altitude botanists who came up on a six-month survey grant twenty years ago and quietly transferred over to maintenance payroll so they wouldn't have to leave.

Around them, while they eat, the smaller citizens of the rig come out to beg or forage.

A flock of the iridescent, flightless rails—plump, chicken-sized birds with oversized yellow feet that let them trot across floating algae mats without sinking—have learned that humans scraping scale off a chassis means a windfall of cooked amphipods and fat, stunned grubs. Seven or eight of them patrol right up to the bulbous rubber bladder-wheels below the tenders, whistling in tiny, liquid trills, necks darting as they peck mineral flakes off the hubs. Higher up, along the warm copper pipes under the mylar shade, a pair of hand-sized, translucent geckos—endemic nowhere else on earth, evolved over half a million years from some storm-blown ancestor trapped in this solitary hot pocket—cling upside down, watching the humans’ Bread crumbs with huge, lidless obsidian eyes, their rapid little hearts plainly visible beating pinkly behind wet, see-through ribs.

Nobody shoos them. Nobody drops a wrapper, or spits phlegm into a pool, or carves initials into a woody shrub stem. It isn't out of mystical reverence so much as an acute, practical understanding of margins: the bowl is only five miles wide, sealed in by ice and sterile scree, recycling its own heat and water in a tight, feverish loop. Drop an unsterilized apple core from the lowlands into that warm bacterial soup, or spill two gallons of unrefined grease onto an ochre pan, and within six months you would watch a foreign rot bloom unimpeded from terrace to terrace until the root-mats died, the silt washed down into the fissures, the vents clogged, and the whole ancient engine choked on mud. Even human waste goes into sealed catalytic bladders tucked behind the harvesters’ main turbines, cooked down by waste heat into sterile grey ash and flown out under the aerostats right alongside the lithium slurry.

Late in the afternoon, the wind outside the massif usually shifts, and you see the strangest boundary in the world.

Up at the jagged lip of the outer rim, a thousand feet above the highest terraced bogs, a bank of freezing grey cloud spills halfway over the stone teeth—and instantly vanishes, eaten alive by the rising column of invisible thermal heat from the bowl, except for brief, ragged streamers of snow that fall two hundred feet toward the greenery before melting in midair into warm rain. 

Down on the middle shelf, night arrives abruptly as the sun drops behind the western crags. Within ten minutes, the stark cobalt overhead deepens to indigo, and as the upper air cools by five or six degrees, the entire five-mile conservatory exhales at once. Thick, milky ground-steam rises waist-high, then shoulder-high, turning the tangled thickets into dark, submerged reefs.

The harvesters do not turn on floodlights; bright white beams would draw millions of night-midges and blind the nocturnal browsers that do most of their heavy feeding in the dark. Instead, faint, deep-amber marker diodes—dim as glowworms—wink on along their hulls. 

Inside the belly of each machine, between the insulated water-jackets of the silent turbines and the curving bulk of the dorsal slurry tank, is a dry, cork-lined crawlspace six feet wide and nine feet long, kept dehumidified by a bleed-valve off the air condensers. One or two tenders climb up the slick alloy rungs before full dark, stripping off their wet harnesses and rinsing themselves under a warm condensate spigot on the deck before ducking through the oval hatch and dogging it shut—not completely tight, leaving an inch of gap so they can still hear the valley.

Others prefer the hammocks slung high in the thicker ironwood-shrubs near the upper terraces, zipped inside breathable gore-mesh cocoons where the breeze off the hot rocks rocks them five feet above the wet turf.

Once the hatches close and the zippers pull shut, the basin belongs entirely to the dark and the water. Through the cracked seal of a crawlspace, a waking tender hears nothing human at all. Only the endless, heavy *pat-pat-pat* of condensation dropping from swollen leaves onto wide, sleeping backs; the hollow, wooden *tok* of a giant snail’s shell bumping stone as it grazes along a warm fissure; a chorus of tiny pink frogs chiming like glass beads shaken in a wet palm; and underneath it all, felt through the hull plates right into the sternum, the deep, slow, pneumatic breathing of sixty tons of metal drinking hot salt from the roots of the mountain, pausing for three minutes while a herd of heavy, invisible shapes splashes slowly past in the mist, then sighing once and moving six inches on.
