You realize something else is here only when you notice what is missing from the floor.

Directly beneath a vault ceiling packed with thousands of roosting swifts, bats, and pigeons, the pale *leucobryum* moss should be choked and burned white with guano. It should be littered with cracked eggshells, shed primary feathers, the dry, papery husks of hawk-moths, and the stiff, occasional corpses of fledglings that misjudged their first drop into the empty middle air. Instead, the wide floor is immaculate. The pale green cushions swell up smooth and unbroken from buttress to buttress, clean as a swept chapel.

If you stand completely still—letting the *chug-hum-chug* of the distant turbines travel up through your ankles and settle into your ribs until your own heartbeat syncs to the industrial bass—you will eventually see the moss move. 

Not the moss itself, which is rooted, but a cushion of it slightly broader than the others, perhaps the size and low dome-shape of a sleeping hound or an overturned bushel basket. It detaches seamlessly from the surrounding pale-green mat and glides six inches to the left. There is no sound of footsteps, though even if it dragged iron chains you wouldn’t hear it over the engines and the screaming birds. It moves with a smooth, hydraulic steadiness, sliding over the damp peat on a broad, muscular foot, or perhaps a dense brush of hundreds of short, cartilaginous legs hidden entirely beneath a heavy, scalloped mantle. 

There are maybe forty of them in the room at any given time, spaced far apart across the two-hundred-pace expanse, tending the floor.

Up close, their disguise is not mimicry; it is husbandry. Their upper carapaces—leathery, flexible plates colored like wet slate and old silver bark—are deeply pitted like volcanic tufa, and in those pits they cultivate a living pelt of the exact same *leucobryum* moss that carpets the chamber. Droplets of cold mist bead on their backs just as they do on the floor. When one pauses and tucks its margin flush against the peat, your eye slides right off it. Only when a shaft of crisp upper daylight strikes one directly as it crosses an open patch do you see the slight, rhythmic ripple of breathing beneath the green velvet, and the pale, glistening fringe of sensory filaments fanning out from its front edge, testing the vibrations in the floor.

They are creatures of the bass note. Blind, or nearly so—possessing only a scatter of simple light-pits along their rims to tell day-shafts from shadow—they inhabit the room strictly as an acoustic and tactile map. Because the distant facility never throttles down, the giant wooden cello of the fused trees contains standing waves: permanent, invisible architecture made of sound. There are dead spots near certain braided root-arches where the frequencies cancel out, and heavy, throbbing nodes out in the center where the turbine-hum presses against the eardrums like deep water. The grazers navigate along the seams of these vibrations as surely as rail-cars on tracks.

Watch one work, and you see why the floor stays pristine. An exhausted swift feather spirals down through sixty feet of cool, clear air, catching the light once, twice, before touching the moss. The impact is weightless, utterly silent, lost beneath ten thousand shrieking swallows and a million horsepower of steam and steel half a mile away. Yet within seconds, two of the low green domes twenty paces off pivot smoothly toward the spot. They haven't heard the feather land; they have felt the microscopic interruption of the turbine-hum rippling across the taut, drumskin surface of the compressed root-mat. 

The closer one arrives first. Its front margin lifts slightly—revealing flesh the color of a peeled mushroom stem, tough and cold and gleaming with clean mucus—and rolls over the feather. Beneath that mantle, silent, rasping radulae reduce keratin, bone, droppings, and fallen bark-scales into a fine, mineral-rich paste in minutes, which passes straight through their flat bodies and is pressed directly back into the spongy peat behind them, feeding the roots of the colossus that walls their world.

They are the slow, immaculate housekeepers of the hollow, older than the engines, older perhaps than the grafting of the trees, remnants of the ancient coastal pan that crept inside when the trunks first rotted hollow and never found a reason to leave.

Yet they are not the only things down here, and the iron ground-cable proves it.

If you follow that thick, braided iron rope across the room, your eyes expect it to be rusted orange-red in the constant cellar damp. Instead, the middle sixty feet of it—where it bows slightly above a shallow trough in the moss—has been rubbed to a dull, pewter shine, greasy with lanolin or tallow, and polished by heavy, habitual contact. And tucked into the deep, dry gothic embrasure where two massive root-buttresses braid together just above where the cable vanishes into the swallowing bark, there is a structure that was neither grown by the tree nor dropped by the birds.

It looks, at first glance, like a massive burl or a wasp gallery made of chewed silver wood. Only slowly do your eyes pick out the weave. Twigs from the upper canopy, shed pigeon primaries stripped to the quill, strips of fibrous inner cedar bark, and thin, bright copper wire—scavenged from God knows where outside, years ago—have been plaited together under tremendous finger-strength into a dry, cantilevered cradle twelve feet off the ground.

What lives in that cradle is solitary, or down to its last one, and right now it is forty feet above you, clinging motionless to a vertical flute of silver-gray bark.

It is roughly the size of a lean ten-year-old child, though built on entirely different proportions—all long, flat leverage and terrifying grip. You might call it an ape if you saw only the shoulders and the broad, barrel ribcage built for bellows-lungs; you might call it a marsupial or some huge, archaic arboreal mammal if you saw the long, counterbalancing rudder of its hairless tail pressed flat against the wood, or the way its ankles rotate fully backward so it can walk straight down a sheer trunk headfirst. Its coat is dense, plush, and water-repellent, banded in vertical mottles of lichen-pale gray and wet-charcoal dark that break up its outline completely against the weeping streaks of condensation on the bark.

It has no external ears left to speak of—just tight, muscular slits folded flat and sealed with waxy fur against the relentless acoustic assault of the room. Instead, its hands and feet are enormous, pale-palmed, and splayed like a gecko’s, with long, spatulate fingertips pressed wide against the wood. It listens with its palms. Through the living timber, it reads the entire hollow at once: the sway of the canopy boughs in the coastal wind two hundred feet up, the shift of squirrel colonies along the eastern curve, the steady, reassuring *chug-hum-chug* of the world’s heart beating half a mile away.

Its eyes are huge, set forward in a flat, solemn face—not the golden, nocturnal eyes of an owl, because the hollow has normal daylight, but clear, pale-slate eyes with horizontal pupils like a goat’s, giving it a panoramic sweep of the vast, uncluttered cylinder of air. 

Everything about its body has been pared down and tuned by eighty years—four or five of its generations, perhaps—of living inside an engine-strummed wooden bell. Outside, whatever sparse cousin-stock it came from probably vanished decades ago when the plain was cleared or dammed or industrialized. Inside, this one thrives in an acoustic fortress. Nothing large enough to threaten it can bear the noise long enough to hunt here; nothing small enough to be eaten can hear it coming.

Watch it hunt, and the eerie discipline of the room makes sudden, sharp sense.

It does not climb into the chaotic, screaming rookeries at the roof. Up there, ten thousand tight-packed wings and sharp beaks and frantic squirrels make footing treacherous and movement obvious. Instead, it waits forty feet up the clean, bare middle wall, utterly still for an hour at a time, breathing the cold cedar-and-ozone air so slowly its damp flanks barely rise. Its flat slate eyes track the shafts of daylight.

High above, two wood-pigeons jostle on a narrow bark-scallop. One loses its footing, drops ten feet before catching the air, and banks lazily out into the open central volume to circle back up—taking the easy, uncluttered middle air where nothing ever grows and nothing ever reaches.

The creature on the wall doesn't crouch to telegraph its spring. One instant it is part of the fluted gray bark; the next, it has pushed off perpendicular to the wall with silent, explosive violence. Spread-eagled in the cool shafts of sun, a loose fold of heavy furred skin between its wrists and high flanks catches the damp air—not enough to glide far, just enough to flatten its arc and buy it a half-second of hang-time right in the pigeon’s flight path. 

Two vast, pale-palmed hands clap shut around the bird mid-beat. There is no audible snap, no squawk that carries a yard through the din of turbines and swifts. 

Gravity takes them both immediately. Dropping forty feet straight toward the floor, the hunter twists like a cat, spills air from its side-folds, and lands on all fours squarely atop the iron ground-cable—using the taut, swallowed metal line like a gymnast’s bar to bleed off the shock of impact before stepping lightly onto the moss. Even in the rush of the kill, it is fastidious: before climbing back toward its woven cradle, it pauses to rub its chin and forearms briskly along the slick iron cable, picking up the faint static tingle of stray ground-current that bleeds continuously from the distant plant into the living wood, grooming its dense coat with the tiny, sharp smell of ozone that keeps lice and ticks from ever taking hold.

A dozen yards away, three of the mossy domes have already felt the thump of the landing on the cable and are gliding slowly, blindly inward to claim the six grey contour feathers drifting down toward the green floor.

Up on its root-arch, tucked dry beneath the weeping bark, the solitary climber settles back into its copper-and-cedar basket to pluck its meal. It does not make a sound—unless, right at dusk, when the shafts of upper light turn steel-blue and the swifts pack tight for the night and the bats drop down to take their shift, it tilts its flat, solemn face toward the resonant center of the room, inflates its deep chest, and lets out a long, low, vibrant hoot pitched to the exact harmonic fifth of the distant turbines. 

You wouldn't hear it as a voice. Standing on the moss, you would simply feel the wooden walls around you ring suddenly warmer and fuller for ten seconds, like a chord resolving inside a vast instrument, before dropping back into the ancient, thunderous, unbroken hum.
