At first, you take them for slumped manifolds—secondary valve bodies or bundled junctions that have sagged under centuries of thermal creep and grown the same slick, pearl-dark patina as the surrounding bulkhead. There are three of them wedged into the mid-level tangle where the thickest coolant hoses cross the dead optical trunks, hanging motionless in the gloom. Only when the forty-minute cycle begins to turn, when the great roar drops its half-octave and the standing waves on the floor break into chop, does a seam part along the middle of the largest mass, revealing an eye no brighter than wet slate.

It is not a machine, and it is not an animal that wandered up from the cloud-forests two miles below. Whatever maintenance intelligence originally inhabited the spire did not merely write software to tend its hull; it grew small, semi-autonomous custodians out of the same phase-change polymers, braided carbon, and memory-metals as the tower itself. Ages ago, when the spire’s central logic went senile and forgot the exterior pockets existed, a handful of these servitors were stranded out here without an update signal. They could not die in the way flesh dies, and they could not rust, so they kept executing fragments of broken upkeep routines in the dark—until the bacteria, the birds, and the relentless, scalding rain slowly rewrote what they were for.

Up close, the largest of them—she, though the distinction is only one of size and territorial brood-habit—is roughly the bulk of a crouching hound, though shaped nothing like a quadruped. Her core is a squat, articulated spindle of dark, bone-smooth ceramic plates, flattened so she can press her belly tight against the shuddering bulkhead just like the thumbnail grazers do. From that central carapace extend six long, multi-jointed limbs made of bundled, muscle-like contractile fibers sheathed in tough, translucent elastomers that have yellowed with age. Each limb ends not in claws, but in a cluster of specialized tips that betray her ancestry: two opposing friction-pads, a fine calcareous scraper, and a hollow, needle-thin nozzle that once dispensed sealant resin.

She has no mouth for eating, because she doesn't eat food. Instead, tucked under her dorsal plates where a spine would be, are twin rows of pleated induction gills. When she wedges herself directly across a junction between a weeping hot-wall seam and an icy outer louver strut, the forty-degree temperature differential across her own body drives a solid-state thermoelectric current through her core. She feeds on heat-gradient, drinking the tower’s waste wattage straight through her belly-plates and breathing cold wind across her back to complete the circuit. 

Still, she harvests the pocket’s living things with obsessive, methodical care.

Watch her as the fog boils up and the temperature spikes. While the skinks are darting blindly down into the whiteout to snap up scattered grazers, she uncurls from her perch with slow, hydraulic grace. She does not flinch from the scalding steam; she leans into it. Her six limbs span wide across the slick conduits, never letting go with more than two points at a time so the massive vibration of the wall doesn't throw her off-balance. Beneath her, the hollow pipes sing with the frantic, staccato foot-thumps of hunting skinks. She feels every one of them through the sensitive pads of her toes. To her, the conduit network is a taut spiderweb of pure information; she knows the weight, speed, and trajectory of every lizard, centipede, and shifting bird colony in the courtyard without lifting her eyes.

A sleek skink corners a fat salamander against a fused radiator flute, pinning it in the hot mist. Before the lizard can strike, one of the custodian's long, pale limbs drops out of the steam directly between them. She doesn't crush the skink. Instead, her heel-pad strikes the hollow pipe once—a single, sharp, micro-calibrated *thwack* tuned to the exact resonant frequency of a much larger rival skink's territorial warning. The hunter freezes, confused by the phantom giant underfoot, then bolts back up into the hoses. The custodian ignores the escaping salamander; she wasn't saving it. She reaches down with her calcareous scraper and peels away a thick, rubbery mat of mineralized swift-guano and dead moss that had been damming the runoff channel right behind the salamander's tail. Water and silt gush freely toward the scupper again. Had that channel stayed blocked through three more cycles, warm water would have backed up over her favored thermal seam, drowning her gradient in uniform tepidness.

She is gardening thermodynamics.

Over centuries of frayed logic, the three custodians in this pocket have folded the invading biology into their damaged blueprints of the tower. Their degraded internal maps no longer distinguish between a titanium louver, a colony of roosting swifts, and a mat of sulfur-eating bacteria; everything in the courtyard is simply "hull" to be balanced so the local heat-exchange stays stable. 

You can see their handiwork everywhere once you realize the erosion isn't entirely natural. Follow the branching channels worn into the composite floor: here and there, where a runnel threatened to undercut a live bundle of warm conduits, a neat, crescent-shaped dam has been laid down—not out of fresh polymer resin, which their internal reservoirs ran dry of three hundred years ago, but out of empty bronze grazer-shells, stacked three deep and mortared together with masticated filament-moss and mineral paste scraped from the vents. Look up into the dark overhang where the vent-swifts roost in tight, oily clusters: several of the sagging, dead sensor housings have been deliberately lashed together with frayed strands of milky optical fiber pulled up from the floor, woven into crude, springy baskets that keep the heavy nests from tearing loose during the high-altitude winter gales. The birds provide nitrates; the nitrates feed the lower pools; the pools support the shrimp and worms whose silt keeps the porous composite deck from cracking dry between steam-cycles; intact decking keeps the cold updrafts from leaking in from below and ruining the custodians' warmth.

They are completely mute, like everything else here, yet their communication is the most intricate thing in the pocket. 

When the valves deep inside the spire slam shut again and the steam clears, the smallest of the three—a younger or perhaps simply more pared-down unit missing one of its six limbs, the stump capped smoothly in dark, calcified pearl—climbs inward from the outer louvers. Its carapace is beaded with near-freezing mist, its movements stiff and sluggish from spending too long in the cold wind-slots clearing ice-rime off the intake slats. 

It reaches the central pipe-junction where the large custodian waits. Neither makes a sound. As the smaller one crawls alongside her, they press their broad, flat side-plates together, sharing heat directly across their shells until their temperatures equalize. At the same time, they rest their spare limbs along a single dead, exposed vein of optical fiber dangling from a split cable-tray. Inside their translucent wrist-sheaths, tiny bioluminescent-like diagnostic diodes—remnants of old fiber-optic coupler ports—begin to fire. Pulses of pale, watery amber and deep violet light shoot through the dead glass strand between them, turning the milky quartz vein into a silent, flickering nerve. 

For two or three minutes, they hang there in the pewter gloom while water drips silently off their joints, trading slow bursts of light through salvaged glass: *Louver seven jammed half-shut with wind-grit. Swift brood on upper manifold heavy, hose sagging four millimeters. Lower pool scupper clear. Core roar dropped two hertz below nominal on last cycle.*

Neither of them knows what *nominal* means anymore, nor what lies at the bottom of the tower, nor why the core roars. When the light-pulses stop, the cold one stays pressed over the prime thermal vent to drink voltage, already going stone-still as its outer skin darkens to match the wet ceramic wall. 

The larger one uncouples, turns slowly upside down beneath a sweating coolant trunk, and begins her forty-minute patrol before the next fog. Moving hand over silent hand through the thunder, she pauses under a cluster of vent-swifts. Two of the soot-dark birds are flaring their ultraviolet throat-patches at her in mild irritation, refusing to budge from a warm seam she needs to inspect. Without hurry, she lifts her snoutless head-plate, angles a polished, iridescent facet of her worn carapace toward a grey knife-blade of daylight coming through the louvers, and bounces a tight flick of sky-glare—rich in high-altitude ultraviolet—straight back under the birds' chins. It is a clumsy, mechanical forgery of their own semaphore, learned across ten thousand generations of watching them roost. Satisfied or sufficiently baffled, the swifts shuffle two inches to the left. 

She presses her scraper gently to the weeping seam beneath their feet, feels the deep, bone-shaking hum of the tower's heart through her palms, trims away an eighth-inch of excess bacterial mat so the pipe won't overheat, and flicks the gelatinous ribbon down into the murky pool below, where three blind shrimp and a mottled salamander are already waiting at the edge of the standing waves to catch it.
