To see what truly rules the avenues, you have to stop looking for movement and start looking for weight. 

You will likely mistake the first one for a fallen buttress, or a drift of chalk-dust wedged against the base of a bloated succulent. Only when a pale browser steps too close—and smoothly, without panic, parts its path around the mound—do you notice the slow, rhythmic expansion of its ribs. A breath drawn in over the course of a full minute, held while the dry body extracts every stray molecule of dampness from the air, and then released through wide, valved nostrils in a silent puff that barely stirs the gravel.

They are the reason the lower doorways are polished into such wide, greasy ovals. 

Up close, the creature is immense—longer than an ox and slung nearly to the ground, built on the ancient, sprawling blueprint of a tortoise or a heavy-boned badger, though it is neither. Its hide is a loose, heavy mantle of dusty grey leather, thrown into deep, vertical folds at the neck and shoulders like draped canvas. Over decades of squeezing through the compressed, petrified tunnels of the city’s oldest districts, its flanks and back have been rubbed entirely hairless, burnished to a dull, slate-like sheen studded with flat nodules of bone. In the creases where the walls don't touch, silver lichen and dry, powdery moss have taken root directly in the waxy secretions of its skin. It carries the colors of the masonry on its back.

Everything about the animal is an adaptation to a world without water and without silence. 

It has no visible external ears—in a basin that vibrates like the inside of a struck bell, delicate eardrums would have ruptured ages ago. Instead, its broad, shovel-shaped skull is dense and heavy, the lower jawbone thick and flattened underneath. When it rests, which is almost always, it lays its chin flat against the swept cobbles or presses its cheek against the side of a fluted tower. It listens through its teeth and bones. Through the solid, resonant mud-brick of the warren, it can feel the shift of a rodent colony three tiers up, the slow, subterranean scratching of the blind termites repairing a gallery beneath its belly, and the heavy, deliberate tread of another of its kind four plazas away.

Its eyes are small, sunken deep beneath shelves of calloused brow, filmed over by thick, translucent third eyelids that wipe away the constant chalk-dust without wasting precious fluid on tears. In the shadowless pewter gloom, sight is secondary anyway. 

Watch one wake—if waking is even the right word for a transition so gradual. 

First, the great, broad forefeet shift, tucking beneath the chest. The claws are not curved for tearing, nor sharp for digging quick burrows; they are massive, blunt chisels of horn layered with pale calcium, wide as spades. Slowly, with the creaking, unhurried leverage of shifting stone, the creature raises its bulk an inch clear of the gravel and begins to walk. Its gait is a heavy, swaying amble that leaves a wide, smooth trough between its footprints where its thick tail drags, unintentionally sweeping the avenues clear of dried leaf-chaff and fallen twigs. This is why the plazas look tended. Thousands of years of dragging tails have pushed the debris neatly against the bases of the walls, creating rich, dry windrows that feed the mosses and shelter the beetles.

It approaches a hanging curtain of forty-foot air-plants. The goat-sized browsers do not scatter; they simply step aside, still chewing, honking their low contact-calls over its back. Here, in this bone-dry metropolis, spending energy on fear or chasing is a fatal extravagance. 

The heavy creature rears up—a slow, astonishingly stable movement, bracing its thick tail and broad hind feet to lean its chest against the ochre wall of the tower. With one heavy forelimb, it hooks down a tangled mat of the tough, strap-like bromeliads, pulling it not to tear it free, but to pin it gently down. Then, from its narrow, beak-like muzzle, a long, dark, leathery tongue unrolls. It does not eat the plant—the fibrous leaves take decades to grow back. Instead, it licks the pale, armored cicadas right off the stems and masonry. 

Crunch. Crunch. Crunch. 

Even its feeding is absorbed immediately by the surrounding roar. Its flat, crushing molars grind the chitinous insects into a dry paste, extracting the rich fats and precious body-moisture the cicadas sucked from the deep roots of the bloated shrubs. It will stand like that for twenty minutes, methodically harvesting a single square yard of wall while the remaining insects vibrate deafeningly an inch from its snout, entirely indifferent to being culled. When it drops back to all fours, it turns toward the base of the mound and uses a single chisel-claw to pare away a thin, curated slice of the ancient, compressed guano-rock—licking the mineral salts from the fresh scar before moving on. 

They are the city’s unwitting architects of the second order. The termites raise the mud and cement the silt, building blind, choked labyrinths; these lumbering giants follow decades later, wedging their massive shoulders into narrow fissures, slowly grinding away collapsed partitions, widening chambers so air can circulate, and compressing the floors beneath tons of patient weight until the porous earth turns to load-bearing stone. When a lower tunnel finally grows too tight even for them, they simply lean against an interior pillar and scratch an itch for six months until a new archway opens.

And if you follow one down—ducking beneath the polished oval of a doorway, past the barking, thick-furred rodents clinging to the ceiling ledges, down into the dark, cool gut of a central mound where the insect-roar is muffled to a deep, somatic hum—you find what the whole slow machine rests upon.

In the deepest, oldest chambers, where the air is still and almost faintly humid from the trapped exhalations of ten thousand creatures above, the giants sleep piled loosely against one another like river boulders. 

And further back still, in dead-end alcoves sealed off from the drafts, lie the ones that stopped waking up. 

Nothing rots in the basin. When one of these creatures reaches the end of its second or third century, it simply walks down into the foundation of its home mound, settles its chin onto the stone one last time, and dries out. Over centuries, the blind termites come up through the floor, cementing silt and saliva over the leathery hide, packing the hollow of the ribs with earth, turning the heavy skeleton into a permanent, calcified vault-rib. Half the load-bearing walls in the center of the city have a spine inside them.

Out on the avenues, another column of pale doves lifts lazily into the flat, grey light. A giant emerges from a darkened doorway three hundred yards away, blinking chalk from its eyes, pausing for ten minutes on the threshold just to feel the song of the basin settle into its jawbone before taking its next step into the dust.
