At first, you will think the city is abandoned, and then you will think you have seen a boulder move, and then you will realize that what you took for a person standing on a rooftop four miles away is actually a person standing three feet to your left, and their hand is currently resting on a stone lintel half a league behind you.

The people of the shield are still here. They never left, because leaving a place whose borders are measured in variable infinities is an errand without a clear direction, and besides, the cabbages still need tending.

Seen whole—on the rare occasions when a metric calm settles over a courtyard and allows a human being to occupy a single, contiguous yard of air—a resident of the plateau is compact, heavy-boned, and astonishingly wide in the ribcage. Their chests are barrels built to pump oxygen out of air as thin as glass, and their skin has the dense, matte, slightly waxy look of smooth river-slate, darkened at the cheekbones, knuckles, and ears to a deep plum-indigo where rich nets of capillaries sit close to the surface, suffused with natural glycols that keep their extremities from freezing solid in a sudden mist-squall. They move slowly. Not out of lethargy, and not out of reverence, either, though visitors—if visitors could ever manage the climb—might mistake their deliberate, flat-footed gait for some kind of monastic trance. They move slowly because fast movement in thin air wastes heat, and because in warped space a hasty stride can catch a shin on a kerbstone that looks forty yards off, or send an elbow plunging through an open window into a patch of freezing shade six miles away.

They dress in heavy, overlapping skirts and capes of retted sedge-fibre, beaten soft with wooden mallets and oiled with the thick, bitter resin pressed from frost-shrub seeds, over undergarments felted from the pale brown wool of bog-cotton and dried sphagnum. Pinned at the shoulders and hems are heavy broad-headed brooches and plumb-weights of cold-hammered copper. The copper is not entirely for beauty, though it is bright green and black with damp patina and pleasingly worked with thumb-dents; the weights hang on braided cords from their sleeves and hems so a walker can always see, by the angle at which a copper bob pulls away from their knee, whether they are brushing past the invisible flank of a ghost-mountain before they stumble up an unseen talus slope in empty air.

For a long time you will not hear them speak, and when you finally do, you may think it is only water knocking in a split-log pipe. They have very little use for shouting across the tundra; sound dilates and thins across the metric weather until a cry turns into a sub-audible, twenty-minute sigh that rattles a slate roof three districts away. Instead, they talk across thresholds. Because every doorway, window-frame, drain-arch, and wall-chink remains topologically faithful to the room it was built into regardless of how many leagues of cushion-moss have swelled between its stones, the architecture itself is their commons. Stand inside a split tenement—just a single standing wall of dry-laid slate rising out of the red moss—and put your head near the square window-hole, and you will hear the warm, low, breathy murmur of three people sitting around the other three walls of that same room nine miles to the south. One of them reaches across the hearth-stone to pass a wooden bowl of steeped fern-broth; from the outside, watching from a rise in the gneiss, you would see a dark-plum hand emerge cleanly from an empty window-frame in the middle of the wilderness, hang six inches above the bog, and be taken by fingers reaching out of a freestanding stone doorway half a horizon west. Inside the room, to the people sitting on their rush mats, they are simply sitting knee to knee.

It is when they leave the houses to work the plateau that they become harder for an unpractised eye to hold together.

A body is only an agreement between parts, and the plateau has spent ten thousand generations teaching their bodies to take the same liberties with distance that a braided reed awning does. A woman weeding a sunken garden bed may stand with her broad, greased-leather boots planted firmly on a dry paving slab in the afternoon sun while her torso leans through a pocket of compressed space to prune the steaming, pungent outer leaves of a heat-cabbage growing three quarters of a mile away. From the road, she looks severed—or worse, smeared: her shoulders vast and sharp-edged in the foreground, her forearm tapering abruptly to the thickness of a willow-twig before vanishing into a bank of mid-air mist forty yards off, her fingers huge again where a lens of dense light magnifies them among the dark green leaves. It does not hurt her. Her blood pumps evenly across the seam; the pulse leaving her heart crosses half a mile of dilated afternoon in the exact fifth of a second it has always taken to reach her wrist, cooling no more than if her arm were tucked against her ribs.

Still, this kind of living puts peculiar habits into a culture. You never strike at anything; you never jerk back when startled. Children—round, solemn creatures bundled in layers of dry peat-fluff until they resemble walking tussocks—learn before they can talk to test the air ahead of them not with sticks, which can snap when half their length enters a slow time-puddle, but by blowing a small puff of warm breath from pursed lips. In the cold, honest light, the tiny cloud of white vapour betrays the metric immediately: if it hangs compact and round, the step ahead is ordinary; if it stretches instantly into a thin, mile-long thread of white silk across the tundra, the child sits down on a cushion-plant and waits for the street to come back, or goes home through the nearest drain-culvert’s stone arch.

And what are those cold hearths for, stacked with unburned peat that breathes out frost?

Watch one toward evening, when a household gathers—some of them sitting on the cobbles right beside the pit, one leaning in through a doorway from the next valley of phantom air, an elder propped against a timber post that currently stands alone in a field of giant liverworts two leagues north, yet still holds up the roof-beam directly above their heads. Under the flagstones of every hearth, packed deep in wet clay, grows a domesticated bulb—a blind, pale cousin of the steaming courtyard cabbages, bred over uncounted centuries to run its chemistry backward. Where the outdoor plants burn stored sugars to melt the frost off their leaves, the *hearth-root* drinks ambient warmth out of the room to pack dense, waxy starches into its buried rings. That is why the peat stacked over the cobbles is unburned: it isn't fuel, it is insulation and moisture-wick, laid over the cold stone to slow the root’s draw and keep it from freezing the sleepers' toes off, while slowly curing in the dry, frigid draft until its bricks turn hard as horn. Twice a year, they pry up the hearthstones, slice away half the pale, icy flesh of the root, and boil it in copper kettles hung high in the air—suspended from the woody sky-vines where focused lenses of metric compression concentrate the pale sunlight into invisible, scorching ovens forty feet off the ground. Boiled, the cold-root turns sweet, heavy, and greasy as marrow, the staple fat of an animal-less world.

They are not entirely alone in the masonry, though you would have to crouch a long time by a cistern with very quiet eyes to notice their livestock, if livestock is even the word for something without nerve, bone, or pulse.

Nothing with legs survived the long planing of the mountains or the thinning of the sky, and nothing burrows in the sterile mineral grit beneath the pavers. Yet there are tasks in a dilated city that plants rooted in one spot cannot do, and people cannot always reach across a sudden ten-mile tear in a marketplace to mend a slipped peg. Look closely at the braided sedge awnings, or along the rims of the stone water-conduits when a mist-bank clears. Coiled in the damp joints of the masonry are things the length of a forearm and the thickness of a thumb, braided from dead rush-pith and living, silver-green moss-strands, bound at intervals with tiny rings of hammered copper wire. Left in dry sun, they are completely inanimate—just neat bundles of thatch-twine. When a sudden condensation front drenches them, however, the different plant fibres—some cut on the bias from high-vines, some stripped from twisting frost-shrub bark—swell at wildly different rates. They uncoil, roll, and inch along the stone grooves with a slow, blind, hygroscopic persistence, dragging trailing aprons of spongy sphagnum behind them.

They are half-tool, half-grafted bryophyte, cultivated and knotted by the old men who sit on the warm roofs of the bathhouses. As these wet, twisting ropes of living moss and dead reed creep down the miles of split-log pipes, they scour away mineral scale, carry spore-paste from one severed orchard wall to another, and—when a drying breeze hits them—contract with enough slow, stubborn tension to cinch loose timber joints tight again. Every courtyard has six or seven of them creeping imperceptibly across the wet slates with a faint, fibrous *creak*, smelling of crushed green sap and cold copper, tending the house while the house wanders the horizon.

You might wonder what occupies the minds of people who have no predators to watch for, no herds to drive, no seasons sharp enough to panic over, and no neighbours who stay at the same distance for two days running. Look at the paving stones of the great avenues again; look at the boundary markers set out across the Cushion-plains where no wall has ever stood.

They are surveying the ghost-mountains.

Not with ink or parchment, which rot in the sudden mists, and not to conquer them, for you cannot quarry a granite peak that vanished before complex life crawled onto land. They map them because the water lives up there, and because the human eye craves a shape to set against all that terrifying, exhausted flatness. Out in the middle of the featureless shield, miles from the nearest drifted tenement, you will come upon three people standing a hundred yards apart—or five miles apart, though they pass a single long cord of braided hair-moss back and forth between them—watching pebbles they drop from long wooden spoons, or releasing puffs of yellow roof-spore into the crisp, windless air. Where the spores catch on an invisible crag eighty feet up and slide sideways down an unseen couloir of ancient gravity, the surveyors nod, walk patiently beneath the spot, and set a flat cairn of grey gneiss on the moss below. Over centuries, they have paved the shadows of the vanished range. Walk the plateau long enough and you begin to read their ground-marks: rings of white quartz pebbles marking the bases of invisible cliffs; copper pins driven into the bedrock directly beneath high, phantom tarns of pooled sky-water; tilted slabs of slate propped at precise angles in the flat bog to match the sixty-degree pitch of a mountain face that only falling water and climbing vines can still feel.

And when a deep compression front rolls in—not a twenty-minute gust, but one of the great, slow metric tides that arrives once every few years and draws forty square miles of scattered city inward until walls kiss walls, awnings bunch into thick thatched roofs, and the scattered avenues click together like the bones of a closing hand—the silence on the plateau changes quality.

The air between the crowded slate walls grows almost balmy, forty-five or fifty degrees, thick with the nutty steam of cabbages, wet wool, bruised thyme-moss, and human skin. Doors that opened onto ten miles of empty tundra an hour ago now open onto the crowded flagstones of an actual street. Nobody hurries even then, because hurry is not in their marrow, yet every window fills with dark, plum-cheeked faces. Baskets of cured peat, bundles of hammered copper wire, jars of fermented shrub-sap, and freshly braided moss-ropes pass from hand to hand without needing a threshold’s trick to bridge the gap. Children climb out onto the bunched, overlapping roofs and step from house to house across the whole gathered municipality in ten minutes, marvelling at how small the world has briefly become, touching the faces of cousins they usually know only as disembodied hands reaching through a pantry arch or huge, distorted silhouettes looming on the northern rim of the sky.

Someone brings up a hollow gourd stretched with cured bladder-kelp; someone else taps a row of suspended copper gutter-spouts with a padded stick. The music is quiet, dry, and knock-kneed in rhythm, timed to the *tick-drip* of snowmelt falling off peaks nobody has ever seen. They eat warm root-fat from slate platters, trade cuttings of sky-vine, and repair the mortises of each other’s rafters while they can reach both ends of a beam at once.

Then, around midnight or the following noon, the air cools. A wooden peg groans in its hole. Between two neighbours still finishing a sentence over a shared cup, six inches of crimson bog-moss wells up without a sound, widening smoothly to twenty yards, to half a mile, to a league of gleaming, frost-beaded green under the honest sun. Neither of them turns away or raises their voice to finish the thought; the one holding the cup simply steps back across his own stone threshold, reaches his arm back out through the doorway across the widening plain, and sets the vessel down on his neighbour’s sill, right beside her boot, before going inside to check the cold-root under the floor.
