At first, if you sat on the top tier of the western amphitheater from morning until noon, you would swear the hare and the choughs had the basin to themselves. The doors of the twenty buildings—thick planks of silvered larch hung on bronze pins—stand either shut fast or propped open at precise, unchanging angles by dressed blocks of stone. Nothing flutters in a window opening. No smoke rises, because there is nothing up here to burn and no soot stains the pale soffits of the eaves.

Then, around the hour when the shadow of the tall granary detaches itself from the eastern retaining wall, one of the figures seated on the low curb beside the third cistern stands up. 

You looked straight at him twenty minutes ago and read him as a bollard—a stubby column of grey wool and slate-colored leather, dusted at the shoulders with the same pale, dry cast as the limestone. When he rises, the movement is neither stiff nor brisk; it simply happens, smoothly, without the small preparatory fidgets—the shifting of weight, the clearing of a throat, the brushing of knees—that ordinarily announce a body about to act. 

There are eleven of them in the basin just now. In winter there are sometimes fourteen; in high summer, after the lower passes clear of rotten snow, as few as six.

They are human, though bred and weathered to the exact tolerances of high rock. Most are past forty, or look it, though age among them is hard to read because they lack both the softness of youth and the slackness of decline. They stand rather short, deep-chested from the thin air, with wide wrists and short-fingered, heavy-knuckled hands whose skin has the dry, finely crosshatched grain of cured rawhide. Their faces are broad across the cheekbones, burned not red by the upland glare, high UV, and cold reflection, but to a uniform, permanent walnut-grey, paler only in the deep creases around the eyes where they squint against the white sky. Their hair, regardless of whether they are forty or seventy, seems to settle early into the color of sheep’s horn or unbleached linen, worn cropped close to the skull with dull shears so the ridge-wind cannot whip it across their eyes when they climb out over the rim.

Watch the man by the cistern walk across the middle plaza and you understand at once why the stairs are dished to a pewter polish yet never chip. He wears heavy, ankle-high buskins cobbled from triple thicknesses of oiled goat-hide, the soles fitted not with hobnails—iron would scar the limestone and strike vulgar sparks—but with pegged plates of dense ram’s horn, trimmed flush with the welt. On stone, horn makes a sound softer than wood and harder than leather: a dry, muted *tock... tock... tock* that does not echo off the cliff walls, dying within ten paces of his feet. 

Over linen shirts washed grey in cold snowmelt, they wear long, sleeveless coats of felted wool boiled and pounded until the fabric is nearly half an inch thick, windproof as plank and heavy enough to hang straight down without flapping. A coat here outlasts its first owner. You can see the history of a garment in the neatness of its triangular patches at the elbow and hem, stitched with sinew thread in matching grey, the wool gradually mineralizing over decades from stone dust and hard water until an empty coat stood upright in a corner stays standing.

Why are the buildings thirty and forty paces apart? Stay until late afternoon, when three more of them emerge from separate doorways into the lower courts, and the geometry of the settlement suddenly makes sense. 

They do not cluster. Two of them pass on a broad avenue twenty feet wide; one walks near the northern gutter, the other along the southern curb. Neither alters course to close the gap. As they draw abreast, one lifts two fingers slightly from the seam of his coat; the other lowers his chin half an inch. No word is spoken, yet there is no hostility in the distance between them, none of the brittle tension of people ignoring one another. Rather, they move like heavy hulls in a small harbor, each carrying around himself an invisible, mutually respected berth of quiet air. In a stone bowl where the drop of a single bead of water carries eighty yards, two people talking at an ordinary pitch would fill the entire sky of the settlement; five people conversing in a tight alley would turn the basin into a drum. The wide courts are acoustic insulation made of emptiness. The town was laid out for people who find proximity loud.

Their work, when you finally begin to recognize it as work, consists almost entirely of minute adjustments to the truce around them.

An older woman with braids pinned flat under a felt cap comes down the wide eastern stair carrying nothing larger than a flat bronze scraper, palm-sized, worn crescent-shaped at its blade, and a twist of dry sedge grass in her pocket. She kneels beside one of the dark ribbons of water creeping across the plaza. A hairline crust of white calcium carbonate—lime leached from the sweating cliff—has begun to build at the lip of a chiselled turn in the runnel, nudging the film of water half an inch wider than its bed, dampening stone outside the moss line. With three slow, firm strokes of the bronze edge, she pares the mineral deposit flush back to the ancient chisel-mark. She wipes the pale dust onto the twist of grass, tucks the grass back into her pocket so the lime won't blow into the moss and burn it, watches the dark edge of wetness retreat to its proper millimeter, and rises. The whole task took four minutes. She will not need to touch that corner again until autumn.

Another spends an hour inside one of the isolated square storehouses. If you peer through the deep-set, unglazed slit of its window once your eyes adjust to the gloom, you see walls lined with stacked slate shelves holding flat rounds of hard, grey-rinded ewe’s cheese rubbed with ash, stoneware crocks sealed with beeswax and bladder-skin, bundled fleeces, cakes of salt, and spare larch pegs stored in dry sand. He is turning the cheeses—lifting each twenty-pound wheel, wiping its underside with a dry linen cloth to discourage blue mold in the damp cellar-chill, flipping it over, setting it back on the exact pale circle it left on the slate. Everything smells faintly of lanolin, cold stone, dried thyme, and cellar water. There are supplies enough in those twenty buildings to feed fifty people for three years, though fifty people have not stood inside this rim at one time in living memory, except for three days every fourth autumn.

Because this is not a dying village, and these eleven are not survivors clinging to the shell of something larger. They are a permanent, rotating garrison of custodians—the *still-folk*, or simply *those up inside*, as the transhumant shepherds and valley-bottom farmers two thousand feet below call them when looking up at the notched shoulder of the ridge.

Down in the valleys, life is mud, timber rot, boundary disputes, spring floods that tear out millraces, iron rusting in wet grass, loud markets, smoke, and crowded graveyards. Long ago—so long ago that the dialect carved onto the boundary stones at the rim uses cases no one speaks anymore—the scattered clans of the wider massif built this basin together, or improved what an older people had begun, as an incorruptible civic heart. Everything down below spoils or shifts; up here, in the cold dry preservative air above the treeline, nothing does.

In three of the narrow two-story buildings near the upper rim, behind doors fitted with bronze shoot-bolts that slide home with the oiled precision of clockwork, there are no cheeses or fleeces. Their upper rooms are lined from flagstone to rafters with narrow pigeonholes of split slate, and in each slot rests a thin tablet of fine-grained black shale or cured boxwood, incised with a steel stylus. Marriages, grazing rights on forty miles of summer pasture, water-shares for lower streams, blood-debts settled and sworn off, lineages, the weights of silver and wool owed between valleys—all the volatile memory of three thousand noisy people living below the clouds is kept up here in stone that cannot burn and wood too dry and cold for worm.

Every fourth year, before the first hard snows lock the goat-tracks up the grey bedrock, delegations climb up out of the pale air at the basin’s edge—forty or sixty valley people at once, breathless from the ascent, sweating in greasy wool, smelling startlingly of woodsmoke, garlic, horse-dung, and wet soil. When they step over the rim onto the clean blue-grey paving, the sheer architectural composure of the place strikes them dumb. Voices drop instantly to whispers. Boots scraped clean at the threshold stone still feel clumsy on those pewter-polished treads. For three days they sit in the stepped stone amphitheaters—which suddenly face something after all: two disputing elders standing thirty paces apart in the court below, speaking one sentence at a time into the clear, merciless acoustics of the bowl while the custodians listen from the curb without expression, fetch down the relevant slate from an upper room, lay it on a cistern cover in the level light, and say what is cut there. 

Then the valley people leave sacks of barley meal, salt blocks, olive oil, bronze stock, and boiled hides at the edge of the lowest plaza, climb back down out of the sky, and vanish. For the next forty-seven months, the amphitheaters go back to facing nothing in particular.

The eleven who stay behind are chosen—or choose themselves—from every valley clan, usually in middle life, after their children below are grown or if they were born from the start with the particular temperament that finds lowland life intolerably cluttered. Once a person comes up to keep the basin, they rarely go down again, even to be buried; outside the southern rim, where the cliff drops sheer into nine hundred feet of empty valley air, there is a narrow ledge worn smooth beside a slot in the rock, and the lammergeiers—bearded vultures with nine-foot wingspans that ride the updrafts below the sill—have known for six hundred years what that ledge is for.

At dusk, the light does not redden much; the washed sky overhead simply darkens through successive shades of pearl, pewter, and slate blue until the flagstones and the air are nearly the same tone. The sweating northern cliff grows black. Night cloud begins to spill silently over the eastern notch like cold milk over the lip of a bowl, sinking slowly toward the terraces.

Now, for the only time all day, they gather—if *gather* is the word for seven or eight dark shapes converging quietly upon the lowest court where the twisted rowan tree stands in its unpaved square. 

Inside the largest ground-floor room opening onto that court, there is no open hearth, only a broad, enclosed masonry stove fed sparingly once a day with dried sheep-dung cakes carried up on pack-mules in August and small twists of resinous juniper root gathered two ridges away. The stove barely warms the room ten degrees above the outside chill, just enough to take the bone-ache out of stone air, its tiny flue breathing a thread of invisible heat that smells faintly of dry turf. Around it they sit on low larch benches polished glossy by generations of wool coats, eating cold barley-cake, slivered cheese, pickled ramsons, and small wooden cups of snow-water steeped with bitter gentian root.

Sometimes, for an hour before full dark, one of them speaks. Their voices have adapted to the stone bowl even indoors: low, unhurried, pitched entirely in the chest without sharp consonants that hiss or click off hard walls. A sentence is offered—that the ice behind the second cistern pried a curbstone half a finger’s breadth out of true last winter and will want wedging before frost; that the hen chough has only one fledgling this year; that fog is rising early from the south pass—and after it falls, nobody rushes to fill the space behind it. Two or three minutes pass. Someone chews slowly, swallows, gazes through the open door at the spherical crown of the rowan tree holding perfectly still in the gathering mist, and answers in four words.

A small fat-lamp—a hollowed cup of soapstone with a rush wick burning clarified mutton tallow—sits in a wall niche, throwing steady yellow light no farther than their boots and hands. Outside, in the blue dark, the night fog settles onto the limestone flags, feeding the lichens, glazing the steps, gathering on the diagonal cliff-grooves to renew the dark ribbons in the gutters. One by one, without taking formal leave, the dark wool figures stand, step out onto the damp stone in their horn-soled shoes—*tock, tock, tock* fading across thirty paces of empty court—and pull their heavy larch doors shut behind them, leaving the plazas to the snails unlocking themselves from the curbs in the wet dark, and the cisterns brimming silently to the lip, drop by measured drop.
