Watch one of the wide, rust-colored blisters where the hot water has sat undisturbed for three or four hours. 

Usually, when the pavement beneath it decides to become a sunken bathhouse or a dry-stone trench, the red crust simply pulls taut like boiled skin, splits, and weeps its clear, iron-smelling jelly onto the disappearing bricks. Most of it goes down into the dark between the courses, pinched out of existence as the deeper masonry zippers shut. 

Sometimes, however, a torn flap of the tallow-crust does not sink. Driven by the sudden scalding draft of a rearranging wall, the ragged sheet curls inward upon itself before the mucilage can wash away. It rolls like a wet parchment scroll, trapping a pocket of lime-heavy water, a pinch of the phantom chopped straw from the mortar, and a scattering of sharp terracotta grit inside its own sticky lining. 

It seals. And then, instead of waiting to be torn apart again, it pulls itself upright.

They have no bones—nothing with a rigid skeleton survives the sudden, silent clamping of ten thousand tons of tufa—so they borrow their posture from pressure. Picture something the size of a butcher’s apron, or occasionally as large as a draft horse, shaped vaguely like a swollen waterskin or a heavy leather bellows standing on its thick, pleated hem. Their skin is that same translucent, reddish-amber glaze as the puddles they came from, tough as cured rawhide on the outside to resist the razor-sharp edges of the freshly fired brick, yet soft and wildly hydraulic within. Through the murky, tallow-colored hide, you can dimly see what holds them up: suspended knots of swallowed mortar-grit, shifting ballast-stones, and a slow, churning core of white lime paste.

They move like spilled mercury wrapped in oiled silk, sliding over the steaming flagstones on a self-secreted cushion of clear slime. When a section of the plain suddenly ripples like shaken canvas, they do not brace against it. They instantly drop their internal pressure, flattening out into broad, rubbery pancakes that ride the heaving wave of brick as effortlessly as kelp on a swell, before pumping themselves back up into tall, listening columns once the masonry clicks into its new alignment.

At first, across the dim miles of tallow-lit fog, you might mistake a gathering of them for abandoned equipment—a cluster of wet leather sacks slumped beside a newly risen cistern. They are utterly silent, and they spend long stretches completely motionless, pressed flat against the mortar joints, grazing. 

A grazer spreads its broad base over ten feet of fresh masonry, sealing the edges tight against the stone. Into that vacuum it pumps an acidic, fever-hot digestive liquor that dissolves the fresh lime mortar right out of the seams, sucking up the calcium, the mineral salts, and the stubbled, never-grown straw. When it slides onward twenty minutes later, the pristine wall behind it looks oddly middle-aged—its crisp joints deeply recessed and hollowed out, though the bricks themselves remain untouched and razor-sharp. Five minutes later, of course, the wall un-lays itself anyway, fanning upward into the copper dark to become an aqueduct half a mile off, its stolen mortar mysteriously restored on the way down. The world feeds them endlessly because the world cannot remember having been eaten.

That alone would make them mere stomachs—ambulatory blisters skimming a perpetual soup. What makes them more than stomachs is the *ticking*.

That deep, dry vibration felt in the teeth—the subtle telegraphy that precedes a drafting error—is the center of their existence. Soft tissue cannot hear well in air this thick and wet, so they have turned themselves into living plumb-lines. Inside the central cavity of an older specimen hangs a single, heavy lump of dense calcified mortar, grown layer by layer like an oyster’s pearl, suspended from the crown of its body by a thick, taut cord of sinewy cartilage. 

Before a squat mud ziggurat un-stacks itself, before a trench yawns open into the endless dry-stone dark below, the local gravity goes crooked. The copper plumb-bobs on the timber scaffolds swing three degrees east; at the exact same moment, inside every leather-skinned creature within half a mile, that internal pearl pulls sideways against a ring of sensitive, raw nerve-ridges. 

Watch them when the pearl swings. 

A score of tall, amber-skinned shapes will suddenly arrest their slow gliding across a flooded courtyard. Together, in eerie, muffled unison, they lean away from the crooked pull, craning their blind, tapered upper ends toward the empty air where ten thousand bricks are about to arrive. They do not flee the turbulence; they crowd right up to its sheer, invisible seam, lining the exact plumb-line of the impending change with an accuracy down to the quarter-inch. As a towering timber scaffold silently un-lashes its rawhide thongs and drops piecemeal out of the copper clouds to reassemble fifty yards away, the creatures stand calm and slick within hand’s breadth of the falling beams, letting the displaced, scalding wind wash over their hides, catching the rich, mineral-thick sweat shaken loose from the newly born stone before it even hits the floor.

The small ones only follow the shifts to feed. The very old ones—the great, scarred, mahogany-dark patriarchs that have avoided being pinched in a closing trench for decades—begin to meddle with the tools.

Because nothing rots and nothing erodes, an individual that avoids being crushed can grow immense and strangely complex, budding off smaller, specialized muscular lobes, splitting its upper trunk into three or four thick, prehensile trunks as supple and strong as an elephant’s, lined on the undersides with rows of rough, horny suction-plates. Their skins darken to the color of dried ox-blood, dusted white in the creases with precipitated lime. 

You will come across one of these elders in the middle of the glistening flats, wrapped entirely around one of the giant, torso-sized wooden mallets, hauling it through an inch of steaming water. 

It takes tremendous effort. The creature has to anchor its wide skirt to the terracotta, swell its muscular hauling-trunks with hot fluid until they bulge like firehoses, and drag the unmarked timber block six feet at a time, leaving a wide, dry wake in the shallow pool that immediately fills back in with boiling condensation. It is not building anything—nothing built by flesh would be permitted to stay—nor is it using the mallet to strike. 

Instead, it drags the massive tool over to a hairline seam where its internal pearl tells it the floor is preparing to ripple. With slow, meticulous care, it heaves the mallet directly across the joint, wedging it against a knee-high lip of rough tufa. Then it backs off ten paces, draws itself up into a seven-foot pillar of glistening red hide, and waits.

Thirty seconds later, the drafting error arrives. The flagstones try to lift and shuffle. 

Usually, the masonry ignores obstacles, passing around or tossing aside whatever sits upon it. Yet the forgotten tools share some stubborn, foundational grammar with the brickwork itself. Where the giant mallet lies wedged across the seam, the ripple *stutters*. Five square yards of herringbone brick fail to un-lay. For ten, twenty, forty minutes, while the entire surrounding courtyard churns into a stepped amphitheater and back into a flat plain twice over, that single wedged patch of floor remains locked—trembling slightly, sweating scalding mineral water at triple its normal rate from the strain of its own arrested rearrangement, bleeding rich, milky mortar-broth out of its seams.

Into that quivering, feverish puddle, the elder settles its broad base, joined presently by three or four smaller, translucent kin it tolerates at its margins, all of them drinking deep from the jammed, sweating stone until the world’s silent pressure finally overwhelms the wedge, flings the unmarked mallet twenty yards into the steam without a splash, and snaps the tardy bricks smoothly into place.

Higher up, out of sight where the copper cloud-vault swallows the tops of the tallest barkless scaffolds, lives their other phase—or perhaps a cousin that parted ways with the floor-crawlers eons ago.

If you climb one of the wet, creaking timber towers before it decides to become a retaining wall, ascending past the first hundred feet where the heat grows even fiercer and the sourceless yellow light turns the fog into thick brass soup, you begin to see dark, glistening hammocks slung in the angles where the peeled beams cross. 

Up here, there is no lime mortar to dissolve, only the peeled wood, the copper plumb-lines, and miles of damp rawhide lashing that groan and shrink in the heat. The creatures of the upper frames have thinned themselves out into long, ribbon-like sheets of muscle, pale amber and translucent as oiled vellum. They wrap themselves around the timber joints right alongside the world’s own rawhide thongs, mimicking the lashings so closely you only spot them when a broad, flat strap suddenly ripples, unbinds itself from a crossbeam, and loops upward like an inchworm made of wet glue to catch a higher spar.

They drink the hot, resinous condensation off the wood, and they lick the green verdigris bloom from the copper plumb-bobs—which gives their pale, translucent flesh faint, branching veins of bright turquoise. When a scaffold un-makes itself high in the murk, the beams simply sliding apart as their rawhide bindings vanish and reappear elsewhere, these ribbon-dwellers whip outward through the thick air like cast nets, catching the humid updrafts like flying squirrels, sailing slowly down through the scalding brass twilight until their sticky undersides slap wetly onto the rising parapet of an aqueduct quarter of a mile away.

Sometimes one misses its mark and falls all the way down to the pavement. 

It lands with a muffled, wet *smack* in two inches of steaming water. Immediately, its turquoise-veined skin flinches away from the razor-sharp edges of the fresh terracotta; built for smooth timber, it tears easily on the raw floor. Before it can haul itself fifty yards to the nearest vertical post, the heavy, dark-red elders of the flats have felt the impact through the water. 

They converge from three directions out of the yellow gloom—silent, six-foot bells of dark tallow gliding rapidly over the brick without raising a ripple. There is no violence to it, no snapping jaws or thrashing limbs. They simply surround the fallen sky-ribbon and lean inward together until their slick hides meet, forming a sealed, tent-like dome over their stranded cousin. Beneath that living bell, out of the dim light, you hear only a quiet, rhythmic hydraulic *slurp*, like a wet boot pulling out of clay. Five minutes later the elders part and slide their separate ways across the courtyard. Nothing remains on the sharp red brick—not a drop of turquoise mucilage, not a scrap of skin—only a clean, dry circle on the wet pavement that immediately beads over with fresh hot sweat.

And then there are the baskets.

Every few miles across the basin sit those woven reed baskets overflowing with wet grey mortar, waiting beside stairs that have already vanished. For reasons only their shifting internal pearls could explain, the creatures never eat from the baskets. A starving floor-drifter, torn half in two by a sudden trench-zippering and desperately needing lime to knit its leaking hide, will glide right up to the rim of an untouched mortar basket, press its blind, sensitive crown against the damp braided reeds, and shudder slightly—before turning away to hunt for mortar already laid between stones.

Instead, when two mature, mahogany-dark elders meet out on the endless, empty flats, it is almost always beside a tool or a basket. 

They draw up opposite one another across the brimming grey mound, towering slightly in the shadowless yellow haze, dripping clear water from their scalloped hems. Slowly, they extend their thick upper trunks and press them flat together over the untouched mortar. Their hides where they touch turn temporarily clear as glass, and through the fused window of their skin you can watch them trade their pearls—each creature’s heavy, calcified gravity-stone passing slowly through a dilated valve into the body of the other, carrying with it decades of accumulated weight, decades of memory written in concentric rings of lime concerning how the floor shifts, how the towers fall, and which angles of crooked pull mean nourishment and which mean the crushing dark below.

Before they part, each pinches off a small, fist-sized blister of its own red flesh, packed tight with grit and milky paste, and drops it—not onto the dangerous, shifting flagstones—but delicately onto the broad head of an unmarked mallet, or high up onto the rim of the woven reed basket. 

The world leaves the baskets alone. Whatever feverish drafting error tears up the ziggurats and shuffles the slate watchtowers like cards seems to treat the tools as fixed reference points on its invisible blueprint. So the nurseries sit safe on the rims of the mortar-baskets and in the lee of the copper plumb-bobs: little gleaming cabochons of rust-red jelly, pulsing faintly in the steam, completely still while stairs build themselves into the clouds three feet away and vanish again, waiting until their skins toughen enough to drop down into the scalding puddles and begin listening to the teeth-rattling tick of the endless foundations below.
