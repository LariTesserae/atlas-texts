If you sit very still on a warm transformer casing for an entire morning, watching the primer-gray birds doze along a porcelain bus-support, you will eventually realize that the fourth insulator from the left is breathing.

It has the exact white kaolin glaze of the others, and the same stacked, bell-skirted silhouette, until it shifts its weight. Then the skirts separate into stiff, overlapping plates of calcified hide, and a blunt, eyeless snout—gray and finely stippled like sand-cast aluminum—emerges from beneath the bottom flange to lick a bead of cold sweat off the steel bracket.

There are hundreds of them across the switchyards, though you can look directly at a tier for an hour and count only hardware. They are heavy, slow-blooded things, built something like pangolins and something like high-voltage bushings, weighing forty or fifty pounds apiece. Their backs are armored in dense, silicate-rich rings they secrete from the zinc dust and mineral crusts they scrape out of boiler seams. They do not merely imitate the insulators; they participate in the grid. Their bellies are broad, calloused pads of dark, conductive flesh that grip the copper busbars directly, bleeding off stray eddy currents to warm their sluggish insides while keeping their vitrified backs completely non-conductive. When a line surges, you can see a whole row of them subtly tighten their rings, soaking the sixty-cycle hum into their fat reserves with the placid contentment of lizards on a sunny wall. Once a week or so, one unclasps its toes, trundles six feet down a girder at the pace of cooling grease, mates silently with another bushing, and locks itself onto a fresh bolt-pattern before its core temperature drops.

In a valley without soil or rot, biology has had to learn the habits of hardware, or else step into the gaps where the hardware forgets itself.

Follow the silver mice when they dart into the dense, oil-smelling gloom of the middle tiers, and see why they run fast enough to pull their own bodies out of alignment. Suspended between two sweating cast-iron housings will be a patch of air about the size of a dinner plate that looks slightly off-plumb—as if you were viewing the rivets behind it through a flaw in old window glass. Nothing is spun there. There is no silk. Instead, clinging to the underside of an adjacent flange by eight long, caliper-thin legs of dull brassy chitin, is an arachnid the size of a closed fist, completely flat, holding a tiny tensioned frame of its own front tarsals at a precise angle to one of the ridge-top surveying towers two miles up.

It is an alignment hunter. It spends days micro-adjusting the posture of its body until it catches a tiny, localized sightline overlap—marrying three square inches of empty air directly in front of a mouse-run to the interior of its own waiting mandibles four feet up under the dark flange. When a silver mouse sprints along the pipe-belly, its leading, solid body hits that invisible seam and steps straight into the spider’s mouth, vanishing in mid-stride. For a quarter of a second, the mouse’s translucent blue lag-image continues trotting forward down the empty steel pipe alone, slows down, sniffs at nothing, and evaporates like alcohol on warm metal. That is why the mice bolt everywhere at top speed: if a predator catches only the trailing blue ghost, the mouse feels nothing worse than a sudden chill along its spine and a brief numbness in its hindquarters, and lives to run another day.

Higher up, where the glare off the zinc plating is blinding enough to sunburn the roof of an open mouth, larger things move through the open latticework—creatures that treat the valley’s broken rules not as traps, but as shelter and tool.

Look for the patches of misplaced basement light scattered across the bright causeways. You will rarely find one empty for long. Curled inside a twelve-foot square of dim, amber, shellac-smelling gloom—while fierce eleven-thousand-foot ultraviolet hammer-blows bleach the steel three inches past their noses—lie what look at first like discarded heaps of greasy Canvas and heavy rubberized tarpaulin.

When one stands up, it unfolds to the height of a tall man, though its proportions belong to nothing human. It is a knuckle-walker of the vertical trusses, long-armed and barrel-chested, covered in a dense, matted pelt the exact olive-drab and slate of heavy machine felt. Its face is flat, dark, and hairless, split by a wide, leathery mouth adapted for stripping wire-creepers and licking tallow out of deep journal housings, and over its eyes it wears a thick, translucent nictitating membrane tinted the dark green of welder’s glass.

They are quiet, deliberate animals, living in bands of six or eight, and they carry things.

Not sticks or stones—there are none—but stamped steel knockouts, spare shims, sheared cotter pins, and heavy crescent-shaped burrs pried from the undersides of gusset plates. Because nothing in the valley ever rusts or dulls, a steel curl dropped by a punch press is a permanent knife, sharper than obsidian. Yet the primates rarely use them for cutting. They use them as wedges against the geometry.

Watch an old male climb out onto a cantilevered girder as one of the great surveying towers on the rim approaches the end of its eleven-week turn. A herd of rebar-horned ungulates is crossing there, stepping casually off raw steel into a concrete viaduct a mile and a half away across the basin. The bridge is narrowing; as the brass mirrors up on the ridge creep fractionally eastward, the visual overlap between near beam and far parapet thins from four feet wide down to ten inches, then six. The last few yearling goats hurry across, their bodies briefly halved by distance. Just before the sightline severs cleanly into a three-hundred-foot fatal drop, the felt-coated ape ambles out to the tip of the steel, squints up toward the distant mirror-tower through his green eye-shields, and carefully sets a three-pound, mirror-bright zinc stamping at an oblique angle on the flange.

The bright metal catches the glare and throws a false edge back up toward the ridge. For another twenty minutes, by sheer optical stubbornness, a four-inch sliver of the bridge holds.

One slow, lagging doe—heavy with young, her rubbery hooves slipping slightly on the morning condensation—reaches the beam late. She puts a trembling forehoof onto the four-inch seam. The ape reaches out with one long, grease-darkened arm and flicks his zinc shim off the girder into the abyss.

The seam snaps shut while the doe is halfway through.

There is no blood—almost nothing in this dry, cold glare bleeds openly if it can help it, because spilled liquid freezes at night and fouls the footing—just a quiet, clean transaction with distance. On the far viaduct a mile and a half away, the front half of the doe stumbles forward two steps on tiny, blue-hazed forelegs and collapses silently onto the concrete, feeding the nickel-colored crabs and primer-gray birds on the western wall. Right here on the near beam, the heavy hindquarters drop heavily onto the diamond-tread grating with a dull, meaty *thud* that dies ten feet away. Before the shim has even finished falling into the condenser pit below, the whole band of felt-backed apes has gathered around the meat in the silent noon sun, pulling off strips of tallow-sweet fat and carrying them back into the nearest pocket of warm, dark indoor air to eat in peace.

Go down below the apes, though. Go down past the middle tiers where the water mains pass ghostlike through turning iron spokes, down through the brass-hinged hatches where the drill-shavings still gleam like new gold, into the fifth and sixth sub-levels where the sun never reaches and the ambient light comes only from faint corona discharges along lead-sheathed cables and the occasional square of misplaced noon sky trapped inexplicably between two boiler bellies.

Something else keeps the lower levels. Or rather, the lower levels have grown a keeper.

You notice its habits long before you see it: small, fastidious arrangements that serve no mechanical purpose. On top of a dark cast-iron housing, forty brass hexagonal nuts—none of them missing from any nearby bolt; the valley produces slight surpluses where two stamped assemblies intersect—will be lined up in precise, diminishing order of size, except that every seventh nut has been turned thirty degrees out of true. In a dry settling pan nearby, curls of bright copper swarf from a hinge-boring are woven tightly around the dried skull of a goat, packed with yellow bearing-grease so it gleams like an icon.

If you wait in the cool dark beside a counterweight shaft, listening to the faint, dry *tick... tick...* of thermal contraction in a pipe three yards away, they come walking straight out of a solid riveted boiler shell.

They do not use the hatches. To them, the crowded, overlapping machinery of the deep levels is porous in ways a surface creature cannot read. Where two cast-iron volumes intersect—where a pump housing shares six cubic feet of space with a concrete pier—there is a seam of mutual cancellation, a narrow tunnel of zero-density iron that looks entirely solid to the eye, marked only by that glistening line of frost.

What slips out through the frost-line is long, pale, and segmented, built low to the floorplate like a cross between a cave-salamander and a draftsman’s articulated rule. Eight feet from nose to tail-tip, no thicker than a man’s thigh, with skin the smooth, translucent yellowish-white of oiled vellum. Beneath that skin you can dimly see dark, sliding internal rods that are not quite bone—more like graphitic linkages that fold at sharp, forty-five- and ninety-degree angles when it turns a corner. Its head is flat and spade-shaped, without eyes or ears, fringed instead with dozens of fine, trembling copper-colored whiskers that read electrostatic fields, tiny thermal gradients, and the subtle pressure-changes of solid steel passing through solid steel.

There are not many of them, and they do not hunt the animals above. They graze on *friction*—or on what this valley substitutes for friction. Everywhere two incompatible objects pass through one another without breaking, a thin, bitter rime of displaced matter and frozen static weeps along the seam, looking like hoarfrost and smelling faintly of ozone and crushed flint. The vellum-skinned things lick those intersections clean with broad, grey, file-textured tongues. Without them, perhaps, the frost would build up into sluggishness; pipes might begin to remember they are solid, and flywheels might finally seize against the mains they bisect.

They are utterly indifferent to an observer until you carry a straightedge or drop a shadow across a seam. If you stand in one of the subterranean patches of misplaced daylight, your shadow falls across the floorplate—and if one of the pale, hinged creatures encounters that dark edge, it stops dead. Its copper whiskers flare wide. It will press its flat chin down against the line where light meets dark, testing it as if it were a physical step, and then slowly, meticulously, begin arranging small curls of metal and pebbles of hardened tallow along the outline of your coat, pinning your silhouette to the zinc plate before sliding silently back into the belly of a compressor, leaving its small brass tokens behind when you walk away.

And at the very top of the basin—four miles wide, eleven thousand feet up in the coin-thin air—there is the mountain shadow cast by the quarter-mile gantry crane.

Nothing that lives on the lower terraces will cross into that shadow if it can help it, except for one thing.

When the ragged, dark outline of wind-tossed pines slides slowly across a sunlit switchyard—inch by patient inch over fresh steel and white porcelain—the primer-gray birds wake up and shuffle sideways along their perches to stay in the blinding glare. The rebar-horned goats pause at its border, lowering their square-pupiled eyes to sniff at the dark steel two inches ahead, where the silhouettes of invisible needles tremble slightly in a breeze they cannot feel on their faces, and then detour half a mile down a ladder-cage rather than step over the edge. Even the silver mice will skid to a halt, let their blue ghosts snap back into their spines, and turn tail.

Because things live *inside* the shadow that are nowhere on the steel.

Stand just outside the dark line as it creeps over a flat expanse of galvanized diamond-tread. The metal inside the shadow is just as new, bolt-studded, and dry as the metal under your boots—except that where the dark pine-boughs sway across it, the diamond-tread is intermittently depressed, silently, one small oval dimple after another, by something heavy walking along a steep, pine-needle-strewn slope that isn’t there. A dry, resinous smell—crushed fir cones, damp granite, rotting autumn duff, odors completely alien to this grease-and-zinc world—drifts up for three feet out of clean, cold zinc plating and vanishes into the dry altitude.

And looking closely at the crane-shadow itself, moving among the dark silhouettes of the non-existent trees, you can see other shadows moving independently within it. Large, thick-shouldered, hump-backed shadows shuffling between the trunks; swift, long-tailed shadows leaping from one dark bough to the next across forty-foot drops of empty switchyard air; once in a long while, standing upright at the very crest of the phantom ridge where the crane’s main box-girder cuts the cobalt sky, a tall, narrow, two-legged shadow leaning on something long and straight, looking down into the bright basin.

They never step out onto the sunlit steel—there is nowhere out there for their feet to land—unless one of the valley’s pockets of misplaced indoor gloom happens to lie directly in the crane-shadow’s afternoon path.

When the dark pine-ridge creeps over twelve square feet of dim, amber, basement-lit causeway, the two impossibilities overlap, and for twenty minutes the air inside that square thickens like cold glass. If a stray kid from the goat herds has lingered asleep in the warm shellac-scented dimness, it does not wake up screaming; there is no sound here sharp enough for a scream. From twenty yards away in the fierce glare, you see only the dark boughs enter the amber patch, a sudden, silent thrashing of heavy invisible brush that knocks loose three real steel rivets from a nearby handrail, and then the kid is simply lifted—upward along an invisible forty-degree incline, climbing steadily through empty air above the catwalk as something huge and unseen carries it upslope into the trees—fading cleanly out of sight the moment it crosses the upper edge of the crane’s shadow into the blinding blue sky.

Ten minutes later, the shadow inches on. The handrail sits bright and unmarked in the sun, three fresh, unrusted holes gleaming where the rivets used to be, beads of cold mountain sweat already gathering along their sharp inner rims, waiting for the sedge-roots or the crabs to find a use for them.
