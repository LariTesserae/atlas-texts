To see the people of the basin, you have to step out of the wet, fern-choked lanes and climb a spiral iron stair into the corrugated tin skyways, or duck through a heavy, felt-gasketed door into one of the factory sheds. 

When you first meet them in the amber gloom of a corridor, they strike you as stout, barrel-bodied, and curiously deliberate, moving with the unhurried, fluid economy of deep-sea divers. Like the dwarf laurels and the terraced brick, they have been shaped by the barometric reality of the roof. Generations in the oxygen-thin air have given them immense, vaulted ribcages to house oversized lungs, and thick, muscular necks. Their skin has the pale, slightly translucent cast of people who live beneath a permanent ceiling of cloud, underscored by a dense, plum-colored flush across the cheekbones, knuckles, and ears—the mark of blood rich and thick with extra red corpuscles, pumped hard by heavy hearts.

Nobody shouts here; nobody wastes breath. In the thin air, raising one’s voice requires a bellows-effort that leaves a newcomer dizzy, and the moss-muffled acoustics of the town would swallow a yell fifty feet away regardless. Consequently, the townsfolk speak in low, resonant chest-tones, standing very close to one another so their words don't have to travel. Better yet, they don't speak at all. In the humming dimness of the flywheel sheds and the tin-echoing catwalks, they rely on an intricate, rapid-fire sign language mixed with percussive clicks of their brass-tipped tools against iron railings or steam pipes. A grease-monkey three tiers down in a cable trench doesn't look up when he hears a dull *tink-tink-tink* travel through the cast-iron stanchion beside his head; he simply reaches out with a hooked bar and adjusts the tension on a sheave, answering with two flat raps of his wrench.

Their clothing is a study in keeping the wet out and the scarce heat in. Cotton rots here; bare iron rusts in an afternoon, and untreated leather grows a pelt of pale green mold within a week. The workers dress in heavy, densely felted wool sheared from the semi-feral, lichen-shagged browsers that wander the plateau, boiled and beaten until it is stiff as pasteboard, then impregnated with whale-oil or refined mineral wax. Beneath these slick, dark carapaces, they layer vests quilted with the grey-green wool harvested from the giant chalice-plants—an insulator lighter than down that refuses to hold water. Every machinist, cable-walker, and peat-cutter wears a wide brass chatelaine at the belt, hung not just with spanners and pressure-keys, but with small, perforated copper tins stuffed with glowing peat-charcoal, tucked inside their coats to warm the core without throwing an open flame.

Because fire is lazy, sputtering, and starved at this altitude, the kitchen hearth as lowlanders know it does not exist. Domestic life clusters tightly around the municipal steam-mains and pneumatic manifolds. Inside the slate-roofed tenements—whose interiors are cozy, low-ceilinged burrows lined in dark, resinous ironwood and hung with thick tapestries to trap warmth—cooking is done in heavy, clamped cast-iron autoclaves bolted directly to the floorboards. Without pressure, water boils here at a temperature too tepid to soften a root or stew a tough cut of browser-mutton. So every meal is pressure-cooked, hissing softly on a brass bypass valve bled off the neighborhood steam-trunk. 

They drink constantly to combat the dry, invisible theft of moisture that high altitude inflicts on every exhaled breath. In every workshop niche and tenement parlor sits a squat copper urn jacketed in felt, dispensing a thick, steaming decoction brewed from roasted ironwood bark, fermented cloud-lichen, and a splash of heavy, salted tallow. It tastes sharply of turpentine, bog-smoke, and broth, and newcomers gag on it, yet after a week in the mist, the body begins to crave its dense, bitter heat.

If the humans are the mind of this quiet, pressurized symbiosis, they are very far from its sole inhabitants. The convergence of steady industrial warmth and impenetrable alpine thicket has bred an entire shadow-ecology in the crawlspaces, trenches, and tangled canopies just outside the brick walls.

Down in the greased stone channels where the traction-cables slide endlessly (*shhh-clack, shhh-clack*), live the *grease-backs*—flattened, pale amphibians the length of a man's forearm, blind and slick-skinned, with wide, spatulate toes. Originally native to the cold, deep peat-springs of the plateau, they migrated generations ago into the warm microclimates beneath the iron sheaves. They feed exclusively on the heavy vegetable-and-tallow lubricants that drip from the cables, and on the pale, thread-legged cave crickets that swarm near the warm bearings. The trench-crews leave them strictly alone; a healthy colony of grease-backs keeps the drainage runnels clear of the gelatinous, iron-eating bacterial mats that would otherwise choke the sumps in a month. You see them only when a maintenance hatch is lifted: dozens of translucent, tallow-colored shapes slipping silently over one another into the warm, oily dark.

Out in the chest-high canopy that crowds against the factory walls—where the dinner-plate blooms of the steam-vines open to the fog—there are no bees or butterflies; the wind would tear delicate wings to tatters in seconds. Instead, the blossoms are pollinated by something flightless and heavy-footed. If you watch a pale flower quivering near a brass relief valve at dusk, you will eventually see a creature the size of a terrier haul itself up the python-thick vine using long, curved, sloth-like claws. The locals call them *valve-apes* or *moss-badgers*, though they are closer to ancient, surviving marsupials. Their dense, olive-black fur is grooved down each hair to channel water away, hosting its own living patina of green algae for camouflage. They move slowly through the interlocking ironwood branches, burying their long, hairless, leathery snouts deep into the scentless flowers to lap at the warm, starchy nectar within, snorting little plumes of vapor before curling tightly around an uninsulated pipe-joint to sleep through the freezing night.

Even the air inside the factories has its quiet tenants. High up under the moss-furred glass roofs, where the giant flywheels spin in silent blurs and throw off a faint, warm draft scented with ozone, hang colonies of *felt-moths*. They have wingspans as wide as two open hands, covered in a pile of grey-brown fur so thick they look like scraps of dropped wool upholstery. They cannot fly in the wind outside; they belong entirely to the vast, dim, calm-aired sheds. When a flywheel slows for maintenance, hundreds of them detach from the iron trusses and drift downward like heavy ashes, settling onto the warm brick housings to lick the condensed mineral sweat from the mortar.

And then there is the peat itself—that ten-foot-deep, waterlogged mattress of slow-rotting roots upon which the entire town floats and anchors. Nothing large burrows easily through a tangle that dense, yet the mattress is restless. On very still evenings, when the wind-turbines on the rim drop to a low, sub-audible throb, workers walking the lowest stone terraces sometimes feel a slow, peristaltic heave pass beneath the cobbles. Deep down at the granite bedrock, where the geothermal ghosts of the ancient mountain roots meet the warm greywater seeping down from the town’s baths and mills, live blind, segmented worms as thick around as sewer pipes and twenty feet long. They spend centuries chewing slowly through the petrified, iron-hard heartwood of buried cloud-laurels, turning the compressed history of the plateau into rich, black silt. They never surface—the thin, cold air above would suffocate their skin in minutes—except once in a generation when a steam-main ruptures underground and cooks a section of the bog. Then, in the pearlescent dawn, the cable-crews look down from their tin catwalks to see great, glistening, slate-blue loops surfaced briefly among the Cushion plants, steaming furiously in the chill, before sinking back forever into the green dark that swallows everything.

The people of the basin watch such things from behind condensation-streaked panes of thick glass, sipping their bitter bark-tea, entirely unperturbed. They have lived on the planed-off bones of the world long enough to know that survival here is not a matter of conquering the mountain, nor of resisting the creeping green. It is simply a matter of keeping the pressure up, keeping the cables greased, and letting your heartbeat slow down to match the heavy, greased turn of the iron pines on the rim.
