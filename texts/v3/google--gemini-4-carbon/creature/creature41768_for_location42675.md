To find them, you cannot rely on hearing, because the roaring bass of the gall drowns out anything softer than a falling timber, and you cannot use a lamp, because even if you brought light into this place, you would look right at them and see only the architecture.

Look instead—with your hands—at the fifth and sixth tiers of the western curve, along a narrow gallery where three ventilation chutes converge. Reach out to the balustrade, the one thickened like a row of old molars. Run your palm along the cool, tallow-slick wood. Two balusters, three balusters, a supporting post, and then a fourth baluster that is a fraction of a degree warmer than the wood beside it, and vibrates at a slightly duller harmonic. 

Leave your hand there for an hour. Two hours. If you are exceptionally still, you will feel the grain of that post shift laterally under your skin, sliding by the breadth of a poppy seed, accompanied by the faintest dry rasp—like two pieces of heavy vellum being drawn across each other under great weight.

It is not a post. It is a knee, or what serves for a knee, braced between the floor-plank and the handrail to keep its owner from being shaken loose by the acoustic battering of the room.

They have no name for themselves, because names are spoken into the air, and the air here belongs entirely to the drone. If they are descendants of the original adze-wielders who hollowed and grafted this chamber epochs ago, they have diverged so profoundly to survive the desiccation that the ancestry is purely notional—a ghost of bilateral symmetry and tool-use preserved in creatures made of dry horn, parchment, and heavy grease. 

At first touch, an individual feels like a bundle of seasoned, bark-stripped sticks lashed together with flat rawhide thongs and rubbed thoroughly with beeswax. They are roughly the height of a child, though folded so tightly against the wooden surfaces that they rarely show their full length. Their limbs are elongated, flat-sided, and sharp-edged, resembling carpenter’s folding rules or the levers of a loom. There is no soft flesh on them anywhere; water vanished from their chemistry back when the plains overhead were still merely arid. Instead of blood, their shallow, sluggish vessels push a viscous, camphor-scented lipid—stolen directly from the tree’s own slow sap—through tissues built of cross-linked keratin and dry elastin. You could snap a finger off one of them (though it would take a heavy blow with a stone) and the break would not bleed; it would show a pale, waxy cross-section like old ivory or hard soap, dry to the bone, smelling faintly of coriander and dust.

Because they have no water to spare for mucus, tears, or breath-vapor, they have no open orifices. Eyes were abandoned long before the doors of the upper tenements began to pinch shut; where a face might have been, there is only a broad, slightly concave shield of thin, resonant horn—a dry tympanum stretched over a lattice of sinus-bones, angled to catch the rebounding standing waves of the void. They do not breathe air through lungs. Along the undersides of their forearms and the hollows of their ribs lie rows of tight, valvular slits, guarded by stiff electrostatic bristles that comb the flour-fine chaff out of the gale before admitting tiny, measured sips of dry crustal gas directly into waxy diffusion-tracts. 

Nothing in this city can afford friction, and nothing can afford hurry. Metabolic heat without water to sweat it away is fatal, and swift movement grinds dry joints to powder. So the inhabitants live at the speed of wood-warp. 

It takes a citizen of the tiers forty minutes to unhook a three-clawed foot from a rung, swing it twelve inches through the roaring dark, and lock the heel-spur into a mortise worn smooth by ten thousand generations of that exact same step. Watch them—or rather, trace them—across a century, and they would blur into a frantic, industrious crowd, scurrying along the narrow ring-streets, tending the city. Seen in human time, they are fixtures. One stands cantilevered out over the fifty-yard drop at the edge of a tiny plaza, three limbs wedged into structural notches, its fourth arm extended straight into a screaming air-vent, holding a thin baffle of split root-wood at an exact forty-two-degree angle against the rushing draft. It has been adjusting that baffle since Tuesday. By next month, having deflected the airflow downward toward a clogged viaduct two tiers below to scour out a drift of spore-dander, it will retract its arm, fold itself flat against the wall to let another pass, and spend a season resting in a shallow niche.

For they are still urban, and they are still builders, though they put down their flint scrapers and bone chisels millions of years ago. Stones dull; bone turns brittle and shatters; and when the wood crept over their ancestors’ tool chests, sealing them into amber-hard burls, they simply learned to work the gall with their own bodies.

Instead of carving, they splint, pinch, and drink.

Where an ancient doorway threatens to sphincter shut—cutting off an essential arterial corridor or muting a resonant chamber needed for navigation—two or three of them will gather in the aperture, wedging their flat, dense torsos directly into the encroaching ring of cambium. They lock their joints like pit-props and stay there for years, letting the tree push against their horn-sheathed backs, forcing the callus to divide and flow *around* them, training a single closing hole into twin arches. 

While braced there, they feed. Beneath the flat chin-plate, tucked safely out of the desiccating wind, lies a muscular, corkscrew-tipped proboscis of hardened dentine, slick with natural wax. With infinite slowness, using the six-hour tidal flex of the surrounding district to lend leverage to their own lean muscles, they drill a pinhole through the polished inner bark until they strike a deep lipid vein. They do not gorge; the tree’s sap moves too sluggishly for that. A single tap yields perhaps a drop of heavy, mineral-rich oil a week. When they withdraw the drill a decade later, they plug the microscopic wound with a pellet of their own dry, resinous dung so the timber does not weep or scar.

And the city’s terrible, continuous music is not an accident of erosion, nor a ruin humming in the wind. It is their sight, their speech, and their municipal ledger.

In pitch darkness, where scent is whipped away instantly by the draft and touch reaches only an arm's length, the standing acoustic waves are the entire sensible world. Every open room in the ten thousand tenements acts as a Helmholtz resonator; every taut vascular cable is a string; every doorway is a flue pipe. When the atmospheric tide rushes down from the dead continent above and sets the hollow booming, the inhabitants read the three-dimensional interference pattern of the roar through the horn-shields of their skulls and the dry, sensitive pads of their clamped feet. 

They feel the exact shape of the fifty-yard void in the way the bass throbs against their sternums. If a room eight levels up and thirty yards across the curve finally seals itself off, a tiny overtone vanishes from the global chord; every citizen on every tier registers the sudden flat spot in the harmony and knows that gallery is now a dead end. 

More than that: they play it. 

Those tremendous *cracks* that slam from wall to wall every few minutes—the sound of braided cables slipping over wooden bridges—are rarely unattended. Go up to the high suspension galleries under the curve of the skull-ceiling, where the thigh-thick vascular ropes groan under centuries of warping tension. Clinging to the underside of a taut fiber, upside down like a dry sloth, you will find an elder, older and darker and denser than the timber it grips, its carapace polished to a mirror sheen (if there were light to gleam on it) by ages of drifting dust. 

Its flat, hooked hands are wrapped around a wooden peg wedged beneath the cable. For six months it has been feeling the tectonic strain gather up from the deep crustal faults, listening through its wrists as the fiber stretches toward breaking—a break that would whip across the upper balconies and smash three levels of woven latticework. Waiting for the exact crest of the six-hour structural inhale, when the ambient bass hits its lowest trough, the elder leans its small, dense weight onto the peg. 

The seal breaks cleanly. *Crack.* 

The cable slips half an inch across the bridge without snapping, bleeding off forty years of lethal tension in a single shockwave. And because the elder braced its own resonant chest-shield against the bridge at the moment of release, the shockwave carries an asymmetric chime—a distinct, sharp timber-click buried inside the thunderclap. 

Across the black void, clinging to staircases hollowed like spoons, wedged into capsule-dwellings behind woven shutters, dozens of silent, horn-plated listeners feel that specific transient strike their ribs. They read the elasticity of the cable, the health of the upper crown, and the identity of the rigger who tripped it. Slowly—over the course of the afternoon—three figures on the lower tiers unhook their spurs and begin the week-long climb toward the crown, bringing thin strips of dried tendril tucked under their folded belly-plates to lash a fresh brace beside the slipped one.

Down at the very bottom of the bowl, where nothing ever crawls, the crescent dunes of powdery chaff sweep back and forth in the acoustic wind. Now and then, something small and angular tumbles down from the vertical dark above, clattering dryly down the terraced flanks before rolling out onto the smooth floor: a shed husk, or else an inhabitant whose lipid-tracts finally crystallized with sheer age, whose joints locked solid, and whose claws finally let go of the wall during a hard snap. 

It does not rot. There are no beetles, no fungi, no dampness to unmake it. Vibrated continuously by the standing waves, scoured day and night by the migrating ripples of flour-fine wood-skin, the brittle, horn-dry corpse slowly buffs away over millennia—turning thin as shave-grass, then translucent as mica, then breaking down into fine, camphor-scented powder that joins the crescent dunes, rises on the six-hour draft, and goes whistling back up through the flutes of the city to polish the faces of the living.
