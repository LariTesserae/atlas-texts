To find the valley’s truest inhabitants, you have to stop listening *through* the racket and start listening *to* its cadence. 

Stand long enough by one of the forty-second water-shelves, counting the intervals between those flat, concussive *slaps*, and you may notice that on the twelfth cycle, the fifty gallons of falling water do not hit the mud. They hit a woven hurdle of dark willow switches propped at a precise, canted angle two inches above the silt, splitting the falling mass so that half of it shoots sideways into a shallow ditch lined with river-smooth slate, and the other half shatters into spray directly over a bed of pale, trembling club-moss. 

Ten minutes later, someone will come out of the alder scrub to collect the hurdle.

At thirty paces, through the thick pewter air, you would swear you were looking at an animated bundle of the fen itself—a rounded, top-heavy hummock of wet peat, layered sedge-thatch, and tarnished bog-iron that has somehow risen onto two thick, pillar-like legs. Only when they draw near, moving with a rolling, unhurried gait that never once triggers a spatial shear or slips on the greased slate, does the thatch resolve into a heavy cape of braided hair-grass and split rushes, worn slick and silver at the cuffs.

There are perhaps three or four hundred of them scattered across the miles of the sunken expanse, living in low, dispersed knots of two to six—never enough in one place to weigh down the peat, never enough to raise their voices against the valley’s endless hydro-acoustic roar. They have no name for themselves that translates as a noun; in their own quiet register, they refer to one another by a gesture that means *those who step between the clicks*.

Physically, they look vaguely human in the way a cave-salamander looks vaguely like a lizard that once knew the sun—softened, widened, and thoroughly edited by a thousand generations in the cool, dripping half-light. They stand barely four and a half feet tall, built low and dense, with wide, barrelled chests and virtually no visible neck; their heads sit tucked neatly down between broad, sloping shoulders like boulders settled into moss. Their skin has the cool, dense, slightly rubbery grain of river-washed tallow or pale mushroom-flesh, shaded from pewter-grey at the extremities to a faint, bruised plum under the jaw and along the inner wrists, where great slow veins carry blood rich enough to hold heat through twenty hours of ankle-deep soaking. 

Their feet are extraordinary: broad as spades, completely unshod, with thick, splayed toes webbed halfway to the first knuckle and soled in grey, leathery calluses deeply grooved like the underside of a slug. When a fen-dweller sets a foot down into the black silt, those grooves spread and grip the wet sphagnum without breaking the root-mat, distributing their considerable, compact weight so evenly that the opal sediment barely clouds around their ankles.

Look at a face up close—if one pauses on a hummock four yards off, tilting their head to regard you—and you see immediately what the valley’s broken optics have done to them. Their eyes are small, dark, set deep beneath heavy, hairless brow-ridges that act as gutters to shed constant condensation, and they do not quite track together. One eye will fix directly on your chest while the other drifts three degrees outward and slightly up, focused on the refracted Ghost-image of you leaking around a nearby spiral of light-bending rushes. They do not suffer double vision; their brains long ago gave up the assumption that an object occupies only one visual angle at a time. To look at a thing properly, in their estimation, is to watch where it is and where the air smears it simultaneously.

More striking still are their hands and the sides of their throats. Extending past their short, blunt fingers are two disproportionately long, pale, flexible digits—the fourth and fifth fingers stretched out like the feelers of a bottom-feeding catfish, perpetually bare and trembling slightly in the wet air. Along the jawline and down the thick column of the neck, the skin is raised in neat, parallel rows of tiny, sensitive papillae, almost like the lateral line of an eel. They feel barometric drops before they happen. Watch one walking near a thumping low-pressure sphere: three seconds before the air implodes, the skin along their neck ripples, gooseflesh flaring silver, and they lean casually into the coming suction at forty-five degrees, letting the vacuum tug at their heavy rush-cape to pull them effortlessly up a slick incline, using an invisible anomaly as a handrail.

They do not speak aloud. Trying to shout syllables over the *thud-thud-thud* of locked standing waves, the iron-quench hiss of linear rain-curtains, and the million-fold dry popping of phase-boundary grass would have torn their throats ragged ages ago. Their mouths are small, calm, and usually closed. Instead, they speak *into the ground* and *through the seams*.

Beneath the rush-capes, slung at every hip on cords of braided sinew and bog-myrtle root, hang three or four small, flat clappers of dense bog-iron and cured swamp-birch—wood harvested exclusively from the static, rot-immune bands of fallen trees, hard as horn and incapable of swelling in the damp. To speak to a companion fifty yards away across a churning channel, a dweller crouches slightly, sinks a bare heel firmly into a flooded runnel, and taps two iron-shod blocks together beneath the water’s surface. *Tak-tak. Tak-trrrk-tak.* Water is continuous across the valley floor; sound travels through the drowned peat mattress five times faster and infinitely cleaner than through the fractured air. Fifty yards off, the companion pauses, lifts one broad foot slightly so the sensitive arch rests just at the water’s skin, feels the crisp Morse of vibrations through their ankle bones, and answers with a single, heavy heel-thump into the silt.

When they are close together—huddled beneath an overhanging shelf of slate while a razor-line of mist hisses into rain inches from their noses—they use a softer, stranger intimacy. They reach their long, trembling outer fingers into the same spatial fold. Two people sitting ten feet apart on dry hummocks will each slip a hand wrist-deep into an invisible horizontal pinch among the bog-myrtle twigs; twenty paces downstream, high over an open pool, two pale hands emerge side by side among the rootless, floating leaves and touch fingertips in the empty mist, tapping rapid, silent rhythms against each other’s knuckles where no obstacle lies between them.

Everything about their material life is an effortless, opportunistic parasitism upon the phantom machine. They own no metal tools they forged themselves, burn no fires—nothing in this valley has been dry enough to catch a spark since the bluffs were sharp—and build no walls that the mud would only swallow. Yet they live in mild, steaming comfort.

Their dwellings are called, in the heel-tap tongue, something equivalent to *stillnesses*. They seek out wide, tangled domes of dark willow and alder that happen to straddle one of the valley’s thermal or entropic snags. By bending living branches into a tight basket-weave and plastering the exterior with heavy mats of living liverwort and black silt, they enclose a pocket of space perhaps eight feet across. Inside, they do not light a hearth. Instead, at the center of the floor, they dig a neat basin six inches deep right across a microscopic phase-boundary—the same kind that makes dew pop on the metallic grass blades. They trick a slow, steady ribbon of cold marsh water into trickling across that invisible line. As the water hits the boundary, millions of droplets flash-freeze and instantly vaporize in a continuous, tightly contained *fzzzzz-pop*, releasing a steady, gentle plume of latent heat and warm, sterile mist that keeps the interior of the willow-dome twenty degrees warmer than the pewter chill outside, smelling richly of steamed mint-herbs and warm stone.

For light inside these dark, root-woven chambers, they keep shallow bowls of static-wood filled with the fine, pulverized opal sediment stirred from the bog’s footsteps, mixed with clear slime scraped from the rapidly decaying five-minute bracket fungi. Placed near an acoustic thump-zone, the rhythmic vibration keeps the heavy sediment perpetually suspended in the fungal gel, catching and multiplying whatever dim stray luminescence leaks through the weave until the bowl glows like a cool, milky moon on the peat floor.

Their kitchens and workshops are scattered across acres of open marsh, marked only by subtle arrangements of sticks and stones that an outsider would walk past twenty times without seeing. 

Look closely at that fallen swamp-birch with the three-foot band of frantic, five-minute rot. Pushed carefully into the edge of that stutter-zone is a woven basket of tough, bitter mud-lily tubers and a gutted, translucent mud-eel wrapped in broad sedge leaves. Left in the ordinary bog, the tubers would take days of boiling to soften and leach their poison. Pushed four inches into the entropy-band—tethered on a long willow pole—their tough starches break down, ferment into a sweet, dark, cheesy paste, and cure the eel-flesh to a buttery tenderness in ninety seconds flat. A round-shouldered figure stands patiently in the wet moss, counting their own slow heartbeats—one, two, three, up to sixty—before hauling the pole back with a practiced jerk. Pull it out five seconds too late, and the basket comes back empty, its contents liquefied into black ink and washed away; pull it out on time, and dinner is warm, pungent, and safe, whereupon it is immediately tucked ten inches down the same trunk into the *static* band, where leftovers can sit unwrapped on the pristine bark for three months without attracting a single spore of mold or losing a drop of moisture.

They hunt very little, and never with weapons thrown through the air; throwing a spear in a valley full of invisible shear-lines and bent light is a good way to skewer your own calf from behind. Instead, they are trappers of geometry. They watch the gaunt, slate-gray wading herons for days, squatting motionless under their mossy capes like extra hummocks until they map every spatial shortcut around a weeping boulder. When they know exactly where a heron’s lunging beak emerges five yards out in the empty marsh, they don’t bother with the bird—the bird is stringy, sour, and too clever to waste. They simply anchor a funnel-trap of peeled willow twigs right over the exit-mouth of the spatial pocket under the mud, wait for the blind mud-eels and fat, eyeless basalt-frogs to come swimming up the shortcut toward the warmer surface water, and lift the full basket out from twenty paces away while sitting comfortably dry on a granite rise.

Because nothing rots unless they put it in a rot-band, and nothing freezes unless it crosses a freeze-line, they have no anxiety about winter or famine. The valley’s climate is locked in its perpetual, bruised-plum autumn by the sheer mass of atmospheric moisture and the endless churning energy of the anomalies. This leaves the inhabitants with immense, oceanic stretches of unhurried time, and it is in what they do with those hours that their true strangeness surfaces.

They are listeners, and they are tuners—though whether they are maintaining the ancient machine, playing it like a vast earthen organ, or simply tidying up noises that bother their sleep, not even they would frame as a philosophy.

Sometimes, moving quietly across the central fen where the standing waves churn *thud-thud-thud* over flat silt beds, you will come upon three or four of them standing waist-deep in the cold, foaming channels, eyes half-shut, rush-capes floating around them like lily pads. With tremendous, patient effort, two of them will roll a hundred-pound cobble of dark, smoothed slate six inches to the left on the channel bed. Instantly, the locked wave shifts its pitch. The heavy *thud-thud-thud* drops a third into a deeper, rounder *boom-boom-boom*, and fifty yards away, a razor-line of hissing mist suddenly goes silent, its condensation shifting twenty feet north to patter harmlessly onto a thicket of black alder instead of eroding the bank near a dwelling-dome.

The elders among them—ancient, squat figures whose pale skin has grown subtle, lichenous patches of symbiotic green algae in the creases of their knuckles and ears, and whose eyes drift widest apart—carry mental maps of causal chains spanning miles of mud. They know that if a patch of spiral rushes grows too dense in the west basin, bending too much pewter light away from a bog-iron lip, the hovering pane of water half a mile east will drop at thirty-nine seconds instead of forty, throwing off the cross-rhythm of the peat-vibrations so badly that no one in three neighboring domes can hear a heel-tap cleanly. So twice a season, an elder wades out with a sharp sliver of slate-stone and trims back forty stems of rush—no more, no less—until the distant *slap* settles back into its proper heartbeat, the mud hums clear again, and the eyeless basalt-frogs resume their slow, pulsing chorus in the roots.

If you stay on your weathered granite hummock without shouting, without waving shiny gear or stamping clumsily into the runnels, one of them will eventually come to inspect you. 

You will not hear them approach; the roar of the channels covers everything, and their grooved soles make no splash. You will simply look down from watching a floating crown of bog-myrtle shiver in the mist, and realize that one of the mossy mounds ten feet below your boots has two dark, mismatched eyes peering out from beneath a dripping shelf of braided sedge.

One eye will look right into yours; the other will gaze calmly past your left ear at the smeared, refracted ghost of your shoulder blade hanging in a lens of damp air. Along their thick, plum-tinted throat, the rows of tiny sensory bumps will twitch and ripple, tasting your rapid heartbeat, the dry crackle of your synthetic coat, and the anxious heat rolling off your skin.

After a long minute—perhaps three complete *slaps* of the nearest water-shelf—they may slowly lift one hand out from beneath their sodden cape. Between the short thumb and forefinger, they hold a broad, slick leaf of marsh-marigold folded into a neat cup, containing two spoonfuls of clear, cold water holding three suspended, unpopped beads of phase-locked ice that chime faintly like tiny glass marbles against the leaf’s green skin, alongside a thumb-sized lump of sweet, fermented tuber-paste wrapped in rot-less birch bark.

They will not walk the last ten feet up the granite to hand it to you. Instead, they will reach sideways and push the leaf-cup gently into an invisible seam six inches off the ground beside a clump of horsetails—where their pale, six-fingered wrist vanishes abruptly into empty air—and right beside your boot, out of a bare patch of weeping grey slate, pale fingers will emerge from nothing, set the offering neatly on a cushion of silver-green sphagnum, tap twice softly against the stone—*welcome, step slow*—and draw quietly back into the mist.
