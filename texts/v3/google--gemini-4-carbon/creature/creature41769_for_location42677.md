To find what governs the town—as much as anything can govern a self-grown resonator—you have to look away from the frenzied outer boulevards and peer into the one inch of dead air between the wooden facade and the acoustic wall. Or better yet, wait for a vascular flush to clear an arcade, and watch what does not run for cover.

They are easy to miss at first because they move at the exact cadence of the plant’s sap-pulse, advancing in smooth, hydraulic surges twice a minute, pausing while the conduits contract, then gliding forward again on the rebound. Local movement is all staccato—scuttling chitin, snapping jaws, jittering wings—so something that slides with the heavy, deliberate grace of oil down a banister hardly registers as an animal at all. 

They are flat, supple vertebrates, related distantly to the heavy-bodied geckos overhead, though centuries in the town have pressed and stretched them into something closer to a cobbler’s strap or a draughtsman’s French curve. The largest are four feet long, nearly three inches wide from rib-edge to rib-edge, and scarcely thicker than a stacked pair of thumbs. Their skin is fine-grained, matte, and poreless, colored in the exact grey-silver of weathered, sunlit hardwood on their dorsal side, and the damp, greenish-cream of inner pith along their bellies. They have four legs, tucked so deeply into lateral grooves along their flanks that they seem to skate on their ribs; only when one turns a sharp ninety-degree corner around a pilaster do you see the wide, splayed, hookless toes reach out, test the grain, and pull the ribbon of the body around the bend without losing contact with the wall for a microsecond.

Everything here is shaped by the tyranny of the boundary layer. Out beyond thirty-six inches from the masonry, the standing waves will pulp a soft body or tear a stiff one apart at the joints. Consequently, these creatures—call them *lath-skinks*, though their skulls are too broad and strangely hinged for any ordinary lizard—have surrendered the third dimension entirely. Their eyes sit nearly flush with the top of a wedge-shaped head, lidded with transparent scales thick enough to keep their corneas from vibrating into blindness. They have no external ear openings; if they had eardrums, the town would burst them at birth. Instead, a row of silvery,fluid-filled lateral pits runs from snout to tail-tip along both edges of their flattened bodies. They do not hear the roar as sound. They read it as topography. To a lath-skink, the air three feet to its left is not empty space; it is a sheer cliff of vibrating granite, pitted with shifting caverns of silence and treacherous overhangs of overpressure.

Watch one hunting along the third-story cornice of the west wall. 

It does not chase the terracotta crustaceans or snap at the mica-winged wasps. Let the forearm-geckos and the yellow-throated jumpers fight over street meat. The lath-skink slides up beside a blind dormer window that was not there yesterday—a fresh extrusion of pale green wood-flesh still stiffening in the afternoon heat—and presses its flat throat against the sill. For twenty seconds it lies motionless, feeling the deep, complicated rumble of the hollow tenement behind the facade. 

Inside the papery pith-walls live the architects’ parasites: blind, thumb-thick grubs with mandibles like router bits, boring through the struts, drinking sap from the pulsing vascular mains, and warbling at a fierce, high C-sharp that contributes the shrillest layer to the plaza’s din. A heavy infestation weakens a wall; worse, from the plant’s blind vegetative perspective, their chewing alters the resonant pitch of the hollow chambers, throwing off the acoustic geometry that props up half the overhanging gables.

The lath-skink shifts half an inch to the left, brings its blunt, heavy snout perpendicular to the bark, and begins to hum.

It has no vocal cords. What it has is a reinforced, ossified ribcage lined with dense muscular plates that can lock into resonance with the plaza’s own racket. By angling its flat back slightly outward—just half a thumb-width into the ragged edge of the acoustic wall—it catches a tiny fraction of the immense mechanical energy battering the square and channels it straight down through its skeleton into its chin. It becomes a living chisel driven by the town’s own noise. A focused, phase-inverted thrum passes from its lower jaw into the wood. Two inches behind the facade, inside the dark gallery, three boring grubs are instantly liquefied inside their own cuticles by the localized spike of vibration. 

The skink waits ten seconds. Presently, the porous wood of the windowsill flushes dark, sweet-smelling amber as the dead grubs rupture and soak into the thirsty grain, mixing with leaking sap. Only then does the skink extrude a broad, velvety tongue like a damp chamois cloth, wiping the enriched sweat from the wood in neat, overlapping strokes before gliding onward. The plant gets lanced of its rot; the skink gets a warm broth; the wall stays in tune.

That would be grotesque and clever enough on its own, if it were all they did. Wait longer, though, down in the shaded lower arcades where the stairs loop back under themselves and the doorways cheat across the square. 

Because the town’s space is knotted, territory means nothing normal here. A hunting lizard that claims ten square yards of wall might actually be patrolling four disconnected balconies on three different sides of the plaza, linked by blink-delayed archways and impossible corners. Most of the town’s inhabitants navigate this by pure reflex and rigid lane-discipline; the terracotta crustaceans follow chemical trails laid down generations ago, marching calmly upside down under root-viaducts and emerging two stories lower without the faintest neurological equipment required to notice that they never walked down a hill. When a sudden growth spurt seals a doorway or extrudes a useless, ornamental balustrade across an ancient highway, the crustaceans pile up in clattering, waist-deep drifts of terracotta shell until geckos and wasps strip the jam clear, or until a fresh path happens to open beside it.

The lath-skinks do not pile up. They *garden* the renovations.

You will see three or four of them gathered at the base of a fresh green shoot where the plant is furiously trying to grow a row of blind, hinged-shut doors across a major drainage gutter. The plant grows where it is irritated; friction and vibration tell its cambium to lay down callous-wood, moldings, steps, curbs. Left alone, the town would choke its own alleys with dense tumors of civic ornament inside a month. 

The skinks arrange themselves around the swelling green bud in a bracket—one above, two flanking, a fourth curled neatly along the lip of the gutter below. None of them touch each other, yet their rib-plates twitch in exact, shared time with the twice-a-minute sap pulse. Together, they angle their flat silver backs slightly away from the wall, catching the edge of the plaza’s standing roar like four trim-tabs on an aircraft wing. 

They bend the local noise. 

By bouncing thin, focused ribbons of high pressure onto specific millimeters of the growing shoot while shielding adjacent patches in the acoustic shadow of their own bodies, they trick the plant’s mechanoreceptors. Where they focus the battering air, the tender green wood-flesh hardens prematurely into silvered lignum, stopping outward growth; where they cast a pocket of quiet, the cambium swells rapidly outward toward the relief. Over the course of three warm afternoon hours, shifting their bodies by hair-breadths every few minutes as the sun moves across the open sky above, the four flat creatures coax what would have been a fatal blockage into a clean, vaulted culvert with a lipped kerbstone that carries the next vascular downpour neatly over the crustacean highway and dumps it into a stairwell shaft.

Did they intend a culvert? Look into the flat, glassy-scaled eye of one as it slides past your boot-sole—assuming you have pressed yourself thin enough against a recessed doorway to survive standing there—and there is no civic planner looking back at you. There is no mammal warmth, no tool-maker’s calculation. There is only an ancient, cold-blooded intolerability toward turbulence. Bad angles in the wood make ragged eddies in the sound; ragged eddies scrape unpleasantly against their sensitive lateral pits and ruin the clean resonances they need to liquefy grubs behind the lath. Smooth arches, plumb lintels, evenly spaced balusters, and right-angled corners keep the standing waves crisp, locked cleanly out in the center of the court where they belong. Over centuries, generation after generation of irritation-avoiding reptiles trimming the growing buds for acoustic comfort has forced a chaotic jungle parasite to grow into a polite, slightly mad provincial municipality. The town looks human-built only because rectangular doors, colonnades, and stepped gables happen to be the exact geometry required to keep a seventy-pace wooden box from shaking itself to splinters when filled with a hundred and forty decibels of continuous insect noise.

Still, they are not the whole story of the town. Anything that builds clean infrastructure eventually invites squatters who use it sideways.

Look out—carefully, squinting against the oily, rainbow-edged shimmer of the bent light—into the uninhabitable void of the central court itself. Twenty-five feet up, suspended in empty sunny air midway between the leaning eastern gable and the weeping western colonnade, hangs one of those spinning clouds of shredded beetle-husks and powdered dry leaf, trapped at a nodal intersection where six invisible walls of sound slam together and cancel out into a furious, fist-sized whirlpool of calm.

Every few minutes, something flickers from one dust-knot to the next.

You cannot see it in transit, because nothing can linger in the high-pressure slabs between the nodes; anything crossing from pocket to pocket has to drop on a ballistic arc timed to the millisecond between phase-beats, or be torn to mist. What lives out there in the clear daylight has no business being related to anything on the walls, yet clearly it once was, very long ago, before a clutch of eggs got blown off a fourth-story ledge and lodged in a dead pocket instead of smashing on the spotless floor below.

They are no bigger than grass-seeds when folded, and no heavier than wood-ash—colonial, spider-limbed things spun entirely out of flexible, translucent horn and taut membrane, like miniature box-kites crossed with harvestmen. Locally, they have no weight at all; gravity is a negligible rumor compared to the acoustic grid they inhabit. A dozen of them share a single suspended dust-ball, clinging to the spinning crumbs of pulverized chitin in the calm eye of the node, filtering airborne organic flour and misted sap-droplets out of the vortex with feathery forelegs. 

They never touch wood. Wood, to them, is the dark, wet, predator-crawling abyss at the edges of the world. Their entire universe consists of roughly four hundred invisible, spherical islands of dead air arranged in a three-dimensional lattice hanging above the empty court, shifting slightly upward and eastward as the afternoon air warms, contracting downward when a cloud crosses the mild sky overhead and cools the plaza by two degrees. 

When a node begins to destabilize—say, because four lath-skinks over on the north arcade just finished curving a new downspout and subtly altered the echo off the opposite facade—the tiny kite-spiders feel the walls of their three-inch sanctuary begin to shudder inward. All twelve draw their long, glassy legs tight against their bodies, unfurl a single triangular vane of iridescent gut-skin, and thrust themselves straight into the screaming wall of air at the exact instant two bass chords from the upper bladder-pods interfere. 

*Snap.* 

They do not fly; they are shot like watermelon seeds squeezed between giant invisible fingers, ricocheting off three successive pressure-ridges across fifteen feet of blinding emptiness in less than a twentieth of a second, snapping their braking-membranes open just in time to catch the calm center of a fresh node forty feet above the floor. Occasionally one misses the beat by a fraction of a millisecond. There is a tiny, dry *tick* lost completely inside the roar, a microscopic puff of pale dust, and the spotless wooden paving forty feet below remains spotless, because even the pieces are swept back up into the vortexes to feed its siblings.

As the plain afternoon sun finally slides past the western cornice and real shadow fills the hollow from the bottom up, the town’s metabolism shifts gears.

The eastern face cools; the great vascular bundles beneath the arches slow their pumping from twice a minute to once every three. Down in the alleys, the indoor rainstops, leaving the worn hollows of the steps dry and chalky. Up along the fourth-story rims, the gas-bladders lose turgor, dropping their brassy sustained chords down an octave into a loose, rattling bassoon-groan before wheezing shut altogether—except they never quite shut, because if the sound ever failed completely, the leaning three-story gable on the eastern wall, deprived of the dense knot of acoustic pressure propping up its overhang, would groan, split its amber scar-resin, and crash forty feet down into the square.

The town knows this without knowing anything. As the daytime wasps pack into their hollow window-slits and the yellow-throated jumpers tuck themselves under dry eaves, reducing the general din to a dangerous, structural minimum, the night shift emerges from the blind doorways—those eerie portals that connect opposite walls without crossing the middle.

Out come the stridulators: heavy, barrel-chested Orthoptera the size of cobbles, plated in resonant mahogany shell that matches the nighttime shadow-wood, marching out onto the rounded balustrades of every balcony on all four sides at once. They lock their spiked tarsals into worn grooves notched into the railings by ten thousand ancestors who stood on those exact spots before them. Raising crooked, saw-toothed hind legs, ten thousand nocturnal engines strike up a single, sawing, phase-locked drone—ugly, steady, industrial, and immense. 

In the dimming light, the shivering grid of air in the center of the court snaps back to full tension just as the eastern gable gives a tiny, settling creak and comes to rest once more against nothing at all. Along the cooling lintels, the flat silver lath-skinks uncurl from afternoon crevices, slide smoothly through an archway on the south wall, emerge instantly on the north balcony three stories higher, and press their chins to the vibrating rail, checking the pitch of the night’s scaffolding while above them, seventy paces wide by forty paces long, the first pale stars appear in a quiet, ordinary sky that has nothing to do with any of it.
