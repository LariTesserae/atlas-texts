If you stand beside that shaded landing long enough after the bulkhead latches shut, another will eventually cycle open down the tier, and one of the city’s builders will step out into the heat.

At first glance, encumbered in street-rig, they look like deep-sea divers forced to climb a boiler-house. They are short—rarely clearing five feet—and built with the dense, compact economy of creatures whose ancestors never had to outrun anything on flat ground, only outlast cold and gravity. Out in the alleys they wear heavy aprons of vulcanized mineral-felt over loose, quilted trousers, and boots with three-inch soles of stacked ceramic plates linked by brass hinges, clicking softly like wooden clogs whenever they shift weight on the slag paving. Over their faces sit bulbous masks of spun copper and mica, connected by twin corrugated hoses to a curved brass lung-pack slung across the lower back. The pack is not an oxygen tank in the ordinary sense; it is a chemical scrubber and pressure-bellows packed with damp, grey peroxide-salts that hiss faintly with every fourth breath, enriching the thin outer air and cooling it just enough so it does not sear the throat.

To watch one walk down a stepped alley is to understand why the cable-cars move so slowly. Nobody hurries at twenty-four thousand feet. Even with the breather-packs, rapid exertion in this atmosphere is a fool’s game—a quick way to bring on the silver-blindness and a pounding behind the eyes that takes two days in a pressurized bunk to clear. So they move with the exact, unhurried cadence of the thermal pistons around them: a steady, rolling, flat-footed gait, knees slightly bent, never taking two steps up a ladder without pausing on the third rung with a gloved hand resting lightly on a warm flange. What looks from a distance like stupor is simply supreme economy. There is not a wasted twitch in them. When a pair of pipe-walkers passes each other on a narrow grating above the pan-works, they do not shout through the thin air—shouting carries barely ten feet and wastes precious moisture—nor do they stop and bow. Their gauntleted hands rise briefly to their chests and flick through three or four crisp, notched positions, fingers snapping into angles like semaphore blades: *Line four holding; creep-rate up an eighth on the east pier; salt-crust under the third return.* Then they pass on without breaking their slow, metronomic stride.

You only see what they actually look like when you follow one back through the double-doored airlock of a residential stack or a guild-ward.

Inside, the world changes at the turn of an iron dog-wheel. The heavy outer door thuds home against greased felt gaskets; a pull-chain vents a thick, cool sigh of dense air from an overhead tank until your ears pop twice; and the inner hatch swings wide into a cramped, softly lit warren that smells overwhelmingly of lanolin, pickled fungus, damp clay, and warm bodies.

Here, stripped of the copper masks and ceramic-soled boots, the citizens are broader than they are tall, with immense, barrel-vaulted ribcages that taper sharply to narrow waists and short, bowed legs. Their skin has the close-grained, matte texture of oiled walnut or dark soapstone, shot through over the cheekbones, knuckles, and collarbones with faint, bluish-purple traceries where vast networks of surface capillaries lie close to the air. Even in the artificial two-thirds-sea-level pressure of the interior wards, their hearts beat only thirty times a minute, pushing blood so thick and iron-rich that a scratched knuckle beads nearly black before clotting almost instantly. Their eyes are large, dark-irised with pale amber rims, protected against the brutal ultraviolet glare of the saddle by heavy, double-folded lids and thick fringes of coarse black lashes that grow in two overlapping rows. Almost all of them, male and female alike, keep their thick, wiry hair cropped close to the skull or braided into tight, greased cords tucked behind small, flat ears—anything loose gets fouled in mask-straps or wicks sweat away too quickly when out on the catwalks.

Their hands tell the whole story of the city. They have five fingers, like lowlanders, yet the thumb is set farther round the palm and is nearly as thick as a big toe, built for locking onto brass valve-wheels and torquing iron spanners without slipping. Beneath their thick, horn-ridged nails, a permanent grey-green crescent of metallic dust—antimony, copper carbonate, plumbago grease—has worked so deeply into the quick that even newborn infants seem to acquire it within a month just from clutching their mothers’ knuckles.

Inside the ceramic-brick towers, life is vertical, dim, and intensely communal, arranged less like streets of houses and more like the compartments of a submarine standing on end. Floor space is too precious to waste on wide corridors or private parlors; every square yard of footprint must sit atop those massive, adjustable iron leveling-shoes down in the clefts of the rock. Rooms are octagonal or circular—shapes that hold pressure best when a shifting crag twists a building’s frame half a degree out of plumb—with curved walls lined inglazed cream and ochre tiles that bounce the pale yellow light of mineral-oil wicks and zinc-acid glow-jars into every corner. Instead of chairs, people lounge on stepped tiers of woven wire-mesh hammocks and bolster-shelves built right into the bulkheads, arranged by subtle gradients of temperature. A cold-water return pipe running along the floorboards keeps the lowest bunks crisp for sleeping; six feet up near the vaulted ceiling, a jacketed brine-conduit radiates steady oven-warmth where grandmothers sit wrapping coil-armatures or shelling brine-beetles into lap-bowls.

Nothing in these rooms is made of wood. Wood is a substance out of fairy tales and lowland trade-ledgers, brought up the lower cliffs once a year via five-stage freight-hoist at ruinous expense, hoarded in tiny cedar splinters inside brass lockets just for the miraculous, resinous smell of it, or shaved thin to make reeds for small, quiet chamber-pipes. Everything mundane—spoons, bed-frames, combs, gaming tiles, marriage-chests—is cast slag, spun aluminum, glazed stoneware, felted mineral-wool, or salvaged horn from the pewter-shelled crag-tortoises.

Because there is no soil, their agriculture happens in the dark, warm bellies of the residential blocks, three tiers down where the masonry butts closest against the radiant basalt walls of the trough. If you climb down the spiral iron rungs into the cellar-wards, the air turns thick and bready, warm as blood. Here sit hundreds of revolving stoneware drums and long, tiered troughs filled with crushed porous slag irrigated by cooled, de-sulfured condensate and enriched with every ounce of human night-soil, scrubbed ammonia from the breather-packs, and mineral tailings from the pan-works. Nothing with leaves grows here. Instead, the troughs are packed with dense, meaty cushions of domesticated blind-crops: fat, ivory-colored tubers bred from deep-cave sulfur-fungi that taste faintly of chestnuts and smoked marrow; tangled mats of velvety maroon rock-kelp that thrive in warm magnesium brine; and trays of heavy, blind mud-snails the size of clenched fists that feed on bacterial mats in the settling tanks. Once a week, a family stews these together in sealed cast-iron pressure-crocks that cook without losing a drop of steam, seasoned with sharp, citrusy crystals of purified alum-salt and pungent fermented oil pressed from yeast-vats.

It is a quiet society indoors as well as out, though not a grim one. Living twenty people to a three-room pressure-cell breeds an intricate, low-voiced courtesy. Loud voices rattle the ceramic partitions; sudden movements jostle someone else’s elbow over hot solder or precious soup. So their spoken tongue has worn down over centuries into a soft, rhythmic murmur full of liquid consonants, clicks of the tongue against the molars, and long, humming vowels that carry effortlessly through iron bulkheads without waking a shift-sleeper three feet away. Arguments are conducted in fierce, rapid whispers accompanied by sharp, angular flicks of those expressive hands—a furious husband and wife can have a blistering row across a kettle of yeast-broth while looking, to an outsider, like two card-sharps calmly settling a complex wager.

What truly occupies their minds—their religion, their civic pride, their gossip, and their art—is the Book of Creep, and the endless, four-dimensional chess game of keeping five hundred thousand tons of cast iron balanced on the back of a growing beast.

They do not think of the mountain as solid ground; they speak of it in the grammar reserved for living livestock or rolling seas. Every child of seven knows how to read a plumb-wire and a spirit-level calibrated in hundredths of a degree. Hanging in the center of every ward’s common-room, suspended on a hair-thin wire of drawn platinum from the top of the tower down through small brass grommets in every floor to the cellar below, is a heavy lead bob submerged in a clear glass bowl of pale oil. Etched onto the bottom of the bowl is a spiderweb of concentric rings and compass-marks. Every morning before eating, the elders of the stack gather round the glass bowl with small brass calipers, checking how far the needle on the bottom of the bob has drifted overnight as the gabbro ridge beneath Shoe Number Four heaved upward by half a millimeter while Shoe Number Seven settled into a cooling pocket.

Out of this necessity has grown their strangest caste: the Jack-Wrights, or Under-Walkers.

You rarely see them on the upper catwalks in daylight; they work down at the bottom of the dark, scorching slots where the iron stilts of the city meet the bare, razor-edged black stone. Down there, fifty or eighty feet below the paved alleys, the heat off the fresh trachyte reaches a hundred and thirty degrees, and the sulfurous breath of the fissures hangs in shimmering, oily curtains. The Under-Walkers wear full articulated suits of double-walled quilted asbestos-cloth and mirrored tin, cooled by flexible umbilical hoses that circulate chilled brine from the upper condensers around their torsos before dumping it onto the hissing stone. Moving in teams of four through the perpetual twilight beneath the buildings, carrying massive hydraulic worm-jacks and long listening-rods of solid brass, they press the flared bells of their rods against the stone and against their own jawbones, feeling through their teeth for the sub-audible *creak-creak-hiss* of deep basalt preparing to shear.

When a tower tilts even a fraction out of true, the Under-Walkers lock ratchet-collars onto the great threaded leveling-screws at its base—threads as thick as a man’s torso, smeared in black graphite paste—and hook them up to local torsion-cables bled off the nearest tectonic bridge. Using the mountain’s own outward heave to turn the gears against itself, they spend three weeks slowly cranking a ten-story tenement a quarter-inch skyward on its northeast corner until the platinum plumb-wires in twenty living rooms above drift silently back to dead center.

This relationship with slow, inexorable force colors everything they care about. Lowlanders down beneath the endless white cloud-sea—whom the saddle-folk call *Cloud-Bottomers* or *Rot-Walkers*, half in pity, half in mild disgust at people who live among mud, fast-spoiling weeds, and wildly gusting winds—measure wealth in land or gold. Up here, land splits in half on a Tuesday, and gold is merely useful bus-bar cladding that doesn't corrode in acid vapor. Real wealth on the saddle is measured in *Head* and *Span*: stored potential energy and refined strange-metals.

A wealthy guild-house does not show off with gardens or marble facades; it owns three forty-ton slag-blocks winched to the very top of a private drop-shaft, sitting there quietly on greased pawls, representing six months of guaranteed mechanical power should a fissure cool off or a ratchet-bridge seize. Dowries are paid in ingots of mirror-pure bismuth, jars of heavy mercury, lengths of seamless lead-lined tubing, and shares in pan-skimming tracks. Their jewelry consists of small, mesmerizing mechanical pendants worn outside their quilted vests: palm-sized brass astrolabes driven by bimetallic coils that wind themselves solely from the temperature difference between the wearer’s warm chest and the cool room air, turning tiny faceted crystals of blue copper-sulfate sealed under glass at one revolution per lunar month.

Out in the alleys, their dealings with the native creatures of the ridge are marked by the same unsentimental, symbiotic patience.

Nobody hunts the six-limbed pewter tortoises—called *fin-backs* or *clinker-hounds* in the ward-cant—and nobody shoos them off the copper bus-bars. In fact, pipe-tenders carry small brass scrapers hooked to their belts specifically to groom excessive alum-crusts off the hinges of an older fin-back’s shell when they pass one sleeping on a warm transformer housing. The creatures are living pyrometers and leak-finders. Their internal chemistry runs on metal-sulfide bacteria hosted in heavy, chambered guts, and they have an uncanny sensitivity to shifts in electrical current and subsurface heat. If three or four fin-backs spend two days laboriously hauling themselves off a perfectly warm stretch of eastern terrace to pile up in an awkward, clicking heap on a shaded iron stair-landing, the ward-wardens do not ask questions: they shut down the borehole pumps in that sector immediately and send Under-Walkers down into the clefts to bolt expansion-bellows across whatever unseen hairline fracture is about to vent superheated steam. When a fin-back finally dies—which takes upward of a century; there are individuals shuffling around the older smelter-courts with date-stamps and ward-glyphs cold-punched into their marginal scutes by great-grandfathers—its heavy, fluted metal-organic carapace is reverently unbolted into segments, polished to a dull silver gleam, and built into the lintels of airlocks or the resonant bellies of low-pitched chamber-harps.

As for the pale, ribbon-flat lead-worms gliding along the damp undersides of the aqueducts, children out on their first Masked Apprenticeships learn to leave them strictly alone; without their constant grazing, sulfur-slime would pit through a lead pipe-sheath in five seasons. Only the immense, bone-sparred gliders circling miles overhead in the cobalt sky are treated with something approaching awe.

Because the saddle-folk bury nothing—there is nowhere to dig, and nothing rots cleanly in the sterile, mineral heat—their dead go back to the mountain’s engines and its sky. When an old lathe-master or chemist dies in the quiet of an octagonal room, the body is washed in warm, clean distillate, wrapped in unbleached mineral-linen, and carried up by silent gondola to the highest, starkest crags on the horn-peaks, five hundred feet above the topmost condenser roofs, where even the iron walkways give out onto raw, glassy trachyte blades too hot to touch barehanded. There, in open cradles of riveted iron strapwork overhanging the abyssal drop toward the cloud-sea, the body is laid out under the flat, dark-blue zenith. In that bone-dry, thin air under unfiltered sun, flesh neither putrefies nor attracts flies—there are no flies within vertical miles. Within weeks it mummifies cleanly into light, dry parchment. Then the great translucent gliders drop down out of the foundry thermals on ten-foot, motionless wings, folding around the iron cradles like silent sheets of smoked mica, using long, needle-fine beaks to pick clean the dried sinews and mineral-rich marrow that sustain their hollow, brittle spars. Whatever heavy bones remain by year’s end are gathered by gloved priests, ground in slow tectonic ball-mills into clean calcium ash, and stirred into the glaze-vats that coat the interior tiles of new tenement wards—so that every room in the city is lined, quite literally, with the vitrified, light-reflecting dust of the generations who leveled its shoes.

Toward sunset, if you linger on that high catwalk, you can catch the closest thing this place ever knows to rush hour.

The sun does not soften into lowland reds and purples; it drops crisp and blinding white right down to the dazzling horizon of the lower cloud-sea, then slices beneath that endless white floor like a hot coin dropped into milk. Instantaneously, the sharp black shadows between the towers leap upward and merge. Out in the thin air, without moisture-clouds overhead to hold the day’s warmth, the sky-facing roofs begin radiating heat straight out into space; within twenty minutes, iron handrails five stories up are cold enough to burn bare skin with frost, while ten feet below them in the narrow courts, the trapped vapor from the fissures grows thicker, turning into clinging, lukewarm fogbanks illuminated from within by dim amber portholes.

That sudden thermal snap is the city’s evening bell. All across the three-mile trough, thousands of bimetallic spines and salt-wax bricks begin their nightly contraction at once. For half an hour, the near-silence fills with a delicate, multitudinous music that carries twenty paces and no farther: a soft, percussive patter like dry rain on tin roofs as millions of rivets, cooling louvers, expansion-sleeves, and mercury trip-switches tick over into their night-gearing.

Along the overhead cables, two dozen pressurized gondolas slide slowly past each other through the warm alley-fogs, their round mica windows glowing soft yellow, silhouettes of broad-shouldered figures visible inside with masks already unlatched and hanging round their necks, hands flickering lazily in conversation after a twelve-hour shift at the skimming pans. Down on a grated walkway just beneath you, a lone Night-Watcher emerges from an airlock in a heavy quilted coat, breath-pack sighing *shhh-tick* every twenty seconds. Carrying a warm zinc glow-pot in one hand and a stick of white soapstone chalk in the other, she pauses beside a frosted copper conduit, squints closely at a dial whose needle moves once an hour, updates a column of neat, sixteenths-based fractions on a wet slate board, gently steps over the edge of a sleeping fin-back whose corrugated shell is steaming faintly in the cooling dusk, and continues slowly down the steps into the rising mist.
