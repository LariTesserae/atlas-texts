To see the largest things that live here, you have to stop looking for silhouettes and start looking for interruptions in the glare. 

A mile away, where sky and water fuse into a single blinding band of silver-white, three dark shapes stand spaced fifty yards apart. Because there is no intermediate brush to judge them against, they might be boulders left behind by a vanished glacier, or low, slumped termite mounds, until one of them takes two slow paces sideways and lowers its head into the mirror.

They are grazers, massive and barrel-bellied, built on the general plan of a buffalo or a hippopotamus, yet adapted to a pasture that has no grass and water too shallow to swim in. Up close, they stand five feet at the shoulder, slung low between thick, pillar-like legs that end not in neat, cleaving hooves, but in broad, four-toed pads splayed wide like dinner plates. Each toe is capped with a heavy, blunt nail worn flat on the bottom. When one lifts a foot from the submerged quartz sand, it makes no sucking mud-sound—there is no mud here—only a soft, wet *shhhk* of polished stone grains sliding off tough, calloused leather. It sets the foot down again with careful, unhurried deliberation. On wet gneiss slicked with olive biofilm, a slip means a shattered pelvis; nothing that hurries survives long enough on the shield to leave calves behind.

It is their hooves, combined with the dragging bellies of the ironstone reptiles, that have spent the last half-million years rubbing those quarter-inch grooves into the solid bedrock between the deeper basins. They walk those faint tracks in loose, single-file processions during the cooler hours of morning and dusk, following memories worn directly into the basement of the continent.

Everything about them is shielded against light from below. Most open-country animals are dark on top and pale underneath to break up their shadow, an old trick that fails completely when the ground shines brighter than the zenith. These beasts are reversed, or rather, armored underneath: their bellies, chests, throats, and the insides of their legs are slate-black, thick with melanin, and nearly hairless, tough enough to endure eight hours a day of UV radiation bouncing straight up off the water sheets. Their backs, by contrast, are a dusty, pale kaolin-grey that sheds the direct overhead sun, sparse-haired and crusty with dried white mineral rings where shallow pan-water has evaporated off their flanks. Around their eyes, heavy bony ridges jut out not merely above the brow, as in an eagle, but *beneath* the lower lid as well—thick shelves of dark, wrinkled skin and coarse lashes squinting upward to cut the upward knife-edge of the glare, leaving their dark, horizontally slit pupils peering through narrow, shaded slots.

Watch one feed, and you understand why its head hangs so strangely heavy on its short neck. It has no incisors at all. Instead, the upper and lower jaws terminate in wide, squared-off plates of dense horn, ten inches across, straight-edged as a carpenter’s plane. Lowering its muzzle into six inches of bath-warm water, the animal presses that flat bevel flush against the submerged gneiss and pushes forward with the slow, hydraulic weight of its shoulders. Beneath the surface, you can hear a dull, rasping *scritch-scritch* carried clearly through the water and stone. It is peeling up the leathery ochre and purple algal mats in thick, dripping ribbons, careful not to gouge the pure quartz grit underneath, though over decades even quartz dust gets in; an old bull’s molars are monumental blocks of folded enamel six inches deep in the jawbone, grinding down fraction by fraction until, in his thirtieth year, he runs out of tooth and quietly lies down on a dry whaleback for the storks and crabs.

While its mouth works underwater, its nostrils—set high on a muscular boss near the top of the snout, almost between the eyes—stay dry above the film, exhaling slow, warm sighs that send tiny, circular ripples expanding twenty yards across the windless pan before vanishing.

Because the landscape is leached to the bone of calcium, phosphorus, and salt, these grazers are haunted by a quiet, perpetual hunger for minerals that the rich algal mats alone cannot satisfy. This makes them patient, methodical scavengers of their own neighbors. As a cow scrapes a shallow basin, she does not step around the pale freshwater crabs; if one is slow, or newly molted and soft, the broad horn-plate scoops it seamlessly in with the velvet slime, and she crunches it down with meditative thoroughness, eyes half-closed against the brightness. Late in the dry weeks, when the water creeps back and leaves faint white salt-rings on the stone, small family groups of three or four will spend hours licking the bare quartzite with long, black, rasping tongues that sound like coarse sandpaper on wood. Around the solitary, flat-topped trees, you will sometimes see a bull standing motionless beneath the low branches where a heavy-billed stork nests, waiting with geological patience for a dropped fish bone, a bit of eggshell, or a splash of chalky guano to hit the rock so he can lick it clean. Even shed antlers or horns would be too expensive here; neither sex grows them. Disputes between bulls are settled by shoving contests chest-to-chest in shin-deep water, sending great, flashing sheets of spray leaping into the pale sky and booming across the flats like distant thunderclaps, until one yields a yard of polished groove and turns away.

Around their ankles, the smaller life treats them as moving islands of relief. Two or three stilts often walk directly inside the narrow ribbon of shade cast by a grazer’s barrel belly, snapping up the water beetles and wrist-thick lunged fishes flushed from the algal mats by the scraping muzzle. Speckled frogs ride occasionally on their caked, grey rumps, silent while in transit, immune for an hour to the reptiles waiting in the tepid pans.

And the reptiles wait everywhere, though there is an uneasy truce between them and any grazer past its second year. The broad-snouted, ironstone-colored crocodiles—not true river crocodiles, which are built for deep ambush and drowning lunges, but flatter, wider-ribbed cousins whose limbs can still push their heavy bellies clear of the rock when they choose to walk—cannot hide in four inches of transparent water. Deception here relies on stillness and boredom, not cover. They lie dry on the whalebacks until mid-afternoon drives their blood too close to lethal heat, then slide into the lukewarm pans and settle until only their nostrils and flat, periscopic eyes break the mirror, looking precisely like banded ripples in the underlying gneiss. A calf that wanders ten paces outside its mother's shadow may take an ironstone snout for a stepping-stone; there is a sudden, thrashing explosion of blinding white water, an immense slap of a broad tail against solid rock that echoes past three horizons, and within two minutes the pan is flat again, stained briefly cloud-pink before the tadpoles and crabs crowd in to clean the quartz grains spotless.

By two in the afternoon, when the water in the dark-bottomed saucers passes a hundred degrees and wisps of vapor curl off the ironstone bands, the open flats grow nearly too hot even for the scrapers. The waders fly off or stand one-legged atop the baked clay termite pinnacles where a faint breeze moves six feet above the mirror, panting with open bills. The frogs do not stop—nothing stops the metallic, pebble-rattling roar of the frogs—but the pitch shifts slightly as millions of tiny bodies retreat an inch deeper into wet fault-cracks and beneath the overhanging lips of the pillowy stromatolite domes.

Even the termites inside those six-foot clay spires are peculiar children of this drowned basement. With no dead wood to eat except when a lightning strike splits one of the solitary fault-line trees once a decade, they farm what the stone provides. At night, when the glare dies and the rock cools to the temperature of skin, narrow, cement-roofed galleries are extended across the dry whalebacks right down to the water’s high-tide line. Inside those dark tunnels, blind pale workers harvest dried crusts of black lichen, stranded ribbons of dead algae, and the papery husks of molted crab shells, carrying the crumbs back into the kiln-hard pinnacles to mulch their subterranean fungus gardens above the reach of any creeping flood.

And then there are the runners—the things you almost never see until dusk, because during the day they fold themselves away like pocketknives against the base of the termite mounds or under the solitary grey trees.

In a world without cover, where any vertical shape broadcasts its presence for three miles across acoustic water and mirror-stone, a hunter cannot stalk. It can only sprint across the open pan faster than sight can turn into footing, or wait for the dark. These are lean, jackal-sized carnivores, though stretched upward onto absurdly long, flat-boned shins like a cross between a maned wolf and a secretary bird. Their coats are a pale, shimmering quartz-silver flecked with rust—the exact visual static of wiry sedge clumps reflected in bright water. Their paws are wide and webbed right to the tips of curved, dull claws that click faintly on dry stone like dropped bone needles.

All through the blistering afternoon they sleep curled tight in the tiny patch of shade behind a clay pinnacle, sliding slowly around the compass of the spire from west to east as the sun moves, ears—huge, thin-veined dishes lined with white hair—laid flat to dump body heat without catching the sawing scream of the cicadas.

When the sun finally drops toward the flat, wavering horizon, the world undergoes ten minutes of unbearable brilliance. Both sky and land turn to molten copper; every one-inch sheet of water catches fire from beneath, turning the spaced storks, the barrel-bellied grazers, and the distant termite spires into jet-black cutouts floating in red-gold flame. Then the rim of the sun slips below the basement rock, and the bottom falls out of the light.

Night does not bring silence—if anything, freed from the sun, the frogs swell their chorus until the air itself seems to vibrate against your eardrums, joined now by deep, resonant *booms* from larger, nocturnal bullfrogs sitting out on the warm stromatolite pillows, sounding like wooden mallets striking hollow casks. Above, stars appear not only overhead, but scattered smoothly between your feet in ten thousand dark, breathless pools, so that walking across the flats feels like stepping out onto thin glass suspended midway through space.

Now the long-legged runners get up, shake the kaolin dust from their silver flanks, and trot out into the starry water. They hunt entirely by sound and vibration, heads cocked crookedly toward the surface, listening beneath the frog-wall for the distinct, heavy *slosh-scrape* of a wrist-thick lungfish hauling itself across a damp rock threshold between basins, or the brittle *skitter* of a large crab straying too far onto a dry whaleback, or a roosting ibis shifting its weight in two inches of water. When they strike, there is a brief, frantic drumming of webbed paws kicking up arcs of warm, star-shattered spray—a sharp squawk cut short—and then just the wet crunch of small bones muffled under the unending wire-drone of the pan.

Far out across the dark mirror, the great grazers do not lie down on the dry whalebacks to sleep; stone cools faster than water after midnight, and dry rock leaves them stiff and exposed. Instead, small herds gather in the deepest permanent saucers—twelve to fourteen inches of water that holds the afternoon's stored warmth right through until dawn—and fold their thick, pad-footed legs beneath them. Lying belly-down in the warm bath, half-submerged like dark islands, their pale grey backs blend into the starlight while they chew a slow, mineral-gritty cud of fermented purple algae. Every few minutes, one lets out a long, wet breath across the black water, and three miles away, where no hill has stood for two hundred million years to block the sound, another bull hears it through the singing of ten million frogs, shifts his weight in his own shallow saucer, and sighs back.
