If you wait near that sulfur-smeared shelf—bracing your knees against the bucking porphyry and breathing shallowly through your teeth to keep the airborne mica out of your throat—you will likely feel them before you pick them out of the glare. 

A sharp, rhythmic *thock-thock-thock* travels up through the soles of your boots, distinct from the erratic cannon-cracks of bursting wood. A moment later, fifty yards up-canopy, a massive, overarching bough of silicate-wood shudders, drops six inches, and splits cleanly along a pre-scored seam, dumping a ton of thrashing, electrostatic spider-webbing into the lower mulch instead of onto an unseen trail.

Then the dust clears, and four of them drop down onto the newly opened span, moving with the loose, shock-absorbing gait of sailors crossing a pitching deck in a gale.

They are human in proportion, or near enough that the eye reaches for the word before correcting itself. They are lean to the point of looking kiln-dried—all ropey tendon, flat muscle, and wide, flared joints built to distribute sudden, violent shear forces. There isn't an ounce of soft fat on them, nor a drop of sweat; in an atmosphere this thirsty, sweating would kill an animal in twenty minutes. Instead, they radiate excess body heat through broad, cartilaginous crests behind their ears and wide, fluted collarbones flushed dark violet with dry-running blood, fluttering subtly like gills as they pant hot, dusty air.

Up close, you realize they have no hair at all—hair in this static-charged furnace would stand straight out, snarl with sharp chaff, and draw stinging arcs of dry lightning from every passing frond. Their skin is deeply corrugated and matte-gray, heavily calloused with overlapping plates of keratin along the shins, forearms, and shoulders, shedding continuously in fine, papery curls just like the forest around them. Over their eyes, they have two sets of lids: heavy outer folds crusted with protective horn-dust, and rapid, translucent inner membranes that flick sideways five or six times a second, squeegeeing away flecks of volcanic glass and ash without interrupting their sight.

Because the shadowless amber glare washes out depth perception, they navigate half by touch and vibration. Their bare feet are extraordinary—wide as paddles, with long, prehensile toes tipped in thick, hooked nails like horn chisels, gripping the vibrating bark to read the tension building inside a limb minutes before it blows. 

They do not speak. Nothing with lungs could shout loud enough to carry five paces through that roaring wall of friction without rupturing its own throat, and their ears aren't even open holes anymore—just taut, leathery tympanums flush with the skull, protected by bony ridges that shutter tight when a nearby trunk detonates.

Instead, they talk through the wood, and through copper, and through skin.

Watch the lead figure—an older female, judging by the scarred, silvered density of her shoulder-plates and the heavy belt of hammered copper wedges slung diagonally across her chest. She crouches, presses the butt of her horn-handled maul firmly against the trembling bough they are all standing on, and strikes the shaft twice with a brass knuckle-ring. Forty feet back, the youngest of the group—lanky, still pale-plated, carrying a wicker-and-carapace basket full of harvested pith-grubs—instantly lifts a foot, feels the double-tap race through the dry timber at three thousand meters a second, and pivots left before a surging column of velvet-husked grazers can barrel down the adjacent branch.

When they cluster together on the porphyry shelf, inspecting the cracking rock where the sulfur tallies are already pulling apart, their hands never stop moving against one another. They conduct whole conversations by palm-slapping forearms, hooking fingers, and rattling bundles of hollow bone-chimes at one another's collarbones—a rapid, tactile shorthand born of living inside an endless explosion. 

You can see the strange, unsentimental briskness of their minds in how they react to the ruined tally-mark. There is no frustration that the stone has split their map. In this basin, memory cannot be stored in places; there *are* no places, only trajectories. Those yellow sulfur geometries aren't property lines or monuments—they are timestamps and vector-notes left for trailing kin. *Three bands passed here moving upslope; the fault-heat beneath us is rising; this ridge will blow into tall timber before the next sleep-cycle.* The older woman simply scrapes a fresh cake of yellow mineral from a pouch at her hip, spits a thick, waxy bead of oil onto the hot rock, smears two quick parallel slashes across the newly opened crack to update the rate of spread, and moves on.

Everything they wear and carry is greased. Where moisture doesn't exist, oil is water, mortar, and medicine all at once. Rendered from the fat-bladders of high-canopy gliders and stored in cured gut-tubes coiled around their waists, that heavy, amber grease is worked constantly into their leather harnesses, smeared over their bone swivels so friction won't char them, and rubbed into the creases of their elbows and necks to keep their tough hides from splitting like dry timber. Even from ten yards away, cutting right through the ozone and hot-flint stink of the forest, they smell overwhelmingly of warm tallow, crushed sulfur, and scorched chitin.

And they are *gardeners*—though gardening here looks like demolition.

Half of their waking life is spent fighting the speed of the world, turning the forest’s manic violence against itself. Watch two of them harvest dinner from a newly erupted stand of telescoping bone-stalks. They don't chop a stalk down; chopping takes too long, and the tensioned wood would whip back and take a head off. Instead, one hunter jams a copper wedge into a hairline seam near the root-fault and gives it a single, ringing blow with an obsidian maul. Copper conducts heat and grounds piezoelectric charge; its sudden insertion disrupts the mineral-electric current feeding the stalk's frantic expansion on one side while the far side keeps ballooning. Within ninety seconds, the thirty-foot trunk bends into a groaning, catastrophic bow—and *snaps* itself apart mid-air, raining pale, warm, nutrient-dense marrow-chunks straight onto a stretched hide tarp held ready by two others below. Before the splintered base can re-root, they have scooped up four stone's weight of sweet, flaky pith, rolled the tarp tight, clipped it to a braided tendon-line, and vaulted clear as the first rust-winged scavengers dive-bomb the wreckage.

They eat on the move, chewing dry cakes of compressed pith, toasted spore-meal, and crushed beetle-meat bound together with glider-grease. Their jaws are heavy, squared at the hinge, housing broad, flat molars reinforced with dark iron-salts—teeth that grind down continuously against inevitable grit and grow continuously from the root, like a rodent’s or a horse’s. When one of them laughs—or makes their version of it, a sharp, visible shudder of the ribcage accompanied by a rapid clatter of knuckles against their breastbone—you glimpse a mouth lined in slate-gray enamel and a thick, dark, dry tongue like rough suede.

To understand who they really are, though, you have to follow them when exhaustion finally forces them to stop.

Living inside ninety-five decibels of unbroken roar does something terrible to a nervous system if it isn't rested, even one bred for it over a thousand generations. Every ten or twelve hours—there is no night here, only the rhythmic waxing and waning of tectonic swarms deep beneath the gabbro—a band has to make *quiet*.

They don't look for stable ground; stable ground is a trap waiting to fracture under your spine. They look for an intersection of three or four young, flexible canopy-arches high above the main stampede lanes of the hound-grazers, somewhere beneath a dense overhang of older, chaff-shedding fronds that breaks the worst of the thermal updrafts. 

Working with dizzying, practiced choreography, the four of them string long guy-lines of braided scale-worm gut between four separate, heaving trees, anchoring each end not with knots, which would bind and snap, but with greased bone-rings looped loosely around smooth, stripped boughs so the lines can slide twenty feet up or down as the trunks grow while they sleep. In the center of that four-way web, where the opposing pulls cancel one another out into a strange, floating equilibrium, they rig their pod.

They don't carry the whole shelter—only the swivels, the tension-rings, and the stitching-awls. The shell itself they build fresh in twenty minutes, stripping broad, tough sheets of parchment-bark, slathering them with sticky root-resin and ash to kill their triboluminescence, and lashing overlapped carapace-plates scavenged from dead filter-bats onto the windward side to form a curved acoustic baffle. 

When the last seam is pinned shut with horn-skewers and they haul themselves through the bottom sphincter-flap, pulling it tight behind them, the transformation inside is physical shock.

The blinding amber afternoon vanishes into deep, warm, maroon twilight. The tearing, jaw-rattling roar of the valley drops away behind five layers of greased hide, ash-paste, and curved chitin, damped down to a heavy, muffled surf—the sound of blood heard from inside a womb.

In there, suspended forty feet above a churning hell of self-cannibalizing timber, swinging gently in wide, ten-foot arcs as their anchor trees rocket toward the ceiling and collapse and re-sprout in the bright madness outside, they finally unspool.

They unclip their copper belts. They pry the caked dust from around their nostrils and ear-drums with small bone spoons. They open a precious pot of scented fat rendered from cave-grubs and oil one another's cracked knuckles and scraped shins in slow, deliberate strokes. Without the glare and the static, their flushed violet collarbones fade to cool slate; their fluttering inner eyelids finally still.

And here, in the dark, ten-foot bubble of artificial silence they have stolen from the turbine of the earth, you hear the one sound they save only for the pods. 

Pressing their foreheads and chests together in a tight, tangled heap so every breath passes straight from ribcage to ribcage, they hum. It is a low, sub-vocal thrumming, pitched well below the high, rasping crackle of the outside forest—a deep, resonant chord vibrated directly through skull-bone and sternum. In a world where nothing stays whole for an hour, where every path splits beneath the heel and every shelter must be cut loose by morning before its own anchor-lines strangle it, that shared, trembling bass-note in the dark is their architecture. It is their hearth, their lineage, and the only monument in the five-mile trough that doesn't crack.

Four hours later, one of the guy-lines outside hits the end of its greased travel as an anchor-bough splits, sending a sharp, unmistakable jerk through the central swivel.

Instantly, the humming stops. Four pairs of double-lidded eyes snap open in the maroon gloom. Within sixty seconds, the bone pins are pulled, the swivels and copper hardware are stripped back onto their belts, and the four of them drop cleanly out the bottom of the pod into the blinding, roaring amber light—already angling their hooked toes for the next rising branch, leaving the empty shell of bark and carapace behind to be crushed, shredded into sparkling chaff, and swallowed whole by the upward-rushing wood before the grease on its seams has even cooled.
