If you watch one of the amber-lit window niches long enough—staring past the frantic, bright threads of diving bubbles until your eyes adjust to the tea-colored gloom—you will notice that a section of the bricked-up embrasure is breathing. 

At first, it looks like nothing more than a patch of old lime scale or a heavy leather gasket blown loose from an upper flange and pinned flat against the masonry by the current. It is the exact color of boiled bone and tarnished bronze, mottled with the rusty stain of dissolved pipe-dope. It occupies the shallow recess of a second-story doorway, pressed so tightly into the rounded brick courses that the mortar grooves show through its back as faint, raised ribs. The roaring water shears straight across it without catching an edge. Like the teardrop ladder rungs and the melted bolt-heads, its profile has been surrendered entirely to hydrodynamics: thickest at the center—perhaps three inches deep—and tapering away at every margin into a tough, translucent skirt that seals flush against the stone.

Then an upstream boiler blows down. A plume of water ten degrees hotter than the surrounding spin sweeps across the east wall, and the slab shudders, ripples from margin to center, and peels half an inch of its leading edge off the brick to let the scalding surge wash underneath its belly.

There are seven of them in the shaft, though you will never see more than two at a time, and even then only if you know to look for missing shadows rather than shapes. Whatever riverine stock they came from—perhaps flathead bullheads sucked in as fry through a cracked harbor intake during the refit sixty years ago, or sucker-mouthed bottom-dwellers washed down from the municipal reservoirs—the centrifuge has hammered them into something altogether native to the lung. 

They have no swim bladders; an internal pocket of gas in this wildly fluctuating head-pressure would rupture or crush them in minutes, and buoyancy here is death. Anything that floats is dragged into the central column, spun down into the lower grates, and sheared apart against the iron lips of the intakes. Instead, they are dense as wet vulcanized rubber. Their skeletons have gone largely cartilaginous, softening to absorb the incessant, bone-shaking acoustic battering of the turbines forty yards away. If you could pry one off the brick and bring it into the air, it would collapse over your forearms like a heavy, scalding-warm saddlebag, nearly forty pounds of flat muscle and slick, scaleless hide, smelling sharply of ozone, hot copper, and distilled water.

On the underside—the hidden face pressed perpetually against the submerged tenement walls—the mouth has migrated backward from the snout, widening into a broad, muscular oval framed by concentric rings of horny, rasping plates. Around this mouth, the pectoral and pelvic fins have fused into a single continuous suction-girdle that runs nearly the whole circumference of the body. They do not swim. They *clamp*. Moving across the vertical courtyard requires the slow, methodical labor of a glacier: the creature anchors its forward rim, humps its dense spine a fraction of an inch to break the vacuum at its tail, draws its rear margin forward, and slams it back down before a rogue eddy can get a finger underneath it. When crossing a gap—moving from a window lintel onto the curved flank of a vertical manifold—it pours itself over the bronze cleats like heavy dough, never allowing more than a fifth of its underside to lose purchase at once.

Eyes have long since shrunk to pinpricks buried beneath thick, milky skin, useless in a world where nothing approaches from the empty middle except lethal debris, and where the dim green-glass cage lights never change. In their place, the lateral line system has hypertrophied into an intricate fretwork of pale, sunken canals crisscrossing their backs like the seams on old masonry. They do not see the chamber; they feel its precise hydraulic geometry. Every subtle shudder of a pressure-relief clacker, every micro-shift in the velocity of the three lower intakes, registers across their backs as a vivid, three-dimensional map of safe dead-zones and fatal shear-planes. Where the cavitation bubbles collapse near the floor with that endless sound of dumped gravel, the shockwaves travel up the walls and vibrate through the creatures’ cartilage; they avoid the lower ten feet of the shaft entirely, treating the shriek of boiling-cold vacuum bubbles the way terrestrial animals treat fire.

For twenty hours a day, the largest of them—an ancient, broad-backed female nearly four feet long, bearing a pale, smooth scar across her flank where a torn tie-rod struck her decades ago—remains locked over a warm brass thermometer housing on the third-story facade. She is not sleeping; sleep as a still-water creature knows it would mean loosening the seal of her skirt. Rather, she rests in a state of taut, humming torpor, letting the blood-warm water do the work of her metabolism while the mechanical thunder vibrates steadily through her ribs. 

When hunger stirs her, she slides sideways off the brass housing and inches toward the narrow dead-water alleys behind the armored electrical conduits. 

She is far too wide to fit into the one-inch gaps where the pale, crampon-legged crustaceans graze on bacterial grease. She doesn't need to. Positioning her tapered, muscular snout precisely at the upstream lip of the gap between cable and brick, she lifts just two inches of her heavy margin—turning her own body into a temporary baffle. Instantly, the screaming current deflects off her raised shoulder and knives straight into the quiet crevice behind the conduit. The sudden, localized jet blasts the velvety bacterial mat loose in pale ribbons and blows half a dozen clinging crustaceans tumbling out the downstream side—directly under the waiting vault of her arched belly. She drops her skirt flat, trapping the eddies beneath her, and her rasping underside-mouth works quietly in the dark, sorting the crunch of chitin and slick biofilm from stray grains of calcified mortar before spitting the grit out through low, valved gill-slits near her tail.

The creatures are solitary, ignoring one another unless two happen to converge on the same sun-warmed plume above a leaking sluice-seal. Then there is a slow, silent shoving match—two flat, ghost-colored skins wedging their tapered edges beneath one another, each trying to peel the other’s vacuum seal loose and hand its rival over to the spin. Neither ever pushes hard enough to actually send the other into the center; as soon as a corner lifts and the violent tug of the vortex grabs at the loser's flank, it yields, flattening instantly and sliding rapidly sideways down the brick courses toward an empty balcony sill.

Only one event alters their flattened, two-dimensional lives, and it is the same violent stutter that draws the solitary harbor eel out of its culvert.

Roughly twice a week, during peak municipal load shifts, the turbines drop out of phase with the harbor outfalls. Down at the flagstone floor, one intake chokes while another surges. The central centrifuge falters. For three seconds, the fifty-foot column of spinning water brakes against itself in a massive, silent-seeming concussion—the roar dropping an octave into a dull, chest-crushing thud as clockwise and counter-clockwise walls of water collide and cancel each other out. 

Along the tenement facades, every creature freezes, clamping down with maximum force as chaotic cross-currents rip at their margins from unpredictable angles. 

Except during one brief stretch of the year—nobody above knows which month, for the water is ninety-eight degrees in January and ninety-eight degrees in July, though perhaps some vestigial lunar pull or subtle chemical shift in the deep groundwater still reaches them. When that stutter hits at the right moment, two or three of the smaller, narrower males hanging near the fourth-story cornices do the unthinkable. 

The instant the spin dies and the middle of the room goes slack and turbulent, they let go.

They do not swim so much as fling themselves sideways off the old ceramic street-number plaques into the amber-streaked void, curling their heavy, flat bodies into tight, dense cones. For two heart-stopping seconds, they join the pale eel in the empty center of the shaft—tumbled together in the boiling foam dragged down from the compressed air-lens at the ceiling, colliding clumsily flank-to-flank in a frantic, rolling cloud of milt and eggs released straight into the brief, suspended zero-gravity of the stalled room. 

Then the intakes roar back into alignment. The centrifuge snaps shut like an iron trap. 

The eel whips back into its upper cornice. The heavy flat-bodies snap themselves open like parachutes, catch the outward centrifugal fling of the restarting vortex, and are hurled violently back against the brick facades two stories below where they started—slapping flat against window sills, sluice frames, and iron ladders with dull, fleshy *thwacks* lost entirely inside the thunder, locking their suction skirts down inches above the hungry pull of the drains.

Nearly everything released into that three-second calm is instantly vacuumed out through the upper harbor channels or pulverized into froth against the intake lips. Almost all of it. Here and there, a microscopic, sticky bead is flung outward before it is dragged down, driven deep into the porous, bread-loaf curves of a submerged brick or wedged behind a bronze cleat right at the edge of the bacterial slick. There, glued fast by proteins tough enough to resist scalding condenser run-off, vibrated day and night by the roar of forty thousand horsepower through solid bedrock, something no bigger than a grain of sand flattens itself against the masonry, holds tight, and begins to rasp.
