Notice the mammal jawbones hanging from the flint tripod. Notice the mud on the cairns. 

There are no mammals in this room, and there is no soil. Everything native to the pocket unspools into chalk when it dies, or lacks the third dimension entirely, or flickers out of the rock without leaving dust behind. Yet there is dirt here—true, dark, humic earth from the blind world forty feet overhead, chewed with saliva into little domes. And there is heavy, coarse hair braided into string, and three tiny mandibles with brown-tipped shrew teeth still locked in their sockets.

Whoever built the monuments is not gone. You are simply looking for a continuous body in a room that refuses to allow continuous distance.

Watch the stretched interval between the two boulders—the gap that a single stick bridges, yet takes twenty paces to cross. If you stare directly into the empty air three inches above the stick, the clinical glare begins to bend, as if passing through clear grease. A breath fogs the nothingness. The fog does not drift; it hangs in a tight, wet sphere for four seconds, then snaps sideways and vanishes. Two paces to the left, resting on a razor-sharp bevel of grey chert without being cut by it, sits the heel of a calloused, dirt-ingrained thumb. Nothing is attached to the thumb. It ends cleanly at the base joint, not in blood or bone, but in a flat, mirror-bright cross-section that slides smoothly through the air as the invisible hand turns over to test the dampness of the stone.

Call him the Surveyor, though he has not had a whole skull at one time in long enough that names have slipped out through the gaps.

Forty feet above, perhaps a year ago, perhaps ten minutes ago, something heavy collapsed—an old badger sett, a rotted taproot cavity, a sinkhole after hard rain—and dropped a living creature downward at the precise fraction of a second the pocket below tried to calculate its ceiling. The ceiling closed behind him in a flat sheet of blinding white vacancy, sealing the topsoil out. What arrived at the bottom was a man-shaped mammal, heavy with ordinary mud, carrying shrews in his pockets or burrs in his coat or simply the thick, greasy reality of the upper earth. 

The room could not digest him. He had too much history. Every cell in him had been rubbed by time, weathered, scarred, fed by rotted things. When the pocket’s broken geometry intersected him, it did not flatten him like the sea-slaters or turn him to chalk; instead, it scattered his occupancy across the unfinished grid.

He is all here, every ounce of him, distributed across the twenty-pace intervals and angled gravities like a reflection in a dropped mirror. 

Over near the pulsing liverwort, a ribcage and one shoulder lean comfortably against a pane of cold white light that hasn't become limestone yet. You can see the coarse, matted wool of an old coat—not woven cloth, which unraveled into unspun grease-locks the first week he walked through a folded corner, but raw felted fleece he has pressed back together with wet hands—rising and falling in a slow, patient breath. Ten feet away, down in the tilted niche where the multi-jointed amphibians run along the walls, two bare, sinewy calves and mud-caked feet stand planted at a thirty-degree slant, toes gripping a slick patch of slate until the wetness relocates upward, at which point the toes curl once against the sudden dry chill. His eyes are currently six feet above the flint tripod, drifting slowly across the ceiling glare without a head around them—just a pair of dark, wet, intensely watchful human eyes suspended in two coin-sized windows of normal shadow, blinking in unison, tracking a ribbon-hunter as it loops toward the seventeen arranged shells.

He feels entirely whole to himself. That is the strangest truth of his survival. When his knees bend in the tilted corner, his torso by the liverwort drops six inches closer to the floor. When the severed-looking thumb by the boulders presses too carelessly against a fresh blade of orthoclase and splits open, a bead of dark, shocking crimson wells up—the only true red in the entire bleached room—and five yards away, in a pocket of air beneath the gypsum overhang, a chapped mouth tightens and sucks breath silently through yellowed teeth, before reaching across three broken intervals to bring the bleeding thumb directly into his mouth across six yards of empty space.

He cannot die properly here because rot was left out of the room, and because time loops back on itself whenever the bark-ribbons pass overhead. Hunger comes over him in sharp, twenty-minute waves, peaks, and then resets when he walks backward through the stretched gap between the boulders, leaving his stomach full of whatever shrew or root he ate the hour before he fell, endlessly redigested and unspent. The shrew jawbones on the plumb lines are from the one dead thing that fell through the earth with him; he plucked its fur to spin the heavy cords, used its gut to lash the flint, and ate the meat once, which was enough for forever provided he keeps track of where the reset-lines sit. The mud of the cairns was scraped from his own boots and matted hair during his first days here, mixed with his own spit, hoarded like gold because once a crumb of upper-earth soil gets sliced by new feldspar or dropped into a white wall-pane, it forgets how to be dirt and hardens into sterile grey slate.

Look at how he moves when the new feldspar shelf clicks into existence through the middle of his mud cairn.

He does not startle. Panic requires noise and momentum, and this room smothers both. Slowly, methodically, thescattered pieces of him converge toward the edge of the stretched floor—not coming fully together, because the geometry there won't hold five and a half feet of continuous mammal at once, but crowding into adjacent facets like someone pressing their face and hands against separate panes of a greenhouse window. 

A forearm appears out of midair above the sliced cairn, covered in pale scars from a hundred invisible rock-edges he walked into before he mapped them. Behind the forearm, at a slight parallax angle so that it flickers in and out of an anhydrite boulder depending on where you stand, looms half a weathered face: heavy brow, beard grown long and then sheared short in jagged steps with a flake of black flint, skin bleached to the exact tallow-white of cellar sprouts by the ruthless, shadowless noon of the walls. 

His fingers—whole when viewed from the east wall, sliced into four hovering knuckles from the west—reach down to the feldspar shelf that just ruined his marker. A paper-thin hunter darts across his wrist. Where it crosses his skin, its chalky internal veins pulse frantic violet, stealing a microsecond of mammalian warmth before skittering off onto the sweating stone. He ignores it. With infinite care, he pinches the two separated halves of his mud-and-spittle cairn, testing whether they can be lifted off the new feldspar. 

The left half comes away damp and pliable in his palm. The right half, though touching the exact same crystal plane, refuses to budge; in the three inches to the right of the feldspar’s edge, gravity has locked sideways at ten times normal weight, pinning the thumb-sized lump of dirt to the rock as if it were bolted iron.

You can see the corner of his hovering mouth twitch upward—not quite a smile, more the dry satisfaction of a carpenter finding out *why* a door won't shut.

He does not speak, because early on he shouted until his throat tore, and the pressurized hush ate the scream an inch past his lips, turning a roar into the sound of two dry fingertips brushing together. He learned quickly that trying to force sound into the pocket only bruises the lungs. Instead, he communicates with himself through the monuments. Memory is unreliable when walking four feet to the left can undo the last three minutes of your nervous system's chemical state—just like the seventeenth carapace twitching back to life under the drifting ribbons. If he discovers a rule and walks through a time-eddy on his way back across the room, his brain turns clean and blank of the discovery, reset to three minutes prior, while his thumb is still bleeding from the lesson.

The room erases thought unless thought is made out of matter and placed where the ribbons don't swim.

Watch him work now. With his left hand—currently operating down near the floor three paces away—he selects a fresh razor-splinter of grey chert that snapped into reality twenty minutes ago. His right hand brings the rescued half-lump of mud over to the slab of seventeen shells. He rolls the precious dirt between gritty palms, spits once—catching the hovering bead of saliva before it can drift sideways into a mist-ribbon—and molds a tiny, crescent-shaped ridge onto the base of the stick that casts the backward shadow. 

The backward shadow changes immediately. The dark, razor-sharp line on the pale floor splits at the tip, forking like a snake's tongue, pointing directly at the new feldspar shelf and the pinned lump of mud. 

He squints with his high, detached eyes, checking the angle of the forked shadow against the three shrew-jaw plumb lines hanging from the flint lintel. One of the jawbones—the leftmost one, which points forty degrees toward the east wall—shudders slightly on its hair string, swinging half a millimeter further east to account for the mass of the newly born feldspar.

He has figured out what the pocket is doing. Or at least, he is closer than anything else down here.

The grazers, the brittle-star ambushers, the multi-jointed jumpers—they are rough drafts, sketched onto the two-dimensional panes and half-finished angles by an earth trying to remember how life works without consulting the surface above. They live fast, silent, frantic cycles because they have no weight in them. He is the single heavy foreign grain caught inside the pearl-sac, irritating the room, forcing it to calculate shadows and three-way gravity around his stubborn, three-dimensional meat. Every time he builds a cairn or balances three flints, the white panes nearby curdle into solid stone a little faster, trying to wall around the contradiction he has pointed out. Half the chert ledges on the west side only snapped into solid rock because he hung hair-lines in front of the glare until the vacancy couldn't bear being measured anymore and collapsed into slate.

He is trying to finish the room. Not to escape it—he knows forty feet of blind clay sits above that hard, radiant ceiling, and if the ceiling ever decides to become ordinary dirt while he is standing beneath it, it won't open a tunnel; it will simply drop forty tons of pasture onto his head. No: he is trying to make the courtyard coherent enough that he can pull himself back into a single piece in the middle of the floor, sit down on one boulder without his spine leaning against another ten paces away, and sleep without a drifting bark-ribbon resetting his dreams every ninety seconds.

A broad sheet of cold sweat materializes all at once across the felted wool over his ribs. Before it can detach and float sideways, a pale brittle-star beneath the gypsum overhang senses the sudden patch of moisture—which to its blind radial nerves reads just like wet stone—and flings a sticky white feeding thread into empty air three inches to its left.

The thread emerges out of nothing right beside the Surveyor’s floating ear.

Without haste, without a whisper of sound, a disembodied rough hand intercepts the thread two inches from his skin, pinches the sticky line between calloused thumb and forefinger, and gives it a sharp, practiced tug. Across the courtyard, yanked backward through its own folded shortcut, the boneless predator tumbles out of its gypsum crevice and slaps onto the dry chert floor with that tiny, pillowed *thup*. 

Before it can unspool or scramble back into tilted gravity, the Surveyor steps—legs in the corner striding once, hands in the center dropping low—and sets a flat plate of soapy anhydrite neatly over three of its arms, pinning it harmlessly astride a seam where two rocks occupy the same space. Now, as the two rocks flicker back and forth depending on the angle of view, the trapped brittle-star flickers with them: solid, gone, solid, gone, held as a living metronome so he can time how fast the east wall is hardening compared to the three-minute pulse of the green liverwort.

He wipes the chalky slime of the feeding thread onto his felted coat, retrieves his precious crumb of upper-world mud from the base of the shadow-stick so no grazing slater can comb it away, and tucks the dirt safely inside his own cheek—the one place in the chamber where distance never stretches and moisture stays warm. 

Then the pieces of him settle back into stillness around the courtyard: feet braced on the tilted wall, back resting against the blinding cold glass of unborn stone, two dark human eyes hanging quiet and sleepless in the clinical glare near the ceiling, watching the white gaps, waiting for the soft *tick* of the next piece of the world arriving wrong so he can go over and measure it.
