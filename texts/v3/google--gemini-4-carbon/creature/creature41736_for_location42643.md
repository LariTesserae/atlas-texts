If you stand on the catwalk of the center drum and let your eyes unfocus against the glare of the LEDs, you begin to notice that the water does not sheet across the concrete evenly. Near the southern expansion joint, forty yards out from the rock wall, the inch-deep flow parts around an obstacle that isn't there, forming a long, braided wake that catches the sodium light like spun brass.

Wait long enough—longer than the technicians ever stay, longer than it takes for sweat to soak through a heavy shirt—and the obstacle lifts its head. 

At first, it looks like a discarded bundle of grey lagging, or a slab of cement slurry spilled during the pour that somehow grew ribs. Then an amber light catches the wet curve of an eye, set high up on a flat, shovel-shaped skull, blinking away distilled condensate with a slow, milky membrane.

There are eleven of them in the basin. No one brought them here, and they do not exist anywhere along the cold basalt coast outside the perimeter fence. Whatever came up the storm-water culverts five or six years ago—something small, brackish, and desperate to escape a winter gale—found the unvarying forty-degree bath, the endless fall of cooked midges, the lack of seasons, and simply broke open its own biology to fit the room.

They are roughly the size and weight of wet burlap sacks half-filled with gravel, though stretched low and wide so that when they flatten themselves down, the shallow water runs cleanly over their backs without breaking surface tension. Their skin has lost whatever littoral mottling their ancestors carried; it is now the exact matte, broom-finished grey of Portland cement, darkened when wet to the slate tone of the curing fillets along the cliff base. Along their flanks, tucked into heavy, overlapping folds of hide that mimic the expansion joints in the slab, runs a faint, greasy iridescence—the precise bruised purple-amber of sodium vapor reflecting off wet steel.

Everything about them is built to drink the roar. Outside, in the tidal pools beyond the cliffs, an animal needs ears tuned to the snap of a gull’s wing or the turn of the surf. Down here, conventional hearing is useless—a liability that the constant hundred-decibel wall of the axial fans would quickly shred to pain. Their external ear openings have pinched entirely shut, sealed over with smooth, calloused scales. Instead, they listen through their bellies and their lower jaws. Pressed flat to the flooded concrete, their broad, cartilaginous chests act as sounding boards against the slab. They feel the deep baritone hum of the three cooling drums as a steady, comforting architecture—three massive pillars of vibration holding up their world. Against that unshifting bass note, the tiniest variance rings across the hydraulic apron like a stone dropped on glass. The sideways, needle-tipped tap of a land crab’s legs twenty yards away; the soft *plip* of a stunned swift striking the water; the heavy, cushioned thud of a rubber boot stepping onto the top iron tread of the corner stair three stories up. 

Long before the steel fire door swings open on Tuesday mornings, all eleven have slid smoothly across the slick algae mats and wedged themselves beneath the overhanging lips of the cooling drums or flat against the dark basalt shadow-lines, motionless as poured footings. The technicians walk within ten feet of them, shining flashlights at the analog gauges, never knowing that the slab beside their boots is breathing.

They do not hunt so much as they garrison the gradients. Warm-blooded things burn too fast in forty-degree steam; the herons stand panting, their throats fluttering rapidly to dump heat, and even the swifts must eventually rise into the cooler stratus above the building roofs to rest. The flat-bodied ones on the floor have no internal furnace to stoke or cool. Their blood is the exact temperature of the runoff. Because they spend nothing to stay warm, and because the basin never cools by half a degree at three in the morning or in the dead of February, they have shed the frantic urgency that drives most living things. 

One will lie for sixteen hours at the precise boundary where the scalding outflow from Drum Two cools enough for the bottle-green algae to give way to bare cement. It keeps its wide, lipless mouth slightly ajar beneath the waterline. Inside, the flesh of its palate is pale yellow—not the organic yellow of fat or bone, but the harsh, industrial safety-yellow of the painted bollards. To a mottled frog washed down from the perimeter gutters, or a young land crab scuttling blind through the glare, that narrow strip of yellow looks like a dry edge to climb onto, an interruption in the wet grey infinity. The crab reaches up with a pale claw to test the purchase. There is no thrashing, no dramatic strike—just a sudden, hydraulic drop of the creature’s throat floor that sucks a gallon of hot water and the crab straight inward with a dull, hollow *clack* completely swallowed by the transformer buzz. Shells are crushed against broad, flat plates at the back of the jaw; a minute later, clean, pulverized carapace drifts out through fleshy gill-like vents behind the skull, immediately swarmed and carried off by tiny scavengers before it can settle.

Yet calling them mere ambush predators misses the stranger thing the basin has made of them. In a world without weather, night, or winter, their territoriality has turned entirely geometric, mapped onto the unseen thermal contours and acoustic shadows of the plant. 

Between the three grey drums, where the overlapping fan washes create dead zones of thick, swirling fog, the largest two—old females nearly five feet from blunt snout to broad, rudderless tail—maintain what looks remarkably like a nursery, though nothing is built. Here, the cast-iron drainage grates drink the sheeted water with a steady, sucking gurgle. Around the rims of the grates, the females arrange heavy, waterlogged debris that survives the crabs: shed heron primaries coated in mineral scale, stripped lengths of plastic cable-tie dropped from the switchgear louvers years ago, smooth chunks of basalt spalled off the drill-grooves during some forgotten tremor. They nudge these pieces with their heavy snouts until the water eddies differently, pooling half an inch deeper and slowing down just enough to drop a fine silt of dead midges and cooked algae spores.

Into these warm, slack-water pockets they lay strings of leathery, translucent capsules, anchoring them directly to the iron bars of the grates with a clear, resinous spit that cures hard in hot water. You can see the embryos coiled inside, twitching in time with the sixty-hertz hum of the rim transformers. When they hatch—no bigger than a thumb, pale as the house geckos up on the cinderblock—they do not head for the storm culverts or the sea. Sea water, cold and pitching and full of salt, would kill them in ten minutes. Instead, they skitter straight into the rust-colored thermophilic algae at the drum bases, burying themselves in water hot enough to scald a human wrist, grazing on midge larvae until their skin thickens to cement-grey.

Sometimes, when the coastal stratus presses down unusually low and thickens the pewter gloom at the bottom of the cut, three or four of the adults will gather in the wide open expanse between Drum One and Drum Two, right where the amber sodium reflections cross the cold white beams of the catwalk LEDs. 

The egrets give them a wide berth, stepping stiffly away on long black legs without quite flying off, watching sideways. The creatures do not fight, and if they are mating, they make no show of it. They simply press their flat flanks together, snout to tail, forming a rough, glistening grey circle six feet across on the flooded slab. The shallow water hits their joined bulk, dams up to three inches on the upstream side, and spills smoothly over their backs like a miniature weir. 

They stay like that for hours while the condensate drips fat and steady from the overhead pipe bridges onto their broad skulls, each heavy drop flattening against their skin and vanishing. Their milky third eyelids slide shut. Their throats pulse slowly, once every two or three minutes, pushing warm water past their jaws. Down through their pressed bellies, through twelve inches of reinforced Portland cement and the fractured coastal basalt beneath it, they hold the deep, unvarying chord of the hidden inland turbines—vast, blind, and utterly secure inside a summer made of megawatts, waiting for nothing at all because nothing here has ever stopped.
