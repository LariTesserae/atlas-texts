If you look closely at the plum-colored catwalks of a counterweight station, or peer into the shaded gallery beneath a rotary kiln’s trunnions, you will eventually spot the people who tend the iron. At first, you might mistake one for a bundled tool-bag hung on a railing, or a particularly narrow, vertical outcrop of the rusted plating itself. Like the shield-lice on the cables, they have learned the stillness of things that cling to vertical walls.

They are tall, spare humans—if the word *human* hasn't stretched slightly out of shape over the dry millennia—built with the attenuated, high-leverage proportions of crane-flies. Their limbs are long and ropy, all tendon and dry bone, with wide, flat feet shod in soft-soled boots of layered floater-hide stitched with copper wire. Bare skin is rarely seen, not out of modesty, but because the upward wind is an eternal desiccant; to leave an arm uncovered for an afternoon is to invite the talc-dust to drink the marrow right out of you. Instead, they wrap themselves from ankle to collarbone in close-wound swaddlings of heavy, static-shedding linen—woven from the spun, mineral-rich bristles of the cable-grazers—dyed the exact bruised terracotta and matte-ochre of the cliffs. Over this, they wear aprons and shoulder-yokes of oiled chitin plates, overlapping like roof-slates so the falling silt slides off them with a faint, dry *shhh* whenever they shift their weight.

Their faces are hidden behind dust-cowls and wide, wrap-around goggles ground from pale yellow slag-glass, fitted with side-vents of fine brass mesh. When they come indoors—into the dim, pressurized cylinder-wards that smell of warm grease, crushed sulfur, and recycled tea—and pull the cowls down, their skin has the smooth, fine-pored, faintly waxy cast of tallow candles. Their eyes are dark and extraordinarily patient, ringed with deep, dry creases from squinting across four miles of sunlit void to read the tilt of a distant derrick. Their voices are pitched very low. High, sharp sounds tear the throat in this air and die twenty feet from the lips anyway; when the ward-folk speak to one another, it is a chest-deep, resonant murmur that you feel through the iron floorboards as much as hear, punctuated by wide, deliberate semaphore of those long arms when two grease-riggers are working opposite ends of a hundred-yard winch drum.

Water is not an element of the landscape to them; it is an heirloom. Every drop in the suspended archipelago is locked inside a closed loop—distilled from the trace moisture baked out of deep-trench clays in the kilns, condensed in dark, cool labyrinth-pipes bored fifty yards into the insulated heart of the dolomite cliffs, and piped into the tenements through thick, lead-jacketed veins. Nobody here has ever seen rain, or a puddle, or mud. Water is a heavy, clear oil kept in stoppered stoneware flasks clipped to the belt, drunk through a valved reed so nothing is lost to evaporation. A child born in the cylinder-wards knows the precise weight of their own sweat; they are taught from infancy to move without breaking one, adopting a gliding, pendulum-like gait that borrows momentum from the sway of the suspension bridges and never fights gravity head-on.

To watch a maintenance crew at work on a radial turbine is to watch a ritual conducted in geological time. Three or four figures clip their copper-braided grounding lanyards to the main busbar—an instinctive gesture, done before anything else, producing a tiny blue *snap* as their bodies equalize with the cliff’s electrical charge. If you forget to ground yourself after walking a quarter-mile along a catwalk in the dry wind, touching an iron wrench will knock you flat. Once grounded, they do not swarm the machine. One rigger sits cross-legged on the curved housing, motionless for forty minutes, holding a long wooden listening-rod against his jawbone while its brass tip rests on the main bearing casing, feeling the ten-minute rhythm of the shaft’s rotation for the tiniest dry scrape of silicate grit. Another slowly works a lever-pump, feeding thick, amber grease—rendered from the waxy bladders of harvested static-beetles—into a journal box the size of a parlor. They do not chatter. They pass tools with the unhurried grace of sleepwalkers, pausing whenever a flock of floaters drifts close enough that the pale, feathery trailing arms brush across the gantry rails, waiting politely for the dry membranes to clear the rigging rather than hacking them away.

Because everything takes hours to arrive, their lives are organized around the great, predictable intervals of the drop. Time is not measured in minutes, which are nervous, useless things, but in *hauls* and *turns*—the twelve-hour ascent of a dredge-bucket, the six-turn bake of a batch of slag. While a gondola makes its half-day crossing from the plum-rusted foundry on the West Flank to the sorting docks of the Spindle Ward, the pilot does not stand at a helm. There is no helm—only a heavy brass brake-wheel and an anemometer needle that scarcely trembles. Instead, the pilot sits in a slung canvas hammock beneath the gondola’s belly, shaded from the glare, carving intricate geometric lattices out of porous floater-bone or playing long, silent games of *shale* on a magnetized iron board with the two dock-hands riding back from shift. Beneath them, four miles of amber nothingness slides by, warm and bright, while a kettle of lentil-paste and dried beetle-meat simmers slowly on a resistor-plate wired directly into the haulage cable’s ambient current.

They have an intimacy with the chasm’s creatures that has nothing to do with sentiment and everything to do with shared physics. Every tenement cylinder has its "roof-herd" of static-beetles crusted around its transformer cones, and each family knows the particular pitch of their herd's clicking; a sudden silence on the roof means a drop in line voltage from the turbines below long before the ward’s filament-lamps begin to dim. Old folks whose knees have stiffened too much for the catwalks spend their afternoons on the sun-warmed balconies with long, soft-bristled brooms of copper wire, gently sweeping the dinner-plate-sized shield-lice off the window-shutters—not killing them, just tipping them off into the thermal, knowing the flat creatures will catch the updraft like falling leaves, plane half a mile across the gulf, and clamp onto a lower cable without taking the slightest harm. And when the gaunt porcelain scissor-crabs drop onto a newly risen dredge-bucket, the dock-foremen lean on their iron railings and watch which part of the silt-pile the crabs crowd toward; the creatures have a sharper nose for high-grade yellow sulfur than any assaying-glass, and where the porcelain legs cluster thickest, the foremen swing the primary suction-chutes.

Deeper down—though never all the way to the bottom, where the air presses like warm lead and the brass haze blinds even slag-goggles—live the Bell-Divers. They are a breed apart even among the cliff-folk, thicker in the chest, with heavy, barrel-ribbed torsos adapted to breathing the dense, soupy atmosphere two miles below the lowest tenements. They descend in open-framed iron cages hung beneath the exploratory winches, wearing heavy brass-weighted boots and breathing through thick, quilted filter-trunks stuffed with crushed charcoal and grease-wool that hang down their chests like elephantine snouts. Down there, in the perpetual amber twilight where the sun is just a diffuse, shadowless glow in every direction at once, the cliffs bulge close together and the thermal draft roars upward in a warm, heavy river of talc. 

The Divers go down to inspect the great lower anchor-pitons hammered into the stone by ancestors so distant nobody remembers what kind of drills they used, and to harvest the *deep-plates*—immense, solitary relatives of the cable-lice, ten feet across, whose shells have grown layers of pure, biogenic copper and trace gold precipitated out of the pressurized dust over centuries. A Diver might hang in the warm, yellow soup for two days at a stretch, clipped to a cliff-flute smoothed like an ivory tusk, sleeping upright in their harness while the thick air buoys their arms upward like water, chipping away old mineral crusts with a bronze bar whose strikes make only dull, muffled *thuds* in the dense atmosphere. When their cage is finally winched back up into the clear, thin daylight of the upper wards, they have to sit for a full shift in decompression alcoves, letting the dissolved nitrogen hiss slowly out of their joints while ward-children gather at the thick glass portholes to stare at the pale, glowing dust-mites still clinging to the pleats of their grease-suits.

Nobody leaves the archipelago for the rim above. Four thousand feet over the highest foundry, the dolomite shelves eventually top out onto the continental plateau, where the air is thin, bitterly cold, and scouring-fast, stripped of the chasm’s banked, ancient warmth. Up there, nothing lives—not even the shield-lice—and there is no thermal lift to turn the great wheels. To the people of the cables, the rim is a dead ceiling, a place of sharp shadows and cruel haste. 

Even in death, they stay in the column of air that made them. There is no soil to dig, and firing a kiln hot enough to ash bones wastes good current. Instead, when an old turbine-wright or cable-walker dries out for the last time—passing quietly in a warm ward-bunk until they weigh hardly more than a bundle of reeds—their kin wrap them in unbleached bristle-linen, weighting their ankles with a small, balanced measure of ceramic slag. At evening, when the west walls have gone deep violet and the center of the chasm still holds a pillar of clear, dusty gold, they carry the bundle out to the tip of a cantilevered derrick. They stitch the shroud’s harness-loops to the trailing tendons of two or three mature, half-tamed floaters bred specially in the high net-pens for the task—creatures just buoyant enough to almost, *almost* hold the weight. 

When they slip the bronze toggle, the dead do not plummet. Held by the pale, translucent bells above them, balanced to the ounce against the thick, rising warmth of the gulf, they sink at an imperceptible crawl—ten feet an hour, drifting out into the sunlit miles between the silent foundries, turning once or twice in an afternoon, descending gently over days into the warm, brass-colored haze below, until the dust takes them in and files them away to nothing, feeding the wind that turns the wheels.
