At first, you take them for utility housings that have not yet finished extruding, or else low, tarpaulined bundles left behind by whatever absent surveyor laid out the ward.

Look down from an upper terrace into a court paved in hexagonal dark glass, and you might see one wedged into the ninety-degree crease where a bone-porcelain bulkhead meets a warm conduit-stack: a dense, motionless mass the size of a kneeling calf, draped in heavy,overlapping plates of something that looks like oiled slate or stiffened felt. For twenty minutes it does not stir. Rime builds along its leeward ridge in neat, rectangular grids, matching the frost on the wall beside it. A slow ribbon of condensation slides down a gravity-rod six inches from its snout, taking ten minutes to fall, and the creature does not even blink.

Then a millimeter of new basaltic sill snaps into existence three alleys over—*tick*—and the bundle shifts its weight.

What unfolds is not a beast, nor quite a citizen, though it possesses the weary, methodical deportment of a night-watchman making rounds in a factory whose purpose he did not invent. Standing, it reaches barely to a human chest, yet it gives an impression of immense, compact mass, as if packed with wet clay and clock-weights. Its body is low, barrel-chested, and subtly asymmetric, carried on five short, pillar-like limbs that end not in paws or hooves, but in broad, articulate pads of dark, flexible horn, split into three opposing digits like a mason’s calipers. Three of these limbs stay planted on the horizontal paving; the fourth routinely walks along the vertical wall beside it to steady its center of gravity; the fifth is kept folded tightly against its brisket, tucked beneath a heavy apron of pleated hide, brought out only when the creature needs to reach *across* a refractive seam into a second room at once.

It has no true head—only a forward-sloping cowl of matte, charcoal-grey cartilage that shields a recessed cluster of sensory organs from the biting air. Peer under that hood when it pauses in a shaft of flat autumn sunlight, and you see no wet mammalian eyes, which would freeze or go blind trying to reconcile two buildings occupying the same coordinates. Instead, set deep within dry, velvet-lined grooves, are four dull, metallic-looking bullseyes of bismuth-colored tissue, arranged in a skewed diamond. They do not focus light so much as read phase and pressure, catching the hair-thin colorless seams in the air the way a trout feels the eddy behind a boulder. Beneath them sits a narrow, vertical cleft bordered by four nimble, finger-length palps of pale, chamois-soft flesh, forever twitching slightly to taste the ionized nitrogen and stray thermal gradients bleeding off the masonry.

If there is only one of them, the ward would feel haunted; in truth there are perhaps two dozen sharing the acre and a half, though you will rarely see three in the same hour. The folded metric of the town swallows them. One will amble down a four-foot lane, turn its shoulder into the hair-thin flaw where Wall A and Wall B share space, and simply step into the four-degree rotational offset, vanishing from your lane without ever having crossed an opening, though if you lay your palm against the cold porcelain a moment later you can feel the slow, heavy *thump-thump-scrape* of its gait passing directly through the stone under your fingers.

They are ectotherms of a strange, opportunistic sort—creatures of differential. They do not eat the wire-stiff grey sedge, nor do they hunt the slate-colored pipits or the stretched dusk-hares. A warm-blooded furnace of a body would starve here inside a week. Instead, they graze the mismatches.

Watch an old one—if "old" means anything in a town whose eldest foundation stone is scarcely cooled—working a narrow lightwell at midday. It approaches a vertical pane where two incompatible pressures meet and the half-lichen, half-frost crust grows in concentric rings. Planting three feet on the glass floor and one against an overhanging soffit that ought to be six feet too high to reach, it unfurls its fifth, central limb. This arm is longer and leaner than the walking-legs, pale grey, multi-jointed like a folding carpenter's rule, terminating in a flat, spatulate scraper of translucent quartz-like nail. With immense, unhurried care, it peels a curl of the ionized frost-crust from the wall and tucks it into the vertical cleft beneath its cowl.

That is only half its meal. Still chewing—a faint, dry sound like sand ground between two saucers—it presses the broad dorsal plates of its back flat against a patch of matte-white ceramic that happens to sit thirty degrees warmer than the freezing air, while simultaneously extending a fan of dark, papery gill-flaps from its left flank into a pocket of sub-zero draft an inch away. It sits there for an hour, a living Peltier engine, bridging the thermal seam with its own flesh and letting the sluggish current of heat-to-cold drive its slow, mercury-thick circulation. While it feeds, its breathing makes no steam; the air leaves its vents already wrung dry and matched to the exact temperature of the alley.

For a long time, watching them creep along cornices and straddle misjoined thresholds, you might assume they are mere scavengers—highly specialized vermin that crawled out of whatever elsewhere this architecture is arriving from, clinging to the town like barnacles on a hull.

Then you watch what they do with the water, and with the looms.

Down in the cramped, damp courts, where droplets take ten minutes to slide down the gravity-rods and pool in dry-rimmed sumps without depth, the arrival of new stone occasionally fouls a drainage line. A millimeter of fresh feldspar extrudes across a channel; the reluctant film of liquid stalls, creeps sideways toward a cold zone, and threatens to freeze into a solid plug that would fracture a bundle of horsehair conduits. Long before ice can form, one of the cowled creatures arrives. It does not hurry—nothing here hurries—but it comes directly, drawn by some minute shift in the local echo or a sour note in the air’s electrical tension.

Squatting beside the fouled channel, it extrudes a thick, clear resin from glands along its palps, working the stuff between its caliper-digits until it pulls like cold taffy. It lays a neat, square-edged bead of this putty along the stone, redirecting the weeping moisture half an inch to the right so it catches the gravity-rod again. Within minutes, the resin hardens into something indistinguishable from the dark, oil-smooth fullerene of the walls. Half the low curbs and baffling-ridges in the lower tiers, when you look closely, are not cast architecture at all; they are old resin-work, laid down with plumb-line neatness by three-toed hands.

Stranger still is their commerce with the interiors—those narrow, window-lit rooms buried four tiers deep that have no exterior volume to house them, filled with motionless apparatus like looms spun from smoked ice.

There are no doors into those rooms. Even a hare cannot slip into them. Yet at late afternoon, when the shadows point back toward the low sun and the ward settles into its deepest, most brittle quiet, you may catch sight of a figure moving behind one of the high, unshuttered panes.

To get inside, two of the creatures have to cooperate. They meet in an alley beneath the window, touching palps briefly in complete silence—no clicks, no grunts, only a slow, mutual tasting of the air between them. Then one braces itself squarely across a refractive pocket, planting two feet in one building’s coordinate system and two in its twin’s, holding its heavy, asymmetric body rigid like a chock wedged into a doorjamb. The local geometry strains slightly around its dense mass; the hair-thin optical flaw widens to the breadth of a ribbon, shimmering colourless and cold. Through that held-open crease, the second creature folds itself—compressing its pleated hide, tucking its limbs until it looks half its size, and sliding sideways along an angle that makes the human eye water and look away.

A moment later, it is four stories up and inside the volumeless room, walking calmly among the racks of smoked-ice machinery.

What it does there has nothing to do with hunger. Moving down the narrow aisle, it uses its long, rule-jointed fifth arm to pluck microscopic motes of marl-dust from the crystalline threads—dust blown over the basin’s rim from the raw, earthly gravel outside, which has no business being in this immaculate, arriving place. Where two spun-ice shafts have grown slightly out of true during their slow extrusion into our world, the creature grips one with its horned calipers and leans its whole heavy weight against it for twenty minutes, patiently coaxing the phase-matter back into alignment before it finishes crystallizing. Sometimes it simply stands before a dead, loom-like frame, opens the sensory grooves beneath its cowl wide, and sways slightly from side to side, as if listening for a signal along horsehair conduits whose other ends have not yet been born into the marl.

They have a nest, if you can call it that, down at the very bottom of the ward’s vertical knot, where the embryonic bedrock extrudes out of nothing.

Few things go down there; the air is dead and sharp enough to burn the lungs, and the spatial folding is so tight that a six-foot alley must be crossed in eleven sideways steps. Tucked beneath a foundational plinth, where a doorway opens directly onto a vertical face of black basaltic glass still growing outward millimeter by millimeter, five or six of them gather during the long high-latitude nights. They do not curl together for warmth—to huddle would erase the temperature differences they need to live. Instead, they arrange themselves in a precise, spaced ring around a dry sump, each beast straddling a different micro-climate, one foot in frost, one shoulder against a warm conduit, forming a slow, breathing circuit around the dark.

Pinned to the raw basalt wall above them, cemented there with dabs of hardened resin, are the only things in the entire town that show age or disorder: a neat, horizontal row of objects salvaged from the raw country outside the clay rim. A bleached hare’s pelvis. Three grey-and-white primaries shed by a gull passing high overhead. Five sharp pebbles of blue-grey glacial marl, arranged strictly by size. Every so often, one of the resting creatures reaches up with its pale fifth limb and shifts a pebble a fraction of an inch to the left—not at random, but tracking the slow, silent advance of the extruding bedrock behind it, measuring the town’s arrival against the plain, unbent stone of the earth.

They have no name for themselves because sound dies too strangely in the alleys to hang a name on anything; if one dropped a call, it would return behind its own cowl three seconds later, flat and small, from an empty court. Yet if you stand up on the raw gravel rim as dusk thickens, watching the ink-crisp shadows fall in three contradictory directions at once, you may see one surface briefly on an uppermost parapet.

It stands square against the ordinary pale sky, no taller than a milestone, frost-grids already crystallizing across its slate-dark plates. Its four metallic eyes turn outward, past the sharp clay bank, across the empty, windswept flats of late autumn where a mile is always a mile and water always freezes at zero. For a long minute its pale palps taste the plain, un-ionized wind. It looks like a harbor-master standing on the breakwater of a port that has only half-cleared customs into existence—waiting, without impatience, for the rest of the streets to arrive and bring whatever citizens these lintels and stairwells were actually cut to fit, or else knowing perfectly well that nothing else is coming, and that the town, the looms, and the cold, sweating walls belong entirely to the keepers now.

Then a fresh fraction of cornice clicks into being beneath its feet. Without looking down, it shifts one caliper-foot sideways onto a roof thirty paces away, folds its cowl against the cold, and goes back down into the seams.
