At first, you think the rigging is moving on its own. A wicker basket the size of a bushel barrel rises six feet through a gap in the scaffolding, pauses to let a panicked dove clear its rim, and settles onto a corbeled brick shelf with a dry, practiced *thump*. Only then do you notice the counterweight twelve yards away hasn't dropped—someone is hauling the line by hand, tucked sideways into a flue three tiers down where the amber light barely reaches.

They are called the *Klin-folk* by the plainsmen who occasionally trade twists of salt and bundles of raw hemp down through the cedar collar, though among themselves they have no collective noun at all, naming individuals solely by the tier and niche where they sleep. There are perhaps forty of them in the pit at any given year—never many more, never many fewer, calibrated as strictly to the condensation basins and the nesting jars as the emerald moss is to the overflow drips.

To look at one of them standing on open ground—which you never will, because they do not go up—would be to see a body shaped entirely by tight clearances and vertical masonry. They are small, compact people, rarely clearing five feet, built with wide, flat ribcages made for exhaling and slipping sideways between twin buttresses without scraping flesh. Their posture has a permanent, fluid crouch to it; their necks sit slightly forward so the crown of the head never strikes a low oak lintel, and their knees turn outward easily, allowing them to press the sides of their feet flat against vertical brickwork while scaling a pier. 

Everything about their surfaces has been burnished by terracotta. Their skin, naturally a warm olive-brown, is perpetually dusted to a matte, velvety ochre in the creases of the knuckles, behind the knees, and along the collarbones where brick-flour settles and mixes with sweat. On their palms, heels, and the outsides of their forearms and shoulders—the exact points that catch a body when turning through a crooked passage—the calluses have thickened into smooth, horn-colored pads as slick and toughened as old rawhide. You can hear one coming before you see them, not by their footsteps, which are uncannily quiet even on the ceramic shingle at the bottom, but by the soft, dry *shhh-shhh* of their tough, wrapped linen kilts brushing against curved clay jars.

They wear almost nothing above the waist because cloth catches on splinters and soaks up the warm, damp air. Instead, they grease their skin lightly with rendered dove-fat steeped in the bitter-herbs—wormwood, rue, and crushed caper-leaf—that grow in the mortar seams. It keeps the mortar-mites and bird-lice off them, and gives them the exact smell of the pit itself: pungent, warm, and medicinal. Their hair is universally cropped to skull-fuzz or bound tightly in greased leather thongs; loose hair in a narrow scaffolding full of twisted gut-lines and rough cedar pegs is a snare no child survives twice.

Watch an older woman working the eastern jar-wall at midday, and you begin to understand that the pit is not a ruin they inhabit, but an instrument they are constantly tuning. 

She hangs twenty feet above the potsherd drift, one bare heel wedged into a chipped brick frog, the crook of her left knee hooked casually over a peeled-sapling strut. She uses no safety line. At her hip hangs a gourd of lime-putty and a flat, leaf-shaped trowel carved from the shoulder blade of an ox—iron would strike sparks, rust in the sweating niches, and chip the soft, precious lips of the ancient stoneware. With three quick, rhythmic strokes, she packs fresh lime around a loosened nesting tube, wiping the excess clean with the heel of her thumb before reaching shoulder-deep into the adjacent jar. 

The rock dove inside doesn't even burst out. It puffs its slate feathers, strikes weak, indignant blows against her wrist with the bend of its wing, and coos furiously while her fingers count the squabs by touch, checking their crops for fullness, pinching off a flat-fly, and slipping a single unfertilized egg into the pleated pouch at her waist before withdrawing. She makes a low, bubbling click in the back of her throat—an eerie, exact mimicry of a mated cock-bird settling on a ledge—and the dove immediately subsides.

Sound is different for them. Living inside a drum that never stops roaring has ruined them for whispering and cured them of shouting; shouting in the pit simply shatters against ten thousand curved clay surfaces and comes back as an unintelligible ringing blur. Instead, they speak across the gulf in crisp, percussive syllables pitched right into the narrow gap between the low thunder of the doves and the high rasp of the crickets. More often, they don't use voices at all. A knuckle rapped twice against a hollow flue-pipe carries cleanly down three levels to whoever has their bare sole resting against the base of that same pier. A quick pluck on a taut rawhide stay-rope sends a thrumming tremor across sixty paces of open air to tap the wrist of a boy mending hawk-chimes near the upper collar. They read the rigging like a spider reads its web.

Follow that boy down when the light begins to thicken toward evening—when the direct shaft of sun climbs up the eastern wall and pulls out through the wooden collar, leaving the whole forty-pace depth steeped in a deep, glowing, brownish-red twilight. 

Down in the third tier from the bottom, where the old kiln-chambers bulge outward like buried beehives, the domestic life of the pit crowds into spaces the size of bread ovens and wine-casks. There is no fire here. Fire eats air, blackens the reflecting tiles that bring daylight down to the lower runs, and sends panicking flocks smashing blindly into the scaffolding until wings snap and catwalks tear loose. Nothing has burned in this pit for ten generations. 

Instead, they cook with lime and blanket-heat. In the recessed alcoves, thick-walled earthenware crocks sit nested inside larger jars packed around the base with walnut-sized chunks of quicklime traded from the surface. A cup of collected condensation poured into the outer jacket sets off a fierce, silent, hissing heat—enough to simmer bruised caper-buds, cracked grain, and pickled dove eggs into a thick, salty porridge without raising a single wisp of smoke. When the lime is spent and slaked, nothing is wasted; it is kneaded with brick-dust and stray feathers plucked from the air to become next week’s mortar.

In these warm, dry chambers—curved brick vaults rubbed so smooth by generations of backs and hips that they shine like polished walnut in the gloom—the families fold themselves together. Because floor space is scarce and every flat surface is needed for drying herbs, sorting guano into woven panniers for the plainsmen’s fields, or coiling cordage, they sleep tucked into shallow, tiered alcoves stepped up the walls like bookshelves, rigged across the front with loose nets of hemp to keep sleepers from rolling out into the dark. 

Look closely at the elders sitting on the worn thresholds, working in the near-dark while the warm updrafts stir the chimes overhead. Their eyes are extraordinary: dark, wide-pupiled, surrounded by deep crow’s-feet not from squinting against sun, but from decades of reading hairline fractures in dry mortar through swirling sunlit dust. Nearly all the oldest among them have stiff, thickened joints in their big toes and the middle two fingers of both hands—the climbing digits—and their hearing has gone strangely selective. An old man seventy years deep in the pit will not turn his head if ten square yards of pottery-gravel slide loudly down the bottom drift, because gravel always slides; yet if a single oak peg twenty feet overhead gives the tiny, high *tick* of dry-splitting wood under load, his hand will shoot out and catch his grandson’s ankle before the boy takes another step onto the catwalk.

And down at the very bottom, on the shifting three-foot floor of rounded terracotta pebbles, nobody lives, though someone is almost always there. 

Children too young for the high ropes crawl and tumble across the sherd-drift alongside the tame, half-feral civets that tolerate human knees in exchange for cracked eggs and skink-tails. Every movement down here sends up a bright, dry, musical crunching, like walking through a shallow surf of tiny bells. This is the sorting floor. Here the very old and the very small sit cross-legged in the warm, tea-colored gloom, sifting through the fallen centuries with nimble, dusty fingers. They are looking for pieces of the pit that have finished their shaping: a potsherd tumbled to the exact curve needed to shim a wobbling basin; a sliver of dense, vitrified kiln-brick sharp enough to scrape rawhide; a hollow bone bead dropped three lifetimes ago from a higher ledge, polished smooth by the slow creep of the drift, found again, blown clear of red dust, and strung back onto a gut cord around a warm, greased throat. 

When night finally seals the rectangle of sky overhead, turning the timber collar black against the stars, the pit does not go quiet—it merely changes register. The doves tuck their heads and drop their cooing to a sleepy, rhythmic purr that vibrates right through the brickwork into your ribs. The crickets swell to fill the gap. Moisture beads faster on the cooling counterweights, accelerating the *plink-plink-pat* into the green-rimmed bowls. And in forty hidden niches up and down the dark masonry, forty warm bodies shift in their rope-slung shelves, listening in their sleep to the tension of the stays, the drip of their water, and the breath of the baked earth holding them up.
