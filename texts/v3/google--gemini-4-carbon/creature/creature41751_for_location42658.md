To find what truly keeps this place—not merely the rays and the blind shrimp that rent its rooms, nor the stacked turtles using its amphitheaters as resting-shelves, but the proprietors—you have to wait for one of those cold downdrafts to clear the streets. 

When the million small shells click shut and the catfish drop into the heated ruts of the avenues, a secondary movement begins. Out of the deepest, unlit vaults beneath the plazas, rising directly through the sulfurous, bath-warm plumes of the cellar grates, come shapes that look at first like thick, billowing skeins of pale silt, or shed skins caught in an updraft. 

They do not swim with the muscular, side-to-side thrust of fish, nor the crisp jetting of squid. They travel by peristalsis and glide, undulating broad, fleshy mantles that ripple along their flanks like heavy wet linen. Up close—though in this blindness, "close" is a matter of water-pressure and smell rather than sight—they are massive, unhurried creatures, longer than a man is tall, though rarely stretched to their full length. They have the dense, boneless weight of giant slugs or shell-less gastropods, pale as boiled tallow, their skin entirely scaleless and slick with a heavy, mineral-rich mucus.

They have no eyes at all—not even the scarred, sunken pits of creatures that once knew the sun and lost it. Their heads are broad, blunt cowls of sensitive flesh, fringed with dozens of short, stubby, finger-like palps that constantly taste the mortar-less stone and the drifting organic snow. Beneath this cowl sits a circular, muscular mouth housing a wide, rasping ribbon of chitinous teeth. 

Listen closely in the hush after a cold front, and beneath the settling groan of the arches, you will hear them: a slow, wet, rhythmic *shhhk-shhhk-shhhk*, like coarse sandpaper dragged across wet soap. 

They are grazing, and they are building. 

For a long time, an observer drifting in the dark might mistake them for mere scavengers licking bacterial mats from the bone-smooth balustrades. Watch one pause at a cracked lintel, however, or hover where an ancient archway has begun to sag into the riverbed, and the truth of the architecture reveals itself. The creature presses its broad, pale belly against the fracture. Its mantle flares, sealing the area off from the drifting silt, creating a warm, private pocket of water against the limestone. From glands along its flanks, it secretes a thick, chalky paste—a slurry of digested river-clay, dissolved shell-grit harvested from the dead bryozoans, and binding proteins refined from the sulfurous vents below. 

With slow, deliberate passes of its cowl, it packs the crack. It rasps away the crumbling, rotten stone with its teeth and lays down fresh, calcifying plaster, smoothing it with the heavy pad of its foot until the patch matches the soapstone curve of the surrounding wall. Within days, fed by the mineral-heavy water of the thermal plumes, the paste hardens into living rock. 

They are the masons. Or rather, their ancestors were the masons, and they are whatever remains when the builder and the building stop asking where one ends and the other begins. 

Whether this town was ever dry is a question the stone can no longer answer, because every inch of it has been eaten, digested, and re-laid a hundred times over. Perhaps something with legs and iron tools once laid a grid of streets on a dry valley floor ten thousand years ago, before the basin flooded; if so, these pale, massive crawlers found the drowned ruins, liked the way the walls trapped the heat, and simply kept up the maintenance. Or perhaps there were never men here at all—only an ancient lineage of giant, colonial mollusks that learned, over geological ages, that if they built their reefs in tiers, vaults, and chimneys, they could farm the thermal vents, channeling the hot, sulfur-rich water past indoor gardens of bacterial tallow and trapping clouds of blind shrimp in dead-end courtyards.

As you follow them deeper into the city's vertical canyons, their habits grow stranger and more domestic. They are solitary when they work the outer avenues, gliding effortlessly past the yard-long catfish—which part for them with sluggish deference, never brushing their trailing barbels against the masons' bitter, chalk-tasting hides. Nothing eats them; they taste of lime, sulfur, and sharp ammonia. In turn, they ignore the traffic of the streets entirely, except when a dying ray or a crushed turtle settles onto the cobbles. Then, three or four of the pale shapes will converge, draping themselves over the carcass like heavy blankets, dissolving flesh and bone alike over a period of days, harvesting the calcium to carry back to the upper towers.

They do not live in the mud-choked ground floors where the eels knot. They prefer the mid-level apartments—rooms with two or three oval apertures where the thermal currents can be regulated. If a room grows too cold, a resident will spend a week gradually thickening the window-rims with secreted stone, narrowing the aperture by fractions of an inch until the draft is choked off and the interior stews at a steady, stagnant hundred degrees. If a room grows foul, they rasp a neat, round vent through the ceiling into the courtyard above, letting the stale waterchimney out. 

Inside these dark, warm chambers, they gather in heaps. Three, five, sometimes a dozen of them lie braided together on clean, swept limestone floors, resting between bouts of grazing and masonry. They communicate entirely through touch and chemistry, passing slow shivers along their mantles that travel through the whole pile. When one moves, sliding heavy and slick over the backs of its housemates, it picks up the chemical record of where the others have been: *the third bridge in the east canal is thinning; the bacterial mat in the deep cistern is thick and sweet; cold water is leaking through the outer terrace.*

And they tend things. Not the pale, waxy creepers near the slate-colored ceiling—they rarely go so high, disliking the cooler, thinner water near the top of the towers where the faint, bruised memory of daylight presses down. Instead, in the absolute pitch-black of the inner vaults, right above the hottest cellar grates, they cultivate dense, velvety orchards of rust-colored sponges and sulfur-eating filaments. You can find them hanging upside down from the ribbed vaults like pale chandeliers, holding themselves fast by suction, gently fanning warm water over the bacterial mats with the edges of their mantles to encourage growth, plucking away the thumb-sized crustaceans whenever the little grazers threaten to strip a patch bare. Occasionally, carelessly, a mason will flick a trespasser crab into its own rasping mouth with a dull *crunch* that dies instantly in the thick water, adding another pinch of shell-lime to its gut.

Near the very center of the basin, where the plazas are widest and the upwelling heat is almost scalding, stand structures that have no parallel in any dry-land town: immense, hollow pillars riddled with smooth, fist-sized pores, humming faintly like throat-pipes as the geothermal vents force water up through them. 

Here, the eldest of them go when they grow too heavy to glide. 

They never truly stop growing. In the deepest basements beneath these central chimneys live individuals the size of overturned skiffs, their flesh grown translucent and veined with chalky deposits, their mantles tattered and scarred by centuries of slow abrasion. Moving now only once in a season, their skins have begun to calcify in patches. Some have stayed still for so long in the warm, mineral-saturated dark that the silt and the bryozoans have overtaken their lower halves, fusing them seamlessly into the foundation piers. 

Even half-turned to stone, their cowls still lift slightly when a current shifts. Their stubby palps still taste the dark broth rising from the earth. When a cold downdraft rolls down from the unseen rim and chills the outer districts, these ancient, mortared-in giants feel the drop in pressure through the stone itself. Slowly, deeply, they contract their massive bodies inside their limestone hollows, forcing great lungfuls of hoarded, scalding water out through the porous pillars and into the avenues—a deliberate, hydraulic sigh that pushes back the cold, warms the cobbles for the waiting rays, and calls the million clicked-shut shells to open once more.
