The stone-knapper emerges from behind the bronze fronds feet-first, pushing off a hexagonal jamb with one broad, calloused sole and letting the water carry her across the lane. She does not swim so much as glide on a single spent impulse, limbs tucked loose against her ribs, trailing a faint eddy that sets the nearest reed-screen leaning an inch out of true.

She is heavy-boned and wide through the chest, built like all of them with a barrel ribcage that houses lungs twice the volume her height would suggest. Yet in the water she has the unhurried poise of drifting timber. There is very little fat on her—in a basin perpetually held at the temperature of blood, insulation is only a burden that makes a body bob toward the ceiling when it wants to stay down. Instead, she is dense: thick-muscled, heavy-limbed, her skin the matte, oil-dark slate of wet basalt, paler across the broad palms and the tough, horn-plated soles of her feet. Where the olive light catches her shoulders, a fine grain shows in the hide—not scales, not quite leather, a tight pebbled texture that sheds water cleanly when she reaches an arm up into an inverted hearth, never wrinkling or going soft despite a lifetime spent submerged.

Her feet and hands are the first things a stranger’s eye would catch, because they are never entirely still. Even while gliding, her long, thick-webbed toes flex and curl, testing the slow drift of the current, ready to hook a notched lintel or pinch a guide-rope in passing. Her fingers are long, flattened slightly from side to side, joined halfway to the second knuckle by tough elastic webbing, and tipped with dark, spatulate nails thick enough to pry open a split-reed lashing without reaching for a bone awl. Across her forearms and shins, old scars show as smooth, pale-gray seams—every child in the caldera learns early what fresh volcanic glass does to careless skin, and no one reaches adulthood unmarked by the town’s own foundations.

She catches the middle guide-rope with two toes, pivots effortlessly until her head points up toward the tethered canopy of a public square, and exhales a single, walnut-sized bubble that wobbles lazily ahead of her. Then she rises to breathe.

They are breathers of air, not gill-folk, though the distinction seems small to watch them live. Beneath the broad, anchored roof of the square, four others are already hanging at rest around the wide quicksilver mirror of the ceiling-pool. They do not haul themselves up into the pocket—the air space is shallow, barely a foot deep between the water’s skin and the pitched basketry of the trough, and above water their own weight feels sudden and clumsy to them. Instead, they float upright in the gloom beneath it, chins tilted back so that only their faces break the trembling silver surface from below.

Seen from underneath, their heads appear headless, replaced by dim ovals ripples where skin meets gas. Up inside the warm, resin-scented pocket, their faces are broad and flat-bridged, with nostrils set high on the nose and ringed by muscular flanges that seal shut like pinched valves the instant they dip back under. Their eyes are large, dark-irised, and fitted with second, smoky-clear lids that slide sideways from the inner corners; up in the dry air they blink often, shedding tears of thick oil to keep the corneas from stinging in the curing-fumes, while down below those inner membranes stay drawn, turning the world crisp and glare-free. Hair would be a snagging nuisance among obsidian edges and tight-woven screens, so they have none—only a low, fleshy ridge running from crown to nape, subtle as a folded seam, which stiffens slightly when the water cools near the outer caldera walls.

Around the upside-down hearth, conversation happens in the brief intervals of breathing, and it is a quiet, close-mouthed thing. Because the basketry trough acts like a whispering-dome, loud voices boom unpleasantly against the ears of everyone sharing the pocket. They speak in low, resonant hums and soft consonantal clicks, trading news while their submerged hands work just below their chins—twisting kelp-cord, scraping resin off a gourd’s lip, passing a bundle of dried bark from one set of webbed fingers to the next so its cured tips never touch the wet.

When the stone-knapper breaks the mirror beside them, her nasal valves flare open with a soft *pop* of equalizing pressure. For two full minutes she hangs there, exchanging half-whispered remarks, while her chest expands and falls in slow, enormous draughts, purging the stale gas from her blood and packing the red muscle of her tissues with fresh oxygen. Then she draws one final, cavernous breath—not filling her lungs to capacity, which would make her too buoyant, good only for work near the upper rafts, but taking a measured three-quarters lungful—lets her nostrils pinch shut, and drops gently out of the ceiling.

Down in the avenues, they do not speak with air. Wasting breath is a toddlers’ habit. Instead, a vocabulary of sharp, economical percussion runs constantly through the terraced wards, tuned to the peculiar acoustics of clean stone and siltless water. Every adult wears a striker-ring of dense, banded pebble-stone snugged over one thumb, and a small, flat plaque of resonant basalt or fire-hardened wood strapped to the outer thigh or tucked into a braided harness. A double-tap of thumb on thigh—*tink-tink*—carries clearly across sixty paces of open plaza to draw a neighbor’s eye; a dry, rasping slide across the notched edge of the plaque signals caution when a heavy lintel is being bled down into its notches overhead. Once two people are looking at one another through the olive gloom, their broad hands take over: pale palms flashing against dark chests in a rapid, fluid sign-speech that loses nothing through a curtain of drifting moss.

Their clothing is minimal, built for pockets, protection, and ballast rather than warmth. Most wear wide, cross-chest harnesses plaited from bleached plant-fiber and split reed, set with bone toggles and small mesh pouches where obsidian blades rest inside wooden sheaths—an unsheathed flake of glass in a belt is a death sentence if a current nudges one sideways against a wall. Around their waists or ankles hang trim-weights: smooth, drilled hexagons of small stone strung on cord with quick-release knots. A quarry-worker heading down into the middle depths to pry columns from the gorge floor strings on three or four extra stones so she can stand squarely on the ledges and swing a two-handed maul without lifting herself off her feet at every blow. A gatherer bound for the high bladder-forests leaves her weights pegged by the door, letting her body’s natural trim carry her easily up among the rust-red ribbons.

Up near the surface—fifty feet above the highest cantilevered plaza—three of the younger adults are working the air-skins, the least liked chore in the town.

Here the water loses a fraction of its fever-warmth, chilled by the wet wool of the mountain clouds resting directly on the caldera. Pumice rafts the size of flagstones clack dully together in the dimness. The haulers wear heavy hoods of greased bladder-leather tied snug under their jaws to spare their bare scalps and shoulders when they breach into the biting, thin highland air. One by one, they push wide-mouthed sacks of cured plant-skin above the surface, holding them open with hoops of springy water-shrub wood until the cold gray fog fills them, then twisting the necks shut, plunging them back down into the comforting warmth, and clipping them to a weighted haul-line. None of them lingers with head above the pumice longer than three breaths. Up there, everything is flat, crushing, and desolate; their own shoulders drag downward like wet clay, the thin air scrapes raw at their throats, and the world smells of nothing at all except cold ash. They duck back under with relief, kicking down along the guide-ropes toward the dim, glowing amber of the terraces below, dragging fat, straining teardrops of sky behind them.

Lower down, where the town’s masonry meets the wild thickets of the lava dome, life moves at the pace of the basin’s great thermal rotation.

In a wide courtyard carpeted with velvety maroon algae, an elder sits cross-legged on a stone bench, weighted down by a heavy basalt apron laid across his thighs so he will not drift while he sleeps. He has been down forty minutes; every ten or twelve, without waking, his broad foot pushes once against the bench, lifting him three yards on a tether-cord until his tilted face breaks the surface of a private sleeping-bell hung beneath an eave, takes four slow, automatic breaths, and sinks smoothly back to the bench as his lungs empty slightly. Around him, four or five very small children—paler than their parents, plump with infant fat they will lose by their sixth year, tethered by soft ankle-braids to a central ring—tumble in slow motion just off the floor, chasing a translucent, gill-frilled slug by blowing tiny, precious beads of stolen hearth-gas under its belly to watch it curl and right itself.

Farther out, along the sheer lip of the central gorge where the town’s stone staircases end and the dark plum water drops away toward the young fissures, the farmers tend the drift-nets and the acid-gardens. To root a new food-shrub or bladder-vine on bare volcanic glass where no soil has ever existed, they scrape the glossy mineral skin with rough pumice blocks, then press a poultice of crushed snail-slime and sour plant-mash onto the raw black rock beneath a weighted cup of woven reed, holding the seedling fast until its own rubbery holdfast etches a pit into the basalt. Beside the terraced plots hang long, spiral-baffled baskets braced open in the rising thermal plumes, harvesting the faint snow of mineral-fed bacteria and blind silver fry that drift up from the hot dark below.

Nowhere in the settlement is there a kiln, a forge, or an open flame, yet they are creatures of fire’s aftermath, using heat the way surface-folk use wind. Down at the base of the bloated dome, where hairline cracks in the glossy rope-lava breathe water almost too hot to touch, two women kneel on wooden grates, turning bundles of woody shrub-stems in the shimmering, syrupy shimmer of a floor-vent until the timber softens enough to bend into roof-ribs. Above them, channeled through tall chimneys of hollowed stem, the bitter, sulfurous burps of the mountain rise into sealed curing-bells where no one ever puts a face. Inside those sour pockets—visible from below as tarnished, brass-colored mirrors—obsidian-hafting pitch bakes hard around driftwood handles, and strips of silver fish dry into translucent, salty leather without ever meeting sun or smoke.

When the dim olive twilight finally thickens toward true black, signaling that somewhere far above the mountain fog the sun has dropped behind the world’s rim, the town draws inward vertically. Guide-ropes along the outer abyss are unclipped and coiled back so nothing unseen from the deep clefts might follow them home—not that anything large has ever risen out of that young, sterile dark, though a people living over miles of cracking, newly born stone have their own quiet superstitions about what the fissures might still hatch. Woven reed-screens are drawn across the open faces of the cellular rooms and latched with bone pins.

Inside dozens of basalt chambers, families unhook their ankle-weights or clip on sleeping-stones according to preference, gather beneath the trembling dark silver of their ceiling-hearths, and settle into the warm, buoyant hush. For hours the town hangs suspended in its stone honeycomb: dark shapes poised motionless at middle depth, rising singly in their sleep to kiss the trapped air overhead and sinking gently back, while through the empty avenues outside, tiny beads of gas keep detaching from the cooling heart of the mountain—*tick, tick, tick*—climbing past unweathered walls toward a sky no one misses.
