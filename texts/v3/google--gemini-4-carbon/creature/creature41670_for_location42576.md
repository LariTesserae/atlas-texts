Down on the wet stone itself, before one even reaches the height of a sedge cushion, the basin belongs to things that treat the lack of channels as an open highway.

In the thousands of shallow pan-pools, where the water is only an inch deep and tastes faintly of freshly split iron and magnesium, live translucent amphipods the length of a thumbnail. Because there is no mud to burrow into, their carapaces have flattened into wide, sharp-edged shields that match the geometry of the fractured slate beneath them. When a drop of condensed mist falls from an overhang and shatters the surface tension, they do not dart away; they clamp themselves flush against the rock with tiny suction-cupped pleopods, becoming invisible ripples in the stone. Sharing the water with them are colonies of dark maroon flatworms that graze the microscopic film of diatoms growing on the submerged basalt. Because the pools spill over their sharp lips continuously without ever forming a true current, these worms migrate uphill and sideways across the wet, sweating rock faces between basins, riding the millimeter-thin sheen of surface condensation. A whole plateau-wide network of aquatic life moves overland here, sliding across the slick greywacke as if the entire ten-mile expanse were the bottom of a single, infinitely shallow lake.

Where the autonomous brine-skimmers pass, they leave a brief, invisible alteration in this sheet-water: a faint thermal wake, three degrees warmer than the ambient melt, and a slight electrostatic charge lingering in the wet mineral paste along the cracks. Within minutes of a rig’s pneumatic rollers squelching onward, black, wingless stoneflies crawl up out of the fractures to congregate in that invisible lane. Their bodies are coated in velvety, hydrophobic hairs that trap a silver skin of air around them when sudden sheets of drizzle wash over the ledges. Right behind the stoneflies come the mountain plovers, running on yellow, thickly scaled legs that seem impervious to the razor edges of the rock. These birds have learned the exact cadence of the machinery. A plover will stand two inches from the bulging sidewall of an eight-foot rubber roller, waiting for the compliant bladder to deform over a jagged step and squeeze a hidden pocket of rock-flour out of a crevice, exposing the pale, sluggish crane-fly larvae coiled inside.

The camelids—local shepherds down in the cloud-choked valleys two miles below call them *llareta-ghosts*, though no shepherd ever climbs the unweathered passes to see them—are built specifically for a floor that cuts. Their split hooves do not have the soft, spongy pads of lowland llamas; instead, the sole is a dense, fibrous horn that constantly frays and regrows in tough, overlapping plates, gripping wet, glassy basalt like vulcanized felt. Their lower legs are wrapped in tight, coarse guard hair as stiff as wire brushes, shedding ice-water before it can chill the tendons.

Watch a herd of three hundred drift around a stationary core-rig, and their social structure reveals itself through the machine’s thermal map. The heavy inverter housings on the port side radiate a steady, dry thirty degrees Celsius; this prime leeward real estate is claimed exclusively by pregnant females and mothers with week-old calves. The calves are gangly, oversized things born with coats as thick as sheepskin rugs, already able to leap cleanly up a three-foot angular rock step an hour after dropping onto the wet stone. Further out from the hull, where the warmth thins into the pewter fog, the bachelor males stand in ragged semi-circles facing outward, chewing harsh mouthfuls of needle-sedge and black umbilicaria lichen with slow, sideways grinds of their jaws. Their slate-brown wool collects mist in millions of tiny, distinct beads until each animal wears a shifting silver halo. Every quarter hour, one will shudder violently from neck to rump, throwing off a pint of cold water in a single musical *whuff* before going back to staring into the cloud bank.

They have good reason to face outward. The sharp, unweathered geometry of the plateau favors ambush. Because the greywacke breaks along clean, right-angled planes, the basin is a maze of sudden two- and three-foot vertical steps. A predator does not need brush or tall grass here; it only needs to keep its spine below the lip of a ledge.

The pale-furred cats—broad-skulled, heavy-forelimbed creatures intermediate between a snow leopard and a lynx, with tails nearly as thick as their torsos—move through this stepped terrain like water pouring in reverse. Their fur is not white, which would glare against the wet dark stone, nor solid gray, which would silhouette against the pearlescent bright spells; it is a mottled, greasy pewter ticked with charcoal, matching the exact luster of wet basalt reflecting a cloud ceiling. To cross ground where every stone edge can slice leather, their paw pads are recessed deep inside thick, calloused rims of hairless black skin and dense interdigital fur, spreading their weight much like the pneumatic rollers of the rigs.

A pair of these cats—often full brothers or long-mated pairs, since taking a calf away from three hundred sharp-hooved adults requires coordination—will use the machines too, though differently. While the wind hums in the braided aerostat cables above and the drill string sends its rhythmic, low-frequency thrum into the bedrock, the cats press their chins flat to the cold stone half a mile off. They read the drill’s vibration through their jawbones, waiting for the deeper, grinding resonance that means the bit has hit dense diabase. During those twenty minutes, the rig’s hydraulic leveling legs hiss more frequently to compensate for torque, masking the faint *scritch* of heavy claws pulling up over a wet rock lip. They slide from shelf to shelf, bellies brushing through frigid pan-pools without raising a ripple, aiming for the stragglers grazing the lime-green moss seams just outside the battery banks' warm apron.

High above this quiet tension, invisible until they drop below the cloud deck, live the residents of the cables themselves.

The braided umbilical tethers rise four hundred to six hundred feet straight into the murk, humming slightly in the wind shear and warm to the touch from the high-voltage lines bundled inside their Kevlar sheaths. For the nine-foot scavenging birds—huge, bare-necked vultures with ruffs of oily bronze feathers and hooked beaks the color of old bone—the aerostats are the first dry roosts that have ever existed over the center of the basin. Before the rigs arrived, these birds had to commute five miles from the outer rim cliffs, burning precious calories fighting heavy, wet air. Now, four or five of them ride the top envelope of each heavy-lift blimp above the clouds, wings spread to dry in the brilliant, blinding high-altitude sun that never reaches the stone below.

When a calf fails to survive a freezing drizzle, or when a talus-rodent up on the shattered rim is crushed by a frost-wedged block calving off the slope, the scavengers do not circle—circling in dense fog among sharp ridges is fatal. Instead, they drop down the tether line itself, folding their massive wings into steep triangles and whistling straight down alongside the black cable like elevator cars, braking hard with a thunderous *whump* of primary feathers fifty feet above the rig’s roof. Smaller birds come down the cables too: tiny, mouse-gray wrens and alpine accentors that ride the warm updrafts off the rig's exhaust stacks, plucking wind-blown spiders and frozen aphids out of the upper shear where the aerostat turbines spin, carrying high-sky protein down into a world of raw mineral.

Up on the perimeter talus, where the basin tilts sharply into the surrounding peaks, the long-tailed rodents—chinchilla-kin the size of small badgers, with ears folded like crumpled black felt—keep the plateau’s only permanent architecture. Because there is no soil to dig warrens, they live down in the deep, clean voids between freshly fallen ten-ton blocks of basalt. Inside those dark, frost-lined chambers, generations of them have wedged chewed sedge-stems, shed camelid wool blown across the basin, and dried black lichen into dense, fermenting mattresses three feet thick. These middens generate their own slight compost heat, staying forty degrees warmer than the screaming night winds outside. During the brief pearlescent brightenings at midday, whole families emerge onto the razor-edged blocks to groom, curling their plumed tails under their rumps like cushioned stools so their hindquarters never touch the heat-stealing stone. Their faint, ventriloquial whistles bounce strangely off the flat, unweathered facets of the rock, making each call sound as if it comes from three directions at once.

And then there is the rarest resident of the basin, though whether he truly *lives* here is a matter of definition.

Once every six or seven weeks, a single maintenance walker comes over the jagged eastern pass—not a fifty-ton rig, just a four-legged, mule-sized cargo frame picking its way silently over the stepped ledges, preceded by one person on foot.

She—or he; there are only four technicians assigned to the entire highland sector, rotating out of a pressurised station two thousand meters down—wears wide, articulated snowshoe-crampons soled in replaceable sacrificial blocks of ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene, because steel spikes skate dangerously on unweathered basalt and leather boots are ribbons by noon. Over an electrically heated undersuit, the technician wears heavy, unbleached wool greased with lanolin that smells remarkably like the wet camelids.

To the animals of the plateau, a solitary human walking at two miles an hour, smelling of grease and ozone, never shouting, never firing a rifle, is simply a very small, oddly vertical rig that forgot its aerostat.

When the technician stops beside a brine-skimmer to swap out a fouled optical sensor or inspect a frayed umbilical clamp, she has to gently shove the resting camelid mothers aside with her hip to reach the access panel. They do not bolt; they merely grumble deep in their chests, fold their front knees out from under the panel door, and settle back down two feet away, resting their damp chins on her tool bag while she works. Mountain plovers hop directly over her polyethylene soles to peck condensed droplets off her wrenches. High overhead, invisible in the pewter glare, the cable goes taut and hums a half-step higher as an upper-level gust hits the blimp; through her thick gloves, holding the steel hull, she feels both the wind half a thousand feet up in the sun and the drill bit grinding cleanly through dark, ancient rock two hundred feet beneath the wet, unmarked floor.

When she closes the latch, wipes the mist from her visor, and walks away into the fog behind her clicking four-legged mule, the camelids shift smoothly back into the warm hollow her body left against the steel. Five minutes later, the drizzle thickens again, washing the microscopic white shavings of her boot-soles off the ledges and carrying them down from pan-pool to pan-pool, mingling tiny, harmless curls of polymer with the fresh grains of feldspar drifting toward the unborn lake.
