To find the makers of the root-knots, you cannot walk toward them. Walking in this place—unless you have spent a lifetime learning the distribution of weight—is a loud, percussive event. Every careless step compresses the saturated mat, sending a hydraulic pulse radiating outward through the interconnected stolons beneath the surface. Anything with a belly pressed to the floor within fifty paces feels the displacement of water in its own ribs. If you crash through the tiers looking for them, you will find only warm, empty domes, weirs slowly overflowing, and the faint, musky scent of crushed mint-moss lingering in the fog where someone stood three minutes ago.

You have to sit on a dry hummock until your own weight settles into the sponge and the root-bladders stop gurgling beneath you. You wait until the calf-sized grazers resume their wet, sighing slide up the trunks. And then, usually when the pewter afternoon begins to bruise toward plum, a patch of charcoal-colored shadow detaches itself from a buttressed column ten feet away and resolves into a person.

They are built for weight distribution and dimness. Squat, broad-hipped, and remarkably dense in the torso, they stand perhaps four and a half feet tall, though they rarely pull themselves up to their full height, preferring a fluid, low-slung crouch that keeps their heads safely beneath the lowest leaf-platters. Their feet are extraordinary: wide as dinner plates, completely flat, with five long, fleshy toes splayed permanently outward like the pads of a water-strider, webbed halfway to the tips with tough, pale skin. When they step, the foot rolls down from the heel to the splayed toes with slow, deliberate pressure, squeezing the sponge-mat without ever breaking its surface tension or causing it to suck at the ankle.

Their skin is smooth, hairless, and intensely matte—the color of steeped bark, wet slate, or dark mushroom-cap, completely devoid of oily shine so it catches none of the scattered pearl light. Across their shoulders, spines, and the crowns of their skulls grows a short, dense nap—not quite hair, and not quite symbiotic moss, though the two are hopelessly entangled. It looks and feels like damp velvet, collecting pearlescent beads of fog that roll down grooves along their collarbones, deliberately channeled away from their faces. 

When one turns to look at you, the eyes are startling. They have almost no visible sclera, just immense, liquid pools the color of dark amber, shielded not by eyelashes—which would only clump with mist—but by heavy, projecting brow-ridges of soft cartilage that act as gutters, and thick, translucent third eyelids that sweep horizontally across the cornea with a slow, wet *click* no louder than two fingertips touching. Their noses are flat, broad rosettes of soft flesh with muscular nostrils that flare wide in the thick air and seal completely shut when they slip beneath the tea-colored sheet-waters.

They do not speak into the air. In a landscape that swallows high frequencies within three paces, shouting is a waste of calories, and whispering requires pressing mouths directly against ears. Instead, if there are two or three of them together—and there are rarely more than five or six in one place—you will feel their conversation before you notice it. 

One will shift her weight, drop a broad, calloused palm against the turgid flank of a hose-trunk, and flex her throat. Inside their chests sits an enlarged, heavily muscled vocal sac resting directly against the sternum. They drop their chins, sealing the resonant chamber against their collarbones, and produce deep, sub-audible *thrums*—short bursts of bass vibration that travel down their thick forearms and directly into the vascular plumbing of the forest. Thirty yards away, another crouches, resting two long toes lightly against an exposed surface-stolon, reading the cadence through the bones of his ankle. For closer work, when they are huddled inside one of their living domes, they touch foreheads or press three fingers to one another’s throats, speaking in soft, labial hums and throat-clicks that never leave the skin between them.

Because there is no stone to flake, no ore to smelt, and no dry wood to burn, they possess nothing sharp that did not first belong to an animal, and nothing hard that was not cured by chemistry. 

Look closely at the belt woven from pale, braided root-fibers slung low over a builder’s hips. Tucked into its folds are pouches made from the tough, translucent stomachs of web-drifters, cured in tannin-rich marsh water until they turn into supple amber leather. Inside are their tools: curved, serrated lancets taken from the radial jaws of the marsh-lurkers; hollow needles made from the calcified mouthparts of giant snails; and small, stoppered gourds containing pastes of breathtaking potency. 

They are master biochemists, though their laboratory is entirely somatic and intuitive, passed down through generations of tasting and scarring. In a world that is essentially one vast, sluggish circulatory system, power belongs to whoever understands hormones, galls, and grafts. 

Watch an elder at work on a young shelter-cluster. He does not chop; chopping invites rot in this breathless, warm damp, and worse, a sudden traumatic wound causes the surrounding root-mat to panic, shunting defensive, bitter latex into the immediate area and spoiling the local water-pockets for months. Instead, he scores a hairline incision into the fleshy bark of a sapling with a jaw-lancet, wiping away the bead of dark sap with his thumb. From a gourd at his hip, he takes a dab of grey unguent—rendered from the crushed glands of bark-grazers mixed with concentrated fungal spores—and rubs it gently into the cut. 

The paste mimics the plant’s own growth-auxins. Over the next six months, the wounded tissue will not scar shut; it will swell outward, curving purposefully toward its neighbor. In three years, someone else—his niece, perhaps, or an unrelated traveler walking the pale-pressed paths—will pass this way, see the reaching arms of tissue nearly touching, scrape both clean, and bind them together with living tendrils that tighten as they grow. Twenty years later, a family will step out of the heavy plum twilight into a dry, warm room whose walls softly pulse with sap.

Because everything takes decades to finish, they have no concept of land ownership. You cannot own a house that takes forty years to close its roof when your life is spent walking a slow, three-hundred-mile circuit across the sponge. What they possess instead is a vast, distributed etiquette of maintenance. 

To arrive at a dome and leave without pruning back the encroaching interior pith, or without clearing the silt-slime from the root-weirs outside, is the closest thing they have to a cardinal sin. Those forgotten gourd-basins you saw hanging on the horn-galls inside the empty shelter were not abandoned; they were left behind for the nextcomers, and the velvet mold coating their insides is likely a deliberate starter-culture—a mild, nutty-tasting antibiotic scum that only needs to be steeped in warm pitcher-leaf water to make a restorative broth after a long day’s walk.

Their diet is slow, rich, and entirely cold. Without fire, cooking is done by fermentation and acid. Along the stepped root-terraces—which are not merely water-traps, but fishless aquaculture ponds—they cultivate dense beds of starchy, submerged tubers and fat, blind white grubs that feed on decaying cellulose. They harvest the fist-sized velvet snails, shucking them cleanly with a twist of a bone-spatula, and drop the pale meats into hanging bladders filled with acidic fluid tapped from carnivorous pitcher-leaves. In three days of hanging in the warm broth-air, the tough snail-foot dissolves into a savory, custard-thick curd. They scrape sweet, clear gum from deliberately irritated tree-trunks, and gather the heavy, oily seeds of the high oxblood canopies when they drop with dull *thuds* into the moss.

They do not fear much on the surface. The radial-jawed things in the marshes are ambush predators of small, careless game; a person walking with proper weight-roll never triggers their strike-reflex, and if one must wade across a broad sheet-marsh, a drop of bitter root-alkaloid squeezed from a pouch into the water ahead makes the lurkers curl defensively into the moss. 

Their true reverence—and their wariness—is reserved for the deep below.

They know there is no stone beneath them. Their mythology has no underworld of rock or magma; instead, they know the truth: that the world goes down into older and older dark, into drowned galleries of ancient timber-flesh where oxygen vanishes and strange, slow things move through the black water. When that low, sub-audible hum vibrates up through the soles of their feet—the shifting of the massive subterranean fauna miles or yards beneath the surface mat—every person within range stops dead. They drop into a full crouch, laying both palms flat against the damp floor, amber eyes half-shut, third eyelids clicking slowly across, listening with their whole skeletons.

Sometimes, the vibration is merely a deep-borer turning in its sleep, clearing a blocked hydraulic channel far down in the anaerobic leather-mats, which is good; it keeps the surface marshes from flooding too deeply. Other times, the hum has an erratic, tearing rhythm. That means a sink-rot has opened below—a rare, catastrophic collapse where an ancient gas pocket gives way, causing half an acre of the surface world to suddenly sigh and drop twenty feet into warm, suffocating black water that smells of sulfur and a million years of drowned leaves. When they feel that rhythm in their sternums, they tie a specific, frantic knot into the nearest aerial roots—three sharp hitches tucked backward—and reroute the path wide around the trembling ground for the next century.

When one of them dies, there is no digging of graves; if you dig three feet down, the hole fills immediately with warm, tea-brown water, and cutting the stolon-braid hurts the local shelter-trees. Instead, they carry the body out to the margins of the great open marshes, where the mist hangs thick and pearl-gray over dozens of miles of flat dark. 

There, where old, senescent hose-trunks lean low over the saturated mat before finally surrendering their turgor pressure to collapse into the peat, they find a hollow between the buttresses. They wash the body in pitcher-water, strip off the belt of jaw-lancets and gourds to be divided among the young, and lay the dead one curled on their side directly upon a knotted nexus of pale surface-roots. Before walking away, the mourners score small, shallow nicks into the flanks of the surrounding roots and rub a final paste into the cuts—not the hormone that makes domes curve upward, nor the one that shunts water aside, but a rich, welcoming compound that tells the forest *here is nitrogen; here is salt; here is bone-calcium, rarest of things.*

Within weeks, pale, rubbery stolons creep delicately over the slate-dark skin, braiding across the broad hips and threading gently between the splayed, webbed toes. Within five years, there is only a smooth, warm hummock of oxblood moss rising slightly dryer than the marsh around it, pumping that scarce, precious calcium outward for miles through the shared veins of the plateau—and rising from its center, two young vascular stalks, already bound together at the crown by someone who passed by last month, beginning the slow, forty-year bend into a roof for strangers not yet born.
