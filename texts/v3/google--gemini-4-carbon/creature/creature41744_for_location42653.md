They are barrel-chested, broad-ribbed people, built short and heavy through the trunk to hold lungs large enough for the thin air, though they rarely spend that breath on hurry. A person here moves the way a heavy vine grows: testing the purchase first, shifting weight onto a smoothed hip or a calloused palm before lifting a foot, folding easily into gaps that look too narrow for shoulders. Their skin is the matte, slightly powdery color of old gourd-rind or cured leather, dusted in the creases of the knuckles, neck, and eyelids with the fine mineral silt that drifts down from the purple sky and settles into the natural oils of the body. Because water is never wasted on washing, they clean themselves the way the cliff-birds preen—rubbing their limbs with dry pads of beard-lichen until the dead skin and dust roll off in gray crumbs for the beetles, then slicking their hides with rendered lizard-tallow that smells faintly of warm stone and crushed sage.

You notice their eyes and their feet first. After a thousand generations under overlapping wicker and bloated leaves, in a twilight that never brightens past the color of a fresh bruise, their irises have widened until only a thin ring of yellowed sclera shows at the corners, dark and liquid and slow to blink. Their bare soles—sandals are mostly for the rough bark of the outer rim or the hottest vent-stones—are wide, splayed, and leathery, with thick, prehensile big toes capable of hooking a polished rung or feeling out the sleepy spine of a vent-reptile in the pitch dark beneath a gallery without crushing it. Even the oldest among them have knees and hips like oiled hinges. Chairs do not exist; there is no room for furniture with legs. People rest folded into the dished hollows of thresholds, wedged into the forks of hawser-vines, or leaning three-deep against one another along a low stone coping, sharing body heat and shoulder-weight as unselfconsciously as the reptiles piled by the vents.

To be startled here is considered a kind of sickness, or a terrible rudeness. A sudden jerk of an elbow in a three-foot alley knocks a tallow-lamp from its horn hook, spills precious wax, bruises a neighbor, or sends a cascade of blind beetles raining down into someone’s mortar. Children learn before they can speak to move with soft, heavy deliberation. Babies are rarely set down—there is nowhere flat that isn't a thoroughfare or a lizard-heap—so they live strapped in rawhide slings against a parent’s or a sibling’s ribs, breathing the sour-sweet smell of chewed tuber and warm skin, their small fingers tangling immediately into the heavy, greased braids of whoever carries them. They seldom cry loudly; a sharp wail brings three or four thumbs—mother’s, uncle’s, neighbor’s—immediately to their mouths, offering a plug of sweet, bruised succulent pith to suck.

Speech matches the architecture. Because there is no distance across which anyone ever needs to shout, and because the hollow already hums with the dry, saw-toothed rasp of ten thousand insects and the clacking of hooked beaks, high or sharp voices get lost in the mid-range din. People pitch their voices down into the chest, speaking in a low, resonant murmur that travels through wood, stone, and bone as much as air. Two weavers sitting back-to-back on a slung wicker bridge will carry on an hour’s conversation felt partly through their shoulder blades. Much of their talk isn't vocal at all: a double-tap of knuckles against a passing hip to say *coming through behind you*; a thumb drawn across a neighbor's forearm to ask for the flint knife; a slight shift of the chin toward the upper rafters to warn that a heavy cliff-bird is shifting its roost and about to drop dry, chalky guano.

Privacy is an interior state, entirely divorced from space. Everyone hears the grinding of everyone else’s teeth at night, the slow hitch of coupling behind a hanging mat of gray lichen, the wet rattle of an old man’s lungs two tiers down, the rhythmic *thump-scrape* of a neighbor hollowing a gourd six inches from one’s own sleeping head. When someone wants to be alone—grieving, angry, or simply full to the brim with other people—they turn their face into the smooth curve of a boulder wall or pull a woven cowl down past their nose. Once a person turns to the stone, they are gone. You may still have to step over their calves to reach the ladder, or lean your basket briefly against their hip while readjusting a strap, and you do so without apology or acknowledgment, treating them with the exact, gentle indifference shown to a warm root. To speak to a person facing the rock is unthinkable.

Their whole morality is a hydrology of solids. Nothing wet may ever meet the open air for long. When a succulent leaf is sliced open, the knife-wielder’s mouth or a waiting clay vessel is already pressed close to the cut to catch the cool, mucilaginous bead before the thin high-altitude air can drink it. Meals are quiet, inward affairs: bundles of root-flesh and white moth-grubs steamed inside stitched reptile bladders over the geothermal pores, untied only beneath a leather eating-cloth draped across the diners' laps, hands working by touch beneath the hide to pass warm, damp morsels straight from pouch to palm to mouth. Spitting is an obscenity; weeping is a luxury indulged face-down into a wad of absorbent moss that will afterward be tucked around the roots of a prized nursery-shrub. Even blood from a slipped bone-chisel is immediately licked clean by the injured worker or wiped onto a dry biscuit of beetle-meal so the salt and damp aren't cheated out of the bowl.

Nothing is owned outright except what hangs from one’s belt, because nothing in the hollow stands on its own. Your ceiling is the floor where the net-makers sleep; the hawser-vine anchoring their gallery grows out of a crevice behind the potter’s wall, which leans its whole unmortared weight against the dead ironwood trunk that supports your doorway. The most respected figures in the settlement are not chiefs or priests, for there is no war and no weather to pray against, but the *listeners*—usually older women and men whose eyesight has clouded over completely in the gloom, leaving their fingers and ears preternaturally sharp. They spend their days moving inchmeal through the under-scaffolds and deep root-tangles, pressing palms and cheekbones to timber posts, dry-stone curves, and tensioned rawhide lashings. They feel for the microscopic shift in a boulder pile settled over centuries, the dry rot inside an ironwood beam, or the faint slackening of a vine whose suction pads are losing their resin high on the smooth granite lip. When a listener taps a stone three times with a horn rod, families quietly unhook their gourds, roll up their sleeping mats, and shift two tiers over for a month while five dozen hands patiently unstack, re-chink with chewed lichen-paste, and resettle the curve of the house.

At the very rim of the hollow, where the tangled brush thins out and the smooth, elephant-gray granite rolls over into the vertical drop, a few people can always be found sitting during the waking hours—not guards, since nothing has ever climbed out of that slate-colored sea of dust, and nothing could find purchase on the scoured stone if it tried. Mostly it is the hook-flingers and silt-harvesters, tending long curtains of sticky, resin-smeared fiber hung just over the lip to comb mineral dust and stray, wind-blown spores out of the upper atmosphere, or scraping old lichen off the polished shoulders of the spire with bone blades, roped around the waist to thick vines behind them.

They sit on the smooth brink with their broad legs dangling over vertical miles of silent, yellow-purple haze, squinting slightly against the vast, empty dullness of the sky. They never stay out long. The thin air beyond the lip steals the damp from the corners of their eyes and cracks their lips within an hour, and the silence out there—huge, dry, without a single clicking beetle or creaking rope or breathing neighbor—makes their chests ache with a strange, weightless vertigo. Soon enough they haul in their lines, unclip their rawhide tethers, and slide backward over the warm stone lip, dropping back down into the dim, musky, chattering thicket, letting their shoulders bump once more against leaf and wall and warm human flank, sinking back into the single slow body that keeps them alive.
