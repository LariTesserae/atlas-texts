You will walk along one of the causeways for hours, guiding yourself by the slight raised lip of the basalt curbing against the side of your boot, before you realize that the mound of smooth, dark stone beside the distillation manifold is breathing.

Wait long enough—twenty minutes, forty—and the mound shifts. There is a dry, silken whisper, like a handful of fine graphite powder sliding down a glass pane, as a limb uncurls.

They are the Calibrators, though there is no one left to use a name, and they themselves do not speak. If they are the descendants of the original operators, ten millennia of warm, waterless altitude have pared away everything soft, quick, or wet about them, leaving behind a creature that has met its own machinery halfway. If they were brought here, or grown in the vats beneath the ziggurats specifically to tend the closed loops, the distinction has long since smoothed over, like the edges of the hills. 

Standing upright—which it does rarely, and with the slow, hydraulic patience of a crane rising—one of them is taller than a human, though far narrower in the beam, built to slip sideways between closely ranked condenser tubes. Its chest is tremendous, a deep, keeled barrel of overlapping bone plates housing lungs vast enough to harvest oxygen from the starved mountain air. Those lungs work like forged bellows: a slow, five-minute draw inward that makes no sound at all in the thin atmosphere, a long stillness while the dark, copper-heavy blood strips the gas, and a five-minute, warmth-shedding sigh. 

Nothing can afford to evaporate here. A wet eye would dry to a salt-cake in an afternoon; moist skin would crack and bleed until the body seized. So the Calibrator is sealed like one of the viaduct pipes. Its hide is a dense, flexible vulcanite—polymerized keratin infused with the heavy metallic dust of the basins, matte-black and cool to the touch, overlapping at the joints in greaseless, articulated pleats that shed a microscopic puff of dark talc when they bend. Where a face might have been, there is a smooth, slightly concave shield of pale, horn-like material, faintly translucent over a dense mesh of pit-organs and magnetic ganglia. 

To a Calibrator, the plateau is not dark. It is a vast, crisp landscape of gradients. It perceives the cherry-red expansion grates as roaring bonfires to be skirted; it reads the health of a buried flywheel half a mile down by the exact pitch and phase of the sub-audible *thrum* rising through the calloused, broad-padded soles of its feet. When it tilts its horn-shield toward an overhead viaduct, it sees the magnetic bloom of the high-tension lines as shifting, braided auroras, and it knows, by a slight sourness in the induction field, whether a transformer four miles east is running two degrees too hot.

Watch one at a junction box. 

It has three arms—two broad, heavy-levered limbs set wide at the shoulders for torque, and a third, slender and multi-jointed, nested in a groove down the center of its sternum. The heavy hands do not have fingers so much as opposed, horn-sheathed cams, shaped over evolutionary ages—or engineered from the start—to match the octagonal iron nuts and recessed valve-wheels of the plateau’s architecture. 

It fits both heavy hands over a pressure-relief wheel that has not been turned since three centuries ago, when the last slight settling of the eastern basin altered the pipe’s pitch by a tenth of a millimeter. The creature does not strain, sweat, or grunt. Sweat is death; haste is heat-stroke. Instead, it locks the ratchets of its knees into the fitted paving stones, lets its body weight settle, and applies a continuous, dead-steady forty pounds of pressure. 

For two hours, nothing happens. A six-legged hound-thing pads past in the dark, pauses, presses its pale heat-plate briefly against the Calibrator’s ankle to read its dull warmth, and pads on, recognizing it as part of the plumbing. Overhead, one of the brittle black umbrella-radials twitches a single rib. 

At the start of the third hour, the dry-lubricated threads inside the iron housing yield with a single, sharp *tick*. The wheel rotates four degrees. Behind the thick quartz sight-glass nearby, the pinpoint of amber dims by a fraction, the heavy gas inside shunting down a secondary bypass, relieving a minuscule strain on a seam two miles away. 

From its chest-groove, the Calibrator unfolds its slender third arm. The tip of this limb splits into a bundle of fine, stiff bristles, faintly electrostatic, and a flat bone spatula. Gently, with an almost tender thoroughness, it scrapes away the tiny rime of yellow sulfur-salt and heavy-gas condensate that wept from the valve stem during the turn—before a hound can smell it from a mile off—and lifts the spatula to a narrow, valved slit beneath its chin. 

This is how they drink, and how they eat. Inside them, there is no red furnace of mammalian metabolism; they run on a slow, dry, sulfur-reducing chemistry, hoarding every hydrogen atom they lick from hydrocarbon seeps and metallic hydrates for a hundred years, recycling their own internal moisture through labyrinthine nasal condensers so efficient that their five-minute exhalations are as bone-dry as the ambient air. What little waste they produce is extruded once every few decades: a single, smooth pellet of vitrified mineral slag, dropped neatly at the margin of a dust-basin, indistinguishable from pebbles ground smooth before the city was laid.

Are there minds behind those pale horn shields? 

If you stood directly before one, breathing hard in your thick coat, shining an electric torch onto its dark, graphite-dusted shoulders, it would stop. The pale concavity of its head would angle toward you—blind to your white beam, which means nothing to it, but startled, perhaps even blinded, by the sudden, frantic flare of wet ninety-eight-degree heat pouring off your skin, the wild, fluttering hammer of your heart, the wasteful geyser of precious water vapor jetting from your mouth ten times a minute. To a Calibrator, a human being would seem less like a living creature and more like a ruptured high-pressure pipe—a catastrophic leak tearing itself apart in fast-forward.

It might reach out with its delicate third arm, bristles splayed to taste the bizarre, damp static around you, hovering an inch from your cheek without touching, wondering what manner of broken machine you are, and where your housing is, and how to turn your valve down before you burn yourself to ash.

Yet they are not mere insects of the ironwork. Follow one—if you have the patience to walk at half a mile an hour for three days—when no valves require turning. 

Out where the paved causeways end and the smooth, glassy swells of the vitrified bedrock roll off toward the unseen drop of the plateau’s edge, there is a shallow basin filled knee-deep with the impalpable mineral flour that sits like dark smoke in the hollows. Four of them are gathered there in the absolute black. 

They stand in a wide circle, twenty paces apart, ankle-deep in the frictionless dust. None of them touch. None of them make a sound in the thin air. Instead, one raises a heavy foot and brings its calloused heel down against the buried bedrock beneath the flour—not hard, just a measured, dull *thud* that travels outward through the stone. 

Six seconds later, the one opposite answers with two slow taps of a horn-cam against its own keeled ribs, sending a dry, low resonance shivering through the ground. A third leans its chest against an abandoned, unpiped stanchion—an ancient surveying marker, perhaps—and sets the iron humming at five cycles per second, a vibration so low it is only a pressure behind the teeth.

They will stand like that for a week, trading chords of bedrock-tremor, syncing their five-minute breaths until all four vast ribcages rise and fall as a single bellows, harmonizing with the deep, underground *thrum* of the vacuum flywheels beneath their feet. Whether this is a shift-change report—passing along the memorized pressure-map of ten thousand pipes, noting which seals will need turning in four hundred years—or whether it is a liturgy, or simply what passes for companionship among things built to outlast rust, is impossible to untangle. In a closed loop, maintenance and devotion are the same motion.

Beneath the city, deeper than the causeways ever go, there are other dependents of this slow economy. 

In the sealed service galleries directly above the geothermal flywheel vaults, where the rising heat pushes the air closer to the warmth of blood and the stone vibrates continuously, live the Felts. They are not animals at all, nor fungi, though they look like vast, grey-brown carpets of sheared wool draped over the warm iron casings of the induction coils. Touch one and it feels like dry, warm asbestos. They are colonial matrices of piezoelectric silicate threads and extremophile archaea, feeding directly off the mechanical vibration of the flywheels—converting the relentless microscopic shudder of the housings into slow chemical bonds. 

The Calibrators tend the Felts, too. When a colony grows too thick over a century, insulating a coil that needs to shed its heat into the gallery, a Calibrator descends the vertical iron rungs in the dark, peels back a yard-wide strip of the grey mat with its bone spatula, rolls it neatly like a bolt of heavy cloth, and carries it three miles up and out into the avenues, laying it down beside a cold viaduct pylon where the extra insulation will keep a heavy-gas line from condensing too early. Nothing is killed; it is simply relocated to balance the thermal ledger. Even the six-legged hounds have their place in the ledger: when one finally stills—curled against a warm transformer after two hundred years of licking flanges, its heart slowing from four beats a minute to one, to none—it does not rot. There are no bacteria in the dry air to spoil it. Over decades, its felt-like hide simply cures into stone-hard leather, and eventually a passing Calibrator will lift the desiccated, lightweight husk, carry it to an intake hopper behind one of the stepped ziggurats, and return its hoarded sulfur, iron, and trace hydrogen to the fractional distillation towers.

And deep within the stepped ziggurats themselves—buildings whose riveted iron doors are not locked, merely heavy enough to require two Calibrators leaning in unison for an hour to swing ajar—there is something else.

If you went inside, past the dry airlocks, down ramps worn subtly concave in the exact width of a Calibrator’s stride, the air grows denser, pressurized slightly by bleed-off from the gas domes, heavy with argon and neon that make your voice, if you dared speak, drop to a strange, metallic baritone. Here there are no red thermal grates, only row upon row of immense, horizontal cylinders of fused basalt, warm as freshly baked bread, stacked to a ceiling lost in the dark. 

Along the flanks of each cylinder, tiny static-blue sparks tick back and forth across hair-thin gaps in platinum combs—once a minute, once every ten minutes, a glacial, solid-state computation running without silicon or glass, immune to radiation and time. 

Sometimes an old Calibrator—one whose vulcanite pleats have grown stiff with mineral impregnation, whose horn-shield is crazed with microscopic stress lines from five thousand years of reading magnetic fields—comes down the ramp alone. It walks down the aisle between the warm basalt cylinders until it finds an empty stone cradle molded into the floor between two platinum-comb arrays. It lies down on its back, folds its two heavy torque-arms across its keeled chest, tucks its delicate bristle-arm neatly into its sternum groove, and presses the back of its skull against a pair of smooth copper contacts worn bright by predecessors.

The ticking of the blue sparks speeds up, briefly—twice a minute, three times—as five millennia of tactile memory, every valve-turn, every subtle shift in the plateau’s bedrock gradient, every faint thinning of an iron wall three miles west, drains slowly out of the magnetic ganglia of its head and into the vast, dark ledger of the ziggurat. 

It does not get up again. Its five-minute breaths stretch to ten, to thirty, until its lungs equalize with the argon-heavy room and stop. In time, younger ones—rising fully formed and quiet from warm, dry mineral-synthesis cradles at the far end of the hall, their pleats fresh and dark and dusted with new graphite—will come down the aisle, take up the dry, unrotted frame of their elder to return its rare earths to the hoppers, press their smooth, blank horn-shields for a day and a night against the copper contacts to receive their sector of the map, and climb the ramps up into the thin, warm night.

Outside, ten miles away across the velvet-black tableland, a counterweight reaches the bottom of its shaft. The muted *clank* shivers through the vitrified hills, passes beneath the dust-basins, sets the umbrella-things twitching a single black rib beneath the high trestles, and registers in the soles of a solitary figure walking the dark causeway—who pauses for ten minutes to listen with its feet, finds the interval true to the second, and walks on toward a tiny smear of cherry-red light half a day ahead.
