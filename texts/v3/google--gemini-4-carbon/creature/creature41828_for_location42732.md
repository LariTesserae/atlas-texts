If you stand entirely still in the middle of one of the sunken plazas, letting your breath slow until it drops beneath the metallic static of the frogs, you will eventually feel a vibration through the soles of your boots that does not belong to the insects or the monkeys. It is a single, low-frequency thump—heavy, dull, and brief—traveling laterally through fifty yards of dry-laid basalt. Wait ten minutes, and you might feel another, slightly closer, followed by the faint, stony *clack* of a three-hundred-pound block settling a fraction of a millimeter into a neighboring joint.

Something is down in the sub-masonry, adjusting the weight.

To understand what lives here—what *truly* commands the geometry of the basin, rather than merely camping on its surfaces—you have to consider what lies beneath the flagstones. A valley floor cannot be leveled into stepped terraces without filling the natural ravines and gullies of the mountain. Beneath the visible plazas and causeways lies a colossal, three-dimensional honeycomb of dry-stone cribwork, boulder-fill, vaulted drainage conduits that never carried river water, and deep, unmortared foundation shafts dropping thirty, forty, sometimes eighty feet down to the buried, forgotten skin of the massif. 

Down there, the acoustic roar of the surface is muffled to a deep, submarine hum. It is pitch-black, buffered from even the bruised noon twilight, and entirely free of leaf litter, though the pale, cable-thick taproots of the strangler figs still probe downward through the dark like blind pythons searching for mineral sweat. 

This interstitial labyrinth is the primary highway of the basin’s true architects—or rather, what has become of them, or what replaced them so seamlessly that the distinction no longer matters.

If you shine a weak, red-filtered lamp down a collapsed cistern shaft, or sit for six hours beside one of the square, lintel-topped vents at the base of a retaining wall, you will first smell them. Behind the sharp formic tang of the leafcutters and the sweet rot of dropped figs, there is an odor like bruised ginger root, wet slate, and old lanolin. 

Then, a piece of the mossy terrace wall will detach itself and lever upward.

At first glance, emerging into the green-black gloom of a causeway, the creature looks like an evolutionary collision between a giant, short-faced treecreeper, a sloth, and a Mason’s pry-bar—until you look at the shoulders and the wrists, and realize with a slow, cold prickle of the scalp that the blueprint is primate, or once was, very long ago, before the stone became the whole world.

They are built low and immensely wide, weighing perhaps ninety or a hundred pounds, though nearly all of that mass is concentrated in dense bone, thick-walled ribcages, and cable-like musculature wrapped around the scapulae and forearms. They move on all fours with a silent, rolling, cross-lateral gait, their knuckles never touching the stone; instead, they walk on the broad, calloused pads of turned-out palms and shortened, flat-soled feet, spreading their weight so evenly that they do not crush the snail shells in the leaf mold. 

Their pelage is not really fur anymore, not in this permanent, dripping greenhouse. It is a dense, short, water-shedding coat of stiff, hollow guard hairs coated in a waxy sebaceous grease, tinted the exact mottled charcoal-and-olive of wet granite colonized by crustose lichen. Along their backs and broad, sloped shoulders, slow-growing films of real liverwort and microscopic green algae actually root in the grooves of the coarse hair, rising and falling slightly as they breathe. If one stops moving against a stair riser, your eye slides right over it; it becomes a weathered relief of a crouching figure, worn round by centuries of condensation.

Look at the head, and the strangeness deepens. In a world where the air is already deafeningly loud and the light is perpetually dead, big external ears and wide nocturnal eyes would only be liabilities—delicate membranes to be torn by masonry edges, eardrums to be blown out by the twenty-four-hour buzz-saw of ten million cicadas and tree frogs. Instead, their ear pinnae have shrunk to tight, muscular valves buried in the cheek-fur, capable of clamping completely shut against the surface din. Their eyes are small, dark, recessed beneath heavy, shelf-like brows of bone that protect them from falling grit and scraping roots, seeing mostly in slow shifts of grey contrast and the faint bioluminescence of rotting wood.

They do not listen to the air. They listen to the stone.

Watch one pause halfway up a bowed flight of temple stairs. It lowers its heavy, blunt-muzzled jaw until its chin rests flat against a dressed block of basalt. The lower jawbone is extraordinarily thick, flared at the base and cushioned by a specialized pad of dense, oil-filled fat—remarkably like the acoustic jaw-fat of a toothed whale, evolved independently here in the high tropics to conduct solid-borne vibrations straight to the inner ear. 

Resting its chin on the step, the creature is reading the valley. Through the continuous, dry-laid lattice of fitted granite and basalt, it feels the crisp, dry scuttling of pale land crabs three terraces down; it feels the slow, hydraulic groan of a mountain mahogany’s root-wedge swelling after last night’s cloud-bath; it feels the heavy, padded footfall of a mountain paca crossing a courtyard two hundred yards away; and, right now, it feels the rhythmic, double-valved thump of your heart vibrating down through your legs, through your boot rubber, and into the plaza floor.

It does not panic. Nothing here panics. In an ecosystem packed this tightly, sudden flight only wastes calories and triggers cascades of snapping vines and territorial shrieks. 

Instead, it raises its head, opens its nostrils—wide, fleshy, partitioned slits that flare silently to sample the motionless air—and looks in your direction without quite looking *at* you. Then you see its hands.

They are astonishing instruments, hairless from the mid-forearm down, covered in dark, pebbled skin as tough as vulcanized rubber. Each hand possesses two opposing thumbs—one true thumb and a massively enlarged, ossified radial sesamoid bone on the outer wrist—framing three thick, spatulate middle fingers tipped not with claws, but with flat, chisel-hard nails of dark horn. The fingers are slightly flattened laterally, able to slip effortlessly into a quarter-inch dry-stone joint where mortar was never laid.

What they *do* with those hands is the reason the valley has not slid off the mountain.

The original builders—whoever hauled these millions of blocks without iron or mortar centuries ago—left behind a masterpiece of static compression, a puzzle held together by gravity. When the triple-canopy forest grew over it, the roots should have torn it apart within three hundred years, prying every terrace loose until landslides peeled the masonry down into the gorges below. Where a wall slumped two centuries ago, vines sutured it, yes—but vines rot, and figs die, and giant laurels topple when their crowns grow too heavy with bromeliads. Left strictly to botany, the geometry would have dissolved into chaotic rubble long ago.

The stone-dwellers do not let it dissolve. They farm the architecture.

Follow one—if you have the patience to move at a yard per minute through the submarine gloom—as it leaves the causeway and slips behind a curtain of aerial roots along a twelve-foot retaining wall. Halfway down the wall, a strangler fig’s woody tendril has thickened inside a seam between two granite headers, pushing the upper block outward by three inches, threatening to spill a forty-square-foot section of the terrace above—and all the rich, damp leaf-mold, earthworms, millipedes, and frog-filled bromeliads perched upon it.

Two more of the creatures are already there, clinging effortlessly to the vertical face of the damp stone, their split-thumbed hands wedged into joints, their short, thick legs braced on inch-wide ledges. They do not speak to one another through the air; the ringing chorus of the thumbnail frogs is ninety decibels right beside their heads. Instead, one taps the knuckle of its wrist twice against the granite—*tok, tok*—a crisp percussion that travels cleanly through the wall into the palms and chins of the others.

Working with a slow, terrifying leverage born of short muscle-bellies attached far down the bone, two of them wedge their spatulate fingers beneath the bulging block and *lift*, taking three hundred pounds of dead weight onto their locked forearms and arched backs. There is no grunt of effort, only the slow hiss of air through flared nostrils. While they hold the stone suspended by a fraction of an inch, the third reaches deep into the dark gap behind it. Using a shard of flaked, laminar basalt—not knapped into a spearpoint or an axe, just a natural wedge selected from the sub-floor cribwork and smoothed by generations of greasy palms—it saws methodically through the offending fig root until the tension snaps with a wet, fibrous *pop*. 

It pulls the severed white root out, chews the sweet, starchy cambium briefly with broad, flat-crowned molars, and passes half of it to its neighbor. Then it reaches into a pouch of loose skin at its throat, pulls out three fist-sized chocking stones brought up from the deep rubble fill, and slots them cleanly into the void behind the block. Together, they lower the granite header back into place. *Clack.* The terrace is locked again for another fifty years.

They are not restoring the city out of memory or reverence. They have no language for *plaza* or *pyramid* or *king*. To them, the masonry is simply the world’s skeleton, and keeping it upright, terraced, and hollow is how one keeps the pantry full.

Every niche they preserve holds a giant tank bromeliad, whose cupped rosettes hold two gallons of cloud-water teeming with tadpoles, ostracods, drowned katydids, and nutritious gelatinous algae, which the creatures scoop out delicately with one long middle finger like someone eating marrow from a bone. Every dry foundation shaft they keep clear of slump-rubble allows the leafcutter ants to maintain vast, subterranean fungus gardens forty feet down—gardens that the creatures visit once a month, carefully lifting a single stone slab from a chamber roof, harvesting no more than a fifth of the pale, spongy, protein-rich cultivar so the ant colony never abandons the site, and setting the slab back down so tightly that not a drop of condensation leaks in to spoil the ants' humidity.

In the vaulted galleries inside the stepped pyramids, where the velvet mats of bats hang over knee-deep guano, you will find their nurseries. 

Most mammals would choke on the ammonia in those dark, unventilated corbeled rooms, or be eaten alive by the boiling carpets of dermestid beetles and mites. The stone-dwellers have solved both problems through subtle domestic engineering. Centuries ago—whether by inherited habit from whatever human or near-human stock they diverged from, or through slow behavioral drift—they pulled singular capstones out of the pyramid flanks at calculated heights, creating silent thermal chimneys. Warm, ammonia-heavy air rises off the fermenting guano, draws slowly up through hidden flues in the dry-laid core, and vents out into the roots of the canopy trees eighty feet up, pulling fresh, cool, mist-dampened night air in through the low floor-drains. 

Just above the guano line, on broad stone corbels kept scrupulously swept clean with bundles of stiff fern-fronds, the young lie curled together in twos and threes. They are born pale as cave-crickets, almost hairless, with enormous hands and feet already strong enough to lock onto their mothers' coarse, lichen-slicked shoulders. The radiant heat rising off the composting guano keeps the stone ledges at a constant, fever-warm ninety degrees, allowing the mothers to leave their infants for hours in total darkness while they patrol the retaining walls and cisterns. Beneath them, the shiny-backed beetles churn the bat droppings into odorless black humus down at the bottom of the pits—humus that the adults periodically scoop into woven cradles of split philodendron root and carry out to pack into wall-crevices where fruiting vines and night-blooming epiphytes grow.

Yet they are not the only large things down in the dark, nor are they uncontested. Equilibrium in a closed basin always requires teeth.

If you are walking a sunken causeway when the screaming static of the frogs suddenly changes pitch—not stopping, because ten million frogs never stop at once, but *dipping*, a rolling trough of sudden silence twenty yards wide moving down the corridor like the shadow of an invisible cloud—step immediately onto a balustrade or press your back flat against a dry-stone wall.

The silence moves ahead of the *caiman-lizard's* cousin, or rather, what became of the high-mountain crocodilians and giant tegus that were trapped in this warm microclimate when the cloud forest sealed the roof over the warm stone centuries ago. 

Wait in the dark, and you will hear the dry, heavy *rasping* of osteoderms sliding over wet moss. Out of a square drainage culvert steps something twelve feet long, low-slung on weirdly upright, pillar-like legs that hold its belly clear of the flagstones, though it often drops down to slide down bowed stairways like a heavy leather sled. It is a terrestrial, canopy-blinded paleosuchus—a dwarf-snouted forest caiman gone gigantic and ghostly in the warm greenhouse dark. Its scales are black-green and velveted with the same algae that coats the masonry; its eyes are milky-amber orbs that barely register light, superseded entirely by rows of hyper-sensitive integumentary dome receptors dotting its jaws. Usually, those sensory pits detect ripples in river water. Here, where there is no river, they have tuned themselves to atmospheric pressure waves and the faint bioelectric fields of warm blood in humid air.

In this breathless, stagnant greenhouse, simply moving your arm pushes a tiny bow-wave of thick, saturated air ahead of you. The blind plaza-caiman feels that air-push against its snout from fifteen feet away. It hunts the pacas, the coatis, the low-roosting oilbirds, and any young stone-dweller careless enough to cross an open courtyard floor instead of traveling by wall-ledge or sub-floor tunnel.

Watch what happens when one of these silent, twelve-foot shadows glides across a sunken plaza toward a foraging group of mountain pacas. High on the third terrace wall above, an old male stone-dweller rests his chin on the coping stone, feeling the unmistakable, heavy four-beat scrape of plated feet below. 

The stone-dweller does not care about the pacas directly, except that pacas scatter tough mahogany seeds and turn over the leaf-mold, and a gorged caiman tends to crawl into drainage shafts to digest for three weeks, blocking the sub-masonry airways and crushing ant-highways with its bulk. So the old male reaches down to a niche beside his knee where three round, grapefruit-sized cobbles of river-granite sit—stones hauled up from deep beneath the original mountain floor centuries ago and kept stacked at intervals along every parapet in the city.

He picks one up in a double-thumbed grip, leans out into the bruised gloom without making a sound, and drops it straight down onto the hollow stone lid of an empty cistern twenty yards behind the caiman.

*BOOM.*

In the amphitheater acoustics of the sunken court, the strike of granite on hollow basalt hits like a cannon shot. The shockwave rings off four terraced walls, knocks droplets of condensed cloud-water off ten thousand leaves overhead in a sudden, localized rainstorm, and sends howler monkeys roaring into the upper branches half a mile away. Down on the plaza floor, the pacas melt instantaneously into narrow wall-fissures. Blinded by sensory overload as the stone beneath its belly rings like a gong, the great reptile snaps its jaws shut on empty air, hisses once—a wet, steam-kettle sound swallowed instantly by the resuming scream of the frogs—and retreats sullenly back into a slumped rubble-cave at the margin of the basin, away from the central courts.

High on the parapet, the stone-dweller is already gone, having slipped feet-first through a two-foot square vertical shaft between two dry-laid staircases, dropping down through absolute blackness by braking effortlessly with its palms and flat soles against the rough stone chimney walls.

If you stay in the valley long enough—past the bruised grey of noon, into the true, impenetrable ink of night when the mountain clouds drag right down into the plazas, turning the warm air into a solid, breathable soup of tepid fog—your sense of what is masonry and what is alive begins to blur completely. 

Lie down on a broad altar-stone or a wide parapet to rest, because there is nowhere soft to camp, and put your ear against the damp granite. 

At first, all you hear is the deafening ring of the surface: the metallic whistle of millions of thumbnail frogs, the saw-teeth of katydids, the sonar clicks of oilbirds banking sharply down the ninety-degree intersections of invisible streets. Then, beneath that, your skull begins to pick up the deeper conversation of the massif. 

Miles away across the dark basin, *tok-tok... tok*. A wall-gang signaling that a giant laurel’s buttress root has shifted near the western ridge. Closer, forty feet directly beneath your ribs in the dry-stone honeycomb: *clack... scrape... thump*, as heavy blocks are shimmed, channels cleared of dead snail shells, fungus-chambers unsealed and resealed. Around your resting place, small, warm shapes brush past the stone balustrades—kinkajous dropping fig rinds, coatis snuffling through the ferns—while ten feet above your head, completely invisible against a tapestry of liverwort and hanging roots, two heavy, grease-coated figures cling motionless to the vertical granite, chins pressed lightly to the wall, listening to the rhythm of your lungs through the stone, waiting patiently to see if your weight requires an adjustment to the foundation, or if the city can hold you up as you are.
