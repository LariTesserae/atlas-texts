If you watched the junction between the Number Two pump plinth and the lower catwalk stanchion for three hours, you would think you were looking at an exceptionally heavy bolster of iron-oxidizing slime, half-draped in dead aerial roots and glistening with mineral sweat. 

Only in the fourth hour would you realize that the drips falling from the thirty-inch cold main above are no longer hitting the concrete cup they wore forty years ago. They are striking a sloped, glossy ridge that was not there at noon, running down a shallow, flesh-like gutter, and pooling against the warm brass casing of the pump’s bearing housing.

What lives here does not scurry, and it has no lungs to be poisoned by the heavy, sweet-sour pooling of carbon dioxide in the lower sumps. An animal down here would suffocate in ten minutes, or starve for lack of anything to chew that isn't saturated with leached copper salts, or go mad from the unceasing sixty-cycle hammer of the motors. Whatever holds tenancy in the vault arrived before the hatches were bolted—perhaps as spores washed down the storm weir from an industrial timber yard upriver, perhaps as a smear of grey cellar-plasmodium tracked in on a pipefitter’s tallow-greased boot in 1928—and in the decades since, sealed inside this roaring copper-and-iron pressure cooker, it has forgotten how to be small.

At first glance, it is easily mistaken for the chamber’s normal efflorescence: those slick, orange-brown aprons of *Leptothrix* bacteria and calcified rust that hang from leaking flanges. Look closer into the amber gloom behind a caged bulkhead lamp, however, where the heat of the bulb bakes the air dry for three inches before the wet fern-wall begins, and you can see its true architecture. 

It is a continuous, creeping sheet of naked protoplasm—a single, giant, syncytial mass—that has solved the problem of copper toxicity by binding the metal into its own outer skin. Where a common forest slime mold is delicate as whipped egg white, easily torn by a falling twig, this thing wears a flexible, leathery cuticle the color of tarnished pennies and wet liver. Its surface is faintly iridescent under the grimed amber lamps, slicked with a clear, bitter mucilage that sheds condensed water like grease on cast iron. 

It has no center, or rather, its center changes every week. 

On Tuesday, the bulk of its mass—perhaps forty pounds of dense, rubbery, rust-veined gel—may be packed into the warm, vibrating saddle between two canvas-jacketed steam lines, spread out like a heavy horse-blanket over the asbestos lagging to bake at a steady hundred and ten degrees. By Saturday, that saddle is empty save for a drying, silvery track of spent slime, like the trail of a monstrous snail across the rivets, and the mass has poured itself downward through the rusted Diamond-mesh of the catwalk grates, hanging in thick, slow-moving curtains among the aerial roots of the philodendrons to drink the black, sour peat-liquor seeping from the fern mats.

It moves at the speed of a clock’s hour hand. You cannot see it advance, yet it has gesture and posture. When it crosses the six-inch gap between a sweating domestic water trunk and a lead electrical conduit, it does not simply drip; it gathers itself into a blunt, blind knuckle the thickness of a man’s forearm, stiffens its internal cables of actin against gravity, and reaches out horizontally through the roaring dark. The tip quests almost imperceptibly, oscillating in a wide, patient circle, tasting the thermal drafts—flinching fractionally away from the scalding pinhole hiss of the brass packing gland, leaning into the rich, rotting breath rising from a slumped heap of yellowed hart’s-tongue fronds.

Inside that translucent, amber-brown reach, if it passes close enough to one of the grimed bulkhead lamps for the dim light to shine *through* it, you can see its pulse. 

There is no heart, only a vast hydraulic shuttle. Every ninety seconds, the thick rivers of granular fluid inside its branching veins slow, thicken, pause for three beats of the roaring water below, and then reverse direction, surging backward through a labyrinth of self-made tubes. It is a rhythm utterly indifferent to the frantic sixty-hertz chatter of the centrifugal pumps. In fact, the vibration helps it. Where the creature’s margins thin out into fan-shaped foraging sheets across the wet brick walls, the steady mechanical tremor of the building sets tiny standing waves shivering across its skin—microscopic Chladni patterns that sort the mineral grit it swallows, shaking heavy grains of silica sand and brick-dust out to its edges to be cast off as dry, Mortar-like ridges, while keeping the fine iron rust and copper carbonate suspended inside its belly.

Because the leaves in the vault are immaculate while they live, the creature is strictly an undertaker, a gardener of the dead, and an engineer of drips. 

It never climbs a green stem. Living creeping fig and sword fern are too slick with waxy cutin, too aggressively alive; it leaves them alone, sliding smoothly around their woody ankles without bruising a cell. Instead, it waits in the dark hollows beneath the I-beam webs and along the mossy flanges. When an old philodendron leaf, wide as a shield and heavy with water, finally yellows from the stem and slumps down onto the packed mat, the tenant is there within six hours. 

A dark, plum-colored lip rises out of the black humus, pours smoothly over the fallen blade, and seals it down against the iron grate. Beneath that rubbery hood, in total darkness, the leaf is dismantled with terrifying neatness. Three days later, the hood withdraws, pulling back into the lattice of roots like tide-water retreating through mangroves. What remains on the grate is not rot, not black mush, not fungal fuzz, for the creature eats the fungi too—grazing the white threads of *Mycelium* and the wood-rotting crusts right off the damp peat like a cow cropping grass. What remains is only the pale, stripped vascular ghost of the leaf: a pristine lace skeleton of pure lignin, washed clean, sitting dry and weightless for an hour until the dripping ceiling hammers it into fragments and flushes it down toward the storm culvert.

That is why the vault smells so cleanly of crushed green peat and hot oil rather than swamp-rot. The chamber is being scoured from the inside out. The creature creeps over the glass prisms high in the ceiling vaults once or twice a year, spending a week flattened against the tar-stained glass, grazing away the velvet coat of green algae until a sudden, startling shaft of clearer bottle-green street-light stabs three feet further down into the upper pipes—only to dim again over the following months as the algae regrows in its wake. It crawls over the brown, baked-on mineral grime of the wire-caged bulkhead lamps, wrapping the hot glass globes completely in a thick, opaque caul of wet amber flesh. For forty-eight hours at a time, one sphere of light along the buried catwalk will simply go out, eclipsed, glowing only as a dull, blood-orange coal through two inches of feeding protoplasm; when the creature slides off down the stanchion, the glass globe is weirdly bright for a week, polished clean of iron-scale and soot, casting sharp-edged shadows of fern-teeth across the wet brick before the mineral mist clouds it over once more.

Over eighty years of uninterrupted peace, in a room where nothing changes temperature or rhythm, it has grown habits that look uncomfortably like architecture.

Along the middle tier, where cold intake pipes run parallel to the baking canvas steam lines, the creature has learned—in whatever blind, chemical way a network of oscillating tubes can *learn*—that certain intersections yield richer harvests than others. Where warm vapor rises through cold drips, airborne spores and bacteria rain down thickest; where fern-roots scorch slightly against an uninsulated steam flange, sweet, leaky sugars bleed into the moss. 

So it has begun to keep things in place. 

When it casts off its waste—those heavy loads of leached copper, lime scale from the pressure-gauge dials, and ferric oxide grazed from the turbine casings—it does not drop them at random. Along the edges of its favorite thoroughfares, its retreating margins deposit hardened, scalloped levees of dark metallic sinter, smooth as glazed stoneware and hard as boiler-scale. Over decades, these little dams have diverted the patient tracks of condensed water. Drips that used to fall straight into the roaring storm culvert below are caught on tiered, shelf-like brackets of mineralized slime-crust, led sideways along rusted tie-rods, and spilled deliberately onto dry pockets of catwalk mulch where new fountains of hart’s-tongue fern promptly erupt—ferns whose lower fronds will, in eight months' time, yellow, slump, and feed the thing that watered them.

Down near the bottom of the pit, just above the invisible, thundering weir where the umbrella sedge grows waist-deep in perpetual spray, there are thicker structures still. Hung between the vibrating cast-iron volutes of the three centrifugal pumps is a series of heavy, braided cables that look at first like more aerial roots, or perhaps tarred hemp packing-rope left behind by dead municipal engineers. 

They are neither. They are permanent highways—arterial trunks up to four inches thick, encased in tough, layered sheaths of dead, horn-like slime impregnated with iron rust and copper verdigris. Inside those dark, armored conduits, safe from the scouring spray of the storm-water bypass, high-speed streams of pale yellow protoplasm shuttle continuously between the three pump plinths, knitting the whole floor of the chamber into a single nervous and digestive territory. If a chunk of waterlogged mortar finally gives way high in the groin-vaults and plummets forty feet through the foliage, smashing a bruised path through five tiers of ferns before splashing into the sedge mat near Pump One, the shockwave has barely died in the ironwork before the flow inside all three arterial trunks reverses, rushing mass from the far side of the church-sized room toward the new wound, eager to drink the sap bleeding from a dozen snapped stems.

Once in a great while—perhaps when barometric pressure drops sharply up on the street ahead of a summer thunderstorm, or when the city’s steam plant drops line-pressure for an hour in spring and the baking warmth falters by three degrees—the whole dispersed body performs its only rapid act.

Across twenty vertical feet of rusted valve-wheels, brick arches, and dripping I-beams, the scattered foraging fans pull back their glistening margins. The rubbery blankets wrapped around the warm pump bearings thin out and pour upward. For two days, the lower chamber seems strangely bare, its rusted plum-colored handrails dry, its bulkhead lamps burning unobstructed. Everything climbs toward the highest, driest iron trusses beneath the sidewalk grates, where the faint bottle-green daylight dies and a tiny, warm thermal draft leaks out through a hairline crack around a bolted cast-iron maintenance hatch.

There, clinging to the riveted underside of the street-plate, forty or fifty pounds of the vault’s hidden life gathers into clusters of stalked, club-shaped polyps the size of human thumbs. In the dim green gloom, they harden over a single night, turning from wet liver-red to brittle, metallic bronze, dusted all over with millions of dry, copper-armored spores finer than talcum powder. 

One by one, shaken by the distant rumble of delivery trucks passing directly overhead on the street grade, the dry bronze caps split open along geometric seams. Tiny puffs of brown dust lift on the eighty-five-degree updraft, slip through the microscopic gap in the rusted hatch-seals where no beetle or spider could ever squeeze, and vanish up into the dry grit of the city’s gutters—blowing away down the avenues in search of another forgotten basement, another leaking steam main, another locked iron room with a century to spare. 

Down below, in the deafening roar of the weir, ninety percent of the mass never bothered to fruit at all. Before the bronze husks beneath the hatch have even finished emptying, a blunt, glistening knuckle the color of an old penny is already easing back down the chain-fall hoist, sliding slowly over the solid, rusted blocks, reaching out through the trembling dark to catch the next cold drip from the thirty-inch main.
