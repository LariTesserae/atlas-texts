At first, you take them for part of the talus. 

Out in the middle of the flat gravel corridors, miles from the dripping walls, the great detached blocks of limestone sit on skirts of their own shattered debris. Where a massive slab did not settle quite flush against the bedrock during the ancient drop, there are horizontal clearances—slots ten inches high and thirty feet deep, dark and dry, floored with velvet-fine rock flour that has never felt a drop of water. 

If you crouch down out of the mid-frequency roar of the bird columns, bringing your eyes level with the gravel, and wait long enough for your pupils to widen in the pewter gloom, one of the flat stones near the mouth of a slot will lift an inch, pivot without a sound, and let out a breath that smells faintly of rendered bird-fat and cold chalk.

There are perhaps forty of them in the entire sunken valley, scattered miles apart, though they are rarely seen alone and never seen in crowds. They are human, or were dropped from the human stock of the plateau long enough ago—when the fault-walls were still shedding dust from their first great shear—that the basin has had time to pare them down to fit its narrow tolerances. They have no name for themselves because they do not use their throats. Down here, a human voice is useless; a shout tears the cords raw in an hour and dies ten paces out in the surf-din of six million wings and clicking beaks. Their mouths have set into thin, quiet seams, the jaw muscles slight except at the back where they crack marrow-bones.

When one slides fully out from beneath a monolith, what strikes you first is the grease, and then the stillness.

They are small, scarcely four and a half feet tall when they bother to stand upright, with long, flat-muscled torsos and bowed legs thickened at the knee and ankle. Their skin is the pallid, dense yellowish-grey of tallow kept in a cellar, though you never see it clean; from scalp to heel they are coated in a deliberate, quarter-inch rind of masticated bird fat kneaded together with dry rock flour and grey chert dust. Against the fifty-degree damp that never lifts, woven cloth or fur pelts from the upper world would soak through with condensation in two days and rot on the body in two weeks. So they wear mortar. It makes them entirely waterproof, seals their body heat inside a windless rind, and leaves them the exact matte, shadowless color of the limestone scree. When one stops moving and draws its knees to its chest, tucking its chin down, the eye slides right off it. Even the wide-eared foxes, trotting past with fledglings limp in their jaws, will step within a yard of a crouched basin-dweller and never turn their heads, smelling only stone dust and the universal, pervasive grease of dead birds.

Only their joints show creases in the grey paste—pale, shiny folds at the armpits, behind the knees, and across the knuckles. Their hair is cropped close to the skull with freshly struck chert blades so it cannot hold damp or harbor the blind lice that drop from the upper ledges; what stubble remains is plastered flat with fat, turning their heads into smooth, grey river cobbles.

Look at their faces when they turn toward the pale slot of the sky, and you see what the sourceless light and the ammonia have done. Their eyes are wide, set deep beneath heavy, grease-packed brows, with dark irises so broad they swallow almost all the white. Across them flickers a thick, pinkish inner corner—not quite a membrane, not quite not—weeping a slow, oily tear that keeps the sharp sting of the guano-dust from blinding them. Their nostrils are narrow, muscular slits they can pinch nearly shut when they work the white-slicked talus cones directly beneath the cliffs. Their ears are small, folded slightly forward, packed permanently in the outer whorl with wads of wet swiftlet down mixed with lime-crust. Without the plugs, the endless, percussive *shhh-klick-lak* of the bird-columns drives a waking brain mad in a season; muffled behind grease and down, the roar drops to a dull, pressureless hum in the jawbone, leaving them able to feel the sharper vibrations that actually matter: the thud of a falling crow-sized fruit bird hitting the scree fifty yards off, or the heavy, rhythmic crunch of boots when the plateau people come down the ropes.

Watch two of them move across an open gravel corridor between monoliths. 

They do not walk like people who live under weather or sun. Up above, creatures hurry against nightfall, or winter, or the shifting shadow of a hawk. Down here, noon is midnight, January is July, and nothing hunts from the air—the columns of swifts and night-crows are too thick for any eagle from the rim to dive through without breaking its wings against a hundred heavy bodies. So the people of the floor move at the pace of the fifty-degree salamanders. They go in a low, flat-footed glide, knees bent, torso tilted forward, toes spread wide and leathered on the soles with calluses half an inch thick, hardened by lime-water and flint-gravel until a razor-sharp chert spall will turn beneath their weight rather than cut. They never waste warmth in a run. Every motion is conserved, greased, economical.

One stops beside a shallow bedrock pan where rock-sweat drips at three-second intervals from an overhang thirty feet above. It does not bend at the waist to drink; it sinks straight down on its heels, lowers its mouth to the black, inch-deep mirror without breaking the white lime crust at the rim, and draws water slowly so the cold liquid warms in the mouth before hitting the belly. With two fingers—thick, blunt-nailed, scarred white from old stone-nicks—it pinches three pale, blind isopods from the margin and tucks them into its cheek like pine nuts, hardly chewing.

Near the base of the cliff, where the ammonia stench sits thick and cold as cellar-water, three more of them are working the fall-line.

They never climb. Climbing burns heat, risks a broken bone in a place where no splint wood grows, and brings a body up into the slick, roaring filth of the ledges where the sharp limestone edges slice through grease and skin like hot wire. Let the men from the upper world dangle on bast ropes with ash poles and bone hooks, sweating through their leather coats, shouting themselves hoarse, risking a three-hundred-foot drop onto jagged stone for a basket of spotted eggs. The dwellers on the floor know that everything on those cliffs comes down on its own.

Out of six million birds wheeling in the gloom, hundreds die or fumble every hour. Eggs roll from shallow seams and smash into rich yellow puddles on the flat slate slabs at the top of the talus; heavy, night-eyed squabs, sleek with yellow fat from regurgitated plateau-fruit, jostle one another off the narrow ledges and hit the gravel with dull, wet *whumps*; adult swifts clip wings in the counter-rotating dark and spiral down stunned, quivering among the black beetles.

The three workers move along the edge of the guano-apron ahead of the rock-martens, beating the beetles to the freshest drops. They carry no baskets, only pouches made from the whole, cased skins of foxes turned fur-side in and tied at the neck with twisted gut. When a heavy fruit-bird drops, the nearest figure glides five paces, picks it up before the beetles can find its eyes, and wrings its neck with a single, practiced roll of the wrist. With a thumb-sized flake of banded chert—struck fresh that morning, sharp as glass, held bare between thumb and forefinger—it slits the crop first.

This is the only way the upper world ever enters them. Inside the warm, muscular pouch of a night-crow sit whole, uncrushed berries, wild figs, oily laurel-seeds, and bitter mountain buds gathered ten miles away in sunlight these people have never seen and could not bear if they did. They scoop the warm, bruised fruit straight from the dead bird's throat into their mouths, closing their wide, dark eyes for a second as the tart sugars hit their tongues. It is what keeps their gums tight around their teeth and their blood from turning black in a valley of nothingness and liverwort. Even the gritty seeds they crack between their molars for the oil.

The carcasses they take back out into the clean, cold air of the middle flats, half a mile from the stinking walls, where the great boulders sit in silent geometry.

If you followed them back to their slab—which you could not do unseen, because across half a league of flat grey gravel anything taller than a fox stands out like a monument—you would find no camp. You would find no hearth, no midden of shells, no stacked stones. Fire requires wood, and wood belongs to the sky; fat burns, yes, with a black, oily smudge on a twist of moss, and sometimes deep inside the horizontal under-boulder slots they keep a walnut-sized flame going in a hollowed limestone knuckle-cup, just enough to take the dead edge off the air in their sleeping-crack. Anything larger would send a greasy ribbon of smoke straight up in the dead, windless inversion, hanging in a grey pillar for days to tell the egg-gatherers on the ropes that something shares the gravel.

Instead, they cure their meat on the stone itself. Up on the flat top of a fallen monolith ten feet off the ground, hidden from the floor below and invisible through the swirling black chaff of birds to anyone peering over the nine-hundred-foot rim above, rows of split swiftlets and plucked fruit-birds lie laid out on thin crusts of scraped lime-salt. Nothing rots quickly in constant fifty-degree air saturated with mineral damp and ammonia trace, provided you keep the beetles off it, and the beetles cannot climb ten sheer feet of greased limestone overhang. Over weeks, the meat turns dark, waxy, and dense as horn.

It is in these horizontal cracks beneath the monoliths that they sleep, packed four and five together like winter salamanders, limbs tangled, grey grease-rinds slick against one another, breathing into the hollow of each other's necks so no warmth escapes into the rock. When a child is born—which happens rarely, perhaps once in five or six uncounted years, whenever enough plateau-fruit has come down in the crops of the birds to fatten a woman past the lean margin—it is born into that dark, ten-inch slot, wiped clean with dry swiftlet down, and greased with warm marrow and stone-flour before it ever sees the pewter light outside. It never cries long. Nobody answers a cry with sound; they answer it with a thumb dipped in sweet mashed fig from a bird's crop, or the press of a heavy, warm palm over the ribs. By the time the infant crawls out onto the gravel apron, blinking up at the swirling smoke of wings, it knows without being told that noise belongs to the birds, and stillness belongs to the stone.

How do they speak? Watch two of them crouching under the overhang where you found the cold charcoal ring and the bundle of ash poles left by the plateau men.

They are inspecting the cache. They do not steal the poles—wood is clumsy, splinters in the damp, and leaves marks if dragged—nor do they touch the three-ply bast rope hanging forty feet up the cliff. If you cut a rope or steal a pole, the tall, loud men in leather come back with dogs or iron, looking for thieves. Instead, one of the small grey figures picks up one of the plateau-men's cobblestone hammerstones, hefts it, and uses it to strike three crisp flakes from a core of local dark chert. Before setting the hammerstone back, it turns the cobble carefully in its greased hands until the faint dark ring of ground-damp on its underside matches the exact depression in the dust where it lay. Then, with the side of a foot, it brushes three beetles across its own shallow print. 

While it works, the second figure watches the distant cliff face through the haze of birds. Suddenly it reaches out and taps two knuckles lightly against the first one's collarbone—*tap, pause, tap-tap*, followed by a short, flat slide of the palm across the greased shoulder. 

Touch is their language in the dark under the boulders; out on the gravel, at twenty paces, it becomes small, sharp angles of the wrist and forearm held low against the pale belly so the gesture doesn't break their silhouette. A crooked finger means *fox-litter in the northeast scree*. A flat palm tilted twice means *heavy drop of night-birds under the red-banded seam*. Two fists pressed together and dropped an inch means *the rope near the drip-chimney is moving; the loud ones are coming down*.

When the ropes do twitch, and tiny, shouting specks in greasy leather begin descending the upper cliffs with egg-baskets slung at their hips and dried brushwood strapped to their backs for a quick tea-fire on the gravel, the forty grey shapes out on the floor do not flee. Running catches the eye. 

Some slide backward feet-first into the narrow slots under the great slabs, pulling a flat flake of scree into the gap to break the dark line of the opening. Others, caught further out on the bare gravel corridors, simply sink down beside a scatter of frost-white bird ribs, tuck their chins between their heels, close their wide, dark eyes behind oily lids, and lock their breathing down to four shallow draws a minute. 

Hours later, when the egg-gatherers have raked their crevices, shouted themselves raw across ten yards of gravel, burned their little bundle of woody stems to grey twig-shapes of ash, and hauled themselves groaning back up through the narrow chimney into the clouds, the valley settles back into nothing—because nothing had changed. 

Half a mile from the cooling charcoal, a boulder the size of a kneeling calf shifts an inch. Grey dust flakes silently from a bent knee. Two dark, broad-pupiled eyes open to the wet-pewter light, checking the sky-slot not for sun, which never comes, nor rain, which never falls, but for the slight thickening in the swirling black chaff overhead that means the heavy fruit-birds are dropping back in from the plateau, bellies full of summer from a world nine hundred feet away.

Slowly, moving no faster than water gathering on cold chert, the figure rises to a crouch and glides back toward the cold ring of ash—not for warmth, which is already gone, but to lick the white wood-salts from the unblown twigs before the blind isopods find them, careful to leave half the twigs unbroken so the windless basin keeps its secret.
