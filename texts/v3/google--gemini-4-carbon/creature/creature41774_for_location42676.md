To look for them across the middle distances is to look at nothing at all. Against a five-mile drop of blinding cream-colored wood and luminous white air, a person wrapped in bleached, fibrous bark-felt and pale glider-hide disappears entirely. You only catch them when they cross one of the rare, razor-edged shadows cast by an overhead truss—a sudden, startling flicker of charcoal-black grease and dull red copper moving steadily along a tilting ridge, vanishing again the moment they step back into the glare.

They call themselves nothing so grand as a nation; they move in cord-bound strings of four to ten, spaced forty paces apart so that a single buckling plate will not drop an entire family into the abyss. Up close, they are lean, sinewy people with wide, barrel-shaped chests adapted to the thin, freezing air, though their bulk is tripled by layers of quilted, beaten bast-fiber. Every inch of exposed skin around their eyes and cheekbones is smeared thick with black pitch-soot mixed with rendered marrow. Without it, the sourceless, clinical glare bouncing off the pale timber from beneath their own feet would burn the corneas out of their skulls in two days. Over this dark mask they wear wide, flat visors whittled from bone-dry heartwood, pierced not with horizontal slits—for hazards here fall from above and erupt from below as often as they lie ahead—but with tiny, cross-shaped pinholes. 

Their faces, when they unmuffle their chins inside a warm growth-pocket, are wind-scoured and deeply lined, their lips stained a permanent, dark indigo from chewing raw cambium-pulp to stave off frost-scurvy. They have virtually no body fat; the Hollow burns calories out of a person as fiercely as it burns water out of the air.

Everything about how they stand and move belongs to an architecture under lethal tension. A newcomer to a flat, quiet room would think them half-deaf or perpetually distracted. They never sit flat on their buttocks, and they never lean their backs against a wall. Even asleep, they curl on their sides with one ear—or the jawbone, stripped of hood and padding—pressed against the wooden haft of a heavy, copper-shod pike driven a thumb’s depth into the floor. They are listening to the grain.

To live in the White Hollow is to possess a vocabulary of forty different words for tension, none of which refer to the mind. Through the soles of their stiff, cross-laced boots, they read the vast, silent mathematics of the truss beneath them. There is the *singing hum* of an aqueduct-thick vine three miles away taking on a new load—safe for another week. There is the *greasy shudder* of young, translucent wood swelling nearby, which promises heat and slush-water within the hour. And there is the *dry tick*—a faint, erratic clicking in the soles of the feet, like cold sleet hitting tight leather. When a scout feels the dry tick, they do not shout. Shouting disappears into the vast, dead air ten yards from the mouth. Instead, they drop the butt of their ironwood pike twice against the timber. Two sharp *clacks* race through the dense, dry fiber at three miles a second. Two hundred yards away, the rest of the string feels it in their ankles and immediately drops flat, driving short, hooked copper-beaked picks into the wood just before the great plane beneath them shears cleanly in two, dropping half the meadow into the dazzling fog below with scarcely more sound than a snapped icicle.

They are carpenters of a living avalanche. Because nothing rots here and there is no soil, they have no clay, no forged iron, and very little true stone—only precious, hoarded flints and river-cobbles carried down generations ago from whatever forgotten throat in the upper crust their ancestors slipped through, traded like jewels from string to string. What they have in abundance is kinetic force, and they use the flora’s own violence as their beast of burden, their quarry, and their hearth.

Watch a grandmother—toothless, snow-blind in one eye, her hands thick-knuckled and scarred by a thousand flint-splinters—make camp at an intersection where two colossal ribs are grinding together. She does not build a hut. She takes a pair of heavy chert wedges from her sled, tests the warmth of the seam with her bare wrist, and drives the stones at precise, oblique angles into the bleeding, steaming lip of a growing plate. She bruises the meristem deliberately with the poll of her adze. Provoked and diverted, the frantic, exothermic chemistry of the plant surges around the obstruction. Within twenty minutes, a translucent green-white sheet of cambium curls up out of the split like a breaking wave, arches over the family’s sleds, and meets the opposing rib. As the freezing air hits its outer skin, the moisture flashes off in a brief flurry of artificial snow, and the dome hardens like bone. Inside, the trapped heat of the wood’s own furious growth brings the air from twenty below zero up to a humid, resin-scented fifty degrees. They do not even need to light a fire unless they are boiling tough spider-meat; the walls themselves sweat warmth for ten or twelve hours before the growth-front moves on, the sap retreats, and the shelter dries into brittle, dead flint-wood that must be abandoned before the shifting load crushes it like an eggshell.

Fire, when they do make it, is small, fierce, and smoky—fed with dry, flinty wood-shards and oily sap-pearls. They prize soot almost as much as meat. Soot is ink, eye-shield, and beacon. In a world without landmarks, where a mountain range of pale buttresses on Tuesday is a collapsed, yawning chasm by Friday, memory is useless for navigation. Instead, they leave black blazes smeared across stable nodes: complex, shorthand glyphs painted in grease and charcoal that tell the next string three weeks hence *how many people passed, who has died, who was born, and which way the great cables were pulling when they left.*

Yet they are not the only minds—or at least, not the only patience—in the bright dark.

If you follow one of the braided root-fiber snares set beside a carved slush-basin, you see what else the glare has shaped. The spider-like browsers—the people call them *tarsals* or *stilt-ticks*—are the cattle of the abyss, though no one herds them. Some have leg-spans wider than a tall man is high, yet their bodies are no bigger than a winter melon, slung high in the center of eight impossibly thin, whip-flexible limbs made of hollow, chitinous tubing. They look less like beasts than bundle-tied shocks of pale straw walking on needles. Their shells are the exact chalky, blue-white matte of frost-dried bark; when they lock their joints and freeze, you can walk close enough to hang your sled-harness on a knee-joint before realizing it isn't a dead spur. 

They have no eyes at all—eyes are an expensive liability where the light comes from every angle at once and blinds rather than reveals. Instead, their two front legs are stretched out four yards ahead of them, feathered with fine, crystalline hairs that taste the sudden plumed humidity of a splitting growth-seam from half a mile upwind, while the hooked tips of their rear feet tap-tap-tap continuously at the timber, mapping the structural stress of their continent by pure resonance. When caught in a snare, they do not thrash; thrashing breaks brittle legs and draws attention. They simply fold themselves into tight, geometric octahedrons and wait, clicking softly in their throats. Their meat is stringy, translucent, and tastes sharply of turpentine and cold zinc, while their hollow leg-tubes make the finest blow-darts, water-siphons, and splints in the Hollow.

Higher up, riding the violent, invisible pillars of warm air that punch upward from newly ruptured bark-vaults into the freezing miles of space, drift the *sheets*. Calling them gliders makes them sound like birds or squirrels; in truth, they are closer to living sails, five or six yards across from corner to corner, scarcely thicker than heavy saddle-leather, with a flat, cartilaginous rib-cage flattened entirely into the plane of the wing. Looking straight up into the dazzling actinic ceiling—a mistake no child makes twice without their visor—you cannot see them at all, because they are translucent enough that the cold glare shines right through their veins, turning them into faint, milky ripples in the air, like heat-haze. 

They never land on flat floors; if a sheet settles onto a horizontal timber plane, it is stranded, unable to haul itself aloft, shuffling miserably on tiny, hooked marginal claws until it starves or freezes dry. They live their entire lives falling slowly across ten-mile gulfs from vertical rib to vertical rib, sleeping on the wing in slow spirals inside warm updrafts, scooping the drifting clouds of white spore-dust and tiny, pale bark-gnats into wide, baleen-fringed mouths slit across their leading edges. Sometimes, however, when a string of travelers climbs an exposed, mile-long tension cable—clipping themselves to the colossal, vibrating vine with heavy horn carabiners—the warm plumes of their own breath and the dark smudges of their charcoal-stained parkas draw the sheets down out of the white sky. Four or five of them will wheel silently in the freezing silence fifty feet out in the void, tilting their broad, featureless undersides toward the climbers. They are opportunistic scavengers of things knocked loose. They have been known to drop out of nowhere in complete silence—not even a whistle of feathers, just the sudden rush of stiff, papery skin—to slap a toddler or a lashed pack off a narrow spur with a heavy wing-edge, following the tumbling prize three miles down into the lower fog to eat what shatters on the vaults below.

And deeper down still—not in the open air, which belongs to the gliders and the tiny, squinting lines of humans, but *inside* the sealed chambers where two convex shells have bowed outward and fused around five hundred cubic yards of stagnant, warm air—live the *borers*, though the people speak of them the way sailors speak of reefs and rogue waves rather than animals. Pale, blind, segmented grubs the length of a man's thigh, armored in overlapping plates of silica-hardened horn, they chew slow, resonant galleries through the load-bearing heartwood of the oldest nodes. The humans hunt them greedily with long, barbed copper gaffs, not only because a single borer renders down into a gallon of rich, clear oil that burns without smoke and keeps leather supple in the deep freeze, but because left unchecked, a colony of twenty borers can silently hollow out the keystone of a six-truss intersection until an entire hanging province of timber—camps, sleds, frozen ponds, and twenty square miles of Pale forest—gives one sharp, dry *pop* and drops away into the bottomless white glare below.

So the people keep moving, wedged between the growth that crushes and the rotless dry-snap that drops. 

When you find one of them who can no longer walk—an old man whose knees have seized from forty years of bracing against tilting floors, or a hunter whose chest was stove in by a whipping root-cable—you do not find a grave. You cannot dig into living ironwood with copper and stone, and leaving a body out on the dry planes simply turns a loved one into pale, flint-hard jerky under the unblinking ceiling, rattled about by the wind for years until the branch falls. 

Instead, the family finds a young, furiously growing seam—one of the hot, steaming fissures where translucent green wood is unrolling like spilled batter. They strip the dead of every scrap of copper, flint, cordage, and felt; nothing that endures may be wasted. They wash the soot from the quiet face with warm slush-water so that, for the first and only time in their life out in the open, their skin is bare to the blinding light. Then they lay the body directly into the hot, split lip of the surging timber, drive two small wooden shims above them to direct the curl, and sit fifty yards back on their sleds, chewing bitter bark and watching in silence. Within an hour, the swelling, exothermic margin rolls gently over the body, sealing it away in clear, steaming amber-white fiber. By tomorrow, that seam will be a soaring arch forty feet overhead, hardening into pale, translucent grain; by next month, a vaulted rib three hundred feet thick, carrying the weight of ten miles of sky, holding a faint, dark, human-shaped shadow deep inside its luminous heartwood where the cold can never reach it again.
