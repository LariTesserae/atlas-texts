If you stand hypothetically on one of the dished stairways and listen past the scraping of the silica fronds, you will eventually hear a mistake in the music of the stone. 

The wind-engine of the cavern ought to be predictable. Because the heat rises steadily from the floor trenches and rolls down the outer salt-walls in a closed loop, any given window-slit or carved lintel should hold a steady pitch, rising half a step only when the ten-second barometric surges roar up from the bedrock. Instead, the chords move. A breathy flute-note in an upper terrace cuts off cleanly in mid-sigh, then resumes a heartbeat later two rooms to the left. Deep within an open-ribbed dome across the gulf, a resonant hum bends downward into a sudden, throaty warble, as if an invisible palm had cupped itself just over the mouth of the flue.

Look up toward the needle-thin shafts of sunlight before they drown in the floating red clay. Most of the dust in those white beams rises straight toward the slag roof or tumbles predictably in the eddies. Every so often, however, a spark of specular bronze or clear, glassy glint shoots sideways right across the grain of the updraft, banking hard at the edge of the light as though burned by it, and vanishes back into the cherry-lit dark.

Nothing walks the streets because the stone is a trap—too abrasive in the scouring drafts, too barren of food, too close to the searing floor vents. Everything alive here belongs to the wind-engine itself. Beneath the vitrified ceiling, the air is dense with buoyant mineral gases and restless enough to keep anything sufficiently light aloft from the moment it hatches until the moment it wears to powder.

Wait for the floor channels to brighten. When the pressure shifts down in the crust and the cherry-red glare swells upward, stiffening the copper vines with that unified metallic *shhhk* and boiling a fresh blizzard of ash-gray pollen through the perforated floors, the middle air suddenly fills with ribbons.

There are thousands of them, pouring out of the lee-shadows behind the domes to intercept the rising plume. Most are no longer than a forearm and thinner than split mica—narrow, rippling pennants of tarnished cupreous skin stretched over delicate frames of hollow silica quill. They have no legs, no jaws, no eyes, and not a trace of moisture in them. Evolved from the same dry, sulfur-hungry chemistry as the thermophytes below—perhaps descended from winged plant-spores that refused to root eons ago—they are creatures of pure aerodynamics and static charge.

In a kiln-dry pocket with no water to make mucus or blood, an animal cannot trap dust on a wet tongue or digest it in a wet gut. As these ribbon-thin skimmers corkscrew through the rising clouds of pollen, they flex their articulated silica ribs rapidly back and forth. Squeezing crystalline silica generates a sharp piezoelectric charge. Across the skimmer’s metallic flank, microscopic gill-scales bristle with sudden static, snapping floating pollen grains and sulfur-rich spores straight out of the air from inches away until the creature is furred gray with dust. Then the ribbons fold inward, sealing the catch inside tiny catalytic pockets lined with porous copper-pumice. For five or six seconds after every gas surge, as the floor trenches dim back to bruised red, whole neighborhoods twinkle with drifting constellations of pinprick orange sparks: thousands of skimmers oxidizing pollen on the wing, burning airborne sulfur and carbon directly in the furnace heat. When the flash dies, they flex their ribs once more, invert their charge, and puff away a weightless smoke of pure white silica ash. That is their only waste. It rises straight to the ceiling to be ground into the red clay haze.

Watch one shoot down a narrow catwalk at full tilt and you expect to see it dashed to pieces against the soap-smooth pillars or impaled on a whipping copper vine. It never touches them. The dry jungle below—thousands of silica fronds scraping ceaselessly against volcanic blocks like whetstones—keeps the entire architectural lattice soaked in triboelectric charge, conducted upward through the spiraling metal vines until every lintel and balustrade hums with a dry, hair-raising field. Carrying the same polarity on their skins, the flyers skid off the invisible cushions around the stone knuckles without ever making contact. A ribbon-shoal poured through a skeletal house looks like water sluicing through a sieve: parting smoothly around columns, compressing into tight streams through window-slits, never grazing mortar.

Higher up, where the blinding sun-pins fade out and the rising heat begins its slow, heavy roll outward toward the salt-walls, larger shapes hold the current.

At first they are nearly impossible to separate from the architecture’s shimmering heat-mirages, or from detached thermophyte fronds tumbling toward the roof. They are broad, kite-like mantles—three, four, sometimes five paces from tip to tip—built of pleated bronze-and-silver pellicle so thin that the dull red glow of the floor trenches shines right through their bodies, illuminating branching networks of gas-filled buoyancy veins and fine copper nerve-threads. In open air, they glide with a slow, undulating ripple at their trailing edges, riding the great toroidal loop around the perimeter of the blister. 

When one enters the lattice of the city, however, its body reveals an astonishing geometry. Approaching a narrow doorway on a fast cross-draft, a four-pace mantle snaps shut along dozens of radial silica creases with the dry *clack* of an iron fan, rolling itself into a javelin-thin cylinder in a fraction of a second. It shoots straight through the aperture on momentum alone, clears the lintel by a finger’s breadth, and blossoms wide again in the courtyard beyond with a muffled *whump* that sends nearby vine-curtains swaying.

These great mantles are the ones playing the city’s organ. 

Blind in a cavern of smoke-dimmed red gloom and lethal white sun-needles, they see entirely by the acoustic shadows of the builders’ stonework. Every neighborhood has its own chord; every pillar splits the roaring wind into a recognizable wake; every hollow dome rings like a bell struck by the updraft. The mantles do not merely listen to this map—they manipulate it to hunt, to claim territory, and to call across the four-hundred-pace gulf.

A hunting mantle will slip silently up the leeward side of a terraced house, using the turbulent dead-air behind the volcanic blocks to climb without effort. Reaching a row of high, hooting window-slits where a shoal of small piezoelectric ribbons is feeding in the draft, the mantle curves its broad body across the stone openings without touching them—acting as a living baffle. Inside the room beyond, the screaming wind stops dead. Deprived of their updraft and disoriented by the sudden, unnatural silence, the small ribbons stall and tumble downward in a glittering shower. Before they can catch the lower crosswinds, the mantle peels away from the wall, dives through the archway behind them, and scoops dozens at a time into a pleated, funnel-like fold along its underside, where rasping plates of volcanic glass grind their brittle frames to combustible dust and swallow their warm lifting-gas.

To speak to one another through the deafening clatter of the plants, the mantles hover before the carved resonance chambers the vanished builders once cut into the lintels. By curling and uncurling their wing-tips across a flue-mouth, they bend the cavern’s monotonous drone into long, sliding phrases—deep, brassy pulsing patterns that cut straight through the whetstone screech of the lower jungle. Another mantle two hundred paces away, drifting past six transparent layers of empty rooms, will bank out of the central thermal to answer, stopping and unstopping a chimney-slot until the whole upper quarter of the city seems to be trading slow, hollow breaths.

Even sleep and breeding happen entirely off the stone. 

Down beneath the lowest plazas, where the deep, throbbing hums roll out of the builders’ ancient dry cisterns, the sound pressure is so intense and uniform that standing acoustic waves form cages of invisible force inside the chambers. Slipping down through wide floor-grates, exhausted skimmers and newly molted juveniles drift into the exact acoustic nodes of those booming bass notes. Held suspended yards from walls and floor alike by the equal push of the sound from every side, they fold themselves into tight, metallic spindles and hang motionless in midair, trembling faintly with the pitch of the room for hours until they unfurl and ride an exhaust flue back to the streets.

The largest mantles never descend that far; their spans are too wide for the cistern grates, and the heat near the glowing bedrock would blister their gas-bladders. They rest high under the sagging slag roof, riding the slow outer turnaround in sleeping pairs and trios, hooked lightly together by static-charged trailing filaments stretching yards behind them like tarnished copper wires. When the exploding plant-gourds below crack like whips in rapid succession—signaling a peak in thermal pressure—mated pairs spiral upward into the hottest pocket beneath the ceiling and release frothy clusters of fused, glassy spheres no bigger than peppercorns. Filled from the start with hot hydrogen sulfide and tiny flecks of catalytic copper, the egg-rafts never fall. They bake hard in the rising furnace draft, split open on their first plunge down the cool outer salt-walls, and spill thumb-sized, transparent slivers that catch the inward sweep of the wind just above the rust-colored fronds, flexing microscopic ribs for their very first spark of ash.

And when one finally fails—torn open by the whip-crack shrapnel of a bursting spore-pod, or grown too brittle after years of flexing in kiln air—it still leaves no trace on the smooth, crescent-worn stairs. What is heavy in it, the sintered silica spars and tiny beads of copper slag in its catalytic chambers, plummets straight through the open grid of the houses and drops into the cherry-red floor trenches, melting back into the glowing mineral crust with a brief green flare. What is light, the broad bronze-and-mica veil, catches the thermal updraft one last time, tumbling upward alongside the shed plant-leaves until the roaring wind pins it against the dark, vitrified ceiling and sands it slowly back into the red dust that swallows the sun.
