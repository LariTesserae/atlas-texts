At first, you would look right at one and think it was a length of peeled root-bark curled around a stem, or a long, pale gall swollen into the joint of a sucker twig.

Even if you knelt in the silt beside the low thicket—careful not to raise the talc-fine dust—and held your breath until the blood beat in your ears, nothing would give it away. There is no rise and fall of ribs. No bead of moisture glistens at a nostril; moisture here is currency too dear to leave exposed to the air. What clings along the underside of the grey-green stem is seven inches of narrow, dry-skinned stillness, flattened tight against the wood from chin to tail-tip, colored the exact chalky ash of lime-silt with faint, lengthwise striations like checked cedar.

Only if you waited out the afternoon, until the pewter patch on the floor slid eastward by a hand’s breadth and faded into charcoal, would you see the bark-curl unseal itself from the wood.

It does not dart. Darting belongs to creatures of the hot plain overhead, things that burn sugar in a rush to escape hawks or catch flies before the sun cooks their brains. Here there are no hawks, and there have been no flies in living memory. The movement begins at the shoulder: one slender, disproportionately long forelimb detaches from the stem with the slow, deliberate smoothness of heated wax bending. The hand at the end of it has five toes—three turned inward, two opposed like thumbs—tipped not with sharp hooks that would rasp and skitter on dry bark, but with broad, dry pads ribbed in microscopic lamellae, soft as chamois leather. It places the hand three inches higher up the taproot. Minutes pass. Then the opposite hind leg follows.

Seen clear of the thicket against the pale thigh-thick column of a root, it looks something like a gecko that has forgotten urgency and grown attenuated over ten thousand generations in a windless room, and something like a tiny, hairless lemur pared down to raw essentials. Its body is narrow in the beam, deep-chested in a spare, bony way, loose folds of velvety skin hanging slightly along its flanks like an oversized coat. Its tail is shorter than its body, thick at the base and tapering steeply—not a whip for balance, because nothing here leaps, and not a fat-store bulging with grease, because large reserves attract nothing here and cost weight to carry up vertical wood, but a stiffened prop shod underneath with overlapping, keeled scales like the belly of a snake. When it stops on the sheer column of a taproot, it presses that tail-base into the grain and sits on it as securely as a lineman on a belt, freeing both long-fingered hands.

Its head is broad at the temples and blunt-muzzled, dominated by two enormous eyes that do not bulge outward like a nocturnal frog’s—exposed wet domes would dry and craze in this air—but sit deep behind flat, transparent spectacles of fused scale, polished and dry as watch-crystal. In the pewter twilight, the pupils do not open into round black wells; they part as four-lobed pinholes that gather the spent, thrice-bounced daylight from the chimney without dazzle, resolving every grain of ochre sandstone, every hairline crack in the digging sticks across the room, in sharp, shadowless monochrome.

There are eleven of them in the chamber. There have been between nine and fourteen of them for longer than the river cobble has rested beside the grinding slab.

They are not cave-blind, and they are not transient strays dropped down the chute to starve. They are *kettled*—descendants of some rock-crevice climber that found this pocket after the water left and before the surface scrub retreated to a few iron-rooted giants, and then stayed while the plain above baked harder century by century, narrowing the crooked chimney until the upper world became an incinerator no soft-bodied thing could cross by day and a windy waste no slow thing could survive by night. While the country outside stripped itself down, the pocket held steady at skin-warmth. And inside that constancy, they shed hurry.

Watch the big female halfway up the easternmost root—the thick one sheathed in curling papery bark. She may be forty years old; she may be seventy. In a room that never drops a degree in winter or spikes in July, where no owl hunts and no fungus rots a scratch, age barely marks tissue. You can tell she is ancient only by the slight polish on the keeled scales under her tail and the pale, healed notch in the crest of her neck, taken decades ago in some silent dispute over a sap-well.

She has climbed six feet above the silt floor, moving at an inch a minute, leaving not a single dislodged flake of bark to fall. Here, where a pair of taut twin roots twist around the larger column, she stops, plants her tail-prop, and turns her flat crystal-shielded eyes not outward into the cavern, but inward against the wood. Her wide, lipless mouth—edged in a clean, continuous bevel of translucent horn like the beak of a box turtle, stained a faint mahogany brown at the seam—presses close to a tiny, darkened dimple in the root’s flank.

For a long time she simply listens with her jawbone.

To human ears the chamber is dead silent. Through the solid horn of her lower jaw pressed tight to the tensioned wood, the taproot is loud. Twenty-five feet overhead, out in the unseen furnace of the lowland plain, wind is wrenching at the crown of an ancient mesquite or ironwood; down here, the anchor cable hums with minute, groaning shifts of fiber. Deeper still, fifty or eighty feet below the silt floor where the root’s blind hairs braid into a sealed lens of Pleistocene water, hydrostatic pressure pushes upward as the upper tree transpires in the heat. Sap—thick with dissolved sugars, bitter tannins, and trace salts—draws up the xylem in microscopic, ticking surges.

She knows this well. Her grandmother opened it; her daughters share it. Around the tiny dimple is a neat, raised collar of grey scar-callus where the root has tried for half a century to seal the puncture and been gently, fastidiously pared back.

Bracing both opposable hands against the bark, she tilts her head and uses the single forward-jutting tooth at the tip of her lower jaw—a narrow, chisel-edged incisor hardened with iron oxide, orange-red like a beaver’s—to lift the hardened amber plug of dried gum that seals the hole. She does not drop the plug. Nothing is dropped here. She takes the brittle bead of resin onto her rough, nearly dry tongue, rolls it back between flat crushing plates in her palate, and grinds it with two almost inaudible *snicks*.

In the unsealed pinhole, under the steady pump of the deep tree, a tiny hemisphere of clear, faintly yellow fluid swells until its skin tenses. Before a single molecule of it can evaporate into the thirsty room and be lost, she fits her horned mouth tight over the callus-ring, forming an airtight gasket, and drinks the drop whole.

Over the next two hours, without shifting her feet, she will take six drops. When the sixth has been drawn, she will scrape a ribbon of fresh cambium shavings no wider than an eyelash from the rim of the callus, chew it into a paste with a minute fraction of viscous mucilage from a gland beneath her tongue, and tamp it neatly back into the pinhole with her thumb-pad until the well is stoppered flush. An untended wound bleeds sap down the bark; wet bark draws mold spores out of the dust or starves the sucker-thickets below; a root pushed too hard walls off the whole vascular bundle and dies down one side, turning to dry ironwood no tooth can breach. Whether by bone-deep instinct honed in a closed box of six trees, or by something slower and cooler that approaches husbandry, they never bleed a cable dry, and they never leave a tap open to the air.

Down on the floor, where the silt lies level around the buried boulders, a juvenile—three years hatched, barely larger than a human thumb, patterned in sharper slate-and-bone bars that will fade as he ages—moves through the pale, leafless thicket of root-suckers.

He does not walk ON the silt. Silt is treacherous to lungs that never sneeze out dampness, and tracks are wasteful work; digging through loose flour-dust takes ten times the effort of bridging across stones. Instead, he travels the low architecture of the room: from root-collar to half-buried limestone block, from block along a fallen, petrified branch of dead scrub to the tilted sandstone grinding slab left by the people who came once and went away.

When he reaches the grinding slab, he pauses on its rim, right beside the river cobble sitting in its dimple of dust.

In the polished oval dish worn into the sandstone lies something that was not mentioned at first glance, because to an eye not tuned to this room’s scale it looks merely like three grains of coarse dark pepper.

It is the scat of the elders. In a body engineered never to urinate liquid water, waste leaves once every several weeks as a dry, faceted pellet smaller than a coriander seed: pure crystalline uric acid wrapped around a microscopic core of indigestible wood-lignin and red mineral grit, odorless, dry as glass beads before it ever touches the stone. For reasons nobody could prove—perhaps only because the polished basin is smooth and catches the dim light, or perhaps because keeping nitrogen and salts off the silt floor keeps the soil chemistry around the roots unchanged—every one of the eleven deposits those tiny, hard pellets in two places only: the hollow of the human grinding dish, and a natural cup in a boulder back in the dark apse. Over centuries, thousands of the tiny pellets have broken down into a tablespoon of sparkling, pinkish-white mineral powder in the bottom of the metate, stirred occasionally when a youngster walks across it and licks up a grain of reclaimed potassium salt.

The juvenile touches his dry tongue once to the glossy center of the dish, tastes the faint alkaline bite on the river-stone, and climbs down the slab’s undercut overhang toward the four flakes of banded brown chert lying below.

Under the lip of the largest chert flake—shelter within shelter, where the stone meets the packed silt—something else lives, the only other pulse in the vault.

They are smaller than sesame seeds: wingless, pearly-white booklice or silverfish cousins flattened thin as tissue paper, blind, coated in a waxy bloom that keeps their minute drop of body-water locked inside them. They graze, one cell at a time, on the papery curls of shed bark around the roots, on the tough leather of the old split puffballs in the dark, and on the crisp seed-husks left beside the slab thousands of years ago—eating so sparingly that the little heap of husks has shrunk by scarcely a handful since the diggers stood up and dusted off their knees. Even the hollow beetle husks caught against the root-collars are not untouched; inside their dark thoraxes, these microscopic white specks have rasped the inner chitin paper-thin, leaving the outer shells intact like false fronts.

The thumb-sized juvenile freezes beside the brown chert flake. His flat crystal eyes tip downward. Beneath the translucent horn of his throat, a narrow gular pouch ripples once—not a croak, no air wasted over vocal cords, just a sudden snap of hyoid cartilage that sends a single, dry click through his toe-pads into the flint.

Under the flake, startled by the vibration in the stone, one pale speck of a bark-louse steps half a millimeter out from the shadow. The juvenile’s head does not lunge; only his tongue moves, flashing out half an inch and back so fast the pewter light barely catches its pale grey tip, folding the speck inward. One speck today. Perhaps another tomorrow evening. It is protein enough to grow a millimeter of bone between now and next summer.

What is strangest about them, if you stayed long enough for your own impatience to quiet down and match the room, is how they keep company.

Creatures so sparse and miserly with calories ought to be solitary, fierce little hermits guarding their inches of wood. Instead, when the grey stain beneath the crooked chimney fades out entirely and true, seamless black fills the vault from talus to apse, they gather—not in a tangled heap, because skin-to-skin piling would trap no extra heat in a room already near blood temperature, and rough scales rubbing together wear out epidermis that takes months to regrow—but in quiet constellations.

Three or four of them will climb down from their separate cables and meet on the two hardwood digging sticks tucked into the horizontal seam of the wall.

They prefer the digging sticks to any stone ledge. The fire-hardened points and hand-smoothed shafts—seasoned to iron hardness before the pyramids were stacked, impregnated long ago with faint traces of human palm-salt and wood-oil—hold the exact warmth of the rock wall while giving their soft, lamellar toe-pads a clean, round grip free of silt. They line up along the upper stick an inch apart, noses all pointed the same way into the dark, tails propped against the sandstone below.

In the pitch black, one will reach out a long, slow, five-fingered hand and lay its dry palm lightly on the crown of its neighbor’s skull.

They communicate entirely by touch and bone-conduction. Through that resting hand pass tiny, deliberate tremors of muscle—slow trills at frequencies too low to stir the air, felt directly through the flat bones of the head. A long, three-second purr of tendons in the wrist; a pause of half a minute; two faint taps of an inner thumb against a brow-ridge. No one watching with infrared glass could translate it, yet the neighbor will answer ten minutes later, shifting one hind foot and sending a slightly different tremor back down the seasoned wood of the digging stick into the soles of all three companions.

Once every four or five years, when some rare, multi-day deluge far away on the upper plain finally percolates a ghost of extra pressure down into the deep aquifer eighty feet below, the taproots swell almost imperceptibly—a fraction of a millimeter more turgor in the wood, a slightly sweeter flow at the callus-wells. They feel that distant rain weeks after the sky above has turned back to brass, reading it through their jaws against the cables.

Only then will one of the females lay a single egg.

It is oval, large for her slender pelvis, shelled not in brittle chalk like a house-gecko’s, nor soft parchment that would shrivel like a snake’s, but in a dense, porcelain-hard ceramic glazed with a resinous secretion that dries impervious to the desiccated air. She does not bury it in the suffocating silt. She carries it in both opposable hands down into the deepest apse of the chamber, searches out a narrow pocket high in the scalloped limestone flute, and cements it to the warm rock with a ring of chewed root-gum.

Then she leaves it—not out of indifference, as a turtle leaves a nest, though she never sits on it; the stone itself is the mother’s belly, holding its unwavering ninety degrees day and night, winter and summer. Every three or four days during the nine months it takes the embryo to assemble itself at the slow, thrifty pace of this world, one of the adults—sometimes the mother, sometimes a male, sometimes an unmated aunt—climbs up into the dark apse, presses a horned jaw lightly against the porcelain shell to listen for the microscopic scratch of developing cartilage inside, and stands beside it for an hour before climbing back down to the roots. When the hatchling finally scores the cap from the inside with its iron-red egg tooth and slips out dry and wide-eyed, it eats every shard of its own shell down to the cement ring before taking its first slow step toward the dim grey glow fifty paces away.

And when one of them finally dies—when eighty or a hundred years of sparing heartbeats reach their end, and the tiny spark simply stops without fever or thrashing—it does not fall into the dust to lie beside the hollow beetles.

Perhaps it is only that their grip locks as muscle dries, as a bird’s toes lock on a perch; or perhaps, feeling the slackness coming on, an old one climbs deliberately away from the living cables and the clean floor. Either way, if you brought a lamp—though a lamp would be a violence here, blinding eyes tuned to pewter shadow and upsetting the still stratification of the air with its hot plume—and shone it high up into the narrow, horizontal seams near the shoulder of the vault, far from the crooked chimney, you would find them.

Tucked back in dry slots of the lime-bound grit, wedged upright on stiffened tail-props or gripping flakes of wall-stone with hands that never let go, sit dozens upon dozens of them.

Because nothing rots here, and no dermestid beetle or damp mold has ever entered to unmake them, they are whole. Dried cleanly in the cedar-sweet warmth to the weight of dry leaves, their loose velvet skin shrunk tight and smooth over delicate ribs, their mahogany-rimmed beaks shut neat, their flat crystal eye-scales clouded just slightly to the color of pale smoke, the ancestors sit in rows along the high ledges. Some have been perched there since before the people came down the talus with the river cobble and the brown chert; some joined them while empires rose and blew away in dust miles beyond the plain; one near the end of a seam still has the faint slate-bars of middle age, having climbed up there only two summers ago.

Below them, the gray stain of morning arrives again down the twice-bent chute, pooling silent and shadowless on the silt. Halfway up the thickest taproot, a curl of ash-colored bark opens two four-lobed pinholes to the light, waits twenty minutes for no reason at all except that time is boundless, and moves one soft, five-fingered hand an inch toward the well.
