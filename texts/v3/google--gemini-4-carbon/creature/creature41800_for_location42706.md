Up against the flat ceiling slabs, where that single hand’s breadth of sour, sulfurous air sits trapped like an ancient bubble, you will sometimes see a nostril break the surface. 

It does not break it cleanly. The water has a skin of bacterial grease that stretches before it parts, clinging to a wide, pale snout that is less like flesh and more like wet dough. There is a slow, syrupy exhalation—barely a sigh, because making a ripple wastes energy—and then an equally slow draw of the stagnant, metallic air. The slit valves shut with the faintest wet *clack*, and the snout sinks back into the violet-tinged gloom before the grease has even finished pulling back together.

There are never more than three or four of them in the entire drowned abbey at any given century. The cistern cannot afford more. 

If you could drain the chamber and lay one out on a dry slab, you would be looking at the ruin of a quadruped—something that might once have been an alpine salamander pinned inside the mountain when the roofs were sealed, or perhaps a semi-aquatic mammal brought into the baths as a curiosity, or a sacred pet, or meat, before the drains choked. Time and the broth have erased the distinction, just as they erased the reliefs on the basalt blocks. Whatever coat or scale it possessed is ten thousand generations gone. Its hide is utterly naked, thick and loose as soaked chamois, hanging in soft, heavy pleats along its flanks so that more skin can touch the water. Through the translucent tallow of its belly, you can faintly make out the dark, coiled smudge of a gut packed with crushed chitin and limestone mud.

In the water, however, they do not look like beasts at all. They look like collapsed masonry, or pale, unspooled linen caught between the columns.

One of them is wedged right now in a narrow clerestory gallery ten feet below the ceiling, where a row of stunted pillars stands barely a forearm’s width apart. It has been there since yesterday, though *yesterday* has no meaning here. Its torso is thick and barrel-shaped, heavy with slow blood, buoyed almost to weightlessness by pockets of oily fat along its spine. Its ribs are cartilaginous and strangely hinged; when it needs to pass through a stone screen or a choked sluice-channel, the whole chest folds inward with a dull, rubbery creak, letting a creature the mass of a calf slip through an opening meant for a bucket.

Its limbs have lost the memory of bearing weight. The legs are long, white, spindly things with too many seeming joints—really just slack ligaments and uncalcified bone—ending in splayed, webbed fingers two spans long. It uses them the way a blind man uses canes, or the way a spider uses its web. Anchored lightly in the gallery, it stretches all four limbs outward in different directions: one long hand resting flat against a rounded lintel above, another hooked lightly around a bundle of parchment tube worms, the hind feet splayed wide against opposite pillars. 

It is listening with its palms. 

Its eyes are gone—not even pits remain, just smooth, unbroken swells of gristle over the cheekbones where the skull knitted shut long ago to save the cost of growing an optic nerve. Instead, the skin of its throat, its broad flat snout, and the undersides of its long, attenuated fingers are stippled with thousands of dark, pinpoint pores, stained faintly turquoise by the ancient bronze that still cycles in trace amounts through the food web. Through these pores, it reads the hall in exquisite, microscopic detail.

It feels the ten-minute curl of a thermal plume rising from the floor vents thirty feet below, carrying the faint, tangy signature of fresh hydrogen sulfide. It feels the dry, ceaseless *scritch-scritch* of ten thousand plum-sized gastropods rasping the walls, mapping the shape of the room by the solid sheets of vibration their jaws make. When a finger-length eel whips out of a drainage flute six feet away, the tiny pressure wave strikes the creature’s outstretched palm like a plucked string. 

It does not chase the eel. Chasing is for worlds with sun in them.

Instead, it waits for the heavy things. Hours pass. A calcified pebble ticks down a submerged stair. The bacterial grease along the ceiling brightens slightly in one corner where a pocket of gas has risen, turning the gloom from charcoal to the color of an old bruise, and then dims again. 

Below the gallery, resting draped across a melted sacrificial bench, one of the flat, ray-like leech-creatures finishes its minute-long inhalation and decides to shift toward a warmer seep. It peels its leathery skirt from the sinter with a soft, mucous *shuck* and gives a single, languid undulation of its margins.

Above it, the pale thing among the pillars lets go of the stone. 

It does not kick. It simply exhales a tiny thread of hoarded air from a throat-sac—three silver beads that wobble straight up to flatten against the ceiling—and becomes just heavy enough to sink. It drops feet-first, limbs trailing upward like the pale roots of an overturned turnip, drifting down through the clouds of twitching, ash-like crustaceans without disturbing more than a dozen of them. 

It lands astride the bench with all the violence of a wet towel dropping into a tub. Before the leathery drifter can complete its next minute-long breath, the long, webbed hands have folded gently under its margins, and the wide, doughy mouth has unhinged. There are no teeth for tearing—tearing scatters scrapsinto the silt for the crabs—only broad, corrugated plates of horn set deep in the palate, like two cobbles fitted together. A single, tremendous contraction of the throat sucks the flat creature whole into the cavern of the mouth; the jaw shuts; the horned plates slide sideways once, twice, with a muffled, wet crunch that dies within three feet.

Then the hunter settles onto the bench where its meal was lying. Digesting that single mouthful in the blood-warm dark will take three weeks. Already its heartbeat—which rose to perhaps ten beats a minute during the drop—is slowing back down to three, then two. Soon the blind gastropods will crawl directly over its splayed feet and up onto its pleated flanks to graze the film of bacteria already settling on its skin, and it will not twitch to shake them off. In time, it will turn around and gently lip those same snails off its own hip.

Yet if you stayed in the cistern long enough—if you could somehow hang suspended in that charcoal soup for months without rotting—you would notice that these slow, folded sovereigns of the hall do more than eat and sleep and rise once an hour to kiss the foul air under the roof.

They have habits left over from a body they no longer own, or else born from the sheer, crushing density of the stone around them.

Down near the bottom of the complex, where the warm inflow rises faintly acidic through the baffled shafts and the silt lies knee-deep and smooth as butter, there is a sunken court ringed by low warming benches. Here, the water is slightly hotter—hotter than blood, closer to fever—and the turquoise stains in the rock are bright where a massive bronze sluice-gate once stood before dissolving into the soup.

Two of the pale creatures are down here in the dark, where even the violet ceiling-glow cannot reach, lit only by the rare, erratic ochre spark of piezoluminescence or deep-rock chemistry winking once in an hour from a stressed mortar joint. 

One is vastly larger than the individual in the upper gallery. Its skin has gone thick and warty with mineral encrustations that even the grazing snails haven't kept clean; small parchment tube-worms have actually rooted in the deep, stagnant creases behind its neck, waving their grey plumes like a ruff as it moves. Its snout is blunted and scarred with smooth keloids. It may be two hundred years old; in a world kept at body temperature with no predators and no seasons, aging is mostly a rumor, a slow stiffening of cartilage and an accumulation of stone in the kidneys.

The younger one—still the size of a hound, smooth as lard—floats parallel to the elder, an inch away without touching. 

Very, very slowly, they turn around one another in the fever-warm plume above the grate. An entire revolution takes twenty minutes. As they pass, they extend their long, spindly arms and run their pore-stippled palms down the length of each other’s flanks, never quite making contact with the skin—staying a millimeter off, stroking the boundary layer of warm water and shedding mucus, reading the chemical history of the other’s body, tasting its health, checking the fullness of its belly. 

There cannot be four and soon be five. That is the oldest law of the cistern, older than their blindness. 

If the great, encrusted matriarch feels, in the chemical plume radiating from the younger one’s gills-slits or cloaca, that the biomass in the upper vaults has grown thick—that the crustacean-ash is clouding the alleys too densely, that three drifters died last month and went uneaten to the isopods—she will permit what comes next, and a single, leathery egg the size of a melon will eventually be wedged deep into a warm flute of the masonry, guarded by four pale hands for a year until a translucent ribbon with fingers squirms out. 

If the water tastes lean, however, nothing happens. Or rather, if a fourth or fifth juvenile has somehow survived in the upper galleries when the snails are sparse, the slow waltz in the lower court changes its nature. Those long, boneless fingers wrap softly around the smaller throat, folding the hinged ribcage inward and holding it down here, thirty feet below that hand's-breadth of ceiling air, gently, patiently, without thrashing, until the small heart slows from four beats a minute to one, to none, and the body can be carried down to the acidic vents so its bones will dissolve cleanly back into the broth for the snails’ shells and the ceiling’s stalactites.

Nothing is cruel when it happens that slowly. It looks exactly like an embrace.

When the elder breaks off from the turn at last, it pulls itself hand-over-hand up a melted staircase, moving with the unhurried, flowing inevitability of rising smoke. Halfway up, it pauses at one of the narrow stone screens pierced with round portholes. 

It reaches one long, four-fingered hand through a porthole and grips the worn basalt rim on the other side. For an hour, it hangs there, gently rubbing the heel of its palm back and forth across the lip of the stone. Over centuries, generations of them have gripped this exact porthole to steady themselves in the rising plume, and their horned palates and leathery palms have polished a shallow, saddle-shaped groove into the ancient builder's masonry—a new relief carved over the dissolved ones, shaped purely to the convenience of a blind hand.

High above, its snout touches the greasy ceiling-skin. The nostrils part. In that tiny, coffined gap of air beneath the corbeled roof, where the creamy stalactites grow down a hair’s breadth every century to meet the pillars growing up, it lets out its breath—a low, hollow, liquid *whuff* that travels across the flat ceiling from vault to vault. 

Sixty feet away in the dark, behind three walls of drowned basalt, another snout rises through the bacterial tallow, parts its valves, and breathes the same air back in.
