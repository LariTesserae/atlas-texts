You feel them before you see them, if you know how to stand. Wait with your knees slightly unlocked on the silvered bark, tuning out the锯-toothed drone of the deep borers and the shimmering wall of cicada-static, and eventually you will catch a dry, deliberate double-tap coming up through your heels. *Knock-knock. Pause. Knock.* Not the scattershot thunder of hooves, and not the rhythmic sawing of insects, both of which belong to the plateau’s endless, unthinking background. This is wood striking wood with syntax. Three miles upwind, someone has taken an ironwood mallet to an exposed rib of heartwood to announce that they are approaching a tap-camp, that their hands are empty of drawn blades, and that a herd-mat is swinging east toward the sunken basins.

When the tapper finally crests the swell of a weathered burl ridge, squinting through the turpentine shimmer, they look half-cured by the same dry thermals that polished the floor.

They are humans, or close enough in the bone to have come from us a thousand generations back, though the plateau has pared them down and thickened them in strange places. They tend toward the lean, barrel-chested proportions of high-altitude walkers, wrapped from collarbone to shin in overlapping aprons of cured cicada-wing and oiled grazer-stomach to keep the sun from pulling the moisture straight through their pores. Their skin is stained a permanent, tea-dark mahogany in the creases of their knuckles, elbows, and eyelids—not entirely by pigment, but by a lifetime of dawn-fogs saturated with airborne tannins settling into their sweat. Around their eyes and nostrils, they smear a thick paste of boiled latex, tallow, and powdered chitin that dries into a flexible, pale mask, keeping the wind-blown epidermis dust from caking their lashes shut and shielding the delicate mucous membranes from the peppery, blistering fumes of fresh ground-bleeds.

Most striking are their shoes, and the way they move in them. Nobody walks flat-footed on the plateau for very long; to let your heel strike the bare, ringing ironwood all day is to invite a slow, cumulative concussion of the spine and inner ear from the sheer mechanical violence of the ground's vibration. Instead, they wear thick, boat-shaped rocker-soles woven from layers of springy cork and spongy dead pith, strapped high around the ankle with rawhide. This gives their gait a rolling, unhurried glide, knees bent, hips loose, absorbing the ceaseless tremor of the floor the way a sailor’s legs absorb the pitch of a deck. When they stop to listen to the country, they kick one sandal off and press the naked, calloused arch of a foot flat against a pale seam of dense heartwood, closing their eyes while their toes splay to read the direction and weight of distant impacts.

For they are nearly all deaf to the air, at least by the standards of lowland creatures.

Children born inside the pith-muffled galls hear well enough for their first four or five years. In the dim, submarine hush of the shelters—where the walls are hung with thick quilts of shredded bark-fluff and stuffed seed-chaff, and the narrow entrance tunnels dog-leg twice to break the soundwaves—mothers croon low, humming lullabies into the crowns of infants' skulls, chin pressed to fontanelle. Young children chatter in a brisk, soft, lip-heavy patois full of clicks and wet bilabials. Outside, nobody bothers opening their mouth against the sonic weather except to shout an immediate alarm, and you cannot shout for eighty years against a continent-sized violin without something giving way. By adolescence, the fine hairs of the inner ear have been flattened and scarred by the inescapable hundred-decibel days, even through plugs of greased wool and beeswax. An adult on the open bark hears the world above fifty cycles per second only as a bright, tinny hiss, like surf trapped in a shell.

They do not mourn this. They consider air-speech childish, leaking and imprecise, fit only for dark indoors and infants who haven't yet learned to hold a *stave*.

Every adult carries a stave: a five-foot shaft of seasoned, resonant ironwood shod at the butt with a cap of horn or native copper, and carved near the top into a flat, spatulate chin-rest. In camp, or standing shoulder to shoulder on a ridge watching the dark smear of a six-legged herd tear up a distant leaf-basin, two people will plant the butts of their staves against the same continuous grain of surface wood, rest their jawbones or mastoid processes against the smooth tops, and speak—not with their throats, though some vestigial hum still vibrates in the larynx, but by rapping their knuckles, copper rings, and thumbnail-guards in rapid, intricate polyrhythms along the shaft. The wood carries the crisp, dry click-trains straight from stave to floor to stave to skull. Five people resting their chins on their sticks around a tap-well can hold an overlapping, hilarious, fiercely argued conversation in the middle of a cicada-squall without parting their cracked lips, their eyes crinkling above their pale grease-masks while their fingers blur against the wood like spiders.

Closer in—when trading, courting, or working side by side over a steaming carcass—they drop the staves and speak directly onto one another’s bodies. Friends walk arm-in-arm so their fingers can tap shorthand grammar against the radius bone of a companion’s wrist. Lovers and spouses argue and reconcile in the pitch-black galls with palms laid flat against collarbones and sternums, humming low chest-tones of varying pitch while drumming fine inflections against the ribs. To touch a stranger without permission is unthinkable; to refuse your wrist to a camp-mate is the coldest possible shunning, rendering them instantly mute to you.

***

They call themselves nothing so grand as masters of the plain; translated from the knuckle-taps and hand-signs they use when trading at the far, dizzying rims where the wood-continent finally drops off into true stone, their name for themselves means something like *the Fleas*, or *Groomers*, or *Those Who Keep the Scabs Clean*.

It is an accurate ecological description. Left entirely to themselves, the camp-clusters around the great spore-chimneys cannot stay fixed forever; they live in slow, generational rotation across circuits two hundred miles wide. A clan of thirty or forty souls might hold three active gall-villages and half a dozen sleeping ones at any time, trekking the blistering day-marches between them along grooved, silvery paths worn smooth by centuries of cork-shod feet. 

You can smell an inhabited chimney-camp long before you reach its perimeter, because the sharp, turpentine-and-vinegar tang of the wild plateau gives way to the sweeter, fermented reek of curing sap and rendering grazer-fat. The towering spore-chimneys themselves—those crooked, three-hundred-foot mesas of fused, dead vascular bundle that vent dusty clouds of rust-colored reproductive powder once every twelve or fifteen years—serve as both windbreak and watchtower. Into their leeward flanks the clans carve steep switchbacks of toe-holds, climbing high above the ground-haze at dusk to hang wind-harps of braided gut that scare off roosting bone-jawed gliders, and to scan the thirty-mile horizon for the dust-plumes of herds or the amber gleam of a fresh latex bleed.

At the base of the chimney sits the architecture of irritation.

Making a home here is slow surgery, practiced mostly by the older women and men whose knees have stiffened too much for long herd-runs. They know the plateau's moods and humors the way a shipwright knows oak grain, and a physician knows sepsis. You cannot simply hack into any callus-ridge to raise a shelter; cut too deep into a pressurized phloem conduit and the ground will fight back, flooding your trench with scalding, pepper-fumed latex that sets like stone in hours, ruining good ironwood adzes and blistering lungs. Cut into wood too senescent and dry, and nothing swells at all—you only expose the hollow, roaring organ-pipes of the deep borers, letting the underground din pour unimpeded into the sky while pale, blind larvae as thick as sausages boil up out of the breach to see what broke their dark.

Instead, the gall-wrights probe first with thin copper needles and their bare feet, listening for young, sluggishly dividing cambium around old herd-scars. When they find a promising swell, they make shallow, crescent-shaped incisions and rub into the raw lip a brown salve brewed from crushed borer-saliva glands, fermented dawn-water, and bitter gall-wasp paste harvested from the parasites of the cicada-kin. The great floor recognizes the chemical lie instantly. Thinking itself invaded by a colossal larva, the plateau rushes rapid, swirling layers of defensive callus up and over the irritant, curling inward like closing petals until they kiss at the crown. Over three or four summers, the wrights return every few months to pare away inward-growing lumps, keeping the interior hollow twelve feet wide and domed like a beehive. When the walls are two feet thick, dense and cross-grained enough to turn a grazer’s chisel-beak, the wrights ring-bark the root of the swelling with hot embers and copper chisels. The sap stops rising; the dome dies in place, cures bone-hard in the sun, and ceases to transmit the living hydraulic throb of the surrounding skin.

Step down through the double-baffled crawlway of an established gall, pull the heavy plug-door of laminated cork tight behind you, and the sudden absence of noise hits you like a drop in barometric pressure. Your ears ring wildly for ten full minutes, inventing phantom cicadas to fill the void.

Inside, light comes from small clay-and-chitin dish lamps burning pale grazer tallow, or else through "amber-eyes"—fist-sized holes bored through the roof-dome and intentionally bled with clear surface resin until they seal over into thick, golden lenses that fill the room with honeyed, underwater sunbeams. Everything is hung from pegs rather than set on the floor, to break the final lingering bridge of vibration. Sleeping hammocks of knotted sinew sway gently a foot above the carpeted pith; gourd-like bladders of cured stomach-lining, plump with the morning's sweetish guttation-water or thicker, fermented sap-beer, dangle like strange fruit around the curved walls. There is no smoke-hole, because nobody burns open fires indoors—charcoal is precious, made carefully in smothered pits lined with shed chitin plates out on the dead heartwood ridges where the plateau won't catch or bleed, and used only in tiny, covered braziers to sear meat or soften horn.

***

They live well enough off the plateau’s blood, though no human gut can survive on sap and dawn-sweat alone. The water from the morning pores is clean enough once skimmed of wind-chaff, tasting faintly of willow-bark, magnesium, and hot leather—bitter enough to pucker a stranger’s mouth, though the people crave it and grow listless without its salts. The deeper sap-taps yield richer stuff: clear, pressurized xylem-broth from forty feet down for washing and boiling, and shallow, syrupy phloem-treacle that must be bled cautiously, a pint at a time, then boiled over tiny dung-and-husk fires with alkaline ash from burned insect shells to neutralize the laxative resins before it sets into dark, chewy sugar-cakes.

For protein, fat, and bone, there is the meat that shakes the floor.

Hunting the six-legged grazers out on the open leather-basins does not require stealth—nothing could sneak up on ten thousand animals whose feet are wired into the same drumhead as yours—so much as cold geometry and an intimate knowledge of how the continent bleeds.

When a herd-mat settles onto an acre of olive-black photosynthetic hide and begins ripping up great, rubbery ribbons with their heavy beaks, the plateau does not respond immediately. Its nerves are chemical and hydraulic, slow as pitch. For forty minutes or an hour, the beasts gorge freely while deep beneath them pressure builds in the defensive laticifers. Experienced hunters—usually bands of six or eight agile twenty-year-olds stripped down to greased loincloths, pale eye-paste, cork sandals, and harnesses bristling with bone-tipped harpoons—trot parallel to the herd’s flank, stopping every hundred paces to drop a bare heel to the hot membrane and feel the thickening turgor underneath. They can tell within three minutes when the floor is about to blow its valves.

Just before the surge breaks, two runners sprint upwind and unstop hollow gourds of concentrated alarm-pheromone rendered from crushed cicada-thoraxes, hurling them into the edge of the mat, or else they simply drive copper wedges into a seam of tight bark and beat an erratic, panicked thunder-rhythm with heavy mauls that mimics a collapsing xylem-tunnel. The herd bolts away from the noise—and if the hunters have judged the grain of the basin right, the outer hundred animals stampede straight across the rawest, deepest-chewed trench just as the plateau’s boiling white latex wells up through the lacerations.

Two or three beasts always hit the slick, sticky surge at a dead run, tangle their six legs, and go down thrashing. The rest of the mat parts around them like dark water around boulders and thunders onward, pursued by screaming flocks of bone-jawed gliders. Before the gliders can drop and before the latex cools from scalding soup into impenetrable rubber, the hunters dash in along thrown planks of light, dry pith, drive longironwood lances behind the trapped grazers' horn-plates to stop their suffering quickly, and haul the six-hundred-pound carcasses free with braided rawhide ropes—working in furious, silent choreography, communicating only by sharp whistles through bone pipes pitched high enough to cut above the cicada-band for fifty yards, and broad, slashing arm-signals through the peppery steam.

Nothing from a kill goes back to the plateau. The overlapping horn-plates become shingle-armor, digging blades, scrapers, and boiling-vessels (filled with sap-water and brought to a simmer by dropping in heated lumps of rare, prized copper or dense char-burls). The six leg-bones frame the dawn-water tripods and litter-stretchers; the sinews string wind-harps and bows; the pale, sweet meat is sliced paper-thin and hung from the shady side of the spore-chimneys, where the arid afternoon gale dries it crisp as bark in four hours flat.

Even the borers underneath have their season. During the dead-hot midnights of high summer, when the deep xylem channels dry out just enough for the giant larvae to crawl near the surface vents to molt, whole camps go out under the blazing, sharp-edged stars with long bone gaffs and ear-trumpets made of coiled horn. Pressing the wide bell of the horn to the silvery bark and the narrow end against their cheekbones, the children—whose ears are still sharp—point straight down at the frantic *scritch-scrape* of a molting chamber three feet under the crust. The adults prize up a trapdoor of cork with ironwood crowbars, hook out a dozen wriggling, forearm-thick grubs before the surrounding wood wakes up, tamp the plug neatly back into place, and seal the seam with chewed wax so the plateau never even knows it was burgled. Roasted in hot chaff-ash, the borers taste of pine nuts and marrow.

***

Because stone and metal do not exist here unless carried across four hundred miles of flat, screaming wood from the unimaginable rim-lands, wealth among the chimney-clans is measured in weight, hardness, and silence.

A palm-sized nodule of black flint, passed from clan to clan for eight generations until it has been knapped down to a thumb-stub, has a personal name, a genealogy tapped out at winter feasts, and a dedicated pouch of softest unborn-grazer skin. Native copper—soft, red, beaten cold between ironwood anvils and polished smooth against shark-skin-rough old bark—is worn as heavy thumb-rings for stave-talking (giving a brighter, more carrying *ting* through the wood than naked bone or horn) and forged into the sacred surgical adzes of the gall-wrights. When a person dies, their copper is never buried with them; metal belongs to the living camp.

Nor is anyone buried at all. You cannot dig a grave in living ironwood, and if you force a body into a dry fissure or an abandoned borer-pipe, either the scavengers drag it back out by noon or the plateau’s slow, warm defense-wood encapsulates the bones and digests the calcium over fifty years to feed its own mineral-starved leaves. To the clans, letting the floor eat your kin feels vaguely incestuous, too blurred—they spend their whole lives keeping the boundary distinct between their brisk, warm animal blood and the slow, hot, turpentine pulse underfoot.

Instead, they carry their dead up the notched flanks of the solitary spore-chimneys, all the way to the cracked, wind-scoured rims three hundred feet above the plain, where the wood has been dead and sun-bleached for ten thousand years and no sap ever rises. There, lashed upright inside wicker cages of split heartwood to keep the bone-jawed gliders from scattering their ribs across the sky, the ancestors dry out cleanly in the sterile, stratospheric glare—turning silver-grey, papery, and hollow, until you cannot tell an old grandmother’s shinbone from a weathered splinter of the chimney itself. In high winds, the dry joints and woven cages knock softly against the dead mesa top, sending a faint, irregular *clack-clack-clack* down the vertical grain of the spire. People resting their backs against the base of the chimney in the evening shade feel that dry rattle in their shoulder blades, smile behind their grease-masks, and tap a brief, respectful greeting back up the wood with their knuckles.

Every twelve or fifteen years, one of the great chimneys wakes up from deep below. For weeks beforehand, the floor for five miles around changes pitch—the deep bass roar of the borers goes silent as millions of insects flee the rising heat, replaced by a low, pressurized hydraulic groan that makes water dance in the storage bladders and loosens teeth in old gums. The clan needs no warning twice. Within two days they have unpegged their deeper horn-taps, rolled up their pith-quilts, loaded their copper, flint, and dried meat onto travois sleds pulled by teams of broad-backed adults, and marched twenty miles cross-wind to a sleeping sister-camp whose galls they have kept pared and dry for just this season.

Looking back across the flat shimmer three days later, they watch their old home spire blow its crown: a silent, mile-high column of rust-red spore-smoke punching straight into the cobalt sky before flattening along the thermal inversion and drifting downwind to dust some unimaginably distant edge of the continent. By the time the clan returns two years later, the camp at the spire’s foot is half-swallowed in fresh, glossy amber bleed and new grey cork, the old bone tripods lifted crookedly into the air by rising bolsters of scar-tissue.

They set down their sleds, kick off one sandal each to feel if the pulse underneath has settled back to its normal, sleepy simmer, and pull out their copper-ringed adzes to begin irritating the skin back into rooms.
