At first, you mistake them for surveyors’ tripods left out to rust in the mist, or perhaps the mobile, counterweight-driven regulators of some sub-street valve system. When one crosses an intersection three hundred yards off, it does not hurry to get out of the wet. Nothing here hurries out of the wet. It moves with the slow, flat-footed, rolling cadence of a creature whose joints are lubricated by hot brine and whose lungs are accustomed to drinking soup.

If you wait by one of the bronze foundation-grates—standing still enough that the whippet-sized chitin-grazers do not shy away—one of the wardens will eventually pass close enough to detach itself from the pewter gloom.

They are human, or were engineered from human stock long enough ago that the plateau has finished rewriting their proportions. Tall—nearly seven feet, though stoop-shouldered—and astonishingly spare, they have the stretched, attenuated look of the city’s own grid. In an atmosphere where sweat cannot evaporate to cool the blood, compact bulk is a death sentence. Everything about them has been pulled outward to maximize surface area, turning the body into a walking radiator. Their limbs are long and flat-boned; their fingers are extended, heavy-knuckled, and webbed slightly at the base with thin, vascular skin that flushes dark maroon when they exert themselves. 

They wear almost nothing that you would recognize as cloth. Woven fibers rot in days here, souring into gray pulp. Instead, a warden wears a loose harness of cured, oil-rubbed leather harvested from the giant louver-moths, slung across a torso that gleams with a permanent, slick sheen of mineral grease. Over their shoulders hangs a mantle of overlapping, paper-thin sheets of beaten copper and vulcanized gutta-percha—not to keep the condensation off, which is impossible, but to channel the hot, saline trickle away from their eyes and the tender, gill-like folds of scarred tissue along their throats. Their skin has the pale, dense, slightly translucent quality of boiled tallow, stained permanently ochre at the ankles, knees, and fingertips from centuries of wading through iron-rich runoff and handling oxidized brass.

Watch the warden’s feet as it stops beside the grate. It wears no boots. Boots fill with hot water and parboil the flesh. Instead, its soles have thickened into wide, splayed pads of horn-gray callus, deeply grooved like the tread of an industrial tire to grip the slimy, pillowed basalt. When it stands entirely still, it shifts its weight subtly from heel to toe, feeling the bedrock.

To live here is to read vibrations through the bone. Eyes are nearly useless past fifty yards in the charcoal soup, and the damp air smothers spoken words before they can cross a courtyard. The wardens are not deaf, nor are they mute, though you could follow one for three days without hearing a cough. Their ears are small, tight to the skull, protected against fungal spores by thick tufts of coarse, coppery hair. Their real listening happens through those broad, calloused feet, and through the long, hexagonal tuning-staff each one carries—a seven-foot rod of phosphor-bronze shod with an iron shoe. 

Watch: the warden sets the butt of the staff against a sunken rivet in the grate’s collar, leans forward, and presses its cheekbone—just below the wide, heavy-lidded eye, which is filmed over by a faint, milky nictitating membrane against the salt—directly against the metal shaft. It stays like that for four minutes. Five. The low *thump* of the condenser tower half a mile away travels through the wet basalt, up the bronze rod, and straight into the warden’s jawbone. It is listening to the seating of a check-valve three hundred feet underground. It can tell, by the microscopic harmonic rattle at the end of the stroke, that a calcium crust is building up on the southern flange, and that in six or seven years, the crane will need to bring a new cylinder over from the foundry.

This explains the crane’s empty cab. There is no one sitting up there in the amber light because no one needs to sit in a glass box baking at a hundred and ten degrees just to pull a lever once an hour. Look down at the tracks beneath the gantry. Half a mile back, sitting on a low basalt bollard with the warm water swirling around its ankles, another figure has a heavy brass key slotted into a junction box rising out of the stone. It turns the key a quarter-inch, feels the distant electric draw hum through the soles of its feet, waits twenty minutes while the cylinder crawls across the sky, and turns it back.

Why the staggering intervals? Why build a city where neighbors are separated by twenty-minute walks through blank, weeping fog?

Follow the warden when it lifts its staff and turns away from the avenue, heading toward a squat, beehive-shaped cairn of glazed brick sitting isolated in the middle of a half-mile square of hexagonal paving. There are no residential blocks here. There are no tenements, no markets, no taverns, no dense warrens of domestic life. If five hundred people crowded together into an apartment block on this plateau, their combined metabolic heat, trapped in the saturated air, would cook them in their sleep—and worse, a localized concentration of organic waste and breath would trigger a bloom of the porcelain-hard blister-fungi ferocious enough to cement their doors shut in a month and choke their lungs in two. 

The city’s emptiness is quarantine and radiator both. Just as the turbine housings and condenser towers are spaced apart so the bedrock beneath them doesn't crack from localized thermal stress, the inhabitants must be diluted across the grid.

They live down, not up, tucked into cool pockets between the thermal veins. The glazed-brick cairn is a stairwell hood. Follow the warden inside, down forty spiral steps cut into dry, sealed basalt, through two heavy counterweighted airlocks of greased leather that strip the worst of the wet heat from the air. Down here, insulated from the deep geothermal conduits by thick jackets of volcanic ash and cooled by closed-loop pipes carrying cold brine pumped up from the plateau’s far, unseen rim, is a single chamber.

Three people live in this vault. Three is the usual number; four makes the air too thick; two is too lonely even for them. 

In the amber glow of a low-wattage carbon filament—the same timeless, indestructible bulbs that burn in the crane cabs—they look less like ghosts and more like amphibians resting in a burrow. One of them, older, its skin marbled with the blue-black tattoos of mineral dust embedded in old steam-burns, sits on a bench of polished slate, scraping thin, translucent ribbons of porcelain-fungus into a stone mortar. Boiled in fresh condensate, cut with sulfur-slime and the rich, fatty meat of the chitin-grazers trapped at the grates, the fungus yields a dense, nutty paste that smells faintly of yeast and hot iron. There is no wood down here, no paper, no cloth. Their beds are shallow troughs of warm, dry basalt sand that shifts to fit the spine. Their records—generations of valve- pressures, fungal migrations, crane schedules, and births—are pricked with a steel stylus onto rolled sheets of thin lead foil stacked neatly in alcoves.

When the returning warden steps out of the airlock, nobody speaks. Instead, the newcomer crosses the cool slate floor, reaches out a long, webbed hand, and lays two fingers lightly against the older one’s collarbone. 

The older one hums—a low, closed-mouth, chest-deep vibration that drops nearly below the threshold of your hearing, modulated with sudden, percussive clicks of the teeth and shifts in pitch that travel directly from sternum to fingertips. The newcomer answers in the same sub-vocal rumble, eyes half-closed. To an outsider standing six feet away, the room is entirely silent save for the drip of the airlock seal. Between the two touching figures, an entire afternoon’s inspection of the eastern avenue is being recounted in rich, resonant detail: the slight wheeze in the number-four condenser, the fatness of the louver-moths on the pumping station wall, the strange, short-limbed visitor standing out in the middle of the intersection, foolishly letting its collar soak through and breathing way too fast.

They are not slaves to the machinery, nor are they marooned survivors clinging to the wreckage of a golden age they no longer understand. If you look at the lead scrolls in the alcoves, the diagrams are crisp, mathematical, exact. They know precisely how the turbines turn, why the plateau tilts, and what lies past the rim miles away where the clouds finally break over cold, oxygen-thin scree. They simply have no desire to go there, nor any desire to speed anything up. 

To live in a machine designed to run for ten thousand years without breaking, you must slow yourself down until you match its clock. Their resting heartbeats sit somewhere around thirty-two beats a minute. They do not experience boredom as you know it; what feels to you like a crushing, twenty-minute trudge through featureless gray soup between two streetlamps is, to a warden, a pleasant, meditative glide across a tapestry of shifting water-temperatures, subtle mineral scents, and the comforting, deep-earth bass line of the city’s iron heart.

Occasionally—perhaps twice in one of their long, slow years—twenty or thirty of them will gather out on one of the four-hundred-yard intersections to replace a primary main or guide the gantry crane as it lowers a fresh sixty-ton rotor into a turbine house. If you are lucky enough to witness this, it looks like a slow-motion ballet performed by tall, gleaming shadows in the fog. No foreman shouts. No whistles blow. They stand twenty yards apart in the ankle-deep, steaming water, connected to the heavy iron casing and to the basalt floor through their phosphor-bronze staffs. One strikes the stone twice with an iron heel; twenty pairs of bare feet catch the cue simultaneously; twenty long-limbed bodies lean against brass chain-falls in absolute, eerie unison. The only sound is the ratchet of oiled gears and the long, wet sigh of the sky.

When the rotor settles home with a deep, bedrock-shuddering *thud*, they do not cheer. They stand still for a full minute, eyes shut behind milky membranes, savoring the clean, true vibration of the newly balanced shaft spinning up beneath them. Then, one by one, without waving or looking back, they turn toward different points of the compass and walk away into the mist, dissolving within a hundred paces, returning the city to its necessary intervals.
