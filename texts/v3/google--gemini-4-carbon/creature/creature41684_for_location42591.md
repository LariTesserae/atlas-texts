You could look straight at a courtyard wall for twenty minutes in the bruised midday gloom—letting your eyes adjust until the lavender plates of coralline algae resolve from the split-granite blocks, counting the plum-sized bladders buoying a curtain of laminarian across an empty doorway—before realizing that a six-foot span of the coping stone is breathing. 

It does not breathe often. Once every quarter-hour, a wide, slit-like valve tucked beneath a ragged fringe of burgundy dulse parts slowly, draws in two gallons of four-degree water without stirring the velvet peat-silt an inch below, holds it for four minutes while cold, copper-based blood strips the thin dissolved oxygen from the brine, and lets it slip back out through a matching vent near its trailing edge. 

If you wait long enough—an hour, two—the entire slab of false masonry will unseal itself from the wall and slide three inches to the left.

At first glance, it seems to be a monstrous cousin of the little blind limpets rasping at the doorsteps: a single, massive muscular foot gripping the rounded stone, topped by a low, saddle-shaped mantle of tough, rubbery cartilage. There is no true shell—calcium is too scarce in this locked, mineral-poor sea to waste on heavy armor, and besides, there are no jaws down here to crack one. Instead, its back is a thick, cold hide of dense collagen, textured in the exact, rhythmic pitting of ancient bronze chisels and patched with living gardens of black wrack and chalky pink crust that took eighty years to establish on its skin. 

They have no eyes. Eyes are expensive organs, greedy for calories and useless during the twenty-hour dark. Instead, their mantles are fringed with dozens of pale, tapering tendrils the thickness of knitting needles, trailing delicately across the mortarless seams behind and ahead of them. They read the continent by touch and taste, mapping the pillowed corners, the rippled ramps that used to be stairs, the subtle chemical signature of granite versus slate.

They are grazers of the rot. In a world where fallen kelp blades lie intact on the avenues for thirty years, slowly turning to ghost-ribbons from the edges inward, these creatures are the only heavy plows the silt ever knows. One will settle over a ribbon of decaying laminarian that has draped across a cobbled causeway since before terrestrial trees grew on the distant shores above. Lowering a broad, cowled mouth to the stone, it does not tear at the tough weed; it presses a wide, warm-for-the-abyss digestive pad directly onto the translucent, bacterial-rich margins of the blade, sealing the edges down with thick mucus and letting slow enzymes liquefy the softened tissue over the course of three days. When it finally glides onward, it leaves behind a neat, pale stencil on the dark flagstones—a clean-licked rectangle of ancient masonry stripped down to the bare chisel-marks, startlingly bright against the surrounding turf of dark moss.

Because they follow the architecture—hugging the ninety-degree meets of wall and floor where the cold-loving bacteria pool thickest in the trapped peat-silt—their bodies have been shaped by the drowned grid. A mature specimen is roughly the width of a ancient wheelbarrow track or a standard cottage doorway. When one encounters a corner in a roofless foundation, it does not turn so much as flow, its boneless, hydraulic bulk folding smoothly into the right angle, molding itself over the sagging lintel or bulging retaining wall like heavy wet dough poured from a bucket, never breaking skin-contact with the dressed stone.

For a long time, watching one cross a plaza at the pace of an hour hand, you would think that is all there is to them: solitary, blind stomachs made of cured hide, keeping the avenues marginally clearer of century-old mulch.

Then the four-hour drift reverses. The vast canopy thirty meters overhead leans ten degrees to the west, and down in the sheltered lee of a collapsed granary, two of them meet on the top course of a boundary wall.

Neither retreats. There is no territoriality where food lies ankle-deep across ten thousand square miles of uninterrupted stonework. Instead, as their trailing sensory needles brush, both halt. Slowly, over twenty minutes, the smaller of the two—perhaps only a meter long, its back smooth and scarcely encrusted, no more than forty years out of its jelly-coat—slides up and directly over the broad, pitted saddle of the elder, clamping its pale under-foot onto the veteran’s leathery ridge. They stay stacked like double-coursed fieldstones while the dim grey bruise of noon fades entirely from the water column above, plunging the avenues into their twenty-hour black.

In that absolute dark, something happens along their flanks. It is not bioluminescence—nothing down here has eyes to appreciate a flash in the gloom. It is heat, infinitesimal and localized, accompanied by a slow, rhythmic contraction of their lateral valves that sends muffled, low-frequency *thrums* rolling down the granite wall. Stone conducts sound far faster and farther than water, and the ancient builders laid their courses tight. A pulse pressed directly into the dry-stacked slate of a paddock wall travels half a kilometer down the grid, felt through the muscular soles of three other grazers paused in distant courtyards and shallow canals. 

From three avenues away comes an answering *thump*—not the sharp *clack* of a falling eaves-shingle, which is flat and singular, but a double-beat, heavy and soft, like a wooden mallet striking wrapped leather.

They are mapping the settlement, or perhaps keeping count of one another across the labyrinth, using the continent-sized masonry itself as a sounding board.

And if you trace those muffled stone-vibrations outward from the low-walled paddocks, moving down the shallow, silt-choked canals toward the slightly deeper basins—where the ancient builders dug broad municipal cisterns and wide, tiered amphitheaters to hold the runoff of a drier age—you begin to notice that the stonework there is not quite right. 

At first it looks merely like frost-heave from before the flood, or an unusually collapsed pile of rubble at the center of a broad plaza. Then you realize that the ancient builders never stacked granite blocks in radial spirals, and they never mortared their seams with thick, ropey cables of translucent, rubbery byssus.

Something else has been moving the stones.

Wait beside one of these circular cairns while the current hangs motionless at the turn of the tide. The tall kelp stipes have been systematically cleared for twenty meters around the structure—not eaten, for their wrist-thick holdfasts remain wedged in the flagstone cracks, chewed through cleanly near the base by something possessing a pair of sharp, overlapping beaks made of hardened, black chitin. Without the tall canopy overhead, the faint midday gloom reaches all the way down to the cobbled floor here, revealing that the loose slate shingles, fallen lintels, and rounded cobbles from the surrounding roofless houses have been dragged inward across the velvet silt, leaving wide, shallow furrows that have not yet filled back in with bacterial snow.

Tucked into the central shaft of this hand-picked cairn—coiled down inside the cool, dark puzzle of re-stacked blocks—is the architect.

It is long, though how long is impossible to judge, because no one ever sees more than two or three segments of it out of the stone at once. It is neither fish nor worm nor true cephalopod, though it shares the slow, blue-blooded economy of the cold-water mollusks. What emerges from a gap between two split-granite headers is an arm—or a trunk—as thick as a man’s thigh, pale as lard beneath a loose, wrinkled sleeve of velvet-black skin. Instead of suckers, the underside of this limb is lined with flat, calloused pads of grippy, ridged cartilage, arranged like the treads of a work-boot, weeping a faint, tacky slime that hardens temporarily on contact with wet stone and dissolves again at a chemical cue from the skin. 

Three of these heavy, deliberate limbs uncoil simultaneously from different chinks in the cairn. They do not whip or writhe; rapid movement in four-degree water burns reserves that take months to replace. They extend with the smooth, hydraulic inevitability of tree roots growing in fast-forward, sliding out across the cleared plaza flagstones, feeling along the seams. 

One limb finds a flat, forty-pound slab of dressed slate that slipped from an eaves-course centuries ago. The pale, ridged pads press flat against the stone’s face; the black skin bunches as internal water-chambers pressurize, stiffening the boneless limb into a rigid crane-boom; and with a soft, sucking *shhhk* as the slab breaks free of the century-old peat-silt, the stone rises four inches into the water, swings slowly inward toward the cairn, and is fitted—with three minutes of fussy, blind adjustments—directly over a gap in the spiral wall, shimmed tight against a rounded granite block so it cannot rock when the continental current leans back to the east.

Why it builds is hard to say at first. There are no sharks in the avenues to hide from, no winter storms reaching seventy meters down to batter soft flesh against the thresholds. 

Look closer at the inside of the cairn’s lip, where the faint green-grey ghost of noon touches the newly laid slate, and you see the point of the masonry. Sheltered from the gentle, persistent sweep of the deep drift, the interior of the spiral acts as a dead-water trap. Every stray piece of shed kelp frond, every ribbon of detached red dulse, every drifting, dust-mote amphipod carried lazily down the miles of straight, canalized streets strikes the outer baffle of the cairn, eddies over the lip, and settles quietly into the still, steep-walled pit at the center. 

It is a weir, built to harvest a current too weak to notice with bare skin, concentrating a square kilometer’s worth of slow, drifting detritus into a single stone cellar.

Down at the bottom of that dark well, invisible beneath an overhanging lintel it dragged ten yards from a drowned stable, sits the rest of the creature: a soft, vast, sac-like mantle that has not left its hole in two hundred years, wrapped around a slow-beating heart the size of a cabbage and a cluster of thirty leathery, grapefruit-sized eggs anchored directly to the ancient cobbles. As the trapped kelp-fall rots in its cellar, thick clouds of pale, blind amphipods breed in the rich peat-soup, and tucked into the crevices between the stacked blocks, hundreds of thumb-sized pale anemones—transplanted intact on the stones the architect stole from distant benches—wave lazily in the enriched gloom. Occasionally, a fourth, much thinner feeding-tendril slips up from the dark center of the pit, delicately plucks a fat anemone or scoops a ribbon of bacterial jelly from the compost pile, and draws it back down out of sight.

Two streets over, through an empty window-frame draped in ten-meter laminarian curtains, one of the great saddle-backed grazers comes gliding down the avenue at its millimeter-per-second creep, following a thick seam of decayed wrack straight toward the cleared circle around the cairn.

When its leading sensory needles touch the fresh drag-furrow in the silt, the grazer stops. It does not retreat, and the architect in the cairn does not lash out with its heavy stone-lifting arms. Instead, one pale-soled trunk slides slowly out from a lower gap in the spiral wall and reaches across the cobbles until its ridged tip rests lightly atop the grazer’s pitted, chisel-marked dome.

For half an hour they stay like that in the freezing hush, while overhead a thirty-meter canopy of dark kelp hangs dead vertical at slack water. The architect’s limb moves with slow, thorough pressure over the grazer’s wide back—weeding it. With small, horny tips tucked beside its lifting pads, it plucks away the taller tufts of wiry black wrack that have begun to create drag on the grazer’s shell-less hide, peels off a loose flake of dead coralline crust, and scrapes up the rich film of silt that collects in the folds behind the respiratory valves, passing these tiny, meager tidbits back along a ciliated groove in its arm toward the dark cellar. 

When the grooming is done, the heavy arm withdraws into the masonry. The grazer vents two gallons of cold water with a barely audible *whuff* that stirs three grains of mica on the doorstep, pivots forty degrees to skirt the cleared radius of the weir, and resumes its week-long crossing of the intersection, leaving its clean-licked stencil gleaming faintly behind it on the ancient road.

They are the second tenants of the grid, and they are in no hurry at all. The bronze-chiseled stones will not dissolve for ten million years; the cold forest overhead makes new leaves out of almost nothing for four dim hours every day and drops them intact into the streets; the water stays at four degrees whether winter rages seventy meters up or summer briefly warms the grey chop. Down among the pillowed walls and sagging lintels, the blind stonemasons adjust their baffles by one shingle a month, and the living flagstones press low hums into the slate courses to let the next parish know they are still grazing the avenues—heirs to a continent already built, leveled, and laid flat for them before their kind ever learned to breathe the dark.
