Look closer at the seams where the great flanks meet. Between two locomotive-sized grazers, where pitted nickel grinds past iron sulfide at the speed of a growing fingernail, there is a vertical slot of violent, localized weather. Two inches to the left, the ambient air is thirty below zero; two inches to the right, a dorsal exhaust port coughs ninety-degree sulfur steam every forty seconds. Below, in the dark, high-voltage sparks pop off the barrel-symbiotes, smelling of scorched hair and ozone. Above, the ultraviolet glare off the quartz scarps is strong enough to peel paint.

Nothing soft-skinned or fast-blooded could survive five minutes in that gap. Anything that lives *among* the titans, rather than *as* one, must be built like a gasket, or a vise, or a refractory brick.

Wait for the steam to clear after a vent-cough, and you will see one of the things that holds the gap. 

At first, it looks like an extrusion of pipe-dope or hardened boiler scale wedged into the hinge of a titan’s third leg. Then a limb detaches from the mass, swings out with the stiff, cranked motion of a ratchet wrench, and hooks onto a neighboring beast’s dorsal rim. 

If you pried one loose—which would take a crowbar and two men, and would probably snap the crowbar—you would find something vaguely crab-like, vaguely arachnid, and entirely industrial in its weight. They are the size of heavy engine blocks, flat as manhole covers so they can slide between grinding hulls without being milled into paste. Their carapaces are not precipitated iron like the grazers, which would conduct the lethal cold right into their organs, nor are they electrostatic weld-slag. They are layered, biological ceramics—silicates and aluminum oxides cooked out of rock-dust and bound in dense, horny keratin, matte-white and chalky to the touch, insulated like space-shuttle tiles. One side of a single animal can be coated in rime ice while the other side sits flush against a blistering exhaust chimney, and its core temperature will not budge a tenth of a degree.

They have eight limbs, arranged radially, none of them built for walking. There is nowhere to walk. Instead, each leg ends in a dual-purpose clamp: a curved, serrated hook of hardened tungsten-carbide bone for catching onto shell-seams, paired with a broad, muscular suction pad greased with thick silicone mucus for sealing onto slick plates. They move through the packed continent like rock climbers ascending a horizontal chimney—hooking, locking the joint with a dry mechanical *click*, hauling their flat bulk forward three inches, and re-locking before a shifting fifty-ton flank can catch them at a bad angle. Occasionally, one miscalculates. You hear it when it happens: a brief, sharp *crack* like a ceramic toilet shattering under a sledgehammer, lost almost instantly under the bass rumble of the digestive mills, followed by the brisk, sizzling rush of tiny scavengers claiming the pieces.

Yet they are not mere barnacles or hull-lice. Watch three or four of them working a square yard of titan-hide, and the nature of their living becomes stranger.

A titan-grazer is too vast and numb to care about its own plumbing. When the heat-seeking creepers weave over its dorsal chimneys, the roots eventually choke the vents. Left unchecked, back-pressure builds in the grazer’s steam-bladders, lowering the burn efficiency of its pyrite furnace until it begins to foul and cool. Worse, when the great flat siphon-worms drape themselves across the herd, their acidic drills drop boiler pressure across four hosts at once. 

The ceramic-shelled climbers do not tolerate a cooling host. Heat is their real estate.

Watch two of them converge on a choked exhaust port where black-green moss and copper reeds have matted over the cilia. They do not eat the plants; the plants are mostly metallic wire and bitter, toxic oils. Instead, the climbers brace four legs against the grazer's hull, lock their hydraulic knees, and unsheathe their front-most pair of appendages. One is a heavy, bypassed shear—two beveled blades of dark, meteoric iron stripped from their diet and laid down over bone, driven by a bulbous muscle mass packed into the shoulder. With a slow, tremendous squeeze that makes their own ceramic shells creak, they snip through a thumb-thick copper reed. *Ping.* Then another. They drag the severed mat away from the vent, not discarding it, but shoving it down into the blind, dark gap between two titans.

They tuck the cut reeds right into a seam where warm exhaust condensation drips steadily down the cold iron wall, wedging the stems into a mash of slag extruded by the bottom-dwelling scavengers. Within days, fed by that deliberate funneling of hot brine and rich waste, the cut ends sprout thick, pale, bloated root-nodules—much softer and richer in glycol-sugars than the tough sun-baked foliage up top. 

They are gardening in the boiler room.

Look further down the flank, where the edge of a threshing-machine-wide siphon-worm lies heavy and grey across a grazer's hip. Four of the pale, flat climbers are lined up along the worm’s rubbery margin. They aren't killing it—killing a ten-ton slab of gristle would just leave a rotting, heat-blocking blanket over their territory. Instead, using thick, chisel-tipped mouthparts and short blasts of scalding formic brine squirted from glands beneath their jaws, they are blistering the worm’s underside, prying it up an inch at a time and shoving fist-sized chunks of raw basalt talus underneath its skirt. Wedged off the iron plate, its acid radula spinning uselessly in thin air half an inch above the nickel, the giant parasite slowly retracts that lobe and oozes six feet to the left, onto an untended beast.

This is as close as the plateau comes to intention, and if you watch them long enough in the blinding, violet-white glare, you begin to realize there is a mind down in the grease. 

It is not a fast mind, and it has no use for eyes. Eyes up top would be burned crisp by the unshielded ultraviolet; eyes down in the leg-crush would be blinded every three seconds by the blue-white arc-flashes of the welding symbiotes. The climbers have no heads to speak of—just a slightly thicker anterior rim studded with pits that read infrared gradients to the hundredth of a degree, alongside stiff,銅-sheathed whiskers that brush the iron hulls they cling to.

Those whiskers are how they see, and how they speak. 

Iron and nickel, packed flank-to-casing under immense pressure, make a phenomenal conductor of sound. To our ears, standing on the obsidian spire, the noise of the plateau is an undifferentiated, deafening roar. To a creature clamped flat against a resonant hull, the herd is a vast, three-dimensional telegraph network. They read the internal health of a titan from the pitch of its basalt-grinding gizzard; they feel the slow, tectonic lean of a neighbor twenty beasts away by the shift in harmonic stress screaming through the pressed plates; they know a grazer has died half a mile off because the steady *thump-hiss* of its ninety-psi gas bladder drops out of the local chord.

And when one of them needs a second shear to pry a wedged boulder out of a warm crease, or six more bodies to lever a dying creeper-vine off a chimney before frost claims the seam, it drops its underside flat against the titan’s casing and strikes the iron with a heavy, calcified hammer-plate on its belly.

*Tang. Tang-tang. Tang.*

High, sharp, percussive pings, pitched entirely above the low rumble of the biological mills and below the dry *snap-buzz* of the electrostatic sparks. The metal carries the tap through a dozen touching flanks, leaping from wet shell to wet shell. Minutes later—which is brisk, here—another pale ceramic disk hauls itself up out of the steaming dark between the pistons of two great legs, hooks onto the rim, and clicks its own belly against the plate in answer.

They have no cities, no tools beyond what grows out of their shoulders, no fire save the furnaces they ride. Yet they live in distinct, multi-generational estates that drift across the shield at an inch an hour. A clan of thirty or forty climbers—grandmothers broad and scarred as anvil-tops, blackened at the edges with permanent grease, down to soft-shelled hatchlings the size of pocket watches tucked safely into the warm armpits of a titan’s exhaust cowl—will inhabit the same cluster of six or seven adjacent grazers for two hundred years. 

Because the grazers inch forward at slightly different rates, an estate is constantly, glacially shearing apart. A clan’s leftmost beast will grind three feet ahead over the course of an autumn, threatening to pull their cultivated brine-troughs and root-mats in two. So the clan works the gap. They braid tough cable-ferns into thick, living hawsers between two animals’ dorsal spines to drag a lagging mount along; conversely, they will deliberately pack abrasive quartz gravel into the leg-joints of a beast moving too fast, hobbling its hydraulic lock just enough to keep their seven-titan neighborhood abreast. Over decades, you can actually spot a tended cluster from up on the obsidian spire: while the rest of the wild herd creeps in ragged, chaotic smears, here and there a tight knot of eight or ten massive shells moves in locked formation, their dorsal vents steaming cleanly in unison, stripped of choking weeds, glistening under neatly pruned canopies of oil-flower, ringed by a pale, shifting constellation of white ceramic dots.

When one of their beasts finally begins the week-long cooling of death, there is no panic—nothing panic-shaped could fit into their nervous systems. As the internal boiler drops below forty degrees and the first rime crystals feather the exhaust cilia, a slow, methodical tapping rings out across the dying hull. 

The clan harvests everything before the bottom-scavengers arrive. With their iron shears, they strip the fat glycol-nodules from the crevices, pack them into rolled tubes of rubbery black moss, and lash the bundles to their own flat backs with vine-tendrils. They unhook the braided fern-hawsers so the corpse won't drag its living neighbors to a halt. The smallest juveniles ride stacked three-high on the elders' ceramic shells, clamped tight against the sudden draft of killing air as they cross the seam onto a fresh, hot grazer grinding past on the flank. 

By the time the piston-jawed smelters boil up out of the dark to dismantle the cold iron shell plate by plate, the estate has already shifted three yards east, hooking its clamps into new nickel, slicing away an overgrown reed, spitting warm brine onto a stubborn worm, and striking three sharp, patient notes into the metal to let the rest of the family know the heat is good here.
