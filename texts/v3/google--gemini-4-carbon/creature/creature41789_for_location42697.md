At first, you only see the water behaving wrongly. 

When the barometric *crack* flattens the mist and snaps the chamber into blinding focus, a section of the wet limestone high on the eastern vault seems to keep sliding after the wind has momentarily died. It looks like a thick lens of runoff—perhaps a meter across—refusing to drain. Then the whiteout boils back up, and it is gone.

If you watch the sheltered margins instead, where the rubbery green straps plaster themselves behind the four sheared plinths, you might notice that the grazing scars on the vegetation are too neat. The tough kelp-mats are not torn; they are shaved down in precise, parallel ribbons, rasped clean to the bare stone by something with a broad, rhythmic stroke. And if you watch the palm-sized, hydroplaning teardrops out on the open walls, you will eventually see one shoot twenty feet across a momentarily dry face to snap up a midge, lock its suction foot down—and simply slide straight into a piece of the wall that wasn't wall at all, vanishing beneath a translucent, muscular skirt that ripples once and settles back against the chert.

They are low, heavy-bodied things, built entirely around the physics of boundary layers. 

To live in a sealed drum of gale-force shears and sideways rain is to treat air the way intertidal creatures treat surf. Anything that stands upright is levered off the floor and smashed down the drainage fissures. So the vault’s largest inhabitants—there are never more than seven or eight of them alive in the room at once, spaced widely across the pale, cobble-smooth geometry—have abandoned height altogether. 

Resting, one looks like a broad, oval shield of milky, semi-translucent cartilage, roughly the size and thickness of a heavy saddle blanket, pressed so intimately against the curved limestone that its edges vanish into the water film. Beneath that smooth, domed mantle, you can dimly make out the blurred silhouettes of dense, dark organs and a heavy, braided ring of muscle, lit from above by the fierce blue-white noon of the suspended nodes. They have no shell—calcium carbonate dissolves too easily in the warm, relentless carbonic wash of this room, and brittle armor cracks when slammed against rock. Instead, their dorsal skin is a rubbery, cross-hatchedelastomer, slicker than the polished bone-stone around them, shedding sideways torrents with almost zero drag.

For hours at a time, they do nothing at all. They clamp themselves into the sweeping parabolic flutes near the ceiling or wrap their bodies halfway around the bases of the ancient plinths, conforming to the stone like cooling wax. Water sheets over them in ankle-deep surges and splits cleanly off their tapered rims. 

Their waking life is measured out in ten- and fifteen-second intervals.

Deep within their mantles, they do not have ears—ears would be ruined by the continuous, sternum-rattling roar of the chamber—but rather a lateral line of fluid-filled pressure bladders running completely around their skirts. They do not hear the storm; they read its geometry. They feel the rising thermal plumes under the three skittering nodes; they track the twin cyclones racing up opposing walls, swelling toward collision.

Two seconds before the gusts meet at the center of the vault, a resting mantle shifts. Its Pale, muscular rim lifts a fraction of a millimeter off the chert, breaking the vacuum seal and flooding its underside with a slick cushion of water. 

*Crack.*

The air shears collide. For a fraction of a second, the mist flattens, the roaring gale stalls into stunned equilibrium, and the chamber blazes with crisp, shadowless clarity. 

In that breath of stillness, the heavy ovals drop off the ceiling. 

They do not fall like stones. As they release the vaulted roof thirty meters up, they spread wide, pleated lateral fins that usually stay tucked beneath their skirts, stiffening their entire bodies into cambered, living hydrofoils. As the rebounding shockwave boils back outward from the center of the room, they catch it. 

For three, four, five seconds, the whiteout is full of sweeping, pale shadows. Two hundred pounds of dense, rubbery muscle banks hard around a blinding node of blue-white fire, riding the screaming thermal updraft like a ray riding a tidal bore. Underneath, hidden from the glare until they bank, their ventral sides flash darkly—deep violet and charcoal-black, pigmented against the bounce-light off the pale limestone floor, lined with rows of hooked, gripping cirri and three radially arranged, horn-rimmed mouths that clack open to scoop wind-stunned insects and airborne droplets of algal froth straight out of the gale. 

They cannot truly fly; the room is too small, and they are too heavy. They are ballistic gliders trading altitude for terrifying speed in a box of violently folding air. One will ride a cyclone halfway down the southern curve, snap its pleated rim tight as a sideways curtain of warm rain threatens to flip it, and hit the sloped wall at forty miles an hour—not crashing, because it meets the parabolic curve at an exact, shallow tangent, bleeding off its momentum in a fifty-foot, hissing hydroplane across the wet limestone that ends with a soft, wet *thwack* of total suction just behind a sheltered plinth, locking down a split second before an ankle-deep surge roars past to drown the floor siphons.

Once anchored in the lee of the ancient architecture, they become slow again. 

They creep over the faint, hair-thin geometric inlays that sit proud of the eroded floor, their undersides rippling in mesmerizing, peristaltic waves. Their horn-rimmed mouths rasp methodically at the emerald-green strap-weed, shaving away the tough growth while leaving the holdfasts intact to regenerate in the endless noon. When one crosses an open expanse to hunt the smaller, teardrop-shaped skimmers, it doesn't waste energy chasing them; it simply slides into a predictable wind-lane, flattens its translucent edge until its refractive index matches the running water, and waits for the little suction-walkers to hydroplane straight into its mouth.

Because there is no night here, and no winter, they have no circadian rhythm and no breeding season. Time in the chamber is purely erosional, interrupted only by the ten-second pulse of the barometric cracks. Yet they are bound together by the one place in the vault where the storm refuses to exist.

Near the northern wall, where the twelve-meter ribbon of matte-black material arcs cleanly out of the dissolved limestone and plunges back into the floor, there is always a cluster of them.

The black ribbon is too thin for a full-grown adult to rest upon—barely the width of a human wrist—and whatever physics governs its millimeter-thin envelope of dry air also repels their suction feet; they cannot grip it directly. Instead, two or three of the largest, oldest mantles will anchor themselves to the wet limestone right where the ribbon emerges from the wall and where it dives back into the floor. From there, they reach out with long, prehensile fringes from their anterior rims, hooking delicately *around* the black band without quite touching it, forming a living, braided bridge suspended out in the roaring air.

They do this to sleep, and they do this to spawn. 

Wrapped around that stubbornly dry, immovable arc, their inner edges press into the microscopic boundary of dead calm that sheaths the black material. It is the only silence in their universe. If you could look closely through the stinging, luminous spray, you would see clusters of tiny, pearl-gray egg cases glued not to the black ribbon—nothing sticks to the ribbon—but cemented into closed rings *around* it, sliding freely back and forth along the twelve-meter arc like beads on an abacus wire, kept utterly dry on their inner faces and continuously bathed in warm, oxygenated mist on their outer ones, immune to the floor-siphons below.

When the hatchlings emerge—no bigger than thumbnails, transparent as jellyfish—they cannot hold the walls against the main gales. For their first few months, they live entirely in the millimeter-wide slipstream trailing off the leeward edge of the black ribbon, and in the tiny, razor-sharp eddies behind the proud geometric inlays at the plinth bases, grazing on microscopic biofilms until their muscles thicken and their pressure-bladders tune themselves to the drumbeat of the vault.

Sometimes, during the brief, blinding clarity of a barometric crack, you can see the oldest one in the chamber. It is nearly two meters wide, its rubbery dorsal shield scarred with pale, healed gouges from centuries-old miscalculations against the chert bands, its edges grown slightly scalloped to match the exact curvature of the flute it favors high near the ceiling. 

It hangs motionless twenty feet horizontally from the central light-node, taking the full, summer-hot blast of blue-white radiance directly on its back. Embedded deep in the translucent cartilage of its dome, dense gardens of symbiotic green algae—the same tough strain that carpets the plinths—glow like stained glass, feeding sugars directly into its bloodstream so that it barely needs to hunt anymore. It simply clings to the slick, bone-pale roof of its tiny, roaring world, flexing its rim slightly every twelve seconds as the shockwave hits, drinking the fierce artificial noon while the limestone dissolves, grain by microscopic grain, beneath its grip.
