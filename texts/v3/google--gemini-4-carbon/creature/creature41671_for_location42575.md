If you stand on that brick terrace long enough for your clothes to soak through and your own scent to stop panicking the immediate foliage, a section of the sandstone wall behind the cascading epiphytes will detach itself and reach out to taste the sweat on your collarbone.

At first, it looks like a slump of wet, rust-red clay that has finally lost its grip on the bedrock, or perhaps a monstrously overgrown patch of the olive moss. Then you see the joints. They are long, double-hinged, and cushioned in thick pads of grey, calloused cartilage that allow a body the weight of a small hog to shift across vertical stone without making so much as a scrape. 

There is only one of it in the pit—there is only ever room for one, though in the deepest mulch beneath the cairns, something the size of a closed fist is usually gestating, waiting decades for the current tenant to slow down and be recycled by the mycelium. It is not the descendant of the people who carved the stairs and laid the dry red brick, though if you catch sight of the flat, spatulate bones inside its forelimbs as it stretches across a shaft of amber light, you might suffer an uncomfortable moment of doubt. Whatever fell or climbed down here centuries ago to survive the baking of the plain has been thoroughly revised by the ninety-eight-degree dark. 

Its body is broad and compressed, flattened like the copper millipedes so it can slide behind the heavy curtains of aroid leaves without disturbing the moth-webs. Its skin is entirely hairless, loose, and deeply pleated—a heavy, velvety mantle the exact mottled terracotta and bruised maroon of the chamber walls. Those deep folds are not wrinkles of age; they are channels. Like everything else here, the creature is a catchment basin. As it hangs motionless for hours against a geothermal fissure, the rising subterranean vapor hits its slightly cooler, blood-shunted outer skin, condenses in the labyrinth of its pleats, and runs down via capillary action directly to the corners of its wide, lipless mouth. It drinks simply by existing in the steam.

Where eyes ought to be, there are only two shallow, saucer-sized depressions lined with pale, wildly innervated velvet, damp and flushed pink with blood vessels. Light is too scarce and too cluttered down here to trust; instead, it sees the pit as a shifting tapestry of heat and breath. To this creature, the chamber is brilliant. The hot fissures in the floor glow like forge-coals; the cold drops of falling condensation streak downward like dark meteors; a gecko digesting a beetle on an overhead vine burns like a tiny, steady lamp; and the synchronized fanning of the moth-chains registers as a vast, rhythmic tide of cooling and warming air.

When it moves, it does so with the slow, hydraulic patience of a starfish. One long, four-thumbed hand reaches out, spanning a gap between a worn sandstone step and a thumb-thick vine. Before it commits its weight, two pale, boneless filaments—modified nasal structures that unfurl from slits above its mouth—flick out to brush the surface. It is checking for the chemical trails of the whipcord snakes, reading the slime-histories of the predatory snails, tasting the mineral sweat of the rock. Once satisfied, it pulls its bulk forward. Its tendons slide in oiled sheaths; its ribcage is flexible enough to deform around a jutting brick rather than scrape against it. In a room where noise is a failure of etiquette and survival, it is the quietest thing of all.

You might think an animal of sixty or seventy pounds in an eighty-foot pit would be a tyrant, gorging itself until the tiny ecosystem collapsed. Instead, it behaves less like a predator and more like an obsessive, blind somatic gardener. Its metabolism is tuned to the slow-oven warmth of the stone; it needs no calories to heat its blood, so a very small meal lasts it a week. 

Watch it work the carved niches along the western wall. A colony of the pale land-crabs is clustered there, scraping algae. As the broad shadow of the keeper slides over them, they do not scatter—scattering makes noise, and dropping blindly into the lower ferns invites the millipedes. They simply freeze, tucking their spoon-claws tight against their decalcified chests. The creature presses its heat-sensing facial discs inches from the cluster. It passes over the females carrying dark, spongy masses of eggs beneath their aprons. It passes over the vigorous, hard-shelled primes. Its four-thumbed hand settles with tremendous delicacy upon a crab whose gill-chambers are wheezing with fungal rot, or one recently crippled in a silent eyestalk-duel. 

It plucks the crab free, carries it down to the semicircular brick terrace, and cracks it not with teeth, but with a slow, vise-like squeeze of its palms, muffled entirely within a fold of its heavy skin so the *pop* of the carapace doesn’t carry. It eats the soft meat, licks the mineral paste from the shell’s interior, and drops the clean shards neatly onto the brimming pile of snail shells in the dry basin. That midden is its work—centuries of tidy, compulsive clearing so the sharp detritus of calcium doesn't choke the soft humus floor where the vapor vents breathe.

Its relationship with the rest of the room is intimately, unsettlingly physical. Because its pleated skin collects rich, mineral-heavy moisture, a miniature pasture of specialized pale lichen and translucent mites lives in the warm creases behind its shoulders. When it rests—wedging itself into one of the dead-end stairways five feet off the ground, looking for all the world like a slumped bolster of mossy sandstone—the small velvet geckos will drop softly onto its back to lick the mites from its folds. The creature never snaps at them. Occasionally, a chain of the flightless moths will anchor a guy-line of sticky silk directly to its hip while it sleeps through a sluggish, three-day torpor; when it finally wakes and needs to move, it will reach back, carefully pinch the silk strand between two blunt thumbs, and transfer the anchor to a nearby fern stem before pulling away, preserving the bellows that keeps its air moving.

Even the three squat river-stone cairns are still under its maintenance, though any memory of *why* they exist has long since dissolved out of its smooth, folded brain. When the vigorous white mycelium swells too thickly between the stones and threatens to topple a stack, the creature squats in the warm mulch, wraps its long forearms around the cairn, and presses the stones back into alignment with its chest, trimming away the excess fungal mats and rolling them into tight, silent pellets under its tongue. 

If you remain there on the bricks—an intruder blazing like a bonfire in its thermal vision, reeking of surface dust, sharp sun-baked ozone, and loud, erratic heartbeats—it will not attack you. Fighting is violent, wasteful, and loud; thrashing bodies would tear down the moth-curtains, crush the aroids, and send shockwaves through the floor that would drive the millipedes into a churning panic. 

Instead, it will climb across the brick overhang until it is level with your chest, clinging upside down to the root-choked rock with four wide, splayed feet. It will lean out into the muggy air until you can feel the radiant, wet heat coming off its pleated hide—slightly cooler than your own fevered skin—and smell its scent: nothing of musk or rot, only crushed fern, wet rust, and old cellar stone. 

Its two pink, velvet hollows will tilt toward your face, drinking the plume of hot breath leaving your nostrils. One long, grey-padded hand will reach out, slow as a growing root, and press two heavy thumbs softly against your carotid artery. It will hold them there, feeling the frantic, wasteful hammering of a surface creature's blood, vibrating against you for three silent seconds just as the crabs do on the narrow stairs. 

Then, having measured you and found you far too hot, too noisy, and too short-lived to belong to the pit, it will draw its hand back, flatten itself against the sweating sandstone behind a maroon leaf, and vanish completely while you are still looking straight at it—waiting with geological patience for you either to climb back out toward the fierce sky, or to lie down in the warm mulch and let the white lace take your bones.
