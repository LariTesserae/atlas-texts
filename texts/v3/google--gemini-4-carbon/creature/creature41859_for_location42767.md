The builders are human, though three or four centuries in the shaft—or perhaps twice that, since time without seasons leaves very shallow marks—have pared them down to fit the geometry of the grid.

Seen from across the void, crouched on a narrow pole forty feet above the pitched floor-slabs, one of them looks at first like another specialized knot in the rigging. They are small-framed and slight of bone, rarely heavier than seven stone, because every superfluous pound is dead load on a sinew cable and a liability on a wet spar. Their chests are disproportionately deep for the thinness of their limbs, barrel-ribbed to haul oxygen out of the high, thin mountain air, yet their breathing is slow and shallow when they are at rest, scarcely fogging the cold dampness in front of their lips. Beneath their oiled skins, their flesh has the dense, bloodless pallor of tallow kept in a dark pantry, crosshatched at the wrists, ankles, and collarbones with pale blue veins and the fine black lines of old basalt cuts into which stone-dust healed long ago.

Look first at their feet, which never wear leather inside the shaft. Centuries of walking slick, cylindrical poles and glassy volcanic ledges have widened the forefoot until the toes splay like stubby fingers, the big toe set slightly apart with a thickened, muscular pad at its base that grips the curve of a peeled spruce spar almost like a thumb. The soles are pale gray and horn-thick against the razor edges of the rock, yet strangely sensitive—spongy with dampness rather than dry and cracked. A builder standing at a sorting platform does not need to look down to know what is happening fifty feet away; through the bare arch of their foot pressed to the intersection of two main arteries, they can read the tension of the chasm. They feel the distinct, slow-surging drag of a gill-slug migration shifting onto the western guys, the sharp rhythmic *thrum* of another person climbing a horn-rung ladder two levels down, the slackening shudder of a wooden gutter whose pitch-seal has begun to weep and gain weight.

Their oiled wraps are tailored strictly against snagging. Loose cloth or flapping hide in a vertical town full of delicate plant-tethers and razor rock is fatal, so they dress in close-laced jackets and leggings stitched from the cured, translucent membranes of the giant air-gliders, layered over inner vests woven from fibrous bast and stuffed thin with the shed, velvety down of the blind joint-quadrupeds. Greased repeatedly with rendered fat from the filter-feeders, these outer skins turn the color of smoked amber and shed condensation in clean, heavy beads. Across their hips and chests they wear harnesses of braided sinew set with flat bone toggles, from which every tool hangs on a short lanyard: crescent-shaped basalt scrapers flaked to a glassy edge just that morning, hollow bird-bone awls, gourd pots of warm pine-pitch tucked under the armpit to keep them supple, and flat wicker panniers curved to fit the small of the back so they do not shift a climber’s center of balance.

There are never more than seventy or eighty of them alive in the chasm at once. The town looks built for hundreds only because nothing represents solely housing; nine-tenths of the grid is catchment, pasture, loom, and counterweight. They know to the basketful how much moisture the cloud-ceiling yields in twenty-four hours, how many square yards of liverwort curtain are needed to feed the pale grazers, and how many human bodies can eat from that loop before the ropes are stripped bare of slime and the cistern-shells begin to starve.

Because of this, those tiny woven shelters hanging thirty feet apart in the dim air are mostly solitary berths. To crowd six warm, breathing human bodies into a single hut would turn it instantly into a sweating rot-box, clouding the wicker with black mold and drawing swarms of blind quadrupeds to chew through the structural lashings in the dark. So the people sleep alone or in pairs, curled like larvae in dry nests of shredded bark inside baskets no larger than salt-casks, swaying imperceptibly whenever a glider slaps onto a neighboring leaf or a cable tightens across the shaft. Only infants and small children stay constantly strapped against an adult’s ribs under the oiled skins, carried through the middle air for the first four years of life until their toes widen and their inner ears learn that *down* is not a floor to fall onto, only a cold, shadowed cellar where the tether-roots anchor and the dead stone sleeps.

What is hardest for an outsider to grasp—should anyone ever scramble up the barren, wind-scoured scree outside the mountain ridge and peer over the jagged basalt lip—is how they speak to one another without ever breaking the silence of the sink.

They have voices, though unused throats make them rusty, flat, and breathy, scarcely louder than the rub of two dry palms. Speaking aloud feels obscene to them in the chill damp, like spitting into a drinking cistern; worse, sharp sound waves in that narrow, glassy box disturb the radial crawlers on the walls, causing millions of hair-fine legs to twitch in defensive ripples that knock precious water-beads loose before they can reach the gutters. Instead, when two builders pause twenty yards apart on parallel walkways, they converse with their hands and with the ropes. For line-of-sight, they use a crisp, economical sign-speech conducted entirely close to the chest—broad waves of the arms would waste warmth and upset balance—using the pale undersides of their fingers flashing against dark oiled amber: *east cable slackening; three baskets of eggs at the fourth cantilever; fresh fracture weeping iron near the bottom.* And when the bruised overhead light fades toward evening, or when a curtain of twenty-foot liverwort hangs between them, they drop one hand to a guy-line and pluck, dampen, and stroke the taut sinew with scarred fingertips. A skilled builder sitting inside a closed shelter with one bare heel resting against the central suspension post that runs through their floor can listen to three conversations crossing the town at once, feeling the dry, staccato *tick-pause-slide* of someone asking for fresh pitch from the lower racks, and answering with two quick thumbnail-scrapes before rolling back to sleep.

Watch one of them at work during the dim four-hour plateau that passes for noon, and you see that they are neither masters of the chasm’s silent fauna nor parasites upon it, anymore than a spider’s frame-threads are separate from the dew they catch. They are the town’s slow-moving hands, maintaining gradients and clearing bottlenecks so the whole suspended gut can digest the cloud.

A woman—forty years old, her dark hair cropped to skin-stubble so it never holds damp, her broad, heavy-lidded eyes tracking tiny motes across fifty feet of gloom—steps out from a slate cantilever onto a single peeled spruce pole wet with gray sweat. She carries no balancing rod; her arms hang loose, wrists slightly turned out, knees bent just enough to drink the bow of the wood. Halfway across the span, two guy-ropes cross in an X, and here a traffic knot of pale, gill-fringed grazers has stalled, four hundred hand-sized bodies piled three-deep into a glistening, rose-white mound the weight of a small hog, pulsing their feathery flanks as they confuse each other’s mucus trails. Left alone until nightfall, the knot will double, stretch the sinew line past its survey mark, and pull a wooden water-gutter three tiers below out of true by half an inch—enough to spill fifty gallons of precious drip down into the useless dark between the floor boulders.

She kneels on the two-inch pole without looking at her knees, hooking one splayed big toe behind an upright hanger-cord. From her harness she unclips a smooth, flat spatula carved from mountain-sheep horn—brought up from the valleys two generations ago and polished glassy by ten thousand afternoons. She dips the horn first into a gutter to wet it, because dry horn tears the creatures’ skins, and slides it gently into the middle of the pulsing heap. She does not pry or scatter them. Moving at their own hydraulic pace, she lifts three heavy, cold individuals, wipes a yard of raw rope clean of crossed slime with a wad of damp liverwort, and sets the largest down facing west. Once its flat foot catches and begins to glide, leaving a fresh, single-direction chemical track, she places two more behind it, then sits back on her heels for five minutes, completely motionless, watching with calm, unblinking eyes as the pale knot slowly unbraids itself along the new vector. Only the smallest, oldest ones at the bottom of the heap—those whose gills have turned brownish and ragged—go into her back-pannier, dropped neatly alongside pared strips of yellowed liverwort margin and three handfuls of pearl-gray glider eggs scraped from the underside of a purple rosette.

Every ten days or so, three of them descend all the way to the lip of the outer world—or rather, ascend to the rim and drop down the mountain’s exterior flank—a brutal two-day climb down wind-blasted chutes to the timberline far below. They hate the outside. Out there, the wind roars like tearing rock, drying their mucous membranes until their noses bleed and cracking the sensitive pads of their bare feet, which have to be bound up in clumsy rawhide boots stuffed with moss. The sun stabs their twilight-dilated eyes through slits of carved bone; birds shriek intolerably in the stunted pines; everything grows in ugly, choking tangles in dirt full of grit and crawling, clicking things with hard shells. They work down there in frantic, miserable haste, felling two straight young spruce trees with stone adzes, peeling the bark on the spot to save weight, trading cakes of dried medicinal liverwort or coils of supreme glider-gut cordage at silent cairns left by valley pastoralists for raw elk-sinew, horn, salt, and lumps of black pine-resin, then hauling the green poles back up into the cloud ceiling before the dry air can warp their lungs. The moment they slip back over the razor-sharp basalt rim and descend twenty feet onto the uppermost horn ladder—pulling off their boots, feeling the cellar-cold, motionless damp close instantly over their hot skin and the vast vertical hush swallow the howl of the ridge—their shoulders drop two inches, and their breathing slows by half.

Below the lowest walkways, down where the bruised light dies altogether among the pitched, angular slabs of the floor, they go much more rarely, though that dark basement belongs to the town too. That is where they lower the pitch-sealed wicker vats of nightsoil, fish-offal, and rotten wood-shavings on long bast ropes, tipping them carefully into the cold, wet clefts between the unweathered boulders. From that rich decay, in total blackness, breed the clouds of buoyant white drift-larvae that rise continuously upward through the shaft like slow, silent snow—feeding the translucent gliders, whose cured skins roof the cisterns and clothe the builders, and whose waste rains finely back onto the cartwheel rosettes and hanging curtains.

It is down there, too, wedged dry and upright on narrow ledges beneath overhanging slabs where neither drip nor rising larva reaches, that the dead are left once their breath stops—wrapped in retired cordage, stripped of every usable bone awl and toggle, cooling within an hour to the exact cellar temperature of the basalt. Nothing down there is large enough to scatter bones, and in the chill, stagnant damp without soil-beetles or flies, the radial crawlers eventually step over them, standing hub-to-hub across motionless ribs and skulls just as they stand on the sheer rock beside them, swaying eight hair-fine legs in unison to comb the faint, rising currents of the abyss.

High overhead, seventy feet up in the dim gray light, a boy barely nine years old edges barefoot along a newly split spruce strut, pale shavings still clinging to his damp ankles. Beside him, an elder pauses at a wedged slate cantilever, testing a freshly spliced main line with two scarred fingers. Neither looks at the other. A translucent glider four feet long detaches from a ledge above them, ripples its muscular ribbon-edge to miss their heads by an inch, pouches a drift of rising white motes in complete silence, and *slaps* softly against the wet underside of a purple leaf ten yards below. Both builders feel the tiny rebound travel through the leaf-stalk into the rock fissure, from the rock into the wedged slate, and up through the grey horn of their heels—a clean, taut pulse telling them the cantilever is seated true—before they turn together and climb separately into the empty air.
