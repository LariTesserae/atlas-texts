At first, you take them for part of the scaffolding. 

Wedged between a dry-stone pier and a sagging canopy of fused, sage-colored leaves, there is what looks like a bundle of pale lath and coarse burlap, dusted heavily with white lime. Only when you have stood very still for several minutes—long enough for the toasted-chaff warmth of the air to settle into your own clothes—do you notice that the bundle is breathing. Like the calf-headed loaves in the rafters, its chest does not rise outward, but rather shifts its thickness sideways into a seam between two flint cairns, vanishing and returning with the slow, dry scrape of shifting meal.

There are three of them in this alcove, though if you peer through the triangular gap beneath one’s bent knee, you can see a fourth sitting twenty paces away in a sunken, wattle-lined pit that has no floor-level entrance, braiding grass by the upward-slanting light. 

They are the Masons, though whether they built the kiln-hall and became trapped in its creases, or whether the hall extruded them out of its own excess of thumb-prints and craft, is a distinction the room no longer recognizes. 

Up close, a Mason is roughly the height of a stooped man, though proportioned with the heavy, bottom-weighted stability of a sack of flour. They have no true skin; instead, they are upholstered in thick, seamless hides of unbleached linen and chamois-soft leather, tightly stuffed with warm, dry bran that shifts and settles audibly whenever they lean forward. Their joints are not ball-and-socket, but clean, dry-fitted mortises of pale boxwood—you can see the tiny, square oak pegs holding their elbows together, completely free of grease or marrow, creaking faintly when they reach for a stone. 

Their hands are their heaviest parts. Disproportionately broad and flattened like wooden paddles, they are made of dense, unglazed stoneware that softens slightly toward the wrists. Look closely at the pads of their wide, blunt thumbs, and you will find the exact, sharp-ridged whorls stamped into every herringbone tile of the floor. Those ridges are never worn smooth because the Masons do not wear down; when a thumb-pad chips against a piece of fire-cracked flint, the Mason simply presses the damaged digit against a warm terracotta stalk for an afternoon, drawing up fresh ochre silt until the clay fills the nick and fires itself whole again.

They have faces, after a fashion, though they have no need for mouths or nostrils in an air that carries neither scent nor moisture. From the collarbone up, a Mason’s head is a smooth, featureless dome of bone-ash plaster, warm to the touch, draped in a heavy cowl of felted wool to keep the stray geometric flakes from settling in their neck-joints. Instead of eyes, two braided plumb-lines of dried sedge hang from notches near their temples, weighted at the ends with small, pierced beads of knapped chert. 

They see by *plumb* and *level*. When a Mason turns its blank plaster brow toward you, the two weighted cords swing out at impossible angles—ignoring gravity to point directly toward the sharpest crease in your boots, or the structural tension of your spine, trembling slightly as they read the volume of space you are displacing.

They are endlessly, drowsily busy. They do not sleep, nor do they hurry; they work at the exact speed the foliage grows, so that a single swing of an ash-wood mallet takes four or five breaths to fall, landing not with a sharp crack, but with a dull, muffled *thump* that seems to happen slightly before the wood actually touches the chisel. 

Watch one at the edge of the copper-leaved thicket. It has found a place where the floor has grown too tight—where three retired, bark-plated hares have squared off and fired themselves into new yellow-ochre bricks, bowing the herringbone course upward into a stiff ridge. If left alone, the pressure might crack the dry bone-mortar and spill an alcove into a place it hasn’t been properly joined to. 

The Mason kneels—its bran-stuffed knees flattening comfortably into the sharp tiles, conforming to the ridges—and opens a pouch woven from split hazel. Inside are no metal nails, no wet clay, only dry things: wedges of seasoned hornbeam, slivers of slate, loops of twine, and handfuls of white lime-cubes picked from the base of the walls. 

With infinite deliberation, the Mason takes a flint scraper and pares away a shaving of the air itself right above the buckled bricks. It looks like peeling a dry curl of horn from a hoof. Where the shaving comes away, a hairline gap opens—not down into dirt, for there is no dirt, but sideways into the back of a timber beam ten yards behind you. Into this seam, the Mason tucks the excess brick-edge, tapping it home with the butt of its stoneware palm. It packs the joint with dry bone-meal rubbed from its own knuckles, ties a knot in a hanging grass cord to hold the new distance fast, and leans back. 

What was a bulge in the floor is now a neat, four-inch step that takes three minutes to step over, already sprouting two tiny, ceramic-stemmed shoots of pale sage. Beneath the arch of that tiny step, a pair of pumice-worms have already coiled to enjoy the fresh, tight corner.

The creatures of the room treat the Masons as moving architecture. A six-legged calf-beast will rest its heavy, resin-eyed chin in the crook of a Mason’s boxwood elbow while the Mason spends an hour sighting along a split-oak rafter; parchment-winged moths roost in the dusty folds of their wool cowls, stepping off a shoulder to arrive instantly at the apex of the woven brick ceiling. Occasionally, when a Mason remains still for several days—calculating how to fold a new ten-foot dry-stone wall into a gap between two gourds—the suede leaves of the surrounding thicket will press against its linen back and fuse with the fabric. When the Mason finally shifts its weight, it does not tear itself free; it carefully unpeels a layer of its own cloth skin, leaving a patch of pale weave behind as a hammock for the bristle-necked hares, while fresh, dry fibers slowly extrude from its bran-filled ribs to mend the bare spot.

They do not speak to one another. When two Masons meet at a crossroads where four different alcoves share a single oak post, they pause. They reach out their wide, stoneware hands and press their thumbs together, ridge fitting into exact negative ridge, testing each other’s alignment. For ten minutes they stand locked like a dry-fitted timber joint, letting the static warmth of the room equalize between them, trading the tiny vibrations of settling walls and shifting silt through their wrists. A faint dusting of dry lime falls from their sleeves onto the floor. 

Then they part—one stepping around the left side of the post into the close, bright heat of the gourd-patch, the other stepping around the right side of the very same post and vanishing waist-deep into the cool, upward-lit shade of a sunken cistern, each carrying a wooden mallet and a coil of grass twine, looking for the next place where the room needs letting out.

And in the deepest, oldest creases of the hall—places you can only glimpse by pressing your cheek flat to the floor and squinting through the dry mortar-gaps between unweathered tiles—you can see what becomes of a Mason when it has absorbed too many angles. There, wedged upright like caryatids supporting the impossible weight of six overlapping ceilings, stand figures whose linen has entirely mineralized into yellow ochre, their boxwood joints fused solid, their braided plumb-lines petrified into slender pillars of limestone. They have become load-bearing alcoves themselves. Their cupped, ceramic hands are held out level with their chests, filled to the brim with warm silt, out of which grow the thickest, oldest copper-leaved trunks in the basin—still bearing, around every knot and bough, the crisp, unworn impression of a maker's thumb.
