You do not see the occupant at first because you are looking for an animal that displaces water, and whatever is wedged into the thickets of the right-hand wall has spent a very long time making sure it displaces as little as possible. 

At first touch, if your numb fingers were to brush it while parting the cartilaginous stalks, you would take it for a sunken bundle of bog-butter wrapped in raw, scraped hide, or a winter-drowned sheep’s paunch that somehow worked its way down a peat-pipe before the rock split. It feels heavy, firm, and tallowy, with the slick, dead-cold resistance of wet leather. Then the hide shifts—not pulling away, because there is nowhere to pull away to, but redistributing its bulk along an invisible crease, the way heavy dough slumps over the edge of a board.

It is roughly the volume of a ten-year-old child, if a child could be unboned at the collarbones and hips and packed neatly into the crevices between overlapping bolsters of black liverwort. 

Yet it is not an invertebrate. Beneath the thick, rubbery outer mantle—which is pale as tallow where it presses against the peat-silt and stained a bruised, mottled slate where it lies near the violet rock-seams—there is a hard, segmented armature. You can hear it before you understand what you are touching: a faint, dry *chink*, like two flat river-pebbles clicking together inside a mitten. It has joints, many of them, though they do not correspond to an arrangement meant for walking on flat ground. Long, flattened plates of pale bone or calcified cartilage slide over one another beneath the grease-thick skin, allowing a limb to fold flat against its own flank, turn a sharp corner around a prism of traprock, and reach three feet away through a gap no wider than two fingers.

Those limbs end in hands—or what have become hands by convergent necessity. Each bears four long, spatulate digits, entirely unwebbed, stiffened with flat splints of bone and tipped with thick, split nails black with iron tannin. The skin on the palms is raised in coarse, concentric whorls like woodgrain, tough enough to grip fresh-cleaved basalt without slicing open. 

Right now, two of those hands are anchored firmly to opposite sides of the vertical sheet of thick, clear water in the center of the chamber. Its main trunk hangs suspended in the right-hand zone where dead fronds sink downward, tucked into a natural seat of black rock, while its left forearm passes straight through the cold boundary into the inverted half of the room. Because of the kink in the chamber’s logic, the arm appears severed at the elbow, vanishing into clear water only for the pale, dark-nailed hand to emerge eighteen inches higher up and tilted backward, calmly pinching a braided hair-cord inside one of the bone-and-pebble lintels. 

It is the builder of the cairns. Or, if not the first to think of them, the current mason keeping the pocket from snapping shut.

If you could peel back the dense curtains of red-filamented stalks around its upper bulk, you would find no head in the expected sense—no neck that could be wrung by a sudden shift in localized gravity, no protruding snout to snag in the interlocking weeds. Instead, the upper curve of its torso flattens into a broad, hooded cowl of gristle and fat, creased down the middle by a vertical sensory groove lined with stiff, pale bristles. It has no eyes; eyes would only ache in the sourceless violet leakage of the stone. Along the margins of the cowl sit four muscular, valved spiracles that clench and relax once every three or four minutes, drawing in tiny mouthfuls of iron-heavy water, tasting the dissolved gasses, and expelling them in slow, soundless threads of silt.

Once every hour or so—though time in the cold thicket is measured mostly by the slow migration of marsh-gas bubbles—a tremor passes through the pale mass. It needs air, very little of it, hoarded by a metabolism slowed nearly to the pace of the liverwort. 

Without letting go of the taut hair-cord in the left-hand zone, it unlatches a third limb from beneath its folded belly—an appendage narrower and longer than the working arms, jointed backward twice like a folding rule. Slowly, so as not to stir the suspended slate-flecks or jostle the balanced river stones, this limb snakes upward through the muscular press of the bladder-fronds toward the highest corner of the ceiling. The flat, split-nailed tip finds the three-inch cleft where the flat blister of stale air clings like mercury against the rock. 

A slit opens in the palm of that reaching hand—not a mouth, just a rimmed tracheal pore—and presses directly into the trapped bubble. For thirty seconds, the creature draws a thin, silent wheeze of old air down the hollow core of its own arm, exchanging stale carbon for stale oxygen, before sealing the pore and folding the limb back down into the dark red filaments. The mercury-flat blister of air trembles, shrinks by a fraction, and settles back against the ceiling. Five minutes later, a thin trickle of exhausted gas leaks from a gill-slit near the creature's lower flank, immediately flattening against an invisible plane in the water and crawling sideways toward the glowing purple cracks.

Watch its working hands, and you begin to see what it is actually doing. 

The fracture in the traprock is not finished happening; it is only paused, caught between two incompatible ways of falling. Where the bruised-violet stain leaks out of the hairline cracks, the rock isn't merely glowing—it is trying to slip sideways into a tighter, flatter arrangement of space that would crush the cavity like an eggshell, or else turn it inside out so that forty feet of drowned coastal fen drains endlessly into a gap the width of a thumbnail. 

The little cages of split bone and river stone are wedges, plumb-lines, and counterweights. 

When a marsh-gas bubble *tocks* downward into the solid stone of the floor, the violet stain along the nearest crack brightens almost imperceptibly, turning the color of a fresh plum-skin. Instantly, the bristles along the creature’s blind cowl stiffen. The three braided hair-cords inside the nearest lintel slacken by a millimeter as one of the localized "ups" wavers and tilts six degrees toward the back wall. 

With immense, freezing patience, the pale bulk shifts in its cradle of liverwort. From a deep fold in its greasy mantle—a pouch tucked near its groin that clinks faintly when it moves—a hand withdraws a single flat gray pebble, foreign to the basalt, worn smooth long ago by surf a mile away on the coast, carried down through the peat-pipes before the slump sealed them, or passed down from hand to hand through hair-thin spatial gaps we couldn't push a needle through. 

Its fingers part the cold, slick stems. Reaching across the kinked boundary so that its wrist bends at a clean, bloodless right angle in empty water, it slides the gray pebble beneath the base of the upside-down cairn on the overhanging shelf. 

It does not use mortar. It lets the aberrant pull of that ten-inch corner take the stone. The pebble clicks softly against the underside of the shelf—*upward*—settling into place with a sudden, disproportionate heaviness, as if made of lead. Immediately, the three braided cords in the neighboring frame snap taut again, humming once, very low, a vibration swallowed within two inches by the rubbery mats of black moss. The plum-colored flare in the crack dims back to a dull, cold bruise.

The mason does not retreat when the adjustment holds. There is nowhere to retreat to; this six-foot thicket is the whole of its country. It simply relaxes its joints until its plates slide flat again, letting the pale bladder-fronds press back together over its tallowy hide. 

From its pouch, using two blackened nails with delicate, blind precision, it pinches a scrap of scraped marsh-reed pith and holds it near the seam in the water. When the white, paper-thin flatworm eventually comes gliding out from beneath the bone lintel—half its body grazing the ceiling, half creeping across the gravel floor—it passes directly over the waiting palm. It does not flinch from the woodgrain skin. The worm pauses across the cold boundary, wraps its blind head around the bit of reed-pith, and feeds, while the thumb beside it rests motionless against a razor-sharp prism of black glass, waiting to feel which way the weight of the earth tries to lean next.
