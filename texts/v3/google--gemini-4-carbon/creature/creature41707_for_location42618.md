You do not notice them at first because your eyes are calibrated for distances, and they live in the seams.

Step out of the blinding glare of a bare chert avenue into the lee of a great sail-tree, and let your pupils widen in the sharp blue shadow. Look closely at the massive, cork-armored trunk where it meets the satin-polished bedrock. There is no soil here, only a wide, flared skirt of pale, woody buttresses gripping the stone by molecular friction and centuries of slow conformation. Tucked into the deep vertical pleats between two of those buttresses hangs something that looks like a folded bolt of unbleached felt, dusted white with talc.

Watch it for twenty minutes. Nothing happens. Then, as the afternoon sun shifts two degrees westward and a lance of raw ultraviolet light slices toward the pleat, the bolt of felt draws itself smoothly around the curve of the trunk like a curtain pulled by an invisible hand, settling back into the cold dark on the far side without making a sound.

They are keepers of the seam, though they have no word for *keeper*—primarily because they have no spoken words at all. In air this thin and dessicated, opening a wet throat to push out sound would be a slow suicide. Every breath exhaled from a damp lung would surrender irreplaceable hydrogen to a sky that never gives anything back. 

Instead, when the curtain of felt unfurls a fraction to adjust its grip on the cork, what steps out is dry all the way down to the architecture of its breath. 

Standing upright, one reaches perhaps to a tall human’s chest, though their proportions make height hard to judge; they are narrow-hipped and remarkably wide across the shoulders, built like living kites that have chosen to fold themselves up and walk. Everything about them is sealed. What looks like unbleached felt from ten paces is actually their own coat: a dense, shingled pelage of hollow, overlapping scale-hairs the color of pale chamois and old mica. Each hair is coated in a microscopic film of hard lipid, slick to the touch yet matte to the eye, scattering the murderous high-altitude sun before it can reach the dark, heat-hoarding skin beneath. 

Their faces are flat, almost avian masks of flexible, horn-like keratin—smooth and warm-toned as polished bone—interrupted by wide, forward-facing eyes protected behind double lenses. The outer lens is fixed, clear, and tinted deep amber like smoky quartz, filtering the UV glare so sharply that they can stare directly toward the blazing silver horizon to read an incoming dust front forty miles away without squinting. Beneath the mask, where a nose and mouth ought to be, there is only a neat seam of interlocking velvet valves. When they breathe, their deep ribcages expand slowly—once every forty seconds or so—drawing frigid, thin air through convoluted internal labyrinths lined not with watery mucus, but with heavy, non-volatile oils that trap stray dust and warm the oxygen before it ever touches blood, reclaiming every micro-gram of internal vapor on the slow way out.

If you are very still, a second shape will detach itself from the pale root-buttress beside the first, and then a third from higher up among the cascading silver ribbons of the lower canopy. Rarely more than four or five travel together. The plateau’s strict economy forbids crowds. Even a colossal grove that has spent eight hundred years hoarding a cellar of rich, waxy sap inside its trunk cannot afford a village drinking at its veins; it can afford a family, briefly, if the family knows manners.

Watch two of them speak. 

The elder—broader in the chest, the keratin of its face worn to a satiny ivory around the edges by decades of windblown silt—lifts both arms slightly away from its ribs. Stretching from wrist to hip is a loose, pleated patagium, a cape of supple skin lined on the inside not with pale insulating scale-hair, but with fine, directional velveteen fibers that shift from dull charcoal to sudden, iridescent violet depending on how the light hits them and how small subcutaneous muscles pull them taut. 

With three quick, silent flicks of its wrists and a rippling shrug along its spine, a complex chord of dark bands and sharp violet flashes runs down the inside of its open arms. Twenty feet up in the canopy, the younger one answers without climbing down: a single slow sweep of one forearm, flashing pale gold, followed by two sharp clacks of long, three-jointed fingers snapping dryly together—*tick-tick*—indistinguishable from a spark of leaf-static to anything not looking straight at them.

*Dust-bank from the south-east,* the ripples say, in a grammar built of vector, charge, and angle. *Iron-heavy. Two hours before dusk.*

They do not own things in the heavy way valley creatures own things, because stone is everywhere and useless to carry, metal does not exist near the surface in smeltable veins, and fire is both terrifying and nearly impossible to sustain nicely in thin oxygen where every spark threatens to turn a centuries-old lipid-rich grove into a sudden, grease-fed torch visible from orbit. To strike a flame here would be madness; it would be burning water.

Instead, their wealth is tension, thread, and static.

When the elder turns away from the trunk, you see the bundle slung tightly between its shoulder blades—not leather, since skin from the rare stilt-walkers is far too precious and rare to base a daily craft upon, and never metal, which bites skin to dead frostbite in three seconds of shadow. Everything they carry is woven from the shed husks of the plateau’s flora, rescued before the wind can grind it to talc, and cured with stolen plant-waxes until it is tougher than rawhide and lighter than balsa. Long telescoping poles of hollow bone-reed from the rolling tumble-ferns; coils of braided filament combed from the silver cushion-colonies; small, sealed gourds fashioned from cured canopy-bladders, sloshing faintly with dense golden oil.

Now that the word has passed about the dust-front, the small band moves out onto the open stone.

This is where their strangeness comes fully into its own. Stepping out from the grove’s wind-shadow into the thirty-knot current sweeping down the wide chert corridor, they do not hunch forward and trudge against the gale the way a heavy, wet-world mammal would. They lean sideways into it, spreading their heavy capes just enough to catch a spilled fraction of the draft. Their legs are long, slender, and spring-loaded at the ankle with thick elastic tendons, ending in broad, twin-toed pads shod in replaceable soles of felted reed. Catching the crosswind on an angled hip and an extended elbow, they take twenty-foot, skating bounds across the satin-finished granite—*touch, glide, pause, touch*—moving over the pale swells of the ancient mountain range with the effortless, tacking geometry of ice-boats.

Half a mile upwind of their host grove, where two whale-backed domes of feldspar pinch the air into a subtle trough, they stop and go to work.

They are setting a weir, though nothing wet will ever pass through it.

Working with rapid, practiced synchrony—glancing constantly at one another’s flashing inner arms—two of the younger ones anchor the ends of their hollow reed-poles into natural flutes in the polished stone, wedging them tight with bone-dry wooden cams rather than hammering, never chipping the sacred smoothness of the rock. Between the flexed poles they string a wide, low net, forty yards long and six feet high, woven from thousands of twisted strands of shed plume-hair and amber sail-membrane. 

On its own, the net has wide meshes; wind passes straight through it with barely a whisper. Then the elder steps to the center post, draws a block of hardened, glassy resin from its belt, and begins briskly stroking the master-cord of the net with a pad of rough, silver walker-fur.

Up and down, twenty, thirty, fifty times. In this zero-humidity air, where nothing ever grounds out through damp earth, the triboelectric charge builds instantly to a ferocious pitch. Every fine hair woven into the forty-yard net suddenly bristles outward, stiff as steel needles. The air within three paces of the net begins to taste faintly of ozone; the scale-hairs along the workers’ shoulders lift and shiver in sympathy. If you laid a bare hand on that master-cord now, the snap would knock you flat onto the granite. The keepers handle it only with tongs of dry, non-conductive horn, anchoring the charged net on insulators of pure, clear quartz scavenged from exposed veins in the basins.

Then they retreat fifty yards back to the grove, climb twenty feet up into the cradling, springy forks of the lower branches where the silver chamois-ribbons hang thickest, wrap their capes tightly around their knees, and wait.

Look at them up there in the late afternoon glare, motionless among the billowing foliage, and you begin to understand what time feels like to a mind shaped by this plateau. Because sound does not carry and humidity never blurs the air, nothing ever sneaks up on them except night and winter, and both of those arrive right on schedule. A mind that can see forty miles in every direction from childhood onward does not startle easily. Their thoughts are long, clean, and spatial, preoccupied with trajectories. Looking out from the swaying porch of the grove, the four of them are simultaneously watching:

—The golden wall of mineral dust twenty miles south-east, climbing the shallow curve of the basin floor at twenty-eight knots;
—A solitary stilt-walker seven miles west, reduced by distance to a bright silver comma drifting between two dark groves, and three miles behind it, the tiny, flat shadow of a patient hunter sliding from boulder-lee to boulder-lee;
—High overhead, almost lost against the hard indigo zenith, the razor-thin white scratch of a sixty-foot glider banking into a weakening thermal;
—And closer, twelve miles north along the horizon line, a brief, rhythmic glint of amber and violet flashing from the crown of another giant grove.

The elder lifts one arm out of its folded wrap, catches the sun on a small mirror of polished mica sewed into its palm, and flashes three slow pulses back toward the northern horizon. Another family, twelve miles off. They may not stand within ten paces of each other for the next three years. They are currently arguing, with great wit and precision, over whether the walker seven miles west is an old bull with cracked hooves or a barren female, and whose turn it will be to claim the hollow leg-bones and the priceless insulating fat if the low-slung hunter pulls it down by Tuesday.

Then the golden shadow arrives.

The sun dims from blinding white to a strange, brassy twilight as the thousand-foot wall of dry silt swallows the grove. There is still no roar—only an immense, rushing *shhhhhh* as billions of microscopic grains of feldspar, iron oxide, phosphorus, dried spore-cases, and pulverized leaf-wax stream past at thirty miles an hour. High overhead, the great violet-black plumes of the tree stiffen and spread wide, ticking softly as they drink their fill from the upper cloud.

Down on the stone avenue upwind, the family’s net is invisible inside the yellow murk, except when an overloaded knot suddenly discharges against an airborne clump of iron-rich dust with a tiny, silent pinpoint of blue spark.

For forty minutes the brass fog pours through. Sitting motionless in the branches, the keepers pull hooded cowls of fine membrane down over their amber eye-lenses to keep the silt from scratching them, tucking their breathing seams deep into the warm crook of their elbows. Dust piles in neat white Epaulettes on their motionless shoulders, sliding off the slick lipid-coated hairs into little drifts along the bark beneath them.

And then, as cleanly as a ship passing a lighthouse, the back edge of the front clears the grove. Behind it, the air is scrubbed to an almost painful transparency, and the sun is suddenly low—a fierce, flattened oval hovering just above the curved rim of the world, turning thirty thousand square miles of pale stone and migrating talc-waves into blazing, crystalline rose.

Now they have perhaps twenty-five minutes before the deep cold drops like an axe.

They come down out of the tree fast—not climbing, simply dropping from thirty feet up, flaring their capes at the last second to cushion their landing in the soft white lee-drift with barely a puff. Racing out to the weir on long, bounding strides, they ground the master-cord first with a long copper-bearing stone rod wedged into a deep bedrock crack—*crack!* goes a single fat purple spark, the loudest sound the basin has heard all week—and instantly the bristling net goes limp, heavy and fat with everything it stripped from the wind.

They do not shake it out onto the windward rock. Quickly unstringing the poles, they roll the whole forty-yard net inward upon itself like a baker rolling pastry, trapping the thick, dark grey paste of electrostatic catch inside. 

Back at the grove, as the massive violet sails overhead are already rolling tightly inward with a vast, whispering sigh of velvet against velvet, two of the keepers haul the bundled net up into the central crown—right into the deep, warm heart of the closing spire where the great fronds are drawing together for the night.

This is the bargain that keeps them alive, and keeps the trees welcoming them instead of armouring their bark with poison.

A stationary tree can only catch the wind that hits its canopy; forty yards of bare stone upwind goes unharvested. Climbing into the darkening, warm interior of the folding crown—where the ambient temperature is already thirty degrees warmer than the plunging purple twilight outside—the keepers unroll their net directly over the broad, pale digestive creases at the base of the massive fronds, brushing pounds of concentrated phosphorus, trace nitrogen, heavy mineral salts, and rich lipid-spores straight into the plant’s waiting mouths, keeping only a careful tithe of heavy, sweet-smelling pollen pearls and oily seed-cysts scraped into their own pouches.

Fed thrice what a single storm would normally bring it, the ancient tree responds through slow, chemical generosity. Down along the inner trunk, sheltered inside the zipped-shut tower of insulated foliage, small, specialized nodules—smooth, swollen burrs that look like polished amber—swell slightly under internal pressure. 

The youngest of the family—small enough still to curl comfortably in the fork of a single rib-branch—produces a slender tap carved from a glider’s hollow wing-quill. With extreme delicacy, cleaning the spot first with a dry velvet thumb, it slips the beveled quill two inches into a natural valve at the base of an amber burr.

No sap drips onto the wood. Nobody here has spilled a drop in ten generations. The free end of the quill goes straight between the youngster’s parted breathing-valves, into a hidden sip-tube behind the keratin mask. Slowly, rhythmically, its throat pulses as it draws four ounces of warm, thick, golden fluid—dense with synthesized sugars, liquid fats, and precious bound hydrogen—straight from the tree’s centuries-old cellar into its own insulated gut. When it pulls the quill free, it seals the tiny prick instantly with a dab of warm resin from its belt, smoothing it flat so not a single molecule of the tree’s hoard will bleed into the night.

One by one, in total silence, the four of them drink.

Outside the closed spire of the grove, the plateau has gone instantly, fiercely nocturnal. The sky is black as obsidian from zenith to rim, packed so densely with brilliant, steady, untwinkling stars that the pale whalebacks of polished stone glow with cold starlight, veined by the faint silver ghosts of migrating talc-drifts. Along the pointed tip of their own tree, fifty feet above their heads, a silent brush of violet St. Elmo’s fire flickers for three seconds and goes out as the folded canopy finishes equalizing its afternoon charge. Far off across the basin, dozens of other faint purple ghosts wink once and vanish along the dark spires of neighboring islands.

Inside the heart of the closed crown, it is pitch dark, utterly windless, and snug as an oven heated by sun-warmed wood. The great corky trunk ticks softly once an hour as its outer bark contracts in the plummeting chill outside, sixty degrees below zero on the exposed chert avenues.

Wedged into a dry cradle where four thick, velvet-haired boughs meet the main stem, the four keepers hook their harnesses lightly to the bark so nobody tumbles in sleep, and pull themselves together into a single, seamless ball. Overlapping their wide capes—scale-fur facing out, warm charcoal velveteen pressed against velveteen inside—they share the day’s hoarded sun-heat and the slow, rich burn of the golden sap in their bellies. Their breathing slows to one shallow, oil-warmed breath every two minutes.

Before they close their inner eyelids, in the pitch dark where flashing velvet cannot be seen, they talk a little longer—pressing long, three-jointed fingers gently against one another’s wrists and collarbones, tapping out slow, dry rhythms of static-click and pressure: recounting the shape of a funny dust-eddy seen at noon, speculating on where a favorite sixty-year-old tumble-fern will roll by next spring, mapping the forty-mile glide they will take four days from now when the wind shifts north-west and carries them—leaning into the hiss of silk on stone—to the next wide grove over the curve of the world.
