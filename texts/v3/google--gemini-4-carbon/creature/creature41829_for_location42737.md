You notice how they move before you ever get a clear look at a face. In the slots between the dry-stone walls, no one walks square-shouldered. A person descending a stair-channel turns sideways at the hip without breaking stride, leading with a shoulder, rolling their weight onto the outer edge of a boot so the sole doesn’t skate on the wet basalt. When two people meet in a corbeled passage, there is no pause and no spoken excuse; they rotate past one another chest-to-chest or back-to-back in a practiced, fluid pivot, eyes cast down to watch the water-line, hands tucked high into the opposite armpits or resting flat against their own ribs so their knuckles won't catch on the flinty rock.

To have elbows that stick out in the pit is to bleed. Children learn to pull themselves inward before they learn to speak in full sentences, moving through the dark, stacked maze like crabs through reef-rock.

Up close, they are compact, thick-chested people, bundled to the chin in heavy, grease-heavy wool hauled in from the scrubland traders, belted tight with cured amber Skin. Over the wool they wear aprons, capes, and shoulder-guards of the same calcified pellicle, oiled with tallow until it turns the color of dark honey and sheds water like slate. Because the Skin is stiffer than ox-hide, their outer garments don't drape; they stand out from the body in sharp, angular cones and cylinders. When a worker squats by a gutter to clear a blockage of black liverwort, their stiff amber coat bells out around them like a tent, keeping the cold seep off their woolens, crackling faintly like thin ice whenever they shift their weight.

Their faces are broad and pale from a life lived below the rim, save for a sharp, wind-chapped band across the cheekbones and nose-bridge among those who work the sedge-baskets on the upper lip. The damp chill and the peat-smoke from the curing kilns leave their mark on everyone: eyelids are often thickened and slightly rimmed with red, and their skin has a dense, waxy cast. 

Their hands tell the whole story of the town. From the wrists down, they are bare—wool sleeves are kept rolled back and bound tight with sedge-twine because wet wool rots the skin and drags in the troughs. Years of plunging into iron-heavy, near-freezing water give the people of the split swollen, crimson knuckles and fingers thick with muscle. Beneath the nails and settled permanently into the creases of the palms is a rusty, orange-brown staining from the mineral seep that no lye or fuller’s earth will take out. Nearly everyone carries a web of fine, white, dead-straight scars across the backs of their thumbs and forefingers—the casual bites of young basalt and flint scrapers.

They are quiet, not out of gloom, but because the architecture punishes volume. A shout in a three-foot-wide stone slot batters the eardrums, while a normal speaking voice gets lost thirty feet away in the endless, pattering percussion of ten thousand drips. Instead, they speak in a low, clipped, rhythmic murmur pitched just under the sound of trickling water, heavy on hard consonants that carry cleanly off wet slate. Across distances—from a high roof-courtyard down to a catch-basin two tiers below—they don't call out at all. They tap. A knuckle-bone or a small flint striker rapped twice against a dry retaining wall sends a sharp *tock-tock* shivering down through three stories of interlocking, mortarless stone. Every household knows its own cadence. You can sit in a lower room and feel, through the soles of your wooden-clogged boots, a neighbor two levels up asking for the flume-gate to be shifted left.

Because the town is an engine built of stacked weight and diverted water, its people have no concept of private maintenance. In the pit, negligence is structural violence. If you let a bone spout clog outside your threshold, the cold seep backs up, finds a dry seam in the stacked basalt, lubricates the friction-fit stones, and threatens to bulge a wall into the sleeping-room below yours. A person walking home with a bundle of peat will stop automatically to flick a pebble out of a stranger's step-channel or adjust the pitch of a wooden gutter by half an inch, listening until the *splatter* turns back into a clean, silent *glide*. 

This shared obsession has bred a particular kind of temperament: watchful, unsentimental, and intensely alert to change in sound. A mother stirring porridge over a tiny, enclosed peat-brazier is simultaneously listening to forty different water-notes outside her Skin-covered door. If the steady *clack-thump* of the ore-hammer down in the lower workshop misses a beat, or if a high spout begins to *chug* instead of *hiss*, three different doors will swing silently open on leather hinges before the overflow even reaches the stairs.

Down at the bottom, where the light goes green-grey and the air smells sharply of sulfur, wet iron, and the faint, bread-dough sourness of the bacterial mats, work the Skin-pullers. They are the stillness at the heart of the pit's motion. While the upper tiers require constant climbing and channel-tending, the sump demands an almost meditative patience. A ripple ruins a Skin. If a drop falls from an overhead flume onto a forming pool, it punches a pinhole in the pellicle that creates a weak spot when cured. So the sump-tenders move with agonizing slowness along the narrow stone causeways between the dark pools, sliding their feet rather than lifting them, breathing shallowly so they don't cough. 

They are usually the older folks whose knees have grown too stiff with pit-rheumatism for the switchback stairs, though their shoulders remain broad from hoisting wet mats. When the third day comes, two of them work a pool together in total silence, sliding long, fire-hardened wooden poles tipped with smooth bone hooks just beneath the grey-white, rubbery surface. They lift it an inch at a time, letting the black water sheet away without splashing, while the blind, pale crustaceans clinging to the slimy underside drop back into the dark with tiny *plips* like falling beads. 

Once the raw Skin is hauled up to the middle tiers, the work turns brisk and pungent. On stone tables under amber awnings that bathe their hands in greasy yellow light, the scrapers—often teenagers and young adults—work the wet mats with flat, unhafted blades of local basalt. *Shhhk, shhhk, shhhk.* They peel away the slick bottom-slime (which is gathered in buckets, mixed with hearth-ash, and carried up to fertilize the sedge-baskets on the rim, nothing wasted) until only the tough, fibrous mineral weave remains. They stretch it on pegged frames until it hums like a drumhead, then carry it into the low, corbeled curing-rooms.

Inside their dwellings, space is treated like the inside of a ship's hull. Rooms are tiny, low-ceilinged boxes of dark stone, dry only because they sit inside an envelope of channeled air and stretched Skin. To keep the damp out of their bones, they line the interior stone walls with removable panels of woven sedge-matting backed with cured pellicle, leaving a finger's breadth of air behind them so any rogue seep can trickle harmlessly down into a floor-groove. Floors are paved in flat slate, kept warm near the center by sunken clay fire-pots burning compressed bricks of scrub-peat and dried liverwort. 

They sleep folded into recessed stone alcoves—beds built like drawers into the thickness of the dry-stone walls—piled deep with raw, unwashed fleeces that smell fiercely of sheep-fat and smoke. There is almost no wood furniture; timber has to be dragged for miles across the grey scrubland, so every stick of it belongs to the flumes, the paddles, and the stretching-frames. Instead, they sit on low stone ledges padded with folded Skins, eat from bowls of molded, smoke-hardened pellicle, and store their dried mutton, barley, and salt-blocks in amber canisters stitched with sedge-cord and sealed with pitch.

What do they eat, down where nothing grows? They eat what the Skins buy. Once a month, caravans of flat-wheeled carts creak across the featureless scrubland and halt ten yards back from the invisible lip of the basin—the draft-mules won't come closer, spooked by the sudden drop and the damp, mineral smell rising from the earth. The pit-folk climb out into the blinding, windy expanse of the upper world, squinting fiercely against the flat sky, looking strangely dense and dark-armored beside the tall, dust-colored plainsmen. Up on the rim, stacked neat as slate, wait bales of amber sheet, seamless water-buckets that never rot, lightweight overlap-armor that turns a bronze spear-point, and translucent window-panes for distant lowland halls. In return, sacks of grain, tubs of mutton-tallow, raw wool, peat, and bog-timber are lowered down the cliff face on greased Skin-chutes into the waiting arms below.

The outsiders never come down the stairs. To someone raised on the open scrub, the pit feels like walking into a cold, ticking trap—forty tons of unmortared razor-rock hanging overhead, held up by geometry alone, slick with black water and smelling of iron and cured slime. 

The pit-folk don't linger on the rim either. Up top, the wind dries the throat and steals warmth faster than the damp ever could, and the horizon is an empty, unsettling waste with nothing to brace a shoulder against. Within an hour of the trade finishing, the last bale of wool disappears down the chute, and the lip of the split is empty again. 

Down in the green-lit bottom, the counterweights *clack-thump* in the dark. A girl on the third tier shifts a slate shingle two-tenths of an inch with the toe of her clog, silencing a drip that had started to strike an awning wrong. Behind thick curtains of amber Skin that glow softly from the tiny peat-coals within, red-knuckled hands rub warm tallow into stiff boots, listening to the water run exactly where it is told to go.
