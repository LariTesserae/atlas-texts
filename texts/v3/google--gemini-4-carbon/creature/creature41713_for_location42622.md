Up close, they do not smell like monkeys. They smell of wet slate, sour tallow, and the sharp, coppery tang of blood that carries twice the hemoglobin of any lowland creature’s. If you could bring a lamp near them—though the sudden flare would send a slow, delayed ripple of distress through their wide, milky-rimmed pupils—their skin would look neither black nor pink, but a deep, plum-colored slate, darkened by sluggish, over-rich capillary beds lying just beneath the epidermis.

They are built like bellows with hands. Their torsos are huge, conical barrels that flare outward from narrow shoulders down to heavy, bowl-shaped pelvises, accommodating lungs that fill nearly two-thirds of their body cavity. Because a long neck is merely dead space where warm breath cools before reaching the chest, their heads sit directly between their collarbones, tucked low like boulders settled into turf. Their faces are broad and dish-shaped, dominated by cavernous nasal apertures lined with scrolled, cartilaginous baffles. When one inhales—a slow, five-second draw that expands the ribs visibly even in the black—those baffles warm and wet the ammonia-laced air; when it exhales, they strip the moisture back out before it can be lost, leaving beads of clear water hanging from the coarse bristles of its upper lip.

They are never dry, and they are never clean, yet they are fastidious in a slow, viscous way. Their sparse hair grows like bog-rush, stiff and widely spaced so it cannot mat into soggy felt, and their skin secretes a thick, waxy sebum that repels the stinging alcali of the bird droppings. Every hour or so, without looking, one will reach across to a neighbor, pinch a lock of slick hair between a broad thumb and the side of a crooked index finger, and draw it slowly from root to tip, stripping away condensation, guano flecks, and blind cave-mites, then licking its fingers clean with a thick, purplish tongue.

There are seven of them on the ledge right now, though it is hard to tell where one heap of limbs ends and the next begins. At the back, pressed tight into the warm crease where the phosphorite shelf meets the sweating andesite wall, sits the oldest female. She is nearly hairless across the shoulders, her skin polished to the same dull sheen as the stone behind her by forty years of leaning into that exact hollow. Her breathing sets the tempo for the shelf. *Hhhhhh-uh.* A pause while the thin oxygen crosses thickened alveolar membranes. *Hhhhhh-aaah.* Wedged against her flank is a younger female with an infant—only one; twins do not happen at twenty-two thousand feet, and births come five or six years apart. The infant is the size of a skinned rabbit, almost entirely ribcage and skull, clinging not to its mother’s sparse hair, which would give way, but hooking its long, spatulate toes into the deep skin-folds of her groin and armpit, its flat face buried directly over her axillary artery where the heat is strongest.

They do not chatter. Speech, or even the hooting cries of forest apes, wastes breath and drowns instantly in the ceaseless hail-roar of the echolocating birds overhead. Instead, their world is tactile and percussive.

When the big male at the lip of the ledge—the one with the river-stone anvil between his heels—finishes prying the muscular foot from the cracked snail shell, he does not eat it all himself. He tears the rubbery grey meat in half with his thick, flat-crowned molars, chews one piece into a warm paste, and reaches backward without turning his head. His arm is long, heavy-boned, corded with slow-twitch muscle that never cramps and cannot sprint. His fingers—short-nailed, with pads broadened and ridged like damp crepe rubber—find the knee of the nursing female, travel up her thigh to her chin, and press the chewed bolus against the corner of her mouth. She takes it without breaking her breathing rhythm. In return, her hand slides down his forearm and gives two slow squeezes to his wrist. *Here. Still here.*

Look closer at their hands, and the strangeness deepens. They have the heavy, curved phalanges of climbing animals whose ancestors once clung to cliff-faces or timberline scrub miles below, before the mountains finished rising or the ice locked the passes. Their thumbs are thick, set low, swinging across the palm with a slow, crushing vise-grip. In the pitch dark, vision is useless for craft; their fingertips have become secondary eyes. When the male sets aside his bone pick and reaches into the shallow clutter beside his hip to select a fresh chopper, he does not fumble. He rolls three pieces of black basalt across his palm in one ponderous motion, reading the flake-scars, the micro-serrations, the greasy dullness of a spent edge versus the glassy bite of a fresh one, all in half a second of skin-contact.

The stones themselves are older than any living body in the cave. Because nothing hard grows in this throat of baked mud and fossilized dung, every true rock on this twelve-foot shelf is an heirloom. The green river-cobble between the male’s calloused heels came from a glacial outwash plain four thousand vertical feet down the mountain, carried up perhaps three hundred years ago by some ancestor who made the starving, freezing trek across the shale desert during a brief midsummer thaw, clutching a ten-pound stone against its belly for reasons half-instinct, half-tradition, pausing every twenty paces on the dead scree to gasp at the black sky. Since then, ten generations of thumbs have turned that cobble in the dark. The dimple in its center is three-quarters of an inch deep, glassy smooth, stained dark brown with ancient snail-slime and marrow fat. If a stone slips from a slick hand and tumbles off the lip into the beetle-choked dark twelve feet below, everything on the shelf stops. Two of them will climb down—moving like sloths descending a wet trunk, three points of contact always anchored on the tallow-slick rock—and spend an hour sweeping their wide hands methodically through the writhing, plum-sized pill-bugs and warm guano paste until their fingers strike the familiar density of river-jade or basalt. Nothing is abandoned.

Hunger here is slow, constant, and easily answered if you do not mind what you eat; thirst requires more patience.

A juvenile—four years old, still small enough to move across the vertical wall without its own weight peeling it off the slick biofilm—stirs at the far end of the ledge. It uncurls from its uncle’s lap, turns face-inward to the rock, and begins to climb sideways toward the water basins beneath the twilight slot. It moves at the exact pace of the room: reach, test the slime with the palm, press until skin-ridges bite stone, pull, breathe twice, reach again. Its splayed big toe works like a second thumb, jamming into wet flutes in the andesite directly alongside packed clusters of rock-rodents.

A rodent’s whiskers brush the juvenile’s ankle. Neither creature reacts. The rodent continues its deep, synchronized purring with its pile-mates; the young primate levers itself past, placing a palm squarely over the shell of a grazing snail until the snail’s slow suction gives way slightly, then shifting up to a dry knob.

When the juvenile reaches a palm-sized drip-basin, it waits. It hooks one elbow over a smooth boss of stone, drops its chin to the rim of the bowl, and opens its mouth. Above, sixty feet up in the cold draft, water gathers on the ceiling, swells, hangs, and falls. *Slap.* Cold mist sprays its flat cheeks. It laps the basin dry with six slow sweeps of its tongue, then stays there for ten minutes, letting three more drops strike its bare crown and run down the channels behind its tiny, folded-in ears, cooling the thick venous sinuses at the base of its skull.

On the way back, it hunts.

Four feet above the primate shelf, the lowest tier of the chestnut-plumaged birds begins—old individuals, mostly, or injured ones pushed down from the prime condensation-line roosts into the warmer, fouler air below. They hang three-deep, clicking steadily into the black—*tock, tock, tock*—their oiled backs rising and falling. The juvenile stops beside the outermost bird, a heavy cock with milky cataracts and pale lice clustering around its nostrils.

There is no pounce. A pounce would burn ten minutes of oxygen in half a second and leave the hunter blind with vertigo, chest heaving against the thin air. Instead, the juvenile brings both thick hands up with the slow, inexorable drift of tree roots, sliding fingers under the bird’s warm breast feathers from behind, right over the keel-bone, while its other hand closes gently—almost tenderly—around the whiskered head, thumb pressing over the nostrils, palm muffling the beak.

The bird’s tongue clicks twice against the primate’s calloused palm. Its scaled feet *scritch* once against the stone, claws raking harmlessly across the hunter’s tough, greasy forearm. One broad chestnut wing unfolds halfway in the cramped dark, brushing a curtain of rubbery spleenwort and knocking loose a slow shower of feather-dust and tiny pale beetles, beats twice with heavy, drugged lethargy, and goes limp. Hypoxia does ninety percent of the work; in air this thin, forty seconds without a breath simply snuffs the candle out. Even the birds overlapping its back do not scatter. They merely shift their hooked toes an inch inward to close the gap, clicking continuously into the resonant dome as the warm space beside them empties.

Back on the phosphorite threshold, the juvenile settles into the curve of the group. Now the tools come out in earnest, passing from hand to hand through the dark with a quiet clink of stone and bone.

They do not fight over the carcass. Aggression, like panic, is a lowland luxury; animals that bicker and chase each other in an anoxic chimney die of heart failure before their teeth draw blood, and that ruthless arithmetic sorted their lineage out two hundred millennia ago. What remains is a heavy, somatic communism.

The big male uses a freshly struck basalt flake—held pinched between thumb and curled forefinger, cutting edge down—to slit the tough, oiled skin along the bird’s breastbone. The sound is wet and small, swallowed entirely by the cavern’s roar, though everyone on the ledge feels the tug and give through the pressed-together contact of their thighs and shoulders. Nothing is wasted. The old female takes the soft viscera first, her worn teeth mashing liver and full crop—still packed with half-digested high-alpine carrion seeds and frozen insects the bird scavenged miles downslope yesterday—into rich, salty gruel. The nursing female gets the dark, myoglobin-black breast meat and the brain, scooped from the thin skull with a narrow vulture-ulna spoon worn shiny as ivory.

Even the feathers have a use, though not for warmth. They pluck the heavy flight quills one by one, bite off the hollow tips, and suck out the bloody pulp inside the shafts; the downy body-feathers are wadded into greasy clumps and used to wipe the stinging guano-slime from the infant’s eyes and crease-folds before being dropped over the edge.

Then comes the sound that belongs only to them.

The male lays the bird’s stripped humerus across the green river-stone anvil, steadies it with two thick fingers, and lifts his hammerstone.

*Clack.*

In that split second, if you were listening from the crooked basalt entrance fifty feet away, you would hear the chamber answer. None of the ten thousand birds stop clicking, none of the rodents stop their bass purr, yet in the half-second following the strike of stone on bone, the acoustic texture of the cave shifts—a faint, collective tightening of ten thousand eardrums, a ripple in the echolocation roar as that sharp, high-frequency transient bounces off the soapstone dome and tells every blind and half-blind thing in the chimney exactly where the heavy hands are sitting.

*Clack.*

He twists the splintered shaft, slides the polished bone pick down the canal, and draws out a cylinder of warm yellow marrow. Instead of eating it, he reaches into the dark corner behind him and smears half of it across the infant’s closed lips, then rubs the leftover grease thoroughly into the working edge of his bone pick so it will not dry and split in the ammonia air, tucking the tool back into a narrow horizontal fissure in the wall—a natural shelf worn smooth by five thousand previous placements of that exact kind of bone.

Midday arrives, or what passes for it here.

High up in the throat, the rime-choked slot catches the outside glare off the snowfields, and the single diagonal shaft of bruised grey twilight leans down the eastern wall. It never touches the primate ledge; it stops twenty feet away, illuminating the pale, leathery curtains of spleenwort and turning the falling ceiling-drops into brief, plummeting sparks of pewter before they vanish back into the dark.

Yet the troop knows it is noon. Two of them—a subadult female and a battered second male missing half an ear to frostbite from some long-ago venture outside—turn their broad, flat faces toward that distant smear of grey on the opposite wall. Their eyes are huge, set beneath heavy, rounded brow-ridges, with irises so dark they swallow the sclera entirely, leaving only a faint blueish ring at the edge where a tapetum-like sheen gathers stray photons. For twenty minutes, while the dim smudge inches across the wet stone and sets the floating feather-dust glowing like cold smoke, the two sit motionless with their chins resting on their drawn-up knees, simply watching light exist.

The frostbitten male is the only one currently bearing the marks of the outside. Every few weeks, when the deep mineral cravings hit—when the endless diet of snail-flesh, bird-viscera, and blind arthropods leaves their blood starving for certain salts or roughage, or when a young male must leave this ledge to seek one of the two or three other fumarole-caves rumored in scent-memory across the dead range—someone has to step four paces outward through the crooked basalt lip.

They prepare for it for days, gorging on bird fat and marrow, coating their slate-purple skin in double layers of tallow-slime and their own waxy sebum. Even then, stepping out into the twenty-two-thousand-foot shale desert is like stepping onto the moon. Out there, their slow-motion cave bodies suddenly have to move steadily just to keep their core blood from sludging, digging under dead frost-shattered slate for salt-crusts or frozen lichen, or trudging bent-double along the spine of a ridge under a black noon sky, ribs heaving like smithy-bellows, following the microscopic thread of sulfur-and-ammonia vapor on the wind toward another warm chimney fifteen miles away. Most who try the crossing between peaks freeze into grey stone beside the glaciers and subliming snow. A few arrive, crawling over a strange basalt lip with blackened ear-rims and cracked knuckles, carrying a new nodule of flint tucked under one arm as a bride-price, smelling of foreign rock-iron, to be sniffed over for hours in the dark and folded slowly into another troop’s breathing pile.

Here on the shelf, the grey smudge on the eastern wall narrows to a pencil-line, dims to violet, and goes out. Night—indistinguishable from afternoon except for a fractional slowing of the ceiling-drips as the upper rime thickens—settles back over the throat of the mountain.

The troop rearranges itself by touch. Nobody lies flat; lying flat lets fluid pool in heavy, high-altitude lungs. They sleep sitting upright, wedged back-to-chest and hip-to-hip in a tight, inward-facing crescent against the warm andesite wall, forming a single shared mass of slow-burning flesh. Chin rests on shoulder; heavy forearm drapes across neighbor’s ribs; wide, calloused feet tuck beneath another’s thighs. In the center of the knot, completely roofed over by barrel chests and coarse, damp arms, the infant suckles twice and slips into dreamless torpor.

Only the old female at the back remains half-awake for another quarter-hour. Her broad right hand rests palm-down beside her hip, fingers curled loosely around the cool, dimpled belly of the green river-cobble—not working it, just holding on to its weight so it stays warm, and so she knows, through the roar of ten thousand clicking throats and the endless, wet turning of the dark, right where the center of the world is.
