To see what lives here, you have to stop looking for movement at the speed of blood and start watching at the speed of grease and calcification. If you stand on one of the lower grated catwalks—your boots vibrating with the twenty-beat-per-minute throb of the brass turbines—and stare at a single, travertine-encrusted bearing housing through three cycles of the twelve-minute steam vent, you will realize that a pale hump of mineral scale on the warm iron has shifted four inches to the left.

At first, they look entirely geological, or perhaps like industrial waste that has solidified in the damp. They are flat, heavy, shield-shaped masses ranging from the size of a dinner plate to the width of a manhole cover, clinging to the wet brick, the submerged copper coils, and the undersides of the iron beams. Their upper surfaces are tiered Plates of precipitated calcium carbonate, iron oxide, and verdigris, indistinguishable from the fluted travertine aprons deposited by the misting pipes. Even the velvet-green bryophytes grow across their backs in undisturbed cushions. 

Beneath those stone-and-rust carapaces, however, is a dense, muscular foot of pale, translucent gray gristle, warm to the touch and slick with a heavy, viscous mucus that smells faintly of ozone and wet slate. 

They do not scurry because nothing hunts them, and nothing they want can run away. They are creatures of pure, unhurried maintenance—biological shims that have either evolved within this sunken block over centuries of isolation or crawled up through some ruptured subterranean river-vein long ago and let the machinery remake them. They have no eyes; in the bruised, greenish-gray twilight and periodic whiteouts of the sump, vision is useless. Instead, their mantles are fringed with dozens of blunt, fleshy filaments the color of tallow, which they press flat against the vibrating steel and wet masonry. Through these, they read the sump entirely as a map of heat gradients, fifty-cycle electrical hums, and low-frequency mechanical tremors.

If you pry one loose—which requires a crowbar and considerable leverage, as their suction on the wet vitrified brick is immense—you find their underside laced with fine, dark-copper threads. They graze at the exact intersection of the metallurgical, the mineral, and the electrical. 

Watch one over an hour as it glides imperceptibly up a weeping brick pier toward a frayed, tar-cloth cable. Where the weak, fifty-cycle current leaks into the wall-water and blooms into branching patterns of glowing, bluish-white lichen, the creature settles. It drapes its heavy mantle over the exposed wire. For twenty minutes, it stays there, absorbing the mild alternating current directly through its conductive flesh—a tiny, localized drop in voltage causes the nearest caged amber bulb to dim by a fraction of a lumen—while its radula, a ribbon of microscopic magnetite teeth, scrapes away the electro-trophic lichen and the soft, wet mortar beneath it, leaving a clean, crescent-shaped scar on the brick.

Others work the moving parts. Because the machines operate at such a patient, rhythmic pace, the creatures treat the oscillating pistons and turning shafts not as hazards, but as rolling topography. A sluggish, yard-wide specimen will ease itself onto the rim of a massive, petrified gear wheel, riding the slow arc upward through the drenching spray of an overhead pipe. As the wheel turns, the creature presses its muscular underside into the lime-crusted troughs between the bronze teeth, rasping away the buildup of scale and lubricating paste—a mixture of ancient, saponified machine tallow and thick algal slime that collects in the grease-traps. It rides the wheel right up to the point of mesh, and then, reading the subtle intensification of acoustic pressure through its sensory fringe, smoothly hitches itself sideways onto the stationary housing an inch before the gleaming bronze crescents grind together, leaving a trail of slick, pearlescent mucilage that instantly quiets the groan of the bearing.

In a very real sense, they are the reason the cistern-court has not seized up or shaken itself apart over centuries of unattended labor. By grazing the precipitated lime from the sliding sleeves and packing their dense, rubbery bodies into the vibrating joints where bolts have rusted away—holding tight for weeks at a time to drink the rich friction-heat of a loose bracket—they act as living gaskets, dampers, and descalers. 

Every twelve minutes, when the counterweighted lever on the western wall groans downward and the long plume of white vapor floods the court, a subtle change passes through them. 

As the visibility drops and the heavy roar of the turbines muffles into a thick, cushioned throb, every carapace in the sump lifts slightly—just a quarter of an inch off the brick and iron, supported on stiffened columns of gray flesh. From under the rims of their calcified shells, they extrude delicate, pleated gills of a startlingly vivid, metallic copper-red, looking briefly like raw meat or oxidized wire blooming in the fog. For three minutes, while the giant ferns drink the cloud, these hidden inhabitants breathe the hot, mineral-heavy steam, filtering trace sulfates and iron bacteria directly from the vapor, motionless in the warm white dark. 

And there is something larger down in the three feet of warm, iridescent water below the lowest walkways, though you will never see all of it at once.

If you lean over the mossy iron railings and look past the broad-leaved water plants floating in the oil-skimmed eddies, staring down into the dark current where the submerged copper coils glow dimly with heat, you might notice that the layout of the bottom vaults changes slowly from week to week. What looks like a submerged, silt-covered flywheel or a collapsed groin-vault of dark brick will, over several days, uncoil a yard of thick, segmented, slate-colored hide, shifting its bulk closer to the warm exhaust of a brass turbine. 

These are the elders of the basin—the final stage of the pale grazers above. Once they grow too heavy with accumulated iron and travertine to cling to the vertical walls, they drop cleanly into the slow current, just like the yellowing leaves, though they are far too dense and broad to be drawn into the intake rollers. Down in the flooded vaults, buoyed by the lukewarm water, they never stop growing. Over decades, their shells fuse with the fallen masonry and the sunken pipework, becoming massive, sluggish ridges of living conglomerate that channel the dark water through the sluices. 

They lie in the perpetual vibration of the turbine housings, blind and deaf and utterly content, filtering the mulched, immaculate leaves that drift down from the intake grates and soaking in the endless, tepid bath. Occasionally—perhaps once or twice a day—one of these sunken masses shifts its weight against a submerged intake gate to find a warmer eddy. When it does, the flow of water alters just enough to change the pitch of the court's saturating roar: the twenty-beat-per-minute *thrum* deepens by half a semitone, the counterweighted sluices sigh in compensation, a tremor runs up the vine-wrapped drive shafts to shake a single rain of warm condensate from the high canopy of leaves, and the whole drowned block adjusts itself around the slow, heavy breathing of its masonry.
