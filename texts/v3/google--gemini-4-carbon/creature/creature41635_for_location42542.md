Wait long enough on that lower platform—long enough for the damp cold to soak through your soles and for the green fungal chalk to burn a faint, reliable map of the bridges into your retinas—and a piece of one of the woven sedge tenements will detach itself from the wall and step onto an aqueduct.

You do not see them at first because you are looking for bare skin, or fur, or the pale, greasy tallow of cave-dwellers, and they show none of it. Furthermore, you are likely looking for something at the scale of the miniature doorways—something the size of a rat or a stoat to match the hand-split willow stairs and the dovecote roofs. 

What detaches itself from the tenement is the size of a five-year-old child, though built nothing like one, and it never goes inside the buildings at all. 

As it moves out onto the narrow ledge beside the water-trough, the winter-moths do not scatter. They stay packed three-deep across its back, its shoulders, and the broad, sloped shelf of its neck, shivering in their slow, dry waves. The creature wears a thick, tiered mantle woven from the exact same split sedge and braided hair-moss as the houses, tailored with rows of downward-facing wicker eaves specifically designed for the fat, greasy moths to hook their tarsi into. Beneath that living, vibrating thatch of insects, the wearer is kept in a pocket of trapped, musky warmth twenty degrees milder than the chasm air.

There are perhaps nine or ten of them in the entire courtyard-sized world. Any more and even this furiously productive cistern-engine could not feed them through the winter.

Watch one crouch by a clay-caulked basin to clear a jam of shed crab-shells from a wooden paddle-wheel, and its proportions slowly untangle themselves from the gloom. Beneath the moth-cloak, its body is dense, compact, and barrel-chested, wrapped in a loose, heavily wrinkled hide the color of leached bog-oak and slick with a thin, tallowy sebum that smells faintly of peat smoke and lanolin. Its legs are short, bowed, and extraordinarily thick at the knee and ankle, ending in broad, splayed feet with four opposing, hook-nailed toes—two forward, two back, like a chameleon’s or a woodpecker’s, though heavy as ironwood wedges. It does not stand ON the narrow wooden ledges so much as clamp onto their sharp edges, locking its tarsal tendons so it can lean forty-five degrees out over the pitch-black drop without effort, leaving both hands entirely free.

Its arms are disproportionately long, corded with slow-twitch rope muscle, tapering into startlingly delicate, narrow hands. Each hand bears six fingers—two thumbs opposing four long, flat-knuckled digits whose tips are broadened into pale, sensitive pads constantly trembling against the wood. 

When it turns its head toward the center of the chasm, there is no glint of an eye. Where orbits ought to be, smooth, taut skin stretches unbroken over shallow depressions in the bone, dusted with a few long, stiff vibrissae as fine as copper wire. Instead, its nasal cavity has widened into a broad, fleshy, double-slitted rosette dominating the center of a flat, heavy-jawed face, continually flexing in tiny, silent muscular ripples as it reads the thermal plumes rising off the tenements and the sharp chemical map of sap, chitin, and cold iron. Its ears are wide, hairless, shell-thin saucers set low near the hinge of the jaw, capable of cupping forward independently to catch the change in pitch when an aqueduct begins to overflow three tiers overhead.

They are not primitives playing at miniatures. They are hydraulic shepherds, and the "city" is not their shelter—it is an externalized digestive and heating apparatus built at the exact scale of the creatures they farm.

Watch the worker at the cistern. Having cleared the paddle-wheel so its rhythmic *clack-clack* resumes agitating the water, it reaches into a pouch of stitched eel-skin slung beneath its dry cloak and draws out a tool: a flake of black flint lashed with sinew into a handle of split rib-bone, no longer than a human thumb. Stepping down onto a hair-moss suspension bridge—no wider than two fingers, yet stretching taut as a bowstring under sixty pounds of compact muscle—it walks out over the black void with slow, terrifyingly sure-footed grace. Halfway across, where no wall is near, you understand why the center of the chasm was left so starkly empty. 

The empty air is a firewall and a paddock. The flightless moths will not cross thin, freezing hair-moss exposed to open drafts; the milky, fist-sized woodlice cannot climb braided fiber; the blind salamanders stay near the wet timber. By leaving yards of dead air between neighborhoods and linking them only with tightrope-thin spans, the builders can quarantine populations, control grazing density, and keep the ravenous woodlice strictly penned against the encroaching callus-wood where their teeth are needed, rather than letting them chew through the woven tenements.

Reaching the far side, where the colossal bog-tree is pushing a pale, glistening bulge of scar-tissue against a retaining wall of notched twigs, the keeper does not cut the wood away. Instead, using a short awl of cold-hammered bog-iron, it drives three precise, shallow punctures into the tight cambium five inches *above* the bulge. Clear, freezing sap immediately beads from the wounds, releasing that sharp wintergreen tang into the damp air. Within thirty seconds, the translucent coin-crabs along the nearest aqueduct alter their two-way traffic, streaming up the vertical wood toward the scent of fresh sugar, while two heavy, milky woodlice lumber upward from the retaining wall to rasp at the newly bled fibers. With three silent pricks of iron, the keeper has steered its livestock six inches north, relieving pressure on a sagging wicker street without lifting a timber.

While its right hand puts the awl away, its left reaches back under the eaves of its own wicker cloak, plucks two fat, torpid winter-moths from between its shoulder blades, and pops them whole into its mouth. Its broad jaw slides sideways with a muffled, wet crunch of grease and soft chitin. It swallows, wipes a smear of grey wing-dust across its chest to waterproof its hide, and reaches into a dovecote tenement to pluck three fresh, warm moths from the clustered ceiling, tucking them under its collar to replace the two little furnaces it just ate.

Where do they sleep, if the huts are for insects and the cisterns are for eels?

Look down—past the lower platforms, past the waste-chutes, to the flooded V-shaped keel at the bottom of the split root-pocket, and then look *up* at the underside of the largest buttress-ledges just above the waterline. Slung in the deepest, blindest angles where the fractured heartwood overhangs the water, completely invisible from above, hang five or six bulbous, enclosed hammocks the size of wine-casks, woven from triple-walled sedge stuffed with shed moth-fur and waterproofed on the outside with grey marsh-clay. 

Right now, three or four of the keepers are curled inside one of those dark pods together, knotted limb-over-limb like hibernating badgers, breathing slow, shallow drafts of warm, ammonia-tinged air while a dozen winter-moths cling to the exterior thatch. Outside, the rest of the clan works the dark shifts. Two are high up near the ceiling’s bruised-grey pinhole, dangling on braided moss ropes like plump, methodical spiders as they reset stone-chipped wedges into the surface roots—carefully keeping that single crack open wide enough to vent stale carbon dioxide into the mire above, yet narrow enough that surface frost and diving bog-otters cannot enter. Another sits motionless on a lower stair, legs locked to the wood, both six-fingered hands submerged to the wrist in an icy cistern; it waits without shivering for twenty minutes until a fat, eyeless mud-eel coils around its warm fingers, then lifts the creature smoothly out, breaks its spine with a pinch of twin thumbs, and begins skinning it in the pitch dark by touch alone.

Nothing is spoken among them. When two keepers meet on a narrow vertical ladder—one climbing down with a bundle of freshly shaved willow lath harvested from a lesser root-vein, the other climbing up—they do not yield or call out. The descending one simply locks its four-toed feet into the grain, leans backward until it hangs horizontal over the black drop, and lets the climber walk directly over its chest and belly. As their bodies pass, their six-fingered hands brush briefly against one another’s cheeks and jawlines—a swift, fluttering Braille of skin-temp, grease-smell, and fingertip-taps that conveys which sluice is silting up, where the tree is swelling fastest, and how thick the eels are running in the lower basins. Then they separate without a sound, swallowed back into the sour green glow and the dry, endless rustle of ten thousand shivering wings.

They were not trapped here when the frost split the tree seasons ago. Up through the root-systems of the coastal mire, there are older hollows—dying ones where the host-trees finally rotted through and flooded with black peat-slurry, or healed shut entirely, crushing old wickerwork into fossilized seams inside the timber. When this colossal root tore open with a subterranean boom that shook the marsh-bed miles away, scouts digging along the root-cambium felt the shockwave through their palms. They tunnelled in through a thumb-wide sap-duct, widened it with flint and bone just enough to squeeze their flexible ribs through, sealed the tunnel behind them with clay and iron pins, and brought the starter-eggs of the moths, the gravid crabs, and the pale woodlice wrapped inside warm pouches against their bellies.

In ten years, or thirty, the great bog-tree will either die of its hollow heart and drown this courtyard in black mud, or finally win its slow war against the woodlice and squeeze the chasm shut. Until then, the nine blind architects hang in the freezing dark, pruning their living walls, steering cold water through thumb-wide troughs, wearing coats of warm insects, and keeping their tiny, silent metropolis running three stories beneath the mud.
