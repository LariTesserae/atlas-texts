If you watch the stone bowls beneath the gut-skin sheets for long enough—watching from the deep blue shadow where the glare cannot bleach the color from your eyes—you will eventually notice that the water level drops even when no human climbers have crested the eastern notch for months. 

At first it looks like evaporation, or a leak in the chipped andesite. Then a section of the talus beside the bowl detaches itself from the black slate, unfolds a set of startlingly long, spindly limbs, and dips a narrow muzzle into the sun-warmed water.

Wait longer, letting your eyes adjust to the violent contrast between the white-violet sky and the ink-black crevices, and you realize the basin is not merely visited by toolmakers. Something else is already here, living full-time in the narrow margin between the geothermal breath and the killing frost, quietly borrowing the works of the lowlanders and making strange, meticulous alterations of its own.

It is an animal that has not quite finished becoming whatever it will be in another ten thousand years—a stray branch of high-slope snow-monkeys or cliff-lemurs that found this cracked summit a few centuries ago, slipped inside, and never went back down. There are perhaps forty of them in the whole nave of the mountain. More than that and the moss banks would be stripped; fewer and they would freeze in the twenty-hour winter nights when the sun never clears the jagged rim at all.

Up close, one of them is an unsettling mixture of fragility and dense insulation. Standing upright—which it rarely does, preferring a low, spider-like scuttle that keeps its belly near the warm fault lines—it would scarcely reach a human’s hip. Beneath its coat it is all cage and lever: flat, wide ribs like the basking lizards to house lungs oversized for the thin air, long collarbones, and disproportionately lengthy forearms ending in black, hairless fingers as tough and scaly as falcon feet. The sharp, unweathered andesite slices soft pads to ribbons; these creatures touch the rock only with hooked, slate-colored nails and calloused knuckles thickened like horn.

Over this rangy frame grows a pelt that mimics the silver rosettes so closely that when one crouches in a thicket, it vanishes completely. The guard hairs are hollow, translucent, and four inches long, trapping a thick envelope of dead warm air while bouncing the blistering ultraviolet radiation back into the basin in a soft, metallic sheen. Only when it turns away from the sun, opening the vents of its coat along its spine and flanks, do you see that its skin underneath—and the fine underfur parted along its chest—is soot-black, drinking heat just like the alpine moths whenever it flattens itself inside a stone windbreak.

Its face is bare only around the eyes, which are immense, set forward in deep bony orbits, and startlingly dark. There is no visible white to them at all; the sclera is pigmented jet-black to absorb stray glare, and over the iris sits a heavy, amber-tinted third eyelid that slides sideways across the eyeball whenever the creature crosses out of the blue shade into the raw flood of noon. In the sunlight its pupils contract not to pinpoints, but to crisp horizontal slits like a goat’s, crossed by a tiny vertical notch—four-way shutters cutting out the blinding light from the sky above and the silver foliage below. Its ears are almost gone, trimmed down by frostbite and ancestry to tight, furred valves flush against the skull that seal shut against the wet chill of the vents.

Like everything else in the cleft, they have given up on sound. Their chests heave rapidly in the starved air, too busy pulling oxygen from the thin atmosphere to spare breath for shrieks or chatter. Even when two young ones tumble through a cushion of emerald moss in mock combat, biting at each other’s heels, all you hear is the crisp *crunch* of the frozen moss-rind breaking and the rapid, dry clicking of their nails on slate.

Instead, they talk with their eyelids, their hands, and stolen light.

Watch an adult female perched atop one of the low beehive cairns built by the lowland gatherers. She sits hunched into a silver ball, barely distinguishable from a frost-rimmed boulder, until a pale falcon drops from the white glare above. She does not bark an alarm. She simply throws open one long forearm. The inner skin of her wrist, hidden until that instant under her silver mantle, is hairless and flushed a brilliant, arterial crimson by a dense web of surface capillaries pumped full of thick, high-altitude blood. In the diamond-clear air, against the black andesite and white-silver plants, that sudden flash of wet red screams across the basin louder than a whistle. Instantly, three hares freeze, and four other silver shapes thirty yards away snap their arms shut and drop flat between the talus plates, becoming grey-white lichen on black stone.

When the danger passes, they converse across the thermal cracks in rapid, jittering bursts: flashing a crimson wrist, rolling back their heavy dark lips to show startlingly pale gums, flicking the black skin of their palms against the reflective white fur of their chests in quick, rhythmic codes. *My fissure. Cold coming. Watch the eastern notch.*

Their relationship with the humans who climb up through that notch is a bizarre, silent truce built entirely on observation and theft.

When the lowland gatherers haul themselves gasping over the eastern lip—wrapped in oiled skins, squinting behind slotted bone snow-goggles, dizzy and vomiting slightly from the altitude as they hurry to harvest the glowing bladder-sedges—the basin appears wholly devoid of primates. Every last one of the forty residents has wedged itself six feet deep into narrow, steaming chimneys in the fault-rock where no human shoulder could ever follow, breathing the warm, sulfurous vapor in tight, motionless knots of silver fur. 

From the dark of the cracks, amber-shielded eyes watch everything the clumsy giants do.

The moment the humans scramble back down out of the cleft before the late-afternoon freeze can kill them, the silver things emerge, moving with feverish, clock-driven urgency before the shadow wall sweeps the floor.

They inspect every cairn and windbreak first. They do not understand mortar or archways, so when they try to copy the humans’ stone stacking, their walls fall down; instead, they maintain what the humans leave behind, jamming fresh pads of torn moss into gaps between the sharp black blades of rock where the cold leaks in. They drink from the sun-warmed stone bowls, carefully lifting the sinew drip-cords with two hooked fingers to lick the condensed water straight from the twisted fibers before it can freeze at dusk.

Stranger still is what they do with the mirrors.

The people prop quartz dishes and obsidian flakes on three-stone tripods to throw light onto rare, shade-bound glass-stalks so the plants will swell before the next harvest. The cliff-dwellers have watched this, and while they care nothing for medicine, they care fiercely about warmth and moths. Within an hour of the humans’ departure, two or three older elders—their silver coats matted with grey mineral salts from sleeping directly over the vents—scuttle up the vertical chisel-marks of the walls with terrifying ease, hooking nail-tips onto edges no wider than a coin. They reach the tripods and nudge the volcanic glass and polished quartz by fractions of an inch.

They aim the reflected beams not at the herbs, but straight into the mouths of their own nocturnal sleeping-cracks, pre-heating the rock inside before the sun vanishes behind the western wall. Others angle the mirrors onto damp patches of moss still trapped in blue-black shade; within minutes, a small, frantic swarm of soot-black alpine moths drifts out of the cold gloom to gather in the artificial coin of sunlight, where juvenile monkeys wait crouched at the dark margin to pluck them out of the air with two-fingered snaps, stuffing the velvety insects whole into their mouths.

Down at the eastern notch, where the bleached beast-ribs stand wedged in the scree, hung with copper and mica, the largest male of the troop often sits for the final twenty minutes of daylight as the sweeping shadow swallows the basin floor. He has pulled two of the mica discs loose from their sinew ties—not out of malice, though the next human party to crest the rim will mutter prayers against wind-demons when they find their shrine disturbed—and holds one in each black, scaly hand. 

He does not bang them together. He tilts them.

Catching the last, horizontal lance of violet-white light grazing the highest western tooth of the peak, he throws quick, silent needles of brilliance down into the darkening nave below. Down in the deep blue gloom where the rime ice is already spreading inward across the wet boulders with faint, crystalline ticks, little answers flash back from the mouths of the warm vents: a sudden blink of crimson wrist, a pale flash of gum, another stolen shard of split mica turning once, twice in the dark, counting heads before the long, breathless cold locks them underground until morning.
