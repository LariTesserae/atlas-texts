You do not hear them approach, because they do not make their own noise; they borrow the valley’s. When a bloated bladder-fruit bursts with a wet *thwack*, or when a mat of tallow-fronds tears loose and crashes into a ravine, a half-dozen shadows drop from the swaying vine-cables in exact synchronization with the racket, absorbing the shock of landing in deep, frog-like squats before freezing completely still.

Up close—if you were to brush against one in the pitch-black, or catch a glimpse of a flank in the brief, sickly yellow flare of disturbed rot-fungus—they are startlingly dense, compact creatures, built low and wide to survive falls onto raw basalt. They are roughly hominid in proportion, if a hominid had been reshaped over ten thousand generations by dark, heat, and razor-wire terrain. None stand taller than four and a half feet, yet they weigh as much as large men, packed with thick, ropey muscle and armored where it counts. 

Their skin is completely hairless—fur would only mat with slime, trap parasites, and rot in the perpetual steam—and ranges in tone from translucent mushroom-gray to the bruised purple-black of cooled slag. Across their shins, forearms, shoulders, and the backs of their necks, the hide thickens into overlapping, calloused plates, deeply grooved and hardened by constant abrasion and mineral sweat until it resembles pale horn. You can hear basalt shards *tink* harmlessly against their calves as they move. Their feet are wide, flat, and splayed, with tough, vulcanized soles nearly an inch thick and long, prehensile big toes set far back like thumbs, allowing them to grip slick obsidian ridges or clamp onto wet vine-cables with equal iron certainty.

Their faces are startling, not because they are monstrous, but because they have discarded what the basin cannot use and exaggerated everything else. Where eye sockets should be, there is only a smooth, uninterrupted sweep of heavy brow bone covered in taut skin; the eyes have not merely gone blind, they have vanished beneath the flesh entirely to protect delicate wet tissue from snapping twigs, formic acid sprays, and obsidian dust. Above that blank brow, however, the skin of the forehead and cheekbones is exquisitely thin, laced with tight, pale ridges of blood vessels and nerve-pits that read subtle shifts in radiant heat—the warm plume of a fresh steam-vent ten yards off, the hot, frantic pulse of a bat-moth tangled in a frond, the cooling body of a freshly crushed crab.

Dominating the sides of their low, wide skulls are their ears: vast, mobile, bat-like dishes of ribbed cartilage that swivel independently, folding flat and sealing shut in an instant when a bladder-fruit detonates nearby, then flaring wide again to drink in the returning echoes. Their noses are broad, muscular slits that flare and seal like those of diving seals, filtering out the choke of dry ash under the roots and sorting the thick chemical soup of the air into distinct trails.

When they move through the mid-tier of the canopy, they move with six points of contact—two hands, two grasping feet, and two heavy elbows or knees hooked over adjacent stalks, testing every fleshy rung before committing their weight. Nothing in this valley can be trusted to hold for long; a vine that supported an adult twenty minutes ago might be half-chewed through by grubs now, or slick with newly condensed tallow-sweat. So they flow through the dark in a continuous, tactile dance, fingers flickering ahead like antennae. 

Those hands are extraordinary. Four fingers and two opposing thumbs—one on either side of a broad, leathery palm—tipped with thick, flat nails worn square from prying shell and flaking stone. The pads of their fingertips are swollen and whorled like a star-nosed mole’s snout, vibrant with nerve endings. Watch one crouch beside a raw nodule of volcanic glass in the absolute dark: it does not fumble. It rolls the stone once between its palms, reading the internal stress lines, grain, and micro-fractures through vibration and surface tension alone. Then, holding a dense piece of columnar basalt as a hammerstone, it strikes once—*tik*—just as an arboreal amphibian shrieks overhead to mask the sound. A flake drops into its waiting palm. Without pausing, its thumb-pads sweep the freshly parted glass—an edge one molecule thick, sharp enough to part skin before pain registers—testing the bevel with a touch so feather-light it draws no blood, before tucking the blade into a belt of braided strangler-vine.

They carry their entire culture wrapped around their waists and ribs. Because nothing can be cached on the ground—where the pale creepers would swallow it by morning and the acid-grubs would digest the bindings by noon—everything they value stays on the body. Slung tight against their flanks in pouches made from cured seed-bladders, so nothing rattles or swings loose to snag a thorn, are their kits: obsidian cores, bone awls, bundles of stripped tendon, stoppered hollow reeds packed with rendered crab-fat, and dried lumps of fiery, alkaline lichen used to cauterize cuts before the airborne rot can take hold. Even their spears are modular—short, dense lengths of fire-hardened tuber-wood socketed with crab-shell ferrules so they can be broken down in seconds to crawl through a low basalt squeeze, or snapped together into a six-foot pike when a scavenger swarm boils up out of a chasm.

Fire, you noticed from the spears, they know—though there is no open flame down in the basin, where dry wood does not exist and smoke would hang in suffocating pockets beneath the rubbery fronds. Instead, they farm heat from the rock itself. Deeper in the labyrinth, where the tectonic fissures glow dull cherry-red down in the dry ash beds beneath the root-mats, they thrust peeled green stalks into the superheated cracks, baking the sap out and charring the fibers hard in natural geothermal kilns while they wait above, scraping meat.

They travel in tight, fluid knots of eight to twelve—small enough to huddle together inside the twenty-foot bubble of a single steam-whistle shelter, large enough that someone is always awake and listening while the others sleep. 

When they construct one of those screaming organ-pipe sanctuaries along a sharp ridge, the transformation in their behavior is immediate and profound. Out in the raw acoustic storm of the valley, they are taut, silent ghosts, communicating only in sharp, localized clicks of the fingernails against their horn-plated forearms, or by pressing three fingers briefly against a companion’s collarbone as they pass on a vine—a rapid, shorthand braille of *vent ahead*, *unstable mat*, *heavy prey below*, *follow*. To open your throat and shout in the open basin is to paint a bright, wet target on yourself for every velvet snare-worm and sonar-shrieking leaper within fifty yards.

Inside the discordant, roaring wall of the steam-pipes, however, they are invisible to the valley, and they unfold.

They drop onto the sharp stone in a tight pile, tangled together like litter-mates, limbs draped across shoulders and bellies. Touch is their hearthlight. As soon as the perimeter is deafened, hands begin moving over skin—not anxiously, but with slow, methodical intimacy. They groom the slick mineral sweat and stinging caterpillar hairs from one another’s neck-folds; they press bruised muscles; they inspect the soles of feet for embedded glass slivers, popping them free with crab-claw tweezers and smearing the punctures with bitter fungus-paste. Infants—pale, oversized heads all ears and grasping hands—are unlashed from leather slings across their mothers’ chests and passed from lap to lap, mouthed gently on the crown, learning the thermal signature and callous-pattern of every adult in the band.

And inside that howling ring of steam, beneath the register of the pipes, they hum. 

They do not speak with tongues and lips the way surface creatures do; the air is too loud for crisp consonants to survive three feet. Instead, they have deep, barrel chests and twin pharyngeal sacs in their thick throats—convergent with the very amphibians they blind with their whistles, though pitched much lower, down in the sub-bass where sound travels through stone and flesh rather than air. Sitting shoulder to shoulder on a continuous spine of basalt, they press their heels and tailbones flat against the rock and let out long, resonant, undulating chords that vibrate directly through the bedrock and up through the bones of everyone touching them. 

If you lay your palm against the basalt ten feet away, you would feel a complex, braided pulse—part gossip, part map. They sing the shifting topography of the last six hours into each other’s skeletons. A rising, staccato rumble in the sternum marks a newly collapsed canopy gap teeming with stunned bat-moths; a sharp, grating drop in pitch warns of a fresh obsidian rockfall or a dry-ash sinkhole opening to the east; a slow, rolling harmonic recalls a sweet, sulfur-free seep of hot water three ridges back. Because the landscape erases itself weekly, their maps cannot be static memories; they must be constantly rewritten and harmonized every few hours, each scout contributing the tremors of what they just climbed through until the whole band shares a single, updated three-dimensional sense of the dark around them.

Watch them eat in this brief truce. Nothing goes to waste, and everything is processed at furious speed before the smell of cracked meat draws the blind, heavy things that hunt by scent rather than sonar. Two elders hold a yard-wide scavenger crab steady while a third drives an obsidian wedge precisely into the nerve-ganglion beneath its mouthparts—one sharp *crack*—instantly killing the thrashing claws. Within four minutes, working entirely by touch in the dark, four pairs of double-thumbed hands have disarticulated every joint, cracked the asymmetrical pincers on stone anvils, scooped the hot, yellow hepatopancreas and white muscle into waiting mouths, and selected the two cleanest claw-tips to be lashed onto fresh spear shafts with wet gut salvaged from an amphibian killed an hour prior.

They do not linger to digest. They never sleep more than forty minutes at a stretch. 

Somewhere at the edge of the shelter, a juvenile whose job is to keep a bare toe wedged against the base of the whistling stalks feels the pitch wobble—a fat, eyeless grub has chewed halfway through the lashingvine, or the geothermal pressure beneath the ridge is dropping as the vent chokes on mineral scale. The juvenile taps twice against its shin-plate: *tik-tik*.

There is no groaning, no stretching, no gathering of scattered belongings. Everything not already eaten or bound to a belt is instantly abandoned—the scraped shells, the chipped flakes, the bruised anvil stone. Mothers cinch infants tight against their ribs; spears click together in practiced palms; great, ribbed ears swivel forward into the dark. 

Just as the first cut pipe blows loose from the fissure with a wet sputter and the deafening, multi-tiered roar of the basin slams inward like surf filling a breached hull, the last of them is already twelve feet up a swaying cable of ivory vine. A nearby bladder-fruit, throttled twenty minutes ago with a braided noose left deliberately behind as a parting distraction, reaches critical pressure and bursts—*THWACK*—sending a frantic hailstorm of blinded bat-moths and shrieking leapers crashing down onto the empty stone shelf they occupied five seconds before. 

High above the sudden feeding frenzy, moving sideways along the underside of a rust-colored frond tier where the warm steam collects, ten compact shadows flow smoothly into the dark, already listening half a mile ahead for the hiss of the next good vent.
