At first you are certain nothing lives here, because everything here is already busy, and busyness of this kind leaves no room for a life.

Stand in one of the wide, slick lanes between the roaring green pillars and let your ears adjust—not to the volume, which never softens, but to the lockstep. Everything in the basin runs on exact divisions of a single invisible clock: the sixty-cycle shudder of the puddles, the three-step *CLANG* of the copper-wired birds, the four-second drop-and-*clack* of the insect grids, the pneumatic *hiss-chak* of the leaves, the timed white puffs from the seal-things high on the rim. Nothing hesitates. Nothing shifts its weight. Nothing changes its mind.

That is how you catch the first sign of a resident: you hear something hesitate.

Underneath the drop-forge slam and the band-saw whine of wings comes a low, un-metered sound from the empty stone thirty paces down the nave—*shhhk… shhhk-thump…… shhhk.* A pause too long to be a cycle, followed by two quick, uneven scrapes, like a heavy boot sole pivoting on flint and reconsidering its angle. At the same moment, the basin’s phantom smell of hot cutting oil changes character. Everywhere else it hangs thin and uniform in the freezing air; drifting down this lane, it thickens into a warm, musky current tinged with dry wool, hot slate, and scorched horn, as though a stove door had just been cracked open nearby.

Look down at the glossy black stone ahead of you and you will see its footprints before you quite make out its bulk: broad, rounded patches of dull, matte-gray rock marching slowly across the wet floor. Everywhere else, the basalt gleams dark under its continuous film of cold runoff. Beneath this moving thing, the water flashes instantly into vapor, leaving dry, chalky-gray flint exposed to the pewter light for two full seconds after a foot lifts, before the surrounding sheet-flow snaps back over the razor edges with a tiny, tight *tsk*.

What stands at the head of that trail of vanishing dry spots is roughly the mass of a heavy draft pony, though built on no quadruped’s plan. It rests on three thick, columnar legs arranged in an asymmetric triad—two set wide beneath a broad fore-bulk, one heavier keel-limb centered behind—the only stance that can plant itself anywhere on unplaned, fractured stone without ever wobbling, because three points always find a flat plane even on raw knives of flint. Its soles are round, stacked pads of translucent amber horn six inches thick, layered like pressed rawhide mallets. Watch it pivot onto a particularly wicked ridge of black glass: the rock does not dull by an atom, of course, so a crisp, pale ribbon of horn pares cleanly off the creature’s sole like a wood-shaving off a plane, curls onto the nearest vibrating puddle, shudders violently in the sixty-hertz standing wave for half a second, and atomizes into a puff of dry, oil-scented haze. Higher up the leg, around a hot, swollen coronet, fresh horn is already extruding downward at a crawl to replace the thousandth of an inch just lost. Even wear, here, has learned not to accumulate.

Everything above those three horn pillars is wrapped in a heavy, drooping mantle of dense gray-brown felt, four inches thick and matted tighter than an industrial press-blanket. In a room where every surface streams cold water, this coat is bone-dry. A subtle envelope of ninety-degree air clings a quarter-inch outside the nap; cold mist settling from the cloud-lid hits that thermal skin, skids sideways like water droplets on a hot skillet, and falls clear of its flanks without ever wetting a fiber. Touch that felt—if you are brave enough to reach out when it passes—and no grease comes off on your fingers. There is still no oil in the basin; the creature’s dry hide simply radiates the fierce, clean smell of it, concentrated by body heat vented from deep, pleated crimson creases that open and close lazily beneath the eaves of its mantle, four breaths a minute, then six, then three, entirely out of time with the surrounding machinery.

Its head hangs low at the end of a short, muscular neck: long, blunt-muzzled, plated in smooth dark keratin the color of surface-ground cast iron, scarred nowhere, yet unmistakably weathered by habit—the way an anvil’s horn looks used even before it dents. And set into that iron-gray skull are two eyes that do not match, and never look at the same place at once.

The right eye sits deep beneath a heavy ridge of felt near the centerline of the face: round, wet, dark as anthracite, with a steady circular pupil that fixes directly on whatever lies ahead of it. The left eye sits high and far back toward the hinge of the jaw, bulging slightly, smoky-yellow, crossed by a wide horizontal slit angled forty degrees off its line of travel, permanently aimed into the periphery.

Watch the creature approach the nearest twenty-foot column of bottle-green foliage and you understand immediately why its skull is twisted that way.

It walks straight up to the wet leaves with its left, smoky-amber eye shut tight beneath a leathery lid, fixing the column dead-on with its dark central eye. Held in direct gaze, the hummock remains ordinary cold botany—overlapping waxy leaves dripping water, coiled ferns weeping thick beads of amber sap onto the rock seams. Stopping eight inches short of the foliage, the creature extends a long, flat, slate-gray tongue and neatly lifts three uphill-rolling beads of sap off a sharp basalt seam before they can re-enter the roots, swallowing them with a slow, throat-warming gulp. Then it shuts its dark central eye, turns its blunt head twenty degrees away from the column, and cracks open the lateral yellow one.

You cannot see what its peripheral eye sees unless you flick your own glance sideways to match it—and when you do, the green pillar next to its cheek is no longer leaves at all, but a solid emerald spindle screaming around its vertical axis at three thousand revolutions per minute, flinging horizontal sheets of water outward to vanish six inches from the blur. Right on that six-inch vanishing line, the creature parts its heavy, pleated lips. It does not drink off the shivering transformer-puddles on the stone; it catches the centrifuged mist at the exact fractional inch where water drops out of visible category, drinking from the seam between states until its crimson neck-pleats flush dark plum-red and steam gently at their edges.

Nothing is stolen from the basin’s closed ledger by this meal. Stay long enough—though time is hard to reckon under the unshifting milk-pewter lid—and you will see the same heavy walker amble back to the base of that hummock hours later, press a warm vent beneath its rear mantle against the sharp root-seam, and deposit three clear amber beads of identical volume right back onto the uphill track, while its neck-pleats exhale the borrowed water in slow, warm sighs that condense ten feet up the cliff and rejoin the sheet-flow. In a lossless circuit where nothing may be spent or saved, life is simply a warm detour: matter borrowed between stem and root, held inside a dark, unhurried belly for a while, and returned intact.

Once your eyes learn the trick of spotting dry matte gray against wet black gloss, the basin stops being empty.

Look back toward the center of the nave, where a rigid three-dimensional block of five hundred slate-gray insects screams like a band saw, dropping three feet instantaneously without passing through the air between. You looked right through that three-foot gap ten minutes ago and saw only the far cliff wall. Look harder at the empty horizontal slot directly beneath the lowest layer of hovering wings. Wedged flat on its side in midair, legs tucked tight beneath a charcoal-dark felt mantle, a second creature is fast asleep—floating effortlessly inside the skipped dead-stroke of the swarm. Every few minutes, the entire five-hundred-insect grid vanishes from above its spine and *clacks* into place three feet lower, just underneath its ribs, hovers four seconds while the sleeper rises half an inch on a slow breath, and snaps back overhead without brushing a single dry hair. The insects do not avoid it; rather, it has learned that the machine’s skipped interval is as buoyant and quiet as a featherbed, suspended three feet clear of the wet knives below.

And over near the seventh green pillar, what you first mistook for an irregular hump of unfractured stone sitting seven inches outside the snapping leaves is a third—smaller, rounder, pulled completely inside a pale pewter-colored mantle like a boulder under dry moss. Every few seconds, as the broadleaves compress the cold air—*hiss-chak*—and throw a pale blue spot-welder’s arc toward the cliff, the bolt does not jump straight to the basalt. It strikes a stiff, mineral-crusted ridge along this hunched creature’s shoulder, runs harmlessly across the dry insulating nap in a silent web of violet static, and leaps off its rump to the rock wall with the usual sharp crack. A tenth of a second before every discharge, a pair of thick, smoky-amber secondary eyelids—dark as welder’s glass—flick down across both its eyes, then slide back up as it shifts its shoulders slightly, soaking up stray inductive warmth from twenty thousand volts the way a mill-cat sleeps against a boiler pipe.

Count them carefully up and down the cathedral-narrow room. Walk every clear lane from end wall to end wall. Look along the upper ledges beside the swiveling, steam-venting seals.

There are no others. Not hidden in caves—there are no caves. Not foraging outside—above the cloud-lid is dead mountain silence and bare scree where nothing like this has ever walked. Because no young are born here and no corpses rot, there is no herd, no breeding stock, no lineage. Species and population are the same thing: there are simply *these three*, fixed in count since the mountain split, each slightly shaped by an eternity of private preference. The broad, warm pacer in the lanes; the narrow, dark-mantled sleeper in the insect gap; the compact arc-basker by the seventh pillar.

Watch them long enough and you realize survival takes almost none of their time. Grazing three beads of sap and a mouthful of vanished mist takes two minutes; horn grows back on its own; nothing hunts them; the temperature never drops or climbs. That leaves all the rest of forever inside a sixty-foot-wide stone stamping plant where not a pebble can be pried loose, not a stick snapped, not a mark scratched into rock.

So watch what happens when the broad pacer stops in the middle of a wide, bare lane, settles smoothly onto its three horn pads, and parts the front of its heavy felt mantle.

Tucked up inside the dry, ninety-degree pocket against its chest—kept clean of mist and cold like precision instruments in a lined case—are two long, startlingly slender forelimbs that never touch the ground for walking. They unfold on three supple joints, covered not in felt or horn, but in smooth, matte-black skin like fine dry chamois. Each hand ends in seven flat, spatulate fingers graded in thickness like a machinist’s set of feeler gauges: a stiff, wedge-tipped thumb at one edge, tapering across the palm down to a seventh finger no thicker than cigarette paper and translucent at the tip.

Leaning low over a broad, flat puddle that shudders in crisp sixty-hertz squares, the creature lowers both hands until nine or ten of those graduated fingertips barely kiss the surface tension of the water—not touching the razor stone beneath, only the vibrating film.

Instantly, between its hands, the uniform square grid of the puddle fractures into a breathtaking interference architecture. By damping this node and splitting that crest to the thousandth of an inch, it drives the sixty-cycle mist upward into standing liquid vaults two feet high—nested polyhedra, counter-rotating fluted columns, gossamer trusses of cold spray held motionless in the air by balanced phase alone. Look directly at the puddle between its wrists and you see an intricate fountain of geometric mist humming like a choir of transformers. Let your gaze slip twenty degrees to the left—catching the spray in your peripheral sight, just as you caught the spinning plants—and the mist locks into apparent solidity: miniature, pewter-colored rooms with arched roofs, bridges spanning dry chasms, spiral gears meshing at impossible angles, whole quiet landscapes of smooth, rounded contours that exist nowhere in this razor-edged basin.

Sometimes the narrow, dark-mantled one drops down from its insect cradle—landing on all three horn soles with a muffled *thud* and three fresh curls of shaved amber—and comes to watch. Before it reaches its companion, it detours deliberately into the concentric rings of copper-plumed birds marching around the fourth pillar. It plants one broad horn foot directly in the path of the inner ring, and a second foot half a stride offset in the outer ring.

The squat, wire-feathered birds do not falter, scatter, or lose a single millisecond of beat; one after another they simply wink out against the front of its horn pad and reappear in mid-stride on the far side. Yet because skipping the eight-inch width of a foot advances each bird’s *position* along the ring while leaving its clockwork tempo untouched, the spatial phase of their unison *CLANG* shifts around the circle—turning the ring of striking bills into a steered acoustic array. Forty feet away, right where the broad pacer bends over its puddle-sculpture, two opposing sound waves cancel cleanly out.

Step into the invisible cone cast behind those two planted feet and the deafening drop-forge roar drops by thirty decibels, hollowed out into a sudden, startling pocket of library-quiet air where you can hear cold water trickling into hairline cracks and the dry, papery rustle of felt.

Inside that steered wedge of quiet, the broad one holds its liquid architecture steady for another minute so the dark-mantled one can tilt its iron-gray head sideways and study the smooth, rounded phantom hills in its yellow peripheral eye. Then the broad one lifts its fingers a fraction of an inch.

*Snap.* In a single sixtieth of a second, the vaults, bridges, and smooth hills collapse without a splash; the puddle is once again a blank sheet of square waves; the dark-mantled walker lifts its feet from the bird-ring, letting the deafening *CLANG* slam back to full volume; and both of them tuck their chamois hands back into the warm folds over their chests. Nothing has been built. Nothing remains on the floor. Every contour they have ever invented sits solely behind those heavy, iron-plated brows.

When they stand together afterward for an hour or two before parting, they press the flat slate plates of their foreheads gently together, shut all four of their mismatched eyes, and let their crimson neck-pleats flare in close proximity, mingling their thin halos of dry air into a single warm pocket. If you stand very near them while they rest like that, you can hear what they are listening for inside the narrow acoustic shadow of their own thick coats: the slow, wet, gloriously irregular *lub-dup…… lub-dup… lub-dup* of two hearts that do not beat in unison, and never keep sixty-hertz time.

And if you stay still long enough, sooner or later one of them will open only its dark, round, forward-facing eye—holding you carefully still in direct sight so you do not blur or spin—and come over to examine you.

It will not teleport past your ankle like the copper birds, nor ignore you like the bellows-throated amphibians. It will walk up the slick black lane at its own hesitant, three-legged gait—*shhhk… shhhk-thump*—leaving two-second ovals of dry gray stone behind it, and stop a pace away so its warm, oil-scented thermal skin brushes the cold mist off your knuckles. When a line of marching rails approaches between you, it will step sideways onto a fresh flinty edge, sacrificing another thin curl of amber horn just to give you comfortable room. Then, unfolding one long, dark-chamois arm from beneath its dry felt hood, it will reach out with its seventh, paper-thin fingertip and touch—with infinite, calibrated lightness—the split rubber edge of your boot sole, and the frayed cuff of your soaked trousers, studying with patient, single-eyed wonder a thing that comes into the room already wearing out, losing pieces of itself onto the stone, and carrying no way to put them back.
