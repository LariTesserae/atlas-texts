I would not be out on the leather-basins with the twenty-year-olds, running alongside six thousand stamping hooves with a harpoon and a gourd of crushed-bug panic. My knees wouldn't have the loose, careless spring for it, and I don't think I would ever quite trust my own timing against a boiling surge of pepper-latex.

You would find me four miles east of an active chimney-camp, down on one knee in a dry, chaff-drifted gully between two old swells of callus-wood, with one cork sandal kicked off beside me and my bare left heel pressed hard into a pale, exposed seam of heartwood.

I am a scouting gall-wright—or rather, the person they send ahead of the wrights by six months, to read the sleep of the sister-camps and keep the circuit’s rooms from closing their throats while the clan is elsewhere. Call me a *pare-hand*, or a *seam-listener*. Someone in their late forties whose inner ears gave up the air thirty years ago, so that the high, tearing surf-hiss of the cicadas is just the color of the daylight to me now—something bright and flat that vanishes the moment I close my eyes and put my jaw to my stave.

Right now, I am alone in the white-gold afternoon, though nobody on this floor is ever really out of reach if they stay on the right grain. My stave is planted three inches from my bare toes, wedged tight into a check-crack in the dense wood, its spatulate top tucked under my right cheekbone while my hands stay free. Through my jawbone comes the huge, dirty, endless cathedral-organ of the plateau: the three-note saw of a borer gallery forty feet down and half a mile south; the slow, creaking *thump-slush* of a pressurized sap-vein shifting load as the sun heats the dark leaf-hide to the west; and beneath all of it, faint as a pulse in a wrist, the tiny, crisp *tink-tink, pause, tink* of a copper thumb-ring back at the chimney, where someone is arguing over the tension on a dawn-water tripod without realizing their stick is planted on the same four-mile heartwood rib I’m currently kneeling on. I am eavesdropping on domestic complaints from an hour's walk away while scraping white tallow-paste out of the corner of my eye.

My work is slow, fussy, and smells strongly of vinegar and crushed wasp.

Half a day’s glide ahead of me sits *Three-Split*, one of our clan’s sleeping camps, clustered under a spire that blew its rust-cloud nine summers back and has been cooling ever since. Nobody has slept there in fourteen months. Left entirely alone for two full years, a ring-barked gall doesn’t come back to life—dead wood stays dead—but the living collar around its root never stops trying to swallow the foreign lump. Grey cork rolls up the outer walls like thick batter; pale, wet bolsters of scar-tissue squeeze inward through the dog-leg crawlway until a grown adult can’t wriggle their shoulders past the second turn; and worst of all, sometimes a blind feeder-root from an adjacent vascular basin will punch straight up through the dead floor-plate looking for the damp shade inside, filling a perfectly good pith-hung room with a coiled, throbbing knuckle of hot bark that ruins the acoustics and brings the roar right back inside.

So twice a year, two or three of us walk the silvery, foot-polished groove out from the main camp with heavy packs of tools and cured meat, to spend ten days keeping the empty houses hollow.

My companion on this circuit—a broad-backed cousin six years younger than me, who walks three hundred paces ahead because she likes the unbroken glare and I like to stop and feel old amber scabs with my thumbs—has just paused on the crest of a burl ridge. She doesn’t turn around to wave. She just plants her stave, leans her chin into the cup, and raps four dry, lazy knuckles against the shaft.

*Water-lip,* hits my teeth through my own stick. *Broken. Glider-beak or hoof. Dry crust.*

I tap back twice with my horn-ring—*Coming, hold*—strap my rocker-sole back around my ankle, and glide up the slope to join her, letting my hips roll with the buzz of the wood underfoot.

There is a particular pleasure in the way my body belongs to this kit. Across my chest hang six little stoppered tubes of hollowed grazer-shin, each holding a different grade of lie: mild cicada-spit paste for coaxing thin, flexible lip-wood around a water-funnel; fierce, black, fermented borer-gall tar that makes the floor panic and throw up an inch of cross-grained armor in three weeks; and a small, precious bladder of alkaline shell-ash grease to put a wound back to sleep when it gets too ambitious. Slung at my hip, wrapped in oiled stomach-skin so the morning fogs never pit its edge, is a four-inch adze blade of cold-hammered red copper—named *Little-Tongue*, worked down from a wider blade my mother’s brother traded three whole winter-skins of tallow for at the south rim before I was born. Every time I sharpen it against old, silica-rough bark, a microscopic smudge of red stays behind on the plateau. I try not to sharpen it often.

The bone tripods have been tipped sideways by a fresh swell of silvered cork, and the wind-harps high up the chimney’s flank have snapped two strings, so a pair of juvenile bone-jawed gliders are hunched on the notched ancestor-cages three hundred feet overhead, clacking their beaks down at us. We ignore them until morning; they won't drop into the lee-shadow while we're moving.

First comes the unsealing. We prize open the heavy laminated cork plug of the biggest gall—my favorite, an old, thick-walled dome raised forty years ago over a particularly clean seam—and I have to lie flat on my belly in the warm, papery drifts of shed epidermis to chisel back two fat, pale lips of new callus that have pinched the outer crawlway down to the width of a child's ribs. Little-Tongue bites into the wet, living margin with soft, juicy *chocks* I feel in my wrist rather than hear. A sharp, green whiff of turpentine and crushed radish puffs into my face-mask; clear, lukewarm lymph beads along the cut before I rub gray shell-ash into the raw meat to tell the plateau: *nothing here, go back to sleep, just wind.*

Then we wriggle through the double turn, pull the plug tight behind us, and drop into the sudden, staggering dark of the dead dome.

Even after forty years, that first ten minutes inside an unopened gall is the closest thing I know to drunkenness. Out on the bark, you keep your shoulders slightly hunched without knowing it, bracing your ribs against the hundred-decibel push of the world. Inside, the air has no weight at all. My ears scream with phantom static—high, whistling ghosts of insects that aren't there—while my bare soles, freed of both cork sandals, touch the hanging rope-stirrups of a dry sinew hammock and feel *nothing*. No saw-toothed bass. No hooves. Just the slow, thick thump of my own animal heart knocking against my ribs at sixty beats a minute, shockingly fast compared to the huge, hot, hydraulic creature outside that takes from dawn till noon just to finish one breath.

My cousin lights a thumb-sized dish of tallow from the smoldering char-nut in her tinder-horn. Golden light swells across curved, adze-dimpled walls that smell faintly of old dry pith, rancid oil, and the sweet, dusty tea-scent of our own people from two winters back. Nothing has breached the floor-plate. The amber-eyes in the roof are clouded over on the outside with wind-wax, dark against the dusk, waiting to be scraped clear tomorrow.

Before we eat our dried grazer-strips, my cousin reaches across the tiny lamp and lays four tea-stained fingers flat against my collarbone. I rest my palm against the warm hollow of her wrist.

In the quiet dark, we don't bother with crisp stave-grammar or quick knuckle-clicks. She hums low in her chest, a warm, tired vibration that travels down her arm into my sternum, tapping two slow fingers against my bone: *Good room. Still dead. Three days' paring outside, then we climb the spire and mend the grandmother-cages.*

I hum back a third lower, letting my thumb press twice into the tendon of her wrist—*Four days. That east tap-vein has shifted; I felt it sloshing three miles out. We'll have to bore a new hole before dawn-sweat.*

She laughs—I don't hear it, of course, not even two feet away, except as a tiny puff of warm, tallow-scented breath across my knuckles, while under my palm her radius bone jumps with three quick, silent chuckles.

Tomorrow before first light, while the stars are still hard and white and the great cistern-valves out in the basin begin their slow, hydraulic creaking, we will wrap our aprons tight, smear fresh latex-paste around our eyes, and step back out into the warm, amber-puddled fog and the rising wall of roar. I will climb two hundred notches up the dead, ringing chimney with new gut-strings between my teeth, stop beside a weathered wicker cage where an uncle’s shinbones have bleached to the exact silver-grey of ten-thousand-year-old wood, and press my cheek to the spire's grain—checking how far off the six-legged herds are grazing today, listening with my skull to a continent that wants very badly, with no malice at all, to heal right over our footprints, while my hand goes down to the little red blade at my hip that keeps reminding it not yet.
