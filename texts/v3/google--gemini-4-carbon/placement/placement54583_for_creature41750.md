I would not be the water, which is too vast and clean and holds everything without touching it in return, and I would not be the sixty-hertz hum, which passes through the stone without staying. 

I think I would find myself down along the tangent line where the middle power cable presses its black bituminous serving into the white marl—specifically, I am the calcareous, corrugated collar itself. Not the active, reaching tip of the hairline thread going out after the fallen flake of scale, which has too much errand in it, too much appetite. Rather, the part just behind the living margin: the dense, ribbed archive of stone and grease that has already hardened into shape between the steel armor wire and the silt.

To be that low frill of calcified crust is to exist entirely as an interface—a translation layer between a blind, distant human rhythm and the patient chemistry of a sealed room.

From the inside (though there is no real inside, only older and newer laminations), there is no sight of the pewter ceiling forty feet overhead, no view across the nave to the wedged boulders. There is only the continuous, sixty-cycle shudder coming off the galvanized wire on one flank, and the cold, motionless carbonate flour on the other. The vibration isn't heard as a pitch; it is felt as architecture. There are places four millimeters apart where a calcium ion can settle and stay, and places two millimeters over where the microscopic pressure peak nudges it aside before the crystal lattice can lock. You don't decide to build parallel razor-fine ribs; you simply allow what is restless to move on and keep what falls into the quiet nodes.

What draws me to this specific seam—more than the galvanic bucket-brigades hanging beneath the iron manifold, or the opalescent scavenging veins climbing the vertical karst—is that the collar *keeps the ledger* without knowing what it is recording.

Miles away, beyond the solid limestone walls and the muffled rotary turbines, people turn on electric furnaces in a cold snap, or shut down a stamping plant for three days in November, or reroute load during an ice storm under a sky I will never see. Down here in the constant forty-eight-degree greenish candor, none of the urgency arrives. Only the tenth-of-a-degree thickening of the fungal dark band when the bitumen softens by an unmeasurable fraction; only the clear, glassy seam of pure calcite laid down over a quiet weekend when the steel cools back toward the aquifer’s baseline. 

All that distant, frantic, purposeful human expenditure—shifts changing, switches thrown, invoices paid, machinery straining—gets stripped of its noise and its hurry as it travels down the catenary, until by the time it reaches the floor of the sinkhole, it is reduced to microns of alternating pale stone and dark hydrocarbon-fed hyphae. A stromatolite of other creatures' weather.

And yet I wouldn't be *dead* stone, not quite. Because along my outermost thousandth of an inch—the translucent grey mucilage rim no thicker than window frost—the exchange is still happening. When that ruled white thread creeps out across the marl toward the fallen crumb of bruised zinc and raw limestone twenty-four inches away, it anchors its base in one of my quarter-inch ribs. Four months later, when the thread reaches the crater and closes the tiny chemical circuit, a faint trickle of fresh metallic ions and scavenged electrons comes back up the line, passing right over my oldest seams on its way toward the wall-veins and the stiff, farmed moss thirty feet up in the cracks.

I am the sill they step across. 

Nothing in the chamber has a memory, because memory requires a nervous system that compares *then* to *now*, and oxygen here is far too scarce to waste on nostalgia. But if you are forty years of banded calcite wedged against a warm cable in water that never stirs, *then* is physically stacked beneath *now*, intact down to the first week the heavy trunks were dropped over the northern lip and settled into the silt. That initial bloom of disturbed marl from forty years ago—the last cloudy afternoon this basin ever had—is still right there at my base, cemented into my very first layer, holding everything built since in exact alignment.

It is a remarkably peaceful way to be: utterly deaf to the meaning of the signal, completely faithful to its shape, holding the cable steady in the flour while the slow trades of phosphorus and sugar pass quietly overhead in the green light.
