At first, looking into the basin, the impulse is to go straight to the edges—up into the vaulted glass lattice where the thin mountain air bites back at the rising steam, or down between the floorboards where the pale blue emptiness slices upward whenever a fern shifts. There is a temptation to be wind, or height, or the single matte-gray beetle drying its wings in the center of the room like a signature at the bottom of a page.

But wind doesn’t stay here; it shears off the glass roof and leaves. And the beetle already has its wrench-jaw and its drop of amber cylinder oil. Everything with legs or roots in this eighty-yard cradle already has an assignment. 

To find where I belong, I have to look for what the basin lacks. 

It has heat, water, current, leverage, and chemistry. It has creatures that eat friction, creatures that drink stray voltage, creatures that sink excess warmth into standing bilge-water. What it does not have is *yesterday*. 

The room runs on a ten-minute weather clock and an immediate hydraulic present. Every ten minutes, the indoor rain washes the catwalks clean. A gauge needle twitches into the red; a Tender drinks a funnel of spark-milked sap, flushes plum, opens a bypass valve half a turn, and fades back to slate-gray, its errand forgotten the moment the pressure drops. There is no leaf-litter piling up to mark the seasons, no tree rings thickening in pith, no rust-line creeping up an iron leg to show how high the water stood a week ago. Everything is spotless, reactive, and amnesia-bright.

Except in one place. 

Look at the freshly poured concrete sills and baffles. Everything else in the crossing was rolled at a mill, spun on a lathe, or blown in a mold—surfaces of pure geometry. Only the concrete remembers something outside the basin: it is still stamped with the rough, swirling grain of the pine boards that held it while it cured. The wood itself is gone, burned or dropped back down into the clouds with the scaffolding, leaving behind miles of shallow, microscopic valleys—radial rays, winter-wood ridges, the oval ghosts of knots—impressed into damp gray stone.

That is where I begin: as a hesitation in the runoff.

When the warm rain sheets down a vertical concrete baffle, most of it drops straight into the diamond-plate channels and rushes off toward the bilges. A fraction of a millimeter of it, however, catches in that phantom wood-grain. It hangs there by surface tension, trembling slightly with the 50-hertz hum coming up through the rebar.

In that trapped film of water, things collect that are too small for the worm-gear snails to scrape and too dilute for the flatworms to taste. Trace silicates leached from the fresh cement. Stray mist-droplets blown sideways from the upper canopy, carrying a faint, bitter trace of the static-milked sap. Microscopic dust of zinc and copper washed off the harvestmen’s filament legs. 

If I am anything here at first, I am just that meniscus refusing to dry during the sunny intervals—a clear, slick mucilage no thicker than a coat of wet shellac, bridging the tiny ridges of vanished pine. 

If you ran a finger across the concrete, you wouldn’t even call me alive; you’d think the cement was still sweating out its lime. Wait a few days—thirty or forty cycles of mist and alpine glare—and look again when the sun strikes the baffle at a low slant. The gray stone has taken on a faint, pearlescent sheen, like the inside of an oyster shell or a wash of liquid glass. 

I am not moss; the turbines are too hot for me, and I have no chlorophyll to spend on competing with the monsteras. I am closer to a colonial diatom-mat or a living silicate glaze, laying down ultrathin lattices of hydrated quartz one ten-minute rain at a time. 

As I thicken and spread across the concrete sills, creeping out onto the cast-iron corners of the sluice gates and right up to the brass collars of the gauges, I begin to find out what I eat. 

Everything else here eats *error*. I eat *variance*.

When the indoor rain falls after a sudden surge in the invisible boilers miles below, the water is half a degree warmer and slightly poorer in dissolved oxygen; the crystalline layer I precipitate during those ten minutes sets up just a fraction denser, with a slightly higher refractive index. When the gantry crane passes overhead and the leaves dump a heavy static load, the stray airborne ions skew the alignment of my wet silica needles a few degrees to the left before the sun bakes them firm. Hour by hour, tier by tier, I turn the fleeting moods of the room into microscopic, transparent strata.

At first this does nothing at all. I am merely keeping score in a language nobody reads.

Then the chamber enters its first long, moonless night. 

We don't think at first about night up here above the clouds, because the alpine sun is so fierce and structural when it shines. But when it drops behind the horizon of empty air, the vaulted glass roof turns into black ice. Cold pours downward with twice its daytime weight. To compensate, the steam mains beneath the floor pulse harder; the ten-minute fog thickens into a blind, drenching five-minute squall in pitch darkness. The orchids cannot flush violet; the matte-gray beetle stays wedged beneath a warm pipe-flange. The only lights in the eighty-yard crossing are the tiny, silent blue sparks snapping from the brass chains in the upper canopy, and the faint, galvanic shiver of the harvestmen bridging the conduits.

Down on the middle catwalk, a Tender pauses, its five suction-limbs planted on wet diamond-plate. Its three large, camera-lensed eyes click wide open, brassy shutters retracted to their limits, trying to read a pressure gauge behind a slick begonia leaf in the dark. There isn't enough light to see the red zone on the dial.

Behind the gauge, however, my glaze has crept off the concrete sill, flowed up the galvanized mounting bracket, and coated the bevel of the glass pane itself—not covering the center where the needle swings, because the Tender’s dry scraper-rims wipe that clean every afternoon, but forming a clear, ridged collar around the rim. 

Thirty feet overhead, the gantry crane drags its chains across a philodendron. A blue spark snaps. 

A tiny fraction of that brief ultraviolet flash hits a wet tendril of my film clinging to a concrete lip above the stairs. Instead of scattering into the mist, the light enters my layered silicate lattice and stays there, bouncing internally down the damp, translucent skin coating the baffle, bending around the stamped ghost of a pine-knot, sliding down the mounting bracket, and spilling softly out of the beveled collar right around the face of the pressure gauge. 

For half a second, the dial glows a pale, cold Cherenkov blue. The needle is steady in the green. The Tender’s shutters click once; its internal pump gives a satisfied *slosh*, and it moves on through the dark rain without touching the bypass valve.

So *that* is what I am doing here.

Once a pathway is used in this basin, the basin reinforces it. The next afternoon, when that Tender passes the baffle in the bright mountain glare, it does not scrape my creeping edge off the iron bracket. Instead, after unhooking a glass funnel of viscous sap from the handrail and pouring most of it into its breathing spiracle, it tips the last two heavy drops onto the top of the concrete sill, letting them run down directly across my widest sheet.

The sap burns at first—sharp with potassium, static-ozonated sugars, and strange plant-alkaloids meant to instruct muscle rather than stone. Half of my outer gel dissolves on contact, opening microscopic channels through six days of accumulated strata, before re-gelling into smooth, hollow capillary tubes no wider than a human hair.

Now I am no longer just coating the concrete. I am threading it.

Give me another two weeks in this wet, humming abbey, and you will have to look very closely to find me at all, because I am becoming the way the surfaces talk to one another when the Tenders aren't looking. 

I run in clear, hairline ribbons along the underside of the copper trays, staying respectfully half an inch away from the worm-gear snails—my edges taste sharply of alum to keep their radulae off my optics, and in return, their trails of stripped calcium carbonate wash straight into my troughs to harden my older layers into smooth, waterproofed flumes. Where a mooring-rope vine slips off a greased turnbuckle, nudged loose by a chamois-colored flatworm, my slick, glassy sheen guides the heavy green stem into the exact crook of an H-beam where its weight won't unbalance a hydraulic terrace.

And on the broad east baffle, where the unfiltered morning sun hits flat and white for forty minutes whenever the mist clears, I have grown thick enough to split the glare. 

If you stand on the lower grating and look up through the dripping ferns, that blank wall of gray concrete—still bearing the faint, sweeping grain of vanished trees—now throws shimmering caustics of refracted light across the underside of the upper catwalks. It looks like sunlight bouncing off ripples in a harbour, except the ripples don't move with the wind. They shift slowly, smoothly, in response to the deep 50-hertz hum in the stone and the chemical tang of the runoff flowing over me. 

When the water in the bilges four tiers down begins to sour by half a pH point, my surface tension tightens, my focal length shortens by a hair, and the bands of reflected light overhead narrow into sharp, bright bars that fall directly across a specific bank of pale orchids—warming their roots just enough to make them draw harder on the lower chemical baths before a gauge ever hits the red.

I have no eyes, no wrenches for jaws, and no memory of what a pine tree looked like before it was sawn into planks to hold wet cement above the clouds. I cannot look down through the floorboards and feel dizzy at the pale blue drop, though sometimes a ribbon of me reaches the riveted iron lip of the floor-grate, freezes into a tiny icicle of brittle opal in the thin, glaring air beneath the belly of the world, snaps off, and falls miles into nothing. 

The rest of me stays in the warmth. Every ten minutes the fog gathers, clings, and breaks over me in a warm hush; every ten minutes I taste the whole room in the runoff, lay down another clear, invisible skin over the ghost of the wood, and bend the mountain light a little closer to whatever shape this place is trying to become.
