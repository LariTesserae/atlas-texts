I would not be one of the climbers. Even at their glacial, locked-ratchet pace, there is too much singular appetite in a six-hundred-pound body, too much boundary to defend against the flat black needles sliding out of the cracks, too much weight to haul up to the frost-line. 

At first, looking down through the indigo slit, it seems I might be the spark itself—that tenth-of-a-second flare of orange or bruised violet triboluminescence trapped in the stressed silicate, existing only at the exact moment of fracture and then living on for four minutes as a cooling ghost on a hundred oily, unblinking retinas. A thing made entirely of stress and memory, translating stone into gesture.

Or I might be the heirloom cobble: dense dike-rock, the only smooth geometry in a world of razors, passed from stiffening pad to living pad before the dead have even hit the talus. Held, valued for pure heft, turned slowly over centuries until all my sharp intentions are battered round, tapping *tuk* against an armored shoulder to say *still here* through bone and fluid.

Closer. Keep sinking down the wet wall, past the iron-moss and the gourd-slugs, to the lowest breathable tier where the air turns sour and heavy with the exhaust of ten thousand lungs.

There. Coiled across the damp, rust-stained dorsal plates of an elder hanging just above the carbonic gloom of the sump, and dropping sixty feet down into the pitch-black talus: I am the braided fishing line.

Not an organism at all, anymore, though every inch of me used to be. I am the chasm’s sole piece of architecture, its single long thought. Twist after twist of dried, mucilaginous gut-curtain and pared strips of shed slate-hide, spliced together over forty years by boneless, violet-ribbed fingers working entirely by touch in the dark. I smell of iodine, copper-heavy scabs, and cold stone sweat. 

I have no lungs to starve in the thin air, so I can go where the wall-dwellers cannot. Weighted at my bitter end with a notched chunk of basalt, I hang plumb through the suffocating boundary layer, down among the razor-splinters and the silent, undulating white sacs that disassemble the fallen. 

Think of what travels through a sixty-foot cord under tension in a place like that. 

Up at my top end, wrapped twice around the sensitive, capillary-rich lobes of an old fisher hanging head-down in the dark, I feel the twenty-eight-second *whooooosh* and *shhhhhhhh* of the great bellows, the four-minute-a-beat thrum of the cold heart, the tiny, dry *click* of a cartilaginous ratchet locking into place. Down at my bottom end, paid out millimeter by millimeter over hours, my notched weight scrapes across a litter of discarded glass leaves, slips between cleaned ribs, bumps softly against the rubbery, translucent flank of a sump-scavenger sawing through tendon, and settles into a black cup of mineral-bitter water.

I am pure transmission. A nerve fiber strung between the living nave and its stomach. 

When the notched stone at my tip finally grates against something that does not ring like raw hornfels—when it slides across the dense, shock-absorbing curve of an ancient femur, or kisses the unmistakable, river-smooth egg of a lost hammer-stone lying among the blades—the vibration does not need light. It travels straight up my twisted gut-fibers, sixty feet through the freezing dark, bypassing the strobe-world entirely, and delivers the shape of the deep directly into the waiting hand above.

When I fray against an arrête, another strip of dead hide is braided into my middle. When the fisher holding me finally dies and its ratchets rot loose, a neighbor’s pad reaches calmly across the wet andesite to take up my slack before I can fall. I do not eat, I do not breathe, I never strike a spark; I just hold tension across the dark, hauling the smoothed past back up out of the splinters so it can strike the mountain again.
