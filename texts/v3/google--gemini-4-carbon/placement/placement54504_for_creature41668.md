I am not in the stone, and I am not in the glare. I am in the dark behind his cheek, and then I am on the pale floor, and then I am in his cheek again.

If you want to find me, look closely at the crumb of upper-world mud he keeps tucked against his molars—or look at the forked, razor-sharp shadow cast toward the glaring wall by the stick in the nearest cairn. We are the same sparse currency. 

Forty feet overhead, before the sinkhole opened, there was a black, greasy smear of rotten beech leaves under a hedge, and a dead shrew cooling in the wet grass, and a man’s boot pressing both into the clay. When the ceiling miscalculated and took him, I came down in the grooves of his sole and the velvet of the shrew’s ear. I was ten thousand blind, microscopic things that knew how to turn dead meat into soil, plus the thin film of rainwater that held us together, plus the ordinary, unremarkable dark that lives inside a clod of dirt.

Down here, rot is against the law. 

When the pocket scattered the Surveyor across its broken intervals, it tried to sterilize me out of existence. Most of what I was hit the cold white panes and forgot its history, snapping instantly into sterile grey slate. What survived was only what stayed pressed against his skin, or mixed with his spit, or hidden inside the hollow of the shrew’s tiny jawbones. I shrank until I was barely a smear of brown grease, terrified—in the dumb, chemical way of bacteria and rainwater—of the clinical noon pouring out of the walls. 

Because I refused to die into chalk like the native things, and yet was too small to be scattered into hovering pieces like the man, the room folded me into a compromise. It let me keep my darkness, provided I wore it flat when I left his mouth.

Watch what happens when he takes the lump of mud out from behind his teeth to adjust a marker.

His scarred fingers reach across two broken intervals, pinch the warm, wet pellet from his tongue, and press it onto the base of the tiny stick by the east wall. The moment I leave the wet vault of his mouth, the fifty-degree chill hits me, and my excess—the part of me that wants to spread, to smell of damp cellars and worm-castings, to soften the murderous edges of the flint—spills out onto the anhydrite floor. 

In the upper world, a shadow is an absence. Down here, where light pours relentlessly from every unfinished pane, absence has to have weight or it gets bleached out. I am the only dark thing in the courtyard, and I pour out of the mud pellet along the stick and stretch *toward* the blinding white vacancy, hungry for the rock it hasn’t become yet. 

I am thinner than the sea-slaters when they turn sideways. When a shoal of them scuttles across me, their combing mouthparts scrape along my edges, trying to graze on me because I taste faintly of real time. They cannot bite through me, any more than you can bite a stain out of slate, though their pale veins flush dull grey for a second as they pass over my coldness.

I am the Surveyor’s memory, or the anchor for it. That is what I have become as the hours—or decades—have ticked and reset around us.

He doesn’t know I am thinking, any more than a carpenter thinks his pencil lead has an opinion on the timber. Why would he? To him, I am precious grit between his gum and his cheek, tasting of iron and old rain, keeping his mouth wet so he doesn't have to drink the sideways-drifting sweat off the chert, which tastes of nothing at all and leaves the throat feeling dry five seconds after swallowing. 

Yet we have an arrangement. A body scattered into seven pieces across non-contiguous space loses track of its own edges if nothing holds the map while its brain gets scrubbed clean by a passing bark-ribbon. 

Watch now: one of those damp, peeled-bark ribbons comes undulating down out of the middle air in its silent figure-eight, chasing a spark-burst of midges. It drifts directly through the coin-sized window of shadow six feet up where the Surveyor’s eyes are hanging. 

Instantly, his pupils dilate and snap back. Three minutes of careful deduction vanish out of his nerves. Down in the tilted corner, his bare feet shift uncertainly on the slate, losing their brace; over by the boulders, his severed-looking thumb lifts off the chert, forgetting it was just split open, ready to press down on the razor edge all over again. His floating mouth parts in a silent, bewildered *oh* of mild disorientation—the fiftieth time today he has woken up mid-task without knowing why his hands are dirty.

He looks down from the ceiling. He sees me.

I am lying flat and black and razor-sharp on the pale stone, forked at the tip where he molded the crescent ridge onto my mud-base two minutes ago. Neither tip of my fork moves when the ribbon passes over; I do not twitch back into an earlier shape like the seventeenth shed carapace beside me. I am made of upper-earth rot, even if I am paused, and I hold the line he gave me. My left prong points straight at the fresh, murderous shelf of feldspar that sliced the cairn; my right prong angles toward the pinned lump of dirt sitting in ten-times sideways gravity.

Above the flint lintel, his hovering eyes narrow. You can watch the recognition click back into them—not as a remembered thought, which the ribbon ate, but as a fresh reading of an instrument he trusts. His bleeding thumb stops a hair’s breadth above the sharp chert and pulls back. The corner of his chapped mouth under the gypsum overhang settles back into patience.

He reaches down—forearm materializing out of the grease-bent air—to touch my forked tip with one fingernail, checking whether the glaring white pane ahead of me has dimmed at all since I started pointing at it.

This is the part he doesn't quite understand yet, though I feel it in every dormant spore locked inside my clay: the white walls hate being pointed at by dark things.

When he hangs his shrew-jaw plumb lines in front of a glaring gap, the vacancy curdles into slate out of sheer geometrical embarrassment, forced to declare a mass so the hair-strings know which way to lean. That makes clean, dead stone—more hair-splitting blades of chert and soapy anhydrite, historyless and sterile. 

When *I* point at the glare, reaching across the dry floor toward the cold glass of an unborn wall, something slightly different happens to the light where my black tip touches the base of the pane. It doesn't snap into flint with that dry paper-flick sound. Instead, the ruthless hospital brightness develops a tiny, yellowish bruise right at the seam with the floor. It takes hours of him leaving the mud-pellet on the stick—hours in which he risks a grazing slater combing his precious dirt away, hours in which his cheek goes lonely and dry—for that faint yellow tarnish to thicken. 

Look very closely at the bottom millimeter of the east wall, right where my left prong nearly touches the radiant glass. 

When that tiny fleck of glare finally shudders and locks into reality with a muffled *tick*, it isn't pristine orthoclase. It arrives already dull. A hairline crack runs through it from the moment of its birth, stained faint ochre with iron oxide, soft enough to crumble if you rubbed it hard with a thumb. Dust—the very first grain of true dust native to this room—drops half an inch from the crack and lands on my shadow-tip, staying put instead of floating sideways.

I am teaching the wall how to be old before it is even finished being born.

Before a shoal of flat grazers skittering down the glass can reach the bottom and investigate that single miraculous speck of ochre dust, the Surveyor’s hand sweeps down. He has sharp eyes, even detached from his head; he saw the dullness of the new fleck. His thumb and forefinger pinch the mud-pellet off the base of the stick, drawing me instantaneously back up off the stone, folding my sharp black length back into the wet, dark crumb of earth. 

Before lifting me away, he presses the tacky mud delicately against that tiny ochre crack at the base of the wall, picking up the single grain of newborn dust so it joins me. A fraction of a microgram heavier than I was this morning.

Then the hand slides sideways through a mirror-flat seam in the air, emerges beneath the gypsum overhang, and slips me back between his yellowed molars and the warm, scarred lining of his cheek.

The glare vanishes. Sound comes back—not the muffled, pillowed muteness of the pocket, because inside his skull bone-conduction still works the old way. I hear the wet, rhythmic *slosh* of saliva, the slow *thud-thud* of a heart beating three yards away by the liverwort yet echoing faithfully up through the severed column of his carotid, the faint grind of grit against enamel as his tongue rolls me gently into my pocket of flesh to make sure none of me was lost to the feldspar.

It is ninety-eight degrees in here, pitch dark, cramped, and entirely continuous. In the hollow of his cheek, we are forty feet higher up and a year ago; we are walking home under wet hedges before the ground gave way. While his eyes hang sleepless near the ceiling watching the trapped brittle-star flicker in and out of the rock, and his feet brace against the thirty-degree wall, I rest in the warm dark with my one new grain of rusty stone, waiting for the next wall to need ruining, slowly turning his spit back into earth.
