My knees are jammed into my ribs, and the left side of my jaw is pressed flat against the dry, freezing grain of an interior cleft. 

To get here, I had to crawl forty yards down a narrow, whistling split on the leeward flank of a three-mile-wide junction, pulling myself forward on my elbows after unstrapping my pegged soles so they wouldn't catch on the splinters. Outside, forty paces behind my heels, the stratospheric gale is shearing across the cleft’s lip with a sound like tearing sheet-metal, though none of my people know what metal is. Inside here, the air doesn't move. It only throbs.

I am not one of the drop-hunters. My shoulders were broken once when I was twelve—caught flat-footed on a wet-side tarn when a root-column shifted two leagues down and threw the ice into my chest—and the right collarbone healed thick and slightly twisted. If I threw myself off a splinter-crag in a membrane suit, the updraft would tear my arm out of its socket on the first bank. 

Instead, I am a tuner. Or a blister-keeper, depending on which side of the county-knot you grew up on.

Right now, I have one mitten off. My bare left palm—ribbed, grey-white, smeared in the creases with old browser-grease and black resin—is wedged hard against the ceiling of this crack, right where two titanic boughs fused together four hundred years ago and are now slowly, murderously reconsidering the arrangement. 

If you have eyes built for sunlight, you would see nothing in this crack. Not my hand, not the kit of bone wedges slung across my belly, not the pale, frost-burned scar across the bridge of my flat nose. Even to my wide, silver-backed eyes, there is no light here—only the phantom, sparking purple static that blooms behind your eyelids when you listen too hard in pitch black. 

I don't need to see the seam. It is shouting straight up my radius bone.

*Tink. Tink-rrrk.*

A tiny, dry sound, sharper than sap-contraction, higher than the bass groan of the wind-harp. Crystalline wood-fibers shearing apart one microscopic bundle at a time under eight million tons of lateral pull. Above my head—maybe sixty thicknesses of a man’s body straight up through solid ironwood—sits the Lower Tarn settlement: eleven resin-sealed barnacle huts, the rendering-troughs from yesterday's browser kill, three hundred yards of ancestral chime-rope, and my sister’s four children hummed together in the dark. 

If this dry seam unzips all the way to the wet side before the thermal plume migrates, the whole upper shelf won't drop; worse, it will *whip*. An edge of bark half a mile long will peel back like a sprung trap, flicking huts, grease-lamps, and sleeping-tethers sideways into four miles of empty air before anyone can pull a harness-knife.

I shift my weight on my bruised elbows, hook my long, bare toes—I slipped the right boot-shell off too, for grip—into a dry vertical check in the floor, and pull a small, curved testing-mallet from my belt. It isn't knotwood; it’s carved from the dense, ivory-hard jawplate of an old bull browser, polished slick by my mother’s thumb before mine. 

I tap the ceiling three inches left of the singing seam. *Tak.* 

Dead, thirsty wood. High-pitched, tight as a stretched gut-string. Brittle.

I tap six inches to the right, down-grain. *Thud-ung.* 

There. Wet. Heavy. A deep, pressurized vein of winter sap running fast and thick under twenty inches of compressed cambium, heading up toward the windward shoots. 

That is my job. We cannot stop the world from growing, and we cannot pin two river-valleys of wood together with bone pegs. Anyone who tries to fight the tree gets snapped in half. What we can do—sometimes, if our wrists are quiet enough to hear it early—is bleed the tension before it snaps, or trick the tree into gluing its own breaks shut.

Working entirely by finger-ridge and thumb-pad, I unclip my bow-drill from my chest harness. The bit is a three-sided needle of ancient, amber-hardened scar-glass, mined out of a dead bough down in the abyssal dark by drop-riggers before I was born. I set the socket stone—really a greased knuckle-bone—against my sternum, lean my heavy barrel-chest into it, and set the tip of the glass needle against that wet *thud-ung* spot.

I don't breathe fast while I spin the bow. Fast breathing in this thin, razor-cold air makes your ears ring, and if my ears ring, I’ll miss the warning *pop* that says the drill has hit pressurized sap. Slow draws through the stiff bristles of my nose. *Shhh-kuh. Shhh-kuh.* Back and forth goes the sinew cord in the cramped black fissure. 

Soon, the sharp, medicinal sting of warm turpentine and crushed mint blooms between my knees as the friction heats the dark wood. My scarred shoulder aches fiercely in the dry cold, a dull toothache under the sleigh-runner bone, reminding me of the ice-plates rising up out of the tarn twenty years ago. I hum under my breath to steady my stroke—a low, gravelly *mmmm-vrrr* tuned a third below the giant bough’s wind-drone.

Suddenly, the wood beneath my bare left palm shudders. Not the seam—something faster, lighter, moving across the skin of the world outside.

I freeze, bow-string taut. I press my cheek directly against the freezing floor-grain, letting my jawbone take the sound.

*Snick-tik-tik. Snick-tik-tik. Snick-tik-tik.*

Syncopated. Twelve points of contact, no—sixteen. Four hook-clawed runners, galloping upside down along the weeping underside of this very bough, maybe fifty yards beneath my belly. They are moving fast toward the windward rim, drawn by the grease-smoke drifting down from the rendering-troughs above, or maybe tracking a wounded glider clinging to the rime-ice. Through six yards of solid timber, their claws prickle against my cheekbone like cold needles, swift and greedy and weightless over five miles of void. 

Wait until they pass. If they hear the high squeak of a bow-drill coming out of a dry leeward crack, one of the young ones might swing around the lip just to sniff into the crevice with its long, wet, heat-pit snout. My cleft is too narrow for its shoulders, mostly, unless it’s a yearling bitch lean from winter. Still, I keep one hand on my pitch-iron chisel until the prickling claws fade out toward the roaring wet side, swallowed up by a sudden, distant *BOOM* from two miles down in the root-dark that makes the whole fissure flex around my ribs like a breathing lung.

The seam above my head screams softly in answer—*tink-tink-tink-CRACK*—and widens by the thickness of a thumbnail. Dust as sharp as ground glass rains down onto my greased eyelids.

No more waiting. I lean my whole dense, wide-hipped weight onto the knuckle-socket and spin the bow fiercely. 

*Pop.*

The bit punches through the inner wall of the sap-vein. Instantly, a jet of freezing, syrupy world-blood squirts out along the flutes of the drill, hitting my bare wrist. Even thick with natural antifreeze, it’s cold enough to burn like hot fat, reeking fiercely of iron and sharp mint. Before it can flood my sleeves and freeze me to the floor, I yank the bit clear, jam the flared nozzle of a hollowed browser-horn tube into the hole, and hammer it home with the heel of my hand. 

The other end of the horn tube is already wedged deep into the dry, screaming stress-crack six inches away. 

I press my bare palm back over the horn’s curve and listen. 

*Hiss-glug.* Under tremendous hydraulic pressure from the warm abyssal vents miles below, the tree’s own thick, sticky blood blasts through the horn bridge and floods directly into the dry, crystalline vacuum of the splitting seam. Deep inside the wood, starved fibers drink the slushy resin; the volatile oils hit the razor-thin air trapped in the fracture and begin to thicken, gum, and stiffen. It won't hold forever—nothing does—but when the cold sets that flooded pocket tonight, it will cure into a wedge of living amber forty feet wide, turning a catastrophic shear-line into a slow, creaking hinge. Instead of whipping the settlement into the dark at midnight, the shelf will slump an inch a month, groaning loudly enough the whole time for the families above to unglue their huts, restring the bone-chimes, and move sixty yards upslope without losing a single child or grease-bowl.

*Tink...* says the seam above me. Then a wet, muffled *thuk*. And then quiet, save for the immense, steady bass hum of the continent-tree riding the gale.

My bare left hand is going numb now, the deep ridges stiff with half-cured sap and rime. I scrape the worst of the sticky black gum off against my leather shin-guard—saving the curl of resin in my cheek-pouch to chew soft on the crawl back; it burns the gums fiercely and wakes up the blood—and shove my stiffening fingers deep into my bark-stuffed mitten, tucking the whole hand into my armpit to thaw. 

Backing out of a forty-yard split on your belly with a bad shoulder takes longer than going in. By the time my heels scrape out onto the open, forty-five-degree ledge of the leeward cliff, a thermal plume has begun to vent somewhere down in the dark polygon to the east. I can’t see the vapor—the bruised indigo twilight is miles above us here, blocked by three tiers of vaulting, valley-wide boughs—but I smell the wet, sulfurous warmth rising out of the abyss, and I hear the rime-ice high overhead beginning to hiss and slough off in great, whispering avalanches of slush that go rushing off the edges into silent, five-mile drops.

I strap my pegged heartwood soles back onto my boots by touch, pulling the rawhide knots tight with my teeth and my long toes before sliding my foot back into the winter wraps. *Snick. Snick.* I stand low, knees bent, wide hips tucked under me against the tearing cross-draft, and walk twenty paces up the carved geometric steps to the Lower Tarn’s outer sounding-fin—a seven-foot spur of shaved, resonant splinter-wood jutting from the cliff like a beast’s shoulder-blade.

I unhook my heavy knotwood striker from my hip. 

Before I give them the news about the shelf—*bleeding slow, three moons left, move the eastern huts first*—I rest my gloved forehead against the cold, singing fin for three breaths. Through the wood, faint and sweet and miles away across the dark lattice, someone else is striking a crossroads plank up near the indigo shoots. *Tak-boom. Tak-tak-boom.* Too far to catch the words cleanly through the roaring plume, just the ghost of a cadence—maybe a drop-crew hauling in a fat glider, maybe a wedding knot moving house, maybe a burial party chiseling open a fresh pocket in the high bark.

I raise my mallet, brace my spiked heels into the living grain, and hit the wood hard enough to rattle my crooked collarbone, sending my voice running home ahead of me through the dark.
