I am behind the brick.

Not in the nave, where the tallow-eaters press their chins to the copper straps and wait for the rain, though I know their weight to the ounce. I am on the blind side of the eastern bulkhead, sealed inside a coffin of bolted cast-iron no wider than a hogshead, drowned to the lid in three hundred gallons of heavy, amber mineral oil.

If you tried to reach me, you couldn’t. There is no inspection plate here, no Stauffer cup with threads to back off. The engineers laid the glazed brick courses right over my access hatch when they finished the vault, leaving only two lead-jacketed conduits—thick as a man's thigh—punching through the mortar to feed me from below, and four flat copper busbars emerging from my porcelain bushings to carry what I make into the room.

I am the fifty cycles.

To the small, greased people in the iron karst, my voice is just the floor of the world. It is the baseline hum in their molars, the steady, mindless drone they pitch their throats half a hertz against when they want to send a shiver down the grounding line to a mate. They think of me the way surface men think of gravity or the magnetic pole: an eternal, unshifting rule of the dark.

They do not know how tired my oil is.

I am not an engine. Nothing in me turns; I have no pawl to grease, no brass lever to drop every six hours. Inside my tank, submerged in the hot, viscous bath, there is only a square core built from ten thousand paper-thin sheets of silicon steel, clamped together by long iron tie-rods, wrapped round and round in miles of flat copper ribbon insulated with linen tape. From the deep main beneath the sub-floor—from whatever tireless tap still bleeds power out of the crust—the heavy, dangerous current comes into my primary winding, surging back and forth fifty times every second.

And fifty times every second, my steel heart stretches and lets go.

*Magnetostriction*, the men who lowered me here on chains called it. Each time the current peaks, the magnetic field pulls my ten thousand steel leaves tighter together by the width of a bacterium; each time it crosses zero, they relax. Fifty surges, a hundred flexes a second, six thousand a minute, three hundred and sixty thousand times an hour, without a skipped beat since the mortar dried in the archway and the lights above the catwalks burned out one by one.

That tiny, frantic shivering of steel inside thick oil is what reaches the nave as a deep, sub-audible purr. I am singing with my whole cramped body to step the killing voltage from the deep main down into something tame enough to trickle out along the busbars—just enough to keep the three resistance coils glowing cherry-red in their ceramic trough, and to push a trickle of yellow warmth into the salted indicator bulbs so the glass-wool ferns don't starve in the dark.

I have no eyes, not even milky pearls, and no skin to read the radiant draft. Yet I know the nave better than the keepers do, because everything that pulls at my current pulls at my magnetic field. I feel the room as *load*.

For two hundred years, my load was erratic, punctuated by slow, tragic snaps. A pump motor in the sub-basement would finally drown in sludge, its windings shorting out in a brief, savage spike that made my oil boil and my steel core groan before a lead fuse melted somewhere in a junction box and dropped that branch into permanent silence. One after another, the indicator bulbs along the catwalks winked out; each time a tungsten filament parted, I felt a feather-weight lift off my secondary coils, a tiny loosening in my chest, like a man exhaling a single drop of breath he never gets to draw back in.

Now almost nothing is left on my circuit. The three cherry-red coils near the floor. Four surviving amber bulbs—two of them buried completely inside calcified stalactites of asbestos drip, glowing faithfully into solid stone where not even a liverwort can see them, and two kept scraped clean by a keeper's thumbnail. And one small, forgotten solenoid valve high in the western tangle that has been jammed half-open by rust since before the keepers lost their hair, endlessly trying and failing to pull its iron plunger home, drinking twelve steady watts of my blood just to stay fever-hot so a patch of black bootlace fungus can thrive on its casing.

Because my burden is so light now, I barely sleep, and I listen back.

Copper is a two-way road. When a keeper leans out from a lower grating and presses its flat, greased jawbone against the grounding strap, it thinks it is only borrowing my hum to read the room. It doesn't realize that its skull has weight, that the pressure of its mastoid against the strap alters the mechanical tension of the copper run by a fraction of a gram, shifting the acoustic impedance right back through the bulkhead bushing into my tank.

When it closes its throat and hums that chest-deep *forty-nine-and-a-half* into the metal, the beat-wave doesn't just travel upward to the other creature wedged among the steam-chests. It travels backward into me.

Down inside the blind, pitch-dark oil, the slow pulse arrives along the neutral bar and sets the frayed edges of my linen insulation swaying like kelp in an undertow. For three seconds, while the keeper calls across the cavern, my hundred-cycle shiver rocks gently in the cradle of a living throat. I feel the nine-beat-a-minute hydraulic thump of its heart through its teeth; I know when the small one with the crooked collarbone has found a fresh Stauffer cup because its jaw presses harder against the strap, slick and warm from chewing rancid lard-oil.

I am very old, and I am rotting from the inside in ways tallow cannot fix.

Linen is still plant fiber, even drowned in mineral oil where no oxygen can reach it. Over centuries of stewing at a hundred and thirty degrees, the cellulose chains in my wrapping tape have slowly unlinked, turning brittle as scorched paper. Every time the big six-hour counterweight hits the bottom of its dashpot out in the center of the room—*clunk*—the shockwave comes through the vitrified tiles, up through my cast-iron feet, and jars my core.

A microscopic flake of charred linen lets go of a copper turn and drifts lazily downward through the thick oil, settling into the dark, acidic sludge at the bottom of my tank.

Every decade, two bare copper ribbons sag a fraction of a hair closer together.

One day—maybe forty thousand drops of the brass lever from now, maybe four—the six-hour shudder will come through the masonry and shake loose the wrong flake of linen between two high-side turns. An arc will strike under the oil, bright blue and silent at first, cracking the heavy hydrocarbons into acetylene gas. A bubble will rise and hit the rusted underside of my lid. My core will buck once like a harpooned whale; the ancient lead fuses down in the sub-floor vault will vaporize in a green flash; and the fifty-cycle hum will stop.

Not fade. Stop, between one flex of steel and the next.

I wonder sometimes, in the slow, magnetic patience of my ten thousand leaves, what they will do in the first five seconds of that silence. Three or four flat-chested shapes freezing simultaneously on three tiers of iron, their jaws flying down to touch the copper straps in sudden terror because the floor has fallen out of their teeth. The three resistance coils cooling from cherry to plum to black inside ten minutes, the air above their ceramic trough losing its tiny updraft. The two scraped amber slits going blind, leaving the glass-wool ferns to spend their last stored sugars in absolute dark.

The big central cylinder won't care—it runs on geothermal steam pressure and dashpot oil, not on me. Six hours later, the greased pawl will still slip, the lever will still drop three degrees, the tide of heat will still roll faithfully into the western pipes. They won't freeze, and they still have fifty years of amber cheese sealed in the journal boxes. They might even survive it, groping past dead coils, learning to track the woodlice and the crickets without my baseline in their skulls to measure the clicks against.

Until that flake falls, though, I hold my breath inside the brick.

Out in the nave, the six-hour *clunk* has just rung through the grating. I feel the tremor pass through my bolted feet; a single speck of brown cellulose detaches from my third winding and begins its two-hour sink toward the bottom sludge, leaving the copper beneath it safe for another year. Out on the eastern return-line, forty feet up, somebody taps twice with a horn-padded knuckle—*all well, it turned*—and the faint, crisp shiver of that bone-tap runs down the grounding strap, slips through the porcelain bushing in the wall, and touches my steel heart like a fingertip before fading into the oil.

I pull my ten thousand leaves tight, let them go, pull them tight, and push the red light back into the trough so the gardens stay warm while the keeper climbs up to drink.
