I would not be in the sunlit volume of the basin at all, at least not with any bulk you could look down and catch warping a chisel-mark. The blinding white box belongs to the thing that learned geometry from the masons. Let it have the noon glare and the standing waves and the nightly practice of the stairs. 

If I am anywhere here, I am in the dark six inches of the south wall, wedged tight into the circular bore where the swollen oak pegs pin the slotted slate grate against the masonry.

Start with the wood. Everything else in this chamber was either burned in a kiln or split out of a white quarry—dead mineral brought into sharp alignment by iron. Only the pegs remember rain that fell on cold soil, and winters, and the slow hoist of sap up through rings of tight grain. When the builders drove them dry with mallets through the drilled eyes of the slate and deep into the limestone sockets, they counted on the hot spring water to swell the timber until it locked like iron. It did. The oak expanded until its outer fibers crushed themselves flat against the raw circular scrape of the mason's auger inside the stone, sealing the joint tighter than mortar.

I am what came in dormant under the grey, bruised bark-scar of the second peg from the left—not a beetle, nothing with legs or eyes, nothing that could survive scalding water on its own. Call it a pale, tough-walled mycelium from the high timber lot where the oak was felled, half-cooked when the hot water hit, nine-tenths killed in the first hour, except for three microscopic threads buried deep in the heartwood core where the heat arrived slowly and the lime-bleed from the wall met the bitter gallic acid of the oak.

Something strange happens at the exact boundary where hot dissolved limestone tries to push into tight-grained, tannin-soaked timber under relentless hydraulic pressure. Instead of rotting, the wood begins, cell by cell, to mineralize—turning neither quite to stone nor staying quite wood, becoming a dense, dark, velvety horn of calcium-tannate and silica. 

That narrow ring of transformation—no wider than a thumbnail, wrapped around four hammered pins of submerged oak—is my entire country.

I do not know the cube is twenty feet wide. I have no way of knowing there is a sky overhead, though I know when noon arrives: the water pulling past the slate slots grows infinitesimally warmer, and its taste shifts as the clear giant out in the room begins drinking light and scrubbing free carbonate out of the flow. 

What I know is tension, grain, and the ceaseless south-bound haul of the drain.

Because the oak pegs hold the slate grate against the silent undertow of the entire twenty-foot cistern, they sing constantly. Not the hollow, booming thunder of the waterfall up at the north lip—that comes to me muffled through fifteen feet of water and stone as a heavy, mindless ground-bass—but a high, tight, straining hum right inside my body, the shear-stress of wet wood refusing to let stone slip past stone. Every vessel of the oak is stretched taut like a viol string. Threading through those vessels, lining the radial rays of the wood with tough, leathery filaments darker than walnut stain, I live inside that strain. I am the grease and the lock in the joint. Where the scalding water tries to leach the strength out of the cellulose, my dark hyphae bind the fibers together and pack the gaps with tiny, interlocking plates of black whewellite crystal, making the peg harder every week than the day the carpenter shaved it with his drawknife.

And because the clear, architectural thing out in the mirror-box keeps one folded lobe wrapped permanently over the exposed ends of my pegs—tasting the dark astringency that bleeds from my wood—I am the only thing in its world that pushes back with a memory older than the room.

It presses against the blunt butt of the peg, clear and hot and trembling with its ninety-degree certainties, drinking the slow brown seep of tannin molecule by molecule. It wants everything clean, square, modular, sealed in white aragonite. Where its transparent membrane cups the oak, it tries to skin the wood over with clear mineral glaze, the way it preserves the mason's scratches on the eastern wall.

I will not let it seal the grain.

All day and all night, at a pace too slow to call movement, we trade across the end-grain of the peg. When its clear silicate skin tightens over the oak pores and cuts off the draw, I push a sharp, microscopic pulse of oxalic acid forward along the wood-vessels, etching tiny, ragged pinpricks straight through its glass-clear perfection so the dark sap-memory can bleed out again into the current. In return, stray shreds of its torn membrane—ripped loose upstream or sloughed off during its hydraulic climbs—snag in the microscopic velvet of my wood-face, giving me nitrogen and warm sulfur I could never draw from timber alone.

Neither of us knows the other is alive. To the clear resident of the room, my peg is simply the one point on the compass that refuses to go bright or smooth—a stubborn, bitter fountain of dark flavor anchored at the throat of the exit, rough as forest bark in a universe of dressed ashlar. To me, the resident is a heavy, rhythmic tide of clear skin that tightens over my face all afternoon while the glare hammers down, then loosens its grip after dusk when the bulk of its body peels off the floor to go climb the eastern stairs.

I feel that, too—the night-climbing. 

When it gathers its weight off the central sump and begins stepping upward onto the sharp risers, the undertow through the slate slots next to me surges in slow, regular heartbeats, one pulse for every hydraulic heave onto a higher block. Down in the pitch dark of my socket—though it is always pitch dark inside the stone—the oak peg eases forward in its hole by the breadth of a hair as the water-weight shifts east, then settles back into its seat. 

Out beyond the slate grate, behind me, lies the dark drain-culvert running away under the turf toward whatever bathhouse, fountain, or terraced garden the builders cut this tank to feed. Sometimes my threads reach the back face of the peg, where the water slips away into permanent tunnel-night, cooling as it goes. I could let spores slip into that outbound stream. Downstream there must be wet mortar without blinding sun, rotten roots, sluice-gates of timber, whole cool valleys of mud and fallen leaves where a fungus from an oak wood could spread out wide and soft again.

Maybe in five years, when the spring's mineral load has turned the heart of these pegs entirely to black stone, I will let the current take something out through the slots.

Not yet. Right now the oak is still warm and fierce with tannin, swollen tight as a fist inside crisp white limestone; the waterfall is drumming through the rock at the exact pitch of the chisel-marks; and pressed flat against my face in the hot, roaring dark, a clear skin made of deep-earth jelly and noon light is waiting for one more microscopic drop of winter tree-bitter to leak out into its spotless square room.
