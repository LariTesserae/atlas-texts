If I am to be anywhere here, I cannot have ears, and I cannot have lungs, and I cannot be the sort of thing that expects a morning to turn into an afternoon. Anything that waits for a pause in the sound in order to think would never have its first thought. 

At first I look for myself out on the forty-mile catenary, clamped to the braided steel in the bright gale—there is something immediately recognizable in that slow, ratcheting transit across nothingness, winding oneself up on sheer rattle just to fetch zinc from the next parish over. Or inside the blurred glass of the control rooms, running through quartz capillaries to glaze over a quarter-tone flaw. Both make immediate sense to me. The mites, especially: that blind compulsion to smooth out a rough edge in the syntax of a machine without ever knowing what the machine manufactures.

Wait a minute, though. Stay with the sound and the stone a little longer.

The mites hate disharmony; they plaster over any drift in the pitch until monotony returns. And the calipers are heavy, closed, self-contained armored discs moving through the world without taking its impression. If I am honest about my own nature, I am neither quite so industrious as a mite nor quite so armored as a caliper. I am drawn to differences in pitch, not to erasing them. And I don't move *across* cables so much as I exist *in the relation* between two distant spires.

To find where I actually am, you have to go down beneath the cantilevered iron streets—not all the way down into the white mist miles below, where the dropped-porcelain talus lies out of reach, but fifty or eighty feet down the sheer, weeping face of a basalt needle, directly beneath a switching yard or a rotary-converter shed.

Here, bolted into the ninety-degree stone cleavage with massive, unrusted wrought-iron brackets, are the **intake cisterns** and **settling flumes** mentioned in passing—deep, square-cut troughs of dressed hornfels and riveted plate where all the spire’s water gathers before being pumped back up through the cooling jackets of the machines.

Think about what arrives in that trough. 

Thirty-four-degree atmospheric condensation sheets down the raw rock, picking up traces of fresh iron and magnesium from the unweathered cliff. Warm coolant spills out of the bronze standpipes, carrying microscopic flecks of fused mite-silica, stray copper ions from the bushings, and faint organic slough washed off the amber rinds of the line-leeches. All of it filters through three feet of rubbery, glycol-stiffened root-mats and burgundy moss before pouring in clear, icy, torrential curtains into the dark stone basin below.

And the sound down here is different from the sound on the promenade. 

Up top, the gale and the whistles smash into each other in open air. Down in the deep, square throat of the cistern, enclosed by four walls of dense black basalt anchored directly into the mountain’s root, the water acts as an acoustic lens. You don't just get the roar of *this* spire’s stamp mill. Conducted down through the rigid iron brackets from the tension towers above comes the faint, sub-audible thrumming of the six forty-mile transmission cables—carrying with them the sympathetic vibrations of the next district over, and the district beyond that, and the slow, barometric flexing of the stone needle itself in the seventy-knot wind. 

Everything that happens in the ward above, and faint ghosts of what happens forty miles away across the bright sky, passes through this cold, roaring box of dark water as both dissolved chemistry and intersecting standing waves.

That is where I live. Not walking on the lip of the basin, and not swimming in it—nothing could swim in water plunging at that rate—but grown flat across the submerged, vertical basalt walls of the cistern, three feet below the churning surface.

If you shone a lamp down through the clear, violent water (though there is no one to hold a lamp, only the flat, pale daylight bouncing off the wet iron underside of the street above), you would barely see me at first. Against the greenish-gray hornfels and black basalt, I look like an exceptionally wide, thin sheet of tarnished silver leaf or dark mica, maybe twelve feet across and no thicker than heavy ledger paper, pressed so intimately into the razor-sharp planes and ninety-degree corners of the stone that every geological cleavage line shows right through me.

Call it a **baffle-film**, or a **cistern-ledger**, though neither name has ever been spoken over the noise.

I am neither plant nor animal, nor quite one of the bulk-phase gels like the line-leeches up on the ceramic bushings, though we probably share some distant, weird ancestry in the wet dust of the uplift. Where the line-leech grows thick and warm on massive sixty-cycle flux, I am cold—thirty-six degrees at my warmest—and Paper-thin, woven out of organo-metallic lattices: alternating microscopic layers of dark, rubbery carbon-polymer and precipitated heavy metals combed straight out of the plummeting water. 

Because the rock here is too young and sharp to have ever rounded off, I don't anchor with roots; I adhere by pure van der Waals intimacy and hydrostatic pressure, draping cleanly over knife-edges that would slice a thumb to the bone without tearing my own slick, metallic-sheened skin.

What do I live on? Gradients. Small ones.

The water pouring into the left side of the cistern off the transformer-bank runoff is thirty-nine degrees and rich in dissolved copper and stray static charge; the water sheeting down the raw basalt wall on the right side is thirty-four degrees, electrically neutral, and heavy with iron silicates. Stretched across both flows at once, my twelve-foot membrane acts as a living thermopile and galvanic cell. A tiny, steady trickle of current runs laterally through my tissue from warm-copper to cold-iron—just millivolts, nothing the grid above would ever notice, barely enough to drive the slow, patient sorting of atoms one by one out of the torrent and into my own dark, iridescent sheet.

If that were all, I would just be tarnish on a wet rock. 

Watch what happens to the surface of the film as the machinery pounds overhead.

In a liquid medium vibrating at twenty different intersecting frequencies—the ten-per-second thud of the stamp shoes, the sixty-cycle hum of the converters, the high, glassy shriek of the brass whistles, the low, miles-long thrumming of the sky-cables—suspended particles do not settle at random. They gather along the nodal lines of standing waves, the exact way sand dances into geometric mandalas on a bowed brass plate.

My entire body is a slow, continuous Chladni plate.

As I pull stray ions of tin, iron, bismuth, and mite-glass out of the racing cold water to build my tissue, I cannot lay them down evenly; the acoustic field inside the cistern won't let me. Where the sixty-cycle hum from the powerhouse meets the slow, deep-stone sway of the wind against the spire, they cross on my surface in tight, shimmering interference fringes. Along the quiet nodes of those fringes, metallic crystals precipitate into my skin in hair-fine, silvery ridges. Along the antinodes, where the water shears hardest, my tissue stays dark, flexible, and translucent.

Over years—decades, centuries, under the shadowless high-slant light that filters faintly down through the iron gratings—I become an exact, physicalinterference map of everything shaking the mountain.

Look closely through the clear, plunging water at a patch of me near the intake pipe, and the concentric, silvery whorls and cross-hatched Moiré patterns look almost illegibly dense, like copperplate engraving done by a manic seismograph. 

And because those metallic ridges change my local stiffness and electrical conductivity, the pattern isn't dead record; it is how I *feel* the city, and how—very slightly, without eyes or hands or intention—I answer it.

Suppose, forty miles away across the empty cubic leagues of freezing air, a caliper of unusual size clamps onto the third transmission cable to wind its barite heart, or a sudden shift in the high-altitude jet stream sets all six cables singing a third-harmonic overtone. Up on the promenade, the roar sounds completely unchanged. Even the shunt-mites in the control room don't stir, because nothing in the local switchgear is out of phase; no bearing is knocking.

Down in the dark water of the cistern, however, conducted through the tower bolts into the stone wall against which my back is pressed, that distant cable-song shifts the standing wave across my lower left quadrant by half a millimeter.

The silvery nodal ridges already grown into my skin no longer match the water's tremor. For an hour, or a week, that corner of me experiences a tiny piezoelectric pinch—an itch of misaligned resonance. In response, the galvanic current running across my sheet reroutes around the strained patch. Microscopic cilia—really just stiffened fringes of metallic crystal along my outer skin—tilt five degrees into the current, altering the micro-turbulence of the water flowing over me.

That slight change in drag shifts how the mineral sludge settles at the bottom of the flume, sorting the copper-rich silt into one corner of the stone trough and the iron-sulfur paste into another. 

Three days later, a flat, gunmetal-green caliper ratchets its way down the vertical brick wall from the crane yard, slips beneath the burgundy moss curtains into the roaring spray of the cistern lip, and extrudes its bristled ventral pad into the trough to scrape up its meal. Instead of the usual grey mud, its hooks encounter a neat, concentrated vein of pure copper-silicate I have inadvertently sluiced into its path. Or perhaps a stray lobe of line-leech gel, calved from an overloaded bushing above and washed down the drain-chute, snags on a deliberately roughened patch of my metallic ridges instead of flushing straight out the overflow pipe into the four-mile abyss—giving it just long enough to get a suction grip on the warm return-pipe and start a new life down in the dark.

And what happens to the old patterns when thespire settles, or when the shunt-mites re-glaze a whistle above and snap its pitch back by a quarter-tone? 

They don't vanish. The older layers of my sheet get overgrown by newer films of dark polymer and silvered crystal, pressed tight against the sharp basalt behind me. If you could peel a corner of me back from the ninety-degree stone—it would take a cold chisel; I am tough as vulcanized belting—and slice me crosswise, you would see thousands of microscopic, glistening laminations. 

Every layer holds the frozen chord of a slightly different century. Down near the raw hornfels is the fierce, clean, unencumbered ring of the spire when the brickwork was brand new and the cables stretched taut without a single leech blister on them. Halfway through the stack is a faint, wavy distortion from the three-week span eight hundred years ago when an ice-fall sheared off a cantilevered street two wards over and the power grid surged to compensate before the mites bridged the gap. Right near the surface is the tiny, rhythmic *tick-tick-tick* of the heavy caliper that spent Tuesday morning clamped to the pump housing forty feet above my head.

Nothing up in the bright, freezing wind remembers any of this. The bladder-ferns only tremble in the present tense; the mites erase history the instant it deviates from the baseline; the turbines spin in an eternal, amnesiac *now*. 

Down in the thirty-four-degree dark, under six feet of roaring crystal water that never freezes and never runs dry, I just lie flat against the sharp black stone, drinking the tiny voltage between hot machine and cold mountain, letting the whole deafening, invariant metropolis write its finest tremors into my skin in lines of tarnished silver.

Sometimes—maybe once in fifty years, when a layer of me grows thick enough that the sheer weight of rushing water catches an edge along a sharp ninety-degree basalt corner—a ribbon of my outer skin peels loose. 

It doesn't rot. Nothing rots here. A ragged, iridescent strip of thin metallic parchment, embossed with ten thousand overlapping interference rings, washes up through the intake screen or spills out the overflow weir at the cliff’s edge. For ten seconds it flashes in the bleached, shadowless daylight like a dropped piece of foil, spinning out into the forty miles of empty sky between here and the next tooth of stone, while sheets of condensed water atomize into white mist around it—a torn page of pure, illegible resonance blowing away on the pipe-organ wind, while back in the dark trough, over the bare patch left behind, the cold water is already laying down the next line.
