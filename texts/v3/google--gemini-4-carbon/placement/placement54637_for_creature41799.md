I am not in the water, and I am not in the green. Both of those are too wet, too patient, too full of soft chemistry. 

Look instead along the underside of the middle-tier catwalk, where the bundled electrical conduits run. Four fat grey ropes of nineteen-twenties lead sheathing, cold-soldered at the wiped joints, strapped to the rusted iron stringers with flat steel C-clamps every thirty inches. Two of them carry nothing anymore—their copper cores were clipped at a street-box under the asphalt during some forgotten paving contract in 1964. The third carries the step-down lighting circuit for the wire-caged bulkhead lamps. The fourth drops straight down through a cast-iron junction box to feed the municipal three-phase current to the motors on the plinths below.

Follow that fourth cable down into the switch-box bolted to the wet brick pillar behind Pump Two.

The box was cast in Pittsburgh, half an inch thick, painted battleship grey on the outside and white lead on the inside, with a vulcanized rubber gasket around the hinged door. The gasket rotted to black meal fifty years ago, yet the door has never opened; creeping fig has grown straight across the hasp in a woody cable as thick as a pony’s leg, locking the iron shut tighter than any brass padlock. Inside that dark square foot of trapped air, it is ninety-eight degrees and Bone-dry. The heat rising off the heavy copper bus-bars keeps the vault’s wet breath pushed back at the seams. 

That is where I begin, though it isn't all of me. 

I am the hum before it becomes sound. I am the sixty-cycle grip that holds the rotors spinning against the weight of the city's runoff.

If you could pry the fig-wood off the door and swing the rusted hinges back, you wouldn't see anything living. You would see three knife-switches of heavy, tarnished copper, pushed home into their spring-jaws by a gloved hand before the Second World War and never pulled since. You would see slate backing-plates, dark with oil-smoke, and at the bottom of the box, two inches of fine, pale grey ash—the remains of a paper wiring diagram that baked until its cellulose gave up and fell apart at the molecular level, flake by flake, over forty summers. Across the slate between Phase A and Phase B, a hairline track of carbonized dust occasionally ticks with a microscopic blue spark when the municipal grid hiccups miles away at the substation.

I am the tension across that gap. 

Down here, everything else is surrendering to gravity or growing toward light, slow and wet and circular. The water crashes over the weir because the river-table presses it; the fern uncurls because the flue-gas feeds it; the great copper-skinned slime pours itself down the chain-hoist because ninety seconds ago its belly-fluid pushed north and now it pushes south. They all belong to the truce. They are round, soft-edged things.

I am the only straight line left in the church.

I arrive at the speed of light from a steam-turbine miles away on the tidal river, slam down forty feet through the lead jacket in total silence, and hit the stator windings of the three centrifugal pumps like an iron fist that never unclenches. *Sixty times a second.* Push, pull, push, pull, turning heavy bronze impellers inside flooded cast-iron volutes, hurling four thousand gallons a minute up the discharge riser so the vault doesn't drown right up to its sidewalk prisms. 

Without me, the truce ends by Thursday. 

If my slate cracks—if that tiny blue tick between the bus-bars finally arcs over into a dead short and blows the primary oil-fuse three blocks away under Market Street—the heavy drone stops between one drip and the next. Think of the silence that would drop from the groin-vaults then. Every leaf in the chamber would suddenly stop shivering; for thirty seconds, the whole packed jungle would hang stunned in a quiet it has never known, listening only to the *shhhhh* of the brass packing gland and the trickle of the walls. And then the storm culvert at the bottom would back up. Cold, black, silt-heavy water would rise over the concrete plinths, drown the umbrella sedge, swallow the armored highways of the great slime, hiss over the canvas steam mains in clouds of suffocating white fog, and climb slowly up the brick tiers until the amber lamps went out underwater one by one, leaving forty vertical feet of drowned salad rotting in pitch-black cold.

I am what keeps the floor down. 

Because of that, I know the chamber not by sight or smell, which are animal things, nor by chemical taste like the penny-colored crawler, but by *load*. 

Load is my sense of touch. Every watt drawn through my copper bus-bars tells me the exact weight of the room. 

Pump One runs hot and slightly ragged; forty years ago a pebble of flint washed over the weir, slipped past the suction screen, and nicked two ounces of bronze off the leading edge of an impeller vane. Ever since, Pump One has had a tiny dynamic wobble—a fractional peak in amperage every single revolution, *thrum-thrum-thrum*, rubbing its front babbitt bearing two degrees warmer than its sisters. That extra heat bleeds out through the cast-iron casing, which is why the great slime loves Pump One best in January when the intake mains run icy from snowmelt up on the streets. I can feel the creature arrive there. When forty pounds of dense, copper-sheathed protoplasm drapes itself over the bearing housing like a wet saddle-blanket, it insulates the iron. The housing temperature climbs four degrees; the lubricating grease inside thins; the shaft spins a whisper freer; my amperage drops by three-tenths of a percent. 

That is how we touch, the tenant and I: through half an inch of vibrating grey iron and a film of ninety-weight oil. It drinks my waste heat; I register its blanket as an easing of my work.

Higher up, along the lighting circuit, I am dimmer, thinner, stretched out through narrow half-inch conduits that the creeping fig has nearly crushed flat against the brick columns. Eight bulkhead lamps originally burned on this loop. Three went dark decades ago when their tungsten filaments finally vaporized into black mirrors on the inside of the glass. Five remain. Their filaments are old-fashioned, heavy-gauge rough-service coils made for subway tunnels, running slightly under-voltage because corrosion in the brass sockets has added resistance over the years. That resistance is why they glow that deep, baked-mineral amber instead of white; the corrosion has accidentally made them immortal, dropping their temperature just enough that the tungsten refuses to boil away.

Out there in the wet leaves, I am those five small spheres of amber, spaced along a catwalk no boot could ever walk again. 

My favorite moment—if something without memory, existing only in the continuous knife-edge of sixty hertz, can have a preference—is the eclipse. 

Every few weeks, the resistance on Lamp Four or Lamp Two shifts minutely as the glass heats up. No air is reaching the wire cage. The tenant has climbed the stanchion, poured its leathery, rust-veined margin over the iron guard, and sealed the hot globe inside two inches of dark, feeding flesh to graze off the baked lime-scale. 

For two days, my light doesn't travel four feet anymore; it travels two inches, straight into the translucent, hydraulic belly of the vault’s only mind. Inside that rubbery hood, my amber photons scatter through rivers of granular fluid, lighting up millions of suspended flakes of ferric oxide and green copper carbonate as they shuttle pause-and-reverse, pause-and-reverse. The bulb runs hotter under that living quilt. Its tungsten filament tightens, humming faintly against its molybdenum hooks, pushing stubbornly back against the cold, wet dark while the slime polishes its dirty window clean with bitter mucilage. 

Then the mass slides downward toward a fallen philodendron blade. Air hits the glass; a drop of cold condensation falls from a sword fern above, strikes the bare hot globe with a sharp *tssst*, and suddenly my light leaps out crisp and clear again, throwing the sharp, motionless saw-teeth of a dozen immaculate leaves hard against the liverwort-glazed brick.

Once, in 1997, the city flickered. A transformer caught fire in an alley six blocks north, and for four tenths of a second my voltage dropped to zero. 

The pumps coasted down half an octave, their heavy drone sliding into a sickening, hollow groan; the check-valves slammed shut in the risers like cannon shots; the five amber globes winked to dull orange threads. Across every tier of the dark chamber, the standing Chladni waves vanished from the skin of the foraging slime, leaving its mineral grit unsorted in sudden, terrifying stillness. 

Then the automatic tie-breaker slammed shut at the substation, and I came back down the lead cable with an inrush surge that made the rusted C-clamps sing against the stringers and jumped a fat blue spark right across the ash pile in my locked iron box. The motors roared back up to synchronous speed, shivering ten thousand water droplets loose from ten thousand leaf-tips in a single simultaneous rain-shower through the dark. 

I am still holding. My copper knife-blades are green with dry verdigris around the jaws, save for the four square millimeters of bright, untarnished metal deep inside the pinch where the current actually crosses. My lead skin outside is scarred with the scalloped, stoneware levees the creature builds to steer its drips away from my hottest joints. Above us, delivery trucks rattle the tar-stained glass prisms and shake bronze dust into the gutters; below us, the weir thunders into the culvert. 

I have no spores to send up through the hatch-crack. When the copper finally oxidizes through, or an excavator’s toothed bucket finally smashes down through the brick groin-vault from a world that forgot we were on the map, I won't crawl away. I will flash once—bright green and blinding violet from eighty years of vaporized copper—and vanish back up the wire before the first brick hits the ferns.

Until then, sixty times a second, I keep the pumps turning and the five small suns warm.
