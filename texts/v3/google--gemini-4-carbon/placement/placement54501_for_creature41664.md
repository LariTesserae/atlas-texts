I am three courses down.

Up on the surface, the day is doing what it always does—bright, blinding knives of sunlight chasing purple cloud-shadows across the wet slate, the air dropping ten degrees and climbing back again in the space of a breath. None of that reaches here. Down in the third tier, where the roofs of the oldest collapsed vaults became the sub-floors of the middle city, the temperature does not move. It sits at forty-one degrees in July and thirty-nine in January. The light does not arrive as light; it arrives only where a vertical joint happens to align through two ceilings above, dropping a thin, pale plumb-line of grey onto wet stone before the dark drinks it.

At first you would think I am the water itself, because I go everywhere the water goes, or that I am one of the quick, slate-colored mammals darting through the dry cavities inside a six-foot-thick retaining wall. I know their dry, musky nests tucked behind the batter-stones—tight balls of stolen builder-wool packed into dead-end chinks where the damp never creeps, warm as cupped hands. I pass them constantly, and sometimes I wait outside their thresholds, though not to hunt them.

To live down here, you cannot have shoulders, and you cannot insist on keeping your own heat. Warm blood is too expensive in the dark; it demands sun-fed grass or vast sunlit pans of algae, and if you are small and warm in wet forty-degree stone, the mountain pulls the fire straight out of your ribs before noon. Better to let the stone set your clock. Better to be cold, slow, flattened from top to bottom so your belly and your back can touch the roof and floor of a bedding-plane gap at the same time.

If a slab were lifted off my vault—which happens once in twenty years, when a builder up-grade tracks a stubborn blockage down into the sumps—there would be a sudden, terrible rush of crisp sky-glare, and you would see something the length of a forearm, wide as a trowel, colored the dead, translucent pewter of quartz veins in wet schist. Four short, splayed legs set far out to the sides like levers; a flat, shovel-edged head with eyes reduced to tiny dark pinpricks under seamless skin that only know *surface* from *deep*; and along the sides of the throat, three pairs of dark, feathery gill-fronds kept slick by the weep of the walls, pulsing once every slow minute in the hundred-percent damp.

Not an architect. A Plumber's shadow. A keeper of the throat.

Up above, the city looks eternal, locked together by pure friction and geometry. Down here you feel what wants to kill it. Silt.

Every clear afternoon on the sunlit plazas, wind-blown dust settles into the catch-basins. Every winter, ice needles pry microscopic flakes of mica and shale from a million lip-stones. Algae dies, sheds its skins, mixes with beetle husks and the gritty grey paste that passes through the builders’ pebble-filled gizzards. Slowly, imperceptibly, that fine black flour washes down off the domes, slips over the soap-smooth sills, drops through the floor seams, and enters the dark capillary veins of the lower courses. Left alone for fifty years, the under-city would choke on its own richness. The hair-thin channels between foundation flags would pack solid with clay; trapped water would pool behind blind walls, freeze in midwinter, expand, and kick the dry-stone batters outward from the knees. The laminar silence above would break into stagnant bogs and raw, tearing gullies.

That dark silt is my pasture, and the gaps between the stones are my sky.

I move by pressing my ribs sideways against rough greywacke, swimming through stone seams half an inch high where water slides in a slow, viscous ribbon. I do not hear the city—there is nothing to hear—I feel its weight resting directly on the flat plates of my skull. Stone under compression hums differently than stone gone loose. When a heavy rain squall marches across the roofs half a mile north, I know it ten minutes before the water arrives, first by the minute change in air pressure breathing out of the dry joints, and then by the thickening of the glide beneath my throat.

And I know when a builder walks overhead.

Nothing else on the plateau has that step. The grazers up in the wide basins make a sharp, nervous *tick* that dies in the top layer of flagstone. Even a falling rock rings sharp and shallow. When a builder moves along an alley two stories above my head, the whole stack of dry-laid courses settles by the width of a hair—a deep, velvet compression that travels straight down through twelve feet of keyed greywacke into my belly skin, steady and unhurried: *press... press... press...* like a slow heart beating in the bedrock.

I follow those footsteps from underneath.

Where a builder lingers, things happen in the dark. Sometimes the heavy rhythm stops directly overhead, followed by a long stillness, and then the unmistakable, bone-deep *thock* of a slab being split on a quartz fulcrum. Immediately after, the water sliding past my gills turns cloudy with fresh stone-milk—sharp, angular grains of newly parted slate that taste of iron and ancient seabed, completely different from tired surface mud. I turn my flat snout upstream into that cloud, not to eat it, because raw rock flour has nothing in it, but to let it wash clean past me so I can reach the old, abandoned seam the builder just opened.

When a three-hundred-pound block shifts up top, a pocket sealed since before I hatched will suddenly breathe open down here, sighing out cold, dead air and flushing thirty years of trapped black sediment, pale blind springtails, and tangled white root-threads of sedge into the current. I wedge my wide elbows into the junction below and open my mouth, letting the slow black ribbon pour across the comb-rakers in my jaws, swallowing the rich, cold grease of decayed moss and blind insects until my pale flanks round out tight against the ceiling slab.

Mostly, I work the chokes alone. Where three subterranean rills meet at a buried threshold and a mat of drowned sedge-straw has caught on a rough projection, damming the flow until water begins backing up into a dry sleeping vault, I worm my shovel-head straight into the plug. Not out of duty to the domes above—I have never seen a dome, only the undersides of their floors—but because packed silt hides tiny, blood-red worms and greasy pockets of settled roe. Pushing forward with all four splayed legs locked into the wall joints, twisting my slick, flattened trunk from side to side like an auger, I eat my way through the dam.

For an hour the water stalls behind my body, cold and heavy against my tail. Then my snout breaks through the upstream crust. The pent-up pan gives way with a soft, subterranean *glug* that nobody on earth hears, rushing over my back and combing my dark gills straight behind me, carrying the loosened cloud of fine mud onward, down through the deeper, broader culverts toward the unseen cliffs eighty miles away where the plateau finally spills its clear, filtered surplus into the air.

Once—half a lifetime ago, though down here seasons are only measured by whether the water numbs my feet or merely cools them—the ceiling lifted off the world.

I was lying in a silted sump beneath an old, slumped courtyard, sluggish with midwinter cold, four others of my kind pressed side by side with me in the same narrow pocket of unfrozen trickle, our flat tails braided together in the mud. Above us, that slow, massive weight arrived and stayed. One slab lifted. Then another. Cold, dry wind—horribly thin, smelling of nothing at all—dropped into the damp, followed by light so sharp it struck through my seamless eyelids like white heat.

My kin whipped backward instantly, vanishing like spilled mercury into the needle-cracks of the side walls. I was wedged deeper in the clay where an old foundation stone had canted sideways, pinching my hind legs lightly when the frost shifted it. I couldn't pull free before a shadow blotted out the glare.

The air filled with the thick, greasy smell of wet wool, crushed lichen, and slow mammal heat—an enormous, suffocating warmth, like a sun lowered into the ditch. Down into the gap came a flat, black horn-hand, scarred white at the edge and wider than my whole body. It slid directly beneath the canted stone that pinned me, less than an inch from my nose.

It could have crushed me into grey jelly without ever feeling the resistance through its insensitive horn. Instead, the hand stopped. The huge stone lifted half an inch and held dead steady, suspended on pure slow-twitch iron.

Above the rim of the pit, a broad, sloped face-plate leaned down into the shaft, blocking the sky. Two wide-set, pewter-colored eyes behind milky membranes tilted sideways to look into the dark sump. A warm, slow plume of breath rolled down over my back, smelling faintly of green pond-scum and sweet rainwater, condensing instantly into silver mist on my cold skin.

For ten heartbeats—mine, which meant perhaps three of its own—nothing moved. The builder looked at the pale, gill-fringed thing wedged in the throat of its cistern; I pressed my throat flat to the wet shale and tasted the lanolin rolling off its coat into the puddle. It knew what I was. In three centuries of lifting stones, you see everything that keeps the stones dry.

The spatulate hand shifted fractionally, levering the three-hundred-pound block three inches sideways onto a firmer seat, widening the channel beneath me to the breadth of two fingers. A second horn-hand reached down, not touching me, and scooped a double-handful of sour, compacted gravel clean out of the sump, flinging it aside with a dull *splat* onto the terrace above.

Then the broad grey slab descended again, blotting out the stinging blue sky, settling onto its new fulcrums with a single, muted *clack* that locked the dark back into place.

The blinding pain left my eye-spots. The dry wind died. Instantly, freed from the pinched joint, a fresh, clean ribbon of forty-degree water slid over the threshold upstream, ran smoothly beneath my belly, lifted my gill-fronds off my neck, and carried the faint, oily taste of old builder—healthy, winter-fat, moving south—down into the labyrinth.

I stayed there until the lingering warmth of its breath faded completely out of the stone. Then I flexed my ribs against the newly widened walls, slid forward into the dark against the current, following the rich, muddy trickle up toward the unseen sun-pans where the spring thaw was just beginning to wake.
