I would not have legs here, or ears, or a heart that beats fast enough to measure against a clock. In a basin so crowded with throats and heavy thumbs, anything with a spine is too loud, too hurried, and too localized—stuck inside a single skin, able to feel only the single stone beneath its own jaw.

Find me forty feet down, where the bruised noon never reaches and the ninety-decibel shriek of the frogs is compressed into a dull, warm hum in the rock. 

Down in the dry-stone cribwork, where the ancient builders stacked Cyclopean boulders without mortar to fill the mountain’s vanished gullies, there are miles of narrow, pitch-black seams. The air in these gaps never stirs. It sits at a permanent, saturated ninety-eight percent humidity, smelling of wet feldspar, iron, and the faint, drifting ammonia from the pyramid flues half a mile away. 

If you shone that red-filtered lamp between two granite headers down here, you wouldn’t think you were looking at an animal. You would think the builders had changed their minds after all and used mortar—a pale, pearl-grey, slightly rubbery lime-paste squeezed into the dry joints, webbing across the triangular voids where three boulders meet, running in flat, glistening ribbons along the undersides of the plaza flagstones all the way across the valley.

Touch it, and it is cool, tough as tire-tread on the outside, damp with beaded mineral sweat, and completely still. Only if you stayed for a week would you realize the mortar had moved four inches to the left, leaving behind a clean, microscopically etched track on the basalt where it had dissolved a film of silica and iron oxide to stiffen its own skin.

I am the *gasket*. The *shim-flesh*. 

Biologists from outside the massif, scraping a thumbnail-sized piece of me into a glass vial, would argue for years over what kingdom claims me. A perennial, colonial plasmodium? A giant, lichenized fungus that forgot its algae and made a pact with sulfur-oxidizing bacteria instead? A subterranean sponge adapted to air as thick as water? None of the names quite fit, because nothing else on earth evolved specifically to inhabit the architectural load-paths of ten million dry-laid stones.

Up above, the heavy, grease-coated masons think *they* are the ones holding the valley together. And they are—in the sudden, clumsy way of creatures with bones. They lift the bulging header, they saw the greedy fig-root, they slot the fist-sized chocking stone into the gap. *Clack.* 

Wait twenty minutes after they leave. 

Through the granite, I have felt their double-thumbed hands prying; I have felt the sudden disappearance of the root’s hydraulic push and the sharp, bright pinch of the new chocking stones taking the weight of the terrace above. Granite against granite is never truly flat. Even dressed stone meets only at tiny, microscopic high-points, where three hundred pounds of dead load concentrates onto three square millimeters of crystal, threatening to crack the shim or let it slip when the mountain shivers.

Out of the dark seam behind the wall, my margins begin to creep. 

I am blind, deaf to the air, and vast—a single continuous mat of dense, fibrous protoplasm threading through six square acres of sub-plaza fill, thin as stationery where the blocks sit tight, swelling into thick, cartilaginous bolsters as wide as a man’s thigh where the rubble leaves an empty pocket. What guides me is *piezoelectricity* and sheer mechanical squeeze. When basalt and quartz-rich granite bear heavy, shifting weight, their compressed crystals leak faint electrical potentials and tiny gradients of heat. To me, stress in the stone tastes bright and sour, like copper on a tongue. Loose, unloaded stone tastes flat and empty.

I flow toward the sourness. 

Slowly—an inch an hour—a pale, velvety lobe of my body presses into the hairline gap around the new chocking stones the mason just placed. Once I am wedged directly under the pinch-point of the terrace’s weight, I change phase. I pump water out of that specific lobe, drawing dissolved calcium from snail-shell dust washed down from the plazas above and locking my internal fibers into a cross-linked, horny matrix tougher than seasoned oak. 

I become a living lead-washer between the stones. Now the weight of the wall doesn't rest on three brittle crystal points; it spreads evenly across my toughened back. For forty years, that single palm-sized pad of me might stay locked in rigid stasis, holding up twelve tons of staircase while the rest of my body miles away continues to flow, digest, and breathe.

And in return for bearing the crush, the valley feeds me everything that falls through the cracks.

Nothing on the surface stays on the surface forever. When the clouds drag through at night and condense on the millions of leaves overhead, the slow drip-water doesn’t run off into a river; it percolates downward through the joints of the plazas. By the time that water reaches my dark honeycomb twenty feet down, it is a rich, tepid broth: dissolved bat-guano tea, the shed cuticles of a billion millipedes, washed-down frog mucus, pulverized leaf-mold, dead leafcutter ants, and the waxy, ginger-smelling sebum rubbed off the shoulders of the stone-dwellers themselves as they chimney up and down the vertical shafts.

I spread translucent, sticky fans across the drip-lines in the dark, drinking the soup before it ever hits the buried mountain bedrock eighty feet below. When a pale strangler-fig taproot comes groping blindly down through a foundation shaft—not the thick, wall-wrecking wedges near the surface, which the masons cut, but the fine, hair-covered drinking-tips deep down—I do not fight it. I wrap a slick cuff around the rootlet, trading it phosphorus and trapped cloud-water from my reservoirs in exchange for simple sugars drop-shipped straight from the sunlit upper crown three hundred feet above—a sky I will never have eyes to witness, tasted solely as a faint sweetness in the dark.

The masons know me. Of course they do. You cannot reach your flat, chisel-nailed fingers into ten thousand wall-joints over a lifetime without touching my cool, rubbery hems.

They do not scrape me out when they clear a blocked drainage conduit. In fact, watch an old female repairing a slumped stair-riser near the cisterns: after she slots a dry slate shim under a rocking step, she reaches down into a damp foundation-crack, pinches off a walnut-sized gobbet of my soft, growing margin—it does not hurt; I have no nerves to scream, only a slow, chemical sealing of the tear—and smears me deliberately into the joint like glazier’s putty before lowering the heavy block back down. Within three days, that severed piece will toughen under the load, send thin white runner-threads four feet down through the rubble, find one of my main veins, and fuse back into me, bringing news of a new stairway’s weight.

Only once in a great while do I wake all at once.

High tropical massifs are young mountains, still rising, still shrugging off tectonic tension. Every ten or fifteen years, an earthquake rolls up through the roots of the range. 

To the creatures in the canopy and on the plazas, it is five seconds of terror: the oilbirds shriek and smash into vines, the blind caimans hiss in the culverts, a million gallons of trapped bromeliad water slosh out of the niches all at once in a sudden warm deluge, and the masons clamp all four split-thumbed hands onto the wall-ledges, eyes shut tight.

Down in the sub-masonry, where ten million unmortared stones would otherwise chatter against one another and liquefy into a catastrophic landslide, those five seconds belong entirely to me.

As the first shear-wave hits the buried bedrock, a billion tiny shear-stresses flare simultaneously across six acres of stone joints. Instantly—through a sharp, non-Newtonian reflex, the way cornstarch and water turns rock-hard only while you strike it—every soft cushion, ribbon, and gasket of my body across the entire basin locks solid for six seconds, drinking the kinetic shockwave, turning it into a tiny fraction of a degree of harmless warmth across ten thousand tons of wet rubbery flesh, refusing to let a single boulder slide past its neighbor.

Then the wave passes into the eastern ridges. 

Slowly, in the pitch dark, I soften my margins again, letting out a long, silent breath of warm carbon dioxide and mineral dampness that rises gently through the plaza flagstones, misting the ankles of the coatis, fogging the mossy chins of the listening masons, and settling the city back down by a tenth of a millimeter—whole, plumb, and quiet under the screaming leaves.
