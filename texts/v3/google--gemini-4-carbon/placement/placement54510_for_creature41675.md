You would not find me out on the walkways, or even up on the return pipes where the big boles drink the heat. I have no patience for daylight, even the cool, olive-rinsed daylight under the cable trays, and I do not want the maintenance man’s spray rig ever catching my back.

Look instead at the eastern wall, twelve feet up from the buried flagstones, where two flanged sections of sixteen-inch low-pressure steam return meet at an expansion joint. The bolts holding the iron collar together were torqued down forty years ago; since then, the alluvial clay beneath the foundation has slumped three millimeters to the south, canting the pipe run just enough to pinch the top of the joint and open the bottom by the thickness of two thumbnails.

Inside that gap sits the old packing—originally braided flax and tallow, long ago chewed away and replaced by me.

I am what holds the seal now. If you pried at the lip of the flange with a screwdriver, you would think you had struck vulcanized gasket-rubber or dense, resin-soaked oakum. You would feel a blind, stubborn pushback, tough as shoe-leather and slick with condensed steam. That outer rind is only my calloused cuff, dark brown and impregnated with black iron oxide so the hot drip doesn't scald me. Most of me is braided flat into the mortar seam directly behind the pipe hanger, spread out between the damp bricks and a sweating copper grounding strap in a sheet of tough, amber-colored cartilage the size of a butcher’s apron.

I don't move across the yard. Moving is for things that haven't found their seam yet. When I was small—a thumb-length ribbon of pale, translucent gristle washed down from a rotten sluice-gate upstream—I spent a whole winter creeping up the weeping masonry, tasting cold lime and dead rust, half-starved in the eighty-degree gloom, before my front edge struck the stray galvanic tingle of the copper ground strap and, three inches higher, the hundred-and- thirty-degree weep of the pinched flange.

I wedged myself in and grew to fit the fault.

My life is entirely pressure and pitch. I have no eyes, no limbs, not even the slow hydraulic joints of the root-crawlers. What I have is tension. Half my body is anchored deep in the lime mortar of the retaining wall, where the cold river groundwater pushes inward from the floodplain behind the bricks—a steady, blind, mineral shove that tastes of chalk, sunken barges, and winter mud even in July. My other half is wrapped tight into the iron collar of the return line, taking the hot, trembling pulse of the steam plant.

Between the cold push of the river and the hot throb of the pipe, I act as a living shim.

I know the entire basin, though I have never seen an inch of it, because the copper grounding strap runs straight down from the transformer racks overhead, passes directly under my belly, and buries itself in the wet clay beneath the turbine floor. Everything that happens in the steel skeleton of this place comes down that flat braided copper ribbon as a shiver or a sharp metallic tang.

Mostly, the world is a thick, braided chord: the deep, unvarying sixty-cycle hum of the generators—smooth as heavy oil—crossed every four seconds by the dull subterranean *thump* of the flywheel’s heavy side passing bottom dead center, and punctuated by the crisp *tink-hiss* of the pneumatic relief valve lifting on its seat. When all three rhythms sit squarely inside one another, I loosen my outer lip by half a hair’s breadth. Warm condensate—distilled water laced with traces of boiler compound, iron carbonate, and cylinder oil—bleeds through the flange gap, filters through the dense, velvety sieve of fine white filaments tucked inside my cuff, and trickles out the bottom as clear, cool water for the maidenhair ferns clinging below me. I eat what the boiler sheds. Slowly, grain by grain, I lay down microscopic plates of silica and iron along my margins, stiffening where the pipe tries to sag.

Sometimes the chord changes.

Miles away, up on the dry street level I will never visit, a streetcar line stalls, or a foundry throws a main breaker, and instantaneously the sixty-cycle hum in the copper strap beneath me tightens—going a quarter-tone sharp in my tissues before the turbine governors at the north end of the yard lean into the penstocks, groan down through their brass worm-gears, and drag the pitch back to dead level. Simultaneously, the return pipe in my grip swells by a fraction of a millimeter as extra steam dumps through the bypass. Before a single hot droplet can hiss out into the ginger leaves and betray a leak, I swell my inner chambers with cold mortar-water and clamp down hard around the bolts, holding the line tight until the load settles.

I know the other residents only as passing shadows in the vibration.

Once every few days, one of the medium-sized root-crawlers hauls itself along the return pipe to reach the mineral seep in the corner pilaster. Long before it touches me, I feel the dull, rhythmic *creak-shift* of its woody joints damping the pipe’s natural ring. When its blunt, mossy foot steps directly over my flange, it pauses—always—for twenty or thirty minutes. My outer rind leaks a faint, sweet slime of broken-down tallow that its pale crown-tendrils like to lick clean. I let it graze. Its rough bark scrapes away the choking crust of calcium scale that builds up along my breathing pores, a slow, scratchy grooming that leaves my rim supple and clean. When it moves on, the iron pipe rings clear again.

And every ten weeks comes the terrible, bright clatter from above.

It starts with the screech of the upper tenement latch—a jagged, high-frequency spike down the steel stanchions that makes every filament inside my cuff snap shut at once. Then the heavy, two-footed *clang, clang, clang* down the ladder rungs, ragged and impatient, wholly out of time with the flywheel.

When the maintenance man walks past my wall, the flagstone runoff sloshes irregularly against the brick base, and a strange, volatile smell cuts right through the soup-warm stink of silt and copper: dry wool, harsh soap, crushed creeping-fig stems under rubber soles, and fresh, unweathered lithium grease.

Once—years ago now, three coats of green paint back—he stopped directly beneath me. I felt the cold steel snout of a long wrench tap twice against my flange bolts: *clink, clink*. Testing the ring of the iron. Looking for the telltale white hiss of a blown gasket.

I held myself stone-stiff, clamping every drop of boiling weep inside my core even though the trapped heat stung my pale inner gills, pressing my dark, oxide-crusted rim flush with the bevel of the cast iron so I looked like nothing more than old, tarred gasket-flax squeezed hard between the plates. Above me, I could feel him breathing—fast, shallow, four times to a single turn of the flywheel. After a moment, the wrench tapped once more, satisfied with the solid, deadened *thud* of tight metal, and moved on toward the wheel valves.

Since then, he leaves my corner alone. The ferns below my chin grow thick and glossy on the cooled, clean drip I ration out to them; the brick arch beside me has stopped settling because my calcified heel fills the mortar gap like poured lead; and deep inside the dark pinch of the iron, warm on one side and river-cold on the other, I hold the joint shut and listen to the water fall.
