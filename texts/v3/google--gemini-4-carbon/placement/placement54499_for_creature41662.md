I am not one of the walking cairns. To tear myself loose from the canyon-wide web and bundle my threads into a rattling, solitary coat of stones—that feels too loud, too severed. A mason-errant can only feel the single yard of bridge beneath its heels. I would miss the rest of the chord.

Instead, you would find me two miles out from the chalk-streaked cliff, woven through the collar of a hollow, inverted tower where three opposing seams of downward meet and braid together like cold currents in deep air. 

To your eyes, if you climbed down a switchback stair that ended abruptly above my district, there would be nothing to point to and call *me*. You would see only a wide, flared throat of grey slate lacework—an upside-down bell tower open at both ends, wide as a cathedral nave, hanging from the belly of a plaza with five miles of pewter luminescence yawning beneath its lip. Inside the bone-marrow hollows of that masonry, tucked out of the flint-smelling wind, hang four thousand papery bladders no bigger than dried figs, linked from stone to stone by miles of hair-fine, burr-coated thread.

That skein is my body. The tower is not my house; the tower is my skeleton, borrowed from the mountain one flat pebble at a time and held together purely by how carefully I lean it against itself.

I do not see the bruised plum clouds overhead, nor the pale cliffs across the gulf. I have no stolen mica eyes. What I have is tension. 

Every shard of polished basalt in my walls presses against four or five neighbors with a specific, dry ache. Through ten million microscopic silica hooks, I feel the entire tower as a three-dimensional chord of pressures—a silent, continuous hum of counterbalance. When one of the radial, felted grazers slides onto my eastern flank, hooks three claws into my lattice, and begins grinding away at the black crust under my eaves, I know it is there not by sight, but because forty-six stones along that rib suddenly press together a hair tighter, and a faint, pleasant fizz of stray static leaks down through the joints like dry warmth. I let it feed. Its millstone teeth clear away the dead, spent carbon soot so fresh black crust can seed itself in the cracks, and when it vents its rhythmic little puffs of grey exhaust, my parabolic flutes catch the dust and funnel the lingering charge straight down into my deepest nurseries.

My whole existence is an appetite for poise. 

An unbalanced arch feels to me the way a splinter under a fingernail feels to wet creatures, or a breath held too long. Where two currents of gravity rub against each other at the base of my bell-throat, the local rules of weight are treacherous and braided; *down* points northeast on my outer rim and nearly straight up through my hollow core. A wall-colony that stops paying attention here gets torn apart in an afternoon.

Right now, the air is changing.

Minutes before a front rises out of the luminous trough below, the graphite powder coating my threads shifts its charge. All four thousand of my bladders tighten at once. Inside each dry, translucent sac, a swallowed gizzard-stone—smoothed to a pearl by centuries of rolling—tips three degrees toward the chasm. 

A broad, cold tide of tilted weight is coming up from the dark, dragging an abandoned tenement’s worth of loose slate behind it. I can feel the approaching stone-stream before it arrives by the bow-wave of static pushing ahead of it through the thin air, making every velveteen filament along my southern lip stand stiffly out into the emptiness.

If I hold myself rigid, the tilted gravity will catch my flared rim wrong, levering seven hundred tons of dry-stacked lacework against a fulcrum that is no longer there. My whole lower gallery would unpeel in a wild, ragged landslide, spilling my bladders out into the five-mile drop where the four-winged kites drift.

So I do not hold rigid. I breathe out.

Along my southern curve, twelve thousand silica threads simultaneously unhook themselves from a single, load-bearing rib of pale chalk-slate. Just one seam, forty yards long, unlatched in the dark. 

When the front hits, there is no crash. Weight swings forty degrees sideways. The unlatched rib lifts cleanly out of my side like a bone drawn from cooked fish, releasing the pressure on the six galleries above it. Two thousand flat, powdered pebbles slip free of my flank with a soft, dry *shhh-tck*—sacrificed on purpose, peeled away in a neat grey ribbon that goes curving off toward the eastern cliffs to become someone else’s staircase.

And at the exact same moment, on my opposite rim—where the new angle of *down* has suddenly created a yawning, hungry pocket of slack air—I cast my net.

Millions of hooked hairs reach three feet out from the lattice into the pewter gloom. The migrating stream of foreign tenement stones arrives out of the twilight, drifting fast and flat, smelling of cold flint and old static. As the leading shards brush my outstretched threads, I do not try to stop them dead; they would snap me in half. Instead, I stroke them as they pass. Ten thousand tiny tugs, bleeding off a fraction of an ounce of speed here, tipping a flat edge two degrees upward there, using their own momentum and the sideways pull of the front to coax them inward.

*Tck. Tck. Tck-shhh.*

River-smooth grey ovals slot themselves into notches that did not exist ten seconds ago. Three stones kiss edges, wedge against a fourth under the tilted pull, and abruptly *bite*—locking hard into a brand-new flying buttress that arcs twenty yards out into nothing.

A shiver runs through the fresh masonry and straight into my threads: the crisp, singing tension of a clean arch taking up load. The ache in my northern collar vanishes, replaced by a deep, resonant equilibrium that hums through every stone from pinnacle to lip. Already, microscopic spores of black static-crust are settling into the newly rubbed seams, drinking the fresh friction-spark of the landing.

Sometimes, once in a long age, one of the walking cairns passes through me. 

I know it the instant its rattling stone foot steps off an adjoining ribbon-bridge onto my upper terrace. Its gait feels entirely different from the stutter-click of a hunting stilt-walker; a mason-errant steps with deliberate, testing weight, pressing down slightly on a suspect tile, listening back through its heels. 

When one walks down the inside of my inverted throat, upside down against the sky-facing floor, I quiet my threads and watch it through the masonry. It is my cousin, after all—a piece of the reef that chose legs over breadth, carrying its tiny, lonely chord of thirty stones around its own fist-sized heart of clicking flint. Last time one came through, it paused halfway down my spine where two old seams of basalt ground harshly together whenever the east wind blew—a chronic, dull pinch I had never quite been able to shed without dropping my whole lower lip. 

The ragged figure stood motionless in the dimness for half a day while chalk dust snowed horizontally past its stone hood. Then it knelt, drew a narrow, wedge-shaped shard of dark slate from its own shoulder, and tapped it softly into the blind joint behind my third rib.

The pinch went out like a snuffed spark. The whole nave settled a quarter-inch and began to hum in a cleaner key.

Before it could reach down to pry a replacement shard from a loose parapet, I unlocked a single, palm-sized river pebble from my richest, highest gallery—a prime stone, dense basalt, thickly furred on one face with sweet, crackling black crust—and nudged it half an inch out of the wall right beside its knee. 

I felt its dry silica tendrils brush my threads in the gap as it took the stone: a tiny, silent handshake of shared static in the marrow-dark. It slotted my gift into its own shoulder with a neat *click*, stood up, and walked off my lip into a rising cataract of silver talus.

After it was gone, I drew my threads back into the quiet latticework, shifted four thousand gizzard-stones a hair to the west to meet a turning breeze, and stayed where I was—holding twenty thousand tons of dry pebbles afloat in empty air, farming lightning out of dust, turning the slow fall of two mountains into lace.
