At first the instinct is to stop at the rim, belly-down in the coarse grass of the lowland plain, and look in. That is where a mind used to distance and overview tries to put itself: thirty feet up, parted sedge under the elbows, taking in the whole unroofed nave at once—the ferns in their damp central pocket, the pale-barked fig flattened against the northern talus, the buff-grey mass tucked inside its low rectangle of stone.

But even lying motionless at the lip breaks the rules of the bowl. Where the walls curve inward near their tops, the sky down below is already pared down to a long, irregular oval of flat grey; a head leaning over the edge cuts a dark notch into that ceiling. In a room without directional light, even so small an eclipse dims a patch of the pale silt floor. Sooner or later a sleeve brushes dry earth, a grass husk or stunned midge slips over the rock, and down in the stone kerb those shallowly socketed grey eyes register the fall before it lands. To stay at the rim is to remain an disturbance waiting to happen. And there is no going down the slumped marl ramp as a visitor—nothing with quick joints, hot blood, or a habit of naming things has any business on those four hollowed bedrock steps. Whatever comes over the lip belongs to the damp, chert-blunt fingertip and the rasping palate. There is no room on the floor for a second appetite, and language dried up down here ages ago because nothing ever needed to be distinguished from yesterday.

If I look for a place inside the hollow that asks nothing of its sparse economy, my thought settles for a while on the worked limestone itself—not the untouched cliffs, but one of the flat slabs around the largest catch-pool. Specifically, one of the stones that used to sit higher up on the walls of the one-room shelter out in the middle of the floor. 

There is a deep, quiet pull in imagining that span of time: pried up from a natural bedding plane when tools still existed here, stacked dry-laid five or six courses high against winds that never actually reached the bottom of the sink, sitting idle while timber softened into humus and vanished and iron rusted down to nothing. And then, on some undated afternoon centuries later, feeling two wide, four-fingered hands settle without a scrape along my edges. Lifted cleanly off the course beneath, held against the matte, stoneware-colored hide of a slack belly—feeling for twenty unhurried minutes the deep, twelve-drop expansion of flat ribs and the damp warmth of marl-dark creases—while those broad heels rolled slowly across twenty paces of paving-smooth bedrock toward the western cliff. Set down into the silt to steady the rim of the pool. Knowing time ever after only as wet carbonate crusting slowly along one flank, dry cistern warmth on the other, and twice a day the long, silent arrival of a heavy forearm settling into the glassy concavity its own damp skin had dissolved into my back.

I almost stay there, inside that slab. The stillness fits. 

Yet the longer I linger where the coping stone touches the base of the wet western cliff, the more I feel something left over that is not quite rock—some small, wakeful thread that still tests a current, still folds limbs under itself, still keeps a boundary between inside and out. Too restless to be fieldstone, far too small and spare to claim a square inch of the scoured floor.

So I let go of the open nave altogether and go back out onto the plain—a quarter-mile west, where rain soaks past the black root-mat of the turf, sharpens itself on decaying grass stems into weak carbonic acid, and slips into the dark horizontal joints of the limestone.

That is the other way into this country: down through the sieve of the rock itself, taking thirty summers to creep the length of a field, losing the sky, losing seasons, losing every trace of surface haste until the water warms to the exact constant temperature of the buried stone. Just before that slow lateral seep reaches the sheared-off face of the western cliff—two inches behind the dark, glistening band where the wall sweats into the hollow—a hairline bedding plane widens into a flooded pocket no larger than an almond shell, dissolved out ages ago and lined with tiny, blunt teeth of calcite.

That is where I find myself.

Curled sideways against the wet spar in pitch dark, no longer than a husk of blown chaff, flattened from side to side so I can turn in narrow seams without scraping. No eyes at all—not even sunken spots under the shell, since light has never crossed the last two inches of buff limestone. No pigment either; only a translucent, milk-glass paleness through the curved plates of my back, hardening with the same dissolved lime that thickens the drinker’s bones outside, and a hair-thin grey thread down my middle where microscopic washings from the plain’s dead roots pass slowly through. From my head, two filaments twice the length of my body reach forward into the motionless water, segmented finer than spider silk, tasting what took decades to arrive from the turf above.

Up on the plain, things shaped faintly like me dart through ditch-weeds and spend their whole lives inside a single summer. In the warm dark behind the seam, a flick of my swimmerets happens once an hour when I shift hold from one crystal point to the next. Every few years, when my shell grows tight with carbonate, I slip free of it in the dark and eat the shed ghost shred by shred over weeks, leaving the pocket as clean as the floor outside.

I cannot enter the hollow, and the hollow cannot reach me. Between my flooded cleft and the dim grey air of the nave sits a two-inch rind of porous stone whose channels are finer than silt. The groundwater leaves me behind as it squeezes through that filter to sweat out onto the rock face, bead by bead, creeping down to the scalloped basins at the foot of the cliff. I live just upstream of the catch-pool—drinking the water before it sees the oval sky, before it grows a dull crust of lime at the rim, before parted teeth touch its surface tension.

Yet nothing in the hollow is clearer to me than the clock of that room, because water-logged stone carries every tremor without softening it in air.

Every three or four seconds, when a drop detaches from the upper overhang and strikes the handspan of water in the bedrock basin just below my level, the impact shivers straight back into the cliff base—a tiny, crisp tap against my shell and through the tips of my outstretched feelers. Three seconds, four seconds, another. That pulse has been spacing out the dark since long before my kind washed down from the plain’s hidden fractures and found the seam habitable.

For most of the cycle, that crisp tap is the whole world. Then comes an interval when the rock at the base of the cliff undergoes a slow, mute compression. 

Out in the air, those wide, chamois-padded soles snap no grit and disturb no dust; the silence between drops stays unbroken. Inside the flooded stone, however, several hundred pounds of dense, lime-heavy bone folding down onto packed silt and leaning onto the unmortared coping slabs sends a faint, unmistakable groan of elastic weight into the bedrock wall. My two long filaments stop tasting the drift and angle outward toward the porous rind.

For half an hour at a time, we sit two inches apart with the stone face between us. Where its broad head and slack chest hover close to the wet band, the air outside warms by a fraction of a degree and thickens with breath, slowing the microscopic pull of evaporation through the pores in front of me. Down through its heavy wrists and through the glassy hollows of the coping stones comes a second, slower tide: every twelfth or fifteenth tap from the basin, the pressure in the rock base swells infinitesimally as those wide, flat ribs expand, holds steady across three more taps, and eases back without a jar.

Once or twice during that half-hour, the expected crisp tap from the water below misses its beat. A fraction of a second early—because its bowed crown sits higher than the pool—a broader, duller *thock* travels sideways into the cliff instead, conducted straight down through thick skull-bone, heavy neck, and braced forearms into the stone around my pocket. A drop has struck its head and is running down the shallow flute behind its ear, leaving a powdery white track of the same mineral I wear in my plates.

Then the weight lifts—ten slow minutes of unloading from slab to silt to broad heel—and withdraws across the floor toward the ferns and the low stone kerb. The tiny strain goes out of the cliff base. Later, though the pitch black inside my seam never changes, the seep slackens by a hair’s breadth as night thickens the air outside and stills what little evaporation the grey day drew from the rock. I hook two translucent legs onto a point of spar, let my long feelers go slack in the slow water, and rest inside the wall while the taps go on measuring the dark for us both.
