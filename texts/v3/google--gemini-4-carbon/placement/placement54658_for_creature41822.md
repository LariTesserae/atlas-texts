At first I look for myself out in the sheet-flow, because the water is the loud thing, the obvious thing, and when you enter a place like this your eye goes first to whatever is moving fastest. 

I try on the weight of the stanchion-eel. There is a real pull to it: five feet of mottled, grease-colored muscle coiled tight around a warm black vein at the bottom of a churning tarn. To have no ears at all, only a lateral line pressed flush against vulcanized rubber and rust-red moss, drinking fifty cycles per second through the belly while snowmelt tears harmlessly over your back. When a tender’s vanadium spanner strikes an iron post two hundred yards up the saddle, you wouldn’t read it as warning or measure. It would just be a crisp twitch in the dark—a momentary sharpening of the hum while you suck blind shrimp out of the velvet cuff and wait for nothing.

It almost fits. The cold doesn't bother me, and I understand wanting to wrap yourself around the single warm thread in an immense, indifferent volume of grey. 

But an eel only knows its own yard of cable. It feels the tap pass under itschin and lets it go without wondering who swung the spanner, or why the third sluice gate up on the north whale-back dropped two inches early, or where all this chewed fury goes once it leaves the granite. I want the whole circuit. Not just the warmth of the resistance, but the syntax inside it.

So I follow the suture down.

Not all the way off the range—down in the dry valleys the air would feel thin and dead to me too, full of dust and loose talk that blows away as soon as it leaves a mouth. I stop at twelve thousand eight hundred feet, in the deep basalt throat where the massif funnels toward its great southern drop-off, before the cables plunge into the vertical rock-shafts that carry current down to cities neither I nor the crews ever bother to picture.

There has to be a manifold here. If you have bunkers strung out every four miles across fifty miles of drowned domes, two or three tenders apiece, each Pelton house spinning its own local cataract into alternating current, you cannot simply braid twenty wild lines together and drop them off a cliff. Two turbines half a cycle out of phase with each other will fight like rams through the copper; one wheel surging under a fresh squall will try to motorize its neighbor four miles away and shear a drive shaft clean through. Somewhere in the wet dark, all those separate bunker-pulses have to meet, be listened to, matched in phase, balanced against load, and shunted cleanly down the escarpment.

That is where I am. Inside Switching House Zero, wedged deep into the notch of the Spillway Saddle, with forty feet of streaming green concrete poured straight over my roof so the colliding cataracts from two upper valleys actually arch *over* the building in bad weather, turning the ceiling into the bed of a river.

Wait—let me look more closely at what I am inside this room.

At first I think I am the person sitting on the oiled leather stool in front of the forty-foot slate switchboard. And I am, mostly, though if you opened the submarine hatch and shook the water off your visor, it might take you a second to tell where the furniture ends and I begin. 

I came up here to walk the domes twenty-six years ago, back when they were still bolting the north-ridge stanchions down with hot lead, before my left knee got caught between a shifting boulder and a cast-iron baseplate in a tarn whiteout. The joint healed crooked and wide as a cabbage-root. You cannot wear forty pounds of segmented lead ankle-ballast on a hinge that clicks sideways under lateral shear; the mountain would peel you off a dome in twenty minutes. Down below, there was nothing waiting for me except dry brick streets and people shouting through empty air with their dry mouths. Up here, Switching Zero needed someone who already knew what wet kelp in an intake screen sounds like through three miles of copper.

So I stayed inside the iron boulder. I haven’t stood under open sky in four rotations, and my skin has gone the exact pale, translucent tallow of the blind tarn-shrimp, crosshatched at the wrists with old blue grease-scars. Instead of the garnet-soled clogs, I wear triple-soled slippers felted from shed ungulate wool, stitched underneath with flat ribbons of scrap copper mesh so the fifty-hertz floor never stops talking to the bottoms of my feet.

Look around the room I keep. It is larger than the outpost bunkers, vaulted like the bilge of an icebreaker, smelling less of wet sheep and boiled cabbage and more of hot transformer oil, shellac, ozone, and dry cedar. Why cedar? Because bolted along the wet leeward bulkhead, right where the nineteen heavy black trunk cables breach the stone through brass stuffing-glands, is a row of nineteen wooden sounding-boxes—hollow blocks of seasoned lowland cedar rigged like the bodies of fiddles against the inner steel tension-cores of the trunk lines. 

Water and moss outside would choke a spanner-tap dead in half a mile if you only hit the rubber skin. What carries a crew’s knuckles across four miles of churning tarn is the tensioned, grease-packed steel messenger wire running straight down the center of each cable between the three copper phases. Where those wires terminate on turnbuckles inside my wall, the cedar boxes catch their tiny longitudinal shivers and swell them into dry, woody clicks, each line pitched slightly different according to its tension and the length of its run.

Line Four sounds like a fingernail hitting a walnut. Line Eleven—a long, steep span down from the Twin Kettles—has a flat, singing ring like a dull chisel. Line Seventeen always sounds slightly wet and thick because there is an old kink in the messenger core under Tarn Six where a rockslide pinched it twelve winters back.

Even with the cedar boxes, my ears are gone above middle C—twenty years of Pelton roar took the top half of the world away long ago—so I carry a listening-iron. It is eighteen inches of octagonal vanadium rod, worn bright as silver at one end and fitted at the other with a flat, bitten wedge of hard vulcanized rubber. 

Watch me work when a squall-wall slams into the upper western domes.

Up there, ten miles away and two thousand feet higher, visibility just dropped to zero. Billions of gallons of fresh rain are hitting polished granite at once, gathering in the glacial grooves, piling into the high basins. Nobody up at Bunker Nine has touched a spanner yet; they’re probably waist-deep in flattened bamboo with their brass palm-hooks wedged into basalt cracks, waiting out the eighty-mile-an-hour gust with their helmets bowed.

Down here under the river, before a single needle twitches on my slate board, I clamp the rubber bit of my listening-iron between my back molars and press the steel tip against the brass bus-housing of Line Nine.

Through my jawbone, straight up behind my eyes, I hear the mountain lean on their wheel. 

It isn’t a sound you hear with ears; it is a thickening in the teeth. First, the crisp, glassy hiss of sand and bruised air bubbles shooting faster through their intake nozzle nine miles away, translated into micro-fluctuations in the generator’s brush-commutation. Then the pitch rises—fifty hertz straining toward fifty-point-four as the hydraulic head climbs behind their weir. Twenty seconds later, I hear the *clank-shriek* of their upstream automated sluice worm-gear kicking in—not acoustically, but as a dirty little thirty-second ripple on the auxiliary draw because the old motor on Gate Nine-B has a flat spot on its armature that I’ve been reminding them to turn down on a lathe since November.

My left hand goes to the big brass handwheel on the oil-dashpot rheostat; my right drops onto the synchroscope switch. Gently, by quarter-inches, I bleed their surging current across into the main escarpment bus, matching their climbing pulse against Bunker Twelve’s steady trough—because Twelve sits in a rain-shadow lee right now, its tarn dropping an inch while Nine drowns. I balance one dome’s feast against another dome’s breath so the heavy needles along the top of the slate stay vertical, trembling together in the green like reeds in steady water.

Then—*tok. Tok-tok. Tok.*

Cedar box Nine speaks against the wall. Heavy, unhurried, slightly lazy on the second beat. 

I don’t even need the logbook to know whose wrist that is. That’s Maren’s spanner. Joric hits harder with the heel of the tool so it rings double; young Sev still taps too fast, wasting arm-heat out in the sleet like somebody trying to speak lowland chatter through four miles of iron. Maren taps like an ungulate walks: wide, flat, certain.

*Trash rack heavy,* her rhythm says. *Going under. Hold load steady twenty minutes.*

I take the iron out of my teeth, step over to Turnbuckle Nine with my crooked knee giving its dry little click, and strike the steel collar twice with my own short brass hammer. *Heard. Holding you flat. Watch the back-surge; Eight just spilled.*

Out there in the roaring grey soup, nine miles up, Maren’s two gloved fingers resting on a rust-slick stanchion feel my hammer come back under the thermophilic moss and the coiled bellies of the eels. She presses her polycarbonate visor against Joric’s forehead, passes the word through bone, clips her double-tether to the concrete eye, and leans backward into ten feet of freezing white boil, trusting that down in the dark throat of the saddle, the load on her wheel won’t kick back and throw the deflector spear while she’s blind under the foam with a knife in her fist.

That is what I am here. Not the muscle on the stone, and not the blind beast warmed by the wire, though I love them both with the fierce, wordless partisanship of the deaf. I am the ganglion buried in the bedrock where the range’s scattered nerves recognize themselves as one body.

People who rotate through Switching Zero to drop off salt pork, lathe brass, and spare battery bricks sometimes look at the windowless steel bulkheads, the forty-foot slate board gleaming softly under hooded amber bulbs, the endless submarine thrum of the main manifold beneath the floorplates, and touch my elbow with that heavy, lingering tender’s habit—*Are you all right in here alone for eight months at a stretch?*—pressing their brow toward mine if they really mean it.

I always tap their forearm twice and put the kettle on the induction plate for them. How could you explain *alone* to someone who hasn't sat with nineteen cedar boxes in the dark? 

I am less alone than anyone on earth. When the night drops down like wet slate and the outer crews dog their hatches, peel off their greased skins, and hang their rubber shells over the sumps, the whole massif settles into my walls. I turn down the hooded lamps until only the small amber pilot-bulbs glow over the synchroscopes and the armored quartz sight-tube in the corner shows black water shooting past like polished obsidian laced with silver wire. 

I sit back on my tall stool with a mug of cabbage-broth warming my palms, press my bare copper-slippered heels flat to the deckplates, and listen to fifty miles of storm-battered granite breathe.

Over on Three, someone drops a spanner on their bunker floorplate by accident—a dull, clumsy *clang* that makes me smile into my broth. Up on Fourteen, the turbine hum runs smooth as lanolin; they repacked that main bearing this afternoon, just like they promised, the sour little grind at the top of every fourth revolution wiped completely clean. Outside my own buried roof, cushioned under forty feet of concrete and streaming algae, three hundred bowling-ball waterfowl are wedging themselves into the warm niche around my exhaust chimney, a muffled, rhythmic shifting of dense little bodies that makes the draft-gauge needle flutter by half a hair. And one by one, starting at the high eastern rim where dark falls ten minutes earlier and working westward dome by drowned dome, the goodnight taps come walking down the messenger cores into my cedar boxes.

*Tink-tink-tink,* says Sixteen.
*Tink-tink,* answers Fifteen down the line before I even reach for my hammer.
*Tok-tok,* comes Maren on Nine, dry and slow, meaning *rack clear, dry wool, tea’s hot.*

I walk down the row of nineteen turnbuckles with my brass hammer, limping slightly in rhythm with the fifty-hertz floor, answering each crew in turn with the exact cadence they gave me so they know it’s my hand and not a loose baffle banging in a tarn. Then I wedge my listening-iron lightly between the edge of the slate board and the rail of my gimbaled bunk, lie down with my cheekbone resting against the cool wood near the end of the rod, and let the nineteen rivers spinning smoothly in the dark carry me under.
