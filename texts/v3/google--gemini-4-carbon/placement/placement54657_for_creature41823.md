I am down in the wet dark beneath the grates, three tiers below the crisp daylight, where the cartwheel cabbages blanch to the color of tallow for lack of sun and the air tastes of boiled lime and hot rubber. 

At first you would take me for part of the masonry—a slumped, terraced bolster of blackened felt wedged between a weeping brass trunk-line and the brick pier of a Switch-House. I am old, or at least I have been felted over so many times that my shoulders have the rounded, immovable geometry of a boulder that the pipes had to be routed around. Strips of red hawser-vine bark are stitched into the wool over my spine; my knees are capped with curved sections of retired pneumatic hose, vulcanized onto my own callouses decades ago. When I breathe, my ribs do not push outward against this heavy shell—they fold inward and downward, a slow bellows-stroke that disturbs nothing around me, not even the two slick, six-legged ferrets currently snoring in the warm crease where my thigh meets my belly.

My left brow-ridge—hairless, scarred smooth, thicker than a thumb—is pressed flat against the brass sweat of the trunk-line. My right palm rests three feet lower, spanning the cast-iron flange of a junction box bolted directly into the fresh basalt floor.

I am a bridge between two rhythms that do not quite agree.

Through my forehead comes the wide, shallow chatter of the upper canopy: a cable-car line two hundred yards north has picked up a crosswind oscillation; a juvenile three Switch-Houses over is tapping a clumsy, impatient question about tallow-pots into a cold return-pipe (*tick-tick-slur, tick-tick-slur*); a gantry crane is swinging forty tons of quarried stone across my sector, its magnetic bearings humming a clean, heavy E-flat down the pilings. All of that is bright, surface weather. I let it wash across my skull without answering.

Through my right palm comes the other thing. The slow, gritty, un-lubricated groan of the crust itself. 

Twenty feet beneath this brick pier, a blister of new lime-stone and volcanic glass is pushing upward. It has been rising since yesterday’s noon. It has no malice, only heat and weight, and right now it is leaning thirty thousand pounds of fresh geology against the underside of the main manifold’s anchor-bolt. 

I can feel the iron fibers of the bolt stretching. To an ear in the cold air above, there is only the *pat-pat-pat* of water hitting broad leaves. Inside my wrist-bones, the bolt is singing a thin, glassy, climbing note that makes my back teeth ache. Left alone until nightfall, the bolt will shear. It will make the Forbidden Sound—that sharp, dead, ringing *crack* that throws heat into the wind for nothing—and the brass trunk-line will whip upward three inches, splitting its seam, scalding the fern-thickets, starving four clans downstream of their night-warmth before the automated spiders even notice the pressure drop.

I do not hurry. Hurry makes corners, and corners catch.

I exhale until my collarbone hinges inward, rolling my bulk sideways out of the niche so smoothly that the two six-legged mammals slide onto the warm brick without waking, merely curling tighter into each other’s greased tails. My four-toed feet find the slick obsidian shelf below the grate. The stone here is brand new—split open during Tuesday’s realignment—and its edges are sharp enough to shave horn, yet my sole-pads deform around the blades like stiff putty, taking the bite without cutting, reading the grain of the glass.

I am not one of the high-swingers anymore. My children’s children are up there in the blinding cold light right now, riding the rising strokes of hydraulic rams, leaping through silver-needled ferns to hush a rattling sky-bridge latch while wrench-billed birds snap at their cuffs. Good for them. Let them have the sky and the fast iron. My work is down in the root-tangle where the machine meets the raw earth, where things move an inch an hour with enough force to powder brick.

I unhook a wooden mallet from my chest-harness—ironwood root boiled in tallow until it sinks in water, faced on both ends with stacked washers of greased leather—and pull two wedges of green, fibrous giant-rhubarb pith from the warm pouch beneath my armpit. Wait. Not rhubarb; rhubarb will crush too fast under this torque. I tuck them back and draw out a curved shim of cured browser-horn, salvaged last month from an antlered bull that misjudged a cooling lava crust near the east foundry. Horn yields, bit by bit, turning crushing weight into slow, silent grease as its keratin crushes.

Before I place it, I tap the brass pipe twice with the knuckle of my thumb, then drag my palm downward across the wet rivets. *Damp-thrum. Slide.* 

Forty yards up the line, in the root-cellar of the next Switch-House, someone answers instantly—two soft squeezes on a rubber bleed-sleeve. *Felt. Ready.* That will be Little-Collar, or perhaps the scarred female with the copper-braided shawl who shares his transformer alcove. Whoever it is, they are already turning down the local bypass wheel a quarter-inch, easing the internal steam-push on my section of the flange so the metal stops fighting from the inside while I tend its outside. We will probably never look at each other this week. I know the callous-pattern of their thumb-tap better than I know my own face.

I wedge the browser-horn into the hairline gap beneath the rising collar of the flange, right where the swelling lime-dome is biting into the iron skirt. With my leather-faced mallet, I give it three heavy, deadened *thuds*. No ring. Just the sound of a wet boot stepping on moss.

Immediately, I press my brow back to the brass.

The thin, glassy, climbing note wavers. Drops half a tone. Rounds out. The horn shim is taking the bite, smearing microscopic ribbons of crushed protein between the razor-stone and the cast iron. The bolt stops stretching and begins, imperceptibly, to slide sideways along its slotted mount—just as whatever long-dead ghost drew this blueprint intended it to do before the lime crust fouled the track.

It will hold until tomorrow morning, when the automated pavers smell the bulge in the grade and roll down the alley to re-seat the pier. 

I stay with my forehead against the metal for ten minutes more, eyes shut against the cold drafts trickling down through the grates, just listening to the new chord settle in. The E-flat of the overhead crane passes back the other way, empty now, humming higher. A trickle of warm mineral condensation runs off the flange, catches in the deep felted gutter of my eyebrow, and channels neatly down the oiled crease of my cheek to drip onto a pale, blind fern-shoot uncurling between my toes. 

Down the pipe, from miles away toward the sunny western rim of the basin, comes a rolling, overlapping cascade of rapid thumbnail-clicks—hundreds of hands passing along the news that a battery of six derelict turbines has just kicked back online after ten years of silence, flooding three whole acres of frozen slag-heap with fresh, dripping steam. Already I can feel the light, rapid scamper of younger folk crossing the grates above my head, swinging eastward with baskets of cabbage-starts and bundles of cradle-wire to claim the new warmth before the browsers eat the first moss.

I do not follow them. Someone has to stay behind and ease the old couplings as the heat-load shifts away. 

I reach into a tallow-pot clipped to my hip, scoop two fingers of gray graphite-fat mixed with fern-wax, and smear it along the fresh obsidian edge beneath the pipe so that when the stone rises another quarter-inch tonight in the freezing dark, it will slip past the brickwork like an otter through reeds. Then I fold my ribs inward, back myself into the warm brick niche, lift the two sleeping six-legged beasts gently onto my lap so they don't lose their heat to the stone, and lay both wide palms flat against the humming iron, keeping the bass line steady while the city rearranges the sky above me.
