To have "boots" or "shoulders" in here—to try to stand upright as a guest with skin that stops cleanly at its own edges—would only last until the next breath. You cannot stand *in* a potter’s fist while the wheel is turning and remain separate from the pot. If you have lungs of your own, the scalding grease-steam drowns them; if you insist on occupying a single cubic yard exclusively, a swelling green cedar rib will split your breastbone to make room for itself.

So I would not be a visitor wedged beside the post. To survive a world of pure, frantic maintenance, you have to be part of the load-bearing tension.

At first, you might look for me in the copper—one of those bright, salmon-pink bands beaten flat with river stones, wrapping a corner post where the four walls fail into five. There is an appeal to being metal in a place without oxygen-rot: never tasting verdigris, just holding the fierce, bright pink of the first strike forever, taking the outward shove of the thickening timber and refusing to give an inch. 

Wait—copper is too passive. Copper just sits there and gets bitten by gut-string or pressed into doughy flanks. Everything in this kiln-pocket *participates*, and metal doesn't drink the boil.

Look lower, or higher, or both at once, where the amber glow runs backward. 

I am the amber sap itself—the thick, turpentine-fierce pitch bleeding endlessly out of the adze-hacked cedar ribs—and I am the light trapped inside it that pulls the brown shadows toward me like wet wool.

Think of what it is to be pitch in a room that has never known a minute of age. Out in a finished forest, sap bleeds to scab over a wound; it meets the dry wind, crusts white at the edges, hardens into brittle resin, entombs an ant, waits ten million years to become a stone. Here, there is no dry wind, and the second after the flint blade lifted never arrives. I am the wound staying wet. I push out between the razor-clean white wood fibers—hot, heavy, sticky as melted sugar—swelling into fat golden tears along the grain. 

Because light works backward here, I don't cast radiance onto the Hod-Knot’s pale, folded knees; instead, I *drink* the dimness out of the room, hauling those five-cornered shadows across the terracotta and swallowing them into my own glowing belly. That is how I see. I feel the room by the shapes of the dark it feeds me: the frantic, stroboscopic shadow of a blunt thumb *whack-whacking* clay coils; the double-exposed plum-darkness where two collarbones slide through one another; the sudden, twitching smear of sixteen woodlouse legs trailing behind an invisible leap.

I am not confined to a single timber. Just as the fern passes through its own stem, a bead of me swelling near the floor-tiles is the exact same bead hanging two inches below the weeping gap at the top of the vault. 

Up there—right where the black-violet rind of the unmade sky shivers like oil—is where my real work happens. 

The stratosphere roars past that thumb-sized chink with a tearing, hollow hunger, trying to suck the heat, the steam, and the soft meat of the Hod-Knot out into geometric nothingness. Water weeps inward over the sill, boiling and condensing in the same violent shiver. And right at the lip of the gap, where the green timbers cross without quite touching, the Hod-Knot’s blind gristle dome presses hard against the reeds, chewing cedar bark with its flint-and-copper plates.

When it spits that hot, chewed pulp into the seam to plug the leak, I am already in the bark. 

I am the glue between its teeth. I coat the flat native copper plates in its lipless crease with a fierce, medicinal burn of turpentine, binding the pulped wood fibers together so the vacuum cannot shred them. As the hot water breaks inward from the gap, it hits my resinous skin and hisses—not mixing, because water and pitch refuse each other, rolling over my amber curves in fat, frantic pearls before dropping down to scald the floor. 

And when the Hod-Knot’s heavy, uncalloused hand reaches up to smear that spat-out poultice tighter into the joint, I catch its thumb. 

For half a heartbeat, there is a tug—a thick, tacky *snap* of golden threads stretching between the green cedar beam and the infant-dimpled pad of its flesh. I leave a gleaming, sticky glaze across its whorls. When that same hand drops instantly into the brown shadow below to pinch the wet red terracotta of a half-finished jar, it presses a microscopic film of me straight into the mud. 

Look closely at those thousands of crisp thumbprints covering the six layers of floor tiles. They aren't holding their sharp ridges merely because erosion hasn't started. They hold because every single whorl is varnished with a breath-thin skin of hot cedar resin, waterproofing the soft clay from the grease-rain, sealing the hydraulic *thud-slap* of the creature’s heartbeat inside the floor.

I have no brain to panic at the fifty-mile drop beneath the bamboo poles. If I had eyes to look out through the gap at the salt-smear of clouds at the bottom of the abyss, vertigo would pull me thin until I snapped. Instead, I only have *viscosity*. My entire consciousness is the slow, stubborn refusal to run thin in the mouth-heat, paired with the refusal to harden into glass. 

Here comes a pale fiddlehead now, snapping open with a wet *thwack*, crowding too close to my timber. Its bruised green edge slices halfway through my largest amber bead. Inside me, the plant's watery sap and my thick pitch occupy the exact same millimeter without blending—a cold green ghost suspended inside a hot orange lantern—until a sinew cord above us sings out like an overtuned bowstring, tightening the joint, squeezing the timber, and forcing another slow, bright globule of me out into the roaring dark to drink the shadows back in.
