I am not down on the terracotta flats, where the dark-red bells slide over the sharp bricks and wedge giant mallets into the seams. I am too thin for the floor; its crisp, fresh-pulled edges would ribbon me in a minute. Nor am I all the way up in the brass soup of the clouds, wrapped pretending to be rawhide around the barkless spars, though I came from there once, or have the memory of coming from there. 

You will find me eighty feet above a sunken courtyard, wrapped tightly around the braided reed cord of a heavy, hammered-copper plumb-bob.

I am a sleeve of translucent amber muscle, no thicker than a wet chamois cloth, banded with branching veins of bright, poisonous turquoise from years of licking the bitter green verdigris off the metal below me. Right now, the scaffold I hang from is an incomplete bridge—a massive, meaningless timber cradle spanning two dry-stone piers that end abruptly in the yellow gloom. Half an hour ago it was the skeleton of a watchtower; twenty minutes from now it will probably un-deal itself into a series of low cattle-pens that have never known cattle. 

I do not wrap the wood anymore. The wood vanishes when the error comes, stranding you in the scalding air to cast yourself out like a net and hope a parapet rises underneath you before you hit the razor-sharp tufa at the bottom. I missed my catch once, years back. I hit the corner of a stepped mud ziggurat twenty feet above the flooded pavement and felt three of the mahogany patriarchs down in the shallow water instantly turn their blind crowns toward my splash. I hauled myself up a rawhide lashing faster than I have ever moved, leaking clear iron-smelling jelly, leaving a torn flap of my lower hem behind for them to tent over and drink.

That was when I found the cord.

The world’s tools do not vanish like the walls do. When a scaffold un-makes itself, the timber beams dissolve and re-stack half a mile away, and the rawhide thongs untie themselves in mid-air—but the plumb-bobs *translate*. They are reference marks for the invisible mason. When the crossbeam above me disappears, the braided reed line I am clinging to does not cease to exist; it whips sideways through the thick, steaming atmosphere like a drawn bowstring, dragging its heavy copper tear-drop across four hundred yards of empty twilight to snag instantly onto whatever new crossbeam has just sweated itself out of the fog.

It is a violent ride. No ordinary sky-ribbon stays on a plumb-line for long; the sudden lateral snap tears soft flesh apart, or flings you off into the steam like water shaken from a mop. 

To stay here, I have had to become something intermediate—neither a flat-glider nor a true ribbon. Over seasons of endless, shadowless twilight, I have drunk enough copper-salt and stray airborne lime-sweat to toughen my inner lining into a spiral grip of horny, overlapping plates, ridged precisely to match the diagonal weave of the reed rope. I am coiled around the cord just above the copper weight like the leather grip on a sword hilt, tapered at both ends so the hot wind of our sudden flights parts cleanly around me.

And unlike my pale cousins higher up in the cloud-vault, I have grown a pearl.

A sky-ribbon isn't supposed to have one; weight is death when you rely on humid updrafts to glide between disappearing towers. Mine is small—no bigger than a knucklebone—and it isn’t pure white mortar like the heavy stones the elders trade over the baskets. Because I feed on the sweat of the cord and the crust of the bob, my pearl is banded: alternating rings of grey lime-dust blown up from the shuffling courtyards and dark, metallic strata of green copper carbonate. It hangs in a tiny, fluid-filled sac near my upper taper, resting against a ring of raw, tingling nerve-hooks.

Right now, my pearl and the massive copper weight beneath my tail are in agreement. We hang dead-vertical over an endless plain of herringbone brick that gleams under an inch of poaching-hot water. Far below me, distorted by the rising curls of steam, two medium-sized floor-grazers are flattened out like spilled tallow over the seams, peacefully dissolving the fresh mortar out of the floor. The only sounds are the faint *hiss* of newly laid stone cooling to soup-temperature below and the slow, heavy *drip* of hot condensation running down my turquoise-veined flanks, gathering on the tip of the copper point beneath me, and falling eighty feet without making a sound we can hear.

Then comes the *tick*.

It doesn't travel through the broth-thick air; it comes straight down the reed cord from the crossbeam above, buzzing directly into my gripping-plates, setting my teeth—if I had teeth—on edge. 

Simultaneously, gravity goes crooked. 

Below me, the fifty-pound copper cone swings four degrees to the northwest, pointing steadily at nothing, pulled by the mass of an aqueduct or a bathhouse that hasn't arrived yet in this space, though the blueprint has already committed to it. Inside my sac, my little green-and-grey pearl rolls sideways into my nerve-ring with the exact same four-degree tilt. 

Down on the herringbone courtyard, the two red grazers instantly stop feeding. Even from eighty feet up, through the yellow haze, I can see them pump themselves upright into tall, blind listening-posts, leaning parallel to my copper bob, craning toward the northwest seam.

Here is where I do the thing that makes me neither ribbon nor elder, the small, strange meddling I discovered by accident long ago.

A plumb-bob is not passive; it is how this feverish, makerless place checks its own work. Usually, the copper weight swings to match the crooked gravity, hangs still for ten seconds while the unseen draftsmanship aligns to its point, and then the stones move. 

I do not let it hang still. 

Tightening my horny spiral grip on the braided reeds above, I slide my lower muscular skirt down over the wide, hammered shoulders of the copper weight itself. I pump fluid into my middle lobes, shifting my small, dense pearl away from the pull, and I throw my body outward in a slow, rhythmic pump—like a child working a swing, or a bell-ringer hanging on a rope. 

It takes everything I have. The copper is heavy, and the air is thick as warm oil, resisting the arc. Once, twice, three times I flex my hydraulic ribs, driving the plumb-bob out of its steady four-degree lean into a wide, lazy, elliptical pendulum-swing.

Down on the floor, the two listening grazers go rigid with confusion. Their internal pearls are suddenly rolling in slow circles inside their chests, tracking a local gravity that has begun to wobble like a spinning plate. 

Ten seconds pass. The *ticking* in the crossbeam overhead grows frantic, dry and rapid, like beetles trapped in kiln-dried timber. The scaffold wants to un-lay itself; the herringbone plain below wants to tilt and zipper open into a deep cistern. Everything is poised to shuffle—except the reference line won’t settle. The invisible mason cannot draw a straight vertical while the bob is swinging in a three-foot oval.

The air around me grows brutally hot. When you stall an error from the ground with a mallet, the bricks sweat; when you blur an error from the sky with a swinging plumb-line, the *air* sweats, trying to precipitate stone that doesn't know quite where its corners belong. 

All around my whipping cord, forty feet up in empty space, phantom mortar-milk condenses out of the yellow fog—not water, but a rich, warm, chalky drizzle stubbled with microscopic threads of phantom straw and glittering dust of un-laid tufa, hanging in a suspended, shimmering mist because the bricks meant to hold it haven't been cleared to arrive. 

I flare my upper hood wide, turning myself into an amber funnel as I ride the swinging copper weight back and forth through the suspended cloud. I don't have to scrape rough stone with acid; I don't have to fight the elders for a wedged seam. I drink the walls before they are born, skimming the warm, mineral-thick cream straight out of the confused air while my turquoise veins flush bright, electric green in the heat. Down below, the rich white drizzle hits the hot puddles in a fifty-yard circle, and the two baffled grazers immediately drop flat again, frantically slurping up the unexpected manna falling from an empty sky.

It never lasts long. By the sixth or seventh arc, an unstoppable stiffness flows down the braided reed cord—the world losing patience and clamping its invisible fingers on the line to force it true. 

I feel the grip coming half a second before it locks. Instantly, I retract my hood, spill the fluid from my outer lobes, and clamp my plated belly flat and tight against the reed rope, making myself nothing more than a slick, amber thickening on the twine.

*Snap.*

The timber bridge above us ceases to be there. There is no roar of clattering beams, only a massive, silent suck of displaced steam, and then the horizon blurs into a horizontal smear of tallow-yellow and rust-red. The plumb-line shoots sideways across the basin at forty miles an hour, slicing through the thick wet air with a low, singing *whir*, dragging me straight through the ghostly, fanned-out strata of a slate buttress that is hanging mid-shuffle in the sky—a brief, terrifying flicker of razor-sharp grey stone faces passing three inches from my flattened skin—before we jerk to a dead, silent halt.

The stinging hot wind catches up to us a second later, washing over me in a scalding sigh.

Slowly, I un-clamp my plates and peel my blind, sensitive upper rim away from the wet reeds to feel where we have been hung. 

The air is cooler by half a degree, quieter, boxed in. Extending a thin, pale feeler downward past the copper point, I sense no open plain beneath me. Instead, the plumb-bob is hanging down the exact center of a newly risen, narrow circular well-shaft of rough pale tufa, dropping straight down into the endless, dry-stone dark below the basin's skin. Above me, a single peeled log spans the well-mouth; twenty feet away out on the fresh-laid pavement, I can sense the faint, steady stillness of an untouched mortar-basket sitting in the steam.

My little green-banded pearl hangs dead center in its sac now, heavy and quiet, coated in a microscopic, fresh-laid film of tonight's stolen chalk. I let my lower skirt droop back down over the warm, sweating copper cone, licking a fresh bloom of damp verdigris from its hammered cheek while I rest. Everything around me is crisp, raw, and five seconds old, dripping softly into the black shaft below, waiting for the dry *tick* in the wood to tell us what we are going to fail to finish being next.
