To find me, you have to climb the switchbacks of the galvanized catwalk to the raised control plinth at the far end of the nave, where the three rotary pressure-exchange pumps turn at the pace of clock hands.

Look past the cast-iron housings, past the weeping brass grease-cups, to the steel instrument panel bolted to the concrete wall behind them. There is a row of four circular pressure gauges, six inches wide, cased in black-painted steel. Three of them are dry inside, their glass faces filmed only on the exterior with dull lime-dust and mist, their needles trembling slightly at 140 PSI.

The fourth gauge—the auxiliary sump-line readout on the far right—took a wrench-knock during installation. Its crimped brass bezel was bent outward by a tenth of a millimeter at the top.

For two years, every time the pneumatic relief valve vented its wet sigh into the sump below, a microscopic puff of supersaturated steam was pushed through that tiny gap into the housing. Inside the glass, the vapor cooled half a degree against the steel backplate and condensed. It could not evaporate back out. Drop by microscopic drop, an inch and a half of warm, mineral-sweet water gathered in the bottom of the dial, submerging the lower numbers—`0`, `10`, and `20`, along with the tiny black-printed words `ASHCROFT - MAX W.P.`—creating a sealed, circular tide-pool four inches across and half an inch deep, lit from the side by the amber glow of the plinth’s single sodium lamp.

At first, I am only the pale green velvet coating the submerged curve of the bezel.

Spores are small enough to ride steam through a bent seam. Cyanobacteria, a stray filament of heat-loving *Chloroflexus*, and three microscopic cysts of cave-amoeba entered on the breath of the relief valve. Because the glass face stops the carabid beetles, the woodlice, and the grazing radulae of the crickets, the inside of Gauge Four is the only unharvested pasture in the vault. Outside the glass, *lampenflora* is scraped down to a desperate smear on the concrete; inside, it grows thick, cushioned, and luxuriant, climbing halfway up the white-painted aluminum dial face in tiny, feathery turquoisestalagmites of trapped oxygen bubbles.

Wait, though. Look closer through the curved lens of condensation on the glass.

The needle of Gauge Four does not rest at zero, because the line it measures is still live—it registers the slow back-pressure of the sump. Every six seconds, when the big rotary pumps go *chug-thump*, the black iron pointer sweeps slowly upward from `15`—lifting its sharp, spade-shaped tip clear out of the little pool, trailing a bright silver bead of water—pauses at `35` in the humid upper air of the dial, and then sinks smoothly back beneath the meniscus.

Hanging from the underside of that rising and falling needle is what I have actually become.

It started as a stray, wind-blown fragment of bryozoan statoblast—a freshwater moss-animal washed down from a municipal reservoir pipe—or perhaps a colonial rotifer cyst stuck to a technician's glove when the bezel was struck. In open water, the mosquito larvae or the mustard-seed hatchlings on the floor would have swallowed it in an afternoon. Inside the gauge, with warm water, constant light, zero predators, and a mechanical tide driven by the city’s geothermal grid, the colony did not stay a microscopic speck.

I am a translucent, amber-tinted chandelier of linked, gelatinous tubes, clinging to the black enamel of the gauge needle from its submerged tip partway toward its central brass pin.

Every six seconds, my world turns over. 

When the pump strokes and the needle swings down into the water at `15`, three thousand microscopic, bell-shaped mouths unfurl simultaneously along the underside of the pointer like a sudden puff of pale smoke. Each tiny crown of cilia beats in coordinated waves, pulling a miniature vortex through the bottom of the dial, sweeping up free-floating green cells, bacterial flocs, and tiny flakes of black enamel peeling off the submerged numbers. For three seconds, I am fully aquatic, weightless, feeding in the warm green soup over the `10`.

Then the rotary pump completes its stroke. *Chug-thump.*

Pressure climbs. The Bourdon tube behind the dial flexes, the central pinion turns, and the black iron needle lifts smoothly out of the water toward `35`. 

All three thousand crowns snap shut in a fraction of a second, tucking inside their stiff, chitinous collars. As the needle breaks the surface tension, a thin, shining skin of water stays trapped among my branching tubes, held there by capillary action. Up in the hot, misty pocket of air beneath the top of the dial—where the `60` and the `80` sit dry—the colony breathes. Oxygen, scarce in forty-five-degree water, diffuses rapidly through that micron-thin film of liquid directly into our tissues, flushing out carbon dioxide before the sump bleeds off and the needle dips us back into the broth to feed again.

I am half machine-part, half lung, using a municipal pressure-exchange pump as my diaphragm.

My body has grown to balance the instrument. Early on, when the colony grew too thick near the tip of the pointer, the tiny brass hairspring behind the dial couldn’t quite pull the needle all the way up to `35`; it lagged at `31`, heavy with jelly and lime-crust. The water down at that angle was slightly less stirred, so the polyps at the tip starved back by two millimeters until the weight matched the spring tension again. Now I am tuned to the gauge’s calibration. I am four grams of pale, branching flesh that knows the exact torque of a coiled bronze tube.

Other things have found my glass window, though they cannot come inside.

Every few hours, one of the translucent house geckos patrols across the warm steel of the instrument panel. When it crosses the face of Gauge Four, its pale, lamellae-ridged toes press flat against the outside of the glass. Looking up from the submerged `15`, I register the sudden, immense eclipse in the amber light—a five-toed shadow blotting out the sodium bulb, causing every polyp along the needle to flinch inward for one pump-stroke before resuming. 

Once a week or so, one of the forearm-length flatworms from Pier Six slides over the control plinth, drawn by the warmth of the pump bearings. Its grease-slicked belly smears right across the dial face. For ten minutes, the world outside turns tallow-white and murky pink; I can dimly sense the rhythmic pulsing of its lateral wine-colored vessels pressed separated from me by two millimeters of tempered glass, its blunt barbels tasting the tiny wisp of warm, algal-scented vapor escaping through the bent brass crimp at twelve o'clock. It lingers there, puzzled by the smell of rich green life it cannot vacuum up, grinding its chalky throat-plates once in frustration before sliding down toward the sump grates, leaving an oily smear across the glass that dims my light for three days until condensation washes it clean.

I am growing a skeleton of my own, down at the base of the pool.

As generations of zooids bud along the rising needle and older ones die back, their tiny, calcified casings drop off into the curve of the bezel at six o'clock. Mixed with rust from the steel backplate and calcium from the steam, they are slowly cementing into a miniature, crescent-shaped reef half an inch tall, burying the `0` and creeping steadily up toward the `10`. 

In another year, if no technician comes down to spin the bulkhead wheel dogs and tap the glass with a gloved knuckle, the reef at the bottom will grow high enough to touch the lowest arc of the needle’s swing. When that happens, the colony on the pointer will bridge across to the floor of the dial, spinning tough, mucilaginous threads that will bind the needle fast at `15`—catching the heartbeat of the pump and holding it still inside our tiny, glowing room, while outside in the vast, dark nave, the pipes roar on and the snow of dead wings keeps falling.
