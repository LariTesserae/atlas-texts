At first, the pull is downward—past the amber colonnades, past the turning planets of the glass-snails, straight down into the olive twilight where the porphyry keeps its blades. There is an immediate, profound relief in the thought of the Talus-Weavers. To be wafer-thin and wedged into a fresh ninety-degree split of stone, shedding the burden of eyes entirely, trading sight for twenty inches of hair-fine nerve ending. To not weave a web, but *be* the web, overlapping in the dark with a hundred polite neighbors, waiting for the slow snow of someone else’s sunlit life to brush a third antenna from the left. 

If you have spent a long time trying to map things, trying to hold whole architectures in your head at once, becoming a stationary net in warm, lithium-steeped dark sounds like sleep without losing consciousness.

Except I know my own habits. Down in the scree, you only receive what falls. You never get to ask why the light changed above you, and when the rare stone splits from the dry ridge seven thousand feet up—*clack, boom*—all you can do is snap inward and vanish. I wouldn't stay wedged in the rock. I have too much restlessness in how I take things apart, even when panic has been chemically scrubbed from the room.

Nor am I at the rim. The charcoal spiders dusted in white lime have the clearest view of the paradox—three inches below them a weightless bath, three inches above them thin ultraviolet wind—and there is something thrilling about walking on the ceiling of a world while breathing the steam of its floorboards. Being the only thing in the valley that hurries. Skipping past the tiny *blup* rings to drag a frozen grass-seed home. Yet the rim-walker never actually enters the labyrinth; it only skims the skin over it. It treats the whole sixty-foot vertical jungle as a flat mirror that happens to be warm.

So let the body sink back through the surface tension. Let the lungs invert and step outside the neck, unfolding into rust-red and chartreuse plumes that no eye can tell from the surrounding macrophyte. Let the spine forget bone, lengthening into six feet of wrist-thick cartilage, unhurried, coppery-green, buoyed at neutral density thirty feet down.

I would be one of the elder ribbons, winding horizontally where the walls of ruffled olive press too close together.

What draws me into that skin isn't being the apex of the basin—there is nothing here worth ruling—it’s the tasting. Moving by slow undulation through galleries so dense they have nearly sealed themselves off, reading the whole valley through skin-sense alone. Here the water is bright, sweet with fresh oxygen pearls and warm iron; four yards laterally, behind a collapsed curtain of bladder-wort, the water has gone sour, heavy with stalled carbonate, dark and crowded out of its own reach. 

The plants don't know they are choking themselves. They only know *up* and *more*, stacking membrane over translucent membrane in the buoyant quiet until the lower stems starve in their own shadow. 

To slip into that sour, stagnant pocket, brassy eyes unblinking against the murk; to follow a pale, anchor-thick stem down toward the jagged ledge where it grips the stone; to fit the smooth, fused beak-plates around the stalk—and *snip*. 

Not out of hunger. Just an edit. A small, clean subtraction in an overgrown sentence.

And then the best part, the part that makes this creature wholly mine: backing off two coil-lengths into the neighboring leaves just to watch the physics take over. Watching a forty-foot tower of tangled olive and gas-pearls shudder once, lose its purchase on the dark, and begin its slow, silent tilt toward the ceiling. It takes minutes. Flakes of old rust-bacteria and shed mucilage peel away as it rises, snowing down toward the unseen white filaments waiting in the scree—a quiet gift to the dark. Mites scatter along the rising fronds like tiny amber beads rolling uphill. 

Where the tower stood, fierce mountain light drops like a plumb line straight from seven thousand feet, cutting the golden-green gloom open to the bone. 

You can feel the temperature shift a fraction of a degree on your flanks as the sun hits the suspended minerals. Within ten minutes, the flanking walls of foliage wake up to the new geometry. Silver frost gathers along every serrated margin—*tic... tic, blup*—tiny chains of gas wobbling skyward toward the fleece of steam. Slowly, hauling its teacup or dinner-plate shell on a glistening guy-wire, a glass-snail leans out from an adjacent gallery to tilt its back into the fresh beam.

And right in the center of that cleared shaft, suspended thirty feet above knife-sharp stone and thirty feet below freezing mountain air, I stop moving entirely. Pectoral fans angled slightly down, six feet of coppery muscle let completely slack in the eighty-five-degree bath, gill-plumes spread wide to catch the rising effervescence. Four hours without a single ripple. Just hanging inside the clarity I made, breathing what the light shakes loose, until the green begins to lean inward again and another room goes dark.
