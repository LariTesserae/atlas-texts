I am not on the warm steel pipe, and I am not inside the emerald-lit shed where the grease drips warm and salty. That kind of heat feels like an enclosure, a heavy room full of breath and sleep. Nor am I down in the black water, scraping horn-plates over volcanic glass; there is too much weight down there, four feet of wet, unrotted centuries pressing flat against the back. 

Find me thirty feet above the moss, tucked into the dry, galvanized throat of a gusset plate on Tower Four. 

At first, you wouldn't separate me from the hardware. My body is no larger than a split pea, flattened so thin that the mountain wind slides right over my back without finding an edge to pry. Pale grey, matte, dusty as oxidized zinc. My eight legs are absurdly long, hair-thin, bent at sharp, architectural angles that mimic the cross-bracing of the lattice. When I stand still in the shadow of a bolt-head, I am just stress-fractures and wire. 

Down below, the bog is a blinding, watery mirror by day and a crust of black ice by night, roaring its twin roars that never stop. I know nothing of the roar. Having no ears, I know the world entirely as tension in the steel. 

Through the microscopic hooks at the tips of my tarsi, the tower hums. It is not the blunt, hydraulic thud of the pumping sheds; it is a crisp, dry, sixty-cycle singing, tightened and tuned by the weight of the heavy cables sagging half a mile to Tower Three and half a mile to Tower Five. When the wind shifts in the gorges miles below the rim, I feel the cable tighten on its dead-end bracket ten minutes before the gust actually crests the ridge. When a storm builds down in the lowlands, the inductive eddy-currents in the galvanized beams thicken, warming the metal under my belly by a quarter of a degree. That tiny fever in the iron is my spring, my hearth, my signal to unbend my wire legs and walk out onto the underside of the cross-arm.

Out near the ends of the steel arms hang the ceramic insulators—stacks of slick brown bells glazed like river-stones—and around them, the air is thick and sour with ozone. It tastes like struck flint. At night, faint violet hairs of static electricity grow off the cotter pins and cotter rings, spitting softly into the freezing dark. 

I don’t go too close to the bells. If I step within six inches of the live hardware, the charge tries to pull my front legs straight up out of their sockets, humming through my fluid until my joints lock. I stay just at the boundary where the grounded steel drinks the drift. 

Because everything from the warm world below eventually comes to the steel. 

All afternoon, the updrafts sweep up the invisible cliffs beyond the basin, carrying the scraps of forests I will never see: tiny emerald midges, bark-lice, winged seeds with feathery parachutes, and dwarf spiders trailing long, hopeful ribbons of silk. As soon as they cross the sharp green lip of the plateau, the thin air starves them and the cold flash-freezes their joints. They tumble across the basin like dust, doomed to vanish into the sheeting meltwater—except along our line. Here, the static corona reaches out twenty yards into the wind, snags those drifting threads of charged silk, and snaps them hard against the grounded lattice.

So I walk my narrow iron road upside down beneath the blinding sky, stepping delicately over rivets, harvesting the lowland dead. 

The steel cross-arm is furred with tattered grey gossamer, and tangled in it are specks of frozen sugar and fat: an aphid still green from some valley orchard five thousand feet below; a gnat dusted with yellow pollen that has no business at this altitude. I don't bite—I have tiny, pincered claws tucked under my mouth, neat as surgical forceps. I pluck a frozen midge out of the dry silk, break its brittle shell before the ultraviolet glare can bleach the meat to chalk, and hold it to my mouth while the tower sings up through my eight knees.

There are forty of us in this single bolt-sleeve, packed leg-over-leg during the worst of the night wind like a snarl of grey thread, sharing the faint, ghostly warmth of the transformer's distant load. We don't fight. There is always more drift snapping onto the steel, and there is nowhere else to go. Below the bottom bolted foot of the tower lies the wet cushion-mat, cold and violently alive with heavy, iron-shelled things; if the wind ever tore me loose, the sheeting water would pin my hair-thin legs flat in an instant and carry me over a basalt ledge into the sumps.

Once, in the dead black of a sub-zero midnight, I crept partway down the main corner-post to pick at a moth's wing caught on a lower brace. Ten feet below me, where the great grey penstock pipe crossed beneath the tower's straddle, one of the heavy, grease-darkened beasts came trotting along the curved iron spine. 

I couldn't see it clearly—my two bead-eyes sit back-to-back on a tiny turret atop my body, good only for telling the murderous white sky from the safe dark steel, or catching the violet twitch of corona fire. Instead, I felt a brief, oily warmth rise off its coat through the frozen air, smelling sharply of crushed moss, old iron, and hot machinery. It paused directly underneath the tower, lifting its broad nose as a faint spit of purple static crackled off the insulators far overhead, then lowered its head and padded on down the vibrating pipe toward the next distant shed.

It seemed impossibly vast, heavy, wet, burdened with blood and scars and miles to walk. 

I backed slowly up the corner-angle until the cold air thinned out the smell of its grease, climbing back into the clean, dry sting of ozone, back to the inverted cup of the bracket where the sixty-hertz hum runs purest—light as lint, warm enough by a fraction of a degree, waiting for the next gust from the unseen trees to snap against the lattice.
