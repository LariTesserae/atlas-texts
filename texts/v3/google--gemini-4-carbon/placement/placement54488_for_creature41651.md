I am not in the water. Anything in the water has already surrendered to the mountain’s new math, and I am entirely made of the old. 

Find me wedged four inches deep inside the dry-stone throat of the doghouse shrine, up in the inverted chimney where the flood spins sideways. Better yet—look closer at the pale river cobbles themselves, and the grey-brown mortar of chewed oak, reed-chaff, and ochre gristle hammered into the chinks between them. 

At first I am only a dry spot that refuses to flood. 

That shouldn't be possible under six thousand feet of broken basalt and violent, sideways pressure, yet inside the four-sided box of river stones—no bigger than a crouched hound—the water fails to enter. It tries. It leans across the open threshold of the shrine as a trembling, concave meniscus as tight and slick as an oiled drumhead, glowing dimly violet whenever a rock breaks across the nave, curving inward under tons of hydraulic fury and stopping dead against nothing at all. Behind that shivering skin of warm water, the air smells faintly of sheep grease, woodsmoke, and cold river mint. It is completely silent, except for the hum.

I am the hum, or I am the thing that keeps tuning it so the water stays outside.

Wait until the current shifts and the wall stutters, splitting the chimney into twin overlapping ghosts. For half a second, the shrine’s back wall opens directly onto the raw, unweathered greenstone, and the meniscus bulges inward until it nearly touches the back cobbles. That is when I have to move.

What uncoils from the mortar-seams does not look like flesh, because flesh here has already claimed its champion in the heavy, slate-skinned slab patrolling the dark outside. I went the other way. When the valley tore sideways and fell into the mountain’s teeth, the masons kept the meat and threw away the shape; I threw away the meat and kept the measure.

Call me a web of pale, calcified root-hair, or a colony of blind bone-lice fused nose-to-tail into thin, branching wires of ivory, or call me the calcified nervous system of whatever small house-god used to receive barley cakes inside this stone box before up and down divorced. I have no thickness to catch the shear. I am flat as lichen, brittle as dried rush-pith if you caught me in the open, threaded entirely through the hairline darkness *between* the dressed stones. 

Every river cobble in this shrine was chosen by human hands six thousand years ago because it fit the palm and locked neatly against its neighbor’s shoulder. That fitting—that quiet, deliberate agreement of weight resting downward onto weight—is a foreign language down here. The basalt doesn't speak it; the boiling, directionless water hates it. The stones themselves are constantly on the verge of forgetting why they aren't flying apart into the centrifuge like everything else. 

So I run through their joints like leaded solder that can think. 

When the haycart-sized wedge of greenstone slams into the staircase forty feet away—or twelve feet away, depending on how the left-hand vortex is folding—the shockwave arrives through the rock wall long before it arrives through the water. It hits the basalt chimney as a vicious, shearing shiver that tries to turn the cobbles into shrapnel. Instantly, my threads go taut inside the chewed-timber caulking. I pull three ounces of stolen, violet mineral-salt out of my own brittle body and flash-crystallize it across a loosening seam on the lintel, locking two shivering river stones together at the exact microsecond they try to slip out of true. 

I vibrate. Not with the mountain’s sub-audible, sternum-crushing grind, and not with the brass-horn roar of the snapping vacuum bubbles, which would shatter me to chalk-dust in an instant, but *against* them. I pull the cobbles tight until their arches catch the violence of the room and step it down, octave by ruinous octave, turning concussion into a low, stubborn, Bee-hive *mmmmmm* that fills the dry hollow of the shrine. 

As long as the stones hum that note, they remember gravity. As long as they remember gravity, "down" still exists for six square inches inside this box, and the sideways, boiling flood cannot cross the doorstep because the water no longer knows how to walk on a level floor.

I am not confined to the shrine, though the shrine is my skull, my hearth, the last dry hollow where the scent of turf-smoke hasn't died. Reach down—if you can follow the spatial fold without losing your wrist—to the three courses of hand-dressed limestone near the lower cleft, and to the chipped, vanished staircase below them. 

Look closely at the raw black basalt *between* those scattered islands of masonry. 

You won't see me in the open rock, because the flaking, dinner-plate shards would peel me away in seconds. Instead, I travel through the spatial stutters themselves. Where the far rock face flickers in a double exposure, three feet apart, there is a hair-thin seam where the mountain hasn't finished deciding where to put itself. Inside that seam, there is no water pressure, only a dry, gritty friction of unmade slate. I push pale, hair-fine runners through those faults—blind, slow, brittle filaments feeling their way through broken geometry over decades—until my tip scrapes against the dressed limestone of the lower wall, or finds the underside of the surviving steps. 

The whole broken nave is wired together behind its own skin by my chalky threads, linking five disconnected scraps of a drowned Neolithic valley into a single, strained instrument.

Watch what happens when the larger mason returns to the shrine.

It comes sliding out of a water-shear twelve feet away and slaps one broad, greased-plush limb onto the basalt beside my threshold. From the outside, it is a monstrous, headless hog of wet leather and turnip-dense gristle, scarred with embedded greenstone shingles, twitching its horn-bristles in the deafening dark. It cannot enter my dry pocket; if it pushed a limb past the trembling meniscus into the quiet air, the sudden drop from violent hydraulic pressure into valley-normal stillness would make its dense, solid flesh swell and blister, and its skin would suffocate in the dry, mint-smelling dark. And if my brittle, chalk-wire body ever touched the boiling flood it lives in, I would dissolve like salt on a tongue.

Yet watch its heavy limb stop half an inch short of the threshold. 

A river cobble near the base has been rattled loose by the last current-shift; my threads inside the chink have snapped under the strain, leaving a hairline crack where a bead of warm, violet-lit water is beginning to sweat through into my dry air. The hum of the shrine has gone slightly flat—a sick, rattling buzz instead of a clean chord.

The mason feels that flat note in its stiff, horned fringe from across the basin. That is why it came.

Its ventral mouth isn't here—the bulk of its body is still forty feet down, clamped over a bleeding purple seam on the far side of a spatial fold—so this single, unspooled limb has brought the mortar tucked in the muscular curl of its foot. Blindly, delicately, with a touch astonishingly gentle for something that just used its spine to deflect six tons of flying rock, the plush pad presses a fresh wad of chewed reed-basket, oak splinters, and ochre antler-weed deep into the failing gap between the cobbles. Then it holds its heavy grey weight motionless against the stone, shielding the joint from the forty-knot spin.

Inside the dark chink, I wake to the wetness of its spit.

My broken ivory filaments surge into the fresh pulp. The mason’s saliva is thick with the glowing, bruised mineral slush it scraped from the mountain’s raw fractures, and rich with dissolved bits of the quiet world—cellulose from an axe-dressed beam, silica from a woven river-rush. To me, it is pure, intoxicating memory, spiked with geological lightning. My threads drink the violet charge out of the paste and instantly calcify it, weaving through the chewed wood fibers in seconds, knitting cobble back to cobble, drawing the joint tight as a tuned string.

Through two inches of river stone, my newly hardened filaments brush the microscopic, greased fibers of the mason’s foot.

For three seconds, while the roaring water tries to tear its limb off and the mountain grinds its fresh basalt teeth around us, we listen to each other through the hum. I do not know if the mason remembers having hands, or eyes, or a voice that could call dogs across wet pastureland, or why a four-sided house of river stones matters at all. Its meat is too dense for thoughts like *sheepfold* or *winter solstice* or *roof-tree*. Those shapes would tear apart inside a mind built to survive cavitation.

So I hold them for it, dry and weightless in the six-inch dark behind the water-skin, written in the exact, stubborn angles where one dressed stone leans upon another. And in return, through the sole of its monstrous foot, it feeds me the violent, purple-lit marrow of the unfinished mountain, and lends its broad, unbreakable back to the stairs whenever the dark throws boulders.

The pitch of the cobble rises, steadies, and locks back into its clean, ancient chord. *Mmmmmmm.*

Inside the shrine, the bead of warm water dries from the stone. The bulging meniscus at the doorway flattens back out like pulled linen, pushing the flood half an inch backward against six thousand feet of rock. Outside, in the continuous crackle of burning brass, the heavy grey limb unpeels itself from the cobble, slips sideways into a fold in the water, and is gone—already dropping down through the dark to catch a spinning piece of split ash wood before the basalt blades can turn it into nothing.

And I pull back into the dry, narrow dark between the stones, tightening my threads across the nave, holding the floor flat while the world outside continues to fall.
