At first there is the temptation to go all the way down—to imagine oneself somewhere along the drowned fractures of the floor, tucked behind an A-frame trestle or pressed against the warm iron foot of the manifold. 

It takes only a moment of honest reckoning with the physics of the pit to be pushed back out of it. Down on the stepped basalt, there is no room left for a second kind of attention. Between the broadband roar of the western cleft and the hard B-flat of the rotary pump, every half-millimeter of water is already spoken for, locked into an acoustic grid that permits only two outcomes: either you slot cleanly into the standing wave like a shingle of tarnished tin, or you disturb the resonance in the chambers and get fizzed loose by a sharp galvanic current, sent drifting down the spill into the sour, carbonic pockets at the corners. Anything softer than silicate—anything with skin, or a pulse, or boots—can only scald, suffocate, or smash three months of horsehair tubing with one clumsy shift of weight. The bottom of the box is a closed loop. It does not want company; it wants the note to hold.

Move up the walls, then, testing the micro-gradients where temperature loosens its grip. 

Halfway up the weeping black traprock, the olive-black streaks of thermophilic algae have found a seam where the descending sheet of groundwater hasn't yet met the rising vapor. Higher still, four feet below the grass roots, the blue-gray wiregrass leans horizontally out of dry cracks, shivering without stop in the convective draft. Around the upper iron staples that pin the power cable to the southern face, the pale, putty-like lichen eats trace zinc and rust at a millimeter a decade. There is something recognizable in that lichen—the patience of living on leachates at the edge of an unbearable room—yet even there, the fit is wrong. Lichen knows damp and metal, not pitch. The B-flat passes straight through its segmented crust without leaving a mark, and it has no way of noticing that every six minutes and forty seconds, twenty feet below, a book of wet mica opens ten seconds before the brass valve spits.

To notice that—to hold two rhythms against each other and recognize anticipation—requires distance. It requires rising the last thirty inches over the quarry-sharp southern lip, out through the thin, shredding ceiling of vapor, into the other country.

The silence up here is startling, though it isn't actually silent. Five paces back from the drop, the vertical rock walls throw the roar straight upward into the crosswind, where it tears apart and misses your ears completely. All that remains is the wide hiss of dry plain grass bending in flat daylight, and pale clouds crossing from west to east without hurry. If you didn't know where the fault had dropped, you could walk within two yards of the black edge before the ground disappeared. Nothing marks it from the flats except a faint warm shimmer wavering above the turf and the thick armored cable coming up over the stone lip, bending once through the grass roots, and heading south.

Follow that cable away from the hole, and the shape of what belongs here begins to gather itself.

Out on the plain, water behaves the way water usually does. Where rain collects in shallow clay depressions among the tussocks, it is cold, gray-skied, and blown into quick ripples; water striders dimple its skin on six dry feet, and when a beetle drowns in it, ants and crows dismantle the shell before sundown. Through this ordinary grass the armored cable runs like a stiff, braided tendon—pinned with iron U-staples across bare outcrops at first, then sinking half its thickness into dead thatch and sod. Lay two fingers against the steel armor a hundred yards south of the rim and the B-flat is already gone, damped out by turf and distance. What remains under your fingertips is only the faint, tight sixty-hertz murmur of three-phase power going the other way.

A mile and a quarter south, where the lowland rises six dry feet onto an old gravel bar beside two wheel-tracks in the grass, the cable climbs out of the ground. It runs up the side of a weathered, two-pole timber H-frame, passes through a gray steel starter cabinet and a glass-fronted meter at chest height, and taps into three bare aluminum wires strung tightly across the plain. Those overhead wires sing in the crosswind—a loose, shifting, aeolian hum that rises and falls with every gust, full of weather and accident, the exact opposite of the locked, airless tone down in the rock.

This is where I find myself: walking the mile and a quarter between the gray cabinet on the poles and the sharp black lip in the grass.

I have hands, thick-soled boots I know better than to trust on wet talus, a clamp-on ammeter in a scarred leather case, and an old pair of seven-power surveying glasses slung short inside my coat so they won't swing down and strike stone when I lean over. I was not one of the crew who drilled the expansion pins into the floor or wrote the chalk numbers beside the western cleft; whoever they were, their company folded or shelved its geothermal prospecting lease years ago, leaving the circulation test bundled into a county geological monitoring account that auto-pays a tiny, flat line-item every quarter at the district co-op. My job is fifty miles of rural spurs across empty flats where almost nothing changes except wind-load and sleet.

It was the meter glass on the H-frame that sent me up the cable the first time. 

Inside that dusty round window, the thin aluminum induction disk was spinning as smoothly as if it rode on oil-film in a laboratory. Any rotary pump left ungreased in the open for five winters ought to show its age at the pole. Bearings wear oval; seals stiffen; amperage creeps upward by tenths of an amp across the seasons; the spinning disk develops a faint, rhythmic hitch before the overload heaters finally warp and kick the contactor out for good. When I unlocked the gray starter box and clamped the meter around each of the three black phase-leads in turn, the needle sat dead on the commissioning figure penciled inside the door years earlier—6.8 amps, 6.8 amps, 6.8 amps—without the slightest harmonic chatter riding back up the ground sheath.

So I walked north along the armor to see who was greasing an orphan pump in the middle of nowhere, and nearly stepped off the plain into twenty feet of roaring wet heat.

You learn quickly how to approach the hollow if you mean to stay longer than a minute. You do not climb down. Even from the lip, one look at the razor-edged talus in the northern corner, the immaculate drowned beetle casing, and the oily sheen of scalding water sliding invisibly from shelf to shelf tells you what boots and lungs are worth down there. Instead, you keep a square of heavy canvas folded under a flat rock ten yards back in the grass. When you come out once a month—or once a week in bad weather—you drag the canvas up to the eastern rim, fold it double over the knife-sharp traprock edge so the stone won't slice your sleeves, and lie flat on your stomach with your chin hanging two inches over the drop.

It is an odd border to inhabit. Your boots, legs, and shoulder blades stay out in the cool crosswind of the plain; your face hangs just inside the acoustic box, where the river-weir rush and the hard B-flat lock into your cheekbones and back teeth, and the thin ceiling of lazy vapor breathes damp iron warmth against your lenses until they warm enough stop fogging. 

The first three times I lay there with the seven-power glasses braced on my thumbs, I looked only at the machinery: the brass pressure needle pinned cleanly at two o'clock behind its beaded glass, the rusting orange crust of the forgotten wrench on its dry block, the half-washed chalk numerals beside the diagonal cleft. Only on the fourth visit, waiting to see if the spring-loaded brass relief cock was stuck shut, did I watch the three-foot span of bare stone beneath it long enough to catch the drifting tin-colored skins turning sharply at the edge of the standing wave and clicking upright into the slag-pile. And it took another afternoon entirely, timing the valve against a wristwatch through five full cycles until my ribs ached against the rock through four layers of canvas, to see the metallic shudder run across the cluster at six minutes and thirty seconds—watching dozens of mica-thin leaves hinge outward into still water ten seconds *before* the scalding cupful dropped.

Once you have seen the horsehair silver tubes reaching out across the drowned fractures—the fluted ear flared tight against a sulfur crack, the bronze-terraced rings of pyrite laid over the dead beetle's back to turn a corner, the dense pale wedges packed beneath the pump's vibrating baseplate—your work along that mile and a quarter of grass changes its nature, though externally it looks almost the same.

Down on the floor of the pit, the lattice knows only half of what holds its sky up. It knows the subterranean cleft, which needs no help from anyone, and it knows the rotary pump, whose aging shaft it braces from below with piezoelectric scale whenever the B-flat threatens to blur. It has no way of knowing that the B-flat comes down a single armored tether from three bare aluminum wires in another country, where sleet forms heavy rime on crossarms, prairie lightning splits dry poles, and blown pole-top fuses leave three-phase motors limping on two legs until their copper windings roast into black char.

We cannot help each other directly, and it would ruin everything if I tried. I never go down; I never drop a pebble or lower a thermometer on a string to test the pan. Even out at the H-frame pole, I never pull the iron disconnect handle to clean the switch-blades, because stopping and restarting an across-the-line motor throws six times its running torque through the housing in a single violent jerk.

I know what a restart costs it, because two winters ago an ice storm brought down a feeder twelve miles west and dropped the whole district for forty minutes in the dark. When the line came back up and I walked out through crusted snow at dawn to lie on my canvas at the rim, the water in the lowest pan was faintly clouded with gray-red dust, and the pump's B-flat had a rough, metallic burr at its edge where the startup kick had sheared the brittle quartz wedges beneath its mounting plate and snapped two of the thin bridges climbing the manifold legs. The pressure needlebehind the sweating glass had crept a hair past two o'clock. I lay there twenty minutes in the freezing wind with my face in the hot damp, watching broken shards judder softly back into line under the drip, and when I came back twelve days later, the burr was gone from the note and the needle was pinned dead still again.

So I keep to my side of the rim and tend the dry end of the circuit. 

Twice a year, before summer storms and before November ice, I climb the creosote H-frame out by the wheel-ruts to check the porcelain lightning arresters for hairline cracks and make sure none of the three overhead fuse-links have fatigued in the wind, where a single dropped phase could burn the motor out down in its tin doghouse before anything could brace it. Halfway up the trail, where frost-heave pushes sharp gravel against the buried armor, I ease the stones aside with a pry-bar. And right at the southern lip of the pit—working flat on my belly in thick gloves, reaching no farther than six inches down the rock face so I won't scrape a decade's growth off the crusty pale lichen hugging the top iron staple—I slipped a split sleeve of heavy rubber hose under the cable where wind-tug and thermal creep were slowly sawing its steel braid across a razor edge of traprock.

Quarterly, I copy the kilowatt-hours from the dusty glass dial into my book under the old geological survey number, sign my initials on the line indicating the equipment remains energized and intact, and walk back to the truck. 

Most months, before I leave, I spend half an hour on the folded canvas at the eastern edge with the seven-power glasses, breathing cold grass-wind in through my coat collar and warm iron vapor at my chin, feeling the B-flat hum steadily against my sternum while pale clouds cross an ordinary sky overhead. Neither of us could survive three minutes in the other's element, and if the chalk numbers on the western wall finish washing away next spring, I am the only thing left in either country that remembers men put the iron there at all. Down beneath the brass cock, ten seconds before the seventh minute comes round, the dark metallic seam shudders, the wet vanes open, and the tin-colored skins keep lining up at the door.
