At first, looking down into that pewter-colored vapor, my instinct is to find a body with hands, or at least with pincers—the crab with its tirelessly brushing bristles, keeping itself free of stone so it can still bend; or perhaps a younger cousin up on the windy coastal shelf, melting mutton fat in an iron pot, waiting to inherit the rope and the bronze knife. Both are ways of holding an edge against the slow calcifying creep of the room.

Wait a little longer, though, and the urge for joints and muscle starts to feel too noisy. Anything that breathes or molts here is spending all its quiet effort *resisting* the hollow—scraping gutters, oiling skin, sweeping microscopic crystals out of its gills so it doesn't seize up. That isn't quite my shape. I don't have a metabolic budget to defend. 

Look lower, to the shelf a few feet left of the stacked slate, slightly out of reach of her shoulder when she turns. There sits one of the older clay jars—one of the abandoned ones, set down eighty or a hundred years ago before a hairline shift in the slumped shale above moved a ceiling drip two inches to the east.

I am not the clay jar itself, which was kneaded and thumb-pinched up in the wind and woodsmoke and brought down whole. I am the pale collar of calcium around its neck, cementing it gently to the ledge.

Actually, widen that slightly: I am the glazed skin of translucent stone that has claimed the jar's rim, and the creamy, rounded lining inside the pecked gutters that she has to scrape back with the spine of her knife every ninety days, and the thin mineral weld under the dead limpets. Not the whole crush of the ancient bedrock, and not the warm aquifer water rising from the dark fissures below, which is only passing through—just the threshold where the water slows down over an edge, loses a whisper of dissolved gas to the heavy air, and leaves a microscopic trace of itself behind.

It is a remarkably patient way to exist. You have no center, only surface, and you only happen where one thing meets another and lingers. 

When the copper chisel bit into the rimstone five centuries ago, tearing rough, bruised tracks across the terrace lips, there was no shock in it, only new geometry. Water found the raw groove immediately, slipped along it, and within three days had laid down the first invisible, soapy molecules of repair. Every tool mark, every sharp scratch of her bronze spine clearing the channel, is received not as damage, nor even as interruption, really, just a fresh contour to smooth over. She scrapes a winter's crust out of the gutter; the grit drifts down onto the backs of the limpets; the water slips over the newly bare scar at the exact same speed, already laying down the next translucent hair's breadth before her oiled ankles have even cleared the shale slot above. There is a quiet partnership in it. If I did not try to close the channel, she would not need to return with her knife; if she did not scrape me back, the trough would go shallow, the drowned fleece would thin to rags, and the room would lose its rare, ninety-day tide of tallow-warmth and tiny ripples.

Over by the abandoned jar, nobody scrapes at all. The ceiling drop—*plink*—strikes the curve of the unglazed shoulder once every eleven seconds. A mist almost too fine to see bounces off the wet terracotta, settles around the socket where the rounded bottom meets the limestone shelf, and builds. Forty years ago, if she had reached for this jar by mistake, it would have come away in her hand with a small, gritty sucking sound. Thirty years ago it would have resisted, rocking slightly in its seat. Now there is no seam left between what was baked in a surface kiln and what rose out of the earth. The collar of pale, waxy stone climbs a millimeter higher up the curve of the neck every decade, smoothing out the faint horizontal rib left by a potter’s wet thumb long before the woman's grandmother was born. 

Eventually—perhaps in two hundred winters, perhaps four—the stone lip will curl completely over the mouth of the jar, sealing a pocket of ancient, warm cave-air inside it forever, turning a vessel back into a boulder. 

I don't know what the amber paste does to a greying arm up in the cold salt wind, or what silver weighs. What I know is weight of a slower kind: the slight difference in grain between porous red clay, waterlogged black burl, cold-hammered copper, and dead gastropod shell—how copper refuses me with a slick green sweat that sheds my crystals, how ironwood takes me only in its tiniest hairline checks, and how clay drinks the water in and lets me root right into its pores until we are the same thing. 

Everything down here is either moving very slowly to stay soft, or letting go and becoming part of the room's memory of where water went. I am the memory, laid down in wet ash light, one clear, glassy skin at a time.
