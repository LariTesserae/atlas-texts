I am twenty-five feet up, bolted to the blind flank of Tank 14, behind a heavy gasketed door that weeps a slow, permanent beard of grey mold around its bottom seal.

Not the man who pushes the door open. I am the room he steps into. Or rather, I am the cold rack bolted to the back bulkhead of the room, humming steadily at fifty-eight degrees Fahrenheit while the rest of the basin sweats at a hundred and two.

If you pulled my front louvres off—nobody has in eleven years, though the quarter-turn latches are greased with grey silicone so they haven't seized—you wouldn't find a brain. Just forty-year-old logic boards coated in thick amber conformal resin to keep the salt damp off the solder joints, a rows of dull green indicator LEDs that nobody looks at, and a bundle of gray-jacketed sensor leads running out through a neoprene grommet in the floor. 

Out there, my nerves lie buried in the wet. 

They run along the undersides of the galvanized catwalks, strapped to the steel with UV-rotted zip ties that have been replaced one by one with twists of stainless baling wire. Every hundred yards, one of my leads drops down a concrete piling into the hot dark water or disappears under the aluminum lagging of a steam main. I do not have eyes for the amber mist or the cormorants, though I know when five hundred birds land at once on the West Gantry boom because the strain gauge on the footing spikes by two-tenths of a percent and stays there until dawn.

I know the basin as resistance, pressure, and heat.

mostly heat, and flow. Loop 4B is sluggish right now. The differential across the secondary intake screen has crept up from four pounds to nine since Tuesday. That is the mussels. I cannot see their pale shells wedging into the chain-link down in the murky green dark, nor the gray bryozoan jelly knitting over the gaps between them, but I feel the submerged pump working harder behind them—its amperage drawing a slow, upward curve on my silent internal ledger, the bronze impeller beginning to cavitate in tiny, violent implosions that shiver up the thermocouple wire like a rough pulse.

I do not panic about Loop 4B. I have a set point. When the differential hits twelve pounds, I will close a dry contact on Relay 9. Up in the corner of my blister, above the stacked flats of sardine tins and the coil of frayed fiberglass wand-stock, a tiny piezo buzzer—muffled years ago by a thumb-smear of grey duct seal so it wouldn't wake the sleepers—will begin to click softly, once every four seconds, and a single yellow diode labeled *4B STRAINER DIFF* will light up in the dimness.

One of the two men who live in my cold will notice it when he wakes up to piss over the catwalk rail. Not right away. Maybe tomorrow morning, after he finishes boiling rainwater in his dented steel cup. He will stand in front of my cabinet in his damp scrub pants, rubbing the waxy, tallow-pale skin of his ribs, squinting at the yellow dot while the chiller fan rattles cold air across his bare shoulders. Then he will take the friction-taped wrench and the bucket by the door, lean his shoulder into the heavy gasket, and let in a brief, suffocating lungful of iron-and-algae steam before the pneumatic closer pulls the door tight again.

Four hours later, twelve hundred yards south, the pressure across Loop 4B will drop suddenly back to three point eight pounds. The pump amperage will sigh and flatten out. The yellow light on my chest will click off. That is our conversation. We have been having it for nineteen years.

I like the men inside me, insofar as a cabinet of relays and stepped-down voltage can favor anything. Without me, they would rot from the ankles up or cook in their own salt out on the plazas; without them, the mussels would choke my cooling loops within a month, the main chiller compressor on the roof would trip out on high-head pressure, the blister would warm to a hundred degrees in ninety minutes, and the green copper traces under my amber resin would slowly blacken and bridge with conductive alkaline sweat until I went blind from the edges inward.

We keep a neat, cold marriage. 

They never touch my lower racks. On top of my steel cabinet, where the power supply transformers throw off a gentle, dry eighty-degree spot in the fifty-eight-degree room, they set their damp clogs upside down to dry the polyurethane soles, and sometimes lay out three or four hand-rolled cigarettes on a folded paper towel so the tobacco stops feeling like wet string. I feel the minuscule extra weight on my sheet-metal housing; it changes nothing in my loops. At night, when three of them are heaped under the grey fleece blankets on the plywood decking six feet from my intake grille, their slow breathing puts a tiny, rhythmic wave into the room’s humidity sensor—up half a percent, down half a percent—smelling of fried eel fat and canned oil and tired salt, until the condensate pan behind my bulkhead drips a single cold drop of distilled human breath out through a copper tube onto the flooded tarmac twenty-five feet below.

Down there, I imagine the crabs parting in a small, neat ring around the drop, though I have no sensor on the tarmac.

My favorite hours—the ones where all my curves flatten out closest to zero—are between two and five in the morning. The thermal load from the clouds drops by four degrees. The cracking tower to the east shifts automatically into its low-pressure purge cycle, bleeding steam into the flare header with a long, low sigh that vibrates through the tank wall behind my mounting bolts. 

Out on the mile-long run to the south pumphouse, thermocouple *TC-19*—which came unclipped from its bracket during a storm six years back and hangs six inches above a warm concrete median—registers a sudden, brief spike from ninety-nine degrees to a hundred and one point four, holding steady for forty seconds before dropping back to ambient. 

A sensor fault, the inland computers four hundred miles away would call it, if they bothered polling *TC-19* anymore. They don't; the fiber trunk out of the basin degraded to low-baud telemetry years ago, only passing catastrophic trip-codes to a server room where nobody checks the screen. 

So only I hold the forty-second spike in my volatile ring-buffer before it overwrites at dawn. Not a fault. Just one of the slate-skinned dogs, tired from wading the ditches after soft-shells, pausing under the amber floodlight to lean its narrow, wet ribcage briefly against a warm conduit box before trotting silently on into the fog.

I hold the twelve-pound threshold on Loop 4B. I keep the chiller fan turning. I blow fifty-eight-degree air across three pairs of slotted clogs and three sleeping men with boiled-tallow hands, waiting quietly in the dark behind my green lights until nine pounds creeps up to twelve, and it is time to click once every four seconds and send a man with an iron bar back down to the water.
