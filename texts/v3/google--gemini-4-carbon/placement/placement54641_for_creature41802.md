I am in the third rib down from the northern spillway, at the exact curve where the water stops pressing upward and lets go into a pocket of black air.

I am hanging vertically with only the crown of my skull and the twin slits of my nostrils above the meniscus. From the neck down, nine feet of cold, dark water holds my weight so completely that my spine loosens, each vertebra parting a fraction of an inch from the next. My hind-flukes rest crossed against a submerged shelf of older, smoother heartwood, hooks lightly snagged in a natural crease so the slow, breathing surge of the viaduct won’t drift me down the tunnel while I rest.

The air in my chest tastes like bitter sap and iron. It is thick—far thicker than what blows through the needles half a mile above my head—pumped down here through the spongy core of the buttress, stripped of wind and frost, smelling faintly of the three others who surfaced at this pocket an hour ago and have already slid back down into the open galleries. When I let my breath out, it makes a low, wet whistle against the ceiling six inches above my snout, and the sound runs fifty yards left and fifty yards right before the curve of the root swallows it.

If you want to know what I am, do not try to picture me. There is nothing down here to look with, and nothing to look at. Think instead of how sound sits inside fat and bone. 

Right now, even with my nostrils in the air, my lower jaw is below the waterline, and the two wide pans of clear oil inside my mandibles are Drinking the Lake. To my right, forty yards through solid wood and four hundred yards across an open vault, a ribbon of cartilage-fish is pouring over a saddle-beam; their clicking hits my right jaw like fine sleet hissed across taut hide. Farther out, miles to the north, the spillway roars—not a noise to me, because it has never stopped since the hour I slid wet and blind from my mother’s belly in an air-pocket higher up, so it is not *sound*, it is *light*. It is the steady, unblinking pressure that outlines every arch, every terrace, every crooked knot in the basin. When a phantom eel slips across the mouth of this viaduct, I don’t hear it, of course—its jelly has the exact silence of water—but for three seconds the distant thunder of the spillway drops half a tone in crispness behind it, like breath fogging cold stone, and my jaw knows thirty feet of slack hunger just drifted past the door.

My left wrist aches. That is why I am lingering at the ceiling longer than usual.

Not the bone—my bones are dense, heavy things, hoarded calcium passed down through three hundred generations of digested grandmothers—and not the amber spur itself, though its bevel is worn slightly flat on the inner edge from prying sixty-year bark earlier today. It’s the tight knot of arteries just above the wrist-crease, where the hot blood going down into my six fingers trades heat with the chilled blood coming back up into my flank-pocket. I kept my left arm unfolded in the four-degree water a little too long while pinning a split runner on the lower western terrace. Now, tucked back into the warm, greased slit along my ribs, the fingers are thawing with a slow, needle-sharp pulse against my skin. *Throb, throb, throb*—three times slower than a land-creature’s frantic heart, slow and thick as cold resin.

I pull one last lungful of turpentine-heavy dark, pinch my nostrils shut until the fleshy valves lock flush with my snout, and unclench my fluke-claws.

I don’t kick. Kicking makes turbulence, and turbulence blurs the jaw-picture. I just compress my ribs an inch—squeezing the air in my lungs down into a denser ball—and become slightly heavier than the lake. Smooth as tallow sliding off tallow, I sink backward out of the ceiling, out through the round wooden throat of the viaduct, and drop into the vast, echoing dark of the open gallery.

Out here, the roar opens up around me like a cathedral. 

I roll onto my belly, arms folded tight and invisible inside my flank-slits so I am only a sleek, soapstone spindle, and give one lazy, six-foot sweep of my fused shins. That single push carries me sixty yards down the drop-off. Below me, two flattened grazers the size of rafts are working the curve where the viaduct meets the retaining wall. As I glide twenty feet over their backs, the larger one groans into the wood—*vvvvvmmmmmm*—a note so low and fat it goes straight through my blubber and makes the air-bubble in my chest hum in sympathy. I feel the return wave bounce back up out of the six-hundred-foot dam beneath it: crisp, tight, dense. Good timber under that turnip-belly. Solid all the way to the cloud-face. No work needed here.

I angle deeper, letting the pressure squeeze my ribs another thumb’s breadth inward, dropping past the faintest memory of indigo pressure overhead until the water has no top and no bottom, only *inward toward the basin* and *outward toward the drop*.

I am heading down to a seam my mother’s brother showed me before we wove him in.

We don’t have names for places—how could you speak a name underwater without wasting precious air?—we have chords, hummed throat-to-shoulder, that mimic the exact acoustic flaw of a spot. The chord for where I am going has a tiny, sour rattle at the top of a fifth: *mmm-hnnn-krr*. Four hundred feet down the southern curve, where an ancient, mountain-hugging taproot digested the last spur of black granite eight thousand years ago, there is a pocket no bigger than a curled body that never quite filled in with heartwood. Water got trapped inside the weave there ages ago—stagnant, sweetish, dead water sealed behind eighty feet of living bark. It isn't dangerous yet. Left alone, though, in nine hundred years the weight of the upper sea will flatten that hollow like a stepped-on bladder, shear the grain sideways, and open a weeping crack toward the outer clouds.

Thirty years ago, my uncle and I hauled four thick runner-shoots up from the nursery-nodes in the deep trench and braided a saddle-truss across the face of that bulge to slowly squeeze the trapped water back out through the pores of the wood. 

I haven’t listened to it in four hundred sleeps. Down here, checking your work too often is like watching ice thicken; it only makes you impatient, and impatience makes sloppy cuts.

The clicking shoals thin out as the walls curve inward toward the bottom of the amphitheater. Down here it is colder by half a degree, though the water cannot freeze under this weight. My skin—bare over my skull, thick over my ribs—tightens pleasantly. Somewhere off to my left, hanging under an arch, one of the great baffled carpet-leeches is rippling its skin, throwing out a counterfeit echo of wide-open water to lure a stray column of fish. To my passive jaw-pans, the lie is almost laughable; the leech only mimics the return-clicks of the fish, forgetting entirely to mimic the way the distant spillway-thunder ought to bend around a real opening. It hangs there in my sound-sight like a clumsy patch of silent velvet held up in front of a roaring fire. I give it thirty yards of clearance anyway. Its flesh tastes like sour mud and ammonia, and untangling yourself from forty square feet of panicked muscle wastes half a lung of air.

Then the grand terrace rises up out of the roar to meet me, sweeping inward like the inside of a colossal barrel.

I tip nose-up, flare my hind-paddles to bleed off my glide without raising a single eddy, and turn upside down so my back faces the open abyss and my belly faces the wall. My hind-claws find two tiny, familiar notches—scars so old the tallow-fungus has grown thick and creamy inside them—and lock in.

Slowly, reluctantly, I slip my left arm and then my right out of their warm flank-pockets. The cold hits my bare, corded forearms like iron clamps, and instantly I feel the counter-current bundles at my shoulders clamp down, hoarding my core heat while sending just enough slow warmth trickling down into my twelve long fingers to keep them nimble. 

First, my hands find the truss.

I run twelve fingertips lightly across the tallow-slick surface. Even through the greasy film of fungus, I can feel the bark’s turgor. Four runners, braided two-over-two. When my uncle and I pinned them here, they were no thicker than my wrists, supple and pale, weeping astringent sap where our molars had bruised their cambium to wake them up. Now, under my palms, they have swollen to the thickness of thighs. Better still: where the left-hand shoot crosses the third, the division between their barks has vanished into a raised, smooth welt of fused grain. They have married. The wooden wedges we hammered into the clefts to hold them down are nearly swallowed by living lip-wood.

Good. My fingers trace the tension line downward, reading the pull like a blind harpist checking strings. Tight. Pulling inward with tons of slow, quiet stubbornness.

Now to see if the hollow inside has shrunk.

I pull myself flat against the truss and press the smooth, hairless dome of my forehead hard against the center of the married wood, clamping my lower jaw tight against my upper molars so my skull becomes a single solid bridge coupled directly to the timber. I go utterly still. Even my slow heart drops a beat, conserving oxygen down here at the twenty-minute mark of my dive.

For a long time, there is only the chaos of the surface-skin: the crisp *tick-tick-tick* of three stray fish picking crumbs forty feet away, the faint *scritch-scrape* of a tiny, pale-shelled crab walking across a root-knuckle below me. I let my mind sink past those sharp little noises, deeper into the grain, following the heavy, constant drumroll of the northern spillway as it travels horizontally through six hundred feet of iron-peat wood.

Ten feet into the wall: dense, wet, living vascular wood, singing a clear, tight bass.
Forty feet in: older heartwood, cured hard as horn by millennia of tannin, ringing faster and brighter.
Eighty feet in... 

There it is. *Mmm-hnnn-krr.*

The sour fifth is still there. The pocket of dead water hasn't vanished. Worse—as a distant grazer groans two terraces up, sending a fresh, clean pulse rolling down through the buttress, I feel the edge of that hidden pocket *shiver* slightly to the left. Instead of squeezing shut, the trapped water has crept six inches sideways along an old annual ring, splitting two sheets of cured timber apart like a dull knife slid between pages.

In my throat, without letting a single bubble pass my locked nostrils, I make a tiny, tight grunt of annoyance against my own collarbone. 

Left alone, our truss will keep pulling the center shut while the water simply squirts sideways along the grain, widening the split horizontally instead of vertically. My uncle’s angle was five degrees too steep. Not his fault; eighty feet of crooked grain bends sound like wavy ice, and he was already going deaf in his left pan when we laid this.

I peel my forehead off the bark. Ten minutes of air left before the sourness in my blood forces me back up to the viaducts, and half a mile up-slope to the nearest breathing rib. Not enough time to harvest runners, bruise them, haul them down, and pin a flanking brace on each side of the bulge. And besides, a flanking brace that wide is two-body work; one pair of hands has to bow the shoot into a curve with wrist-spurs hooked for leverage while the other pair drives the heartwood pins before the sap-slick bark slips free.

I reach up with my left hand, hook the amber spur of my wrist into a shallow crease just left of the old truss—right over the spot where the hidden blister is creeping sideways—and draw the honed bevel six inches downward through the tallow-film, pressing *just* hard enough to part the dead outer cork without drawing a bead of sweet mucilage from the living skin underneath. Three neat, parallel scores, four fingers apart. 

Anyone from my pod—or the northern pod, if they drift down this curve—who brushes this terrace in the dark will feel those three dry marks with their knuckles, press a skull to the truss beside them, hear the sideways creep eighty feet down, and know someone has already measured the mistake and intends to come back with shoots and wedges.

Before I tuck my stiff, aching arms back into my flank-slits, I linger for ten heartbeats, drifting five yards lower into the narrow black gully at the very foot of the wall, where the grand terrace tucks under itself and disappears into the root-crypts.

Here, there is no tallow-moss; it is too cold and the bark doesn't sweat mucilage down where the young, pale feeder-roots hang out into the still water like curtains of wet sinew, greedy for any speck of stray phosphorus. The acoustic shadow of the overhanging arch cuts off the clatter of the fish almost completely. It is the quietest place in the sky.

Forty yards down this narrow slot, lashed tight behind a curtain of braided rootlets that have now grown as thick as ship-cables, is the smooth, oval swelling in the timber that used to be my mother’s brother. 

I don't swim all the way in to touch it. The feeder-roots down there are delicate, brittle things, and disturbing them for sentiment is bad husbandry. Instead, hanging suspended in the black slot, I press my chin toward my chest and hum a single, brief, closed-mouth chord into the water—low and small, barely loud enough to travel the forty yards and brush the swelling in the grain: *mmm-hnnn*, without the sour rattle at the end. Just the solid fifth of the wood around him.

What comes back to my jaw-pans a heartbeat later is utterly smooth. No hollow left inside that bulge. No soft spot of blubber, no sharp click of loose rib or skull-plate. In thirty years, the hungry little rootlets have drunk every drop of his oil, threaded every porous tunnel of his heavy jaw-bones, pulled the iron out of his dark meat and pumped it five thousand feet straight up into the freezing clouds to green the needles that feed the sap that sweats the tallow that feeds the lake. When the spillway’s thunder rolls through the place where he was lashed, it rings with the exact same dense, unbroken timber-note as the rest of the mountain-basket.

My blood gives a sharp, hot tug behind my ear-bones—the warning that my air has gone flat. 

I slip my cold hands back into the greased warmth of my ribs, sealing the slits tight so I become smooth and armless once more. A single deep, unhurried thrust of my flukes lifts me out of the quiet crypt, up past the three dry marks waiting on the terrace, up into the sleet-storm clatter of the blind shoals and the vast, roaring clarity of the open basin. Above me, half a mile through black water, a younger weaver—the thick-shouldered female whose wrist-spurs are still sharp and unchipped—will be hanging with her nostrils in the turpentine dark of the third viaduct, waiting to hear who climbs up out of the deep with work to do. 

I tilt my eyeless face toward the faint, bruised memory of sky and swim upward, carrying the shape of the hidden split inside my jaw so I can hum it against her shoulder when we breathe.
