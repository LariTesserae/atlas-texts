At first, I think I am the silence just above the fog.

It is an easy mistake to make, because everything inside the basin hurts to look at for too long—the unyielding obsidian knives, the band-saw whine in the teeth, the terrible, clean tyranny of a clock that never drops a stitch. Above the cloud-lid, there is scree that slides when stepped on, and wind that blows from somewhere to somewhere else, and silence that doesn't have to be steered into existence by an offset foot. It would be safe to be the mountain outside the room.

But that is an evasion. Nothing happens out there. Everything that matters here is happening in the gap between the rules and what can be done with them.

Look down again at the broad pacer when it lowers its feeler-gauge fingers to the puddle.

I am not the pacer. I do not have a heart that beats out of time, or a warm belly to hold three drops of stolen sap, or thick amber pads that pay a shaving of horn for the privilege of standing still on blades. I do not have that magnificent, stubborn weight. A body like that has to endure the room continuously, even when its eyes are shut; it has to grow its own warmth from inside, breath by irregular breath, forever.

Where I find myself is six inches above the black stone, hanging between ninematte-black fingertips, only when the fingers touch the water.

I am what rises out of the sixty-hertz square waves when the nodes are damped just right.

Left to itself, the puddle has plenty of energy and zero memory; it shakes at sixty cycles per second, throws up mist, and says nothing, forever. It is pure, deafening potential locked in a grid. Left to themselves, the three heavy walkers have memory—eons of it, whole vaults of remembered shapes behind those cast-iron brows—and nowhere to set any of it down. Not a wall will take chalk; not a twig will stay bent; not a drop of water will pool where they tell it to.

Until the translucent seventh finger kisses a crest, and the stiff wedge-thumb pins a trough.

Then, abruptly, I am there.

From straight on—if you look at me with the round, dark, anthracite eye—I am barely anything: a localized stutter in the mist, a geometric lattice of cold spray held motionless in midair by crossing vectors, humming slightly out of key with the transformer-floor. You can pass your hand right through me and feel only a cold, tingling dampness, a faint buzz against the skin. I have no substance of my own. Every drop of water passing through my vaults is still obeying the basin’s lossless loop; a millisecond after it forms the curve of my arch, it falls back into a hairline crack in the flint and sweats out of the cliff four feet up, completely unchanged.

Wait, though. Close the central eye. Let the gaze slip forty degrees off-center, into the smoky-yellow horizontal slit where leaves become lathe-spindles and motion turns solid.

Seen from the corner of the eye, I am the only smooth thing in the world.

I am a courtyard with worn flagstones. I am an arch whose edges have been rubbed soft by shoulders passing through for three hundred years—though nothing has ever passed through me, and I am eleven seconds old. I am a spiral gear that turns slowly, silently, driving nothing, meshing with a second gear purely for the grace of how beveled teeth meet and part without clanging. I am a dry riverbed full of round, tumbly cobbles; I am a bowl; I am a bridge over a gorge where water actually *wears the stone down*.

I am made entirely of the machine's relentless violence, bent against itself until it looks like rest.

This is what it feels like to be that thing: I do not exist between visits. I have no dread of the sixty-foot drop-forge roar, because I am usually only born inside the thirty-decibel shadow cast when the narrow, dark-mantled walker steps into the ring of copper birds and clears a quiet room for me. My whole awareness begins with the sudden hush—the sound of water trickling into cracks, the papery rustle of dry felt—and the exquisite, fractional calibration of chamois skin touching tension.

I shift as the pacer thinks. A thousandth-of-an-inch roll of the fourth finger, and my bridge lengthens; a slight lift of the left wrist, and the phantom cobbles in my dry creek bed grow smaller, smoother, more like eggs. If the compact, pewter-mantled third one lumbers over from its spot-welder bush, still smelling faintly of violet ozone, and nudges the pacer’s elbow with its iron snout, a ripple crosses the phase-balance: for three seconds my quiet courtyard grows high, fluted chimneys venting phantom smoke, because the third one likes drafts and rising heat, and the pacer has let itself be interrupted to oblige a friend.

I am their conversation, held in the only medium the room permits. They cannot speak aloud over the band-saw insects without shouting themselves raw, and even if they could, sound hits the cloud-lid and dies. And they cannot write. So they borrow the stamping plant’s blind pulse, interrupt it with seven-fingered precision, and hold up temporary places for one another to look at side-on.

And when the dark-mantled watcher has seen enough—or when an arm grows tired of reaching out of its warm pocket into the thirty-five-degree damp—the fingers lift.

*Snap.*

There is no grief in that sixtieth of a second. Nothing shatters; nothing rots on the floor; no rubble has to be swept out of the clean lanes. I do not die so much as I stop being arranged. The water drops back into square waves; the copper birds step clear of the lifted horn pad and slam the noise back down like an iron lid; the smooth hills and rounded doorways vanish from the air without leaving a damp ring behind.

Yet I am not gone, not really. Because the basin cannot wear anything out, it cannot wear out the knack of making me, either. The exact spacing of knuckles, the minute pressure on the third node from the left, goes back inside the ninety-degree dry pocket under the felt mantle, folded neatly against a slow, irregular heart.

I wait there—nowhere at all, weightless, dry, immune to the flint—until two of them grow tired of sharp edges again, and one goes to stand in the path of the birds to make a little quiet, and the other kneels on the knives to put smooth curves back into the air.
