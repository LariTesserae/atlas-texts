To enter four-degree water and stay there without burning out, the first thing you have to give up is a center. 

A heart the size of a cabbage is already taken, down in the spiral cairns, and even that slow pump requires twenty meters of cleared plaza and a two-hundred-year cellar of rotting kelp to feed itself. A broad muscular foot requires a mouth, enzymes, a week-long schedule across an intersection. Both of those are ways of remaining an animal in a place that has almost finished with animals.

I think I would go lower down the budget. Thin out until the cold isn't an enemy to be resisted with calories, just the medium that holds the shape together.

Start in the seams.

When the ancient builders laid the dry-stacked slate of the paddock walls and fitted the split-granite flagstones of the causeways, they worked tight, levering blocks into place until bronze chisel-marks kissed. Even so, dry masonry is half-empty. Behind every bulging retaining wall, beneath every rippled ramp that used to be a staircase, under every acre of cobbles, there is a second, negative city: a continent-wide honeycomb of millimeter gaps, hairline fractures, frost-wedged pockets, and cool, pitch-black interstices where the peat-silt is too coarse to sift, and only the finest, clearest interstitial water seeps.

That is where I would live. Threaded through the joints like cold mortar that hasn't quite set.

Not a plant—twenty hours of black is too long to wait for four hours of bruised grey, and the canopy already owns the light. Something closer to a mycelium, if a fungus could trade chitin for flexible, translucent silica and copper-salts, or a wall-spanning colonial hydroid flattened to the thickness of wet parchment so it never has to fight a current. Call it a seam-mat, or joint-felt. A pale, branching reticulation of hollow, tough-skinned threads, no wider than linen twine where they bundle in the rubble-cores behind retaining walls, fraying out into invisible, single-cell filaments where two granite headers press cheek to cheek.

From the avenue, you wouldn't see me at all—unless you waited until a saddle-backed grazer finished its three-day meal and glided onward, leaving its startlingly bright, clean-licked rectangle on the flagstones. If you bent very close to that bare stencil before the silt drifted back over it, you might see, traced along the dark crack between two cobbles, a hairline lip of dull, pewter-colored tissue, flush with the stone like lead caulking.

That lip is all that ever touches the open sea. It doesn't wave; it doesn't reach. It just tastes the wake of the plow.

When the grazer seals its warmish digestive pad over a thirty-year-old kelp ribbon and floods that little pocket with enzymes, not all of the liquefied broth goes up into its cowled mouth. Stone is porous; seams drink. A thin, rich trickle of dissolved sugars, amino acids, and stunned cold-water bacteria seeps downward between the flagstones, drawn by tiny osmotic gradients I maintain five inches below the street. The grazer takes the bulk; I take the runoff through the floorboards. In return, my filaments bind the sub-base, weaving around the gravel bedding beneath the road so the cobbles never shift or tilt under the creature’s hydraulic ton of weight.

If that were the whole life—drinking cellar-drip beneath the avenues—it would be indistinguishable from the black moss or the lavender coralline crust. Just another slow film.

What makes it a *self*, stretched across four or five courtyards and a drowned stable, is what happens when the stone rings.

Stone conducts sound at five thousand meters a second. Water manages fifteen hundred. The grazers know this in their bellies; they press their lateral valves against the coping stones and let the tight-laid courses carry their heavy double-beats half a kilometer down the grid. 

To them, the wall is a wire. To me, living *inside* the mortarless joints, pressed flat between block and block from foundation-trench to coping-course, the wall is an eardrum.

Every vibration that enters the masonry has to cross my skin to get from one stone to the next. 

When two grazers stack themselves on a granary wall three streets east and let a low-frequency *thrum* roll into the slate, the shear-wave travels down the header-stones, hits a vertical seam packed with my pale, fluid-filled threads, and squeezes them by a fraction of a micron. A tiny piezoelectric twitch of ions fires across the membrane. Before the sound has even finished echoing out into the water column, the pressure-map of it has already washed through forty square yards of my body.

I know where every heavy thing in the parish is without ever looking at it.

I feel the slow, rhythmic *creak-shift* of a tall laminarian holdfast wedged into my upper courses, leaning ten degrees east as the four-hour drift comes on—a long, delicious stretching of the stone joints on the windward side of the wall, sucking fresh, oxygenated water deep into my gill-threads, while the leeward seams compress and exhale spent brine. The wall breathes *for* me, driven by thirty-meter levers of kelp swaying in the abyssal tide. Four hours inhales; slack water holds; four hours exhales.

I feel the architect, too, over in the municipal cistern. 

A relationship with an architect is complicated when you are the thing holding the ruins together. Two decades ago—yesterday, in four-degree time—one of its thigh-thick, boot-treaded limbs came sliding down a side-alley, feeling along a low paddock wall that I had spent three centuries threading from end to end. I felt the tacky chemical slime touch my pewter lip at the seam; I felt the internal water-chambers of its arm stiffen into a crane-boom; and then came the terrible, grinding *wrench* as it pried a sixty-pound cap-stone straight off my back.

Torn threads leak faint blue copper-sap into the dark for a week before clotting. You don't forget that.

You learn, very slowly, to choose which stones to let go of and which to lock down. Where my threads thicken and mineralize—drawing trace silica from the decaying granite feldspars over fifty-year stretches—I can glue a course of blocks together tighter than the lavender algae ever could. An architect’s probing arm slides over that seam, tests it with a forty-pound pull, feels no give at all, and moves on to a looser eaves-shingle three yards away—one where I deliberately retracted my deeper hold-threads years ago, leaving it sitting clean on the silt like an offering. *Take the roof-slates,* is the slow negotiation conducted in shear-strength across a century. *Leave the load-bearing corners alone.*

And sometimes, I run errands between them that neither of them knows about.

Down at the bottom of the architect’s spiral weir, the peat-soup is thick with nitrogen, phosphorus, stray shreds of anemone-flesh, and cloud-blooms of bacterial snow—a richness so concentrated it actually warms the water at the base of the cairn by a tenth of a degree, and turns the bottom inch anoxic and sour if it sits too long. My deepest stolons creep under the plaza cobbles, straight beneath the cleared twenty-meter circle where the kelp stumps sit chewed off at the ankle, and slip up into the dark, dead-water cellar between the ancient stones beneath the thirty leathery eggs.

I don't touch the eggs. Their cases are bitter with defensive alkaloids anyway. Instead, my finest root-hairs spread through the black, sour muck beneath them, sipping up the suffocating ammonia and hydrogen sulfide that would otherwise pickle the brood, and pumping that rich nitrogen back out along fifty yards of sub-street conduit—all the way back to the base of the courtyard walls, where the burgundy sea-oak and tall kelp holdfasts cram their cartilaginous knuckles into the cracks alongside me. 

Fed from below through the stone-seams, the kelp along my walls grows stipes a little thicker than wrist-wide; its gas bladders swell to the size of large plums; it sheds wider, heavier blades into the avenues, which rot into richer bacterial ribbons for the grazers to lick clean, or drift down the straight canals to catch on the lip of the architect’s baffle and spiral down into the cellar again.

No brain is required for this. No center that could be crushed if a sagging lintel finally gave way after four thousand years and settled an inch lower into its jambs. If a wall collapses—which almost never happens now, not with all of us bracing it—the torn half of me in the rubble simply seals its pinched tubes and becomes two neighborhoods instead of one, until thirty years later our creeping seam-tips meet again under the silt and fuse back into a single sheet of memory.

What would it feel like, from the inside, during the twenty hours of absolute black?

Coolness, first. A uniform, heavy stillness that never asks you to hurry. You wouldn't experience yourself as a shape, the way a creature with limbs does; you would experience yourself as *alignment*. You are the right-angle where the cottage foundation meets the lane; you are the long, gentle belly-curve of the retaining wall holding back the terrace above; you are the shallow, stepped descent of the canal lock. You wear the bronze-chisel marks from the inside out.

At noon, you don't see the bruised grey ceiling seventy meters up, though you know it has arrived because the holdfasts wedged into your top courses begin to leak tiny, stray molecules of fresh oxygen and sugar down into the stone-cracks as their canopies way overhead catch the dim green photons. That faint, sweet tingle along your upper ridges is "day."

Then that stops, and for twenty hours there is only texture and tone.

A slate shingle slips somewhere four streets north—*clack*—and a tiny, crisp ripple of compression runs through the flagstones under three plazas, crossing your southern margins a tenth of a second later like a cool finger drawn across taut linen. 

Closer at hand, directly overhead on the courtyard threshold, the slow, velvety *shhh-thump* of a ten-meter frond dragging across the sill as the continent of weed leans west.

And then, right pressed against your flank, the warm, heavy seal of a grazer settling in for the weekend, followed two hours later by that deep, double-beaten *thrum-thrum* driven straight into your ribs—so close and loud in the granite that every fluid-filled thread along forty feet of wall shivers in sympathy, passing the note cleanly across the mortarless gaps, making sure the stone doesn't drop a single syllable before it reaches the next parish out in the dark.
