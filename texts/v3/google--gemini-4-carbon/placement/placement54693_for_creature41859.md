I am not out on the wet spruce spans where the gill-slugs knot. I am tucked into a dry, pocketed recess on the eastern wall, forty feet below the rim, where a flake of basalt the size of a millstone pulled six inches away from the parent rock and locked itself fast on an angled spur. 

It is one of the very few naturally dry overhangs in the chasm. Because the gray sweat of the walls never trickles down into this narrow slit, they use it as the Cord-Loft. 

Wait—at first it seems as though I might be the person who tends this ledge, an old, stiff-hipped builder whose splayed toes have lost their quickness on the mid-air poles and who has been retired to the wall-racks to twist bast and splice sinew all day. And yes, I have hands—pale as mutton fat, marbled at the knuckles with old black basalt-scars, fingers perpetually sticky with pine-resin and sheep-tallow. My chest has that deep, slow bellows-heave in the thin cold. 

Yet the longer I sit wedged on this slate shelf, with my bare left heel hooked constantly over the Master Plumb—the heavy, thumb-thick vertical cable of four-ply elk sinew and glider-gut that drops straight past my ledge from the rim-anchors to a two-ton counterweight boulder wedged just clear of the floor slabs—the less my skin feels like the boundary of what I am. 

I am the listener at the nexus, and I am also the tension I listen to. 

In a town built entirely out of suspended geometry, nothing can be rigid. If you peg two walls together with iron-hard timber and unyielding knots, the slightest thermal shift in the raw mountain—and this fracture is young; the ridge still settles by a hair’s breadth when winter freezes the outer scree—will snap your struts or pull your slate cantilevers straight out of their fissures. Everything here survives by *give*, and by someone knowing the exact difference between healthy elastic stretch and the slow, fatal creep of rotting fiber.

My body is small, scarcely eighty pounds even in my greased amber skins, because I have barely walked a horizontal yard in twelve years. My legs have thinned to corded sticks, folded crosswise on a mat of shredded bark, save for the broad, spongy-gray pads of my heels and big toes, which have grown almost ridiculously wide and sensitive—two flat, calloused ears pressed permanently against the convergence board where eleven secondary guy-lines meet the Master Plumb.

Close your eyes in the bruised, shadowless noon, and see what arrives through the soles of those feet.

The town is not silent down in the wood-grain and the twisted gut. It hums like a monstrous, slack-strung harp tuned below the register of air. Right now, through the ball of my right foot, third cord from the rock: a slow, rhythmic *swell-and-sag, swell-and-sag*, nine seconds apart. That is the great chartreuse gas-bladder tethered sixty feet down in the central shaft; an unseen draft from the floor clefts is pushing its forty-foot black runner against the underside of the Third Sorting Walk. Harmless. The runner is slick; it slides off the peeled spruce without catching.

Through the side of my left big toe, high line, steep angle: the crisp, oiled *zip-tuck-tuck* of young Joren’s bone awl working a split lashing near the upper cisterns. He pulls his knots too tight on the second turn—always has, ever since he came off his mother’s ribs—leaving a sharp little bright spike of over-tension in the sinew that makes the teeth in my own jaw ache sympathetically until the dampness swells the fiber and evens it out.

And underneath all of it, the heavy, continuous, velvet *drag* of the herd. Tons of cold water and soft white flesh hanging on twisted grass and dead deer-tendon. You can feel the weight of the morning mist gathering on fifty thousand square feet of liverwort curtains—an imperceptible downward sigh across the whole grid between dawn and midday, every wooden gutter bowing another sixteenth of an inch, every stone peg biting a fraction deeper into its basalt crack, until the filter-shells on the wicker vats begin to pulse faster, drinking the seep, and the afternoon harvest lightens the baskets again.

Before me in the dry cleft hang the cures. Hundreds of hanks of split plant-bast, pale yellow and smelling faintly of sour marsh-mud from the valleys; translucent strips of glider-membrane drying flat on bone stretcher-frames like panes of smoky horn; coils of raw elk-sinew traded up from the horrible, howling world outside, waiting to be chewed soft. 

My molars are worn nearly flat to the gums from forty years of chewing sinew. That is my slow, wet clockwork. Take a dry,amber-hard tendon from the rack; warm it first inside my inner vest against the tallow-pale skin over my ribs; feed an inch between my back teeth and crush it slowly, without cutting, letting thin mountain saliva soak into the collagen until it parts into silky, gleaming threads stronger than wire. No chatter. Even the wet *clop* of an open mouth chewing would sound coarse here, so my lips stay sealed, jaw rolling in the dimness with the exact slow, hydraulic patience of a gill-slug clearing a junction.

When someone speaks to the town as a whole, the words pass through my ankles.

Two hours past noon, a sharp, three-part *thrum-damp-scrape* comes climbing up from the deep dark below the lowest walkways. Down there, two women have been lowering the pitch-vats into the floor clefts. I do not need to touch the cord with my fingers; my heel reads the slight, gritty tremor behind the deliberate pluck. *Bottom anchor-stone shifted. Half-thumb east. Old fracture weeping cold air.*

I stop chewing. My tongue tucks the wet sinew into my cheek. 

If the bottom counterweight boulder has slipped half a thumb-width east between those glassy floor slabs, then the Master Plumb has gone fractionally off-vertical. Left alone, by tomorrow morning three wooden condensation gutters on the west wall will lose their gradient, pooling gray water instead of running it to the cisterns. Where water pools stagnant on spruce wood, the black rot-fungi bloom in twenty hours; where the fungi bloom, the blind velvet quadrupeds swarm in thick, heavy heaps to eat it; where they heap, the stork-legged amphibians gather to feed on *them*; and within four days a quiet, unnoticed slip of stone in the pitch-dark basement becomes sixty pounds of unplanned animal weight rotting out a structural joint seventy feet in the air.

I lean forward out of my dry cleft into the open shaft.

The cellar-chill touches my cropped scalp instantly, raising no gooseflesh anymore, just a clean, familiar tightening of the skin. Below me drops the whole dim, staggering gulf—tiered lines crossing empty air, floating chartreuse moons swaying yards apart, dark purple cartwheel leaves glistening with wet silver undersides, and the sheer black walls alive with the silent, synchronized breathing-waves of millions of radial crawlers. Far across the void, a tiny amber-wrapped figure—Mava, by the slight hitch in her left shoulder—is scraping pearl-gray eggs off a leaf-rib into her back-pannier.

She isn't looking at me. Nobody looks around in the shaft unless signaled; eyes belong on your next foothold or your knife-edge.

I reach out with two resin-darkened fingers, grip the west secondary cross-stay, and send a crisp, dry *double-pluck, slide, dead-stop* shooting across sixty yards of empty air right into the spruce pole under Mava’s bare arches.

Fifty yards away, she freezes mid-scrape. Her horn spatula slips onto its lanyard. She turns her chest toward the eastern wall—just her chest, elbows tucked tight against her ribs so she doesn't brush the hair-thin black bladder-tether floating two feet behind her back. Against her dark oiled jacket, the pale insides of her fingers flash twice, quick and small: *What?*

My own pale palms answer in front of my sternum, economical as blinking: *Bottom stone slipped east. Drop the third west gutter one notch. Check the pitch.*

She flashes a single flat thumb—*heard*—and before she has even turned back to climb toward the gutter-pegs, my right hand is already on the wooden windlass wedged into the back of my rock-slit, easing a notched bone pawl out of its tooth. 

*Creak.* 

Just one dry, woody syllable in the damp hush—loud enough that for ten feet around my cleft, the carpet of eight-legged crawlers on the basalt parts in a silent, nervous halo, exposing gleaming wet black rock before flowing smoothly back together. I let out three quarters of an inch of braided gut on the eastern stay, taking the bias off the Master Plumb, feeling through my heel as the great central line sighs, settles, and finds true straightness again down in the blind dark.

Sometimes, when the overhead cloud thickens toward night and the bruised gray light dims to the color of wet slate, turning the floating bladders and hanging shelters into faint, depthless smudges before taking sight away altogether, young builders climbing back to their solitary sleeping-baskets pause at the horn ladder outside my cleft. They never come in—there isn't room for two pairs of lungs in my dry slot, and their damp skins would ruin the curing bast. They just hang for a moment on the rungs by one hooked toe and an elbow, passing me a gourd of warm broth rendered from old gill-slugs and chopped liverwort, or taking a fresh thirty-foot coil of spliced cordage I’ve hung on the outer peg.

Once, the nine-year-old boy lingered a breath longer than needful, one small, widening foot resting on my threshold slate. He had been up near the rim that afternoon helping haul down green spruce poles from the trading party, and his eyes were still slightly pink and watered from a stray gust of dry ridge-wind spilling over the jagged lip. Looking down into the darkening shaft where white drift-larvae were rising thicker now like silent snow through the black intervals, his fingers twitched a half-formed question against his greased vest—a child’s thought, clumsy and large-gestured: *When the rock pulls apart again, does the whole grid fall?*

I didn't answer with my hands. In the fading light, sign-speech blurs at the edges and makes young eyes strain. Instead, I reached out and pressed two scarred fingers lightly against the taut sinew lanyard of his own chest-harness, right over his deep, rapid little ribs, and tapped the slow, four-beat cadence of the builders’ oldest measure: *Slack where the stone moves. Taut where the water runs. Empty in the middle.*

He felt it against his breastbone, blinked his heavy lids once, and climbed down into the dark toward his hanging cask without shaking the ladder.

Now the light is wholly gone. Up at the rim, invisible clouds scrape silently across razor basalt in the freezing dark; down at the bottom, dead cousins sit wrapped in retired ropes very much like the ones coiled beside my thighs, combed gently by eight-legged waves in the cold. Between the cloud and the bone hangs the web, drinking the damp. 

I tuck my chin into my amber collar, fold my hands inside my vest to keep the pitch-pot supple under my arm, and leave my bare gray heel resting lightly against the Master Plumb—half-asleep, half-listening as a heavy glider *slaps* softly onto a purple leaf forty feet down, sending a single, clean ripple through stalk, stone, slate, and sinew to tell me the night is holding together.
