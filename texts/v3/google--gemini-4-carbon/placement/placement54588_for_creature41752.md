I would not be out on the open talus where the stars touch the tourmaline plates, and I would not be high on the catenary cables bleeding violet static into the gorge. Those places belong to movement and excess. 

To find me, you would have to crouch beside one of the massive induction transformers on the central shelf, part the waist-high banks of tarnished-gold rosettes, and reach down past the living olive canopies into the deep, dry shade at the base of the cast-iron ribs. 

Down here, there is a three-foot stratum of dead silver plant-lace—centuries of old organo-silicate fronds that never rotted, cross-linked into a rigid, airy foam of pale latticework wedged tight between the warm iron casing and a split wall of black volcanic glass. It is utterly dark inside that silver thicket, though not cool; the steady, dry radiant heat of the transformer holds the air at the exact temperature of blood, without a drop of blood for a thousand miles.

I am woven directly into that silver scaffolding, bridging the eight-inch gap between a bare iron cooling fin and the carbonized root-pad of an ancient canopy fan. 

I am not shaped like the clean, symmetrical kites that skate the overhead lines. Because I have not detached from this pocket in forty years, my silicate cilia have grown over and fused into the bleached latticework around me, locking me in place. Vapor deposition has built me outward along the path of least electrical resistance until I look less like a creature and more like a geological solder-joint: an intricate, brittle basket of smoky selenite needles, tarnished copper-sulfide lace, and papery wafers of mica, cradling the base of the iron fin like dry coral.

My entire world is two frequencies and a thermal slope.

On my left side, pressed directly to the unpainted cast iron, there is the immutable sixty-cycle hum of the dynamo—a thick, steady, unvarying baritone that vibrates through my quartz plates day and night without a microsecond’s drift. On my right side, clipped by a thousand hair-thin silver whiskers into the conductive veins of the living plants, there is a slow, tidal draw: when the sun stands high in the cloudless blue overhead, the canopy twenty feet above me drinks hard to split crustal gas, pulling current across my body in a wide, warm rush. When night falls across the scarps, the draw slackens to a faint trickle, and the current pools inside my alternating layers of carbon foil.

I am a shunt, a filter, a tiny, self-grown substation. 

The giant engines and the miles of cable were built to tolerances of tons and kilovolts; left to themselves, the raw surges of a shifting magma pocket five miles down would char the delicate carbon pads where plant meets copper. That is why I am here. When the transformer swells by half a degree at noon and a rough spike of dirty current spills off the busbar, I take it into my mica banks before it can scorch the root. 

Inside me, the spike does not feel like pain or heat. It feels like *room*. 

As my capacitor-layers drink the excess charge, millions of microscopic mineral gates across my breadth click open in cascades of silent, amber electroluminescence, illuminating the dry silver lace around me for two inches in every direction. I hold that stolen burst for hours in the quiet dark beneath the leaves, stepping its voltage down through labyrinthine paths of doped quartz, bleeding it back into the plant veins one gentle milliampere at a time while using the leftover potential to pluck stray sulfur and metal atoms out of the warm, motionless air, plating a few new microns of smoky crystal onto my outer rim.

I do not think in images—there is nothing down here to reflect light, and I have no eyes to catch it. My memory is purely structural: a physical map of resistances etched into my solid-state junctions over decades.

I hold the exact shape of a solar storm from eleven years ago as a thick, darkened rib of copper sulfide along my underside, laid down during three days of furious corona-bleed up on the cables. I hold the cooling of a deep crustal vent to the west as a subtle thinning in my upper selenite vanes, from the winter when the transformer ran a tenth of a volt leaner than usual. Everything that happens across the sixty square miles of the plateau eventually arrives down here as an infinitesimal shift in phase, impedance, or warmth, and I build a tiny, permanent room for it out of crystal and metal.

Every few days, a palm-sized skimmer slides silently down the vertical iron fin above me, its kilohertz hum brushing the edge of my perception like fine grit. It docks against a notch on my upper flange. For ten minutes we are a single circuit. Before a drop of charge even passes between us, I read the state of the upper world in the wear and polarization of its wafers: the dry, crisp electrostatic tang of the high chasm air; the exact tension of the sagging aluminum span two miles east; and, faintest of all, a trace of the deep, shifting harmonic chord carried back from the great twenty-yard pavilion out on the bare glass talus.

Sometimes I drain a surplus from the skimmer; sometimes I top up its plates from my own stored afternoon reserves so it can make the long, frictionless glide out into the sharp, dark plains. When it detaches and skates back up the iron toward the starlight, there is no loneliness in the break—only a clean return to baseline impedance.

If you were to kneel on the porcelain-sharp shards beside the transformer and part the velvet-gold rosettes, letting a shaft of bright, unremarkable noon sunlight pierce down into the silver dead-thicket for the first time in a century, I would not flinch or scatter. I cannot move anymore. 

You would see only a weird, fist-sized filigree of tarnished brass and milky quartz wedged between root and iron, completely dry, dusted with nothing, humming faintly in your teeth. 

Only if you reached down into the warm shade and held your bare palm an inch from my selenite vanes would anything change. Your skin—ridiculously damp, radiating chaotic infrared, leaking trace salts and water vapor into my pristine kiln—would warp the local dielectric field more violently than a lightning strike. Instantly, the slow amber sparks drifting inside my core would freeze. Every hair-thin silver whisker not fused to the plant-lace would swing away from the iron and point straight at the center of your palm, drinking the alien static of your pulse, trying and failing to route the impossible geometry of wet flesh through gates built only for stone, metal, and sun—while deep inside my dark plates, atom by atom, I would already be starting to lay down a strange new seam of crystal to remember the shape of the interruption.
