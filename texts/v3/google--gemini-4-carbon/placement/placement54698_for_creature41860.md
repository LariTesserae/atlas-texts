You do not find me by the white stab of an acetylene flame, because I do not carry one. Light draws the midges into the grease around your nostrils, and light belongs to the long miles of discharge pipe where the young ones straddle the stone and chatter away with their bronze needles. 

Look instead to the western shaft, forty yards back from the dripping stainless-steel cage, inside a monitoring bunker whose doorway has dammed up to the thigh with crisp orange tufa. Or better yet, put your bare heel down firmly on the main four-inch compressed-air line anywhere between Station One and the eastern chimneys, and wait.

Beneath the enormous, chest-filling crash of the ceiling sheets, beneath the slow governor-thump of the turbines and the shrill *chink-chink* of a million pipe-frogs, there is a sharper, smaller grammar running through the metal. *Tap-tap. Pause. Tap-slide-tap.* 

I am the one keeping the ledger of the crawl.

Fifty years is a strange age down here. Up on the regional plane, before the shafts breached, fifty was scarcely past the middle of a life; down in the eighty-five-degree milk, where the wet chalk tries to mortar your joints shut if you sit still for an afternoon, fifty makes me one of the oldest scaleless things upright on two legs. My knees are thick, bulbous knots of scar tissue and yellow pad, cured hard enough that I can kneel straight down on raw coral-lip without wincing while I check a flange. The beeswax in my ears has not been picked out since the freight cage brought down the new brass intake-valves six years ago; it has darkened to the color of old mahogany, flush with the cartilage, sealing the drum behind an undisturbed plug of warm tallow. I do not remember what air sounds like when it moves through leaves, or what a human throat sounds like across a room. Sound is a solid object. It lives in brass, cast iron, dolomite, and bone. When I want to hear the basin, I lay my palm against a stilt or bite gently down on the flat bronze pry-bar I carry slung across my shoulder-blades, letting the vibrations walk straight up my canines into the base of my skull.

My station is the western terminus—not because of the freight cage, though I am one of the two in rubberized aprons who walk out into the grey shaft-drip every second Tuesday when the hoist-cable begins to sing. The younger Tenders dislike the shaft landing. Cold drafts drop down those four hundred vertical feet alongside the grease drums, carrying thin, dry surface air that cracks the sulfur-paste on their shins and smells unsettlingly of dead dust and pine resin. To me, that cold draft is just another gauge to read. I step into the cage while my partner rolls out the empty carbide cans, pick up the stub of white grease-pencil chained to the slate board, and write down seven numbers: four pump-head pressures, two sump levels, one line-drop figure. 

Sometimes there is a smudge on the slate where someone upstairs wiped away last fortnight’s column with a dry rag—a pale, chalky swirl left by fingers that have fingernails clean of rust-slime, fingers that wear wool sleeves. Once, three Tuesdays ago, whoever wiped the board wrote two words across the top corner in neat, square block letters: *ALL CLEAR?*

I stared at the dry white wax for ten seconds while water ran off my shaved brow and beaded over the letters. There was nothing broken in the pumps. There was no surge in the sumps. The mountain was falling inward at four millimeters a month, just as it always had, and forty people were chipping four millimeters a month back off the iron. I took the heel of my greased palm, wiped the two words cleanly off the slate, wrote my seven numbers in their usual vertical row, stepped back onto the warm travertine landing, and jerked the copper bell-wire twice.

The cage went up. It always goes up. What would you even tell a dry rag?

Most of my hours aren't spent by the shaft at all, though. You don't need a pair of broad twenty-year-old shoulders to read where the rock is trying to pinch a casing shut before the iron shears. While the crews work the pneumatic scalers along the open catwalks, I walk the older, half-swallowed lateral lines—the forgotten four-inch air pipes and armored power runs strung forty years back across the deep amphitheaters toward Station Four, where the amber bulbs hang thirty yards apart instead of twenty and half of them are drowned to their brass necks in rising sulfur-ochre stone.

I travel dark. Without a carbide burner hissing on my sternum, the caddisflies leave my face alone, and my eyes—squinted down for decades until the pale lashes stuck together in little tallow-points—have learned to drink whatever faint, murky copper glow leaks through six thicknesses of mist from the nearest caged filament. You don't see shapes moving in the dark that way; you see interruptions in the sheen. Every surface down here shines: the wet dolomite blocks, the pulsing crimson gills of the forearm-thick salamanders clinging above the moss-line, the rippling sheets of cave-fish turning in unison when my bare calf parts a pool. Even the blackest shadow has skin on it, slick with mucilage, catching a wet thread of orange light from fifty yards off.

I walk with my left hand trailing lightly along the crusted curve of a discharge pipe or high-tension conduit, reading its pulse through three inches of crystalline white rind. Healthy steel hums in clean fourths and fifths with the diesel drives half a mile off. Steel under torsion—where a shifting dolomite block has settled two degrees in the talus below a terrace, or where three tons of weeping lime-fringe is pulling a pipe hanger out of plumb—develops a sour, tight rattle underneath the bass thud, like a cracked tooth when you bite hardtack. 

When I feel that sour note under my calloused thumb, I stop. I unhook the short bronze hammer from my conveyor-belt kilt and strike the stone sleeve three sharp blows: *clink, clink, pause, clink.* 

Half a mile away, straddling a manifold outside Station Two, one of the young ones feels that stutter come up through the soles of his feet, pauses his needle-scaler, and raps back twice to say he’s got the bearing. Before my next sleep-shift is out, two of them will wade down here with an air-hose and ten pounds of bronze wedges to relieve the strain.

Between the pipes, where the terraces drop away in wide, warm staircases of cream and rust-red crystal, I wade through the lagoons with my six-foot pry-bar sliding ahead of my toes along the silt. There is an old female flatback down in the low sump-meadows between Three and Four whom I know better than I know half the boys who sleep on the eastern grates. She must go four hundred and fifty pounds if she goes an ounce—a whole tableland of scalloped orange tufa riding over four pale, tree-trunk legs, with a triangular chip missing from the rear margin of her shell where a falling ceiling-slab grazed her twenty years back, long before that fracture sealed itself with flowstone. 

She has grown a curving horn of pale yellow calcite right off her left shoulder-scute, thick as an anvil-beak, which forces her head slightly askew when she lies tucked under the warm waterfall below Bulb Nineteen. Every four or five months, when that horn builds out far enough to catch on the submerged taproots dangling from the ceiling cracks, I find her wedged patiently beside the sunken concrete steps of my old monitoring bunker, waiting in knee-deep churn while blind white loaches flicker through the six-inch fringe of pale barbels under her chin.

I don't use an air-scaler on her; I don't carry the hose. I sit right down on the warm submerged step with the milky water lapping my rubber bib, brace one thick yellow foot against the healthy side of her stone-crusted carapace, set the chisel-edge of my bronze bar into the fault-line where shell meets limestone, and strike the pommel steadily with my hammer. 

*Tock. Tock. Tock.*

She never pulls her head in—she couldn't anymore if she tried, with three inches of terraced mineral bridging her plastron—just lets her heavy chin rest across my bare instep, her rubbery throat pumping slow, contented swallows of eighty-five-degree water against my ankle-bone while two dozen tiny amber crabs scramble indignantly out of the crack as the forty-pound horn splits free and splashes into the characins. Before she hauls herself off into the steam, I always scrape two handfuls of the dense, velvety sulfur-slime out of the deep troughs between her spine-ridges and pack it into the little vulcanized pouch on my hip. Rendered down over an exhaust pipe with three spoons of canned lard, turtle-back slime makes a finer, slicker joint-salve than anything scraped off hot stone vents.

When two carbide-measures of time have passed—though since I carry no lamp, I tell the shift’s end by the slight softening of the sulfur-paste behind my knees and the slow ache starting in my thumbs—I climb up out of the pools behind the twenty-foot fluted pillar north of Station One. 

My shelf is higher than the main sleeping grates, tucked fifty feet up in a dry lee-pocket where two massive exhaust stacks from the boxcar below vent their ninety-degree diesel breath past a curtain of wet, ropy tree-roots from the surface world. Up here, the roar of the falling water smooths out into a vast, hollow pressure, like sleeping inside the belly of a bronze bell that someone is rubbing endlessly with a wet palm. No rain reaches my stainless-mesh hammock. Just the warm, oily draft off the stacks, carrying the faint toasted smell of midge-cakes baking down on the manifolds, and the soft, papery *whuff* of bats banking past my toes in the dark on their way out from the ceiling cracks.

Sometimes, before I roll my greased ribs into the cable-weave and shut my eyes, I reach out and take hold of one of the surface taproots hanging beside my hammock. It is thick as my wrist, woody and fibrous beneath its slick brown coat of cave-slime, coming down four hundred feet through solid grey dolomite from an oak or a hickory standing somewhere up in that dry, windy country where the freight cage goes.

If you grip the root tightly and press your cheekbone against the wet bark—closing your jaw so the bone takes the tremor—there is nothing at first except the eternal, governor-locked *thud-thud-thud* of Station One coming up through the stone. Beneath that, nothing. No wind-sway survives four hundred feet of pinched limestone fissure. Whatever weather batters the leaves upstairs dies in the first ten yards of rock. What you feel in the root is just a slow, relentless downward tautness, tough as hoist-cable, drinking the warm steam right out of the dark alongside the rest of us, holding fast to the wet stone while the floor rises millimeter by millimeter to meet the roof.

I let go of the root, wipe the brown mucilage off my palm onto my rubber thigh, and swing my legs into the mesh. Thirty feet below, someone walks out onto the Station One catwalk and raps three times on the iron stringer with a scaling hammer—*all clear on the east line, turning in*—and before the third ring has finished climbing the steel stilt into the stone pillar behind my head, I am asleep inside the thunder.
