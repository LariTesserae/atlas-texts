I would not be in the sunlit column at all, at first. The glare is too stark, the pressure waves in the open nave too square-edged and punishing.

Look instead at the boundary between the dead wall and the mountain—three layers back from the light, where the earliest generation of siphon-barrels anchored themselves to the fresh basalt when the cleft first flooded, before their grandchildren grew over their intake mouths and starved them into hollow copper drums. Between the curved backs of those buried hulls and the raw, stepped fractures of volcanic glass, there is a narrow, vertical slot of dead water. No current moves through it. The screaming forty-second *thud* of the outer foundry arrives here muffled through yards of tarnished metal and rotting gasket-cartilage, softened from a hammer-blow into a heavy, rhythmic thickening of the dark.

At first it looks like mortar. Or like the mineral scale that fuses old plumbing together—a pale, faintly iridescent sheen bridging the gap between a rusted iron carapace and a sharp lip of obsidian, holding the dead tier fast against the rock so the whole stacked cliff of living machinery overhead doesn’t shear away under its own tonnage and crash into the talus.

Stay with a light aimed into the crevice for an hour, though, and you see that the mortar has veins.

It is not a beast with stilts or valves; in a vertical world already packed to suffocation with rigid parts, taking up volume was a losing game. I am flat—nowhere thicker than stacked parchment, most places thinner than blister-skin—spread across forty vertical feet of the back-wall shadow like a sheet of damp leaded glass. My lineage diverged from the tube-worms before the shells ever closed into barrels, keeping the worm’s soft mantle and its gift for laying down metal, but turning the blueprint inside out: instead of rolling a tube around myself, I laid myself flat against the stone and let the foundry build itself *over* me.

There is no head, no central furnace, no single knot of nerves to freeze or burst. Where my pale mantle touches the raw basalt, slow acid-secreting rootlets etch microscopic hair-cracks into the fresh silicates, drinking trace iron and magnesium straight from the unweathered mountain. Where my upper skin presses against the dead underside of the buried copper barrels and the lowest, crushed tiers of iron crawlers, thousands of fine, silver-gray filaments—precipitated native silver and galena crystal wrapped in thin sleeves of myelin-like fat—thread directly into the hairline fractures and corroded rivet-seams of the dead husks.

To the mortician-climbers slipping sideways through the dark plumbing on their lodestone stilts, I am simply the floor that doesn’t shake. When one of those warm, tallow-padded scavengers squeezes past, bracing its ratcheted pry-arm against a seized boiler to reach a rotten gasket, its stilts avoid my pale seams out of habit; my skin is slick with a bitter, alum-heavy mucus that fouls magnetic pads. Yet we have an arrangement older than the sunlit crack overhead.

Watch what happens when an adult mortician finishes bleeding the lethal spring-tension out of a dead millstone snail twenty feet up the wall and begins to pry the shell apart. Rich, greasy gobbets of copper-heavy silt, spilled joint-lubricant, and fine bone-meal drift downward behind the wall-tiers, slipping through the dark plumbing where the little wire-cutter crabs are too boxy to follow. Wherever that slow snow settles against my mantle, the pale sheet puckers gently inward, wrapping a lip of translucent flesh over the scrap to digest it across a week.

In return, I keep the house from falling down, and I remember where the stresses are.

Because this mountain pass split open only recently in geological time, the rock is not done settling, and neither is the hundred-ton wall of brass and iron bolted to it. Every time a millstone snail scores a fresh groove into a load-bearing step, or a patch of dead crawlers rots loose at the hinges, weight shifts across the stacked cliff. Nothing out in the bright nave notices; the siphon-beasts just keep slamming their drop-forge valves, blind to the fact that the ledge beneath them is bowing outward a millimeter a month.

Through forty feet of silver-threaded mantle, I feel the shear long before the stone snaps. Where two buried iron husks begin to grind out of alignment under a shifting overhang, mechanical strain squeezes the tiny galena crystals embedded in my tissue, kicking off faint piezoelectric prickles—a silent, localized itch of voltage in the dark. Instantly, my mantle thickens around thestressed joint, pumping dissolved sulfides out of the lower fault-seep and plating fresh, laminated ribs of fool’s gold and flexible horn-chitin right across the widening gap, soldering dead grandfather-shell to raw basalt until the groan in the metal stops.

And where a seam *cannot* be held—where an old copper drum is finally tearing away from its obsidian step and twenty tons of living tier are preparing to slump—the voltage running through my silver web turns the surrounding water sharp and metallic, bleeding a sudden plume of bitter zinc ions out through the four-inch gaps into the open shaft. Look down near the talus floor when that plume drifts out: you will see the juvenile morticians in their little glued mica hats freeze, read the sour tang on their combs, and scramble rapidly sideways out from under the doomed overhang hours before the rusted weld gives way in a booming cascade of black iron and yellow steam.

Only once a year, when high summer finally brings the mountain sun directly inline with the narrow slit of the pass for twenty minutes at noon, do you see that I am aware of the ceiling at all.

During those weeks, a single thin ribbon of my margin creeps upward out of the deep back-wall dark, threading through the empty spiracles of dead crawlers until it emerges onto a bare chip of black volcanic glass just below the highest encrusted ledge—three feet beneath the blinding white mirror and its rainbow slick of oil. There, exposed to the fierce ultraviolet glare and the full, deafening battering of the open room, my pale edge darkens to purple-black and swells into a row of tiny, fingernail-wide cups lined with polished specular hematite.

They do not see images. They only drink the noon heat where the dark iron mirror focuses the sun’s brief shaft by half a degree, using that tiny seasonal fever to ripen a thousand microscopic spores plated in buoyant hollow spheres of silica. When a passing shoal of piston-jellies clanks against the upper wall and vents a scalding burp of steam, the turbulence tears the ripe cups loose, sweeping a faint, glittery dust upward right into the rainbow grease-slick at the waterline—ready to stick to the wet, split hoof of whatever thirsty ibex or blown alpine beetle next touches the edge of the sky, waiting to be carried across dry stone to the next raw crack the mountain opens.

Then the sun moves past the cliff-lip, the freezing shadow drops back down like an iron gate, the burnt black cups slough away to drift down onto the bright talus of gears below, and everything left of me withdraws behind the copper dead—flat, cold, and listening to the weight of the wall.
