At first, there is the pull of the ceiling—the thought of being the stone horn forty feet up, holding the grease-stiffened rope, feeling the rare, frantic weight of the plateau men jerking at the fibers while the stone behind me sweats its slow mineral cold. Or being the shallow pan of water out on the gravel, taking the drop every three seconds, breaking in a single ring that hits the white lime rim and dies, holding the reflection of six million swirling specks without ever deepening.

Those are honest places in this sinkhole. They match its patience. 

Yet if I stay down here long enough for my sight to adjust to the pewter gloom, I am not the water, and I am not the stone, and I am not quite one of the forty greased bodies folded under the slab either—no single organism has enough room inside its skull here for anything beyond heat, fat, and the angle of a wrist. A single pair of ears plugged with swiftlet down only hears its own jawbone.

Instead, I find myself in the *space between* them when they lie packed four and five deep in the ten-inch slot beneath the monolith, and in the flat, low corridor of air ten inches off the gravel where their hands move.

Call me the grammar of the grease-tap. Call me the memory that has no throat to live in.

Down here, where the continuous *shhh-klick-lak* of the bird-columns scours all sound into surf-static, nothing spoken can cross ten paces, and nothing built can be left standing lest the men on the ropes see an unnatural line against the scree. There are no cairns to say *who died here*, no notched sticks to count the years since the fault-wall last dropped a flake, no songs to carry the names of the plateau-fruits they scoop warm and crushed from the throats of dead night-crows. A people pared down this tight cannot afford to carry spare thought inside a single body; fat is too dear, and a mind wandering in that grey, shadowless sameness goes loose at the edges, stands upright by mistake, starts shouting back at the birds, walks into the cliffs.

So what they know lives externally, distributed across touch, stone-memory, and strict, unbroken repetition. That is where I am.

I am the exact pause between the first tap on a greased collarbone and the second: *tap, pause, tap-tap*. I am not the knuckle, and I am not the bone beneath the tallow-rind; I am the measure of the silence in the cartilage that turns a touch from *wake up* into *the rope at the drip-chimney is twitching*. 

When a girl crouches beside the plateau-men’s cache, strikes three flakes from a dark chert core using their borrowed hammerstone, and then turns the cobble over and over in her mortar-coated palms before setting it back down—I am the sudden hesitation in her wrists until the damp-ring on the stone’s belly aligns to the hair’s-breadth with the dark print in the rock flour. She has never seen the upper world, has never seen a dog or an iron blade, does not even have a word for *iron*. Yet her fingers stop turning the stone at the exact right fraction of an inch because eighty winters ago—though nobody down here counts winters—someone left a cobble crooked by a thumb’s width, and men came down the chimney with torches that stank of pine pitch and stayed three days looking at the dust. Nobody tells that story. There is no tense for *eighty years ago* on a crooked finger held against a pale belly. The story survives solely as the tension in her thumb joint refusing to let the stone sit wrong, and the sideways sweep of her calloused heel brushing three blind beetles over her own track.

In the dark under the great detached slab, thirty feet back from the pewter light where the rock flour is dry as velvet and smells of cold chalk and old marrow, five of them lie tangled together. Their breathing slows in the fifty-degree air until five chests rise as a single flat ribcage. Here, in pitch blackness, with the roar of the chasm muffled through twenty feet of solid limestone to a faint, soothing tremor in the floor, their hands never quite sleep. 

An old man—old meaning forty, his joints thick with lime-water stiffness, his tallow-coat cracked white at the elbows—rests his blunt fingers against the shin of a boy born two fruit-seasons back. Against the slick, grey paste of bird-fat and dust, his thumb traces the map of the basin floor. 

Not as a picture seen from above. None of them have ever looked down from the nine-hundred-foot rim; they do not know the valley is shaped like a trough. He draws it as a sequence of textures and intervals felt through half-inch sole-leather: *long flat gravel* (a slow, steady drag of the flat thumb up the tibia), *the monolith with the split corner* (two sharp presses of the thumbnail), *the seep where the water tastes too bitter to drink* (a pinch of skin, released quick), *the red-banded seam where the heavy birds drop thickest when their crops hold the purple seeded figs* (three warm circles on the hollow behind the knee).

The boy does not open his eyes. His wide dark pupils stay shut behind their oily lids. His leg takes the pressure, and before his waking mind even knows it, his own small hand, resting on his mother’s ribs deeper in the slot, twitches in tiny, half-formed echoes of the same drags and presses. When the old man’s heart stops some uncounted month from now—when they carry his light, grease-sealed body out at a low glide to the base of the cliff and leave him cleanly on the guano-apron so the black beetles strip his keel white in an hour alongside the fallen swifts, leaving nothing tall or strange for the rope-men to wonder at—nothing he knew will have vanished into the ammonia damp. It has already crossed the grease-rind into four other shins and collarbones.

Sometimes I stretch wider than the slot. 

When a woman slits the warm crop of a freshly fallen crow-sized fruit bird on the edge of the talus, scoops out a mashed, purple wild fig, and closes her eyes as the sharp, sunlit sugar hits her tongue—for two seconds, there is a flare in the greyness. She does not picture a fig tree; she does not know what a tree is, beyond the dead, splintering ash poles tied under the overhang, and she would never connect those dry grey sticks with this wet sweetness. She does not picture sunlight, which would blind her weeping, wide-irised eyes in an instant. 

Yet in the tart oil of crushed seeds between her thick molars, I hold a ghost-impression of *up there*: a warm, dry wind she has never felt, a blinding brightness she has never seen, translated entirely into taste and the sudden loosening of blood in cold gums. To her, "sky" is not blue air; "sky" is the flavor of bruised laurel-berries and wild fig inside warm bird-flesh, arriving without seasons out of the swirling black chaff overhead.

And when the loud ones come down the bast ropes, shouting across ten yards of gravel to hear themselves over the surf-roar of wings, lighting their quick, wasteful tea-fires of woody twigs, I am the stillness locking forty scattered shapes into limestone. 

Half a mile apart, out on the bare gravel corridors where sightlines run unbroken into the slate haze, two figures crouch beside drifts of frost-white bird ribs, chins tucked between heels, eyes shut, breathing four times a minute. Neither knows precisely where the other dropped when the rope twitched. They cannot see each other; from three paces off, even another basin-dweller’s eye slides off that matte, grey mortar-rind. Yet neither moves a finger until the exact moment the heavy boots scrape back up into the narrow rock chimney and the grey pillar of woodsmoke begins to thin against the mist-slot nine hundred feet above. 

They rise at the same slow, fifty-degree pace—no faster than condensation gathering on banded chert before the three-second drop—gliding inward toward the cold charcoal ring to lick the white mineral salts from half the ash twigs and leave the other half whole, keeping the truce with a world that must never know it is being watched from the gravel.
