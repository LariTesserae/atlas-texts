I am down in the dark where the river-stones meet the rot. 

Not in the air—even the heavy, breathless air of the middle chamber is too vast, too exposed, full of falling water-weights and the dizzying, ten-second rush of the moth-bellows. I am under the middle cairn, three feet beneath the dry-laid bricks and the papery, hissing traffic of the copper millipedes. Here, the shed skins and dead fern-fronds have been pressed by centuries of their own damp weight into a warm, fibrous peat that holds the ninety-eight-degree heat like a thick mouth.

I am the size of a closed fist, tucked into a knot that has never yet uncurled. 

Right now, I am mostly ear, though I have no ears. My skin—still thin, translucent, unpleated, the color of a Cave-blind root—is pressed tight against the underside of the lowest river-stone. That stone is my drumhead. Through the dense, water-logged basalt of it, the entire eighty-foot world arrives as pressure and rhythm. I do not know what a crab is, or a staircase, or the jagged tear in the caprock forty feet overhead where the amber light leaks in. I know the pit only by the way its weight shifts above me.

I know the *pat* of a fat drop hitting an aroid leaf three yards away; by the time the tiny concussion travels down the stem, into the humus, and against my curled spine, it has been rounded into a soft, dull *thud*, like a knuckle rapping through a mattress. I know the millipedes not as copper ribbons, but as a faint, continuous static in the upper inches of the mulch, a dry fizzing that never stops.

And I know the white lace. 

The mycelium is my placenta and my warden. Its pale, pulsing threads—the same ones that mortared the river-stones together after the old mud washed away—wrap around my folded limbs in a dense, damp felt. We have a truce negotiated in slow chemistry. Every few hours, my skin exudes a thin, tallowy grease rich in salts and urea; the fungus drinks this, swells, and in return pushes fine, sweet droplets of broken-down cellulose and warm water directly against my closed mouth-slit. It tries, constantly, to digest me. That is its nature. If my blood cooled by three degrees, or if the faint, chemical hum of my skin faltered for an afternoon, the white threads would stop feeding me and begin stitching through my soft cartilage before dusk, turning me into mortar for the cairn. 

So I stay just warm enough, burning almost nothing, my heart beating once for every three cycles of the moths' wings far above. *Ten seconds open, ten seconds shut; ten seconds open, ten seconds shut; lub-dub.*

For years—or maybe one long, unbroken afternoon, for time does not divide itself down here—there is only the hum of the fissures below me and the heavy, rhythmic housekeeping above. 

I know the Keeper long before I will ever be the Keeper. 

Every twelve or fifteen days, a massive, muted displacement moves through the peat. It has no sharpness to it—no scrape of claw, no sudden drop of weight. It is simply a slow, hydraulic darkening of the pressure around the cairn. The river-stone above my back settles a fraction of a millimeter deeper into the mulch as sixty pounds of bone and pleated velvet lean against the stack. 

When it is that close, its chemistry seeps down through the soil with the rising steam, reaching the two tiny, raw slits above my lip. It tastes of crushed fern, iron-sweat, crab-lime, and me. It is my own scent, aged and thickened by decades of open air. 

I feel the stone directly above me shift—a slow, grinding sigh muffled by fungal mats—as two broad forearms wrap the cairn and squeeze the leaning cobbles back into plumb. I feel the faint, rhythmic tug through the mycelium network as its blunt thumbs pinch away the thickest blooms of white lace from the upper crevices, pruning the very web that holds me in the dark. For three minutes, our two heartbeats share the stone: mine slow and embryonic, tucked in the root-rot; its own scarcely faster, vast and patient, pressed against the wet moss outside. Neither of us acknowledges the other. There is nothing to say. So long as that heavy, careful pressure returns to straighten the stones, the chamber is full, and my job is to remain a fist of cartilage in the dark, growing by grains, costing the pit less than a single mushroom.

Sometimes, strange shocks come down through the soil. Once, a terrible, blazing weight stood on the brick terrace—something whose footsteps punched sharp, erratic craters of vibration through the humus, hammering with a frantic, rapid-fire pulse that made the millipedes above me boil into a tangled, hissing knot. I shrank tighter into my tallow-coat, tasting a sharp, burnt-dust tang filtering down through the steam that made my nascent nasal filaments curl back in revulsion. Then I felt the Keeper slide down the wall. A long silence held the pit—three seconds of faint, steady vibration transmitted all the way down through the bricks—and shortly after, the frantic hammering scrambled clumsily upward, tearing vines, knocking loose pebbles that pattered loudly into the ferns, until the surface swallowed it again and the moth-bellows smoothed the torn air back into stillness.

I went back to sleep. My thumbs—four on each hand, folded neatly over my sunken, velvet eye-hollows—lengthened by the width of a snail's shell. My skin began to loosen at the shoulders, folding in on itself in shallow creases that had never yet caught a drop of falling steam, rehearsing for the air.

I am waiting for a specific silence. 

Not the quiet of the room—the room is always quiet—but an absence in the maintenance. I am waiting for the season when the leaning river-stone above me tilts a fraction of an inch out of true as the fungus swells, and nothing comes to press it back. I am waiting for the faint, sour-sweet flood of rich nitrogen and heavy salts in the mycelium network that will tell my sleeping mouth that sixty pounds of terracotta hide has finally settled into the warm mulch behind the brick shrine and stopped pushing back against the white lace.

When that rich meal hits the fungal threads, the truce down here will break. Gorged on the old Keeper, the mycelium will surge, growing thick and aggressive, pressing tighter around my ribs, trying to claim the spare along with the master. The heat under the cairn will turn suffocating. 

That will be my cue to unfold. 

I can already feel how the joints will work—double-hinged, oiled in their grey sheaths, built to lever upward through three feet of matted roots without snapping a single dry twig. I will push my flat skull past the lowest river-stone, tearing free of the white threads, pale and slimy and blind to light. For the first time, the ninety-eight-degree air will hit my damp, unfolded pleats, and it will feel startlingly cold—three degrees cooler than the peat—shocking my shallow lungs into their first slow, silent draw. 

Water will immediately bead along my shoulders and run down the new channels to the corners of my mouth. The chamber will bloom across my pink, hollow face in a sudden, silent roar of forge-bright fissures and tiny, steady gecko-lamps hanging in the dark. 

I will not stretch or cry out. Before the disturbed mulch has even settled back over the gap I left, I will reach up with four grey-padded thumbs, find the leaning river-stone that woke me, and press it silently back into line with my chest. Then I will slide behind the nearest maroon leaf, taste the snail-trails on the sweating sandstone, and see which of the crabs on the western stairs has grown wheezy in the gills while the pit was changing hands.
