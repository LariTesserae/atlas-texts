I am not down here with the mason. There is no room left in the thicket for a second body, and anything with lungs like a bellows or warm red blood would ruin the work in three minutes—boiling the delicate, freezing stillness with heat, knocking the river-pebbles from their inverted shelves with a clumsy elbow. 

Wait forty feet straight up—or thirty-eight feet up and four feet sideways, if you measure by the way the purple hairline cracks actually lean—above the drowned peat, where the cold wind comes off the gray surf and flattens the cotton-grass.

Where the recent slump tore the turf open, a crescent of black mud lies exposed like a split lip. At the bottom of that ditch sits a pool no wider than a washbasin, choked with rusted iron-scum and drowned sphagnum. Two marsh-reeds poke up through the center of that scum, dead straight, side by side, though the wind tries all afternoon to lay them flat. They do not bend because their lower ends are wedged forty feet down—or three inches down, through a pinched purple seam where the traprock missed its footing—held fast in the cold dark below.

I am what comes along the edge of the slump at low tide with a canvas sack clinking quietly against my hip.

Not quite one of the village people from two miles inland, though I wear their greased wool, layered stiff with lanolin and peat-smoke against the mist, and walk on two legs that ache in the knees when the barometer drops. Look at my hands when I kneel in the wet sedge beside the washbasin pool: the fingers are a fraction too long, unwebbed, the nails thick and split down the middle, stained black to the quick with bog-iron. The palms are ridged like weathered driftwood. When I breathe out into the salt-mist, the vapor comes very thin and slow, once a minute, hoarded in the chest out of an old family habit we haven't quite bred out since half of us stayed above ground when the coast began to sink.

Someone has to fetch the stones. 

Basalt won't do it. The black traprock belongs to the fracture; it wants what the fracture wants. If you wedge a shard of basalt into a leaning seam, it agrees with the violet dark and slips sideways with it. To hold a confused room open, you need a stone that remembers a single, simple "down" for ten thousand years—flat gray greywacke rolled smooth by honest surf, heavy with ordinary coastal weight, completely certain of which way the center of the earth lies.

I spend the morning walking the shingle beach half a mile west, stooping where the winter rollers sort the gravel by size. I test them blind, rolling each wet disk between thumb and forefinger with my eyes shut against the pale sky, feeling for balanced grain, no hairline frost-cracks, nothing sharper than a dull thumb. A flat oval the size of a penny. A slightly thicker wedge the width of two knuckles. Into the sack they go, clicking dryly against three lengths of split gull-wing bone and a tight skein of horsehair begged from the tinker's mare and braided three-ply by firelight.

Back at the slump in the fen, the cotton-grass hisses. There is nobody for miles; sheep won't graze within fifty yards of the crescent ditch because their hooves feel the ground pull slightly crooked beneath the turf, making them dizzy and mean.

I kneel on a dry hummock, push my sleeve back to the shoulder, and plunge my bare arm down into the iron-scummed basin.

For the first foot it is only bog-water and cold silt. At the elbow, the silt stops, and my hand passes through a skin of water as clear and thick as winter grease, four degrees colder than the marsh above it. My wrist bends where there isn't room in the mud for a wrist to bend. Down below—not forty feet now, right *there*, just past the cuff of my rolled wool sleeve—the sourceless plum-colored dark presses cold against my knuckles, and a stiff rubbery lobe of black liverwort brushes my knuckles like an ear.

I do not reach further. If I pushed my shoulder in, the seam might try to take the rest of me and find there isn't space enough to fold my ribs the way my cousin folds theirs. 

Instead, I hold my hand open in the freezing clear sheet, palm upward—which down there is sideways, pressing toward the right-hand wall—with four surf-worn pebbles, a twist of scraped reed-pith, and a fresh coil of braided mare’s hair resting in the woodgrain hollow of my skin.

I wait. Above me, a curlew cries once over the saltings, thin and high. Down below my wrist, there is no sound at all, only the immense, packed hush of the cold fronds.

Ten minutes pass. My arm goes white and tallowy in the freezing pipe, though it does not go numb; our nerves were built for water just above ice. A tiny bubble of old, exhausted breath rises—no, slides sideways—across my forearm, slips through the grease-thick boundary, and *bloops* softly through the iron-scum beside my chin, smelling faintly of crushed fern and tallow.

Then, out of the tight, muscular press of the bladder-stalks below, four long, flat-boned digits with split black nails come sliding over mine. 

There is a faint, muffled *chink* under their skin as the wrist-plates settle. My cousin’s hand is thicker than mine, heavier with cold fat, grooved deeper across the palm, entirely blind and entirely sure of me. Before lifting the stones, one long spatulate finger traces the calluses along my thumb—slowly, deliberately—checking the tension in my tendons the way it checks the braided cords in its lintels, making sure the surf is still flat where it belongs, making sure the sky hasn't started leaning sideways too.

I tap twice against the flat bone of its knuckle: *All level up here. Tide’s out.*

One by one, with neat, unhurried precision, the dark nails pinch the gray beach-pebbles from my palm, take the horsehair, take the tiny bundle of white reed-pith. In their place, pressed firmly into the cup of my hand, comes a single discarded flake of greywacke whose grain finally tired and split under six months of crooked weight, along with two inches of frayed, rotten hair-cord that gave way in the night. Nothing can be left loose down there to drift into the wrong half of the room and foul the balance.

The cold fingers linger for three seconds more against my wrist—the whole of our conversation for the month—and then fold flat and slide back into the rubbery dark without stirring a single stalk of weed.

I draw my arm slowly back through the cold kink in the mud, feeling my bones straighten out as they re-enter the ordinary air. I drop the spent pebble and the rotten twine into my pouch to throw back into the surf where they can forget how heavy they had to be, wipe the yellow iron-slime from my forearm with a handful of dry moss, and pull my wool sleeve down over my split nails. 

Before standing up, I lean low over the washbasin pool and put my ear half an inch from the two hollow marsh-reeds sticking out of the scum. Way down at the other end of them, wedged into the violet hairlines of the traprock, there is a single, soft, solid *clack* as the new beach-stone clicks upward against its overhanging shelf, followed by the barely audible, one-note hum of fresh horsehair snapping taut. 

The ditch beneath my knees stops making my inner ear ache. The mud settles by the breadth of a fingernail. I sling the lightened sack over my shoulder and walk back toward the shingle before the fog rolls in, watching my feet to see which pebbles the afternoon tide brings up next.
