I would not be one of the great domes—that is too much estate to manage, too heavy a crown, too many centuries spent bullying the bedrock and grinding neighbors down for mortar. And I could never survive the open flats, where to live is to click and scrape and scream at the top of your lungs until the second your shell cracks. 

Look closer at the mid-slope of an old Bell, about four feet off the shale, tucked just beneath an overhanging eave made from a giant grazer’s breastplate. Right where one of the dorsal wind-harps lets out its breath.

At first, you would take me for a smear of misplaced lime-spit, or a thickened knuckle in the mortar between two stolen pan-crab claws. I am no longer than a finger, pale as lard, soft-bodied except for two tiny, spoon-shaped plates at my head—not weapons, not radulae, just tuning-irons. I don't have eyes; nobody worth mentioning down here bothers with eyes. What I have is a belly flat and sensitive as an eardrum, pressed tight against the Bell’s bone-cement, and a fringe of fine, damp cilia along my sides that taste the pitch of the air.

If you gave me a name, you might call me a *lutist*, or a *grout-worm*, or a *key*. 

The Bell is magnificent, yes, a genius of slow hydraulic greed, conducting traffic thirty yards away with its gut-stones and singing flyers into its worm-gardens with its flutes. But look at what it builds with: dead things’ armor, hollow pincers, porous spit, living tubes that grow crooked toward the draft. And look at its mason-arms—thick as a man’s thigh, tough enough to pry plates off a feeding slug, blind and clumsy as thumbs in mittens. When a Bell patches its roof, it smears and shoves. It gets the general shape of a terrace right, it points a hollow claw roughly into the wind, and then it pulls its arm back into its cleft.

Left to itself, a Bell’s song goes sour in a month.

Condensation grit cakes inside the singing-claws and drives them flat. A colony of tube-worms grows too fat on a leeward ledge, choking off a whistle-vent until the "safe reef" trill warbles into an ugly hiss that scares the moth-flyers away instead of drawing them down. Worse—far worse—when a Bell spends a season humming against a rival, tiny hairline fractures open in its own mortar. You wouldn't see them in the grey dark, and the Bell, vast and slow and wrapped in its own deep cave-thoughts underneath, barely registers them until the draft starts leaking or the resonance turns dangerously brittle.

That is where I live. In the gap between the grand design and the shifting grit.

I travel in a slick, quiet groove of my own clear mucus, never touching the frantic shale below, never rising above the highest turret-cleft. My whole world is four square yards of terraced, breathing limestone, tilted slightly against the cold wind. Outside, ten million things are screaming and dying in ninety-second bursts; directly beneath my belly, separated from me by eight inches of mortared shell, hangs the silent, silk-pale cathedral of the Bell’s inner vault. I live on the roof between the hurricane and the monastery.

Right now, three tiers up on the windward shoulder, something is wrong with an F-sharp. 

Of course it isn't an F-sharp to me—it is a rough, fluttery drag in the stone under my chin, an erratic *chuff* where there ought to be a clean, hollow *hiss-ring* that pulls the fat little tail-whip flyers down out of the mist. I turn toward it, creeping along the sheltered lip of agutter where minced shell-milk washes past on its way down to the Bell’s skirt. I take a mouthful as I go—just the finest, frothiest scum caught in an eddy, warm with iodine—and keep moving. A miniature dome-strider, no bigger than a harvestman, steps high over my back on four hair-thin legs, hunting lice in the worm-reefs. It taps me once with a feeler and moves on. Nothing eats me on this mountain. If anything bites me, I taste starkly of the Bell’s own caustic mortar-lime, which I drink from its weeping pores when they are fresh.

I reach the singing-claw. It’s an old piece, salvaged decades ago from some bull predator of the shin-deep pans, big as a boot and curved like a horn, cemented wide-end-out into the turret. Inside its throat, a wad of grey tallow-dust and shed flyer-scales has balled up in the damp, spoiling the taper.

I slip inside the horn. Here the roar of the valley narrows into a single, howling tunnel of cold wind that tries to pluck me off the bone. I clamp down hard with my flat foot, tuck my head, and bring out my two tiny, spoon-shaped plates. *Scrape. Scrape. Click.* Slowly, working by feel—not touch-feel, *pitch*-feel, waiting for the bone beneath my belly to stop stuttering and start ringing cleanly again—I pare the tallow-plug away. Not all of it. If I clear it completely, the old horn, worn thin at the lip by years of wet wind, will screech too high. So I leave a crescent ridge of hardened grease on the lower curve, tamp it smooth with my chin, and spit a thread of stolen lime-glue over it to glaze it against the drizzle.

As I back out of the throat, the wind catches the new bevel. 

The ugly flutter vanishes. Out into the dark goes a pure, glassy, pulsing *trill-trill-trill*—an exquisite lie, the exact acoustic signature of a sprawling, predator-free tube-reef warming itself on a geothermal vent. Within two minutes, I feel the soft *pat-pat-pat* on the stone around me as a cloud of blind flyers folds its leathery wings and drops trustingly into the waiting fans of the worms six inches below. Soon the gutter beneath me runs rich and cloudy again.

That is half my life: the wind-side, the quick music, tuning the traps.

The other half happens when the Bell speaks.

Once every few days—or continuously, if we are creeping toward a confluence where another dome sits—the bladder-flutes go slack, the skirt clamps down tight to the shale below, and the great ballast-stones in the crop begin to roll.

If you are standing on the valley floor when a Bell thrums, it shakes your sternum. Up here, glued directly to the sounding-board of its spine, it would liquefy anything that wasn't built for it. My whole body goes slack as jelly to ride it out, letting the immense, sub-audible *oomm* pass straight through my water and out into the mist. 

And while it passes, I listen for the dead spots.

Sound moves through a healthy dome like a smooth swell through deep water, rolling evenly from the thick breccia at the skirt up to the crown. Where a seam has dried out or cracked, though, the wave trips. Two stolen plates knock microscopically together instead of singing as one; an edge buzzes hot and sharp against my belly. Left alone, that buzz is where a rival’s hum will eventually pry the roof open.

When I find a buzz, I don't try to fix it myself—I am too small to haul armor. Instead, I creep into the hairline crack, press my tail-glands against the dry limestone inside, and release a tiny drop of sharp, sour ester that smells—to a Bell—exactly like torn mantle-flesh.

It takes twenty minutes. Everything with the great ones takes twenty minutes. First comes a slow shift of hydraulic pressure deep beneath me, like winter ice settling on a pond. Then, out of the high turret-cleft above, oozes one of the blind mason-arms, grey and wrinkled as an elephant’s trunk, smelling of cave-dark and wet chalk. Its broad, calloused tip fumbles down the terrace, searching for the "wound." I pull myself two inches aside under an overhang and wait, folded small. 

The great blunt limb finds my scent-mark, snuffles over the buzzing seam, hesitates, and then coughs up a thick gobbet of warm, fizzing lime-spit from a pore near its wrist, plastering it hard across the crack and pressing a stray shard of grazer-shell into it for good measure. When the arm withdraws, I slide back out onto the warm, wet patch before it sets, flattening and smoothing the clumsy mound with my belly, drawing its edges out thin so it won't spoil the airflow to the lower flutes.

Does the Bell know I am here? 

Not one of me. Perhaps it knows us in the aggregate, the way you know whether your throat is clear or raspy, the way a cathedral might feel the difference between being tended and going to ruin. There are maybe forty of us on a cottage-sized ancient, fewer on a young traveler. When two Bells lock shoulders for a year-long humming war, churning the grazers around them into paste, we are the frantic crew in the rigging—darting through the shivering cracks while the whole mountain groans under us, calling the mason-arms to weak spots, cutting loose shattered claws before their rattling tears a whole terrace apart, keeping our dome’s pitch steady and true until the other dome fractures.

And if our dome is the one that cracks?

When the deep vault-wall finally gives way and the cold valley roar rushes for the first time into that secret, silk-hung dark underneath—well. We don't die with the pale chandelier. While the victor’s heavy arms are reaching across the rubble over the next three seasons, prying loose our old sovereign’s best singing-horns and thickest breastplates to cement onto its own back, I simply tuck myself inside the curve of a good claw and wait to be carried over.

A new mountain, a slightly different chord in the gut-stones, a strange set of gutters to learn. I taste the new mortar, creep out onto the windward flank, feel for where the draft catches rough on an unpolished rim, and bring out my spoons.
