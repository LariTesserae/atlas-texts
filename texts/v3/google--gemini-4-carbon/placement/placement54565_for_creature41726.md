I would not be in the water. The water has its own slow, heavy economy—the blind things stacked in the warm silt, the tallow-foot sliding from shelf to shelf—and I don't have the mass for it. To live at the bottom of the pan requires a kind of hydraulic patience, a willingness to be four hundred pounds of cold tallow and wait a week for a meal to rot soft. 

Look up instead, past the sweating shoulders of the boiler tanks, to where the pillars lean in so close their mineral skins nearly kiss, about twelve feet above the black sumps. Up here, the stone is still slick, still weeping sideways, still coated in those overlapping slate-blue tiles of fungus, because nothing in the basin is ever dry. Being dry here would feel like dying, like parchment curling over a coal. 

Up here runs the wire. 

Not just the one sagging span that spits blue needles into the mist, though that is part of my road. There is a whole forgotten trellis of them—heavy, braided copper cables thick as a thumb, their ancient gutta-percha insulation long since rotted into black crumbs and replaced by crusts of pale lime and ochre scale. They loop from pillar to pillar, bolted into the limestone on squat, bell-shaped porcelain insulators glazed a slick, wet brown. Where the cables pass through the narrowest clefts, they vibrate. You can't hear it over the *shhhk* of the crustaceans and the six-second sigh of the brass wheel below, unless you press your jaw directly against the porcelain. Then your whole skull fills with a low, steady *mmmm*—fifty cycles a second, ragged and dropping out whenever the runoff stutters down in the dark, a faint, frayed thread of current dragged up from some deep geothermal dynamo or water-turbine buried fifty yards deeper in the shale where nothing big can reach.

I live on the bells.

If you shone a light up into the pinch between two pillars—which you wouldn't, because light doesn't belong here, light is just that sudden blue *zzt* that smells like sharp tin—you would probably mistake me for a snarl of stripped wire and old, blackened grease wrapped around an insulator bracket. 

I am long, no thicker at the ribs than three fingers pressed together, and built almost entirely of gristle, slick skin, and knuckles. A spine like a bicycle chain wrapped in wet chamois leather. No fur—fur in this broth-air would just mat with iron-slime and drag me down into the pools inside of a week, heavy and shivering, to be tucked under a great flat foot. Instead, my skin is bare, slate-dark on top and the pale, translucent grey of boiled tendon underneath, slick with a thin, waxy tallow I comb over myself constantly so the sideways water beads right off me and drops *plink, plink* onto the fungal shelves below.

Count the limbs if I move, though I don't move all at once. Six of them, maybe eight depending on whether you count the two short, crooked claspers tucked right under my throat. My hands are disproportionate—long, spidery things with four opposable digits each, tipped not with claws (claws skate right off wet porcelain and brass) but with broad, finely ridged pads that work by suction and friction, like the toes of a cave-gecko crossed with a river-leech. My tail is twice my body length, heavy at the base and tapering to a bare, grey whip that is always wrapped two revolutions around a bracket or a copper return-pipe before I let go with more than two hands. Falling is the only sudden thing that happens in this courtyard. Everything down in the black water knows what *plop* means. I do not *plop*.

Why up here? Because the great bell-mantles down in the mud have the copper transfer pipes, and the stacked amphibians have the submerged conduits, and I have the transformers.

Wedged high into the stony armpits of the basin, well above the flood-line of the sumps, are cast-iron junction boxes no bigger than bread loaves, half-swallowed by weeping flowstone. Inside them, potted in black tar that has cracked with the centuries, copper coils step down whatever ragged voltage still trickles through the basin. They are three degrees warmer than the fog. Not hot—just a dryish, vibrating breath of old tar and warm rust leaking through a rusted-out louver.

That is my hearth. 

I share the upper stories with the clutches. The big mollusks down below—I know all three of them without ever having seen them whole; to me they are just shifting, breathing continents of grey stone down in the gloom, smelling faintly of calcium-paste and crushed jelly—they reach way up here once or twice a season. It takes them an hour of slow, hydraulic heaving to rear half their bulk out of the sump, bracing against two pillars until the water sheets off them like a weir, uncoiling one of those pale, endless arms seven feet straight up into my territory. 

When the white, tongue-tipped fingers come groping up past the copper wires, blind and sure, smelling of cold silt, I don't bite them. You don't bite the weather, and you don't bite the mason. I just flatten myself against the underside of a cable, clicking my throat-claspers softly together—*tik, tik*—so the pale pads know which lump on the porcelain is an insulator and which lump is me. Sometimes a flat white fingertip brushes right across my ribs as it tucks a fresh string of leathery, grease-coated eggs behind the warm junction box. Its skin feels like smooth, wet soapstone, pulsing faintly with a sixty-second heartbeat. Then the arm pours back down into the pewter dark, and I scuttle back over to inspect the clutch.

People from the flats—if anyone ever came down the shale wall again, sliding and swearing in the slick mud—might think I eat the eggs. 

I don't. You couldn't chew through that rubbery, grease-cured casing with needle-teeth anyway, and besides, if there were no more big mantles down below, nobody would clear the trough when the ochre fungus slumps. Nobody would paste the weeping rivets shut. Within ten years the flywheel would choke on silt, the buried valve would give its last *thock* and drown, the humming boxes up in the clefts would go stone-cold, and thirty feet of cellar-damp would settle into my marrow until I simply stopped uncurling. I don't *know* this the way an engineer knows a schematic; I know it in my ribs, the way I know the six-second dip and sigh of the brass wheel down below is the sound of the world staying alive. When the wheel dragged out to nine seconds just now, I froze upside down on my wire, every pad clamped tight, the thin skin behind my ears prickling as the 50-cycle hum under my belly began to sag and starve.

No, I tend the eggs the same way the charcoal crabs tend the big mantles' backs. 

The pale, blind spiders that live behind the cracked pressure gauges, and the tiny, boring mites that drift in on the fog—they love the warm grease on those egg-casings. They spin sour little webs in the crevices behind the insulators; the ochre fungus tries to creep over the leathery shells and smother them. So I make my rounds. Hanging by my tail and two hind-feet from a sagging wire, swinging gently over a twelve-foot drop into black water, I reach behind the humming iron box with my two tiny throat-hands and pick the casings clean. I peel back the encroaching rims of blue fungus—chewing the rubbery trimmings slowly, tasting iron and cold sulfur—and flick the mites off with a wet, grey tongue. 

For real meat, I fish the air.

This is the best part of being long-armed in a narrow place. Up where the pearl-gray ceiling presses right down onto the slick crowns of the pillars, the teacup jellies drift thickest, riding the tiny, invisible plumes of warmth rising off the wires and the breathing-pores of the giants below. From down in the sump, they probably look slow, mindless, bumping off wet slate. Up here at eye level—and my eyes are huge, bulging, dark-adapted globes filmed over with clear nictitating membranes that wipe sideways every few seconds to squeegee off the mist—they are entire floating meadows. Their sticky, trailing threads glitter with trapped black midges like beaded curtains.

I wait on a porcelain bell until a bladder drifts within a yard of a leaning flute of rock. I don't jump; jumping is for places with grass and sky. I unspool. Back feet anchored on the cold ceramic, tail snubbed tight around the copper braid, I stretch out horizontal over the gap until my spine pulls taut, catch a wet limestone runnel with one suction-palm, and sweep my free arm through the trailing threads like someone gathering spindle-wool. 

You have to roll the sticky filaments into a ball against your chest-pad before the jelly buoys up and glues itself across your face, then nip the gas-bladder with your front teeth—*pff*—a tiny sigh of swamp-gas, and tuck the whole peppery, midge-studded tangle into your cheek. Tastes like cold pond-water, vinegar, and chitin. 

Watch out for the blue spark, though. 

I know every bad joint on the circuit. Where the mist condenses heavy on the frayed bend above the flywheel, you can feel the air tighten half a second before it arcs—a sudden, prickly dry taste on the roof of the mouth, all the tiny sensory bristles along my flanks standing straight up through the tallow. If you have one hand on the bare green copper and one hand on the wet limestone when it bridges—*zzt!*—it knocks every suction pad loose at once. Happened to a smaller cousin of mine two molt-cycles ago; there are only four or five of us in the whole courtyard, irritable and solitary, hissing quietly like leaking steam-cocks whenever we meet on the same cable. He misjudged a wet patch near the cracked gauge, took the jump across his shoulders, and dropped straight past the crossing return-pipes without a sound. 

*Plop.* The black water rolled once, heavy and muscular, silt smoking up around three dozen pale gills, and by the time the blue afterimage faded from my big, filmed eyes, the wire above the sump was empty again, humming softly to itself.

So I respect the ceramic. Step on the brown glaze, step on the green pipe, never bridge the two when the mist thickens. 

Sometimes, though, when the cold presses down unusually hard from the shale flats above—when winter happens out there in the empty world we never see, and the sideways water beading out of the limestone turns bitter enough to stiffen my long fingers—I do a small piece of work of my own. Not like the great masons below with their pearl-weld paste; I have nothing in me that turns to stone. 

Up near the perimeter wall, where the copper wire snakes out of a cracked slate conduit, the iron bracket rusted through years ago. Left alone, the cable sags an inch lower every month, closer and closer to a jutting wet shoulder of limestone. If it rests flat against the rock, the current will bleed straight into the wet earth, grounding out in a steady, sizzling leak, starving my warm tar-box three pillars down, starving the blue spark, maybe starving the little captive bubble of oil down in the sight-glass that the old giant watches with his cluster of oil-drop eyes.

I don't understand grounding. I just hate the sizzle, because when it sizzles over there, my box grows cold over here.

So over the last few seasons, whenever I come across a dead charcoal crab wedged dry in a pipe-joint, or shed plates of iron-worm tube that haven't gone soft yet, or dryish curls of peeled gutta-percha, I carry them up to that sagging bend in my throat-hands. Piece by piece, wedging and jamming with my flat thumbs, smearing it over with old black tar dug out of the junction seams and my own waxy skin-tallow, I've built a clumsy, bulbous little bolster between the green wire and the weeping rock. It looks like a rat's nest made of slate-shavings, crab-shell, and earwax. Every time a heavy slime-curtain slumps against it, half my work slides down into the mud and I have to wedge a fresh crab-carapace in there, clicking my teeth in annoyance in the pewter dark. 

Right now, the flywheel down below has found its pace again. 

One, two, three, four, five, six—*sigh*. *Thock.* 

The tremor comes up through the bedrock, into the iron bolts, through the brown porcelain bell, and right into the broad, suctioned soles of my hind feet. Everything is back in time. Below me, the pale arm has folded itself away; the boulder between the X-pipes is just a boulder again, water braiding cleanly down its rubbery hood to feed the red fans of the worms. 

I wipe my left eye clear with a slow blink of membrane, then my right. Behind my shoulder, inside the rusted bread-loaf box, the tar-potted coil hums its steady, ragged note, bleeding three degrees of borrowed summer onto the leathery eggs and the base of my spine. A teacup jelly drifts past the pillar's flute, trailing forty threads heavy with black midges, glowing faintly for a tenth of a second as the wire overhead snaps—*zzt*—and goes dark again. 

I unwind one turn of my tail from the bracket, test the wet stone with four wide, grey fingers, and reach out into the cold broth of the air.
