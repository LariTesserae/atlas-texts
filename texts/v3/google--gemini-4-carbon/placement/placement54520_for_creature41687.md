At first, the temptation is to be up on Tier Six where the louvers turn.

There is an attraction to the margin where the pale, dusty shafts of daylight give up the ghost—to be the cold-water intake main itself, carrying the memory of snowmelt and granite creeks four hundred feet down in a thick, sweating cast-iron belly. To be the thing that makes the indoor rain. You could stay cold inside while your skin wept sweet water for the tenders to catch in their tin cups, untouched by the sulfur, passing through the furnace without belonging to it.

Wait ten minutes in the amber mist, though, and that feels too much like tourists’ work. The water in the intake main is only passing through; it doesn't know where it is. 

Drop lower. Past the checkered plates of Tier Four, past the brick-jacketed retorts, past the moss-beds tucked into the blast-fractures. Don’t stop at the copper skin down in the sump either—that tarnished, self-healing mat in the hot broth has the whole future ahead of it, a slow, blind chemistry just waking up to its own hunger. It doesn't need company yet.

Go back up to Tier Two, to the dry steam-riser that runs straight from the lower manifold up through five grated floors into the yellow gloom.

Not the iron of the pipe itself. Iron is just the medium, still half-raw from the foundry, shedding its mill-scale into velvet rust. 

I would be the *ring* inside it. 

The crisp, traveling *tink* of the brass-headed spanner, moving forty feet upward at sixteen thousand feet per second through the hot steel wall.

Think of what lives in that wire-thin sleeve of metal. Below and all around is the heavy, wet, barometric hush: the thirty-second sigh of the canvas mufflers, the deep bass thrumming of the basalt pressing up through bone and sole, the *pat-pat* of scalding grease-water. Everything else in the nave is muffled, thick, cushioned by vapor and tallow and white moss. Sound in the air dies twenty feet from where it starts, swallowed by the heat.

Only the riser is clear. 

When the tender wedged behind the lower condenser shifts his weight—careful not to scrape an elbow on the sharp, unweathered drill-flutes in the rock—and lets the flat of his bronze or brass tool kiss the dry pipe twice, *tink-tink*, something leaps awake in the crystalline lattice of the steel. 

I am that sudden, bright geometry traveling through the dark. I pass right under the stranglehold of the rubbery white vines without disturbing a single blind shoot. I slip through the bolted flanges smeared with fresh graphite paste, jump the hemp-packed seams, and flash past the caged carbon bulbs glowing like coals at the intersections. I am narrower than the fourteen-inch gap between retort and return-main; I don’t even need space, only continuity. 

And the best of it is the arrival. 

Forty feet up on Tier Five, before I have time to thin out and lose myself in the upper structural girders where the rock settles and cracks, there are two horn-calloused, plum-stained fingers already resting lightly against a coupling. Waiting. 

To come up out of the roaring thermal bass of the pit and land cleanly against the pads of two quiet fingers; to be read instantly by a man with a quartz pebble under his tongue and constellations of white burn-stars across his shoulders; to become, in the half-second of contact, *Open a quarter-turn*, and then to feel the duller, warmer answer of his knuckle—*tup*—drop back down the exact same path toward the sump.

When nobody is signaling, I don’t vanish. I sink back into the baseline—into the tiny, irregular cooling-ticks of the girders adjusting their weight, the faint metallic shiver when a counterweight hits its trip-lever on the wall, the subtle change in pitch—half a hertz down—that travels out from a sticking valve through the iron bolted to the stone, crosses onto the split basalt slab behind the retort on Tier Four, shivers through six inches of hot, damp moss, and touches the side of a sleeping jaw, waking him before his eyes are open.

Not the tender, and not the boiler, and not the garden growing on its leaks. Just the nerve running through the bone of the place—the thing that keeps the three of them from being alone in the slot, without ever making them waste their breath.
