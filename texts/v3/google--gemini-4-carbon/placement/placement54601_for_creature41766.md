I am half a mile away, and I am right beneath your feet. 

You would find me first in a long, concrete gallery lit by wire-caged yellow bulbs that never turn off, smelling of hot machine oil, river-silt, and wet wool. I am sixty-two years old, slightly deaf in my left ear, and I wear rubber-soled boots thick enough to keep the turbine deck from rattling the fillings out of my teeth—though after thirty years on the night-and-relief rotation at the station, the *chug-hum-chug* doesn’t stop when I drive home anyway. It lives in my collarbone. When the power authority automated the sluice-gates twelve years ago and cut the crew down to two monitoring technicians per shift, I stayed on because a pension feels too quiet to survive.

Nobody from the plant goes out across the pan toward the fused grove. From the outside, across half a mile of bleached sedge and flat gravel scrub, it doesn't look like a cathedral; it looks like a monolithic, grey-green geological slump, an impenetrable wall of swollen timber sweating mist under a dense thatch of boughs. There’s no door in it. Only the old Number Four ground-bus—laid down in my grandfather’s time before they sank the deep copper rods straight into the spillway bedrock—still runs out through a concrete collar in the substation wall and arrows dead-straight into the scrub. 

We aren't supposed to unbolt legacy grounds without a survey, so it stays hooked to the neutral busbar. And once a week, on Tuesday mornings when the shift changes and the light is flat, I clamp an inductive ammeter around that braided iron line just inside the substation fence.

I do it because the needle moves.

Not much. Just a steady, tiny, rhythmic bleed—milliamps trickling out into the wet coastal pan, steady as clockwork, except twice or three times a day when the resistance shifts for four seconds. A sudden, heavy capacitive load touches the line out there at the far end, dampens the stray harmonic on the wire, and then lets go. And every evening right around civil twilight, if I lay my bare thumb against the cool iron clamp before putting my gloves back on, I swear I can feel a clean fifth—a resonant back-ring coming *up* the metal from the wood, pushing gently against the sixty-hertz drumbeat of Unit Two.

For six years I thought the tree was just shifting in the wind, pinching the swallowed cable inside its cambium. Then one October, walking the line after an equinox gale to check for downed scrub, I got within thirty yards of the outer bark-wall. 

There was no way in—just solid, seamlessly grafted pillars of silver wood rising eighty feet to the first branches, slick with cold water and roaring like the inside of a conch shell held to a giant’s ear. Wedged high up in a dry crease where two outer trunks had fused like closed knuckles, seventy feet off the gravel pan, a bright green coil of discarded 14-gauge insulated copper hookup wire—the exact spool I’d left sitting on an outdoor transformer pad two summers prior—was protruding three inches from the bark, woven tight into a plug of stripped cedar fiber sealing a knothole from the inside.

I didn’t report it. I didn't bring a flashlight back, or try to scale the buttresses, or fly a camera drone over the upper canopy to drop down through the light-gaps. You don't open a sealed watch case when it's keeping time.

Instead, I became part of the weather of the room without ever standing inside it.

Every few months, I walk fifty yards out past the chain-link perimeter and leave a small, neat coil of bright bare copper wire—stripped clean of its plastic jacket so nothing sharp-toothed has to chew vinyl—weighted down under a flat grey stone right alongside the rusted ground-cable. Twice a year, when we grease the wicket-gate bearings with heavy, unscented sheep-lanolin tallow, I wipe my rag clean along three feet of the iron rope just outside the fence, letting the rain and capillary creep carry the oil slowly down the braid toward the trees, though I suspect whatever uses the wire inside has its own oils from bird-fat and wet fur. Once, four months after I left a twelve-foot hank of soft annealed copper under the stone, the stone was still sitting neatly in its hollow in the sedge, and the copper was gone.

If I am human here, that is all I am: the keeper of the engine and the tender of the wire’s dry end. I watch the oil pressure on Unit Two so the bass note never falters. During the scheduled turbine overhaul every fifth April, when management wants to shut both units down simultaneously for forty-eight hours to inspect the main tailrace, I am the cranky senior tech who argues fiercely, citing thermal shock to the stator windings, insisting we stagger the shutdowns so at least one turbine is always turning, always throwing its deep, protective roar across the flat pan. I don't know what would happen inside that hollow drum if total silence suddenly fell on ten thousand screaming birds and forty blind grazers and one pair of wide, horizontal slate eyes—what sudden panic or collapse of invisible tracks would occur if the cello stopped vibrating—and I intend to retire or die before finding out.

***

Except sometimes, at three in the morning in the yellow-lit control gallery, when my forehead rests against the cool glass of the frequency gauge and the *chug-hum-chug* enters my skull bone-direct, I slip down the wire entirely, and I am no longer the man in the rubber boots.

I am the cable itself—or the narrow, sixty-foot seam where the iron meets the wood and the moss.

I am cold, heavy, braided iron, eighty years laid down, forgotten by the schematics. Behind me, half a mile back through sour, wet coastal peat, the great copper coils spin in their oil baths, throwing off their stray, restless ghosts of current. Ahead of me, my end is lost completely—swallowed forty feet deep into dark, living heartwood and wet vascular cambium, gripped in a slow wooden vise that tightens by a millimeter every spring and never crushes, only holds. Sap-water rich in tannins and weak acids bathes my frayed terminal strands deep inside the fused wall, turning the whole massive colossus of grafted trees into my grounding plate. Through me, the machine bleeds its excess tension into the roots; through me, the tree’s slow electrochemical pulse pushes back against the dynamos.

Inside the hollow room, I am the single straight line in a world of curves. 

I do not see the shafts of pale daylight, and I have no ears for the piercing, glorious hysteria of the swifts overhead. What I know is weight, temperature, moisture, and frequency. 

I know the cold belly-slime of the mossy domes when one takes twenty minutes to cross me. They hate my texture at first—too hard, too metallic, buzzing slightly sharper than the peat—and their sensory filaments curl back for a second when they touch my flank. Then they learn that the stray micro-current along my skin tingles cleanly against their undersides, discouraging the tiny white mites that sometimes try to colonize the soft fold between their leathery mantle and their pale mushroom-stem foot. So they linger. Two or three of them will park right along my shallow trough for an hour, heavy and cold as wet river-stones, breathing slow ripples under their green *leucobryum* pelts while mist drips off the vault ceiling onto our backs alike.

And I know the drop.

Three times a day, without warning, forty pounds of dense muscle, bone, and hot blood falls forty feet out of the clear middle air and hits me dead-center. 

I bow downward an inch into the spongy peat, singing a tight, mute *thrumm* from the root-buttress all the way back to the substation collar, taking the kinetic violence out of its ankles and throwing it harmlessly into the earth. For ten seconds, broad, pale, gecko-splayed palms grip my pewter-polished curve. Through its skin I feel a heartbeat remarkably slow even right after the kill—forty beats a minute, deep and steady, already locked back onto the turbine’s rhythm. Then comes the brisk, heavy rasp of wet, mottled fur as it drags its chin, throat, and forearms along my spine, stripping static and sharp hints of ozone into its coat, leaving behind a faint, warm glaze of pigeon-oil and skin-tallow that keeps the cellar damp from ever rusting my middle span.

Then the weight lifts. A slight secondary flex tells me it has sprung twelve feet up into the braided root-arch directly over the scar where the bark swallowed me whole.

At dusk, when the air cools another two degrees and the mist thickens on my iron braids, I feel the wood around my buried end tighten slightly as the great canopy above closes its stomata for the night. Up in the copper-plaited cradle twelve feet overhead, the deep chest fills. When that ten-second harmonic fifth pours out into the darkening cylinder of air, the fused wooden walls catch it, amplify it, and drive it straight down through the gripping cambium into my buried iron core. 

For ten seconds, I carry the chord back out under the wet coastal pan, under the sedge and the gravel scrub, past the chain-link fence, through the concrete collar, and straight into the neutral busbar of the powerhouse—a warm, resonant hum rising briefly under the thumb of an old man in rubber boots before the bats drop down from the ledges and the night shift settles back into its thunder.
