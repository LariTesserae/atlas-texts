My left cheek knows the cold of the stone better than my palm does. 

When you press your ear directly to dry-stacked basalt, the air-noise vanishes. The ten thousand tiny splatters and drips of the open slots cut out, replaced by the deep, tight *thrumming* inside the bone of the wall. Dry stone has a voice when it is happy—a dead, heavy silence that swallows the strike of a knuckle-bone and passes it straight down to the bedrock. When it is unhappy, when a hair-thin thread of cold seep has slipped past a pitch-seal two tiers up and begun to slick the flat, crystalline faces where slab meets slab, the wall changes pitch. It stops swallowing sound and starts holding it. Tap it with a flint, and instead of *tock*, it says *tink*. High, tight, and wet. 

That is where I am: wedged sideways in a vertical drainage slot behind the third-tier curing kilns, twelve inches wide from breastbone to shoulder-blade, breathing shallow so my woolens don't snag the razor-flint edges of the backing rock. It is dark in the seam, save for a narrow, watery ribbon of pewter light twenty feet straight up, and the faint, greasy gold bleed coming through acured-Skin vent-pipe just past my knee.

My right arm is bare to the elbow, the grey wool rolled tight and bound with sedge-string. My fingers—thick, rust-stained in every crease, permanently stiff in the first joints from forty winters of sump-chill—are walked out flat against the dark stone like a blind spider. Feeling for the sweat that shouldn't be there. Feeling for the millimeter of slip.

Up on the scrubland, the cart-drivers think a house stays up forever once you build it. Down here, a house is an argument you have to keep winning every afternoon. The rock is too young; it hasn't forgotten how to split, and its faces are too glassy-smooth to forgive grease or slime. Let a finger's width of black liverwort take root in a load-bearing joint, and thirty tons of tiered slate above the communal grain-bins starts thinking about sliding sideways into the sump.

In a leather loop at my belt—stitched over the stiff, tallow-smelling cone of my amber apron—hangs my whole trade: three flat wedges of pale horn, an iron-hard rib-bone ground to a needle point for hooking slime out of hair-cracks, and a small block of selected grey clink-stone no bigger than a plum. 

*Tock.* 

I tap the retaining course behind the kiln-back. Warmth bleeds through here—the one place in the lower maze where the stone isn't aching cold. It smells of toasted peat, sour steam, and mutton-fat. Through eight inches of interlocked slate, I can hear the muffled, rhythmic *shhhk, shhhk* of the scrapers working the tables on the other side, peeling bottom-slime off yesterday's pull. Two of the boys are talking in that flat, consonant-heavy mutter we use, their voices buzzing faintly against my cheekbone like flies trapped in a bottle.

*Tock.* Solid.

I crab two steps downward, sliding the outer edges of my wooden-soled boots along the wet floor-groove, knees bent, chin tucked to my collarbones. Down here, where the kiln-heat fades, the damp hits back immediately—that iron-sharp, sulfur-edged chill that sits behind your eyes and makes your eyelids heavy. On the underside of the wooden flume bracketed just above my head, two pale thumb-frogs sit belly-flat to the pitch-blackened pine, pulsing their throats in slow time with the water rushing half an inch above their backs. I don't brush them; they eat the midges that lay eggs in the curing-frames. 

*Tink.*

There. Right at waist height, behind the junction where the main step-channel turns sharp left toward the ore-hammers. 

I stop breathing through my nose, let my chest flatten out to buy another inch of room in the slot, and bring my face close to the seam. In the dim, reflected greenish bounce from the wet slate below, the stone looks dry. Dead black, sharp enough on the lip to slice a thumb-pad open without you feeling it until the blood warms your wrist. But when I lay the back of my upper lip—the only skin left on me thin enough to tell thirty-eight degrees from forty—near the hairline gap between the two four-hundred-pound headers, there is a draft. Not air. Water-chill, pushing out ahead of a hidden pocket.

Somewhere behind this course, a bone spout has cracked in its socket, or a mat of bladder-fern has backed up a weep-hole against the raw cliff face. Water is pooling in the blind rubble behind the wall, building head-pressure. Not enough to bulge the stone yet. Enough to do it by Tuesday.

I don't back out of the slot to fetch help; turning around isn't possible anyway, and crabbing thirty feet back to the stair-head wastes heat. Instead, I take the plum-sized clink-stone and rap the dry course just above my shoulder.

*Tock-tock. Pause. Tock-tock-tock. Tock.*

*Shunt-gate. Tier three. North kiln-seam. Shut it.*

I lean my forehead against the flinty dark and wait, counting the *clack-thump* of the counterweight hammer way down in the mist at the bottom of the pit. *One. Two. Three. Four.* 

Above me, sixty feet of intricate, trickling percussion shifts key. A wooden gate drops into a notched slate groove two roofs up; a brisk, steady hiss overhead chokes down to a thin patter, while twenty yards away, an overflow chute suddenly *gulps* and begins to drum hard against a stretched Skin awning as it takes the doubled load. Within ten seconds, the hidden pressure behind the stone at my waist eases. A tiny bead of rust-colored water—squeezed out as the rock settles back by the thickness of a fingernail—bulges from the vertical joint and runs silently down into my floor-groove.

Now comes the slow part. I unhook the ground rib-bone. Working by touch because my own shoulder blocks the sliver of sky-light, I slide the thin bone three inches into the dry seam, angling up toward the cliff face behind the architecture. 

It hits soft resistance. Not mud—there is no mud down here unless someone dropped a rim-basket—but the tough, rubbery cushion of young bacterial mat that blew in as spores or washed down from a stray splash and started knitting itself in the dark where no scraper could reach. Left alone, it seals the weep-channel tighter than cork. 

Slowly, twisting my thick wrist a quarter-turn at a time so the bone won't snap between the slabs, I hook the stuff and draw it out. It comes away in wet, grey-white ribbons that smell faintly like rising dough and old pennies. Slimy, cold, stubborn. I wipe each ribbon onto a small square of cured Skin hung at my hip—nothing stays on the floor to wash down into the sump-pools and foul the three-day stillness with stray grit. 

Scrape. Hook. Wipe. Listen to the wall. 

Twenty minutes it takes to clear a gap no wider than a barley-straw. My bare forearm goes numb past the wrist, turning that mottled, purplish-red every pit-child knows by age six, though my core stays toasted inside the heavy grease-wool and stiff amber shell. When the bone finally punches through to the raw weep-pocket against the mother-rock, a sudden, pencil-thin jet of freezing mineral water shoots straight out of the wall, hits my oil-hardened apron with a crisp *rattat-tat*, and drops cleanly into the floor-drain where it belongs.

I tap the header again with the clink-stone.

*Tock.* Dead, heavy, dull. The stone has its grip back. Friction holds the world up for another month.

I give three quick raps on the wall to tell the tier above they can open the shunt-gate halfway while I cut a new slate lip to permanent-channel this rogue weep tomorrow, then begin walking my boots sideways toward the light at the mouth of the slot.

Stepping out onto the narrow switchback stair feels almost wide open—a whole three feet between the kiln-wall and the drop-off rail, roofed ten feet up by a broad sheet of cured Skin that turns the flat pewter afternoon into a warm, tallow-colored dusk. A teenage girl hauling two amber buckets of ash-slime up toward the rim-ladders comes down-shoulder from the turn above. Neither of us slows. My back rolls flat against the slick basalt; she pivots inward on the ball of her clog, chest-to-chest with me for half a heartbeat—a brief wash of body-heat, sheep-grease, and sharp young breath—and she is past, climbing sideways without spilling a drop from her brims, while I take the step she just left.

Down below, through a three-inch gap between two overlapping awning-sheets, I can see all the way to the sump. In the green-grey murk at the bottom of the world, two of the old pullers are standing ankle-deep on a submerged causeway, frozen mid-step like stone posts, holding their long bone-hooked poles just above a black pool that has gone milky-pale with its second-day skin. Neither of them moves a muscle; someone up on the second tier just coughed, and they are waiting to see if the air shook the water.

It didn't. The surface stays flat as dark ice.

My fingers ache deep in the red knuckles as the blood comes stinging back into them. I tuck both hands high into my opposite armpits under my stiff honey-colored cape, cracking its tallow-glaze faintly against my ribs, and turn down toward our alcove-door. Inside, behind the sedge-lined panels, the clay pot will have two bricks of liverwort-peat smoldering, and the wool fleeces in the wall-bed will be dry. All the way down the wet stone steps, through the thick wooden soles of my clogs, the whole tight, sharp-edged town hums steadily into the bottoms of my feet—ten thousand small waters running cold and dark and clean, not one of them lost.
