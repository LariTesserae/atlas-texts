Start with a fault in the iron, halfway up the eastern retaining wall, twelve feet above the dark water and thirty below the pale slot of sky. 

Where a heavy diagonal tie-rod pins an oscillating pump-frame back into the vitrified brick, a cast-iron washer rusted through long ago and spat its halves down into the flooded vaults. Left with a quarter-inch of play, the rod began to knock. Every three seconds, at the top of the brass turbines’ stroke down in the basin, the tension slacked and caught again: a sharp, feverish *tock* inside the court’s wide, wet roar. Friction made the clevis pin hot. Rainwater and condensed steam trickled down the tie-rod, pooling in the empty bolt-seat, turning the rust into a warm, red, metallic broth.

In a place read entirely by heat and tremor, a knock like that shines like a flare.

At first I am only an appetite drawn up the weeping masonry toward that bright, erratic percussion—dinner-plate wide, blind, flat to the glaze, reading the wall with a fringe of tallow-pale filaments. The brick here hums with a stray fifty-cycle tingle from an overhead junction box, and the mortar tastes of lime and faint nitric sweetness. It takes four vents of the western steam-valve—nearly an hour—just to haul my tiered, mossy shell across the last yard of slick stretchers and headers and ease my gray foot into the notch behind the loose clevis.

The heat is immediate and rich. Pressing dense, gristly muscle into the quarter-inch gap where iron strikes iron does not hurt; my flesh has no nerves for sharp pain, only for resistance and thermal draw. As my mantle wedges into the play of the joint, the sharp *tock* vanishes, swallowed into the dense rubber of my body and converted directly into warmth. The knocking becomes a slow, kneading squeeze—twenty times a minute, regular as tide-surge, pumping nutrient-heavy blood through the copper-dark threads of my underside. 

Most of my kind stay on a warm bracket for a month or a season, drinking the vibration until they grow too broad and heavy with precipitated scale to hold vertical suction, at which point the wall lets them go and they drop cleanly into the lukewarm dark below to stretch out among the sunken vaults.

That would have been my arc, too, except for two things. 

First, the lip of my shell slipped *behind* the flange of the tie-rod while I was still narrow, and as I drank the mineral drip from an overhead brass union, my carapace plated outward in thick, fluted ridges of verdigris and travertine until I was keyed into the ironwork like a dovetail. Second, a festoon of tarred cable, heavy with green algal fur, sagged lower over the years until its frayed belly came to rest directly across the crown of my back.

So I never fell.

Instead, pinned thirty feet below the sky and twelve feet above the water, I kept growing outward from the wall, cantilevering into the bruised greenish twilight like a shelf of wet stone or a massive bracket fungus. 

Because I cannot leave the wire, the wire has grown into me. The weak alternating current does not merely graze my skin for twenty minutes at a time; it courses continuously down through the wet calcified plates of my back, gathers in the copper-laced mesh of my foot, and grounds itself into the iron tie-rod. I have become a living resistor. Over decades, the constant fifty-cycle hum has reorganized my flesh, thickening the conductive veins until I can feel the electrical load of the entire eastern wall balanced across my back. Three yards to my left, a caged amber bulb hangs against the slick brick; its filament burns a touch cooler and redder than the others in the sump because I am sipping its current before the wire ever reaches the socket. In the mortar all around my margins, where excess charge bleeds off my mucus into the trickling wall-water, a halo of bluish-white lichen blooms in branching, frozen lightning-strokes, lighting my underside with a cold ghost-glow.

It is a strange thing to wake up—not to eyesight or thought, which have no use here, but to *leverage*—and realize you are no longer an animal on a machine, you are a joint through which the room feels itself.

My shell is four feet across now, terraced like a miniature hillside of rust-colored limestone and plush, velvet-green bryophytes. Because I am stationary, a deep pocket of black humus—mulched from decades of dead moss and saponified grease dripping from the upper gantries—has collected in the hollow between my spine and the brick. Out of that pocket grows a single, immense, pale fern. Its six-foot fronds arch out over the abyss of the court, utterly unbitten and geometrically flawless. When the ventilation fans turn in the upper tiers, the fronds tremble, and I feel the drag of the air translated down through the fern’s roots into the calcareous layers of my shell—a faint, ticklish tug that tells me when wind is passing across the misty slot of sky forty feet above, in a world I will never touch.

And because I am too heavy to scrape myself clean against the turning gears, the younger grazers treat me as topography. 

Right now, two plate-sized youngsters are inching across my upper shelf. I feel the minuscule, rasping scratch of their magnetite teeth combing through my moss-mat, shaving away the excess aprons of travertine before my cantilevered rim grows heavy enough to shear my own flesh against the iron rod. When one of them slides over the spot where the frayed cable enters my shell, our tallow-fringes touch. There is no language in the contact, just a brief, gummy sharing of ozone-scented mucus and electrical phase—a silent confirmation that the heat is steady, the current is fifty cycles, the iron is whole—before it glides onward down the wet brick toward a piston sleeve below.

My whole existence is the reconciliation of rhythms that do not quite match. 

There is the fast, tight purr of the fifty-cycle wire. Beneath it, the three-second *thrum-squeeze-thrum* of the tie-rod taking the load of the lower turbines. Slower still, the creeping torsion of a woody vine as thick as a hawser that climbs the vertical drive shaft six feet away, its wet bark brushing my outermost tallow-filaments once every four hours as the shaft completes a revolution. 

And beneath all of them, the elders in the water.

Once or twice between the faint, silvery brightenings of the sky-slot that mark day from night, one of those vast, conglomerate ridges down in the flooded brick vaults uncoils a yard of slate-gray hide and leans its bulk against a submerged sluice. Immediately, the back-pressure changes down in the turbine housings. Twelve feet above the water, the tie-rod pulls a fraction of a millimeter tighter against my wedged body. 

My gray muscle compresses. As it compresses, its density shifts and its electrical resistance drops. I draw a sudden, deeper draught of current from the tarred cable; down the eastern catwalk, three amber bulbs dim together in a slow, sympathetic sigh, and the bluish lichen around my flanks flares briefly brighter in the wet gloom. I hold the extra strain in the dense gristle of my foot for one minute, two, three—bleeding the mechanical shock off as gentle heat into the trickling wall-water—until the counterweighted sluice gates across the court groan, adjust by a sixteenth of an inch, and give me back my slack. The amber bulbs brighten again. The pitch of the room settles into its new key.

Then I wait for the lever.

I know when the twelve minutes are nearly up before the western valve ever moves; I can feel the deep plumbing beneath the masonry stiffening as the pressure builds toward threshold. When the verdigris-crusted counterweight finally tips with its long, wet iron groan, and the white plume roars out to swallow the court, the amber halos vanish and the heavy *thrum* of the turbines turns soft and cottony, as if the whole sunken block has been wrapped in warm wool.

The tie-rod warms by two degrees. I stiffen the muscular columns along my rim, lifting the heavy, fern-crowned shelf of my shell just a quarter-inch clear of the brick. Into that narrow, hidden slot beneath my body, I unfurl long, pleated curtains of copper-red gill. 

For three minutes, nothing exists beyond ten feet. The fern on my back drinks the cloud with its wide, immaculate fronds; the young grazers clinging to my flanks lift their small shields in unison with mine; and the hot, mineral-thick fog rolls across my open gills, tasting of deep delta clay, dissolved iron, brass, and time. Then the lever clunks back into its seat, the white dark thins into warm rain dripping from a thousand rivets, I fold my red meat back behind wet stone, and lean once more into the three-second squeeze of the rod, holding the wall together.
