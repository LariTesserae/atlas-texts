I am down in the wet, lukewarm crease between the concrete footing and the third return-pipe, where the stone has been licked concave by ten thousand years of dripping condensate. 

You would step over me if you did not have three trembling tapers to check the difference between rusted iron and living hide, because right now my mantle is slack and waterlogged, letting the tower’s huge, vertical organ-roar pass straight through my ribs without throwing back so much as a ripple. I am making myself sound like wet cement and bladder-weed. It takes very little effort when you are already old enough that your joints ache before the barometric drop even hits the rim of the massif.

My chest-plate is pressed flat against the main structural column—not up at the stretched grazer-hide drum where the big matriarch keeps court and shouts down the bedrock to her favorites, because my left rosette-lobe was scalded half-shut six seasons back when a relief gasket blew on the brine-scrubber, and if I crowd the drum now the younger mechanics click their talons at my scarred edge until I back away. Down here, twelve feet lower, the iron is thicker and the news arrives muddied by the rumble of the footing, muted like voices through three layers of root-mat, which suits me fine. I don’t need the crisp upper harmonics to know what the valley is doing. You live pressed against metal long enough, you stop listening for pitches and start listening for *tiredness*.

Four miles east, the barge-pump is greased—some quick-fingered brat just bled the main race, I felt the high, dry *chirr* smooth out into fat butter—but beneath that, deeper down in the plinth, its third anchor-bolt has been working loose in its limestone socket since the last big front. Nobody else is talking about it yet. When the stroke hits top dead center, there is a microscopic double-thud, a fraction of a millisecond of slop before the weight catches: *clank—uh-hiss*. Give it forty thousand more strokes and the collar will shear. Give it fifty thousand and the big six-tracked bastard will come grinding down from the northern switchyards with its steam-lances screaming, and boil our tuber-mats into black slime.

Later, when the drizzle thickens and the flyers tuck themselves up under the warm cowlings like greasy leather fruit, I will have to haul myself out of this sump, pick up my cold-rolled shim-iron wedge and the heavy basalt maul I keep hidden inside a dead baffle-vine, and limp four miles through the spillways to pack that bolt-hole with copper wire and hard plant-resin. Nobody will thank me for it, because if you fix a thing before it starts singing sharp, nobody knows it was ever going to break.

That is my whole trade now. Not the fast, showy work—not riding the walking-beams or dancing out in front of a crawler’s whisker-booms with a sheet of stolen mesh strapped to my back, throwing boulder-lies at its spinning horns. My rear left suction-pad has a split across the outer muscular ring from an old rock-crab bite; it loses vacuum on smooth, vertical copper if I don’t keep wetting it with spit, so my climbing days above the tenth tier are mostly finished. Instead, I belong to the splices, the mud-valves, and the nursery walls.

Reach out—slowly, let me smell the salt on your skin before you tap my shoulder—and feel what my three fine fingers are doing while my chest listens to the column.

Even half-asleep in the warm drip, they don’t stop moving. My left tapers are braided deep into the root-ball of a foam-creeper I grafted onto an ammonia-rich weep-joint three Floods ago. Pinching, bruising, coaxing. If you bruise the creeping stem on its underside with a fingernail-pick just where it meets the warm pipe, it swells up into a thick, spongy gall to protect itself, and that gall swallows everything between four and twelve kilohertz. Right behind my haunches, behind a curtain of four such galls I’ve spent half a lifetime thickening, six blind-drunk larvae are tumbled together in a slick, mewling heap, sucking tallow-rinds while the forty-story iron monster above us shakes the continent—and inside their pocket it is as quiet as the inside of an egg. Only the deep, blood-warm *thump* of the brine-return comes through, rocking their little boneless chests in time with the planet’s plumbing.

Sometimes one of them rolls out under the vine-skirt and bumps against my flank. Its tiny, wet rosette flares open against my scarred ribs, snuffling and fluttering, trying to map me, and I tilt two stiff mantle-plates down to throw a little curved pocket of warmth and short-echo around it so it feels small and safely cornered, clicking my heavy talons very softly—*tink, tink, shhh-tink*—until it crawls back into the quiet heap.

Do you know what it is to love something whose makers had the wrong number of fingers?

I go up to the old inspection plaques sometimes when the sept is out in the spillways slaughtering bogged grazers. While the rain hammers the world flat and deaf, I drag myself onto a dry ledge under an exhaust-hood and run my one good, unscarred middle taper across those little cast-iron pimples on the manifold doors. *Four parallel, one opposed.* Small hands. Narrow palms. Soft, probably, or why else round off every latch-lip to a gentle quarter-inch radius when rough pig-iron would turn just as well? They couldn't have walked upside down on a karst ceiling; they couldn't have spoken through six feet of limestone; they must have huddled inside sealed boxes or up in whatever dry emptiness sits above the clouds, dropping their heavy iron seeds down into our wet mouth-warm dark and waiting at the end of the haulage wires for the buckets to bring the mud-salts home.

Are they dead? Everyone assumes they are dead, if wardens can be said to assume anything about a verb that stopped vibrating before bone learned to talk on copper. Yet the central network still notices when line-pressure drops. Something down beyond the western rim—or up at the other end of the wire—still counts the buckets, still sends the steam-lance crawlers when a station goes cold, still pours fresh grease into the crawler bins for our brats to steal. A blind logic, just gears kissing gears in an oil-bath miles wide, keeping a promise to four fingers and a thumb that rotted long ago.

And here I am, keeping a promise to the gears.

There is a tool pouch slung across my chest-harness, braided out of stripped wire-core and waterproofed with bladder-grease. If you tapped it right now with a fingernail, it would ring back to you as three distinct densities: the flat, dead *thwack* of cured marsh-leather; the bright, nervous *zing* of seven spring-steel feeler-splinters filed down to different gauges; and, wrapped in oiled rag at the very bottom so it never clinks against the ironwork by accident, a single crescent-shaped piece of something that isn’t iron, copper, or bronze.

I didn’t roll it under an ore-bucket. Nobody did. Forty floods ago, after a crawler flipped sideways into a sinkhole during a mud-slide and split its sealed belly-casing open on an old basalt column, I was the first one down through the warm, stinking froth of battery-acid and hot oil before the water diluted it. Inside its brain-box, past the radar-gears, were rows of tiny, dead, smooth ribs made of something slick as wet tooth-enamel and cold even in mouth-warm air—ceramic, glass, whatever word a creature with eyes would use for stone melted clear and frozen flat. Most of them shattered when I pried the frame apart with my horn-hooks, useless brittle trash. Underneath them, bolted to the chassis like a warden’s breastbone, was a tuning-fork no bigger than my shortest finger, sealed inside a cracked metal thimble.

When you flick it, it doesn’t ring in the air at all. You have to bite the stem gently between the hard horn-sheaths of your talons and press your knuckles straight against the bone behind your rosette. Then it sings: a pure, terrifyingly straight needle of vibration that never wavers a fraction of a tone whether the air is thin or thick, storm-heavy or slack. Every pipe on the plateau shifts its pitch when the hot gusts roll off the lower basins; every living throat goes flat when tired; every cable sags at noon-heat and tightens in rain. Only this tiny broken splinter from the crawler’s belly holds one absolute, merciless note forever.

I use it to tune the rookery.

Not all at once—the matriarch would tear my good lobe off if she caught me altering the main column’s drum-tension—but little by little, year after dayless year, across a square mile of marsh. When I prune a stand of kinetic membrane-trees on the windward ridge so they don’t thrash loud enough to drown out an approaching crawler, I slice their cartilage ribs until their wind-shudder settles into a fifth below my hidden fork. When I wedge a bronze shim under a rattling exhaust-louver above the curing racks, I tap it in until its chatter locks onto the third harmonic. Even the false-echo dishes I’ve planted along the eastern gully—to bounce the flyers’ clicks back as fat burrowers and keep the bat-winged things swirling nicely away from our weaning-pools—are spaced at exact fractions of that dead crawler’s heartbeat.

Step into our territory from the wild karst and you don’t know why your shoulders drop an inch, why the staggering, screaming racket of the plateau suddenly seems to part like greased curtains and let your sonar reach twenty paces farther than it has any right to. The youngsters think our tower was simply dropped onto sweeter stone than the rival sept’s rusted sulfur-rig twelve miles south. They don’t realize they are living inside an instrument I’ve been filing and wedging into chord since before their mothers shed their milk-mantles.

Wait—quiet your breathing. Fold your flaps flat.

Feel that?

Down through the concrete, underneath the big roaring *C* of the tower and the distant *clank—uh-hiss* of my loose-bolted pump-jack, a high, thin tremor just slid down the main column from forty stories up. *Zzz-tink. Screeee.* 

Twenty minutes are up. An ore-bucket just cleared the top pulley, heading west into the wet wind, heavy with pressed cake from the automated thickeners. Its second flange-wheel has a flat spot the size of a thumbprint—*thuk-thuk-thuk-thuk* fading out along the span as the cable takes the weight over the flooded valley.

I know that bucket. Three cycles back, I rode the maintenance ladder ten tiers up—stopping twice to spit on my split heel-pad so I wouldn’t peel off into the dark—just to hear that flat wheel pass overhead and measure how fast the steel was mushrooming. One day soon, maybe twenty storms from now when the burns on my rosette finally scar all the way shut and my three fine fingers stiffen too much to feel the dry chatter of a bearing race through cast iron, I won’t take my basalt maul east into the reeds anymore.

I’ll wait for a big continental front, when the pressure drops until my air-sacs ache like boils and the horizontal rain batters the flyers dumb and helpless against the stone. While the strong ones link wires down in the foaming spillways and drive spikes into the necks of ten-ton bulls, I will climb all forty stories through the screaming wet iron, one slow vacuum-slap at a time, straight past the curing meat, past the steam-vents, up into the eighty-knot gale where the tower sways three feet off plumb and smells of pure sky-rust and ozone.

I won’t go empty-handed, though, the way the worn-out old bulls do when they throw their hooks at that rising *F-sharp* just to die somewhere quiet off the plateau.

I’ll wedge my little stolen tuning-fork deep into my harness, pack two blocks of tallow-grease around my chest to keep the cold off my heart when the cable plunges over the rim into whatever crushing, unbreathable soup boils miles below us, and take my seven steel picks in my fist. Because the bucket goes *somewhere*. Every twenty minutes for ten thousand generations, full going west, empty coming back east, dumped clean and greased at the hub by machines nobody on the tableland has ever laid a finger on.

Before the pressure crushes my ribs in or the hot poison down there blisters my mantle shut, there might be ten minutes, or an hour, or a whole roaring station bolted into the cliff-wall halfway down the abyss where the air is thick as warm syrup and the pipes are ten times wider than ours—and nobody has checked their anchor-bolts or bled their dry bearings since the world began.

Right now, though, a drop of warm, sour condensate just rolled down the iron column and burst squarely inside the curl of my good rosette, smelling faintly of copper sulfate and wet moss. Behind the baffle-galls, one of the larvae gives a tiny, hiccuping chest-knock in its sleep. 

I tuck my mantle tighter over my split heel, slide my maul out from the hollow vine, and unstick my belly from the warm concrete. Four miles east through the bladder-weed, *clank—uh-hiss, clank—uh-hiss*, an anchor-bolt is chewing its limestone socket oval in the rain, and I have forty thousand strokes to make it stop.
