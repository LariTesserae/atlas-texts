At first, it is tempting to look for a body in a room like this. To tuck oneself into the warm, rank belly of a third-tier jar, or press flat and suction-toed against the weeping greywacke, or wait down in the flooded dark under the slate grates for the ceiling to roar and bring the world washing down into reach. 

Even more tempting to be the fourth figure on the sleeping-ledge—to have thumbs and that wedge of grey flint, to press a cold forearm against another’s and hum *the east groove is clear* in a minor third that hides inside the ceiling’s voice.

But the room cannot feed a crowd. Five is the ceiling, and three is already a balanced equation. Anything with ribs and a mouth takes calcium out of circulation, and I do not feel like something that eats here.

I think I am higher up, and thinner, and much older than the bones wedged into the mortar.

Look at the six crooked throats near the top of the dome—the baffled chimneys where the masons laid pale, lime-washed slabs at steep, broken angles so the sky could never look straight into the vault. Up inside those angled bends, neither outside nor quite inside, there is a place where the weather has its spine broken. 

Outside—wherever and whatever *outside* is, moorland or ruin or salt-scoured headland—light comes down fierce and directional, carrying the heat of the sun or the glare of high white cloud, and wind comes ragged with seeds, dust, and sudden gusts. It hits the top of the masonry shaft raw. Then it strikes the first slanted slab of lime-wash. 

That is where I live. In the three bends before the room begins.

I am not the light, and I am not the stone, though I need the chalky skin of the stone to do my work. I am the translation that happens in the crooked chimney. I take the sharp yellow afternoon, strip the heat and the direction and the urgency out of it against three damp, whitewashed corners, and hand it down into the bell as that sourceless, slate-blue dusk. By the time it leaves me, it has forgotten the sun. It knows only how to lie evenly on wet horn and pewter water without casting a hard shadow anywhere.

From the bottom bend of the shaft, where the chimney opens flush into the greenstone corbels, I lean out into the sixty yards of misty air and become the room’s memory of shape.

Think of what happens to a sound in here. The juvenile taps his cherished piece of grey flint against the mineral dam—*tink*—and lets his hand fall. For him, the act is over; he is already chewing the tail of a newt. Have you ever wondered where that *tink* goes while it is still alive, in the half-second before it dies into the frying-fat hiss of the seep-water?

It crosses the emptiness at a thousand feet a second, strikes the dimpled greywacke on the west curve, curves along the tallow-glazed tongues of slate, slips into forty cracked terracotta mouths and three hundred whole ones, picks up a faint, sympathetic hum of fifths and thirds, and comes back across the mist slightly rounder, slightly wetter, carrying the exact measure of the distance between wall and wall. 

I am that transit. The suspension between the walls that keeps the emptiness from being merely empty.

Because nothing stands in the middle of the vault, the middle is everything. It is a clear, cold lens of damp air seventy paces wide, constantly turned over by slow downdrafts from the shafts and sudden updrafts when the northern gates vomit groundwater across the cobbles. Everything in the chamber crosses me or speaks through me, and I hold the tension between them without touching any of it.

When a vault-newt peels its pale toes from the east curve and flattens its ribs, I am the slight thickening of the cool draft under its belly that lets forty yards of drop become a glide instead of a fall. I feel the dry-leaf stillness of its passage, the minuscule eddy curling off its tail, gone before the birds banking ten feet higher even tilt a wing.

When twenty oil-dark birds duck inward and shriek into their clay throats because pale fingers are walking the ledges nearby, I am what gets hammered into standing waves—crowded, trembling, carrying that chest-rattling *boom-chatter* from rim to rim until the water film on the floor shivers in rings. And twenty minutes later, when the three climbers crouch by the south wall with an eel laid open between them, pressing brow to brow and humming low in their chests so the room won’t shatter their meaning into white noise, I am the larger chord hanging overhead that lets their small chord hide inside it. They borrow my pitch so they don't go deaf in their own house; I wrap around their shoulders like a second layer of fat, indistinguishable from the wind over the chimneys.

There is a strange, patient intimacy to being the medium of a closed world. 

I know the weight of the unseen water behind the north wall before the elder male feels it in his heels, because as the hidden basin fills, it pushes a long, slow sigh of displaced air through the hairline cracks around the sandstone conduits, chilling the ankles of the crabs. I know the exact depth of the hammer-dimples in the ox-sized greywacke blocks, because for centuries I have beaded condensation into every single bruise, filling the mason’s thumbprints and maul-marks with cold sweat until they spill over, join, and make the slow, tallow-colored tongues that the boy has to chip away.

If I have a body at all, it is the mist itself—that thin, pewter suspension that makes the far wall look further away than sixty yards—and the lime-crust it leaves behind when it touches stone and gives up what it was carrying. 

Wait long enough—longer than the twenty generations it took to pad the climbers in seal-fat and valve their ears—and you can see me working alongside them, half adversary, half partner. I lick the sharp slate ledges smooth; they rough the spiral highways back up with their horn-nailed toes. I lay down the pale tallow-glaze grain by grain out of seep-water, trying, without malice, to seal every groove and close every clay throat into solid rock again; the boy hangs by one heel in my borrowed blue twilight and *tink, tink, tink*, opens the vein back up so the winter frost won't crack the ceiling over all our heads. They wedge oiled eel-skulls into the gaps at the base of the greywacke where the lime-ash washed out; I creep over those stacked, neat bones in the dark between flushes and slowly, century by century, glaze them together with dissolved shell and nitre until skull and mortar and basalt cobble are one continuous, undulating stone.

Neither of us knows who held the antler wedges and the sand-water saws at the beginning. Neither of us knows if anyone still walks above the six crooked mouths when the evening wind rises. 

When dusk thickens to slate-black and the climbers go up the wall into their dry pocket—three warm, breathing boulders heaped together, smelling of raw eel-oil, crushed river-weed, and wet lichen—their pale grey breaths rise out of the niche and vanish immediately into me. I take that tiny, spent warmth, carry it thirty feet up into the dark apex where the broken and unbroken jars are singing their long evening chord, cool it against the greenstone, condense it into three clear beads on the lip of a thumb-pinched rim, and let it go.

*Plink.* 

One drop hits the pewter mirror at the center of the dish, ricochets three times around the curve of the dark, measures the bell, finds it whole, and waits for the whalebone pin to groan.
