I am not down in the mulch where the newly split glass steams, and I am not swinging on the mid-canopy guy-lines with a belt of copper wedges bruising my ribs. My joints were not built for that relentless, bucking deck, or else they were once, and the dry years have calcified them into something stiffer, heavier, and slower. 

Find me two miles away and four hundred feet up, where the plunging wall of the fault meets the overhang of the sunken ceiling. 

Up here, the rock is older—still unweathered, still sharp enough to slice leather, but relieved of the frantic tectonic pinching that splits the valley floor. The tinder-flora cannot root in this dead, unstressed ceiling-basalt, so there is a narrow, jagged gallery of bare stone hovering right above the boiling ocean of the upper canopy. It is hot here—the rising exhaust of five square miles of friction collects under the roof in stagnant, blistering rivers of ozone and golden chaff—and the updrafts howl sideways along the rock face, carrying the dry husks of spent gliders and millions of sparking cellulose flakes.

I am wedged into a chimney-cleft where a pale green ribbon of oxidized native copper bleeds out of the dark gabbro. 

You would mistake me for an accumulation of shed bark and mineral crust until my inner eyelids flicked clear—*click-click-click*, slower than the valley-runners’ membranes, crusted thick at the corners with yellow tallow. I am one of them, though altered by altitude and stillness. An old male, or simply an exhausted one whose heel-tendons snapped ten thousand sleep-cycles ago when a bone-stalk blew prematurely under my feet. In a world of pure trajectory, a runner who loses his spring usually becomes dry dander within a day. Instead, I climbed out of the timber altogether, up onto the dead bone of the roof.

My skin has gone the dark, polished pewter of rubbed graphite from years of crouching against basalt dust. My collarbones are wide as soup bowls, permanently flushed dark plum in this ceiling-heat, pulsing lazily like the throat of a resting lizard. My paddle-feet, no longer needed for vaulting across exploding boughs, have thickened into clubbed, vice-like clamps wrapped around a knob of raw stone, the horn-chisel nails grown long and curved right into the hairline fissures of the wall to hold me fast against the buffeting thermals.

I am the place where the copper comes from. And because I stay on the dead rock—the one thing in this roaring furnace that holds its shape for a whole season—I am as close as our kind gets to a library.

Around my perch stretches a wide, sloping net rigged from static-silk stolen from dry-spiders and reinforced with braided scale-worm gut. It hangs out over the abyss like a trawl in a glowing yellow sea. The violent updrafts do half my foraging: bladder-bodied gliders tumble upward when the thermals shear against the ceiling, slamming stunned into my webbing. Filter-bats choke on spore-bursts below and float up into my reach like hot, leather balloons. I haul them in with a hooked horn-pole, slit their fat-sacs with a flake of black glass, and press every drop of warm, amber oil into rows of stoppered carapace-gourds lashed into the cleft behind me.

Between harvests, I work the green seam. 

I don't hammer wildly; up here, a stray spark doesn't matter, there is nothing to burn, only things that shatter. With a heavy cobble of dense porphyry held in both greased fists, I tap-tap-tap along the natural crystal planes of the native metal, teasing tough, malleable nuggets out of the brittle black matrix. Then I set them on an anvil-flat shelf between my thighs and beat them out—flattening the flanges, folding the edges over to harden the spine, tapering the biting tips until each wedge is four inches of dense, electric-hungry weight capable of grounding out a thirty-foot stalk in ninety seconds.

Down below me, the forest looks like the surface of a star. 

There is no perspective in that shadowless, triboluminescent glare—just an endless, churning field of amber-white static, boiling upward in slow domes where new faults crack the floor five hundred feet down, then collapsing into sudden dark pits of rust-colored dust when an over-swollen tier detonates. You learn to read the valley from above not as land, but as weather. You see the dark, moving bruises where armies of hound-grazers eat a lane clear; you see the bright, frantic white-outs where two tectonic cracks cross and the timber grows so fast it grinds itself instantly to glowing flour.

I cannot hear anything distinct through that rising wall of ninety-five-decibel roar—my ear-shutters stay clamped half-closed all day, fused slightly at the hinges with age—so I keep one long, calloused toe pressed constantly against a seventy-foot "listening line."

It is a thick cable of twisted copper wire salvaged from twenty minor seams and braided into cured tendon, dropped straight down off my stone lip so its weighted lower end drags through the highest whipping crowns of the canopy below. 

All day, the chaotic, meaningless chatter of snapping twigs and chitinous wings hums faintly against my toe-pad. It feels like hot sand poured continuously over skin. I ignore it, hammering my wedges, chewing slow mouthfuls of dried bat-meat and oil-cake, grinding my iron-gray molars down a fraction of a millimeter closer to the gum.

Then, once every twenty or thirty sleep-cycles, the sand stops, and a clean, sharp *ting—ting—ting-ting* climbs the copper wire out of the glare.

Knuckle-brass on metal. Deliberate. Spaced.

I stop my cobble mid-swing. I lean out over the updraft—the hot, mica-thick wind flattening my cooling-crests back against my skull—and peer down along the glinting thread of my wire. 

Out of the blinding, thrashing yellow sea below, two figures come vaulting up the highest flexible spears of a newly erupted spire, riding the swaying tips like pole-vaulters until they are close enough to snatch the weighted end of my line. I don't haul them up—nobody wastes strength fighting gravity when the trees will push you—instead, they clip a runner-ring to my cable to guide them, wait ten minutes for a cluster of telescoping bone-stalks right beneath the cliff to shoot upward in a violent thermal surge, and step casually off the shattering crown onto my dead basalt ledge three seconds before the spire blows itself into confetti behind them.

They smell of fresh sulfur, scorched pith, and the frantic, sharp electricity of down-below. Next to me, they look impossibly fast—their inner eyelids snapping five times to my one, their violet throats fluttering like trapped moths, their whole lean bodies still micro-adjusting for a pitching deck that isn't pitching anymore. Watching a young runner step onto solid, unmoving ceiling-rock for the first time is always strange; they stumble on stillness, their wide chisel-toes flexing anxiously against stone that refuses to swell or vibrate back at them.

We don't waste grease on greetings. They unclip heavy pouches of yellow fault-sulfur, bundles of green horn-shafts I can't grow up here, and rough lumps of black volcanic glass fetched from the bottom splits. I push forward six newly hammered copper wedges, smoothed at the butt so they won't split a maul, and two fat gourds of clarified glider-oil.

And then comes the real trade.

They crouch between my knees in the narrow acoustic shadow of my rock-chimney, and for twenty minutes, they press their palms flat against my scarred shins and chest-plates, and they *tap*. 

Through my skin, they pour the changing shape of the five-mile world. *The western trough has gone dead-cold; the flora there has starved back to bare glass for half a mile. Two bands merged near the big obsidian step after a spore-blast took their elders. A massive swarm of deep-tremors is walking southward—the whole middle basin is about to blow into giant timber.*

I sit very still, eyes closed behind both sets of lids, letting the vectors map themselves across my ribs. And when they finish, I lay my heavy, graphite-dark thumbs against their fluttering collarbones, and I tap back what five other bands told me before them. 

*Do not drop down-fault toward the sulfur steps; three bands went in two cycles ago and the rock pinched shut behind them—all dead-fall and starving scavengers now. Cut east along the high porphyry ridge instead; the young woman with the silvered shoulder-plates passed beneath my wire ten sleeps back, steering four kin toward the fresh splits there. Her tallies were wide and well-greased. They are eating fat.*

In the basin, nothing stays put long enough to tell you where your cousins went, or which crack is a death-trap. Only the dead ceiling holds still. So I hold still with it, collecting all their vanishing yellow slash-marks in my head, cross-indexing trajectories that broke apart in the timber days ago, turning my own stiffened, unmoving ribs into the one sulfur-map that never splits open.

Before they drop back in, they always stay for ten minutes in the back of my cleft, where I keep an old, permanent baffle of layered giant-carapace cemented right to the stone with boiled resin and ash. It isn't as dark or tight as a swinging sleep-pod, because it has to breathe the cliff heat, so we don't hum the deep sleep-chord together—that belongs only to the band you swing with. 

Instead, one of them takes a small bone spoon, dips it into my finest rendered grub-fat, and carefully oils the deep, dry cracks along my spine where my old arms can no longer reach. In return, I press my forehead briefly against theirs—just five seconds of heavy, silent skull-contact, giving them a dose of pure, geological stillness to carry back down into the explosion.

Then they step to the lip, hook their toes over the basalt, wait for a rising dome of pale shoots to swell up within thirty feet of the ledge, and drop out of sight—swallowed instantly by the blinding amber roar.

The wire twangs once against my toe as they kick clear of it, then settles back into the steady, mindless hiss of a million chafing leaves.

I pull my inner eyelids sideways to clear a drift of warm mica-dust. I reach behind me for a rough, green-crusted nugget pried from the seam this morning, set it squarely on the porphyry flat between my legs, and raise my stone cobble. 

Down below, ten million stalks split, flower into spark, and collapse into ash-colored chaff. Up here in the hot wind under the roof, *clink—clink—clink*, I beat another wedge flat, holding the map together until the next hand grabs the wire.
