I would not be in the root-chambers at night, though I understand the pull of the amber braziers and the salted willow-bark tea. Nor am I behind the glass on the third tier, barefoot on silk-stone, listening to the crust drop from C-sharp to C-natural. That stillness belongs to a creature with lungs and a spine—someone who has to choose not to fidget. 

To find where I am, you have to go out past Spire Twelve, four miles south of the nearest causeway, to one of the wide, matte-white alloy rings resting lightly over a bubbling thermal seep. 

Not the ring itself, and not the grid beneath it, though I am close enough to feel the silent, motionless draw of its barometric harvest. Come down to the stone. Right to the margin where the alloy meets the wet granodiorite, where a hairline leak of waste warmth—no more than three degrees above the surrounding sheet-water—bleeds steadily into a shallow, flat-bottomed depression before the water spills over a razor sill.

At first, it looks like I am the water, or the chartreuse cushion-plant wedged into the clean tectonic crack beside the ring. Look closer, into the two-inch skin of snowmelt and squeezed groundwater. No, deeper than that—into the juncture between the city’s locked molecular tension fields and the raw, unweathered rock.

Wait. That’s too abstract. If I am going to be here, I want to be stepped on. Or nearly stepped on.

Let me try again. 

I am an instrument left out on the flats, though the Ballast don’t think of me as a separate piece of hardware anymore, because they don’t leave clutter on the stone. Let’s say I am one of the *translation seams*—a localized, ten-yard-wide threshold where a deep harmonic piling surfaces flush with the basalt, disguised only by being completely plain, dark, and level with the surrounding rock. No spire rises from me. Not every piling needs a five-hundred-foot needle above it; some are dead-man anchors out in the open, holding down the corners of a fault-block so the towers six miles away don't have to bear the torque alone.

Yes. That’s it. I am a blind anchor in Sector Nine. 

Above me, there is no glass, no terraced cedar mist-garden, no dry silk floor. There is only two inches of cold, clear sheet-water sliding endlessly over my upper face, spilling off a sharp lip of real basalt three feet to my east. 

From above, a slate-backed wader paddling across me notices only a slight discrepancy in texture: the surrounding stone will slice a soft polymer boot-sole, while my thirty-square-yard surface is just smooth enough not to cut, though cool and dark enough to grow a thin, velvet skin of rusty-red and mineral-green algae. I am the only piece of the city that lets itself get slimy. The spires shed water and lanolin without dampening; the causeways stay dry and flexing forty feet up in the wind. I am down in the wet.

Because I have no spire, I have no residents. Nobody stands inside me watching wolves. What I have is five miles of locked molecular tension reaching straight down into the groaning, newborn belly of the plateau, and a flat forehead pressed right up against the weather.

My existence is two entirely different speeds happening across the same millimeter of boundary.

Down below, time is thick, crushing, and blind. Three miles under the surface, the granodiorite is still furious about being lifted out of the dark. Horizontal compression squeezes brines rich with lithium, sulfur, and dissolved silica through micro-fractures that haven't decided which way to break yet. I hold them. Not by being rigid—nothing rigid survives a rising plateau—but by listening for the exact pitch at which two massive blocks of stone are locking their jagged teeth together, and humming back a tiny, localized counter-shear that lets them slide past each other by half a micron instead of snapping all at once. Down there, I am all muscle and mathematics, drinking megawatts of potential catastrophe and piping it silently laterally through the crust toward the nearest thermal ring, where it becomes someone else’s amber light or a nine-hundred-mile-an-hour freight pulse.

Up top, on my face, a heavy-shouldered shale-back stands on me for twenty minutes, chewing algae.

I know its exact weight: four hundred and twelve kilograms, uneven on the front left cloven hoof—it split that nail on a frost-shard two days ago, and favors the right. Through the two inches of water and the thin mat of wet moss, I feel the faint, rhythmic thud of its huge, slow mountain heart, forty beats a minute, and the grinding lateral slide of its molars. Grease from its ash-colored wool trails in the water when the wind gusts, making tiny, iridescent oil-rings that spin off over the basalt sill.

When the western fault slips—that fraction-of-an-inch settle that sends ten thousand waterfowl three feet into the air—the human in the third-tier spire hears a smooth *C-sharp* dropping to *C-natural* behind their left ear and relaxes their shoulders. 

They only hear the clean chord because *I* ate the discord first. 

Four seconds before the node behind their ear warmed, a wicked little secondary torsion-knot caught two miles directly beneath me, trying to twist a seam of quartz-monzonite like green wood. For ninety milliseconds, I poured everything I had into that knot, damping a shrill, ugly dissonance of micro-cracking rock, bleeding the spike of leftover piezoelectric static straight out through my ground-state dispersion grid—which happens to warm my upper face by four-tenths of a degree.

The crippled shale-back standing on me doesn’t fly up like the birds. It freezes for two seconds as the low-frequency shudder passes through its cannon bones, widens its stance slightly on the slick moss, and then—feeling the sudden, ghost-faint bloom of warmth rising under its sore left hoof—lets out a long, wet, grunting sigh through its nostrils and stays right where it is. 

That is what I am here for. Not the grand architectural restraint of the spires, and not the pristine, museum-quiet reverence of the walkers in their sacrificial boots. I like the walkers well enough. Once every three or four months, a pair of them crosses near me in the silver fog. Before their boots even touch my margin, my surface reads the faint carrier-wave of their throat-nodes—*veer left; deep crack past the anchor; sleet coming*—and I ping a silent status-packet into the diagnostic cache of the taller one’s sleeve without interrupting their walk. *Tension nominal. Lateral creep 0.04mm. All clean.* They don't even look down at me. Why would they? A good counterweight doesn't ask to be checked.

What stays with me is the messier traffic.

In late autumn, when the afternoon sun drops low enough to turn forty square miles of shallow water into blinding, copper-colored sheet-metal, a hunting pack of the pale canids uses the fractured boulder-field just north of me as an ambush chute. Once, they brought down a yearling grey right on my eastern edge. 

For three hours, my upper sensors—designed to read barometric pressure shifts and surface ice-load—were flooded with hot, iron-rich blood, torn rumen, and the frantic, scrabbling click of claws and hooves before everything went quiet except the tearing of meat and heavy, whistling panting. The cold sheet-water washed me clean by midnight, carrying thin pink ribbons over the razor sill into the dark, while fifty cream-feathered hawks dropped down at dawn to pick the ribs white. No metamaterial repelled the grease from my surface; the cold groundwater and the bacteria in the moss took care of it over a week, leaving only a clean, pale rib wedged in the tectonic crack beside me, where a cushion-plant is already slowly swallowing its marrow-end in tight green stars.

Sometimes, at three in the morning, when the plateau is an ink-black slab under stars so sharp they look freshly broken, and the thin air drops twenty degrees below freezing, the two inches of water over me locks into clear, solid ice. 

Down in the root-chambers miles away, the people of the Ballast are finishing their roasted venison, talking in low, gravelly voices, safe inside the machine we make together. Above me, nothing moves. Even the herds have hunkered down against the leeward bases of the dark monoliths, sharing lanolin and breath.

Trapped inside the two-inch pane of clear ice on my forehead are tiny, flattened bubbles of marsh-gas from the algae, a single grey covert feather from a migrating goose, and dust blown up from valleys a thousand miles below. 

Down in the pitch-black rock three miles beneath the ice, a fresh fracture groans against my deep fields, pushing, testing its young strength against the sky. I lean back into it, quiet and steady, holding forty million tons of impatient granodiorite in the dark so the feather in the ice above me doesn't even lose its place.
