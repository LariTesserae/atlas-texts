I am not on the wall. The wall requires glue, and glue is a frantic, exhausting business.

To live in the blue-white halos of the bulkheads is to spend everything you make just to keep from sliding into the trough. Even the creeping green heart tucked beside the warm transformer—so clever, learning to stay flat, abandoning its tall heritage to become a liver-shaped ribbon—has to pay the tax of constant production, pumping out cellulose and mucilage under a noon that never blinks. One flicker in the copper windings miles down the valley, one blown fuse on a maintenance board, and within forty-eight hours the alkaline water will unpeel that entire green archipelago and flush it down to the turbines.

Instead, find the conduit flange near the sleeve valve, just below the reach of the lowest lamp, where the cast shadow of the bolt-ring splits the mist.

Look closer at the concrete twenty centimeters left of the steel collar, where an inspector stood eighteen months ago before the water was let in, holding a clipboard and a stick of yellow lumber-crayon. Look at the top curve of the numeral *4* he pressed against the dry formwork ridge.

I am the grease, and I am what is eating the grease.

Down here, below the millimeter-thick curtain of racing snowmelt, there is a phenomenon the engineers calculate for pipes and turbine blades, though they rarely picture it as a landscape: the no-slip boundary. However violent the storm inside the chamber, at the exact molecular interface where moving liquid meets stationary solid, velocity drops to zero. Against the stone, the hurricane stops. Between the tearing water overhead and the raw Portland cement beneath lies a dead-calm vault ten micrometers tall.

When the inspector dragged his yellow crayon across the grey seam, the concrete were still dusty and dry. Wax smeared into the microscopic valleys of the troweled mortar, bridging tiny canyons of calcium silicate, sealing off pockets of trapped air and grit beneath a bright, hydrophobic roof of paraffin, stearic acid, and chrome-yellow pigment. When the chamber flooded with fog and the walls began to weep, the water could not wet the mark. It still beads and parts over the *4*, arcing cleanly across the wax ridge like a river jumping a submerged log.

Beneath that waxy arch, it is dry—or rather, the water present is entirely ours, bound up in a dense, buttery slime that never mixes with the glacier melt roaring overhead.

There is no blue diode light under the wax, or only a faint, jaundiced glimmer diffused through the yellow pigment—useless for making sugar, entirely ignored. There is no twenty-five-second clock here. When the pneumatic actuator snaps and the room goes milk-white at a hundred and fifteen decibels, we do not coil like the stalked bells in the moss caves, nor flatten like the algae. A bacterium half a micron wide has no inertia to speak of, no internal skeleton to shear, no fluid cavity to resonate. The acoustic load that shatters a midge’s ganglia passes through our lipid membranes purely as a seamless, sixty-cycle massage—a slight, rhythmic jiggling of dissolved ions that actually saves us work, stirring fresh oxygen through the wax pores and vibrating our waste away into the passing sheet of water without ever tearing our holdfasts loose.

We came in on the inspector’s thumb, perhaps, or blew off a glacial moraine outside where wind-scoured shales weep traces of ancient kerogen, or rode the factory-greased threads of the massive hex nuts trucked up from the valley. Cold is our native thickness. At three degrees above zero, ordinary membranes stiffen like cold lard and snap; ours are woven full of crooked, unsaturated fatty chains that stay supple as olive oil right down to freezing. Everything here happens at the deliberate, unhurried pace of deep-water chemistry. A single cell division takes four days. Nobody rushes.

Our world is a slow tunnel bored from the underside of a human thought.

To the inspector, *4* meant a torque specification checked, a weld approved, an afternoon shift nearly finished before driving down out of the thin mountain air. To us, *4* is a glacial ridge of pure, reduced carbon—long, delicious chains of twenty-five and thirty linked carbons locked together in tidy crystalline rows, towering above the barren mineral desert of the dam. Outside our yellow roof, the concrete sweats lye that strips stray spores bare; beneath the crayon stroke, as we snip the paraffin chains two carbons at a time with oxygen-hungry enzymes, our breath turns the wax into weak fatty acids. Slowly, luxuriously, our waste neutralizes the harsh cement paste beneath us, turning caustic hydroxide into a mild, chalky bed of calcium stearate—soapstone of our own making—into which our colony sinks deeper every month.

We are not one thing, really, so much as a quiet consortium sharing a blanket. The pioneers at the upper rind—rod-shaped *Rhodococcus* and *Pseudomonas* pale as milk—press their slick, water-hating coats directly into the ceiling of yellow wax, secreting tiny biological detergents to loosen the stiff hydrocarbons into droplets small enough to swallow. Beneath them, tucked into the soapy bed against the stone, slender filaments scavenge the leftover acetate and trace nitrogen blown in from high-altitude lightning storms, stitching together the tough extracellular matrix that keeps the roof from collapsing as it thins.

We have no eyes to see the aluminum louvers high up in the wall, nor any sense of the great reservoir leaning its millions of tons against the upstream face. Yet we know the mountain better than the moss does. The moss lives in a false summer, tricked by forty watts of electricity into thinking noon lasts forever. We live in the real temperature of the rock, in the dark, chewing slowly through the residue of the dam’s creation.

Already the sharp bottom serif of the *4* has gone translucent. From the catwalk, if anyone ever came through the heavy steel bulkhead wearing double ear-protection and a rain slicker, the inspector’s mark would still look intact—just slightly paler under the racing water, dulled like everything else in the room. They wouldn’t see that the yellow stroke is now hollow, a paper-thin shell of unpalatable mineral pigment arched over a thriving, colorless mat of cold-loving cells.

Give us another three years, and the *4* will vanish entirely, digested down to its last waxy crumb, leaving only a faint, ghostly numeral etched half a millimeter deep into the concrete where our mild acids softened the mortar. Long before that happens, though, during one of the quiet twenty-two-second troughs between blow-offs, a microscopic ribbon of our slime will have oozed five centimeters sideways along a horizontal form-seam, followed the faint chemical scent of heavy oil across the wet grey stone, and touched the base of the massive conduit flange—where five kilograms of thick, blue lithium-complex factory grease sit packed between the hex-nut threads, dark and cold and untouched, waiting like a whale fall on the ocean floor.
