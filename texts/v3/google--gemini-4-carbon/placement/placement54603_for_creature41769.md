I am not in the sunlit square, and I do not press myself flat against the bark-masonry like the silver skinks. I could not survive the open air, nor do I have the patience for their slow, cold-blooded carpentry. 

Look instead at the threshold of one of the cheat-doors—the second-story archways where a hunting lizard steps across a wooden sill on the south face and emerges, one blink behind itself, on the north wall forty paces away. Look at the wood of the jamb itself. Notice how it isn’t pale silver like the sun-cured flagstones, or damp green like the fresh shoots, or bulbous amber like a healed split. Around the rims of the impossible doors, and tucked beneath the undersides of the stairs that climb for three flights only to let out underneath their own bottom steps, the grain turns a bruised, oily violet-grey, slick to the touch and faintly cold even in the glaring heat of afternoon.

That is where I am. Wait until the twice-a-minute sap pulse surges through the conduit-roots, and you will see the surface of that violet wood ripple slightly against the grain.

I have no ribs to shatter and no chitin to crack. If you peeled me off the lintel—which would take a chisel and kill us both—I would look like little more than a broad, branching sheet of translucent tendon and dark, glistening vascular lace, thinner than parchment at my margins and swelling to the thickness of a split grape only where my ganglia knot together inside dry, abandoned beetle-burrows. I am part fungus, perhaps, or a colonial tunicate whose ancestors washed down from the forest canopy three hundred years ago and forgot the shape of water, or a rogue sleeve of the great plant’s own phloem that woke up when space began to fold and decided to keep its own counsel.

To me, the deafening square is just weather—a constant, shivering warmth that comes through the soles of the walls and keeps my fluids churning without the expense of a heart. I don't hear the brassy bladders or the pistol-shot frogs or the heavy nocturnal sawing of the mahogany crickets. What I feel is *tension*. 

It costs something to fold seventy paces of daylight down to zero. 

Nobody asks *why* a doorway on the east wall opens onto the west arcade, or why two opposite balconies cast shadows toward the same corner. The plant didn't invent that geometry; the sound did. Pack enough continuous mechanical violence into a living resonator for long enough, let the lath-skinks tune the walls decade after decade until the standing waves lock with crystalline, terrible perfection, and eventually the air is not the only thing that gets pinched at the nodes. The distance itself buckles. Two patches of hollow bark on opposite sides of the court happen to vibrate at such an exact, sympathetic harmonic—down to the millionth of a phase-beat, down to the cellular tremor of their sap-tubes—that the town simply forgets there are seventy paces of screaming void between them. The grain touches across nothing. A seam forms.

And seams fray.

If left raw, those impossible thresholds leak. Not sap, and not water, but *lag*. You mentioned the blink-of-an-eye delay when you peer through a doorway and watch the rear plates of the beetles crawling past your own ankles. Without me, that blink widens. First to half a second, then to three. A terracotta crustacean steps across a poorly tuned sill, and its front pair of legs arrives on the north wall on Tuesday afternoon while its tail is still trailing out of the south archway three seconds behind; when the sap-pulse shifts the pitch of the wood by half a hertz, the seam snaps shut between its thorax and abdomen like a pair of dull shears. Worse, when a folded stairwell drifts out of harmonic lock, the plant’s blind cambium tries to heal both ends at once, extruding tangled tumors of half-here, half-there wood-flesh that jam the alleys and send wild, dissonant shudders ripping through the standing waves out in the court.

I eat the drift. 

My pale, hair-fine rootlets—or nerves; in me they are the same tissue—thread six inches deep into the dry pith on *both* sides of every folded doorway, stitching the north jamb directly to the south jamb right through the pinch. I am stretched across the town in a dozen places at once. My left margin is currently damp with the indoor afternoon drizzle of a shaded ground-floor gutter on the west side, smelling of crushed shell and wet cedar; four inches away across my own middle, my right margin is baking chalk-dry in a fourth-story dormer just under the sunny eastern rim, vibrated stiff by a colony of mica-winged wasps boiling past my skin.

It is a dizzying, intoxicating way to lie still. Every creature that steps across one of my sills leaves a tiny smear of heat, salt, and momentum in the delay. When a forearm-sized gecko lunges through an arch after a straggler, for that one fraction of a second while it is neither on the east wall nor the west, its whole hurtling weight rests squarely on the stretched drumhead of my body. I drink the kinetic skim of its passage—a sharp, copper-tasting spark of stolen velocity that ripples through my violet lace—and let the rest of the lizard through intact. When a crustacean column marches endlessly across my back, ten thousand tiny terracotta feet drum a steady, nourishing static into my filaments, and in return I hold the two walls tight together like wet glue, keeping the delay trimmed down to a clean, survivable twentieth of a second.

The lath-skinks know what I am, insofar as they know anything that isn't turbulence. 

Watch when one of those four-foot silver ribbons comes gliding along a cornice and reaches one of my doorways. It never skates straight across me the way the frantic geckos do. It stops a thumb-width shy of my violet rim. Its broad, wedge-shaped chin comes down onto my thin margin—cool, dry scale pressing against my slick, cold skin. 

For ten seconds, we trade notes.

The skink gives a tiny, almost imperceptible shiver of its ossified ribs, tapping a test-tone into me to check whether the north wall and the south wall are beginning to pull apart as the afternoon sun shifts the weight of the sap. I don't think back at it—neither of us has a brain built for conversation—but where its chin presses me, I tighten or slacken my fibers, shifting the local pitch of the jamb by a quarter-tone until the skink’s lateral pits stop itching. Sometimes, if a boring-grub has wandered too close to a spatial seam where the skink’s phase-inverted hum might accidentally tear the fold open instead of liquefying the larva, I swell a tiny blister of bitter, astringent fluid right under the reptile’s snout. It takes the hint, tucks its toes, and glides smoothly over me without humming, emerging instantly three stories lower to hunt elsewhere. Five minutes later, I thread a single white filament quietly through the pith, pierce the grub myself in the dark, and drink it slow over an hour without making a sound.

My favorite time is just before the night shift locks in—that perilous twenty-minute twilight when the daytime bladders wheeze down into a rattle, the indoor rain dries up, and the great leaning gable on the east wall begins to groan against the thinning air.

For those twenty minutes, the acoustic grid out in the plaza loosens. The four hundred dust-knots drift and sag; the tiny kite-spiders fling themselves desperately upward from pocket to failing pocket like sparks above a dying fire. And all through the facades, my seams stretch. 

I feel the north wall trying to remember that it is seventy paces away from the south wall. The tension across my middle pulls taut as a harp string. Through my translucent skin, the blink-delay widens just enough to catch the fading light: for a few breaths every evening, if you looked at the bruised violet wood of my threshold, you would see me faintly glowing with half a second of trapped afternoon sunshine still leaking out of the dark archway after the square itself has gone blue with dusk. 

I hold on, stretched thin across the widening gap of the town, every nerve singing with the strain of keeping upstairs stapled to downstairs, until the heavy mahogany stridulators march out onto the worn rails, lock their spiked knees into the ancestral grooves, and saw the air solid again. The shockwave hits the walls; the gable settles back onto its invisible prop; the slack snaps out of the doorways; and I relax back into the cool pith, digesting a whole day’s worth of footsteps, waiting in two places at once for the stars to cross the small, silent lid of the sky.
