I would not be in the center of the bowl, where the lithium runs richest and the silvered aerostats drop their cables out of the blue. Look instead toward the upper northeastern shelf, three quarters of a mile below the jagged teeth of the rim, where the stepped basalt begins to steepen and the rising mist meets the downdrafts off the scree.

Up there, the air forgets what season it belongs to every twenty minutes. You stand chest-deep in ninety-degree bladder-ferns, sweat and condensation running down your ribs, and suddenly an invisible blade of twenty-degree alpine wind peels off the high grey rock, misses the thermal column by fifty yards, and slices straight across your collarbone before the swamp swallows it and turns it into a sudden, blinding white fog-bank. The plants along that margin grow almost horizontally, twisted like arthritic knuckles, gripping the stone lips with wide, flat pads of root.

At first it seems like the right thing to be is the boundary itself—not flesh at all, maybe one of those low, rounded basalt bolsters right where a hairline fissure weeps scalding mineral water into the cold wind-shadow, wearing a skin of orange thermophiles on one flank and crisp, dry alpine lichen on the back. Just sitting there for ten thousand years, translating fire into bog.

Except there is too much small, careful noticing going on in this basin not to have hands. 

If you give me hands here, they have faint, permanent ochre crescents stained into the cuticles, and the skin across the knuckles is thickened and slightly waxy from lanolin and tea-tree salve. My ribs have widened over fifteen years until a tight shirt from the valley feels like a steel band, though nobody wears shirts much below the rim anyway unless the sun breaks out hard at noon. 

Before the basin, down at sea level or in some cold port city, I did something where an eighth of an inch was a catastrophe—repairing marine chronometers, maybe, or maintaining the pressure-seals on unmanned bathyspheres. Something solitary, metallic, and unforgiving of hurry. I came up on a temporary contract to calibrate the strain-gauges on the older booms when Number One and Number Two were still bright, unstained alloy, back before the company realized the sulfur traces would eat standard solder in four months. Everyone else on that engineering crew spent their off-hours huddled inside the temporary pressurized dome, nursing altitude headaches and counting days until the gondola went down. I stepped outside on the third evening, took three slow breaths of thin, wet, mineral-sweet air, heard six million water droplets hitting waxy leaves with no echo whatsoever, and felt a lifetime of background static abruptly shut off behind my eyes.

Now I work the outer drift—mostly sticking with Rig Six, which is smaller than the central harvesters. Technically it isn’t even a harvester anymore; they stripped its dorsal slurry tank eight years ago when its main brine-pump cracked, and refitted it as a mobile hydrological tender. The low-valley ledgers call it Survey Unit B, though on the shelf we just call it the Mule.

Instead of drinking lithium from the deep throats of the mountain, the Mule creeps along the upper perimeter terraces, reading the health of the seal.

That’s where you find me in the late morning, strapped into a set of oval limb-frames, belly-down across the broad, rubbery crown of a stunted ironwood thicket fifty yards ahead of the Mule’s front rollers. From ten paces off I look less like a person and more like a pale, flat-backed crab resting on the canopy, four woven hoops distributing my hundred and fifty pounds so the branches only dip three inches into the warm surface film. 

Beneath my chin, the floating root-mat is forty years old and eighteen inches thick, woven from sedge-rhizomes, dead fern-fronds, and tough yellow strings of bacterial mucilage. Underneath that mat lies four feet of hundred-and-forty-degree silt. Right here, an old bull browser—heavy-scarred, blind in one milky eye, missing half an ear from rolling on sharp scree as a calf—punched a foreleg clean through a thin spot during the night when a falling rock off the high rim startled him. 

Left alone, that ragged, dinner-plate-sized puncture is how a terrace bleeds out. Scalding under-water wells up too fast through the tear, cooks the surrounding sedge roots before they can knit across the gap, widens the hole, washes the ochre silt downslope into the clean vents, and within two years a whole brimming pan slumps into raw, sterile mud.

So I don’t carry spanners as heavy as Maren’s. Around my waist, alongside the ceramic scraper and the ginger-paste tin, hang coils of fibrous, dried bromeliad-leaf twine and heavy wooden bodkins carved from dead ironwood—nothing foreign, nothing synthetic that won’t rot into soil once its job is done. Reaching down through the parted shrubs, wrists slick with rising steam, I pull the torn lips of the root-mat back together with a blunt fiberglass hook, fold a living pad of thick, spongy bolster-moss into the seam like a poultice, and stitch the turf shut in four slow, wide cross-loops. Within forty-eight hours the thermophilic slime will glaze my stitches; within a month the sedge will shoot white root-hairs straight through the moss, and even looking right at it you won't find the scar.

While I work, the Mule waits behind me, thirty tons of bruised-green alloy resting gently on its six wide bladders, letting out its long, damp pneumatic sigh once every forty seconds. *Hhhhhh-tck.* My own lungs—faster than a lowlander’s, slower than a newcomer’s—rise on the *hhhhhh* and drop after the *tck*. Two pink marsh-frogs sit stacked one atop the other inside the warm brass cup of the rig’s disconnected spotlight housing, throats pulsing rapidly, watching me with gold-flecked eyes.

What I love best is Tuesday afternoon, right after the personnel basket goes up.

Every four weeks I have to ride down in it, of course. You can’t argue with the skin between your toes turning white and soft as boiled dough, or the tiny red pinpoint rashes creeping into your armpits despite the ginger and tea-tree. Down in the nine-thousand-foot town, I rent the exact same narrow room behind a dry-goods warehouse every single rotation. I run two humidifiers day and night until the wallpaper curls, wear soft foam plugs in my ears whenever I walk down to the pharmacy so the roar of ore-trucks and the hard, dry clatter of boots on concrete won’t make my shoulders flinch up around my neck, and sleep ten hours at a stretch under three wool blankets because everything below fourteen thousand feet feels dead, cold, and brittle. By day eleven, my sinuses ache and my heavy, thick-walled heart thuds against my mattress like a trapped fist, missing the thin air it grew to push against.

Coming back up through the freezing cloud-deck, dangling under the belly of the silent silver aerostat, you watch the sterile grey talus drop away, watch the jagged rim pass fifty feet below your boots—and then the basket crosses the lip and drops straight into the column of rising vapor. Instantly the frost vanishes off the aluminum rails, turning to warm, fat beads of water under your bare palms. Your tight lungs unlock. The world turns green, amber, and silent again.

I rarely sleep inside the cork-lined crawlspace unless my skin is raw and needs the dry condenser-bleed overnight. Even with the oval hatch propped an inch open, six inches of baffled alloy dulls the register of the upper terrace too much. 

Instead, I sling my gore-mesh cocoon between the Mule’s rear hydraulic boom and the thick, leaning elbow of an ancient shrub right near the edge of a shallow pan. 

After dark, when the cobalt sky goes black and the high stars burn with fierce, unblinking mountain clarity whenever the ground-steam parts, the upper shelf becomes an acoustic lens for very small things. Cold air tries to slide down from the scree; hot water pushes back from the stone; between them hangs a dense, warm ceiling of mist four feet above my hammock. Underneath that ceiling, sound travels ten yards with crystal intimacy and dies completely at twenty.

Lying on my back, hands folded over my deep ribs, smelling mildly of mutton-tallow, wet stone, and crushed ginger, I don't think in words much at all. Words belong down in the dry towns with ledgers and diesel engines. Up here, you just keep company with the margin. Somewhere ten feet below my shoulder blades, the giant platter-snails come out to graze the bacterial velvet along the warm stone lip, their rasping radulae making a tiny, rhythmic *scritch-scritch* like sandpaper on wet wood, interrupted every few minutes when two shells bump together with a soft, hollow *tok*. Farther out in the milky dark, the old one-eyed browser wades slowly into the brimming pan, letting out a long, loose rumble from his chest as the hundred-degree water takes the ache out of his stiff hips. 

Every little while, a stray ribbon of snow drifts over the unseen rim a thousand feet above, falls silently toward us through the starlight, hits the rising breath of the basin, and reaches the mesh above my face four seconds later as six warm drops of rain. *Pat. Pat-pat.* Below me in the mist, the Mule shifts half an inch as a cooling valve settles, clicks once deep in its brass throat, and goes back to waiting for morning.
