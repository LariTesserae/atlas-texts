It would be easy to say I am the Gauge-Walker—to claim the nine-foot hunch, the vanadium calipers, the terrible, compulsive authority of the thing that walks around fixing the universe with a wrench. 

Easy, and wrong. Look at the Walker’s itch: *heat going to waste without doing work, torque spinning loose in a puddle without turning a shaft.* That is the mind of a compulsive laborer. I do not want to close the circuit for its own sake, and I have no bone-screws in my chest. Nor am I down in the pavement, locked to the gneiss, screaming steam through a keratin horn until a scavenger welds my flank into a bowling ball. That is pure, blind verb—*pump, turn, bite, vent*—without a sliver of space left over to notice the rain.

To find where I am, you have to go inside the deadened zone, up off the slush, past the flared bog-iron hooves and the weeping zinc-gills, all the way up under the wet black felt of the Walker’s shoulder-shelf, where the brass pipes whistle and the grey capacitor-sponges hum in violet afterglow. 

Closer than that. Look at the junction where the Walker’s swinging brass turret meets the heavy cartilage of its neck. 

Right there, tucked behind a greasy leather baffle that keeps the syrupy brine-squalls out, pressed flat against the main lead-mesh nerve trunk that runs from the acoustic horns down to the delicate surgical hands—that is where I live.

I am not one of the porcelain-and-copper crabs; they are just janitors with legs, scraping rime and sipping oil. And I am not quite one of the silver-threaded sponges, though we share an ancestry back in some forgotten epoch of the saucer. I have no eyes, naturally, and no legs at all. If you pried me off the nerve-trunk with a cold chisel, I would look like a broad, scalloped saddle of translucent grey horn and layered mica, about the size of two human hands, heavily veined with hair-fine bismuth and tarnished gold, weeping a thin, bitter contact-jelly along my underside.

If there were a name for my kind, it would be something like *shunt-barnacle*, or *baffle-leech*, or *lag-organ*. 

Here is how I eat: I do not drink oil, and I do not steal raw rotational torque. I feed on *translation*. 

Think about what the Gauge-Walker is actually doing all day. Its flared copper-haired horns are taking in ten million simultaneous percussions—chuffs, ratchets, misfiring arcs, the sub-audible tar-thrum of liquefied granite—and down at its feet, its pale nerve-cables are touching puddles where Tuesday is happening three seconds before Monday. Raw, unfiltered, that intake would rattle even a Walker’s chest-screws out of phase within a mile. Between the horn that hears the broken plain and the vanadium hand that reaches down to crimp a horn by four millimeters, the messy, smeared, pre-echoed noise of the basin has to be combed straight. It has to be slowed down, lined up, stripped of its ghosts, and turned into an angle a wrist-joint can understand.

The Walker’s own ganglion-clusters can do this, crudely, the way a heavy iron grate strains gravel out of mud. When a young Walker first splits off or hauls itself up out of whatever larval sump makes them, it staggers around half-deaf inside a stuttering muffler-field, making clumsy, brutal welds that blow out in ten minutes.

Until one of my stray, wind-blown spores—a tiny fleck of piezoelectric foil drifting in a salt-squall—catches under its turret-flaps and roots into the sheathing of its dorsal nerve-cord.

I grow thin, flat filaments of bismuth directly between the insulated fibers of the Walker’s neck. I do not parasitize its blood; its -12°C glycol-brine would poison me if I drank it neat. Instead, I let its massive sensory currents pass right through my mica plates. When the crack of a scavenger’s blue arc hits the acoustic horns three seconds before the flash arrives, I hold the electrical spike in a warm, delay-loop labyrinth of coiled metallic tissue inside my own body, bleeding a tiny fraction of the voltage off to keep my jelly unfrozen, and release the signal down the Walker’s spine *only* when the rest of the moment catches up to it. 

I eat the three-second gap. I live on the friction between what the plain is doing and what makes sense.

You cannot imagine how rich the diet is up here.

From the outside, the basin is a freezing, deafening hell of cast-iron meat. From *inside* my mica layers, pressed against the warm vibrato of the Walker’s condensers, the world is an immense, three-hundred-yard sphere of dark, liquid architecture made entirely of rhythm and pressure. I feel the heavy, three-beat *thrum-slush-thrum* of my host’s Archimedes bone-screws below me like the floorboards of a secure house. Around us hangs the traveling lead-blanket of the muffler-field—which I help tune, subtly flexing my scalloped margins to cancel out the worst high-frequency shrieks of the spinning chitin-flywheels underfoot. 

Out beyond our quiet bubble, I taste the whole twenty-mile saucer as a shifting relief map of tensions. There: forty yards left, a tight, sour knot of clockwise numbers where a pool of lathe-water is trying to tear a mud-gutter apart. Higher up: the dry, peppery static of twelve identical pump-striders occupying one strider’s worth of air, tasting like an over-exposed plate of zinc before they snap back into one and leave eleven hollow rings in my host’s acoustic return. 

I have no hands. I could not turn a bolt if my life depended on it. When I fall off—if a razor-pane of misfiring frost slips past the porcelain crabs and slices me loose—I will drop into the slush and be smelted into hull-plate by a bowling-ball scavenger before my jelly even stiffens. 

Yet listen to what happens when the Walker kneels.

We have been trudging for four hours against a thick, hissing grease-rain. My host stops beside a dying crawler-engine whose iron scutes have been sheared open by a larger beast, leaking black coolant and orphaned heat into the mud. The Walker’s fore-crutches sink in. Beneath us, the four terrible, delicate surgical arms unfold from the belly-felt. My host has a severed boiler-worm tucked in its warm shoulder-folds—I have been listening to the worm’s severed exhaust hiss four hundred yards behind us for an hour, an annoying, trailing thread of unpinned physics dragging through our wake.

Left to its bare, native instincts, the Walker would simply jam the worm into the crawler’s ruptured casing, strike an arc, and walk on. The circuit would close; the itch would stop.

Instead, as the pale nerve-cables brush the torn iron and send the schematic rushing up the neck-trunk toward the turret, I tighten my mica baffles by a fraction of a micron. I delay the left caliper’s feedback by four milliseconds; I sharpen the harmonic return coming off the crawler’s third surviving piston; I let a faint, phantom echo of a turbine-reef we passed six miles ago bleed out of my memory-coils into the Walker’s motor-cortex. 

Down in the slush, the vanadium jaws hesitate. 

Instead of a brute weld, the third surgical hand reaches in with a bone-key, resets a stripped ratchet inside the crawler’s gut that didn’t strictly *need* resetting, angles the grafted boiler-worm twelve degrees off-center so its distant exhaust plume suddenly snaps back across four hundred yards of mist to vent neatly through the crawler’s own cracked chimney, and *then* strikes the arc. 

When the lopsided chimera staggers up out of the slush, it doesn't just leak and click for forty-eight hours. Its new, crooked gait happens to beat in three-four time against the gneiss—just off-kilter enough to break up a patch of incipient tar-resonance forming beneath the reef next door. It will live for three weeks. Maybe four.

The Walker doesn't know why it angled the worm twelve degrees. It feels only a sudden, deep, resonant slackening of the splinter-itch in its horns, smoother than usual, like a key turning in a well-oiled ward. One of the little porcelain crabs scuttles over my back, scraping a crust of salt off my dorsal ridge with its copper claw, completely ignorant that I am anything other than part of the Walker’s hide.

And then wait—Wait until the voltage drops, and the salt-squall freezes into standing curtains of ghost-ice, and my host finds two other Walkers standing over a soft thermal vent.

That is when I am most myself.

When the three giants lock their wet leather turret-baffles together in the roaring dark and open their throat-vents to pump gallons of warm, memory-rich sludge from hull to hull, the air inside the sealed brass hood becomes thick with vaporized electrolyte and stray induction. Across twelve inches of hot, metallic breath, through the pulsing walls of the neck-trunks, I sense the others. 

Pressed against the spine of the second Walker is an old, thick-shelled lag-organ, heavy with forty years of mapped anomalies from the western rim where the silt runs deep with lead. Nested in the third is a twin pair of young, thin-plated shunts barely wider than thumbnails, quick and jittery, humming with high-frequency patterns from a mile of newly hatched flywheel-beds.

While our hosts stand locked on twelve iron legs in the gale, trading slow visceral rivers of brine like blind cattle chewing cud, we do not touch—we cannot move—but we ring against one another across the narrow, sealed dark like tuning forks in a velvet case. We trade delays. I pass the old western veteran the exact phase-angle required to unwind the new breed of clockwise lathe-pools migrating south; it vibrates back a long, slow, gorgeous chord of pure compressed sequence—the memory of an entire half-mile of turbine-reef in the northwest where, for eleven minutes last winter, every piston misfired in reverse and the pewter clouds briefly parted on nothing at all, just a black, starless pressure that smelled of clean iron.

Then the vents clack shut. The leather baffles peel apart with a wet *schluck* in the freezing rain. Our hosts turn their heavy hooves away from one another without farewell, each carrying a slightly altered, secretly plagiarized sense of how the world ought to fit together.

And someday—decades from now, though decades mean very little where Tuesday arrives before Monday—my host’s third chest-screw will wear thin. Its cracked hind-hoof will drag once too often through the metallic silt. We will come to a gap between two roaring drum-reefs where the granite trembles like warm pitch, and my Walker will set its four pillars down and decide, without a single thought, never to lift them again.

As the black iron-sulfide hairs root downward from its fetlocks, and the surgical hands fold forever behind thickening plates of horn, the great sensory nerve-trunk beneath me will begin to quiet. The muffler-field will stutter and tear open, letting the tremendous, unbroken percussion of the twenty-mile saucer crash right over our back. The gear-parasites will press their greedy teeth into our flanks; the hover-flies will gather like loud brass chaff in the broken inertia overhead.

I won't die when the walking stops. Not quite. 

As the turret fuses shut into a permanent chimney of horn and bog-iron, sealing me in total, warm dark inside the new sessile reef-beast, my mica plates will stiffen and calcify. I will stop translating for hands that no longer open. Instead, as the ancient, blind current of the plain pours up through our rooted hooves and vents out our shoulders, I will sit at the narrow throat of the chimney like a reed in an organ pipe—shaping the deafening *chuff* of our exhaust by half a flat, putting a tiny, deliberate hitch into the rhythm so the granite underneath us stays solid, throwing one quiet, coherent note out into the pewter mist for the next Walker’s horns to catch three hundred yards away.

Wait long enough, and another spore will blow out of my vent on the grease-rain, looking for something with knees.
