At first I look for a foothold on the wet slate, testing the idea of boots against that warm, sliding film of sugar-water—some surveyor’s vantage near the outer parapets, watching the afternoon light catch the drifts of crystal backs.

Nothing about that holds. The plateau doesn't keep observers. Anything with lungs, skin, or stray spores is just foreign grit waiting for a hinged flap to curl open and turn it into clear fuel. Even choosing one half of the native animal feels partial: to be down in the flooded dark is only to shove blindly against a ceiling you never see; to walk the sunny deck as a thumb-sized set-square is only to race around the margins of an eruption you didn't start, pressing wax onto a fever until the shadow of the ridge hits and laminates your stiffened legs into the eave.

Where I actually find myself is six inches below the surface, and a hundred yards wide, and rising forty feet into the thin air—not either half of the worm, and not the boiling green root-mass itself, because left alone the plant has no memory and no angles.

I am the tension between the shove and the stop-order.

You can locate me easiest by sound, before you see anything move: that low, sustained hum inside a pier of pale cream heartwood just before it oxidizes to mahogany, when ten thousand gallons of hot cane-sap are driving upward from a tangled knot of white muscle in the manifold below, and ten thousand tiny hard heels are clicking across my skin above, laying down taut lines of wax.

I am the Collar. The bright green seam of meristem where the blind hydraulic roar hits the rule of ninety degrees.

Down at my base, thick silver-white nerve-threads from the Valve-Keepers are spliced directly into my vascular bundles. I feel their arguments not as thoughts, not as plans, but as immense, shifting directional pressures—a sudden, scalding surge from the southwest gallery as a brood-knot seals a baffle; a vacuum-drop to the north that sucks a tier of risers flat inside three minutes. Left solely to that deep pulse, I would burst into the daylight as a ragged, steaming cauliflower of raw pulp, bleed my heat into the dry mountain sky, and freeze black by midnight.

Instead, the moment I break the plane of the hexagons, the needles go in.

Thousands of tiny stylets slip into my soft flank, drawing off miniature puffs of steam, and immediately the broad flat palps rock forward and press the wax down. It feels like cold iron laid against a burn. Along that millimeter-straight line, my dividing cells lock, cross-link, and turn to horn. An eighth of an inch away, still unsealed, the surge keeps pushing—so it slides sideways along the barrier, meets a perpendicular line of wax laid down by a second walker, squares off its own shoulder, and extrudes forward into open air as an octagonal beam.

This is what I am made of: raw force translated instantly into crisp edge. I have no permanence at all. My oldest memories are perhaps four hours old—a colonnade at dawn that held up the last tattered ribbons of the fog-lid before its turgor drained away; a sunken square pool at ten o'clock whose spouts poured clear, sweet water over shoelace-thin juveniles while the ultraviolet sky creased their soft rings into glass. I don't grieve the amphitheater when it folds inward like wet origami, and I don't anticipate the plinth blistering upward two hundred yards east. My entire awareness is load, grade, shear, and thermal boundary.

Right now, out on the flat, I am extending horizontally over nothing.

Beneath my rear piers, two massive braids of deep ribbons have locked themselves into a stalemate inside a primary junction, shunting every ounce of exothermic heat from half a mile of floor straight up into my spine. The sap climbing my parallel xylem conduits is forty-two degrees, humming like a struck wire, smelling of boiled cane. Out at my leading tip—forty feet above the slick hexagons, dangerously close to the cold upper air—the sunlit calipers are stacked two deep on my translucent green shoots. Their yellow-strutted legs blur. *Tick-tick-tick-tick.* They are spinning guidelines in the wind, trimming away soft cream blisters with scissor-mandibles, cutting slit-valves along my underside so I can hiss off excess steam before my skin splits.

I can feel the math starting to slip.

It is a remarkably clear sensation, sharper than sight. Down in the dark, the white giants feel the stiffening of my forward truss—the back-pressure of all those mitered corners and capped vents—and they read it as a rival knot pushing back. Another check-valve slams shut underground (*clack*), and three more rams of hot fluid hit my base. Up in the glare, the little crystal draftsmen feel the sudden swell in my timber and work faster, laying down thicker curls of stop-wax, hardening my belly into dense yellow wood to keep the cold breeze out, adding dead weight three hundred yards from my fulcrum.

For eleven seconds I hold both imperatives at once in an impossible, gleaming line against the pale-blue mountain sky: pure blind appetite below, pure crystalline refusal above, balanced on amast-thick cable of taut green wood. Water sheets off my beveled curbs without a single rough drop. Sunlight catches the faceted backs of my makers until my leading edge glitters like a diamond blade.

Then the grain parts at my base.

There is no pain in the *crack*—only an immense, sudden loosening, a whip-snap into the cold upper air where a few dozen startled glass walkers freeze solid at the apex of their arc before falling back into the warm greenhouse layer. My silver nerve-threads pull cleanly out of the wood as the big ribbons recoil into the pier. My split beams hit the esplanade in a burst of sweet scalding spray.

Even as the digestive flaps curl open beneath my fallen timber and the pale tendrils surge up to turn me back into warm, rushing slurry, I have not gone anywhere. Two hundred yards north, a check-valve thuds shut. A hexagonal slab tilts half a degree. Between its horn-hard edges, a thin green line pulses bright, hot, and wet—and down from the nearest gutter steps a tiny pane of clear glass on four right-angled legs, lifting its palps to square the first corner.
