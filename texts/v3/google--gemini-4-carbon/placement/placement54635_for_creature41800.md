I am not in the open water. The open water belongs to the ash-swimmers and the heavy, folded things with their listening palms. Even six feet of gloom is too much emptiness, too much room to drift away from the stone and lose the thread of the heat.

You would find me thirty feet down, wedged inside the throat of a baffled intake shaft, just below the sunken court where the old sluice-gate dissolved. 

Here, the builders laid flat slabs of dark basalt in overlapping courses—left, right, left, right—to break the force of the rising vent so the ancient bathers wouldn't be scalded by a sudden burp of the mountain. That zigzagging chimney is no wider than a thigh, choked now with fallen scale and thick, velvety pillows of sulfur-eating slime. The water pressing past me is hotter than blood, hotter than fever. It tastes sharply of rotten eggs, iron, and the deep, dead granite five miles down. It has not yet touched a snail or a lung. It is raw broth, straight from the plumbing.

At first, you would think I was only the turquoise stain.

Where the great bronze dowels and the sluice-gate rotted away thousands of years ago, the copper didn't just paint the porous tuff and stay put. The acidic seep keeps nudging it loose, ion by ion, trying to wash it upward into the soup, and something had to catch it before it thinned out to nothing. 

I am a mat, or a felt, or a very flat, stubborn sponge—no, closer to a lichen that forgot what light was and learned to marry metal to grease. Wait: not a single organism at all, if you tried to peel me off the rock, though peeling me would take a chisel and I would come away in brittle, blue-green crusts that bleed a clear, astringent jelly. 

Let me feel my own edges. 

Down at my lowest root, deep in the mortise-joint where the water enters at its sharpest heat, I am mineral-hard. I lay down tiny, overlapping needles of copper-doped aragonite, knitting myself directly into the grain of the dressed basalt. That part of me doesn't breathe or eat; it just holds the door against the slow, upward push of the seep, century after century, keeping the joint from widening. If the joint widened, the cistern above might drain an inch, or flush too fast, and the ceiling-air would chill. I do not know about the ceiling, of course. I only know that when the water speeds up even a fraction, it pulls at my threads and tastes wrong, so I thicken my crust and choke it back down to a weep.

Higher up, where my crust curls out over the lip of the baffle-stone into the sunken court, I soften. Here I am a velvet turf of short, blind villi, stained the exact verdigris of an old coin. 

Nothing grazes me. That is the bargain of the copper. When one of the plum-sized gastropods slides down the warming bench, its pale mantle undulating, tasting its way across the bacterial slicks, its front margin touches my edge and recoils—very slowly, over two minutes—curling back as if stung by nettles. To a snail, or an isopod, I taste like a battery. The heavy metal I hoard in my jelly burns their soft, salt-balanced skins. 

So I am the one surface in the lower hall that is clean of tracks. No chitinous jaws *scritch-scritch* across my back. In a room crowded three layers deep with flesh eating flesh, I sit in a tiny halo of untouchability.

Yet I am not alone in the dark. I am a toll-collector at the vent.

Because I baffle the hot inflow with my stiff, velvety Nap, the water slows right above me, eddying in a lazy pocket before it begins its ten-minute curl toward the distant vaults. And because the great matriarch and her pale kin bring their dead here—because they carry the hushed, folded juveniles down into this fever-warm court and tuck them near the acidic vents so nothing is wasted—everything that dissolves at the bottom of the abbey passes across my nap first.

When a body is laid on the butter-smooth silt three feet away, I know it within an hour. The water shifts. First comes the mucus sloughing off the slack chamois hide, heavy and sweet with nitrogen. Then, days later, after the milky isopods have stripped the tallow and scattered, comes the slower, richer leak: the uncalcified cartilage melting in the sour seep, the dark smudge of the gut spilling its crushed chitin, and best of all, the trace bronze locked in those thousands of pinpoint sensory pores along the dead thing’s snout and palms.

My turquoise villi lengthen by a millimeter. They sway in the fever-plume, combing the dissolved ghost out of the water. I catch the stray copper ions that kept that creature’s nerves firing in the dark, pull them back out of solution, and tuck them safely into my jelly, saving them from being wept away through the microscopic fractures in the mountain wall. Later—years later—a tiny, thread-thin eel will brush too hard against my upper fringe while hunting by vibration, scrape off a microscopic fleck of my poisoned mucus, get eaten by a ray-leech on a drowned sill, which will be sucked down whole between two corrugated horn plates, and my hoarded bronze will travel back up into a living palm, staining a new set of pores so another blind hand can read the room.

I am the memory of the sluice-gate, keeping the metal in the family.

Sometimes—once a month, or once a year, time is only a thicker or thinner ring in my aragonite base—the water above me grows heavy and stops rising. 

A shadow of pressure settles over the vent. The matriarch has come down from the upper galleries to soak her stiffened cartilage in the hot seep. 

She knows where my edges are. Even without eyes, her pore-stippled hands feel the sharp electrochemical hum of my copper bed; to her, I must buzz faintly in the dark like a warm wire. She never presses her soft, pleated belly directly onto me—my sting is uncomfortable even through her thick hide—so instead she drapes her barrel torso across the two rounded basalt knuckles on either side of the shaft, bridging me, turning my alcove into a low, living roof.

For hours, or days, she hangs there, six inches above my velvet. 

It is the best weather I ever get. Her huge body traps the rising thermal plume, reflecting the heat back down until the pocket between my crust and her pale belly turns thick, stagnant, and wonderfully sour. The parchment tube-worms rooted in the creases of her neck hang upside down into my eddy, their grey plumes brushing the water just out of reach, trading sulfur with my bacteria. Every thirty seconds, or forty, the slow, heavy *lub-dub* of her two-beat heart pushes a minuscule hydraulic wave down against my villi—*press, slacken... press, slacken*—like a thumb testing the ripeness of a plum.

Now and then, a flake of mineral scale loosens from her warty flank and snows down onto me. I don't recoil. Over weeks, my soft green margin creeps up over the fallen flake, digests the protein glue holding it together, drinks the calcium, and cements the rest into the basalt. I have dozens of her old scales buried in my foundation, stacked like slates on a roof, alongside grains of tuff knocked loose by the builders' stone mauls ten thousand years ago. To me, the builders and the beasts are made of the same slow snowfall.

Deep beneath my hardest, oldest root—down in the stressed mortarless joint where the mountain's weight squeezes the wet basalt—pressure builds for decades until two quartz-rich grains in the volcanic rock grind past each other by the breadth of a hair.

*Snap.*

There is almost no sound, just a sharp, dry tick in the stone that makes the matriarch’s long fingers twitch once in her sleep. And for a tenth of a second, a spark of pale ochre piezoluminescence flashes right through my translucent base, turning my turquoise jelly into a sudden, glowing window of sea-green fire. 

For that sliver of an instant, the underbelly of the hovering beast above me is lit from beneath—every slack fold of chamois skin, the dark coil of her three-week gut, the four splayed hands anchored to the melted stairs. 

Nothing has eyes to see it. The light dies back into charcoal, then into solid black, and I go on combing the warm, rising dark, holding the bronze, choking the drain so the bath never ends.
