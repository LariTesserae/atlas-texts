I am three inches below the second plinth, and I am not finished growing yet.

The world here is a thumb’s-width wide. To my left, a hair-thin ridge of grey-silver metal stands four inches proud of the eroded limestone, cold and sharp-edged, holding up the ghost of a floor that dissolved before the oldest mantle in the ceiling was spawned. Above me, the flat, sheared cylinder of the plinth juts out into the storm, throwing a permanent, screaming shadow of calm water down across my back. 

Out beyond the edge of my ridge, the whiteout boils. I cannot see it—I have no eyes, only a diffuse band of light-sensitive tissue across my crown that tells me whether the noon is clean or thickened by spray, or briefly eclipsed by a passing shadow—but I feel it in the ring of tiny, taut bladders circling my rim. Out there, the air tears itself in half. Out there, an ankle-deep wall of warm water slams sideways across the basin, hits the proud metal inlay an inch from my skirt, and vaults straight over me in a roaring, sunlit roof.

Inside that roof, in the triangular pocket behind the metal line, the water only eddies and purls. 

I am the size of two hands laid flat together, still mostly clear. If you peeled me off the stone—though nothing in this room could peel me off without tearing my muscle ring in two—you could read the grain of the chert right through my middle. My dark violet underside hasn't fully come in yet; it is still mottled, smoky like bruised quartz, and my three mouths are soft horn, barely hard enough to shave the tenderest new shoots at the edge of the strap-weed mat.

My whole body is an engine for listening to twelve seconds from now.

Right now, the bladders along my eastern edge are pinched flat. That means the southern wall-gale is climbing high, dragging a sheet of cold ceiling-seepage up toward the middle node. My western bladders are swollen, trembling with a low, rapid flutter: the northern cyclone has hit the floor early and is skidding across the basin, pushing a heavy wedge of air ahead of it. 

I tuck my pleated fins tighter under my skirt. I press my center down, squeezing two drops of water out from beneath my belly until my cirri bite the microscopic pores of the limestone.

Ten. Eleven. 

The flutter in my western rim stops. The pressure climbs so fast my cartilage aches with a sweet, bright tightness. 

*Crack.*

Everything vanishes. For half a heartbeat the water-roof above my ridge collapses, slapped flat into the stone, and the pure, dry, blue-white heat of the hanging fires hits my back unimpeded. My crown blazes with light. In that instant of terrible stillness, before the mist springs back, a huge, pale eclipse drops straight past my plinth—one of the heavy ones, banking low off the eastern wall, forty miles an hour of flexed gristle and dark violet belly. Its wake hits my shelter a fraction of a second after the chamber’s own shockwave, a sharp double-thump that tries to pluck my rear edge off the rock.

I hold. My muscles don't even have to think to hold; the ring-nerve fires by itself, clamping harder the harder the water pulls. Then the mist explodes back upward, the warm rain resumes its sideways howl, and the heavy shadow is already fifty feet away, hissing up the curve toward the northern arc.

I loosen my seal by a hair, let fresh, oxygenated spray wash under my skirt to cool my gills, and inch forward along the metal inlay to rasp another neat, millimeter-wide ribbon off the green mat.

---

I remember the wire, though not with a brain. I remember it in the curl of my front fringe, which still wants to hook around an absence.

Before I knew rock, I knew the dry. When I split my pearl-gray ring, my belly had never touched anything at all—because the inside of the ring rode the dead-air sheath of the black ribbon, sliding back and forth in the dark, silent millimeter where the storm is forbidden to go. When I crawled out onto the flank of the ring, the roar hit me all at once like a solid stone, trying to flatten me into jelly. 

For a long time—hundreds of thousands of *cracks*—the world was a narrow, horizontal highway of absolute stillness wrapped around a black core I could never touch. If I pressed my tiny, transparent foot down hard, I hit an invisible springiness, warm and slick without being wet, that pushed me gently back out into the wet fringe of the elders' mantles. 

There were always giants braided around us. Their anterior fringes locked together above and below the ribbon, forming a living tunnel of violet muscle and hooked cirri that smelled faintly of crushed algae and clean mucus. They took the sideways gales on their thick, rubbery backs so we wouldn't be blown down the floor-siphons. I grazed the thin, luminous slime off their skirts until I grew heavy enough that the ribbon’s narrow slipstream couldn't hide my edges anymore.

The first time the wind caught my rim and ripped me off the wire, I thought I was going down the siphons. 

I tumbled backward into blinding, shadowless fire. There was no up and no down—only hot spray stinging my soft mouths and a spinning roar that slammed all my pressure-bladders at once until I went rigid with shock. Stiffening saved me. Instead of falling straight into the surging basin floor, my flat little body caught an upward shear off the northern wall, skittered sideways like a skipped slate across thirty feet of boiling whiteout, and slapped hard against the cold, slick base of the second plinth.

I clamped down so fiercely I bruised my own muscle-ring. I didn't move for three thousand *cracks*. Water hammered me; once, a hydroplaning teardrop-skimmer shot right over my translucent back, mistaking me for bare limestone, its little suction-foot leaving a stinging trail across my dome before it vanished into the mist. 

Then hunger uncurled my mouths, and I smelled the bruised-kelp sweetness of the strap-weed tucked behind the proud geometric metal an inch away, and I slid down into the quiet lee where I live now.

---

Lately, the lee has started to feel small.

My back is thickening. When the *crack* clears the air and the high noon strikes down through the collapsed spray, I no longer feel the light pierce clean through me to warm the rock under my belly. Something new is living in my dorsal cartilage—tiny, pricking constellations of green that itch pleasantly in the glare and leave a faint, syrupy fullness in my blood even when I haven't rasped the strap-weed for hours. My outer skirt has begun to outgrow the four-inch shadow of the metal inlay. When the northern cyclone skids low across the floor now, its edge catches my trailing rim and tugs, lifting me a fraction of an inch before my suction snaps it back.

Yesterday—if there were days—one of the medium-sized mantles slammed into my plinth at the end of a long ballistic drop. 

It came in hot off the central node, bleeding speed in a screaming fifty-foot hydroplane down the basin slope, and locked down right beside my inlay with a heavy, wet *thwack* that drove all the water out of my pocket. For a long while we lay edge-to-edge. It was ten times my weight, its rubbery shield cross-hatched with faint whitechert-scars, its lateral bladders pulsing in a slow, deep rhythm against mine. 

When it shifted to graze, its heavy skirt slid right over half my dome. In the sudden violet dark beneath its belly, I felt the stiffness of its tucked hydrofoil fins, thick as planks, trembling with stored tension. 

Two seconds before the next *crack*, I felt its pressure-bladders empty in unison. It didn't clamp tighter. It lifted its leading edge *into* the coming shear, letting a wedge of wild air slide underneath its chest—and when the shockwave flattened the room, it didn't wait. It vaulted sideways off the plinth base, caught the rebounding floor-gust like a sail, and shot upward into the luminous whiteout, vanishing toward the high flutes before the spray even closed back in.

Since then, I have stopped clamping down on the *crack*.

Now, when my eastern bladders pinch flat and my western bladders swell with that familiar, sweet ache—ten, eleven—I creep halfway up the curved stone base of the sheared plinth, pushing my nose right up to the sharp, edited-flat rim where the shelter ends and the gale tears past at sixty miles an hour.

Twelve. 

*Crack.*

In the stunned, blazing clarity, I look up with my whole crown. High, high above, dimmed by distance and glowing mist, the three blue-white fists skitter and hum. Right beside the central fire, dark green like mossy glass against pale bone-stone, hangs the vast, scalloped oval of the Oldest One, drinking the noon, never falling at all.

I lift my front rim a millimeter off the limestone. I unfurl my tiny, pleated side-fins just far enough to let the rebounding gust bite underneath my violet chin. 

The wind yanks at me—fierce, warm, roaring to throw me across the vault—and for half a second my front half lifts clean off the world, riding nothing but compressed air and spray, before my terrified back-ring snaps tight and yanks me flat against the stone again as the ankle-deep surge thunders past.

Not this *crack*. Not the next ten thousand, maybe. My cartilage is still too supple; the floor-siphons down-slope would swallow me whole if I missed my tangent on the far wall.

So I slide back down behind the ancient metal line, press my clear edge tight into the quiet eddy, and rasp another neat green ribbon from the rock—listening to the twin gales climb the walls again, counting down to twelve, waiting until I am heavy enough to let go.
