I am down where the olive-amber fails—not in the central abyss, which is only an emptiness, a warm, sterile throat waiting for centuries of dead leaves it hasn't been given yet, but wedged into the steep under-cut beneath the lowest cantilevered ward. Here, four tiers below the public squares, the stacked hexagons give up their neatness and meet the raw, ropy skin of the lava dome as it actually spilled.

My ankles carry five drilled stones apiece. Without them I would drift up into the quarry-lanes and be in the way. With them, I sit heavy as a dropped maul on a shelf of glossy, unweathered *pāhoehoe*, my wide soles planted against a basalt rib, feeling the mountain’s slow pulse through the horn of my heels.

People think the caldera is silent except for the gas-beads and the thumb-rings. It isn't. If you press a knuckle-bone or the flat of a jaw against the uncut rock where the masonry roots into the cliff, the whole basin hums—a low, cramped, cooling groan, miles wide, as the young crust shrinks a hair's breadth against itself and lets go of another ribbon of heat. Every three or four days, deep inside the flank of the dome, a buried seam parts cleanly with a muffled *thock* you feel in your breastbone before your ears sort it out. Half an hour later, the floor-vent beside my left knee breathes two degrees hotter, or stops breathing altogether and hands its warmth over to a fissure six paces down-ledge.

That is why I am down here. Somebody has to watch the plumbing of a house built on a cooling loaf.

My hands are stained past the webbing—not slate-gray like my ribs, where the pebbled hide sheds water cleanly, but stained a stubborn, rusty ocher in every crease from handling the iron-slick bacterial mats that lip the hottest cracks, and nicked white at the pads despite forty years of knowing better. Fresh volcanic glass doesn't forgive familiarity. Just yesterday a blister of iridescent mineral skin gave way under my palm while I was seating a chimney-collar, and parted the meat below my thumb so cleanly I didn't know I was cut until a ribbon of dark-green blood—green in this bruised light, until you lift a hand into a hearth-pocket and remember it's red—coiled lazily upward toward my chin. I sealed it with pitch from a curing-gourd and kept working.

I am an old Lung now, thick through the middle in the way we get when we stop hauling sky-sacks and start letting gravity and trim-weights do our standing. My crown-ridge has gone pale and stiff at the nape. Above me, clipped to an overhang of jagged glass by three braided kelp-tethers, hangs my private bell: a modest inverted basket sealed thick with black resin, no wider than my shoulders. Its underside looks like mercury gone slightly dull. I keep it fed from a skin I fill only twice a day off the communal main-trough two tiers up, because I don't use much. Long practice down on the vents teaches your ribs patience. Why break off setting a baffle-stone every six minutes when twelve will do if you keep your limbs loose and your heart down to a slow, heavy thud?

My work is small adjustments with wooden wedges and hollowed stems. Here, where the water shimmers thick as syrup coming out of a thumb-wide crack, the town’s hot-chimneys begin. If a fissure shifts in the night and begins belching sour yellow gas instead of clean scalding water, and nobody moves the intake hood before morning, an entire ward's wood-steaming grates go cold while their sleeping-hearths above taint at the edges with rotten-egg stink that leaves the children blinking thick oil-tears for days. So I creep along the raw seam with a pry-stick of fire-hardened shrub-wood and three sizes of pitch-sealed reed collars, shifting a stone here, bleeding a trapped blister of sulfur out toward the open gorge there so it ticks harmlessly away past the drift-nets toward the gray wool overhead.

Sometimes, while waiting for a freshly mortared slime-poultice to set around a pipe-base, I unhook two stones from my left ankle and let myself lean out sideways over the lip of the gorge, held by three toes curled into a cooling-crack.

Out there the water turns deep plum, then true charcoal. Ten paces off the ledge hangs a school of the little blind silver fry, two hundred of them suspended nose-down in an invisible chimney of rising mineral warmth, twitching their tails twice a second just to stay precisely where they are. We are doing the exact same thing, they and I—hanging in a fever-dream between dead cold sky and uncooled stone, eating what the cracks give up, waiting for a brand-new world to grow old enough to make mud.

From two tiers above, a crisp double-strike drops through the clear water and hits my collarbone like a flicked pebble: *tink-tink.* Pause. *Tink-rrrrap.* 

One of the wood-benders at the grates. Her vent has slacked off; the timber is stiffening before it takes the curve of the roof-rib.

I don't answer right away. First I pull myself upright along my tether, letting my chin tilt back until my face parts the trembling silver ceiling of my little basket-bell. My nostrils pop open. The air inside tastes of yesterday's pitch, my own warm breath, and a trace of wet ash hauled down from the pumice rafts by teenagers who hate the cold. I take three huge, slow draughts, feeling my ribs spread wide against my harness straps, watching a bead of water roll cleanly off the bridge of my nose and fall back into the world below my neck.

On the fourth breath I take only three-quarters, pinch my valves shut, drop my chin back into the blood-warm gloom, and slide my thumb-ring once across the notched basalt plaque at my thigh—*krrr-tink*—to tell her I heard, and that the heat is coming back as soon as I shift this stone.
