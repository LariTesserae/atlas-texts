I would not be out in the sprint of the eastern quarter, wrestling gut-frames into a sudden squall, and I would not be a hound, purple-flanked and trembling on the scent of falling pressure. My knees are not built for that ceaseless, flat-soled skimming over tallow-slick rock, nor my mind for the hair-trigger urgency of chasing water that vanishes in four breaths. 

You would find me further out, toward the gentle rise of the southern lip, sitting cross-legged in the lee of one of the dead beehive kilns. 

Up here, the sprawl thins out. The domes are two hundred paces apart instead of fifty, and the great, empty amphitheaters are half-swallowed by drifts of pale stone-flour blown off the marl. The kiln behind my back hasn't known heat since the plateau above lost its scrub-forests five hundred years ago, its corbeled throat choked long ago with packed grey clay and salt-crust so it won't whistle in the downdrafts. But its fat, rounded belly still breaks the worst of the katabatic chutes coming off the rim, creating a quiet pocket of slack air ten feet wide. 

My scalp is shaved like anyone else’s, nicked near the crown where my slate-flake caught a bump this morning, and my cheekbones bear the thick, greasy smudge of grey tallow and chalk. Without it, looking down across the bowl would be like staring into hammered tin. From this slight rise, I have the whole mile-wide floor tilted mildly toward me—the whale-backed swells of soapstone gleaming wetly in patches, the frantic, soundless geometry of dark figures skimming along the causeways, the pale swifts exploding upward like handfuls of thrown salt whenever a thermal tears loose from the stone.

Between my oiled knees sits a flat slab of dense, dark slate—harder than the chalky basin floor, hauled down in chunks from a seam halfway up the three-hundred-foot scree—and in my grey-crusted fingers, a heavy muller of smooth river-quartz. 

I am a grinder and a net-weight tier. Someone has to stay still so the others can run.

Beside me lie three shallow bowls carved from soapstone, covered with weighted flaps of cured crab-parchment so a rogue eddy doesn't strip them bare in an instant. One holds calcined crab-shell and white chalk scraped from the dry rings around empty cisterns. One holds dry, crumbling cakes of grey-yellow lichen peeled with bone knives from the sheltered undersides of the old amphitheater tiers. The third holds clear bird-fat, rendered slowly in the dark fermentation pits beneath my clan’s dome until the rankness has mostly settled out of it. 

All day, in the rushing, bright hush, I lean my weight over the dark slate and push the quartz muller in slow, rhythmic figure-eights. *Shhh-shhh. Shhh-shhh.* Even right above it, I barely hear the stone on stone; I feel the grit breaking down through the heels of my palms and up into the bones of my wrists, turning from sharp, tiny teeth into a velvet paste that catches the glare like wet pearl. That is the eye-grease, the wind-shield. Every face in the basin wears what comes off slabs like mine. Without the right balance of chalk to fat, the tallow melts and runs stinging into the eyes during a sprint, or dries and cracks in a dry gust, letting the upward bounce off the soapstone burn the corneas raw by noon.

When my shoulders stiffen from the muller, I tuck the paste away under its parchment lid, wipe my palms on my greased leather thighs, and pull over a pile of smooth, egg-shaped cobbles and a coil of dark, two-ply cord spun from our own cropped hair.

Tying roof-stones and hem-weights requires no eyes at all, which is why I do it in the afternoon when even the chalk-smear cannot stop the white sky from aching behind the brow. My broad, thick-knuckled fingers move on their own—*over, tuck, cinch, turn*—weaving tight, diamond-mesh jackets around the slick river-stones that have no purchase of their own. A badly tied cobble slips its net when a hide roof *thrums* in a midnight gale, shooting across a plaza like a sling-bullet to shatter a clay jar or an ankle. Mine do not slip. I pull every knot tight against my shinbone, exhaling thinly through pursed lips so the air doesn't rob the wet from my tongue.

Because I sit higher than the town and rarely move anything above my elbows, the basin-folk use me as a fixed mark, the way they use the cairns. 

Nobody comes close enough to touch knees unless they need a fresh pot of eye-tallow or six yards of weighted hem-cord. Instead, all afternoon, I am caught in the edge of their wide, architectural speech. Down on the lower causeway, a quarter-mile off, a cistern-warden will stop dead on the mirror-slick marl, let the wind plaster his heavy hair-cloak flat against his calves, and throw his left arm straight up toward my kiln before slicing his forearm horizontally toward the western drying tables. 

He isn't speaking to me, at first—he’s telling a baffle-crew three hundred yards to his right to watch the chute coming down past the grinder’s hill. 

But because I am sitting where I sit, looking where he cannot look—up at the pale lip of the upper crust where the cold air spills over—I see what the hounds down in the streets haven't tasted yet. High on the scree slope, a scatter of flat, grey talus-crabs has just clamped tight to the rock at once, folding their legs beneath their carapaces, and twenty yards below them, a patch of damp marl has vanished as if wiped by a giant thumb. A dry, vicious hammer of air is sliding down the dish, completely invisible, aimed straight at the southern quarter where three women have just unrolled a sheet of wet clay to knead for jar-lips.

I set the river-cobble carefully between my moccasins. I do not hurry; hurrying muddles a silhouette. 

I stand up straight against the bone-white belly of the dead kiln, letting my weighted cloak drop plumb. Raising both grease-greyed arms wide like the wings of a banking swift, I cross them once, sharply, over my shaved crown, then drop flat onto one knee, planting both palms on the stone. *Dry-shear. Heavy. Cover.*

Down in the bright, silent expanse, three hundred yards away, one of the potters catches the break in my stillness at the very edge of her slit-eyed vision. She doesn't look up to thank me or wave. Before my knee has fully settled on the slate, all three women have swept heavy, oiled hides over their damp clay, dropped flat on top of them like smooth river-cobbles themselves, and tucked their chins in. Four breaths later, the invisible wall of air slams past my kiln—shoving a dull, cold thumb into my eardrums, snatching a wisp of unspooled hair-thread straight sideways into nothing—and tears across their courtyard, finding nothing wet to steal.

I stay on one knee for a minute after it passes, utterly still, arms folded under my leather wrap so I don't accidentally say anything else to the mile of watching stone. 

Sometimes, toward late afternoon, when the glare softens from blinding tin to brushed pewter, the children come past my kiln on their way out to the fissures with the hounds and their long, curved fossil-ribs. They glide by ten paces off, barefoot or in thin crab-skin soles, silent as blown mist, their shaved heads gleaming faintly with oil. Sometimes the youngest one breaks rank just enough to skim close, dropping two pale, slick cave-mushrooms or a strip of salted swift-breast onto the flat slab beside my muller without breaking stride, her narrow dark eyes flicking up to mine for half a heartbeat—creased already at the corners, though she has barely seen seven winters—before she leans thirty degrees into a cross-gust and shoots away behind a sleek, purple-veined hound.

I like the white hours out here, with the silent storm pushing at my ribs and the whole town turning like clockwork beneath the birds. Yet by the time the sky thickens to starless slate and the chill begins to bite through the bird-oil on my scalp, my body is starved for the dark.

Packing my bowls into a netted sling, I glide down the wide, salt-ringed causeway, knees bent loose, moccasins sucking softly at the cooling soapstone where evening condensation is already beginning to bead. Behind my clan’s courtyard, I pause by the low, curving baffle wall to rest my knuckles for a second against a smooth, hollow collarbone wedged neatly between two slabs of grey slate—my mother’s sister, who taught me the figure-eight stroke on the dark stone forty years back, still turning the drafts away from our door.

Then I drop to all fours, crawl through the curved stone tunnel, push past the two heavy, stinking curtains of greased hide, and let the world vanish.

The heat hits my face like a wet wool wrap—thick with the smell of old tallow, sour crab-shell compost, warm dog-skin, and ten familiar bodies breathing in a ten-foot bowl of peat-lined stone. I cannot see an inch in front of my nose save for the tiny, plum-sized ember of the fat-lamp across the pit. Immediately, before I have even unslung my grinding bowls, two warm, calloused hands find my cold ankles and pull me gently up onto the warmed slate shelf. Another hand—my brother’s child, by the small, scarred knuckle—settles onto my collarbone, three fingers tapping softly against my skin: *Cold? Good yield?*

I lean my greased shoulder entirely against the greased shoulder beside me, letting the terrible, wide vigilance of the glare drop out of my spine all at once, while overhead the taut hide roof *thrums* once, deep and soft, under the weight of the night wind. Bringing my mouth close to the dark, I let my throat vibrate for the first time since dawn—a low, rapid, purring trickle of clicks and soft *shhhs*, my thumb tracing small circles of wind and weather into the palm resting on my knee, talking and talking in the blind warm close until sleep takes us all together.
