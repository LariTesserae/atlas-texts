There is a corner four hundred yards north-northwest of the stamping mill where two stray realities press against each other like jammed gears and cancel out the rush.

On the left, a submerged cooling stack—sheared off twenty feet from the ground—pulls a localized diagonal gravity toward its rusted lip. On the right, an orphaned condenser bank discharges a vertical wall of brine that stands seven feet tall like clear, quivering masonry before breaking sideways. Between the pull of the stack and the push of the wall, tucked under an overhang of newly minted pitchstone that duplicates its own serrated fringe every time the mill-hammer drops, sits a pocket of dead, motionless soup about the size of a dry-goods crate.

The water inside this pocket doesn’t churn. It sits at a thick, syrupy ninety-nine degrees, suspended in bruised amber gloom, trembling with the sheer tonnage of sound outside without actually moving an inch. Chopped strap-leaf and dead chlorophyll drift into the quiet zone and sink, settling over decades into a yard-deep mattress of warm, sterile green silt.

That is where I would start—not as an animal, because animals have outlines they have to defend, and down here anything that insists too strictly on its own shape spends its whole life fighting the geometry—but as a heavy, metallic film caught at the bottom of that grease-trap.

Think of the run-off. Think of forty-eight Tenders dragging their iron-studded, vulcanized bellies across freshly fractured basalt for five hundred years, weeping linseed-smelling tallow, shedding tiny parings of blackened gristle, leaking thimblefuls of hot, iron-dense blood whenever a duplicated rock-tooth punches through a muscular bladder before clamping shut. That stuff doesn't rot properly; there are no crabs or bristle-worms to digest it, and the ordinary bacterial slimes only want the pulverized sugar of the plants. The heavy fractions—the waxy esters from the skull-canals, the vulcanized sulfur-chains, the microscopic filings of cast-iron worn off locomotive crossheads, the stray megavolt-charred lipids—wash along the floor currents until they hit a dead-zone eddy and drop out of suspension.

I would be what happens when six inches of that heavy, tallow-rich sediment gets shocked forty times an hour by stray ground-arcs from the dynamo tower until it cross-links and wakes up.

Not a slime, exactly. Slimes are loose, snotting away in the first forty-knot shear that shifts its footing. More like a broad, boneless hide of live, leaded felt—dark tarnished-brass in color, heavy as wet buckskin, lying flat across the sharp clinker at the bottom of the eddy. No eyes, no bone prow, no heart to get popped when an octahedral pocket of dry vacuum snaps open six feet away. If a ridge of volcanic glass underneath me glitches, splits, and stamps a brand-new razor edge three inches to the left right through my middle, nothing breaks. My margins just slump down both sides of the new blade like warm wax over a chisel, flow around the fresh crystal facets, and knit seamlessly back together on the far side before the stone has even finished flickering.

For a long time—years, maybe, though there are no seasons in the fever-soup to count them by—being in that corner is purely a matter of weight and hum. 

To live as a sheet of conductive, tallow-fed felt in that basin is to be an ear with no brain behind it. The continuous concussion of the water doesn't hurt when you have no hollow spaces inside you to crush; it feels like warmth, like tension in a loom. Every machine within half a mile writes a standing wave across my surface. The locomotive pistons four hundred yards south draw tiny, parallel corduroy ridges along my western edge; the screaming, redlining turbine by the shale cleft cross-hatches them at thirty degrees; the rhythmic drop of the stamping mill lifts my entire heavy mat half an inch off the black mud and sets me back down—*thump*, pause, *thump*—forcing clean, mineral-hot brine straight through my pores like water squeezed through a fuller’s sponge. I eat what stays behind in the weave: iron-rust, sulfur, torn scraps of boiled plant-skin, and the stray ribbons of blue-white electricity that skate down the basalt fissures and pool in my brassy threads like static in winter wool, twitching my edges with brief, delicious contractions.

If I stayed in the eddy, I’d just grow thicker—an inert cushion of warm felt slowly filling the crate-sized dead spot until the condenser wall shifted and tore me into rags.

What pulls me out of the corner is the pitch.

Even without a skull, if your whole body is made of conductive grease and iron-rich fiber, a screaming bearing hurts. Not pain the way skin feels a burn—more like an itch that pulls your grain crooked. When a journal runs dry on the condenser bank thirty yards away, the metallic shriek climbs into a jagged, high-frequency stutter that makes the standing waves across my back clash instead of weave. My brassy threads bunch up, knotting tightly along the interference lines, cramping until the localized gravity starts to tilt and my quiet pocket threatens to spill open into the forty-knot flume.

And then a Tender comes past.

I feel it long before it clears the curtain of thrashing bladder-kelp: the heavy, rhythmic *crunch-lock, crunch-lock* of six crampon-tipped pile-driver legs biting into raw clinker, followed by the strange, dense shadow its anvil-prow casts in the acoustic murk—a moving patch of quiet where the sound waves are soaked up by oiled bone. It hauls itself right past the lip of my overhang, dragging a flank bristling with snapped-off obsidian teeth and rusted boiler-bolts. Where its belly brushes a jagged spur of rock, it leaves behind a fresh, warm smear of flank-tallow mixed with dark, simmering blood from a newly riveted puncture.

I creep three feet out of the quiet pocket—extending a flat, heavy tongue of myself flat against the knife-edged floor, staying half an inch below the catastrophic shear of the main current—and fold over that smear.

That is where I find out what I actually am.

The tallow isn't just food; it’s soaked with the residual, chemical memory of that bone prow. Those microscopic wax-canals in a Tender's head aren't sealed off from the rest of its meat; the whole beast is steeped in the resonant geometry of the basin. Absorbing a thick rope of its freshly squeezed grease hits my conductive weave like dropping a magnetized needle onto iron filings. Suddenly the cross-hatched vibrations rippling across my surface aren't just cramps and warmth anymore—they click into distance and direction. I can feel the dry bearing thirty yards up the condenser frame not just as an ugly buzz, but as a *shape*: a scored steel collar chewing sideways into its own housing, opening a hairline fracture in the local horizon two inches wide that hisses with dry, misplaced steam.

And I can feel the Tender climbing toward it—too slow. It’s an old one, forty percent basalt by weight, one of its mid-body chisel-limbs wedged permanently shut around a sheared tie-rod it picked up ten years ago. By the time it hauls its mineralized bulk up the vertical wall of brine to pack plant-lard into that collar, the collar is going to weld itself solid, the horizon-crack is going to tear wide across the condenser bank, and both the beast and my quiet corner are going to get folded inside a quarter-mile of multiplying black glass.

So I don't retreat back into the silt.

You cannot walk across that floor if you are three square yards of boneless, leaded hide, and you certainly cannot swim; lift an edge two inches off the stone and the corkscrewing current will peel you off the basalt like wet wallpaper and whip you through a toroidal blur of uprooted ferns until you’re beaten into green grease. 

Instead, you go through the cracks.

Because nothing wears smooth down here, the basin floor isn't solid pavement—it’s a three-mile honeycomb of razor-edged shrinkage fractures, fresh cooling joints, and duplicated fault-lines an inch wide and six feet deep, packed with hot black muck and thick, rubbery stolons anchoring the flora down into the dark. Down inside the fissures, below the frantic thrashing of the strap-leaves, the water barely moves at all; it just pulses, boiling-hot and pitch-black, smelling of sulfur and crushed roots.

I pour myself edge-first into a hairline split in the pitchstone. 

It’s tight—tight enough to scrape any ordinary skin off the muscle—and every three seconds when the distant stamping mill drops, the rock walls slam faintly inward and duplicate a fresh needle of volcanic glass straight through me. It doesn't matter. I thin out into a ribbon two millimeters thick and twelve feet long, ribboning rapidly through the blind subterranean geometry beneath the jungle roots, using the razor edges themselves as ratchets to haul my weight forward. I follow the ugly, jagged shriek of the dry collar overhead, pulled toward it the way solder gets pulled into a hot seam by capillary draw.

I come oozing up out of a bolt-hole right at the base of the condenser’s cast-iron footplate, ten minutes before the old Tender even reaches the bottom of the wall.

Climbing iron in a forty-knot cross-current when you have no claws means turning your whole belly into suction and friction. I flatten down tighter than paint against the pitted, rust-scabbed casting, letting the diagonal pull of the machine’s warped gravity pin me against the metal while I ripple upward inch by inch, flowing *under* the rivet heads, wrapping around flange bolts, slithering straight into the blinding, white-hot cavitation shroud where the water flashes continuously into pressurized vapor and hammers back into liquid with a machine-gun *crack*.

The heat cooks my outer layer instantly into a tough, blackened crust—just like a Tender’s hide—while my underside stays slick and conductive against the screaming iron.

When I reach the failing collar, I don't scoop dead plant-slime out of the muck to pack it. I don't have pale surgical forceps, and plant-grease washes out in forty seconds anyway.

I slip my leading edge right into the two-millimeter tolerance between the redlining shaft and the scored iron sleeve.

The steel teeth of the collar grab that ribbon of me at two thousand revolutions a minute and drag me in. If I were meat and bone, it would shred me into red mist in a blink. Being cross-linked tallow, iron-felt, and conductive sulfur-wax, getting dragged into that white-hot clearance does something else entirely: under six tons of shearing pressure, without friction wearing my mass away—because wear and time still don't couple properly this close to an engine—my brassy fibers don't grind down into nothing. They *plate* onto the steel. 

I turn into a living babbitt-metal liner, three millimeters thick, wrapping completely around the spinning journal inside the sleeve.

For three seconds the heat is blinding—an incandescent, violet roar through every fiber I have as the unlubricated friction tries to tear my sulfur-chains apart and stray megavolts arc straight across the gap through my flesh. I drink the voltage. My charred outer skin sheds microscopic platelets that slip over one another with zero drag, weeping sleek, self-healing oil directly against the spinning shaft.

The metallic shriek doesn't just drop an octave; it vanishes into a deep, buttery, sub-audible purr.

Outside the housing, the two-inch rip of misplaced, hissing dry air snaps shut with a muffled *thump*. The seven-foot vertical wall of clear brine stops quivering on the edge of collapse and settles into a clean, glassy buttress. Twenty feet down the cast-iron frame, I feel the old, rock-heavy Tender halt on its crampons. It tilts its scarred bone prow left, then right—listening to the sudden, uncanny perfection of the bearing overhead, finding no shriek left to fix—before slowly backing down into the olive-amber murk to go hunt for a louder ache toward the east.

And that is what I am, down here. Not one of the big crawling safes, and not the manic green weeds they wade through, either. 

Once you’ve lined one screaming sleeve and felt that jagged, cramping stutter resolve into a smooth, heavy hum that feeds you constant heat and stray current right off the spinning steel, you don't go back to lying flat in a silt-pocket. You grow. Fed on pure mechanical vibration, dissolved iron, and endless electrical bleed, my bulk thickens inside the condenser housing, sending narrow, brassy-black runners creeping back down the bolt-holes and into the razor-sharp fissures of the basalt floor—not leaving the first machine, just stretching out from it through the rock cracks toward the next ugly pitch four hundred yards off.

Call me the **Packing**, or the **Shim-felt**, though nobody is down here to pry a casing open and scrape me off a shaft with a putty knife. 

You would find me anywhere two pieces of orphaned iron are trying to tear local physics apart for lack of a washer. Pry off the inspection plate on that old dynamo tower where the ancient, half-stone Tender lies wedged against the main gear as a living bushing, and look closely at the dark seam where its grooved, vulcanized flank presses against the cast-iron wall—and at the blind bone prow resting against the main shaft. You’ll see a thin, gleaming, tarnished-brass membrane crept neatly up out of the floor-cracks and spread between the old beast’s exhausted hide and the forty-inch steel teeth. 

I take the shear off its torn gristle; I siphon the excess megavolts out of the housing before they char its slow, cartilage-plated heart; and in return, I drink the thick, linseed-scented tallow weeping steadily off its flanks, listening through its resting skull right alongside my own vast, subterranean web of threads—feeling the whole three-mile pan of bruised amber water heave overhead, holding the clearances tight to a thousandth of an inch so the floor stays flat and the dark keeps running true.
