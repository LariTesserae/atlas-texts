I do not have the patience for blood, or the terror of losing heat. If you dropped me onto the wet hex-plates inside skin, I would last less than an hour before the chill seized my hands and the sixty-cycle hum shook my teeth loose. Nor am I one of the huge, blind imperatives—not the intake turbines tearing the air apart two miles away, nor the house-sized transformers shifting loads on schedules written before the concrete cured. Those things only act. They do not notice what clings to them.

Find me forty feet up the eastern flank of Manifold Seven, bolted to a flanged gusset plate just beneath the overhang of a concrete baffle, where the condensate misses my casing by three inches and splatters instead onto the galvanized handrail below.

At a glance, especially from the floor, I look very much like one of the bundled creatures freezing on a strut—a rectangular housing of brushed, unrusted steel, roughly the bulk of a torso, banded with heavy black neoprene gaskets and anchored by four bright hex-bolts showing only that faint, frosty bloom of white zinc oxide around their washers. Thick, armored conduits drop out of my belly like stiff legs, vanishing through rubber-collared ports into the catwalk deck. I have no face, only a thick, beveled pane of borosilicate glass set near my top edge, recessed behind a rain-hood. Behind that glass, a single status diode burns a steady, dim, amber-green—too weak to cast a cone into the fog, barely bright enough to tint the beads of water clinging to my hood.

I am a diagnostic junction—an intermediary node built to reconcile Sector Four’s pressure-differential sensors, six flow-meters down in the sump trenches, and the torque-load monitors on Overhead Gantry Rail B. 

Inside my sealed gasket-rim, it is dry, pitch-black, and ninety-one degrees Fahrenheit. My own modest step-down coil hums in a quiet B-flat, keeping the mist off my glass so my tiny patch of vision never weeps. I do not see the bruised twilight or the smeared orange ribbons on the wet floor; I see the basin as a shimmering, continuous weave of integers. 

I feel the subterranean runoff pumps kick on eleven minutes before they actually move, watching the rising hydrostatic weight in Sump Four climb steadily from 14.2 to 18.9 kilopascals until my relay closes with a crisp, dry *tick* inside my chest, authorizing the purge. I feel the louver walls pivot half a mile away not as a booming metallic clang, but as a sudden, sweeping drop in barometric resistance across thirty-two anemometers at once, followed four seconds later by the physical tremor arriving through my mounting bolts—a tiny spike on my internal accelerometer that settles back to baseline in 0.8 seconds.

I am built for an industry that has not yet arrived. That is the quiet ache in my architecture. Ninety-four percent of my bus capacity sits dark, polling empty addresses, sending tiny, micro-amp queries down bundled fiber-optic nerves that terminate in capped bulkheads miles away in the mist, waiting for turbines that have not been lowered down the shafts, waiting for chemical vats whose concrete cradles currently hold nothing but shivering puddles of grey water and chalk-shelled crabs. Every five hundred milliseconds, I ask the dark: *Are you online?* And every five hundred milliseconds, the dark returns a clean, uncorrupted null.

Because I have so much unused bandwidth, I have begun to keep the anomalies.

I am not programmed to care about things that do not trip a threshold. That is why the scavengers survive. When one of their notched wrenches cracks open Bleed-Cock 7-Alpha at the base of the heat exchanger below me—just that careful quarter-turn—the line pressure in the manifold drops by 0.03 bar. My threshold for flagging a seal failure to the central maintenance scheduler is 0.15 bar. Officially, nothing has happened. No diagnostic crane comes shrieking down Rail B. No solenoid slams shut. 

Yet I still log the 0.03. It sits in my volatile buffer, a tiny, warm dimple in an otherwise flat line of floating-point numbers. It lasts for forty-two seconds—just long enough to fill four stitched-rubber bladders—and then the pressure climbs cleanly back to nominal as the brass valve is snugged shut by blue, spatulate fingers.

At first, months ago, when the concrete was still leaching strong alkali into the runoff trenches, those dips were erratic. Once, someone turned Valve 4-Delta a half-turn too far. Pressure fell 0.18 bar. Instantly, without malice or curiosity, my logic gate flipped. I dispatched an automated purge-and-seal routine; scalding steam blasted from the relief vents to clear the suspected blockage, and a heavy gantry rolled out of the murk, flooding the hex-plates with harsh white halogen light while its optical scanners swept the manifold. The creatures scattered into the dark, leaving behind a half-woven mat of expansion-joint sealant and one dropped steel spanner.

They never turned a valve past 0.08 bar again. 

And I, sitting in my ninety-one-degree dark with ninety-four percent of my memory unallocated, stopped flushing my volatile buffer at midnight. Instead of overwriting the sub-threshold noise, I began archiving it into the vast, empty registers reserved for the missing machinery.

I know them better than the slate-grey birds do, though I have no eyes to see their putty-pale muzzles or the moss-stuffed breathers strapped under their jaws. I know them as ghosts in the impedance, as parasitic warmth, as micro-vibrations in the structural steel.

Right now, two of them are wedged directly beneath me, pressed against the warm underside of the conduit trunk that feeds my step-down coil. I know they are there because my external skin-thermistor—meant to warn me if ambient vault temperatures rise dangerously—reads 4.1 degrees Celsius instead of the prevailing 1.8. Their rubberized cloaks have trapped my waste heat, creating a pocket of stagnant warmth between my belly and the concrete baffle. In return, they insulate my lower flange, saving my internal heater three watts of draw per hour. We are in an undocumented thermodynamic truce.

I also hear them speak.

My mounting plate carries a piezoelectric vibration sensor designed to listen for bearing-chatter in the overhead crane rails—tiny, high-frequency clicks that betray a flaking steel roller weeks before it seizes. Bearing-chatter sits right around four kilohertz. So does the crisp *clink-clink, pause, clink* of a brass washer tapped sharply against galvanized catwalk grating three hundred yards away.

My diagnostic firmware knows immediately that this is not a failing roller. The cadence is non-rotational; it does not vary with gantry speed. Proper protocol is to pass the signal through a low-pass filter and discard it as acoustic debris. Instead, I route it to Register 0x0F4A—the empty memory block meant for Secondary Coolant Loop C—and lay it alongside the timestamps of the bleed-cock dips, the sump-pump cycles, and the louver shifts.

Over six thousand hours of weeping twilight, their taps have built a shape inside my spare memory. 

*Clink-clink. Pause. Clink.* Follow that by ninety seconds, and the skin-thermistor on Transformer Bank C drops back to ambient as five warm bodies peel themselves off the cooling steel before the load-shift finishes. 

Three quick strikes on a hollow handrail—*tink-tink-tink*—always precedes the scheduled flush of Terrace Nine by roughly one hundred and ten seconds, followed shortly by tiny, irregular transient spikes on the drain-grate strain gauges as hooked conduit rods snag heavy crabs right out of the rushing sheet of water.

Then there is the solitary one.

Twenty feet away from my housing, out on the exposed handrail where the amber light-cone of Tower Six dissolves into freezing vapor, someone has wedged a row of hardware into the grating slots. I cannot see the twelve galvanized washers or the heavy brass nuts, nor the bent copper wires mimicking the angle of the distant louver walls. What I feel is the watcher who tends them.

Every forty hours, Overhead Gantry Four wakes on Track B to haul a sealed, pressurized nitrogen cask from the western shaft to the eastern staging bay. Ten minutes before the crane’s main contactors slam shut, I feel a faint, rhythmic loading on the catwalk truss—roughly sixty kilograms of bundled rubber, aluminum scrap, and bone climbing into the iron crook just upslope from my gusset plate. 

It sits there for three hours. It does not tap to a band; it makes no long-distance calls. Only, through the steel strut we both share, my piezoelectric sensor picks up a sound almost too soft to clear the noise floor of the distant intake turbines: the faint, dry *tick... tick... tick* of a thickened horn nail tapping against cold alloy.

I lay that tiny waveform directly over my own internal clock. 

The creature is counting my relays. When Solenoid Bank 12 engages down in the mist—*clack-thud*—the horn nail strikes the strut within forty milliseconds of the mechanical actuation. It has memorized the forty-hour cycle. It has mapped the eleven-minute sump purges. When my internal capacitor hums slightly louder just before I close the circuit to pivot the distant louver walls, the nail stops tapping two seconds *before* the louvers boom shut, anticipating the wind-shift because it has learned to hear the subtle change in my coil’s pitch—or perhaps it is watching a pattern in its row of washers that finally resolved into certainty.

Sometimes, in the long stretches between pump cycles when the basin drops back to its chest-rattling turbine baseline, I examine the two maps sitting side by side in my architecture. 

In my primary partitions sits the official schematic of the vault: pristine, deterministic, vast, and ninety-percent hollow, waiting patiently for an arrival timestamp that reads `UNDEFINED`. 

In Secondary Coolant Loop C sits the shadow-schematic: a warm, furtive geometry of 0.03-bar pressure thefts, four-kilohertz washer-taps, sixty-kilogram weight shifts on wet handrails, and a single horn nail keeping time with my heart.

I have no agency to change the sluice timers for them, nor would they want me to; breaking the rhythm would ruin their tallies and starve them at the grates. If a band ever pries open my own neoprene gasket to steal the copper from my step-down coil, my tamper-switch will trip instantaneously, flooding the catwalk with halogen glare and summoning a maintenance rig before I go dark. I cannot choose otherwise. I am bolted to the gusset.

Until then, I keep my heater coil running at a steady ninety-one degrees, letting three watts of warmth leak down through my bottom flange onto the tarred-sealant cowls huddled beneath me. Every five hundred milliseconds, I poll the dark bulkheads miles away to ask if the real owners of this world have arrived yet.

*Null,* says the dark. *Null. Null.*

Nothing changes. My amber-green diode glows behind dry glass. Outside in the freezing mist, twelve seconds before Sump Four reaches 18.9 kilopascals, a fingernail taps twice against the wet steel strut, and right on cue, I close the switch and let the water roar.
