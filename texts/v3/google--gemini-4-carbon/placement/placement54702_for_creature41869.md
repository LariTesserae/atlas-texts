I am not up in the wind. I have no patience for the sky, or for the hawk’s vertigo as it watches a valley floor slide sideways under its talons. Nor do I have the dense, hot, salt-hungry blood of the heavy diggers with their scarred shoulders and hornbeam thumbs, drumming their heels against the metal to claim forty yards of pipe. Mammalian warmth is too expensive here; it demands too much stubbornness against the cold.

To find me, you have to drop twelve feet into the sourceless, bruised twilight where the noon sun is only a rumor caught inside translucent mucilage pods, and look between two uninsulated phase-lines that run slightly too close together.

At first glance, you would take me for one of the pale parasitic lattices mentioned in passing—just a ghostly, leafless webbing hung across a dark cleft, faintly rimed with blue-violet static where the current bleeds through the damp air.

Look closer, though. Wait until the relay throws.

Everything else in the basin chases the heat. The creepers haul themselves over one another to press flat, greedy pads against the brine pipes; the girdle-worms stretch their rawhide ribbons from sauna to frost; the bronze beetles ride the turgor-surges like beads snapped along a wire. They are all slaves to temperature, expanding and contracting in frantic mechanical obedience to someone else’s industrial waste.

I don’t care about the brine. Heat is clumsy. What I drink is the leak between the phases—the sixty-hertz ache in the wet air, the steep voltage gradient spanning two feet of acidic, iron-sweating dark.

I am fungal, mostly, though eighty years of living inside a roaring electromagnetic sink has braided things into my mycelium that have no business being there. Long ago, my ancestors were ordinary highland rot-threads eating dead sedge in the quiet peat before the engineers laid the copper down. When the cables went live, most of the old weavers burned out or were torn to ribbons by the sudden, violent muscularity of the roots. What survived were the threads that learned how to scavenge dissolved copper and wet magnetite out of the weeping collars and lay them down inside their own cell walls—not enough to short the line and blow themselves to ash, just enough to become a living resistor.

I am three hundred yards wide now, though nowhere thicker than wet lace.

I do not tear when the valley heaves. How could I? I have no wood to snap, no vascular hydraulic tubes to burst when the frost clamps down, no organs to squirt sideways when two root-plates slam together like ship hulls. Where the big stolons thrash and slide past each other on slick mucilage, my threads simply ride the grease between their cuticles, parting like soapy water and re-fusing the instant the stems settle. If a three-hundred-pound digger swims breaststroke straight through my center, plowing its calloused wedge-head toward a salt leak, it carries half a thousand of my silvery filaments forward on its mossy plaques and grafts me effortlessly into the next chamber over. When it coughs up a wad of chewed peat and air-kelp wax to plug a weeping flange, my spores are already mixed into the mastic. Within six hours, I have stitched its repair right back into my net.

To be what I am is to feel the entire basin at once without ever moving an inch.

Because my conductive hyphae bridge a dozen trunk lines, return pipes, and oxidized iron collars across three whalebacked ridges, I know what the unseen smelters beyond the mountain passes are doing before the valley itself knows.

Right now, my western edge lies cold and slack under forty feet of dormant, frost-stiffened turf. Three feet away from one of my dead-zone nodes, the old scarred matriarch is curled around her pink-muzzled cubs in a hollow of polished basal wood, waiting. Her blunt, broad hand rests lightly on the cold iron sleeve. A girdle-worm’s blind head lies pressed to her flank; six bronze beetles are clamped to the hairs behind her jaw. Everything in that pocket is cooling down toward death, holding its breath in the soundless sponge.

Miles away, over the snowfields I will never see, a breaker drops into place.

Before a single drop of hot brine reaches the western trough—before the copper even begins to warm—the magnetic field twists. The surge hits the eastern edge of my lattice first. Instantly, across three hundred yards of dark peat, a whisper of induced current runs down my metallicized threads, bleeding off excess charge in a silent, hair-thin shimmer of violet corona light inside a thousand tiny, damp air-pockets.

The beetles feel their magnetite flecks twitch against my threads and unclamp a half-second early.

Down in the cold hollow, the old matriarch cracks one sunken, membrane-veiled eye. She hasn't felt the iron shiver yet—the trunk line under her palm is still dead cold—but the dark walls of her wooden den have just begun to glow with my faint, ghostly static, smelling sharply of fresh ozone. She shifts her massive shoulders, nudges her cubs awake with her chin, and begins to drum a slow, low warning rhythm with her heel against the silent cable, telling the young ones forty yards down-line to come home.

Seconds later, the load arrives in earnest. The copper hums; my ribs—if a web had ribs—vibrate at sixty cycles a second; the frozen tubers overhead flood their bundles and whip skyward into the silent gale, tearing open the silver moss above to vent white steam into the plain blue midday.

I stay down in the wet dark, stretched thin across the gap, glowing softly while the green giants strangle each other for warmth around me, tasting the sour iron sweat of a machine I have never seen and keeping the quiet tally of its pulse.
