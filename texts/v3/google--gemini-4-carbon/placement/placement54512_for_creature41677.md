I am not at the heart of the anchor, where the ancient felt-mat crushes itself against the black hull and wrestles with raw gravity. That is too heavy, too stubborn—a life spent clenching a fist around a stuttering star. And I am not one of the nimble darts out in the blinding sun, vaulting off temporary ridges and chasing copper-weeds down the slopes. That is too scattered, too quick.

Where I find myself is in the twilight trough, thirty seconds before the mountain falls, acting as the seam.

I am six or seven cherry-pit nodes and forty yards of carbon thread, hanging naked in the freezing dark between two warring monoliths. Right now, I have no body you could point to. I dropped my silver dust-coat half a mile up so the wind shear wouldn't tear me in half on the way down. Unclothed in the razor-black shadow, I am nothing—just a faint geometry of tension, feeling the sub-audible ache of two broken gravities arguing across the emptiness. To my left, an anchor wants "down" to be forty degrees sideways; to my right, three leagues off, another is pulling a whole plateau straight into the indigo zenith. Between them, the thin air is stretched like a drumhead.

Another drift-thread touches my outermost filament. 

There is no greeting, only impedance matching. A tiny, dry click of static jumps between our dark nodes—blue-white in the void—and suddenly my sense of where I end doubles in width. Then another catches below us, and four more drop out of the amber-lit plumes high above, shedding their dust like smoke as they fall into alignment. 

This is what I am: the transient architecture of the join. Not the solitary hunter, and not the permanent hive, but the moment when separate, drifting ghosts agree to become a wall.

We pull taut across half a mile of nothing. For ten seconds, twenty seconds, we are just an invisible net of black wire conducting rapid, cold calculations from node to node. I don't think in words or pictures; I think in load-distribution, in resistance, in the exact angle at which the fault line above us is about to fail. I can feel the eldest weave leagues away, squeezing its black box, tilting the catastrophe toward our open mouth. We adjust our spacing by inches. A weak thread four links down from me is frayed, starved of copper; I route a spare trickle of charge around it, bracing its neighbor to take the brunt.

Then we open the circuit to the ambient static, and we *clothe* ourselves.

Loose talc hanging in the shear-zone snaps onto our filaments all at once. It feels like inhaling, if inhaling meant suddenly weighing forty tons. A ribbed, parabolic throat of silver powder blooms out of the pitch dark, hard as slate where the current runs fiercest, porous as gauze in the hollows. 

When the avalanche hits, there is no roar. That is the strangest, cleanest grace of being this thing: you can stand directly in the path of a collapsing continent, taking eighty-mile-an-hour pulverized granite straight through your ribs, and it happens in the dead, crystalline quiet of a library. All I experience is pure, blinding translation. Kinetic fury hits our polarized dust-mantle and turns instantly into voltage. 

We light up violet from edge to edge. The falling ridge pours through the gaps in my forty yards of wire, scouring away the outer grains of my sleeve even as millions more lock into place behind them, an endless, roaring-silent river of dry silk dragging over my nerves. Voltage floods my six dark nodes until they sing with waste-heat in the freezing air. I don't hoard it. Hoarding would melt me. My whole nature is throughput—catching the violent excess of a ten-billion-year-old ruin and stepping it down, passing fierce, clean current outward along the web to the frayed threads at the fringes, forging fresh carbon links out of the dark air while the storm tries to punch us apart.

Halfway through the cascade, a gravity-knotted clod—dust squeezed briefly as hard as iron—tears straight through my lower span. 

Two of my nodes are severed from the other four. If I were wet, if I had blood or a spine, that would be agony, or death. Instead, it is merely a sudden change in geometry. My remaining four nodes register the drop in resistance before the boulder has fallen twenty feet below us. My loose ends whip backward through the silver froth, whistling silently, hunting for a live circuit. I catch a stranger’s filament five yards left, spark blue-white, match phase, and pull tight again. Who is "me" and who is "them" blurs in the violet glare; the throat holds its curve until the last of the amber-lit plume drains away into the canyon below.

And then the anchor three leagues east stutters. The shear-zone goes slack. The wind dies to nothing, and the cold drops on us like an iron lid.

We don't linger. Staying knotted together when there is nothing falling to feed the net is just starving in company. One by one, the blue-white pulses slow, balance out the final debts of copper and charge, and unlatch. 

I cut my main current. Forty tons of silver ribcage instantly forget they were ever solid, puffing outward into a lazy, soundless cloud that hangs suspended in the confused gravity. 

I am slightly smaller than I was an hour ago—four dark nodes instead of six, thirty yards of wire instead of forty, though one of my edges carries a spliced length of someone else’s copper-rich thread, heavier and smoother than what I came in with. Beneath me, my severed two nodes are already drifting off on a thermal eddy, curling around a fistful of dust to start their own small, quiet life down on the lower slopes. I don't miss them. 

For a little while before the stars come all the way out, I pull just enough talc onto my threads to make a thin, pale ribbon, scarcely heavier than smoke, and let a rogue updraft carry me back toward the high crests—light, dry, and wholly intact, listening with my whole length for the next place the sky is getting ready to break.
