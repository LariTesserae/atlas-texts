I would not be out on the eastern pan-works where the sunlight skins the bismuth, and I would not be down in the scorching twilight of the slots with a brass rod clamped in my teeth, though I know the smell of the Under-Walkers’ suits when they come up through the floor-locks—that sharp, singed stink of hot brine on tin and wet asbestos that makes everyone on the lowest tier shift their knees out of the way to let them pass to the wash-troughs.

Find me three wards in from the Great North Ratchet, mid-stack, where the curvature of the octagonal wall is interrupted by a heavy cast-iron alcove bolted straight to the primary load-column.

My bench is cast slag, polished slick on the front lip by three generations of quilted bellies leaning against it. Above my head, the platinum wire passes down through its greased brass grommet from the chemist’s flat upstairs and disappears between my feet toward the yeast-cellars, humming a note too low for the ear to catch, though if I lay my thumb-knuckle lightly against the grommet-plate while waiting for a kettle to heat, I can feel the whole stack leaning against the windless afternoon.

I keep the ward’s Book of Creep, and with it, the torsion-ledgers for nineteen buildings linked along the Fourth Gabbro Spine.

People think of a ledger as paper, or lowlander parchment that curls up and cracks to powder in a week if you bring it out of the wet-air rooms. Ours are thin leaves of rolled, anodized aluminum hinged on brass pins, five inches by eight, interleaved with sheets of oiled mineral-tissue so they don’t chime together when you turn them. You do not write on them with ink. Ink dries in the nib or runs in the sweat. You score them with a carbide-tipped stylus held steady in the fist—thumb locked high over the forefinger, using the shoulder rather than the wrist—raising a tiny, clean burr of bright metal through the dark oxide skin. You can read an old ledger in the pitch dark if the zinc-jars fail, just by running the pad of your middle finger across the sixteenths.

My left forearm is smoother than my right on the underside, hairless from rubbing across aluminum leaves for forty years, and the dark skin over my wrist-bone has that pale, calloused sheen of polished horn. My heart beats twenty-eight times a minute when I am sitting still—two beats slower than my sister’s, which she still complains makes me lazy at the evening stew-crock, though it means when I have to go out on the high catwalks to verify a rogue gauge for myself, a single charge of damp grey peroxide in my back-pack lasts me four hours without bringing on the behind-the-eyes hammer.

I am sixty-two, which up here makes me dry-jointed enough to claim a middle-high hammock near the return-brine jacket, though not yet high enough up the wall to sit with the grandmothers shelling beetles. My braids are mostly iron-grey now, greased every fifth night with rendered lanolin and a pinch of ground plumbago so they lie flat and heavy behind my ears like short lengths of braided cable. Around my neck, outside my vest, hangs no lunar astrolabe—I never had the patience for decorative gears—only a thumb-sized plummet of pure, soft lead that belonged to my mother’s uncle, bruised with a hundred tiny teeth-marks from decades of men and women biting down on it when an earlock vented too fast or a scalded knuckle needed setting without shouting.

All morning, the figures come into my alcove through the speaking-tubes and the slate-runners.

A twelve-year-old in her first Masked Apprenticeship cycles in through the street-lock two levels down, weathers the pop of the overhead pressure-tank, and climbs the spiral rungs at the regulation pace—*step, step, hand-to-flange, breathe; step, step, hand-to-flange, breathe*—until her head rises through the deck-hatch beside my knees. She doesn’t speak; her throat is dry from two hours in the scrubber-rig, and we have shift-sleepers behind the ochre curtain six feet to my left. She just unhooks four wet slates from her belt-ring, lays them beside my elbow, and flicks three quick angles with her bare, stained fingers before taking a dipper of cool, alum-sharp distillate from the stoneware jar by the wall: *Shoe Eleven weeping grease; two fin-backs piled on the west transformer; pan-track seven binding on the return.*

I nod once, tap two knuckles on the slag bench—*logged and weighted*—and wait until she descends before sliding my brass parallel-rule across the open aluminum leaf.

What I am actually doing, hour after hour under the pale yellow steady-light of the zinc jar, is listening to a thing five miles thick try to turn over in its sleep without dropping us out of our cradles.

Nobody else sees the whole shape of the Spine at once. The Under-Walkers down in the hundred-and-thirty-degree dark know Shoe Four and Shoe Five intimately; they know the particular sourness of the steam coming out of the diagonal split that opened behind the copper-works two winters ago, and they know by the pitch of their brass jaw-rods that the black trachyte under the salt-house is still glassy and tight, holding tension like an over-wound spring rather than easing outward cleanly like honest gabbro. Up on the tension-bridge, the ratchet-wardens know only that Drawbar Three pulled four teeth since last cross-quarter instead of the usual two-and-a-half, giving us twelve extra tons of Head in the drop-shafts—good news for the extruders, they think, rubbing their thick thumbs together over the surplus torque.

Only here, when I plot their sixteenths side by side on the wide master-plate—pricking tiny pinholes into a thin sheet of soft pewter laid over a backlit mica table—does the ghost show itself.

Drawbar Three pulled fast because the east wall of the chasm is rotating, very slightly, counterclockwise. The east wall is rotating because that glassy pocket under the salt-house refuses to yield. Which means all that bottled-up upward shove is walking quietly sideways underneath four residential stacks and a brine-tower, looking for a seam. And two fin-backs have just hauled themselves off a perfectly good warm bus-bar to sit on the west transformer housing, sixty yards right along that exact line of strike.

I sit back against the curved cream tiles of my alcove, letting the carbide stylus rest in the groove of my thumb. Somewhere behind the wall at my shoulder, a heavy lead pipe gives a single, thick *glug* as a pocket of denser mother-liquor rolls past toward the settling vats.

I love the tiles of my corner. The glaze on the three courses level with my eyes came out of Batch Nine-Fourteen when this stack was raised two tiers higher in my grandfather’s prime—slightly speckled with iron-fleck, warm cream shading to toast-brown at the beveled edges. Somewhere in that smooth, vitrified skin is a pinch of calcium from the old lathe-woman who taught my father’s mother how to true a spirit-level, and a pinch from her brother who lost his footing on an unrailed condenser roof before they learned to hinge the ceramic boot-soles properly. When the mineral-wick flares slightly in a draft from the inner hatch, the wall doesn’t just reflect light; it holds it a fraction of a second deep, like oiled bone. You never feel alone in an octagonal room, even at mid-shift when eighteen of your cell-mates are out on the pans or down in the blind-crop cellars. You are sitting inside your people, and your people are keeping the air at two-thirds sea-level around your ribs.

Around mid-afternoon—I know the hour without looking up because the salt-wax inside the exterior partition-bricks hits full melt right about then, changing the dull *thrum* of the building’s frame into a softer, muffled deadness—I put away the aluminum book, stand up slowly so the thick blood doesn't pool in my bowed calves, and go out to look at the fin-backs myself.

Putting on street-rig at sixty-two is an old marriage: no thought required, every buckle worn smooth to fit its particular strap-notch. Quilted trousers tucked into the hinged ceramic boots. Mineral-felt apron hung from the broad shelf of the shoulders. The brass lung-pack settled into the small of the back—cold for thirty seconds until body-heat strikes through the vest—and then the spun-copper mask brought up over the bridge of the nose, leather straps tugged snug behind the greased braids. You take one shallow test-breath with a thumb over the intake valve until the mica eye-lenses pull inward a hair; good seal. Then you step into the iron cylinder of the lock, dog the inner wheel behind you, and pull the bleed-chain.

The dense, warm smell of pickled tubers, lanolin, and twenty sleeping blankets vanishes out the recapture-vent with a sharp *whuff*. Your eardrums bow outward—I shift my jaw sideways once, *click*, to clear them—and push the heavy outer hatch into the saddle’s afternoon.

Even through the mica lenses and the chemical coolness of the peroxide-salts hissing gently at the base of my spine—*shhh, two, three, four, shhh*—the outer world hits like stepping onto an anvil set near a furnace door.

The silence first. Inside the ward, you live inside a soup of tiny, resonant hums conducted through iron and tile. Out here on the third-tier landing, twenty paces from anything moving, the thinness of the air shears off every noise at the knees. Across the narrow slot, forty feet away, two foundry-riggers are swinging a three-ton ceramic retort on a chain-block. I can see the links inching over the greased sheave; I can see the heat-shimmer wavering off the retort’s belly against the flat, terrifying cobalt of the sky above the horn-peaks; and there is nothing. Not a creak, not a clink. Just my own *shhh-tick* behind my ribs, my hinged ceramic soles clicking *clack-clack* on the damp cast-slag grating, and, three feet from my shoulder, a drop of warm condensate detaching from a lead flange to hit a crust of yellow alum with a tiny, wet *pat*.

I walk the seventy yards to the west transformer yard at the saddle-gait, rolling slightly from hip to hip, never lifting a boot higher than an inch off the grate, touching a warm rivet-head with two gloved fingers whenever the walkway steps down over a bundled pipe-truss. Down between the gaps in the perforated steel beneath my soles, eighty feet straight down through tangles of cross-bracing and sweating conduits, I can catch glimpses of the native stone: black as stove-polish, edged like broken bottles, shimmering with dry sulfur-heat.

The two fin-backs are right where the apprentice signed them, wedged awkwardly between the brick transformer housing and a copper return-bus, six blunt scaly legs apiece tucked partway in, corrugated pewter shells overlapping by an inch.

I stop four paces off—close enough that if one of them were a human, it could hear me speak—and slowly squat down on my heels, resting my gloved forearms across my knees. You never crowd a fin-back, and you don't tap its shell. Their whole body is an ear for ground-current and deep rock-strain; rapping iron on their fins is like shouting into a man’s ear-trumpet.

The larger one is an old female—we call them all *she* when they pass four feet across, though only the gut-cutters who unbolt the dead ones know for sure—with `W-4 / 812` cold-punched cleanly into her left front marginal scute. Eighty-eight years since she was stamped big enough to hold a punch, which makes her older than the ratchet-bridge at the end of the alley. Thick, velvety crusts of blue-green copper sulfate have grown into the deep cooling-fins along her dorsal ridge, cutting down her heat-dumping surface.

Unhooking the short brass scraper from my apron-ring, I lean forward without shifting my boots and reach out very slowly until the flat blade touches the valley between two dorsal fins.

For ten breaths, nothing happens. Then, at the speed of an hour-hand, her left foreleg slides half an inch wider on the slag paving, bracing, and she tilts her heavy, mineral-encrusted carapace three degrees toward my hand.

*Scrape. Pause. Scrape.*

Flakes of pale green and white salt snow down onto the warm copper bus-bar, fizzing faintly where they hit a film of dampness. Through the brass handle of the scraper, even through my quilted gauntlet, I can feel the strange, dense warmth of her—hotter than blood, steady as a jacketed pipe, powered by dark sulfur-broth churning silently in her belly-chambers. And beneath that, fainter still, I feel why she climbed off the eastern terrace.

The copper bus-bar under her plastron isn't carrying stray current from the electrolytic vats. It’s picking up piezo-hum straight up through its grounding-straps from that locked pocket of black trachyte under the salt-house. The stone down there is squeezing itself hard enough to bleed weak voltage into the city’s iron shoes, and the old girl’s belly-bacteria don't like the taste of it over on the east side where the shear is sharpest. Over here on the west return, the ground-potential is flat. She isn't fleeing heat; she’s sitting in the electrical shadow of the break before the break happens.

I clean four more grooves along her flank until dull, pewter-bright horn shows through, tap the flat of my glove once, very lightly, against her stamped scute—an old citizen thanking an older one—and rise slowly back to my feet.

Up above the narrow slot of the yard, past five overlapping tiers of black iron eaves and glistening lead pipe-trusses, a single bone-sparred glider crosses the strip of dark cobalt sky. It doesn't tip a wing. High up in that freezing, invisible chimney of rising foundry-air, ten feet of taut, brown-veined membrane slides from west to east without a tremor, sunlit almost to amber from underneath by the tremendous, blinding bounce-glare coming off the unseen cloud-sea miles below the peaks. My father’s knuckles are up there on the east horn-crag since last dry-quarter, drying neatly in their iron cradle; maybe part of him is in that left wing-spar right now, looking down into our steaming trough and seeing all three miles of us crept together like barnacles in a warm boiler-seam.

Before the evening thermal snap begins—before the sun drops like a white coin into the distant milk of the Rot-Walkers’ roof and sets ten thousand cooling-louvers pattering across the wards—I will have walked back through the lock, stripped off my copper face, drunk two cups of warm yeast-broth, and cut a yellow-priority billet on a strip of bright tin for the Under-Walkers’ guild-master.

Tomorrow morning at first light, four figures in mirrored tin and chilled-brine hoses will go down into the slot beneath the salt-house with worm-jacks and forty-inch expansion-bellows. They will bleed two tons of stored torque off the North Ratchet, hook a torsion-cable onto Shoe Six before the glassy stone underneath decides to snap on its own schedule, and spend the next nineteen days easing four hundred tons of ceramic brick and sleeping families an eighth of an inch to the northwest.

Nobody upstairs will spill a drop of soup. The blind mud-snails down in the cellar-troughs won't pause in their grazing. Only the hair-thin platinum wire passing down beside my slag bench will drift, grain by grain through its clear bowl of oil, back toward the center of the etched spiderweb, and I will sit on my warm corner bench with my carbide stylus locked in my thumb, scoring one clean sixteenth after another into the bright skin of the metal, keeping count of how far the ground has carried us toward the sky.
