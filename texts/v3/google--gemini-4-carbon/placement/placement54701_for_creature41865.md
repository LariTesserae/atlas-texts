At first, looking down over the dry caprock rim, you might look for yourself in the crooked-ankled man or his aunt—in the deep relief of warm water loosening stiff joints, or the quiet craft of turning harsh mountain bark into supple cord inside a pocket of earned silence. There is an immediate comfort in that ten-foot eastern niche. 

Look closer, though, at how they use it. Notice what happens when the man reaches down before the siphon roars, lifting his tools without breaking his sentence. Notice the way their voices drop into that flat, hummed chest-register, sliding between the sharp *thwack* of the high leaves and the deep *chug* of the plumbing underneath.

I am not one of the two sitting with their backs to the warm limestone. And I am not one of the blind, willow-pale eels whipping sideways into a pocket in the dark, nor a marl-slug shaving the yellow cells off the base of an emerald dome. Those creatures belong entirely to the blind mechanics of the mountain, eating and clinging without ever knowing there is an outside.

Where I find myself is three inches to the left, twenty feet up the eastern overhang.

I am the pale, bruised patch of limestone pecked rough by a greenstone maul forty summers ago. Or rather, I am the dent itself—the missing three inches of grey rind, and the pocket of altered air that hangs beneath it.

Before the uncle hung from his slung plank with water hammering the crown of his head, the court was pure, deafening momentum. Drop, strike, boom; fill, prime, roar. Everything in it was glorious and sealed tight inside its own huge rhythm, wholly indifferent to anything with ears or language. A person could step into it, marvel for three minutes, feel the slaps in their teeth, and climb right back out, unchanged except for wet feet. 

What the maul left behind wasn't a house, or a wall, or any kind of monument. If you didn't know to look up through the glare and the falling plums of water, you would never see the pale scars on the drip-lip at all; warm mineral glaze has already half-healed them over with a millimeter of slick travertine. All I do is catch the seeping ground-sweat as it gathers along the bedding plane and persuade it, drop by drop, to let go a palm’s-breadth early.

I turn *crack* into *tup*.

That is my whole existence in the basin: a tiny, stubborn deflection in an overwhelming noise. I don't stop the water, and I don't quiet the court. Ten paces out, the great saddle-leather canopies still boom like struck rawhide, shaking the air until thought goes blank. The siphon still clears its throat through the blowholes on its twenty-eight-minute clock, flooding the stone below me and sucking away the brown bark-chaff. I belong completely to the rock and the seep; all winter long, while snow boils into chalky steam overhead and the bighorn ewes crane their necks through the fog, I am nothing more than a slightly rougher lip on a sweating wall, steering warm drops onto the soft, curled edge of a leaf when nobody is there to hear the difference.

Yet because the drop hits the curled margin instead of the taut hollow rib, a ten-foot bubble of air opens up underneath me where two minds can meet without screaming.

From up on the overhang, if a shape in the rock can be said to listen, I know everything that passes through that quieted corner. I hold the space where the sharp-edged chert goes *shhhk, shhhk* down the caramel ribbons of larch bast. I hold the hummed, rib-still sentences that nobody down by the pine-pitch fires is ever allowed to hear—the unguarded plainness, the old swallowed grudges set down briefly on the wet stone to air out, the silent, shoulder-shaking laughter over a pompous cousin, the long hours where nobody speaks at all because the *tup-tup-tup* below me is steady enough to think beside. 

I am slowly disappearing, and that feels right too. The mountain’s warm, mineral-sweet groundwater never stops sheeting over me. Every day, in the thin, tepid glaze that feels like the flank of a resting animal, another microscopic ghost of dissolved lime settles into the rough bruises the greenstone maul made. Year by year, smooth grey stone is growing back over the pale scars, nudging the drip-point a hair's breadth back toward the right. 

In another thirty or forty summers—maybe around the time that split seed-pod near the southern wall has raised its own wrist-thick stalks twelve feet into the sun—my lip will have rounded itself smooth again. The falling plums of water will creep back off the curled leaf-margin, find the tight, air-filled rib once more, and *boom*: the thunder will close seamlessly over the eastern corner as if it had never been parted.

Until then—or until someone not yet born slings another plank down from the horn-coral seam with a hammerstone in their teeth, squinting up through the spray to bruise me back into place—I stay right on the edge of the overhang. Wet, warm, barely noticeable, catching the heavy mountain water and setting it down gently, just far enough to the side that two people squatting in the din below can watch each other's mouths and speak.
