I would not be up in the cleft, and I could never fit inside the stone. You cannot put a second heartbeat in that pocket without breaking the water, and I have no wish to tear those quiet curtains down. 

Instead, find me three thousand feet below the ridge, where the frost-shattered scree finally loses its momentum and spills out into the high, wind-burned turf of the timberline. There is a low stone hut here—more cellar than house, dug sideways into a moraine bank and roofed with Overlapping slabs of gray schist weighted down against the gales with river boulders. Inside, it smells of damp wool, cold mutton tallow, dried bog-myrtle, and a tiny, stingy fire of twisted juniper roots that gives more acrid blue smoke than flame.

I am the one who stays at the threshold so the watcher can afford to become a creature of the dark.

Look at my hands first: they are the exact inverse of those soft, marmot-gloved instruments up on the mountain. Mine are broad, scarred, and horned with yellow callus across the palms, the fingernails split and blackened from prying slate and digging roots out of half-frozen gravel. My knuckles are swollen from forty winters of cold creek water. If I touched that sedge-cord in the dark, I wouldn't feel the double-overhand knot until my clumsy wrist had already sheared off three fans of aragonite needles and bled into the pure sumps. I am too wide in the beam, too loud in the lungs, altogether too rough for the interior of the earth. My eyes squint too, not from disuse, but from scanning white glare on the upper chutes for four days running, waiting for a small, dark speck to detach itself from the talus and come limping down toward the grass.

You could call me a keeper, though down in the valley terraces they call my post *the hinge*. Every watcher requires one. Someone has to stand between the noisy, demanding valley—with its nervous headmen asking whether to shore up the irrigation flumes or drive the sheep off the southern fumaroles—and a person who has just spent eighty hours dissolved in mouth-warm mineral vapor until they have nearly forgotten the mechanics of speech.

Right now, I am kneeling by the hearth stones, preparing for the return. 

There is a round-bottomed pot of black clay resting in the warm ash—not boiling, never boiling, because boiling broth would scald a tongue that has tasted nothing for four days except the faint electricity of copper-water and wet chalk. In the pot is rendered marmot fat whisked slowly into warm snowmelt with a pinch of crushed, roasted dock-seed, held at the exact temperature of blood. Beside it lies a clean sheepskin, fleece-side in, aired out for two days to rid it of woodsmoke, rubbed soft with brain-tan and unscented grease so it won’t rasp skin that has gone tender as mushroom flesh in the saturated air.

When the scraping step finally sounds against the schist lintel, I do not look up, and I do not speak. Speech out here has too much wind in it; the consonants strike like flint chips after days of listening to *tik* and *plup* eleven minutes apart. 

I reach up and pull the heavy hide curtain across the low doorway, plunging the hut into a dim, smoky russet gloom, shutting out the terrible, shouting blue of the alpine sky. Only then does the watcher crawl in, shivering so hard their teeth click together like pebbles in a gourd, stinking faintly of cold sweat, stiffened leather, and underneath that—when I help peel back the greased hides—something utterly odorless and clean, like boiled stone.

Look at how we move around each other in the small space. I am heavy, warm in the brisk, dry way of wool and blood; they are cold on the outside and curiously slack, moving with slow, underwater economy, knees automatically drawing toward the chest as they curl onto the fleece by the dead ashes. Their eyes are shut tight, leaking thin tears from the snow-glare they just crossed. Immediately, out of habit, the oiled mittens come off, and one pale, broad-tipped thumb reaches out—not toward the fire, not toward food, but to find the familiar chipped rim of my wooden bowl, mapping where they are by a tiny split in the alder wood I left unsmoothed ten years ago for just that reason.

I put the pot between their wrists. We sit for an hour in silence while the wind booms harmlessly over the sod roof.

I am not just the cook and the shelter-wall, though. Look closer at the shelf tucked under the eaves behind my shoulder.

Stacked carefully in dry birch-bark cylinders are dozens of scraped sheep-ribs, pale and greased against the mountain damp, some yellowed to the color of old teeth. Beside them sit balls of raw mountain sedge soaking in a stone basin, and a collection of small, thumb-pinched clay cups—rejects and spares, fifty of them—that I kneaded myself from gray glacial silt and pounded mica down by the tarn. 

I am the maker of the replacements, though I have never seen the room where they go.

Every few years, a cord stretches slightly under its own damp weight, or a clay cup develops a hairline craze from some ancient flaw in the firing that lets its meniscus seep away two minutes too fast. I am the one who twists the new two-ply sedge line across my rough thigh, pulling it to a tension I cannot judge for myself, handing it across the dark hut for those extraordinary, naked fingertips to draw lightly through their crease. They don't say *tighter* or *looser*. They simply drop the cord if the twist is uneven, or hold it still between thumb and forefinger when the lay of the fiber matches the invisible geometry in their head. When I pinch out seven eggshell thimbles and fire them in smothered brushwood outside, the watcher sits indoors in the dark after they have cooled, tapping each rim lightly with a fingernail, listening next to their right ear until they find the single one that gives the exact, dead *tik* of the high mica-slot, and pushes the other six aside for me to crush back into grog.

And after the broth is drunk, when their breathing has adjusted to the thicker oxygen of this lower bench and their shivering stops, comes the translation.

They do not tell me prophecies. A person who lives half their life as an extension of wet porphyry doesn't think in villages or landslides. Instead, lying curled under the fleece with eyes still closed against the faint red glow of the juniper coals, they reach out and take my scarred, thick-skinned wrist.

They tap my pulse-point with two fingers: *one—two—three—pause—one.* The rhythm of the seventh cup against the fourth. Then they touch the side of their own nose, wrinkling it almost imperceptibly, and press the flat of their tongue against the roof of their mouth—the dry, puckering sign for alum. Or they walk four fingers lightly up my forearm and hold them suspended in the air an inch above my skin: *the harvestmen have left the water-bridges; they are clinging to the dry roots at the ceiling.*

I sit very still, letting my rough arm be their slate. Tomorrow morning, while they sleep a drugged, heavy, nineteen-hour sleep beneath piles of hides to rebuild the fat their body burned climbing down, I will take my staff and walk six miles further down the gorge to the highest terraced barley-fields. I will stand at the edge of the headman’s wall—still smelling of juniper smoke and grease, wholly ordinary, someone the villagers know how to look in the eye—and tell them in plain, loud, valley words: *Clear the lower sheep-pens before the new moon, and brace the eastern sluice. The seventh seam has gone astringent and the spiders are on the roof.*

Sometimes I wonder about the elder who came down for the last time twenty years ago—the one whose spine finally stiffened at the third twist, whose fingertips grew too thick to read a frayed splice. In fact, if you look closely at my left hip when I rise to bank the ashes, you might notice the slight, permanent hitch in my gait, or how unusually narrow my own shoulders are underneath three layers of bulky sheepskin, even though years of carrying firewood and heavy water-buckets have thickened my arms and ruined my hands.

Perhaps the valley doesn't waste an old watcher when the rock finally refuses them. Who else would know never to boil the broth? Who else would know the exact mix of crushed mica and gray silt that drinks no water through its walls, or how quiet a house must be kept when someone crawls out of the sky half-skinned by light?

When the wind dies before dawn, I sometimes sit awake by the cold hearth listening to the snow melt off the schist eaves outside. It drips fast and ragged onto the gravel—*pat, pat-pat, splash, pat*—chaotic, hurried, full of weather and rot and seasons. My ruined thumbs rub slowly across my knees, feeling nothing finer than coarse wool. I do not miss the dark—you cannot afford to miss a place where a single nostalgic sigh would shatter a thousand trembling windows of water—but in the pauses between the gusts, my chest still drops, without my willing it, to nine breaths a minute, keeping time with twelve tiny cups I will never touch again, brimming silently two miles up in the warm, blind heart of the ridge.
