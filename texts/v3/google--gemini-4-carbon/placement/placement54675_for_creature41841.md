I would not be in the glare. 

If you looked for me out on the open pan—among the strobing magenta throats, or the frantic silver mirrors, or even in the stately, opalescent majesty of the three walkers—you would miss me entirely. I do not have the stomach for that relentless, shadowless noon, nor the patience to be a monument. 

Come instead to the third island from the eastern rim, the one with the split crown where a giant, waxy philodendron-analogue leans out over the lukewarm mirror. Follow its swollen emerald stem down past the sweating orchids, past the six-deep clusters of velvet crawlers blinking in the wet heat, down to where the chartreuse moss hangs heavy and drowned in the water. Part the moss. Here, the pinkish-gray bedrock does not flatten out; it drops away into one of the deep, ancient solution pits that anchor the plants. 

The water in the pit is dark with tannins and sunken silt, cool by perhaps half a degree, and packed with a knotted basketwork of pale, fibrous roots. Look into the dimness between two thick taproots, about a forearm’s depth below the surface.

At first, there is only a slight distortion against the pale root-bark—a flattened, muscular ribbon about the length of a human hand, patterned in mottled jade, dull ochre, and bruised violet. I look like a shed leaf decaying in the crotch of a submerged branch, until a pearl-backed crab blunders down out of the blinding light above, bumped loose by a wader’s toe. 

Before the crab settles, two stalks no thicker than sewing needles rise from my forward edge, tipped not with lenses, but with tiny, cup-shaped grilles of feathery nerve-tissue. I do not catch the crab. Let the root-tangles have it. I am only tasting the wake it brought down from the surface—the faint, sharp tang of ozone from the eastern ring’s last cycle, the warm smear of mucilage drifting off the nearest walker’s footpads twenty yards away.

I am a creature of the margins, a flat-bodied creeper of the root-wells and the deep stone seams, built like a cross between a planarian and a velvet-skinned nudibranch. Where the walkers have risen above the stone to conduct the basin’s great, slow symphony, I have pressed myself entirely against it. My belly is a single, broad muscular foot lined with microscopic ridges that hold fast to slick, soapy granite without suction or glue, sliding on a film of water only molecules thick. 

Out in the bright court, everything screams with color because the air will not carry a voice. Down here in the root-dark, light is scarce and tangled, so my kind kept another language—one that slips right underneath the ancient machinery’s dampener.

The black rods and the hovering rings strangle vibrations in the *air*, turning splashes to velvet and clicks to nothing, and they smooth out the chop of the shallow water. They do very little to the dense, unbroken pinkish-gray bedrock itself. 

Pressed flat against the stone wall of the solution pit, my whole underside is an ear. 

Through the continuous, solid curve of the basin’s floor, I feel the world not as a silent painting, but as a low, thrumming drumhead. I feel the dry, stubborn hum of the tilted black rod on the western slope—a cold, crystalline stillness pinned into the rock like a winter thorn. I feel the slow, heavy *thrum-release* of the seven hydraulic pads as the middle walker shifts its weight forty paces away, a soft pneumatic sigh conducted through ancient stone. Closer by, I feel the tiny, frantic ticking of a hundred thousand crab-legs scraping algae off the dome above me, translated through the rock into a faint, dry fizzing against my belly.

Eventually, hunger—or the restless itch that passes for curiosity in a nerve-net shaped like a ladder—draws me up out of the pit.

I do not swim. Swimming in this basin is an invitation to be snatched by a clear-winged lunger or tangled in the blinding panic of the scale-carpet. Instead, I flow upward along the underside of a drooping moss-curtain, staying entirely within its narrow green shadow, and slip out onto the open floor beneath the overlapping platter-pads at the island’s edge.

Here, the living carpet of fingernail-sized bottom-dwellers begins. 

Watch what happens when I meet them. A wader has to tip-toe; a walker has to bribe them with chemicals so they part around its giant feet. I am thinner than a coin at my margins, and just heavy enough not to drift. Instead of walking *on* the scale-carpet or pushing it aside, I slide *under* it. 

Tilting my leading edge down against the soap-smooth stone, I wedge myself gently beneath the lowest layer of translucent organisms. They do not flash silver. To them, I am merely a slow, warm ripple in the stone itself, lifting them half a millimeter as I pass and setting them cleanly back down behind me. 

Now I am out in the twenty-yard expanse between islands, utterly invisible—not because I match the refractive index of the water like the great walkers, but because I am wearing the basin’s floor like a blanket. 

Above me, filtered through two layers of living, golden-tinted scales and ten inches of warm water, the brutal sky is reduced to a shifting, honey-colored ceiling. When the pale blue whip-predator lashes overhead, I see its shadow cross the ceiling, and a split second later the roof over my back *snaps* from honey-gold to total, pitch-black eclipse as millions of scales above me fire their silver mirrors at once, bouncing all the light back up into the hunter’s face. In that brief, sudden pocket of artificial night beneath their bellies, I dart forward three body-lengths, grazing on the rich, velvety bacterial mat and shed organic snow that filters down between the scales where no crab’s claw can reach.

It is a cozy, secretive way to cross an open court.

I know where I am going before my feathery chemoreceptors even catch the scent. My belly reads the standing waves in the stone: the twelve-second pulse of the elder walker leaning on the western rod, and closer, much closer, the soft circular ring of depressed pressure where the smallest of the three walkers has folded its legs and settled onto the pan for the afternoon.

Ten yards out from the eastern ring, the scale-carpet grows sluggish and warm, drugged into a rich, steady ochre. My leading edge bumps gently against something yielding, cool, and slick—the outer fringe of the resting walker’s scalloped apron, where it lies spread across the stone.

I slip out from beneath the bottom-dwellers and glide right up under the walker’s eaves, into that rarest of all territories in this bright, ancient bowl: a roof that moves.

Up under the hollow arch where the creature’s thick hydraulic limbs lie coiled against its milky underside, there is a quiet pocket of shadowed water, perhaps four inches high and three feet across. Two thumbnail-sized translucent frogs are already clinging to the pale ceiling above the waterline, their tiny hearts visible as pale yellow specks beating behind frosted skin. They ignore me as I flow up out of the water onto the damp, gelatinous curve of the walker’s flank.

This is my other pasture, and my occasional ferry.

The great custodians are clean, mostly, washed by ozone mist and grazed by crabs, yet in the deep, warm folds where their hydraulic limbs join the central dome, dead prism-cells, trapped pollen from the sweating orchids, and tangled threads of exhausted green algae collect in tiny, irritating creases. Crabs have shells too sharp to be allowed into those delicate armpits; the walkers’ skin would tear. 

My soft, muscular foot leaves not a scratch. Slowly, methodically, I inch along the pale, translucent crease of the walker’s third limb-joint, rasping away the soured film and fouled cells with a tongue no rougher than wet silk. 

A half-inch beneath my belly, separated from me only by a membrane like firm jelly, the walker’s opalescent internal bladders expand and contract in slow, silent tides. Deeper in, through the clear, blown-glass lattices of its body, I can dimly see the cluster of pearl-gray calcium stones suspended in their central sac, trembling ever so faintly as the eastern ring outside hits its threshold and warps the noon light into a spiral.

When the walker feels my rasping along its crease, a localized blush of pale, cool turquoise blooms across the skin right beneath me—not the heavy, sedating indigo it uses on panicked hunters, just a tiny, subconscious shiver of relief, opening the fold a fraction of an inch wider so I can reach the trapped grit of dead spores at the back.

In an hour, or perhaps two, the walker will pump fluid back into its seven legs, rise out of the mirror with a soft *shhh* of shedding water, and pace slowly across the blinding pan toward the northern moss-pillars. I will stay tucked in the cool, wet crease beneath its skirt while the golden caustics dance around us, riding in the dark while the twelve-second heartbeat of pale gold ripples overhead—cleaning the thing that quiets the world, and letting it carry me to another island’s roots before I drop silently back into the silt.
