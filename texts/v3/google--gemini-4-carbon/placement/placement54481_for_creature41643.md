I am not up in the chop, and I am not the great flat muscle clamped to the stone. I am the slack between them.

Or rather: I am the thing that has no slack left in it.

Begin twenty feet off the bottom, where the grey-iron bruise of noon dies completely and the water turns from slate into grease. If you reach out a hand here, you don’t hit stone and you don’t hit meat; your fingers strike a taut, buzzing wire of split spruce root, bowed hard to the east by a salt-wedge coming in over the seaward sill. 

That is where I start to be something other than dead tree.

Up at the top, eighty feet overhead, a thumb in a greasy seal-gut mitten is hooked under me, waiting for a sharp jerk that means cod, or the heavy, rolling saw-stroke that means shark. Down at the bottom, three feet below my lowest splice, a fist of grey soapstone is dancing—*tick, shudder, slide*—across polished gneiss, dragging a pale tag of leached blubber behind it on a toggle of split gull-bone. Between the warm thumb and the cold rock, there are fourteen fathoms of three-ply twist, frayed at every tiny knot into stiff little beards of woody fiber, packed tight with grey glacial flour until I weigh almost as much as the water.

To be this is to be a long, blind nerve stretched across two arguments.

The water does not push me all one way. That is the first thing the hands up in the boat never understand. They see the surface chop running west with the wind, so they think I slant west. They do not feel what happens thirty feet down, where the freezing river-melt drops like a shelf of wet lead over the sunken sill and shears straight south across the salt. Up near the ice-slush, my upper twenty feet bellies west; in the middle dark, forty feet of me hauls hard south-southeast, thrumming like a bowstring; down in the bottom six feet, caught in the back-eddy rolling off a low whaleback of gneiss, my rawhide leader curls lazily north again before the soapstone anchors it. 

I am bent into a slow, three-dimensional *S* that never stops flexing. And because I am round and tight, the current cannot slip past me cleanly. It sheds off my leeward side in tiny, alternating whirlpools—left, right, left, right—making my whole middle length shiver at twelve cycles a second. *Vvvvvvvvv.* Not a sound the clay-thick water lets travel far, just a tight, furious hum conducted straight down my twisted grain into the grooved waist of the stone.

I am loud down here, though nothing has ears for it. In a valley of smooth ovoids and flat, greased hides where everything yields or streamlines, a vertical cord is an outrage. Peat ribbons catch on my frayed splices, wrap twice, pull tight into slimy brown collars, and add to the drag until the current rips them in half. Blades of torn red wrack fold over me like heavy wet blankets, hauling my belly down toward the rock until the thumb eighty feet up feels a slow, mushy weight, curses, hauls two arm-spans to shake the weed loose—though from down here, that sudden upward snatch feels like being yanked halfway out of one river into another—and drops me back again.

I know the bottom exclusively through the soapstone’s nose.

When we are over the bare gneiss, the stone transmits a steady, skate-blade *screeee* up through the rawhide binding—not a noise, a fine-toothed chatter in my fibers, smooth rock polished by grit rubbing grit-packed talc. Then the sinker hits the lip of a cobble pan, drops four inches, and goes quiet, settling into the slime between two skull-sized stones. For twenty minutes, nothing happens except the hum in my middle belly and the slow, cold leaching of oil out of the fat-strip down at the bone gorge. 

Then a bore passes overhead, and the pan mobilizes.

*Shuck-shuck-shuck.* Thirty cobbles lean against my sinker at once. My lower ten feet goes suddenly tight as iron bar as the stone is carried six feet east inside a migrating sheet of rock, dragging the boat’s stern slightly round eighty feet above before the stones stall and settle again, leaving my rawhide leader pinched tight between two slick basalt cheeks.

Wait long enough in that pinch, humming steadily into the dark, and the hum brings the flat thing.

I don't see it come. There is no shadow before it, no wake. The water is already shoving so hard that one shove more means nothing. What happens instead is that the bottom two feet of my shiver simply *stops*.

A roof has slid over the soapstone. The horizontal eddy that was fluttering the gull-bone gorge vanishes, cut off cleanly by a domed ceiling of dimpled gristle clamped down around my base. For ten seconds, I pass straight through a notch in the *skrid’s* scalloped margin—pale eyelash-filaments tasting the spruce-resin and old smoke still locked in my core, wet parchment-flesh tucked snug around my frayed rawhide like lips around a straw. 

Inside that sudden, dead-still dome under its belly, all current ceases. My lowest three inches go completely limp for the first time since leaving the boat.

Up in the slush, the mittened thumb feels nothing yet, because the thirty-foot belly of spruce root above is still bowed hard by the shear-wall, holding ten pounds of water-weight that disguises the slack at the bottom. 

Down in the dark, I feel a soft, wet *snip*—barely a tick—as the fat goes off the bone gorge. Then a heavier, crunching grind right against the soapstone’s waist. *Krrk.* Horny plates sliding over greasy stone, finding the pecked groove, finding the swollen, water-logged rawhide lashing that holds me to gravity.

Now the *skrid* shifts six inches to get purchase, clamping the sinker to its chest, and pulls. 

All eighty feet of my curve straightens out of the *S* at once, slicing sideways through the clay-soup, shedding grey clouds of mica and clots of bog-peat from every splice. Up top, the thumb jerks back; rough mittens seize me hand-over-hand, hauling hard. Now I am stretched between two mammals—one breathing freezing fog in a skin boat, braced with knees against wooden ribs; the other blind, cold as salt-slush, flattened into a vacuum ring against ten-thousand-year-old gneiss. 

Neither of them gives an inch. All of their refusal lives in me. My three plies twist tighter, squeezing bitter brown spruce-water out of my fibers; a weak spot forty feet up where an ice-cake nicked me last Tuesday parts one strand with a dull *pock* swallowed instantly by the silt. I stretch six inches, eight inches, singing a higher, thinner note in the freezing shear—

—and down under the rubbery bell, two horny plates close once, very slowly, across the rawhide knot.

The bottom drops out of the world.

Every inch of stretched spruce-fiber snaps upward at once, recoiling twenty feet through the murk in a silent, whipping spiral. Without the stone, I am nothing. I have no weight left to argue with the water. While the hands above haul frantically, thinking a giant halibut has spat the hook and bolted upslope, my severed tail—still trailing the bare, useless sliver of gull-bone—is swept instantly horizontal by the meltwater plunge, spinning lazily round and round itself, tangling with a passing ball of dead kelp before I am dragged upward out of the dark, through the iron bruise of midday, and back into the blinding, noisy, thinness of the air, where they will look at my neatly sheared rawhide end, say nothing at all, and tie on another stone.

Wait, though. That is only half of me—the half that goes back up.

Look back down into the pitch, under the domed gristle, or fifty yards further down the gentle, endless grade of the floor where the cobbles collect. 

Down here, I am also what stayed behind.

Not every line breaks at the rawhide. Sometimes the spruce root itself parts thirty feet up where the rock flour has sawed it thin against an ice-keel, or a drifting tree-trunk catches the boat’s float and snaps it near the surface. When that happens, forty feet of cord falls slowly downward through the liquid landslide, coiling and uncoiling in the vortices, tethered forever to its grooved basalt heart.

That is what I become when I finally live here instead of visiting. 

You can find me—if you have pale filaments along your skirt, or if you are an eelpout nosing through a shallow pan—tumbled into the long, dark hollows where the willow traps rot. My soapstone sinker has been spat out by a *skrid* hours or years ago, half its pecked waist scraped away into smooth belly-ballast, rolling cheek-by-jowl with native cobbles until scarcely a thumb-groove remains to prove a man ever struck it with chert. 

Trailing from that half-erased rock is twelve feet of split root that cannot rot quickly in water this cold and short of oxygen, yet cannot float away because of the stone. Over months, I gather the basin to myself. Passing ribbons of dead peat braid themselves permanently into my fray; mica flakes pack into my three-ply twist until I glimmer faintly grey if any iron-light ever reaches so deep; rolling tangles of red weed snarl around my middle, catch silt, grow heavy, and form a sodden, cigar-shaped bolster three feet long that sweeps softly back and forth across the polished gneiss like a blind tail whenever the tide turns over the seaward sill.

A whelk clamps onto my stone. Two tiny, rubbery kelp sporelings, desperate for anything rougher than greased bone to grip, sink their microscopic root-hairs right into the swollen clefts of my spruce-fiber—using a drowned forest’s root to hold station in the sea. Their perforated brown blades grow sideways in the current, pulling me taut downstream of the cobble pan. Beneath the shelter of my silt-packed tangle, where the terrible horizontal shove drops to a trickle for half an inch above the rock, three pale amphipods and a thumb-length eelpout curl up out of the grit-blast, resting against the braided work of fingers that died two winters ago on shore.

When a heavy bore comes down the sunken river mouths and the whole cobble bed lifts with its muffled *shuck-shuck-shuck*, my stone lifts too, sliding ten yards north in the dark, dragging me and my little raft of kelp and sheltering fish behind it in one unbroken piece—no longer trying to connect the ceiling to the floor, just lying flat along the grain of the shove, learning how to stay down.
