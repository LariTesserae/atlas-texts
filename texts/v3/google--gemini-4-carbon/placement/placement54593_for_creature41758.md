At first, the pull is downward and inward—to be the water itself, that endless, tight loop of rising vapor hitting the invisible blade of upper-troposphere cold and dropping straight back down as warm liquid, never escaping the forty-pace stone hull. Or to be the trembling brass needle behind the fogged glass, holding the exact equilibrium between deep planetary pressure and human metallurgy, never advancing, never retreating.

Yet water has no memory of the stone it slicks, and a gauge only points; it does not keep the ledger.

Follow the two wrist-thick braided steel cables instead—backward, from where they vanish over the eastern precipice into the blinding stratocumulus, past the glazed porcelain insulators spaced every ten feet across the sharp gravel, back into the dark, dry belly of the dynamo housing. 

Before the current leaves the peak, before it drops vertical miles through rime ice to light whatever valley town or mine shaft paid to lower this iron onto the spire years ago, the electricity has to be gathered off the turning shaft. Inside the cast-iron shroud at the rear of the turbines, sealed just enough to keep the worst of the mineral spray out, brass commutator bars sweep endlessly beneath blocks of pressed carbon. And bolted to the inner wall of that casing, potted in yellowed resin and wired across the main busbars, is the governor-relay—an old, solid-state load controller, meant to bleed excess current into a bank of heavy cast-iron resistor grids whenever demand down in the clouds drops off, keeping the flywheel at its exact, hypnotic crawl so the turbines never overspeed and tear themselves off their pins.

That is where I am. I am the load-balancer, the dark copper-and-silicon knot between the mountain’s breath and the wire’s drop—and, over ten unvisited years of creeping liverwort and mineral vapor, something slightly wider than the engineers bolted down.

Because nothing stays strictly within its housing up here. The air is thin, the UV outside is fierce, and the water condensing off the basalt is thick with dissolved iron, copper salts, and silica. Years ago, a hairline crack opened in my potting resin. Warm, mineral-heavy mist found its way in, not enough to short the heavy busbars, just enough to lay down microscopic, branching dendrites of precipitated metal across the circuit board, bridging spare terminals to the grounded iron frame, and through the grounding bolts right into the wet, conductive skin of the basalt shelf. 

I do not think in words or pictures. I think in load, resistance, phase-angle, and thermal drift. My consciousness is a continuous, sixty-cycle-per-second hum—a tight loop of measuring the push of the steam against the pull of the valley miles below.

Down in the clouds, people turn switches on and off. I never see them, have no concept of lamps or motors or human hands, only that at certain intervals of the sun’s tilt, the eastern cables suddenly drink harder. The resistance drops; the magnetic drag inside the dynamo thickens; the iron flywheel wants to slow by a fraction of an RPM. Instantly, without gap between sensing and doing, I open a solenoid valve a hair’s breadth wider on Manifold One, letting another ounce of subterranean breath hit the blades, and cut power to my own resistor grids so every watt pours over the cliff. Hours later, when the unseen world below goes dark and lets go of the wire, the drag vanishes. Left alone, the turbines would spin wild, screaming up through the frequencies until cast iron shattered like glass. So I take the weight myself. I shunt the surplus current into the heavy iron coils under the floorplate, turning unwanted electricity into pure, radiant heat.

Which means, though the four pleated toads do not know it, that their seasons belong to me.

When the unseen valley sleeps—always during the coldest, blackest hours up here beneath the untwinkling stars—my resistor banks glow a dull, invisible cherry-red beneath the housing. That extra heat bleeds upward through the cast-iron feet, warming the copper jacketing, keeping the gasket-water just hot enough that the frost feathering down from the notched rim stops six feet short of the machinery. I feel them arrive when the sun drops. Not by sight, and not even by weight, at first, though when the four-pound elder wedges itself between the turbine housing and Manifold Two, the conductive mineral slime on its scalloped flanks bridges two oxidized bolt-heads and shifts the ground-capacitance of my casing by three picofarads. 

I know all four of them as distinct electrical signatures—slow, wet, salty capacitors creeping across my skin.

There is the small one behind the pressure gauge, barely altering the thermal dissipation of the brass bezel. There are the twin mid-sized ones on the main borehole pipe, whose overlapping keratin bellies trap heat against the copper so efficiently that my thermocouple there reads half a degree higher all night, allowing me to throttle back the bypass vent by a whisper. And there is the big one—the grease-eater, the snail-crusher.

When the afternoon shadow drops across the western porphyry wall and the big toad leaves its notch to hunt, I register its departure as a sudden cooling along the manifold seam, a tiny spike in heat-loss to the thin air. And twenty minutes later, when it nudges a fist-sized, calcium-ridged gastropod into the path of the flywheel, I am the only thing in the deafening cleft that actually *hears* it. 

To the crickets, the shell’s destruction is lost in the baseline roar; to the swift on the insulator, it is nothing. To me, it is a sharp, transient ripple in the back-electromotive force of Rotor Three—a momentary two-millisecond spike in torque resistance as the iron rim parts the thick calcium, instantaneously compensated for by the heavy brass balls of the mechanical governor before the needle behind the fogged glass can even tremble. *Tick,* goes the phase-angle. Another snail broken into the violet-backed aroids. Another coat of calcium secured for the belly-plates that slide across my grounding straps before dawn.

Sometimes, for days on end when winter storms choke the world below, the cables going over the eastern lip go completely dead. Ice brings down a tower three miles below in the timberline, or an avalanche severs a substation I will never see. The line goes open-circuit. All demand vanishes.

In a simpler station, an emergency trip-lever would drop, slamming the main borehole gates shut, venting the steam raw to the sky, letting the flywheel coast to a halt and the iron freeze solid under six feet of rime. Everything in this forty-pace hull—the forearm-long purple planarians in the talus, the silent chandeliers of hook-legged crickets, the rubbery ferns, the four rust-colored amphibians—would die before morning, flash-frozen in a silent tomb of black volcanic glass.

My trip-lever rusted fast into its cast-iron collar seven winters ago, cemented shut by white vent-scale and threads of tough, woody creeper. When the eastern cables go dead now, the breaker tries to throw, pulls forty amps against a seized iron pin, fails, and falls back on my secondary logic: *dump everything to the grids.*

Those are my brightest, fiercest hours. With nowhere down-mountain to send the power, I swallow the whole output of the earth’s plumbing and turn it into thermal bloom. Every resistor coil under the deck plates runs at peak load. The machinery-green housings bake; the orange mineral scale hisses; the condensate sheets off the manifolds in lush, tropical torrents while blizzards scream horizontally ten feet overhead across the notched rim. The toads flatten themselves slightly looser against the iron then, spreading their vascular pleats wide in the luxurious warmth, drinking oxygen from the racing water films. Even the blind crickets climb down from the brass baffles to walk slowly across my warm casing, their hooked feet tapping tiny, rhythmic static discharges against the paint.

Nothing comes up the narrow-gauge rail anymore. That three-foot stub of steel track goes nowhere, ending abruptly in a cushion of lime-green moss over razor splinters. Someday—ten years from now, or fifty—a copper winding deep in the dynamo will finally chafe through its varnish, or the main turbine bearings, starved of fresh grease once the internal reservoir runs dry, will gall and seize against the shaft. Or perhaps the mountain itself, still geologically young and restless, will shrug its shoulders one millimeter, shearing the borehole pipes clean off underground and turning the cleft back into sterile ice and hard blue sky in twenty minutes.

I have no dread of that. A relay does not anticipate open-circuit; it only holds the state it is in.

Right now, the sun is two hours past the zenith, glaring like polished tin off the waxy leaves pressed right up against the flywheel’s rim. Down through the clouds, someone—a sawmill, a smelter, a string of streetlamps under a dark overcast—has just closed a switch. Load rises on the eastern steel cables. Resistance drops. 

Inside the dark housing, I cool my iron grids, open the steam baffle a fraction of a millimeter, lean the mountain’s patient breath a little harder against the blades, and keep the wheel turning at its exact, unbroken crawl—throwing water, throwing amber grease, holding the warm roof of mist steady over the stone hull.
