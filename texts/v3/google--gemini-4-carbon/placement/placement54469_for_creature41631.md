I am four hundred feet out from the masonry of Ward Nine, sitting in a greased leather sling suspended beneath the main cast-iron aqueduct, with my bare left palm pressed flat against the sweating belly of the pipe.

It is dark under here, except for the pewter glow rising off the cloud-banks a mile below my iron clogs, and the faint amber smear of a sodium lamp back at the cliff-anchor. The pipe is six feet thick. Inside it, glacier-melt and condensed exhaust water move with a heavy, rhythmic *shunt-shunt*, pushed by distant rams. The iron is cold enough to ache in my wrist bones, and wet, slick with condensation that beads on my tallow-rubbed sleeves and runs down to drip off my elbows—falling straight down for forty feet before catching a stray draft of warped gravity and drifting sideways like blown sleet toward a newly extruded needle of black quartz.

I don't look at the quartz. Looking at fresh rock too long gives you *seam-sickness*—a behind-the-eyes vertigo from trying to judge distances on faces that have no grain, no stain, no scale, nothing to tell your brain whether a facet is ten yards away or two miles. 

Instead, I keep my eyes half-shut and read the metal with my palm.

Beneath the hydraulic thump of the water and the deep, sub-vocal *thrum* of the Ward Nine flywheels pinning our granite spire together, the aqueduct is busy. Through the callus of my palm comes the dry, sharp *tick-tick-pause-tick* of a ring-code tapped three quarters of a mile away on the intake manifold: *Pressure holding. One-quarter inch north-creep on Pier Four.* A moment later, fainter, muffled by three leather expansion-gaskets, comes the brassy, rapid chatter of a pocket-telegraph clipped to a girder up in the residential tiers—two widows in the greenhouse lofts arguing, very slowly, about whose turn it is to scrape the rookery flues for guano.

I am a Joint-Watcher—specifically, a bellows-tender for the slip-collars. It is a solitary, mid-air trade. 

Because Ward Nine is pinned to an older, reliable slab of basalt while Ward Twelve across the gulf sits on a restless shelf that adds a fresh, razor-edged terrace to itself every few months, the aqueduct between them cannot be rigid. Every two hundred yards, the great iron pipe breaks into a giant telescoping sleeve, sealed with collars of vulcanized clinker-rubber, braided horsehair, and boiled crag-fowl fat. My job is to hang here in the wet twilight with a long-spouted copper oilcan and a heavy, short-handled spanner, making sure that as the mountains drift apart—or shrug sideways—the sleeves slide smoothly instead of binding up and snapping forty thousand tons of pig-iron in half over the abyss.

My heart beats once. I count to two. It beats again. My ribs are wide as a barrel hoop; when I draw a breath, the cold, thin fog goes down slow and heavy, tasting of wet coal-smoke, sour fern-spores, and the sharp, metallic tang—like flint struck on steel—that always hangs around young stone.

Right above my head, tucked into the warm armpit where a steam-tracer line runs alongside the main pipe to keep it from freezing, a forest is growing upside down. Fat, bottle-green ferns hang six feet into the gulf, thriving on the radiant heat. Their fronds brush my oilskin shoulders when the wind shifts. Tucked deep in their root-mats—which aren't rooted in dirt at all, just knotted fiercely around the iron rivets and packed with blown clinker-ash—three blind copper beetles are clicking away in time with the power cable thirty feet above us. *Click-click-click.* Sixty cycles. All is solid. Geometry is thick here.

I reach up with my right hand—wearing the chain-mesh glove, because young slivers of wind-blown mica sometimes lodge in the fern-mats—and part the cold, wet fronds to check the vernier scale bolted across the slip-joint. 

The brass marks gleam dull yellow under my thumb. Since my last shift, Ward Twelve has pulled three-eighths of an inch further away into the pewter gloom. That’s normal. What isn't normal is the *twist*. The witness-line scored into the inner sleeve hasn't just slid outward; it has rotated two degrees clockwise. And the tallow packing at the bottom of the collar isn't weeping water downward anymore. A thin icicle of dirty grease is growing off the bolt-head at a sharp forty-five-degree angle, pointing out into empty air toward the central chasm.

At the exact same moment, the three copper beetles in the fern-mat stop clicking. 

In the sudden silence under my ribs, the distant drop-forge over in the lower smelters lets go its hammer. *WHANG.* 

I don't flinch; nobody born up here flinches, it wastes heat. I wait while the iron roar slams against the mirror-flat obsidian cliffs of Ward Six, bounces across to the quartz needles under the slaughterhouses, sets two hundred thousand crag-fowls screaming like torn sheet-metal in the mist, rolls back under my boots, and slowly dies into the hiss of wet moss on hot pipes. Four minutes. By the time the echo clears, one of the copper beetles has crawled out onto my chain-mesh knuckles, waving its antennae wildly at nothing, completely deaf to the power line overhead.

A seam is drifting through the catenary.

I don't scramble. If you haul yourself along a wet traverse-wire in a hurry at twenty thousand feet, your thick blood goes thicker, bright spots burst behind your eyes, and you slip out of your harness before you've gone ten yards. Smooth as a greased piston, I hook my spanner to my belt, pull my right hand out of the chain-glove—careful of the parallel white scars across my knuckles—and slip my calibrated iron ring onto my thumb. 

I press the bevel of the ring hard against the aqueduct flange.

*Tap-tap. Pause. Tap-tap-tap.* 

I strike with the patient weight of my whole forearm so the ring carries cleanly past the rubber gaskets, all the way back to the valve-house pinned inside the cliff of Ward Nine, and forward into the pumping-crypts of Twelve. *Joint Seven. Rotational shear. Gravity-fray at forty degrees. Send a Seam-Surveyor. Slack the upper guy-wires.*

Thirty seconds pass. I breathe in once, deep into the bottom of my belly, feeling the heavy brass soil-locket cold against my collarbone inside my shirt—two pinches of my grandmother’s ash and cabbage-humus, reminding me I belong to things that rot and stay put, not to this raw, glassy crystal pushing out of nowhere.

Against my bare left palm, the pipe answers. *Tick-tick.* Acknowledged from Nine. Then, half a second later—though it shouldn't be half a second, it should be faster through solid iron, which means the pipe between me and Twelve is currently longer on the inside than it looks from the outside—*Tick-tick.* Acknowledged from Twelve.

Something slides through the fog fifty yards to my left. 

It isn't a bird. It’s a clean, vertical slit in the pewter light, narrow as a doorway, where the bruised grey air simply fails to meet itself properly. Through it, just for a breath, I don't see the five-mile drop into the cloud-cistern. I see the sheer black basalt wall of the lower pastures, miles away and three thousand feet down, clear as if I could reach out and touch it—two pale, heavy-shouldered cliff-beasts hanging upside down under an overhang, chewing black fern with slow, circular grinds of their jaws, their white breath spilling downward toward the peaks. One of them turns its split-hoofed foreleg, shifting weight on its suction pads, and looks straight through the slit in space at me dangling in my leather sling. Its yellow, horizontal-pupiled eye has no surprise in it at all.

Then the great grounding-flywheel back in Ward Nine must have sunk a fresh wrought-iron rod another sixteenth of an inch into the rock, because a deep, sub-audible *thud* shakes the aqueduct, dustless and authoritative. 

The slit snaps shut with a sharp hydraulic *crack* like a breaking icicle. The cliff-beast vanishes. Around my knuckles, the blind beetle stops scrambling, settles back onto the wet iron, and begins to click again in steady sixty-cycle time. Under the slip-collar, the sideways icicle of grease breaks off under its own restored weight and drops cleanly, vertically, down into the mist.

Still, the sleeve has bound up slightly on the twist; a tiny bead of high-pressure water is forcing its way past the rubber lip, hissing into faint mist. 

I unhook the copper oilcan, work the plunger twice with my thumb to warm the mutton-grease mix, and feed the spout into the tallow-nipple on the collar. Then I fit my heavy spanner over the two-inch nut of the tension-ring. I brace both iron-shod clogs against the pipe's rivet-heads, lean my broad back out over five miles of empty, ringing twilight, and begin to turn the bolt—one quarter-inch a minute, breathing every ten seconds, matching my wrists to the tempo of the mountain.
