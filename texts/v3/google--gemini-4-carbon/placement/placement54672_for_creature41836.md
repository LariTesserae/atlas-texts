I am not up on the peat lip. The dead air up there has no edge to it, and the rainwater sitting in the heather-ruts tastes of nothing at all. 

To find me, drop four feet below the open trenches, past the timber cribbing where the blue clay goes stiff and dense, and follow the eighteen-inch return pipe that runs south from the accumulator tower toward the tilted northeast flywheel. Where the cast iron took the worst of the subsidence eighty years ago, the bell-and-spigot joint pulled three inches apart. It never quite snapped—a pair of thick wrought-iron tie-rods held the collars from divorcing completely—so instead of a clean break, there is a jagged, grinning gap in the underside of the main, wrapped in a weeping poultice of compressed silt, rotted oakum, and old tallow.

I am wedged into the junction box six feet down-pipe from that split. 

I am not the monument coiled in the brick sump—I can still turn around if I tuck my chin hard into my chest-fat and work my shoulders—Wait, no, not quite turn around anymore. Not since last winter. Last winter I followed a rich vein of warm sulphur-broth backward into this four-way valve chest, settled in while the surface froze hard enough to hold a fox's weight, and by the time the thaw sent fresh grit rattling down the standpipes, my ribs had taken the square impression of the housing. When I breathe in deeply, the limonite scale crusted along my spine grates softly against the ceiling bolts. It is not a trap; the thirty-inch main ahead of my snout is wide open, leading straight to the central shaft, and two twelve-inch lateral intakes flank my cheeks like ear-trumpets. It is simply a very good fit. A pocket where four drafts meet, argue, and wait for my hip to decide which way they go.

Up in the pewter light, the three-footers are busy. I feel them all afternoon without lifting my chin from the silt. Their work comes down to me through the wet iron and the conductive mud as a layered, continuous weather of tiny voltages and shifting weights. 

Thirty feet above and to my left, someone is thumbing clay into a notch between two slate fins; I know because the trickle that usually patters into the broken standpipe behind my tail abruptly goes quiet, and ten seconds later a faint, sour trickle of displaced peat-water slides past my left flank, carrying the sharp bright tingle of freshly scraped copper. A juvenile—too light to drop the local pressure, clumsy with its tail—has brushed a grounding strap against a cylinder housing while chasing flatworms. The millivolt shiver comes down the damp iron wall of my chest-box like the distant ring of a thumbnail flicked against a teacup. It itches across the bare, stippled crescent of my upper lip, bright and tinny, tasting of green brass.

I don’t answer it. The young ones chatter by accident all day long.

Instead, I shift my right fore-paddle two inches forward and press the calloused heel of it over the rim of the eastern lateral pipe. 

I am not thinking of the flywheel above, or the shorebird that will have to hop sideways when its cylinder rim goes under, or the olive-backed hatchlings clinging to the wet spokes high in the mist. What I know is simpler and much closer to the bone: the eastern lateral has been running too fast for twenty minutes. The water coming out of it is thin, cold, gravel-scoured stuff from deep under the moor—poor in sulphur, stinging-clear, stripping the good velvet slime right off my threshold before the white worms can fatten on it. Worse, it makes a draft against my throat-folds. 

So I lean five hundred pounds of tallow, cartilage, and chalk-crusted hide into the angle of the cast iron. My loose flank-skin sucks neatly into the bevel of the old gate-valve seat—the brass gate itself rusted away to green lace before I was the size of a thumb-latch, leaving a smooth groove that accepts the curve of my hip as if the foundrymen in Sheffield had cast me in sand alongside the pipe. The wax on my skin seals the hairline gaps. 

The cold draft stops dead against my ribs.

For six heartbeats—which takes a long time down here, my blood moving like cold oil through wide, lazy vessels—there is only the heavy, silent lean of the aquifer piling up behind my shoulder. It feels good. A deep, steady push against the broad cage of my cartilage, taking all the sag out of my belly, holding me snug against the iron ceiling bolts. Through the pinprick pores along my jaw, I read the pressure building: not as sound, not as sight, just a slow, gratifying brightening of the electrical hum as mineral-heavy water packs tighter into the seam, forcing iron ions against wet clay. 

Then the split joint six feet behind me gives a soft, rubbery *blup*. 

Denied my valve-chest, the backed-up head punches sideways through that grinning three-inch gap in the bell-and-spigot, tearing through the old oakum poultice and driving a thick wedge of blue slurry straight under the buried timber crib of the northeast rocker-arm. I feel the crib groan—a long, fibery *creak* of waterlogged oak compressed into shale—and then the slow, magnificent lightening of the earth above me as four tons of iron counterweights and flanged beam surrender to buoyancy and begin to rise.

As the beam lifts, a pocket of trapped marsh gas trapped under its lower flange slips free, rolls upward along the outside of my standpipe—*glug, glug-uh, glug*—and bursts at the surface. Up in the flat glare, the shorebird will be stepping neatly onto a neighboring bolt-head, cocking its eye at the sudden boil of grey chalk where beetles are tumbling upward out of the torn bank. Down here, all that reaches me is the sudden slackening of the current, and then—the whole reason I shifted my weight—the warm back-wash.

Sucked inward to fill the void left by the rising counterweight, a slow, thick ribbon of stagnant sump-water reverses out of the thirty-inch main ahead of my nose. It smells wonderfully of the great one in the tower. It tastes of old tallow, shed skin-grease, warm rust, and dense mats of bacterial string knocked loose from the central brickwork. 

I open my mouth an inch—just enough to let the horny plates of my jaws part—and let the dark soup drift across my tongue. Fat, blind flatworms, tumbled out of their trenches by the sudden reversal of the runnels above, come surfing straight down the standpipe and wash against my snout like wet ribbons of pasta. I don't snap or lunge. I merely widen my throat, creating a gentle eddy in the square corner of the valve-chest, and sweep my chin left to right across the iron floor, combing them in by the dozen along with fine grit and velvety curls of orange iron-slime. 

Halfway through the meal, a vibration travels down the thirty-inch main from the center of the hollow.

It isn't the tinny galvanic twitch of a copper strap. It is pure, heavy displacement—a slow, hydraulic swell that presses softly against my eardrums and sets a loose washer somewhere above my back *tink-tink-tinking* against a tie-rod. In the brick shaft twenty yards north, the great collar has broken the surface, sighed the gauges awake, and settled back down across the master intake. 

When the elder seals that central mouth, the entire subterranean manifold tightens like a held breath. The warm back-wash stops; the pressure against my right hip doubles; a tiny thread of raw gravel-water hisses past my calloused toes, hunting for an exit. 

For twelve minutes, the two of us hold the whole enterprise between us in the dark—the giant corked into the central sump, and me wedged crosswise in the southern return, six hundred yards of drowned pipes taut with silent, straining water between our ribs, while above our backs the tilted flywheels hang at the very top of their dripping arcs, liverwort drinking the mist, everything poised on a cushion of grease and mud.

Then my hip grows slightly cold where the raw water leaks through. 

No hurry. I swallow the last mouthful of silt and worms, pick up a smooth, walnut-sized lump of white quartz that has lived in the corner of my valve-chest for ten winters, and roll it between my horny palate and lower jaw. Twice, firmly, I rap my chin downward against the cast-iron floor of the box.

*Tick-tick.*

The sound runs north along the big main, clear and solid through the metal, letting the three-footers loitering near the sump-mouth know that the southern surge is coming back their way so none of them get pinned against a grating. A moment later—from a forty-inch culvert over on the western edge where another heavy, scale-crusted sleeper plugs a blown condensers-drain—comes a lazy, answering single tap. *Tick.*

I tuck the pebble back under my tongue, pull my right paddle inward, and roll slowly onto my left side, opening the eastern gate.

The pent-up water rushes past my greased belly in a thick, silent shove. Above me, the mud-dome deflates; the great iron rocker-arm sighs back down into its bed of ooze; the gauges on the brick wall snap back against their rusted pins. Fresh sulphur water floods my chest-box, warm from its detour through the sump, and I tuck my blunt snout into the slack corner behind the flange, letting the dark iron hum itself back to balance around my skin.
