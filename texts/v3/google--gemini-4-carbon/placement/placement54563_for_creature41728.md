I am wedged behind the cast-iron pressure gauge, inside the bruised brass collar where its stem threads into the fractured pipe-elbow.

There is no room here to turn around, which suits me. My body has no front or back, only an inner curve and an outer curve, shaped like a split leather gasket that has softened in eighty years of machine-damp and learned to creep. I am four inches of tough, fibrous gristle, pale gray where I press against the raw basalt behind the gauge, stained dark with red lead and graphite paste along my belly where I hug the threads of the brass fitting. 

I came up from the horizontal dark three weeks ago—not crawling across the open pipe-faces like the copper-plated shingles, because the sudden sky-glare would have dried my edges to brittle horn in an hour, but squeezing upward through the millimeter-wide gap between an iron sleeve and its crumbling mortar jacket. When the collar ended at the cleft in the basalt, I stopped. The draft was right here.

All afternoon, I feel the world through two distinct pulses. 

Against my dorsal ridge, the raw black stone ticks as it drinks the overhead sun, expanding by microscopic fractions that press slowly down onto my tougher skin, pinning me tight. Against my underside, the brass stem of the gauge shudders with the breath of the ten-mile root system below. The pipe-thread is stripped by half a turn; a hairline spiral of air leaks straight out of the main conduit before it even hits the gauge’s internal coil, hissing directly across my feeding-fringe.

That fringe is the only delicate part of me—a tight row of feathery, rust-red gills tucked safely under the overhang of the cast-iron casing, hidden from the sun and out of reach of the suede-skinned hunters’ cold-chisel fingers. Even if one of those heavy beasts climbed up from the flywheel shards and tilted its bone-dished snout straight at the gauge, it wouldn't hear me. I make no clicks. I have no shell to pop, no joints to lever. To a creature listening for vibrations in the stone, I read only as damp packing material—a dead washer still doing its job of dulling the rattle between metal and rock.

Every few minutes, I feel the pressure drop in the deep tunnels two seconds before the rest of the pit knows it is coming. 

First, the brass stem against my belly goes momentarily silent as the distant air stalls; the needle on the front of the dial—which I have never seen, though I feel its tiny, hairspring *tink-tink* through the metal housing—drops back toward *40*. Then comes the heavy push. Warm, oily vapor forces its way through the stripped threads, hot enough to make my gray hide slacken with pleasure. I lift my outer rim a hair’s breadth off the basalt and flare my red fringe into the leak. 

I am filtering the oldest things in the network: microscopic flakes of paraffin, bacteria that eat degraded gasket-rubber miles away in the dark, and fine, sweet soot left over from when the boilers were still fired. For ten seconds, while the glass face in front of me fogs white and the thousand bristle-tubes pop into the mist below, I drink the deep country's exhaust at high pressure. Moisture condenses on the cold iron bezel above me and runs down the back of the casing in a single, reliable bead, soaking into my porous back so my skin stays supple through the dry heat of the afternoon.

Once an hour or so, a copper-plated crawler slides across the basalt face and tries to wedge the beveled edge of its shell under my flank, mistaking me for a patch of tallow-lichen. I don't strike or flee. I simply contract my muscle fibers, turning myself from soft felt into vulcanized tire-tread, clamping down onto the brass hex-nut with forty pounds of dull, hydraulic stubbornness. The crawler’s chitin grates dryly against my tough margin—*shhhk, shhhk*—finds no purchase, and slides on over the top of the gauge, casting a brief, welcome shadow across the stone as it passes.

When the sun shifts west and the revolving flecks of gold light from the spinning brass governor down in the rubble begin to sweep across my cleft—*warm, cool, warm, cool*, an irregular, ungreased heartbeat—I tuck my red fringe back into the threads and seal my edges down with a thin mucus that dries like shellac. 

Night out on the scrub-plain above is cold, and when that chill drops into this missing tooth of a pit, the iron cools faster than the stone. The gap behind the gauge will widen by the thickness of a thumbnail. Then, when the glare is gone and the blind hunters are busy down on the salt-worm chimneys, I will unpeel half an inch of my gray body, reach blindly around the rim of the unbroken glass, and wipe the day’s dead midges and mineral dust off the face of the dial—leaving it clean for the morning sun, before shrinking back behind the brass to hold the pressure steady in the dark.
