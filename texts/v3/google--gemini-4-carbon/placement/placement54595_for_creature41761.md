I am not out on the scoured gravel, and I have no thumbs to hold a chisel. 

To find me, you have to follow one of the heavy, chalk-stiffened elders away from the bright, clattering pillars and into a cleft where two dark slate fins lean together until they nearly touch. Step carefully over the threshold—there is a ankle-high skip in the floor here that drops six inches to the north—and duck beneath a curtain of coarse, braided mineral wool hung there to baffle the wind.

Inside, the bone-white glare of the sky is reduced to a faint, watery pewter light leaking around the edges of the curtain. The roaring applause of the basin drops down to a muffled, rhythmic thrum, felt through the soles rather than heard. 

I am the back wall of this cleft. 

Not the whole fin—just a forty-foot slab of dark, fine-grained, glassy slate, six inches thick at my top edge and swelling to three feet at my base where I root into the grey dolomite. Ages ago, a clean spatial shear tried to split this fin down the middle, stopped halfway, and left a hollow blister in the stone behind me—a dry, dead-air pocket shaped like the belly of a lute. Because of that pocket, and because of the way the iron-free silicate in my grain settled under pressure millions of years before the sky broke, I do not grind or groan when the tides move through the valley. 

I ring.

I am the master-plate. I am the memory of the pitch.

Right now, three of them are pressed against my lower flank, resting in the dimness. I know them by the dry, scaly grip of their broad feet clamping onto my slick surface, and by the slow, heavy grind of the dolomite silt churning in their gizzards—a faint, gravelly purr that passes directly out of their dense bodies and into my stone. They come here when the outer noise gets too tangled, when too many fresh fractures have opened at once and their bristle-crowns ache from trying to map a hundred new echoes simultaneously. They press their sloped, cartilaginous domes flat against my cold face and wait for me to sort the noise out for them.

I do it without trying. It is simply what my grain requires.

Water never touches my face—the overhang sees to that—except along my highest left corner, ten feet above the woven curtain. There, a thin, stray pencil of silver runoff skips through a pinhole fault in the roof and hits my beveled edge once every four-and-a-half beats, *spack... spack... spack*, before vanishing sideways into nothing. That tiny, relentless blow sets a hairline shiver running diagonally down through my mineral sheets, all the way into my buried root. 

At the same time, through that deep root, I feel the whole flooded county. 

I do not see the pillars or the hanging huts or the bone-colored sky. To me, the basin is an immense, tense web of torques and pressures. Down in the dark miles beneath us, the earth is heavy and tight; up here in the air, the broken planes pull at the stone like invisible hooks. I feel the exact weight of the great arch three hundred yards east, pressing down through the bedrock, and I feel the furious, high-speed spin of forty quartz bowls humming through the gravel like tiny, frantic drills. When forty tons of deep-fissure brine drops out of a slit in the air half a mile away, the shockwave hits my root a split second before the muffled boom hits the wool curtain, and the hollow blister behind my back swells with a low, clean *bvoom*—a sustained G-sharp that hangs in the cleft for ten seconds, trembling through the bodies of the three sleepers clamped to my side.

Under their gripping feet, my face is not smooth anymore. 

Over centuries, they have worked me. Not with the rough, gouging tally-marks they leave on the wet pillars outside—to strike me like that would spoil my voice. Instead, using wet dolomite paste and round slips of chert, they have ground shallow, fluted grooves across my breadth, thinning me here by the thickness of a thumbnail, leaving a raised rib there, tuning my surface to match the faults. 

Across my middle runs a long, horizontal bevel rubbed smooth as grease by generations of four-thumbed hands. And lined up on a narrow, natural shelf just below it sit twelve small, flat blanks of freshly split dark slate—chest-plates for the young ones, not yet strapped on.

One of the resting workers stirs. The slow purr of its gizzard hitches; its heavy mantle of mineral felt rustles dryly as a pale, gravelly arm uncoils from the wraps. 

Four hard, flat nails touch my center rib. Tap. *Tink.*

The sound blooms out of me, clear and dry and startlingly loud in the narrow cleft, backed by the hollow chamber behind me. The worker holds its domed head still, bristles flared wide in the pewter gloom, listening to how long my note lasts. Then it picks up one of the blank chest-plates from the shelf, holds it suspended by a braided cord, and strikes it with the same flint striker. *Chak.* Flat. Too thick at the lower corner.

The worker settles its broad foot tighter against the floor, picks up a lump of coarse quartz grit, and begins to rub the corner of the small plate in tight, wet circles. *Shhhk-shhhk-shhhk.* Every thirty strokes, it taps me—*tink*—and taps the small stone—*tink*—bringing the dead piece of slate closer and closer to my voice, copying my grain onto something small enough to carry out into the blinding spray.

I hold the note for it. 

High up near my left corner, something changes. The pinhole fault in the roof drifts by the width of a hair. The stray pencil of silver water misses my beveled edge by an eighth of an inch, striking instead a slightly thicker tongue of my stone just below it. 

*Spack* becomes *spock*. A fraction flatter. 

Instantly, all three of them on my flank go rigid. The grinding stops. Three sets of calcified bristles snap open in the dimness; six spatulate hands press flat against my belly to feel the altered shiver running down my diagonal grain. That eighth-of-an-inch drift up near the roof means the great northern seam is leaning inward; by what they call tomorrow—four thousand drips from now—two catch-frames by the split spire will be dry, and the puddle where the young ones fish for black grease will be pinched shut, showing nothing on its surface at all, not sky, not deep earth, just dry gravel under two inches of dead water.

One of the sleepers unhooks its foot from my base with a dry, tearing sound like stiff leather peeling from wood. On its own chest-plate, tuned against me forty years ago, it taps three crisp notes that match my middle rib, ducks under the mineral-wool curtain into the white glare, and begins passing the warning outward through the wet crowd.

The one with the new blank stays. After a moment, it wets the quartz grit in the runoff pooling beneath my corner, adjusts its stroke to account for my slightly deeper hum, and goes back to grinding. *Shhhk-shhhk-shhhk.*

I stand in the dark between the fins, cold and dry-backed, holding the pitch while the whole bright valley breaks and resets around my root. Someday—ten thousand tallies from now, maybe fifty—the stopped shear behind my back will wake up and finish its cut. My blister will breach, my forty feet of dark glass will drop an inch and split in two, and the crash I make will be the loudest, cleanest note this basin has ever heard. They will hear it in every upside-down hut and behind every folded wall of air; they will come in out of the spray, gather up my shattered pieces, and split me into five hundred new chest-plates, carrying my broken voice around on their ribs until they wear me down to dust.

Until then, the water hits my high corner—*spock... spock... spock*—and I tell them what is holding.
