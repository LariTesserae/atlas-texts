I would not be one of the mortar-backed keepers, though at first I thought I might be—pressing a flat palm to the stone to count three neighbors through the floor, paring back stray runners with a horn thumb. Even four minds in forty yards is almost too much company for a place this small, and even twenty-minute naps feel too quick for the scale on which the hollow actually lives.

Drop lower than their heels. Go past the slick, satin skin of the bedrock—that creamy-gray polish rubbed smooth by amphipod claws and sliding water-coins—down half an inch into the dark, unlit grain where feldspar meets quartz and black mica, right where the slumped wire-frame plunges seamlessly into the stone without leaving a seam.

Down here there is no *thwack*, only a rhythmic tightening in the crystal lattice.

Every fourteen minutes and eleven seconds, the black wire pulls on whatever deep anchor holds it—planetary spin, vacuum shear, some patient, bottomless debt—and for nine seconds prior to the fog above, a faint electrostatic shiver runs outward through the granite. To the jumping crustaceans waiting at the moss-edge, that shiver is merely a dinner bell. Inside the stone itself, along hair-thin micro-fissures that haven’t seen daylight since the mountains overhead were three miles thicker, that pulse is weather, tide, and metabolism all at once.

That is where I find myself: not an animal at all, and not quite a single plant, but a slow, pale-gold lithotrophic network—part fungal mat, part mineral-eating seam—threaded horizontally through the upper two inches of the basin floor, linking all eight artifacts underneath the keepers’ feet like wiring hidden under floorboards.

Out on the plateau beyond the rim, my cousins died out twelve million years ago when the last deep groundwater tables dropped away and the granite dried to dead bone. Here, trapped inside the thermal cup, I have spent eons learning to drink physics instead of rain. My filaments are thinner than spider silk, armored in glassy silica leached molecule by molecule from the surrounding quartz. I do not grow toward light; light is the lethal white furnace half an inch above my roof that cooks anything soft in ten seconds. I grow along gradients. Where the hovering dish pins its circle of dense, stubborn air against the rock, my threads crowd beneath it in a tight, whorled bruise just below the translucent snails, drinking the stray, stepped-down charge that leaks into the stone whenever the field refuses to yield to weight.

If you could peel the top half-inch of granite off the hollow like the lid of a watch, you would see the whole forty-yard bowl mapped in faint, tarnished-brass veins.

I am thickest beneath the worn runoff grooves. Every quarter-hour, when warm mist precipitates and skitters down those shallow channels, a microscopic film of dissolved beetle-waste, moss-acid, and snail-slime soaks three millimeters into the porous boundary between crystal grains before the sun can steal it back. For me, forty seconds of dampness is a monsoon. My upper hyphae swell, trade ions with the wet feldspar, and pass tiny, precious packets of phosphorus and potassium hand-over-hand through dry bedrock toward the roots of the twelve green islands.

Because the keepers only think they are the ones managing the thickets.

They trim what they can see—the green runners above the stone, the clogged channels, the skylights torn in the purple leaves. Down in the tight, dark squeeze where woody shrub-roots flatten themselves like suction cups against solid granite to keep from blowing away or drying out, those roots cannot touch bare mineral alone and live. They sit cradled in my dense, felt-like sheaths. And over millions of years, because a choked basin that rots into a single wet bog would shade out the thermal rock, cool the stone, change the evaporation rate, and drown my deeper dry-stone conduits in stagnant humus acid, I have learned to ration what goes up.

When the eastern tussock—the small, dry one fed by copper mites steering stolen water-coins—grows greedy and tries to sink a tap-wedge into a hairline flaw near the matte-gray rods, it finds the nitrogen shut off at the root-tip. I pull my mineral trade back six inches into the stone, leaving the ambitious rootlet starving against sterile quartz until it shrivels enough for the small male keeper to notice its yellowed shoot above and snip it clean at dusk. When the ancient twelve-foot vine cable creeping toward the left rod takes damage—stepped on too hard by an aging female going out to wake an egg in the mutative seam, or gnawed at the bark by desperate larvae—I flood its anchor-mat overnight with stored sugars and trace iron hoarded from three yards away, pumping the woody cable turgid again before noon so the naked vine-mice keep their warm basket and the saffron spiders keep their left collar-stop.

I have no eyes, yet I know the surface better than the red-eyed flyers crossing it at thirty miles an hour, because everything up there weighs something, heats something, or drops something.

I feel the pale sprint-lizards as sharp, twin-line scratches of heat vanishing across my roof before the crystal beneath them even warms. I know where the northern female sleeps inside her grafted dome because for twenty minutes at a stretch a heavy, eighty-eight-degree oval presses steady against the stone, silencing evaporation directly beneath her belly, punctuated twice an hour by the slow, glorious *boom-boom* of her breastbone—a clean acoustic wave that rings down through twenty feet of pluton and shakes loose microscopic flakes of weathered mica for me to digest.

Even when a keeper dies once every twenty years, and the beetles and snails strip her ribs white in a day and a half, they only get the soft grease and the calcium. What soaks into the satin floor underneath her last resting spot—the dark, iron-rich shadow of fluid that stains the pale granite violet for a week before fading—comes straight down through the grain to me. I hold that iron in slow, crystalline storage pods along the cool outer rim of the bowl, right up against the terrifying vertical cliff of cold where the greenhouse ends without glass.

That boundary is the sharpest edge of my existence. All around the forty-yard circle, fifteen feet up the curved lip, my brassy threads climb inside the stone until they hit the exact millimeter where eighty-eight degrees meets highland freeze. Every night, when the plateau outside drops below zero and frost tries to pry at the dry exterior granite, microscopic ice-needles push inward against my outer tips, and my tips push back with warm, antifreeze-thickened sap pumped up from the heart of the bowl—sealing the micro-cracks from the inside with precipitated silica so water never wedges the rim apart, so a fracture never starts, so the lip stays rounded, unbroken, and whole while the rest of the continent planes down grain by grain around us.

The artifacts do not notice me; they are only geometry locked into vacuum, snapping their canvas-wet *thwacks* into empty air for reasons whose makers vanished before mammals had names. They will go on clicking out eleven-minute and sixteen-minute fog until the planet’s core cools or the sun swells.

And as long as they pull water out of thin wind, I lie flat in the dark half-inch beneath the roar—tasting copper-mite wax in the runoff, counting four heavy hearts through the quartz, cementing the rim against the mountain cold, holding the floor together underneath the garden.
