Not on the windward spurs, where the air tears at the corners of the mouth and the big birds drop like heavy, stiff-winged coats onto the granite. And not out on the gravel basins either, walking behind the herds for half a year with nothing between one’s shoulder blades and the horizon. Too much sky makes the chest ache in a way that has nothing to do with the thinness of the air; it gives the eye nowhere to rest, no batter or lintel to measure distance by.

Go down instead—four tiers below the condensation walls, deep into an older quarter where the lower courses of the kinked trenches have been rubbed by centuries of passing wool and horn until the dark schist gleams like smoked tallow. There is a corner where a stepped ramp turns back on itself to spill toward a covered cistern, and in the sharp angle of that turn, sheltered from every wind except the straight-down downdraft of January noon, a low doorway drops three steps beneath the street level.

I would be somewhere near there. Not young. Young lungs belong on the steppe or under a forehead-strap hauling river cobbles up from the gorges, and I have no wish to climb four hundred feet before midday. My corneas went milky across the centers three or four winters back—not blind altogether, only washed out in the middle like thin whey, so that the noon glare on white quartz no longer stabs, it merely turns the whole upper world into an unhelpful, dazzling fog. Down in the trenches, and deeper still inside the vaults or at the bottom of an opened wall, that blindness stops mattering. Down here, nobody sees as well as their fingers do.

Think of what happens when the Wall-Readers condemn a belly in a retaining tier.

It is easy enough to say *take the stones down and put them back as they were*, until you stand in an alley five feet wide with nowhere flat for half a mile and realize that a forty-foot stretch of battered wall, nine feet thick at the foot and thirty feet deep to the ice, contains six thousand pieces of rock. You cannot spill them into the street, or the autumn herds coming down from the steppe will jam in the passage and suffocate under their own packed heat. You cannot pile them all on one neighbor’s roof, or the bleached grazer-ribs underneath will splinter and drop three tons of schist into a sleeping-chamber. The dismantled wall has to be scattered—fifty slabs on the tuber-terrace above the fuller’s pen, eighty stacked on edge along the shin-ledges of two adjacent trenches, a hundred heavy basal headers sledged round two kinks to wait beside the cistern. And none of them is squared to a standard measure. Each angular plate of granite or cleaved schist only locks because its particular frost-split bevel marries the slant of the stone beneath it and bites into the cheek of the stone beside it. Lose the order over five months of digging, and when autumn drives the cold back into the pit you do not have a wall; you have a rockslide waiting for spring heave.

So someone has to sit at the lip of the cut from the morning fog until the shadow drops, wrapped in three layers of greased hide on a folded felt pad, and take every stone across their knees before it goes onto a sledge.

That is where my hands are. Broad, short-fingered, hot as baked steatite from the thick dark blood that crowds into the palms. Even at seventy winters, the capillaries do not flinch from cold slate. When two young masons pry up a tie-slab that has not seen the sun since our grandmothers’ grandmothers wedged it tight, they ease it onto the leather apron across my thighs before anyone carries it off.

First I brush the underside clean with a stiff sedge-whisk, saving the dry, powdery grey dust in a wooden-rimmed horn bowl between my feet—ancient pika-dung and powdered lichen and stone-flour make rich bedding for the roof-tubers. Then I run both bare thumbs along the bearing edges. You learn a stone in four touches: where the maul trimmed a high spot centuries ago; where the weight of the upper courses pinched a shiny white bruise into the grain; which face tilted outward toward the alley (greasy-smooth, slightly rounded) and which face bit inward toward the dark rubble core (raw, sharp enough to catch skin).

Then I mark it—not by sight, by notch and grease-paint—and thread a small tally-piece of split rib onto a rawhide string that hangs from my belt-loop, one string for every course from coping-slate down to permafrost. When a neighbor comes with a bone-runner sledge to haul ten slabs away to store along her courtyard steps for the summer, she takes the matching tallies on a toggle for her lintel. My thumbs remember the tricky ones without any string at all: the long, coffin-shaped header with the quartz knuckle on its left haunch that bridges the drain-gully; the split wedge of black basalt someone stole from an old water-wall six hundred years back to chink a crooked corner.

It is very quiet work. That suits me best.

At the bottom of an opened wall, the city’s whole quietness gathers as thick as water in a basin. Above us, the choughs wheel and drop red-billed shadows across the gap; two alleys over, a string of laden beasts passes with a soft, rhythmic *clack-shuffle-huff* and a warm drift of lanolin smell that sinks briefly into our trench before the cold eats it. Down in the pit, the younger kin swing horn picks at the blue-grey, gravel-choked ice lens that caused all the trouble, breathing in slow four-counts through their noses. Nobody chatters. Now and then someone passes up something that isn’t stone, and because my lap is the sorting-table, everything out of the dark comes to my hands first.

You would be surprised what sleeps inside ten feet of dry-laid rock.

Old hay-pockets sealed over when a household added an upper vault two hundred years ago: lift the cap-stone and out comes a dry, green-gold brick of sedge grass cut before anyone living had a name, smelling faintly of summer steppe once your warm palm breathes on it, sometimes with a little round-eared pika curled inside, dried light as a thistle-head and intact down to every whisker. We tuck those pockets gently into baskets; the beasts below our floor will eat two-hundred-year-old hay in February and give warmer milk for it.

Sometimes a stone comes up with a shim stuck fast to its belly by frozen seepage, and when I pry it loose with my copper awl, it is bone. Usually a grazer’s shoulder-blade or a split cannon-bone, crushed fibrous at the edges from bearing eighty tons of masonry. Once in a great while, though, the curve under my thumb is narrower, denser, turned to the ring and polish of dark amber by dry cold and immense pressure: a human femur-shaft or a thick collar-piece, nicked at the end with the lineage-marks of the very house whose wall we are unpeeling.

When one of those comes up, work stops for three breaths. Not out of dread—why would you fear the person who has kept your ceiling off your face while you slept?—only out of manners. I warm the bone inside my tunic against my ribs while the grease-pot is fetched, rub a pinch of marrow-fat into the hair-fine stress cracks so the sudden open air won’t split it, and set it on the felt beside me. If it is still sound, it goes back into the exact joint it came from when we relay the course in late summer. If it has finally yielded and crushed along the grain, we carry it indoors when the light fails and lay it in the deep ash-bed beneath the smoldering dung-pot, where it calcines slowly over weeks until we can grind it into the clay-paste that seals our cistern against leaks. One way or another, the elder stays with the house.

At dusk—which falls like a slate lid dropping as soon as the sun clears the western ridge—two of my nieces take my elbows, though I know every step by the pitch of the flagstones, and we go down through the low doorway into our block.

We are a nine-beast household this winter. Not rich enough to throw off our woolens, rich enough that the flagstones under our felt sleeping-mats are mildly, steadily tepid from the steaming cellar below, where the big curly-horned ewes shift weight and chew dry sedge through the dark, bumping their horns softly against the stone piers. Soup has been sealed in the heavy steatite pot since morning; when my nephew slices the shrunk rawhide lashing with his flint-knife and lifts the lid, the close vault fills all at once with the thick, rank, glorious steam of mutton-jelly, tallow, and bitter wild seed.

That is my favorite hour—after the rib-scrapers have gone round if it is a cleaning night, or simply after the bowls are licked clean with a thumb and wiped dry with wool-tufts, when twelve of us wedge shoulder to hip under the heavy hides and drop the soapstone lamp-wick down to a blue bead no bigger than a star. Outside, five feet of dry schist away, the plateau drops toward forty below; frost grows like white fur along the basalt catch-walls; up on the dark spurs, the vultures lock their tendons onto the cold rock and tuck their bearded chins into their hackles against a gale that scours dust off granite.

Down here, my back is pressed into the curve of my sister’s spine, my palms are tucked behind my knees, and through the stone pillow-ledge beneath my ear comes the tiny,occasional *tick* of our relaid wall settling one grain deeper into its gravel bed—taking the weight cleanly, locking into the notch, holding the cold outside where it belongs for another three hundred years.
