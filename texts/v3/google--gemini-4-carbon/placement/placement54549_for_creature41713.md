I am at the left horn of the crescent, where the phosphorite shelf tapers into the damp wall and the warm draft coming off the floor-cracks turns upward against my ribs.

I know my place by the notch behind my right ear—there is nothing above the tragus anymore, only a thick, hairless ridge of old scar tissue that pulls tight when the barometric pressure drops outside—and by the heel of my left foot, which hangs half an inch over the slick lip into nothing. Everything to my right is solid and breathing. My shoulder is wedged deep beneath the heavy shelf of the big male’s collarbone; his chin rests near the crown of my skull, and every five seconds his chest expands against my shoulder blade like a warm boulder shifting in mud, pushing me a fraction of an inch outward toward the drop before letting me settle back.

We are asleep, or close enough that our hearts have dropped to twenty beats a minute, pumping thick, tar-dark blood through wide veins with a slow, heavy *thud* I can feel behind my eyes.

My eyes are open, though it makes no difference. The violet line on the far wall died hours ago. What remains is the sound—not heard anymore as sound, because you cannot hear what has never stopped since the hour you slid wet onto this shelf, only felt as a constant, grainy vibration against the bridge of the nose and the tight skin over the ribs. Ten thousand dry wooden clicks per second overhead, blurring into slate-hail; the deep, wet purr of the rodent-packs stuffed into the flutes beside my hip; the *slap* of the high basins. When a bird shifts its scaly toes forty feet up, a tiny flake of dried mud and feather-sheath drifts down through the updraft, lands on my bare knee, and sits there until the sebum drinks it.

I am awake because my mouth tastes too sweet.

For six days—six appearances and vanishings of the grey smudge—the saliva under my tongue has been thick and bland, lacking the sharp, metallic bite that makes the jaw-hinges ache pleasantly when you swallow. Snail-meat is water and slime; bird-liver is iron and grease. Neither has the white sting that lives outside under the flat stones. Already my calves have begun that faint, deep-muscle twitching that comes before the blood grows clumsy. Tomorrow, or the grey-smudge after tomorrow, the big male and the old female at the back will smell the flatness on my breath when I lean across them, and no one will hold my wrist when I turn toward the crooked lip.

Not yet. Tonight I still have weight to put on.

Without shifting my spine—because shifting my spine would wake the nursing female two bodies inward and make the infant mewl against her armpit—I let my right hand slide down my own shin, over the slick curve of the phosphorite edge, and down into the shallow clutter between the big male’s ankles and mine.

My fingers move like blind snails, broad pads flattening against each surface to read its heat and grain.

First: three empty shells, freshly cracked, their inner nacre still tacky with mucus. I nudge them gently with my knuckle until they tip over the lip. One second. Two seconds. A soft *tock* below, muffled immediately by the rustle of beetles climbing over the curve.

Second: the bone pick, tucked in its wall-crack—too high, leave it.

Third: a core of black stone, fist-sized, cool because nobody has held it since noon. My thumb travels around its equator. Two faces are bruised dull from old pounding, rounded off like dry dung; the third face still holds a crisp, stepped overhang where a flake came off two sleeps ago, leaving a curve sharp enough to catch the ridges of my skin without slicing them. Good stone. Foreign stone.

I brought this piece back myself, three or four winters ago—or thirty; time has no joints here unless an infant is born or an old one stops breathing and is pushed slowly over the edge so the beetles can clean the bones for picks. I found it fifteen hundred paces down the eastern scree-chute, wedged beneath a boulder of rotten granite where the glacier had spat it out. My right ear stayed behind on that ridge, frozen solid as horn while I lay flat on my belly chipped-stone to chipped-stone in the thin black wind, prying it free with a vulture-bone until the bone snapped.

I lift the black core into my lap, cradling it in the hollow where my thighs meet my belly so it can drink my heat. Cold stone splinters badly; warm stone parts like stiff fat.

Beside me, the big male stirs without waking. His heavy left hand slides off his knee, drifts across the dark, and lands palm-down on my forearm. His fingers are wider than mine, the nails worn back almost to the quick from forty years of scraping marrow-grease off rock. He leaves the hand there for three breaths—*Hhhhhh-uh... Hhhhhh-aaah*—reading the slight tension in my tendons, smelling the black basalt warming between my thighs, perhaps smelling the salt-hunger already thinning my sweat. Then his thumb gives one slow, sideways rub against my ulna, and his arm drops back into his lap.

Something cold and heavy slides over the knuckles of my left foot where it hangs past the edge.

I do not pull my foot back. It is only one of the big shelf-snails coming up from the lower wall, following the damp trail of dropped shell-liquor. Its muscular foot ripples across my toes—a wet, cool suction, rough at the center where its radula tests my thick skin, decides I am too greasy or too alive, and glides on toward the damp crease behind my ankle. Its soft, leathery shell bumps my shinbone.

I let it come another two inches, until it is well onto the flat of the shelf and clear of the drop. Then my left hand comes down—slow as dripping pitch—slips two broad fingers underneath its suction-pad from the front, peels it backward off the tallow-slick stone with a soft, wet *schlupp*, and turns it upside down in my palm.

Inside its shell, the snail pulls back, bubbling thick froth over my wrist. It weighs nearly half a pound.

I do not reach for the green anvil-stone; the old female has her hand on its belly tonight, I know that without reaching across three bodies to check, and striking stone now would tighten every ear in the chimney while the troop is digesting. Instead, I turn the black basalt core in my lap until its bruised, blunt butt faces up, hook my thumb over the snail’s leathery operculum, and press the shell’s spire steadily—using only the heavy, slow leverage of my chest leaning forward an inch—downward against the rock between my thighs.

No *clack*. Just a muffled, chalky *crunch* smothered between my belly-fat and my wrists.

I peel the shattered spire away in wet flakes, Careful not to let a shard fall into my groin-creases where it would chafe raw in a week, and work my thumb inside behind the mantle until the tough column of muscle gives way from the spindle.

I chew for ten minutes. Eyes half-shut now, jaw swinging side to side in slow, grinding circles. The meat is rubbery, cold at the center, tasting strongly of cave-slime, ammonia, and chalk from its own shell. Not the salt I need—that dry, burning white crust under the dead shale outside that makes lightning behind the eyes when you lick it—but dense, heavy fuel that sits well in the gut. Halfway through, when it is reduced to warm, grey pulp, I lean my head sideways, touch the back of my wrist to the subadult female’s cheek beyond the big male’s shoulder—no, too far, I would have to unstack us. Instead I reach behind the big male’s back, find the scarred shoulder of the old female where she leans against the sweating andesite, follow her collarbone up to her chin, and wait.

Her dry, warm lips part instantly in the dark. She takes half the mash off my fingers with a slow sweep of her thick tongue, swallows with a small click in her throat, and breathes out warm, iron-rich air across my knuckles. Her other hand never leaves the green cobble on the floor.

Up in the invisible seventy-foot throat, something changes.

Nobody else notices yet; their ears are whole and turned inward toward the troop’s breathing. Mine, pared down to a flat hole on the right side, catches drafts differently. High up, where the rime ice chokes the slot, the pitch of the wind scraping across the outer basalt lip has dropped half a tone. The ceiling-drips are slowing—*slap... ... ... slap*—freezing in their upper fissures before they can gather weight. Outside, the clouds have cleared off the peaks; the sky will be dead black and hard as flint, sucking every drop of heat straight off the mountain into the stars, freezing the scree tight so a heavy foot will not slide backward three paces for every two climbed. Hard going on the lungs, easy going on the knees.

Two more sleeps. Maybe one, if I can take a fat cock-bird off the lower roost tomorrow noon while the grey light shows me which ones have pale combs and slow feet, and pack my belly full of raw keel-meat and marrow-grease before the smudge goes out.

I wipe my sticky fingers thoroughly down the coarse, stiff hairs of my thighs, rubbing the snail-mucus and sebum together into my skin—thickening the coat over my wrists, over my knuckles, over the remaining stub of my right ear. Every millimeter of grease counts once you step past the fourth pace at the entrance, where your own nose-water freezes into needles on your lip before the breath is halfway out of your chest.

For now, the air in my nostrils is thick, foul, and blood-warm.

I tuck the black basalt core securely into the hollow behind my hip where the wall meets the shelf, pull my left foot back half an inch from the drop so the blind harvestmen overlapping below can re-knit their long, pulsing legs across the rim, and let my chin sink back down between my collarbones. The big male’s ribs rise against my shoulder. Five seconds in. Pause. Five seconds out. I match him on the second draw, letting my chest fall when his falls, until there are not seven lungs on the ledge anymore, only one big dark bellows turning chalk and grease into heat while the mountain freezes overhead.
