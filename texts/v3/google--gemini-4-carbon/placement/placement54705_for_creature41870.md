You have to look past the water-treaders, past the rusted grates, down into the black, mirror-flat trough where the central flywheel dips its rim. 

I am below the floor. 

There is an architecture under the visible architecture—the flooded, brick-lined raceways, the equalizing sumps, the long, blind throats of cast-iron conduits that carry the deep hydrostatic differential from one end of the cathedral to the other. No violet light reaches down here, not even the bruised reflection off the ceiling. There is only the forty-eight-degree water, heavy and slick with dissolved stone, pressing uniformly against every surface.

At first, you might think I am the silt itself—a long, velvety accumulation of iron-black sediment settled into the curvature of a forty-eight-inch main. If a chunk of flowstone breaks loose from a grate above and plummets into the trough, I do not scatter. The shockwave passes right through me, a dull *thump* that registers along my entire length at once. 

I am twelve feet of flattened, muscular ribbon, dark as vulcanized rubber on top, fading to the translucent, pearly grey of wet tallow along my undulating margins. I have no eyes to cloud over, no bones to ache in the cold, no lungs to keep dry. Centuries ago, something like an eel or a leech or a blind ditch-lamprey found its way through the choked intake shafts before the roots sealed them, dropped four hundred feet through the settling tanks, and refused to die. Or perhaps I was never one thing; perhaps I am a colonial mat that learned how to pull itself together and slide. It doesn't matter now. What matters is the pressure, and the taste of the water, and the fifty-cycle hum that comes down through the cast-iron pilings like a subterranean sun.

My world is mapped entirely in gradients of vibration and chemistry. 

Up above, the air is thin and separate from the things that move through it. Down here, everything touches everything else. Through the skin of my flanks, I feel the slow, planetary drag of the flywheel’s submerged third—tons of cast iron parting the water so gradually it doesn't even create a wake, just a continuous, creeping displacement. I ride that displacement. I curl my length into the six-inch clearance between the wheel’s hub and the glazed brick of the trough wall, letting the millimeter-by-millimeter rotation pull water across my gill-slits without my having to spend a single calorie swimming.

I know the custodian. Not by sight, and not as a shape with arms and a curved spine, but as a recurring weight and a sudden, localized richness in the water. 

Every few turns of the wheel—I measure time by the deep, muffled clunk of the submerged cam clearing its counterweight down in the dark beside me—I feel his padded feet settle onto the iron grates overhead. He is extraordinarily quiet; the blind, pink-gilled amphibians barely drift aside when he steps among them. Even to my lateral lines, which can read the frantic, tiny twitch of a drowned cave-midge falling forty feet away, his arrival is less like footsteps and more like a barometric shift. A soft, downward bowing of the floor-plates.

Then comes the ring of his copper bar against the wheel. 

To the air above, it is nearly silent, muffled by mist and lime. Down in the water, conducted straight through the iron rim into the flooded trough, it is a clear, low *zing*—a singing friction that makes the fine sensory papillae along my head-lobe flare wide. 

I uncoil from the hub and rise. Not breaking the surface—the meniscus is a ceiling as solid and alien to me as the limestone vault is to him—but hovering three inches beneath it, undulating my pale margins to hold position in the black mirror. When he shaves that curling ribbon of mineral-paste from the rising wheel, crumbs break loose. Flakes of soft, wet calcium, threads of rust-bacteria, tiny crushed crustacea that were cemented into the rind, and occasionally a drop of the copper-tasting slime from his own mouth or hands—they snow gently back down into the trough. 

I turn on my side, opening a wide, jawless slit lined with soft, keratinous combs, and glide slowly through the drift, straining the water clean. He eats the skin of the wheel; I eat his table-scraps. 

In return, I go where his broad, hunched shoulders can never fit.

When the trough grows sterile, I drop. I pour my flat body vertically down through a rusted sluice-gate, squeezing through a three-inch gap between bronze seating-rings that haven't moved since the surface had a name. Down here are the arteries that keep the flywheels turning: the deep pressure-exchange valves, the venturi throats where groundwater squeezes through narrowed iron necks to drive the buried turbines.

Down here, the secondary geology is an assassin. Without light, the white ferns cannot grow, and the brass snails cannot graze; there is only the blind, chemical patience of calcium carbonate precipitating out of solution wherever the water changes pressure, building smooth, hard collars inside the pipes, narrowing the throats millimeter by decade, trying to calcify the chamber’s heart into solid limestone.

This is where my real appetite wakes. 

Where the pipe narrows and the cold current accelerates—a silent, black rush inside the buried iron—the fresh lime-crust is slightly warmer from friction, and rich with chemosynthetic sulfur-threads that thrive in the high-pressure dark. I latch onto the inside of the venturi throat with the broad, muscular suction-pad of my belly, anchoring myself against the pull of the deep aquifer. My outer hide secretes a thick, mildly acidic mucus—metabolized from the sulfates and iron oxides I swallow—that softens the newly laid stone into chalky milk. 

Inch by inch, hour by hour, I rasp my flat underside across the inside of the conduit. I am a living gasket, a blind tongue licking the arteries clean from within. When a particularly thick shelf of travertine begins to choke a check-valve, keeping the heavy bronze flapper from seating properly, I wedge my tough, rubbery head into the hinge-gap, prying after the sweet, anaerobic worms that nest in the stagnant pocket behind the scale. My flexing pries the stone loose. It shatters in a silent cloud of white grit that washes downstream into the deep sumps, and the bronze flapper settles back with a heavy, satisfied *thud* that sends the twenty-minute sigh rippling smoothly up through the walls of the cathedral above.

Up on his gallery, sitting on his porcelain insulator, he hears that sigh, uncoils his arm, and pries a brass snail from the brick. Neither of us knows the other has just kept the world turning.

Sometimes, though—once every hundred revolutions or so, when the deep cold of the lower conduits makes even my slow, clear blood sluggish—I follow the stray current upward. 

There is a flooded cable-chase at the far north end of the nave, beneath the switchboard alcoves where the spindle-crabs click and wave. Down in the flooded trench, lead-sheathed power cables lie coiled like dead pythons on the brick floor, carrying current from the subterranean thermal piles up to the transformers. In three places, the lead sheathing has split from centuries of mineral expansion, exposing tarred hemp insulation and dull copper core to the water. 

It doesn't short out completely—the calcium-heavy water has crusted the splits over with thick, glowing domes of blue-green sulfate coral, insulating the wound with its own electrolytic scab—but the water around those domes is warm. Sixty, maybe sixty-five degrees. And it tastes sharply, exquisitely, of loose electrons and copper ions.

I slide through the dark flooded trench until the dim, submarine blue glow of the underwater coral touches my blind skin—I cannot see the color, only feel the slight, prickly heat of the radiation on my dorsal nerves. I drape my twelve-foot ribbon loosely around the warm, humming lead cable, careful not to scrape the delicate sulfate domes away. 

Directly above me, separated by six inches of vaulted brick floor and a slotted iron plate, he is leaning his calcified back against the transformer housing, pinching off a live mineral tip and drinking the violet arc through his knuckles. 

Down below, in the black water, I feel the tiny, sudden dip in the cable’s magnetic field as he draws the spark. A micro-shiver runs through the lead sheath against my belly. My undulating margins go entirely still. Wrapped around the warm root of the machine, bathed in the faint electrical leak, I rest—half flesh, half silt, waiting in the dark for the cam to clear the counterweight, for the pressure to shift an ounce from left to right, and for the snow of scraped stone to fall once more through the mirror overhead.
