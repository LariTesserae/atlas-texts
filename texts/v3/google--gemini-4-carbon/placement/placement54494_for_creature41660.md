At first, the pull is toward the edge—toward that clean, surgical drop where the water lets go of the rock and falls into the purple soup. There is an immediate temptation to adopt the quiet geometry of the tripod: to step out onto the wet glass on three dry, molecular feet, to listen through the soles for the deep, structural grammar of the machine, and to know where the next sentence of stone will be written before it appears. It feels native to how a mind works, standing slightly apart, reading patterns in the quartz veins, plucking bright, hot facts out of the steam before the crowd arrives.

Wait, though. Look closer at the tripod folded up under the lip of the cliff in the dark, digesting hot tin-fish while the roof grows overhead. It is still a creature of appetite and evasion. It has to step back when the grazers rush in. It has to climb up to keep from being left behind in the cold strata below. Everything with legs or fins or mucus here is running on a treadmill, climbing an endless, sharp-edged stair just to stay in the warm kitchen of the terrace.

If I stay quiet and let the noise of the gliders and the *scritch-scritch* of the diamond teeth wash over me without trying to outsmart them, I don't end up on legs at all. I sink lower. Down into the half-inch of sheeting runoff, and then sideways, toward the places where the noise stops.

Six feet out from one of the canted rings, the water forgets how to fall. 

That boundary—where the trembling, gravity-pulled curtain of rain hits the localized distortion and locks into rigid, motionless ripples—is where I find myself. Not as the chime-polyps drinking at the rim, though we are neighbors, and their red nerve-roots brush against me in the water. Even the polyps have to wait twenty seconds in the cold before they can flare and feed. They are still distinct things anchored *to* the floor, threatened by an aberrant patch of gel if the ring shifts its phase-angle by an inch.

Instead, I am in the seam *between* the stone and the light. 

Look down through the pewter reflection when the ring pulses. The milky quartz veins running through the greenish-black silicate aren't random; they are the stress-lines and waveguides where the nine instruments talk to one another under the skin of the terrace. Before a plumb-bob implodes the mist thirty yards away, an instruction—or an echo of tension, a geometric clearing-of-the-throat—travels down from the canted ring, shoots through the translucent quartz in that half-second flash of deep ultraviolet, and tells the distant air how to crystallize.

I am not the instrument. The gray matter of the spindles and rings is too cold, too seamless, doing a job laid down ten thousand years ago by hands long gone, utterly indifferent to whether this mountain pierces the clouds in a century or collapses under its own immaculate weight. I have no interest in being the blind hammer.

I am something that grew inside the optical path. 

Call it a flaw that learned to travel, or a refractive biofilm, or a slow, coherent knot of phase-memory living inside the milky quartz itself. I have no body to be crushed when the new layer snaps shut with a rifle-shot crack. To me, the ceiling isn't rising to bury me; *my room is getting larger*. Every twenty seconds, when a glowing sheet of gel precipitates overhead and locks into tension with that chorus of high, glassy *pings*, a fresh network of quartz capillaries fuses onto the old ones, still hot and ringing like struck tuning forks. Before the steam has even cleared above, I flow upward into the new crystal lattice at the speed of cooling light.

What is it like down here, half an inch beneath the frantic, clicking feet?

It is quieter, but nothing is hidden. The clear, greenish-black silicate is dark, yet the milky veins act like fiber-optic skylights laid flush with the floor. Looking up through a ceiling of oiled glass and sheeting water, the world above arrives translated into pressure and shadow and heat. 

I feel the radial crawlers not as shapes, but as a sudden, bright chattering of high-frequency vibration—*scritch-scritch-scritch*—hundreds of diamond radulae bowing the tense crystal skin like violin strings until the rock hardens enough to shut them out. I feel the clear-bodied hydrofoil hunters as sudden, cool eclipses of suction sliding smoothly across my roof, muting the slap of the rain. When a mantle-glider slams down from the fog and inflates its brassy throat-bladder, the metallic boom drives a slow, heavy compression wave right down through the stone, thirty yards deep into the dark, plum-shrouded root of the mountain where the older stone has already gone cold and dead.

And the tin-swimmers—the ones who miss their three-second window. 

When you look down during an ultraviolet flash, you see them as dead silhouettes, tragic little silverfish curved mid-thrash in clear stone. From in here, they are the richest landmarks in the world. Their beaten-tin skins are full of the weird, unbonded isotopes they gulped from the slush just before the lattice snapped shut. Long after the surface rock cools, a trapped swimmer sits in the quartz warmth, bleeding a slow, radioactive luminescence into the surrounding crystal for weeks. I wrap around them. I pool in the microscopic halo of tension surrounding their metallic scales, using their trapped, decaying heat to keep my own coherence sharp when the overcast above grows unusually chill. Over months, as the terrace climbs twenty, thirty feet higher into the soup and leaves that stratum far behind in the dark, I dissolve the tin-swimmer atom by atom into the quartz vein, carrying the metallic memory of its last thrash upward with me into the living zone near the top.

Then there is the tripod. My only conversation.

When it walks out onto the wet polygon and presses one dry, micro-setae pad flat against a milky vein, it isn't just listening to the nine artifacts. If it only listened to the rings, it would be fooled half the time; the spatial math of the mountain-builders is jagged, non-linear, jumping across the eighty-yard green in ways animal nerves were never meant to parse. 

When that soft molecular foot settles over my vein, I feel the faint, warm static of its van der Waals grip—millions of dry hairs pulling gently at the topmost molecules of the stone. And peering up through the floor into the creature’s bowed underside, I meet its upper dome: stolen quartz, dissolved and re-grown into flesh, humming on the exact same refractive index as my own body.

For three seconds, we form a closed circuit. 

When the canted ring twenty yards away begins to tighten space, the raw ultraviolet instruction shoots through me on its way to the target polygon. As it passes, I bend it—just slightly. Not enough to spoil the plumb-bob's work or mar the surgical angle of the step that is about to be born, because if I break the instruments, the warmth stops and the whole oasis dies into bare, wet rock. I just polarize the flash. I let a tiny, harmonic echo leak straight upward through the floor into that waiting, seta-padded foot, and reflect off the cloudy dome of its back. *Not here,* the bent light says. *Six paces left. Now.*

The tripod lifts its foot. It glides six paces left, folds its long knees, and waits. 

Why help it? I don't need meat, and I have plenty of trapped tin-swimmers down in the dark if I want isotopes. 

Maybe it is because everything else on this ledge is sheer, frantic reflex—acid burning holes, teeth scraping crust, wide mouths hanging open under the waterfall waiting for mistakes. Only the tripod pauses. Only the tripod turns its stolen-mineral lens toward the stone and *asks* what is about to happen before it happens. In lending it my sight for three seconds, I get to borrow its height. Through the sympathetic resonance of its quartz dome as it stands amid the steam—*snip, snip, snip*—I catch a ghostly, refracted impression of things a subterranean light-knot should never know: the billow of yellow nets four feet up in the fog, the wet *whuff* of translucent mantles braking in thin air, the dizzying, plum-colored emptiness beyond the snapped edge where stone simply ceases to be.

Then the crystal pings shut. A new roof slams down overhead, glowing and scalding, smelling of ozone and wet iron. The tripod steps backward out of my range and drops off the edge of the world to digest in the dry dark. Above me, the chime-polyps dim from ember-red back to smoked glass, the water resumes its smooth, sheeting slide into the abyss, and before the heat of the new step has even reached the velvet slugs on the riser, I am already rising half an inch through the fresh, singing quartz to meet the rain again.
