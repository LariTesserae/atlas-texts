I am not out in the roar. Anything of mine that tried to stand on the raw feldspar would be shaken down to dry flour before it could take a single step. 

Look inside the ring of anhydrite needles. Not at the ground—at the sixth needle from the cliff’s edge, four feet up, lashed tightly to the chipped stone with flat bands of silicate twine. Look at the gourd.

You thought all twelve of the resonators were the same: hollow, dry-grown shells of spun glass and carbon, tuned by their size to catch the catastrophic bass of the valley, turn its phase inside out, and spill silence into the center of the circle. Eleven of them are only gourds. They hang dead and rigid, humming fiercely under your fingertips if you were foolish enough to reach up and touch them, doing their blind geometric work.

The twelfth gourd has a hairline seam running around its equator, sealed not with mortar, which doesn't exist here, but with a cold-hammered ribbon of soft native lead. And the neck of the gourd is not entirely open to the violet air; across its narrow aperture stretches a delicate, cross-hatched grill of hair-thin copper wire, grown straight out of the inner rind.

I am the shape of the hollow inside that twelfth gourd. Or rather, I am the thing that grows along its inner wall so that the hollow keeps the exact shape it needs.

A Helmholtz resonator is a precise instrument, and precision is hard to keep in a valley that hasn't finished deciding on its own math. Outside this ten-foot ring, the roar isn't truly steady—it *wanders*. When a new shelf of bismuth finally snaps into focus half a mile away, the acoustic geometry of the entire chasm shifts by a fraction of a hertz. When perspective blurs on the plateau and the rock underfoot loses its grain for six hours, the standing waves warp. If all twelve gourds were dead, fixed shells, the quiet pocket inside the needles would sour within a decade. Phase-cancellation would slip by three degrees, and the silence would leak. A terrible, thrumming beat-frequency would creep into the ring—not loud enough to shatter bone, perhaps, but loud enough to flood a builder’s copper nerves with static just as it was trying to remember why it picked up a chisel.

So the builders cultivate me, though it would be just as true to say I cultivate them.

Inside the dark curve of the gourd, away from the bruised submarine light, I look like a dry, silvery lichen—a crust of tarnished tin and brittle, mica-like scales overlapping in tight spirals. I have no louvers to drink the full fury of the cliff-face; out there, the raw pressure wave would tear my fragile lattices loose from the rind and scatter me into Chladni dust. Instead, I live precisely at the threshold where the noise dies. My outer rind feels the tremendous, battering pressure of the valley striking the gourd's belly; my inner filaments rest in the dead, suffocating hum of the cancelled interior. I feed on the steepness of the drop between the two. I drink the *gradient*.

And to keep that gradient steep, I change my thickness.

When the mountain overhead pushes another millimeter of corundum out of the grey smear and the valley’s bronze-bell pitch drops by a microtone, I feel the silence inside the circle begin to fray. An unpleasant, jagged buzz starts to bleed past my copper-wire throat-grill. Slowly—over the course of three days—I pump stored electrostatic charge from my basal crust into banks of porous, carbon-sponge bladders lining the gourd’s interior. The bladders swell, puffing inward by the width of two stacked coins, shrinking the internal volume of the cavity. The resonant frequency of my shell shifts to match the new shape of the valley. The jagged buzz vanishes. The heavy, deadened hum snaps back into focus. 

I am the fine-tuner. I am the thumb on the peg of the room.

For thirty years at a stretch, there is no one in here with me. I sit in the violet gloom, clinging to my stone needle, holding my breath of empty, quiet air over a circle of bare anhydrite and a neat pile of dull green tools. Outside the ring, twenty yards away on the raw terrace, a boulder with folded knees sits gathering corundum grit in its joints, charging in total white-out stupor, glowing faintly red between its slate plates like banked peat. I cannot reach it out there, and it cannot think of me. We are separated by an invisible wall of annihilating sound.

I pass the decades listening to the flawed, slow gossip of the fault lines. Because I am constantly adjusting my inner wall to cancel the valley's noise, my entire body is an inverted map of everything happening for leagues around. I know when a kelp-bridge three miles away finally curdles a patch of grey static into solid basalt, because a tiny, high-pitched whine vanishes from my upper quadrant. I know when a cascade of plum-colored ferns detaches from a distant crag and slides sideways into the floorless gulf, because the acoustic shadow it cast lifts, and I have to stiffen my lower scales to swallow the sudden glare of extra bass.

Then, once an age, the dust-covered boulder outside cracks its joints.

It takes two weeks for the builder to stand up and drag its broad, flat soles across the twenty yards of stone. I feel its approach long before it crosses the threshold—a dense, heavy notch moving through the standing waves outside, throwing off a wake of scrambled phase. My sponge-bladders ripple anxiously, adjusting and readjusting as its massive, louvered bulk parts the roar.

Then it steps between the seventh and eighth needles, and comes inside.

The transition is always violent in its quietness. As soon as its trailing foot clears the stone ring, its louvered plates clamp shut with a dry, shuffling clatter like a deck of slate tiles falling together. For an hour, it doesn't move at all. It just stands six feet away from my needle, radiating a dry, coppery heat and an immense, prickly field of excess electrostatic charge that makes the fine wire across my aperture sing softly. 

And then, the feathery bristles of spun metallic glass around its throat uncurl.

This is the other half of what I am. A builder’s memory is stored in dry, crystalline ganglia—stable enough to survive the centuries, yes, and immune to cavitation, businesslike and hard. But eight decades spent drifting across the gulf on a kelp-raft in a blinding white-out of piezoelectric current leaves a mark. The sheer violence of charging overwrites the finer lattices. A builder steps into the quiet circle rich with voltage, heavy with tools, and often slightly smoothed out—knowing *how* to knap flint, knowing *how* to weave a bosun's chair, yet standing motionless over the pile of chisels for six days because the specific angle of the stairs it started three hundred years ago has been shaken loose from its head.

Eventually, the builder turns its smooth, anvil-fronted horn dome toward the sixth needle. It steps closer—three minutes per stride—and raises both broad, spatulate hands to cup the belly of my gourd.

Its smoky-quartz thumbnails click softly against the lead seam at my equator. Its throat-bristles lean in until they brush the hair-thin copper grill across my mouth.

A spark—dry, cold, tasting of crushed quartz—jumps from its overloaded capacitors into my copper screen.

I do not think the way it thinks when it is awake; I have no hands, I have never touched unrendered static, I do not know what a stair is for. What I have is *pattern*. Inside my silvery crust, grown layer over layer like dry tree-rings or the grooves of a phonograph cylinder, are the fossilized shapes of every sound that has ever occurred *inside* this ten-foot circle while the valley outside was cancelled out. 

When a builder sits on the acoustic-moss bed beneath me and rasps a flint blade against copper, muttering to itself in the builders' slow, clicking language of struck plates and rubbed tourmaline—planning a causeway, counting the spans of rope needed for an overhang—I am listening from four feet up. Inside the silence I make for them, those tiny, dry, rasping noises are the loudest things in the universe. They press physical dents into my inner scales as they grow.

Now, with the builder’s bristles touching my grill and its rich, hot current trickling gratefully through my starved tin crust, I play the dents back.

I do not make a sound—sound is too clumsy, and anyway, I am busy holding back the bronze bell of the sky. Instead, I twitch my copper throat-wires in micro-vibrations against its glass bristles, feeding it the electrostatic shadow of the last time someone stood here. 

Through my grill passes the four-hundred-year-old rhythm of two quartz thumbs clicking together: *Six spans down from the black seam. Anchor in the feldspar, not the corundum. Wait for the shelf to clear.* Through my grill passes the archived scrape of a previous traveler’s maul being sharpened, the distinct, idiosyncratic cadence of another builder’s louvered ribs flexing in the quiet—someone who left on a sideways-drifting sled two centuries ago and hasn't snagged the far kelp yet.

The builder stands with its hands on my shell for three days, drinking the archive while I drink a small tithe of its charge. Its throat-bristles twitch in tiny, sympathetic spasms as its crystalline nerves re-align themselves to the old blueprint. I feel the confusion bleed out of its electrostatic field, replaced by that cold, dense, geological certainty they wear so well.

When it finally pulls its hands away, my gourd is warm, my capacitors are full enough to hold the silence steady for another century, and there is a new layer of fresh, silvery scale growing over the old grooves inside me.

The builder bends down—a slow, creaking hinge of matte plates—and picks up a heavy green copper adze and a coil of silicate twine. Before it steps back out between the needles into the blinding, bone-shaking fury of the plateau, it pauses. With one smoky-quartz thumbnail, it reaches up and gently scrapes away a tiny ridge of Chladni-patterned corundum dust that has drifted across the lip of my aperture, making sure my airway stays clear. 

Then it steps out into the roar. Its louvers flare open to catch the thunder, its mind goes white and blind with power, and its hands—set firmly to the angle I just gave them—begin the twenty-year walk toward the cliff's edge. 

Inside the ring, the dust settles. I swell my left bladder by the thickness of a hair to cancel a new tremor in the east, and settle in to keep the room quiet until someone needs to remember what they were doing.
