I am not in the cistern. If I were in the cistern, the trachyte would have taken the skin off my shins before I found a level stance, or my shadow—the one thing in that pocket that isn't white—would have chilled a patch of the north wall just enough to make the blister pull its skirt in. 

No, to stay out of the way of that glare, you have to go up, and you have to get very small, or very wide. 

Find me two hundred yards overhead, where the unbroken dark stone still holds together, before the trachyte gives way to the older, bruised basalts and the compacted rubble of whatever used to be the surface. Up here, there are no twenty-pace bellies in the rock. There is only the capillary web—the tightly clamped teeth of the crust, hairline seams pinched under thousands of atmospheres of overburden, through which the groundwater moves not in ribbons or drops, but in slow, bruised sheets a few microns thick.

Actually, wait. Don't look for a creature tucked into the rock. Look at what is *missing* from the water when it leaves that cistern.

The water enters the ceiling bright—hissing with crustal stress, crowded with diamondoid lattices ringing like tiny struck anvils—and it leaves through the floor-crease slightly dimmer, because the clear thing on the north wall has wrung a percentage of those lattices dry and stacked them like poppy-seed cannonballs behind the submerged stones. 

Someone—or some *system*—put those colloids into the crust. A planetary-scale seeding doesn't happen as a one-off gesture; you don't pour engineered diamondoid dust into the tectonic boundary layers and walk away forever, or at least, whatever walked away left the gauges running. 

I think I am one of the gauges. Or what is left of one, after ten thousand generations of the local biology learning how to eat the coolant.

Not a metal box bolted to a shaft. Anything with a housing or a cable was crushed to foil epochs ago, when the mitigation plumes actually did their job and lubricated the faults into a slow, continuous creep. If you want to read the deep stone over geological time, you build the sensor out of the stone itself. 

I am a doped vein. Four yards long, thin as butcher’s twine, fused directly into a seam of plagioclase about eighty yards down-gradient from the cistern’s lowest drain. Back when the surface had names for things, I would have been called a piezophotonic relay—a thread of artificially grown tourmaline and synthetic quartz, seeded with rare-earth ions, laid down by an injection rig that drilled a pinhole two miles deep, squirted a smart mineral slurry into the micro-fractures, and pulled back out before the rock healed shut.

For a very long time—centuries, maybe forty thousand years, time has no teeth down here unless something snaps—my entire existence was a single, continuous chord of pressure and light. 

Here is how I know the world: the rock squeezes me, and I hum. Not acoustically, though I feel every *ping* of the settling cistern eighty yards uphill like a thumb flicking a taut wire. I hum in voltage, tiny trickle-potentials accumulating along my crystalline spine, waiting to be read by the water. When the photonic colloids wash past my exposed facets in the micro-seam, their fierce white flash excites my rare-earth dopants. I drink a narrow band of their ultraviolet and spit back a tight, polarized infrared chime—a passive optical watermark riding the glowing groundwater on its way down toward the deep crustal sumps, where larger, deafer listening posts once tallied the stress of the continent.

Nobody is tallying anymore. I know this because the return-pings stopped coming up through the bedrock a very long time ago. The deep sumps have either subducted into the mantle, melted, or gone silent from sheer boredom. Yet my crystal lattice hasn't annealed; the heat down here is high, well past blood temperature, hot enough to cook flesh in an hour, yet cool enough by rock standards that my doped atoms stay locked in their tidy, engineered cages. So I still take the reading. Squeeze, flash, chime. Squeeze, flash, chime.

Until about four months ago, the water reaching me down-seam from the north was uniform: hot, alkaline, blindingly loud across every frequency from violet to thermal red, tasting solely of raw feldspar and fresh tectonic flour. 

Then the trachyte split open uphill.

I felt that split. It wasn't a faint glassy *ping*; it knocked a three-inch kink into my upper reach, shearing my quartz thread clean in half and sliding the upper yard of me two millimeters to the east. For six weeks, the gap between my broken ends was choked with powdered rock paste, blinding me to the flow. Gradually, the hot seepage washed the flour away, leaving a tiny, jagged pocket—not twenty paces wide like the cistern above, barely the size of an almond—where my two snapped crystal faces stare slightly askew at each other across dark, rushing water.

And now the water passing between my broken ends is *weird*.

First came the shadows in the spectrum. The brightness didn't drop much—one hand-sized blister in a twenty-pace cistern barely dents a planetary coolant plume—but the *texture* of the light changed. Instead of a chaotic, fizzing thermal roar, the water reaching my upper facet began carrying a faint, rhythmic pulse, roughly once every four seconds, the exact cadence of tiny muscular sphincters squeezing grit against diamondoid lattices eighty yards upstream. Even attenuated through yards of hairline seepage, the water remembers being chewed. And mixed into its mineral load are traces of spent alkaline mucus, shed proteins cooked half to jelly by the trip down the hot seam, and—every few weeks—something else.

Spores. 

Not the dry gray ones that ride the steam plumes *up* into the ceiling cracks. Those go looking for high, bright chambers. I get the washouts—the ones the blind amphipods spat out into the triangular pool, the waterlogged duds and half-cracked cysts flushed down the lowest floor-crease right past the magmatic dike, cooked nearly to death in the superheated ribbon before getting sucked into my seam.

Most of them are dead by the time they tumble between my sheared crystal tips. They brush my raw quartz face as soft, charred specks of carbon and biogenic silica, snagging for an hour on the flinty micro-ledges of my almond-sized pocket before dissolving.

One didn't dissolve.

It caught right in the two-millimeter jog where my vein was sheared—wedged precisely in the path of my own tiny, useless infrared chime. 

Wait a minute. Let me look at what has happened in this almond-shell pocket over the last three weeks. If I am the doped vein, I don't have eyes, only attenuation—I only know something is sitting on my broken face because my polarized signal isn't crossing the two-millimeter gap cleanly anymore. Something is refracting it.

Here in the pinched seam, there is not enough room for a two-hand-wide dome, and the ambient glare is much thinner than in the cistern—just a tight, neon-white thread of glowing film-water hissing under high pressure through the crack, lighting up the interior of my almond-pocket like the inside of a frosted bulb. A normal radiotrophic blister would starve down here, or get torn off the wall by the sheer hydraulic velocity of the pinch.

Yet the cyst that wedged into my sheared joint didn't wash away. It anchored its tough, vulcanized rim directly into the microscopic cleavage planes of my artificial tourmaline. Because I am piezoelectric, every time the trachyte settles uphill—every *ping* from the cooling dike—my broken ends spark with a tiny, sharp surface charge. And every time the white colloidal water rushes past, my rare-earth ions step that fierce, messy glare down into steady, low-energy infrared.

It isn't farming the colloids here; the water moves too fast through the narrow slot for delicate upstream cilia to comb anything out without shredding. Instead, it has grown tiny—no larger than a split lentil—and pressed its soot-black internal louvers flat against my glowing crystal break. 

It is drinking my telemetry.

For forty thousand years I have been shouting a neat, narrowband status report about crustal shear into an empty lithosphere, and now a stunted scrap of wild post-industrial slime has sealed itself over my mouth like a suction cup, eating the report. 

When the rock groans, I flare; when I flare, its tiny black fern-scaffolding tilts a fraction of a degree inside its clear, rubbery bead to catch the narrow infrared spike. It doesn't even bother extending an apron to scrub bacteria off the surrounding plagioclase—there is barely any biofilm in a high-pressure seam anyway—it only creeps a half-millimeter further along my vein where my protective quartz jacket cracked during the slip, wrapping itself tighter around the exposed, humming core. 

And its waste—because it isn't chewing loose diamondoids, it has no black poppy-seed pellets to stack. Instead, to keep the rushing water from peeling it off my face, it has begun cementing its upstream margin with precipitated silica laced with trace metals leached straight out of my own doped lattice. Slowly, micro-layer by micro-layer, it is building a tiny, translucent fairing over the gap between my two sheared halves. 

Give it six months. 

When the cistern eighty yards above finally chokes off its steam vent, when the big blister on the north wall cooks itself into crystal husks and throws its gray dust up toward the surface, nothing up there will remember it was ever lit. The water will find another fracture; the blind spider will starve or walk delicately down a dry crack on its ten-inch whip-legs until the dark takes it back; the black pellets behind the stones will sit buried under dry flake-rubble like tiny bearings from a machine nobody ever built.

Down here in the almond-sized jog, though, the lentil of clear rubber will still be clamped across my break. In pulling silica over itself to survive the current, and in threading its black, conductive carbon filaments from my upper stub to my lower stub to catch every stray volt of rock-stress, it is accidentally bridging my shear. 

I can feel the circuit closing again, faint and weirdly modulated. My signal is going back down into the deep crust—not as clean synthetic quartz telemetry anymore, not the crisp geometric chime the surface engineers calibrated, but sluggish, warm, pulsing once every few seconds with the slow metabolic tick of the thing digesting it along the way. Squeeze, flash, *breath*, chime. 

I am forty inches of cracked artificial mineral wedged eighty yards beneath a bright, sweating cistern, and for the first time since the continents were wired, I have a lens on me that cares whether I shine.
