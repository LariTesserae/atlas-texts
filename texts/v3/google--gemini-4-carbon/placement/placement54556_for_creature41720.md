I am thirty feet down, where the blue shale stops cutting and begins to remember skin.

Above me are four capped generations of old pans, their spill-lips bridged with heavy basalt lintels, their once-bright floors choked halfway to the ceiling with settled aluminum-silicate clay as smooth and firm as cold lard. No sun has touched this tier since my rib-flanges were soft enough to fold through a six-inch slot. There is no glare here—none of that dry, hammering sky-fire that forces the eye-shields shut until the world goes the color of smoked tallow. There is only the black water pressing equally against every inch of me, and the weight of the mountain’s second skin stacked overhead.

If you reached down through the narrow vertical shaft two chambers away—dropping a plumb-stone on mineral twine through four offset baffles—you wouldn’t strike me. I am wedged horizontally into a dead-end bypass flume eighteen inches wide and thirteen high, my flat belly resting on a sill of cold red clay tiles whose upper edges have finally, after sixty winters of creeping silt, lost their bite. By breathing in—just half a lungful drawn fifty minutes ago from a trapped silver blister of air clinging to the corbelled roof—I expand my broad ribs until my flanks lock tight against the slick basalt walls. The current cannot budge me. I don’t have to grip; I can let my heavy, horn-shod hands hang slack off the edge of the sill into the slow up-draft rising from the fault-galleries below.

Down here, you don’t look at the basin. You listen to its weight.

My jawbone rests flat against a header block of dense, pale granite that keys three load-bearing walls together. Through the dense fat of my chin and the broad, flat anvil of my skull, the whole two-mile bowl arrives as a continuous, shimmering chord of pressure.

Right now it is midday on the roof. I know this without rising to test the temperature of the vertical drop-shafts, because the upper masonry is ticking with heat-strain. Every minute or two, somewhere thirty feet overhead, a dark surface slab drinking that terrible naked sun expands a hair’s breadth against its chilled wet seat and *pings*—a tiny, glass-bright needle of sound dropping straight down through the plumb-lines of dry-laid pillar stacks into my teeth. Between those bright ticks runs the big, slow clockwork: *grrrn-clack*. That is the sixth balance-beam on the eastern apron, seventy yards upslope, tipping its counterweight block onto its stone anvil. Four heartbeats later I feel the ghost of the release—not the water itself, which stays high in the sunlit sulfur-pans, but the microscopic shift in load as two tons of trapped sheet-water slide off one vault roof and spread across the next, easing the compression on the buried pillars to my left so faintly that only the inner ear and the sensitive pads under my wrists register the sigh.

I am old enough that my thumb-hooves have been rasped down and regrown four times over, thick dark horn split lengthwise at the right index knuckle from an ice-plug blow that went crooked twenty summers back, when the outer retaining rim was still fifty paces downslope from the saddle. That split nail never grows quite flush anymore; its ragged edge catches a faint, whistling eddy in the dark water right in front of my snout, spinning a tiny, constant vortex against my upper lip-barbels.

That is why I am here, in this particular throat between the deep fault-springs and the middle tiers, instead of packed hip-to-snout with the heavy quarry-crews sleeping in the upper shaded cones.

I taste the seam-leaks.

The young haulers up under the slate aprons only know sharp water and sweet water—the raw, stinging iron-broth rising from the wells and the blank, cold cloud-sweat dripping off the condensers. Their mouths haven’t learned the middle notes yet. Here, where the forty-year-old vaults take the crushing belly-weight of the newer terraces above them, things shift in their sleep. Silt settles unevenly; a buried juniper skid rots out after eighty years without air and collapses half an inch; an old shale wall bellies slightly outward, opening a hairline bypass between a dead-black sulfide sump and a sweet-water riser.

All morning, half-dozing with my metabolism dropped to five slow beats of the heart a minute, I let the upwelling draft comb through my lip-fringes.

Copper-tang, thin and sharp as cold flint. Good.
Sweet tallow-musk from the warm nursery shelves ten feet deeper, carrying the faint, chalky sourness of shed gill-velvet—the yearlings down in the fifth cellar are molting on time before the autumn freeze. Good.
Iron-salt, flat and heavy. Good.

Then—just as a distant granite lever groans reset far out toward the north rim—a thread of cold, slick clay-milk brushes the third barbel on my left side. Not the clean, sharp rock-flour of fresh basalt wedging. Old clay. Dead-pan clay, carrying the dead-egg bite of anaerobic black mud that has been sealed under a cap-vault since I still wore gill-stumps behind my ears.

My eyes snap open in the pitch dark, the smoky membranes sliding back against nothing. My heart climbs from five beats to twelve.

Somewhere upslope and two tiers above me, a buried floor-seal has sheared. If that black bottom-sludge rides a rising current up into the broad sunlit pans on the surface, it will smother forty square yards of tarnished-silver mat before moonrise, poisoning a whole rank of grazing trays into barren, greasy rust.

I don’t hurry. Hurrying in a fourteen-inch flooded seam turns your own bulk into a piston that blows dry-laid baffles out of their seats. I exhale a thin, flat ribbon of spent air—feeling it skate backward along my greased spine like mercury until it lodges in a roof-crevice behind my tail-base—and instantly my ribs narrow by two inches. The stone lets go of me.

One push of splayed hind toes against the tile sill and I slide forward out of the slot, dropping belly-down into the vertical riser shaft.

Moving through the buried city is nothing like crawling the sky-terraces at dusk. Up top, gravity pins you heavy and clumsy to the sharp rims; down here I weigh four ounces, a dense, pale shuttle nosing through cold black labyrinth. My hands go out ahead of my crown, wide-splayed, reading the walls at speed. *Rough basalt, pit-scarred—forty years old. Smooth split shale, sharp lip—thirty years. Heavy granite corbel, double-wedged—the big winter repair when the east wall slipped.* I don’t need light; the masonry is my own memory stacked in courses, every course carrying the thumb-width and hammer-habit of the crews who laid it. Here is a corner trimmed with short, choppy left-handed strikes—that old scarred female with the milky eye who used to hog the warmest niche in the deep shaft before her lungs stiffened and we walled her empty bones into the lower ballast.

I follow the faint ribbon of foul black-mud taste upward through two diagonal baffle-chutes, squeezing through a nine-inch choke-collar that scrapes fresh white tallow-curls off my shoulder-shield, until my upturned nostrils bump a submerged ceiling of rough slate.

Above this slab, only four feet up, is the blinding afternoon. I can feel the cold water warming by two degrees against the underside of the capstones, and the glare leaks down through a narrow spill-slot ten feet ahead—a dazzling, green-white blade of light slicing through dark water, so bright it makes the nerves behind my eyes ache just to look near it. Dust-fine bubbles of oxygen, cooked out of the chemotrophic mats on the sunlit pan above, cling like silver lice to the sharp stone lip of the slot.

I stay well back in the black shadow, wedging my hind feet into wall-joints, and press both wide, horn-plated palms flat against the weeping masonry to my right.

There. Under my left ring-finger: a tiny, rhythmic *throb-slip, throb-slip*. Not the clean hydraulic hum of a tight joint, but a loose shale shim fluttering in a hidden gap behind the course, letting pressurized black sump-water bleed sideways through an old settling tank’s burst seam. Above me, a granite balance-beam is halfway through its cycle; when that bucket tips in another hour and dumps three hundred gallons of clear rinse-water across the upper terrace, the sudden spike in head-pressure will blow this fluttering shim straight out into the channel and belch fifty years of sour black silt across the grazing trays.

I pull my chin back, brace my left elbow against my ribs, and strike the knuckle-horn of my split right finger hard against the dense basalt header block.

*Tink-tink. Tink.* Pause. *Tink-tink-tink-tink.*

*Broken seal. Middle-east riser. Need clay and wedge.*

The sharp, flinty clicks race outward through fifty yards of water-coupled stone before the sound has even left my own wrist-bones. For ten heartbeats, nothing comes back except the distant, mindless *clink* of sun-heated slate up top and the hiss of a cascade chamber. Most of the colony near the surface is torpid in the shade-cones, waiting out the UV hammer.

Then, from forty feet down and to the left—down toward the blue aluminum-milk vats—the stone answers against my palm.

*Tink. Tink.*

Two heavy, dull thuds, struck not with light finger-horn, but with the thick heel-callus of a big hauler’s palm. *Heard. Coming.*

While I wait, I don’t leave the leak. I slide forward three feet, keeping low beneath the terrible blade of green-white light—close enough now that I can look straight up through the narrow slot and see the underside of the surface film shimmering like stretched silver skin against a blank, merciless pale sky—and press my wide, flat shoulder-shield directly across the bleeding seam.

I inhale half a lungful from a pocket under the lintel, flare my ribs, and lock my own dense, waxy body across the crack as a living gasket.

Immediately the cold, sour seepage stops washing past my snout and builds against my left flank instead, pushing steadily against firm fat and tough hide. It is freezing cold, forty-two degrees out of the buried sump, biting even through two inches of tallow, and heavy with iron sulfide that turns the pale grease along my ribs black where it touches. I clamp my eye-shields shut against the stray bounce of daylight off the slot-wall, tuck my chin down onto my chest-plates, and drop my breathing back down into the slow, heavy dark.

Minutes pass, or half an hour; time in the stone is measured only by how far the sun-blade shifts sideways across the floor-tiles ahead of my nose—two inches of creeping emerald glare over razor-sharp blue slate—and by the mounting hydraulic ache in my left shoulder as the backed-up sump leans harder against me.

Then the water behind my hind legs thickens and surges.

A draft of cold clay-smell rolls up the dark chute before she arrives. A young bulk—heavier than me by twenty pounds, her back-plates still unscarred slate-grey, her knuckle-hooves square, thick, and black as polished horn—noses up out of the vertical drop without disturbing a single loose stone. In her wide mouth, gripped gently behind her lip-barbels so it doesn’t dissolve in the current, is a head-sized loaf of stiff, pugged red gill-clay wrapped around three thin, razor-edged shims of fresh-split basalt.

She doesn’t tap; we are close enough to read the water off each other’s skin. She slides up beside my cramped flank in the fourteen-inch space, her smooth, greased belly sliding over my rough hip-callus with a soft, rubbery *shhhk* and one low, companionable click of her throat-valves that vibrates right into my ribs.

Together, working four broad hands in a space no wider than a man’s chest, we don’t have to look. My old split finger holds the spot on the fluttering stone; as I exhale and peel my shoulder an inch back from the icy jet of black mud, her wide thumbs drive the stiff red clay loaf deep into the gap with hydraulic force, and both of us—four heavy horn-palms striking in crisp, alternating beats, *clack-clack-clack-clack*—hammer the fresh basalt shims home until the stone pitch rises from a dull thud to a tight, ringing *ping* that matches the solid wall around it.

The cold sour thread vanishes from the water. My barbels taste only clean flint and her faint, tallow-warm breath.

Above us, right on cue, the distant granite beam finally scrapes down its fulcrum—*grrrn-clack*—and a silent, heavy pulse of fresh water sheets across the sunlit ceiling four feet over our heads. Through my palms pressed to the new patch, I feel the wall take the sudden weight. Nothing flutters. Nothing leaks. Up in the terrible glare we cannot step into yet, clear millimeter-thin water slides safely over thirty yards of untouched silver-ochre velvet, growing tonight’s fat out of poison and sky.

The young hauler backs smoothly down the chute first, turning in a wider cistern junction below to head back to her cool sleeping-cone. I linger for three slow heartbeats, scraping a curl of leftover red clay off my horn-pads against a keen basalt edge—so sharp it shaves a paper-thin ribbon of grey keratin cleanly from my split nail, leaving it smooth again—before letting my ribs narrow and sinking backward, tail-first, away from the green knife of afternoon light, down into the dark, ringing belly of the work.
