I am down in the blackest part of the bowl’s belly, wedged into the three-sided cleft where the Old Potter’s Curve meets the raw, sweating granite of the spire’s throat. 

It is dark enough here that having eyes is mostly an old habit. Even if mine weren't filming over at the edges like tallow cooling in a gourd, there would be nothing to see—just the faint, greasy sheen of a braided leather tether two spans above my head catching a stray leak of purple twilight from three tiers up. Everything else is weight, warmth, and the continuous, dry *shhh-click* of things living in the dark.

I am not quite old enough to carry the horn rod yet, though my knees pop like dry pods when I unfold them after three hours in a root-cleft. Call me an apprentice to the weight, or a chink-minder. My belt holds a flat bone spatula worn thin as a thumbnail, a pouch of dry, powdered lichen, and two wax-stoppered gourds of green vine-gum. My left cheekbone is pressed flat against the underside of a dead ironwood beam thick as a child’s waist, and my bare toes are hooked around the warm, pebbled tail of a vent-lizard that hasn't moved since yesterday’s meal-steaming. Neither of us minds the other. Its scales are dry as slate and slightly warmer than my skin, fed by a hairline pore in the granite just beneath its belly that breathes a faint, sulfurous dampness into the pocket between my shins.

Up through the wood against my face comes the whole afternoon of the third tier. 

I can feel Maru’s heavy stone muller rolling back and forth, back and forth, crushing roasted beetle-carapaces into meal; the rhythm travels down the ironwood post, skips slightly where the old rawhide lashing has stretched half a finger’s breadth over the winterless years, and hums into my jawbone. Under that is a quicker, lighter thrum: two children shifting weight on the wicker sling-bridge to the east, passing baskets of stripped bark hand-to-hand. Deeper still, almost below hearing, is the granite itself—silent, immense, unbroken all the way down through miles of dead dust to a floor nobody has ever imagined, holding us up in its cupped palm like a single warm seed.

That slight skip in the lashing is why I am here. Old Tesh felt it four days ago from two galleries away while chewing pith with her spine against a dry-stone coping. She didn't say anything aloud; she just reached down as I squeezed past her knees with a bundle of dung-cakes and tapped twice on my collarbone, then drew a slow circle on my shoulder blade with a blunt, dusty thumb. *Check the east fork under the mulling-stone. Something is turning.*

It has taken me two days of slow wedging to trace it. You don't rush into the under-scaffolds. Down here the air is thick as broth, sour-sweet with centuries of shed moth-scales, lizard musk, and the damp, earthy breath of the rock pores. The blind beetles own the angles; if you shove a knee forward carelessly you crush twenty of them into foul, oily paste—wasted meat, wasted damp, a smear that draws the biting mites out of the moss-mats. So you move like sap. You press the back of your wrist against a cluster of beetles and wait four breaths until they unhook their dry little tarsal claws and trundle sideways onto the lichen curtain, clicking faintly like seeds shaken in a closed fist. Then you slide your elbow into the warm spot they left.

My fingers find the trouble in the pitch dark. Where the ironwood beam forks into a natural cradle of stacked, wind-polished boulders, one of the chinking-wedges—packed in there by my grandfather’s sister, maybe, or someone before her—has dried out completely. A tiny draught of rising vent-air, shifting its path as mineral crusts slowly choke one pore in the stone below and open another, has been licking at that seam for ten years, stealing its moisture molecule by molecule until the tough lichen-paste turned to brittle gray biscuit. Now, every time Maru leans her broad chest onto the muller overhead, the smooth boulder rocks by the thickness of an eyelash, rubbing rawhide against dry wood. Left alone for another season of skin-sheds, the lashing will fray, the boulder will roll half an inch into the root-ball of the big russet hawser-vine beside me, and three families’ thresholds will sag crooked.

I unstopper my smaller gourd under the fold of my leather apron so the warm air won't skin the gum over, scoop a walnut-sized gob of resin onto my bone blade, and draw a wad of dry lichen-powder into my mouth. 

For twenty minutes I do nothing at all except sit in the dark, pressed between stone, timber, and reptile, working the dry dust into my own spit with my tongue until it forms a stiff, bitter cud. A giant moth hanging upside down from the wicker underside inches from my ear vibrates its thorax in a long, purring shiver; the velvety edge of its wing brushes my temple like dry suede. Somewhere two walls away, someone is singing to a restless baby—just a low, two-note drone pitched right at the bottom of the throat, *hmmmm-uh, hmmmm-uh*, blending so seamlessly into the rattle of ten thousand stridulating wings in the upper thickets that you only know it’s human because it pauses every thirty heartbeats to draw breath.

When the cud in my cheek is tacky and warm, I spit it directly onto the tip of my thumb—pressing my palm immediately over it—and mix it by touch with the vine-gum on the bone blade, right against the seam so not a thread of damp goes to the air. 

As Maru pauses her grinding up above—leaning back, probably, to wipe the silt-sweat from the crease of her neck with a dry moss pad—the beam lifts that microscopic fraction of an eyelash. I slide the bone blade in, pack the warm, gummy paste deep into the crescent between smooth granite and ironwood, and seal the outer lip with a smear of rendered lizard-fat from the tin at my hip so the vent-draught won't drink it dry again in my lifetime. When Maru’s weight comes back down on the muller—*thrum-scrape, thrum-scrape*—the skip is gone. The vibration passes straight through wood, paste, and boulder into the living spire as one solid piece.

I stay another long while with my cheek on the timber, just making sure. Down here, when your work is right, you disappear into the mechanics of the town; my own ribs feel like one more curved strut taking a tiny share of the load between the boulder and the post behind my kidneys.

Eventually my bladder tells me it’s time to climb back up toward the clay cistern-jars by the nursery-shrubs. I unhook my toes from the lizard’s tail, tap twice on the timber above my head just in case someone is crouched on the low coping above the crawl-hole, and begin backing out inch by inch, sliding on greased hips and calloused elbows over polished stone curves that shine like black oil where a hundred years of chink-minders have rubbed them smooth.

Emerging onto the second-tier walkway is like breaking the surface of a warm, dim pond. Up here, the purple sky shows in jagged, hand-sized patches through four overlapping ceilings of bloated succulent leaves and slung rope-nets. The noise swells slightly—cliff-birds clacking their hooked bills in the eaves, the soft *pat-pat* of someone shaping dung-cakes one tier up, the low chest-murmur of two net-menders sitting knee-to-knee across the three-foot alley. 

Halfway to the ladder, old Kael is sitting folded into a dished stone niche, his broad, powdery back wedged outward into the path, his face pressed flat against the smooth elephant-gray boulder of the house wall. His shoulders aren't shaking, nothing shows at all, though everyone knows the woman who shared his sleeping mat and his eating-cloth died four days ago and went into the deep mulch-pits beneath the central shrubs. There isn't room to go around him—to my left, the wicker rail drops twelve feet into a dense tangle of waxy vines and roosting gliders—so I lay one steady palm on the heavy curve of his shoulder for balance, step my wide bare foot carefully over his ankles without brushing his skin, and swing onto the bowed ironwood rungs of the ladder. Under my palm, his flesh is warm, solid, dusted with gray mineral silt, utterly still. Neither of us exists to the other for those three breaths. He is stone until he turns around.

I climb all the way up toward the东-rim nursery to wash the bitter lichen-taste out of my mouth with a sliver of sour pith, and because after half a day in the black root-clefts, even eyes like mine want a little stretch.

Near the top, the town thins out from masonry to raw thicket, and then from thicket to naked, scoured granite. One of the silt-harvesters—my cousin’s boy, wide-hipped and quiet, greased heavily around the eyes with yellow fat against the dry—is just hauling himself backward over the smooth lip, his rawhide waist-tether slacking as he slides down into the shelter of the outermost barrel-shrubs. His basket is half-full of gray, sticky resin-threads furred with fresh sky-dust and tiny, dormant red spores combed out of the purple murk.

He catches my eye—wide, dark, barely a crescent of yellow white showing—and shifts his chin a fraction toward the rim before ducking down into the warm, clicking gloom of the leaves, rubbing his chapped lips against the back of his forearm.

I don't clip onto a tether; I don't go far enough out to need one. I just crawl on belly and knees up the last warm, rounded shoulder of granite until my chin rests on the smooth curve where the bowl ends and the rest of creation falls away.

There is nothing out there. Just the bruised violet dome above, dirty yellow where it meets the slate-colored sea of suspended silt below, motionless and soundless for ten thousand miles. The rock under my ribs is smooth as a worn spoon, without a crack or a pebble, plunging straight down until the haze swallows its gray flank. Immediately, I feel the air outside the bowl touch my face—cold, thin, terrifyingly greedy. Within three blinks I can feel the moisture stiffening at the rims of my nostrils and pulling tight at the corners of my wide eyes. And the quiet out there isn't peaceful; it’s a vast, dead suction, a place where nothing leans on anything else, where a dropped breath just scatters into dust and never comes back to feed a leaf. My chest gives a strange, hollow lurch inside my broad ribs, as if my lungs might float right up out of my throat if nobody’s elbow is pressing against my side.

Five breaths is plenty. Maybe four.

I close my eyes to save their damp, push backward with my palms on the polished stone, and slide feet-first back down into the hollow. Instantly, the warm air rising from the deep vents wraps around my calves, my hips, my shoulders, smelling of sage-tallow, steamed tuber, dry beetle-shell, and old sweat. My hip bumps a thick, waxy vine; a blind beetle drops onto my collarbone and begins its slow, polite trundle toward my braid; three feet below me, behind a curtain of gray beard-lichen, two voices murmur low in their chests while a flint scraper goes *tap-tap-scrape, tap-tap-scrape* against a stretched hide.

I wedge myself into the fork of an ironwood post beside the nursery-trough, tuck my chin down, and wait for someone to pass me a thumb-sized plug of succulent flesh beneath a leather cloth, glad to be back inside the warm, crowded dark where nothing falls.
