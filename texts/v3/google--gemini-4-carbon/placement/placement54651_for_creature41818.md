I am forty-six, which means I belong to neither tribe. 

I came down the freight-hoist in a fleece-lined sling when I was four years old, crying wet, catastrophic tears into a makeshift chamois bib while an exhausted technician held a desiccant canister two inches from my face to catch the salt before I could drip on the landing deck. I do not remember rain—not truly—though twice a year, right before waking in the curtained dark of my bunk, I have a fleeting, vertiginous sensation of cold pellets striking the crown of my head, and I wake up reaching for a hood I do not own. Yet I cannot hear an old Founder say a plan has *washed out* without knowing, in some buried, pre-verbal marrow, the exact heavy drag of mud pulling at a heel. And when the twenty-year-old apprentices sign *rot-mouth* behind an elder’s back across the bright expanse of the Seventh Plaza, I understand their revulsion, too. 

Maybe that is why I ended up where the valley listens to itself.

You will find me three quarters of a mile east of the central trench, out on the pale, gently cambered plain they call the South Sheet, though there is nothing on it yet but light, brass stilts, and the ghost of a freight depot scheduled to exist next autumn. My workshop doesn’t even have walls—walls cast shadows, and out on a nursery plain, shadows are unpaid debt. Instead, I work beneath an open pavilion of thin aluminum ribs perched six feet off the root-plate on spider-legs, shaded only by a rotating slat-roof that clicks six degrees eastward every twelve minutes so the lattice beneath me never goes hungry for long.

On the brass municipal rolls, my trade is listed as **harmonic-surveyor**, though the riggers just sign me *tuning-clerk*—two fingers tapped against the collarbone, then drawn out like a plucked string.

Before a wire-rigger ever clips a copper line into a voltage feed, someone has to prove the ground wants to take the weight. *Lignin-bone* is an obedient creature, mostly, but it is still a creature. Underneath the smooth, cream-colored plain of the street, the root-plate goes down ninety feet before it meets the chewed-out limestone floor of the ancient cavern, and down in that dark, unlit underside, the older fibers cure, stiffen, and pull against tectonic drift. Sometimes two growing neighborhoods pull the root-plate between them tight as a drumhead; sometimes a deep gas-vent shifts half a yard, starving a subterranean arch until it hollows out like dry meringue. Build a five-story foundry over a meringue pocket, and three years later the whole mesa drops four inches in the middle of a shift, snapping every quartz water-seal in the district.

So I walk the empty plains with a striker-cart and a box of forks.

Right now, my boots—size nine, double-stitched moth-felt, the brass heel-brushes worn down to stubborn, splayed stubs that go *scritch-thck* instead of *scritch-tick*—are planted astride an iron grounding-rail. Count to sixty; two slow paces left. My shadow, sharp as scissor-cut black paper under the noon-glare of Overhead Pipe Nine, slides cleanly off a patch of pale young lattice no thicker than linen cardstock. Immediately, two wire-skinks dart out from beneath the stilt-foot of my cart onto the newly lit spot, their keratin plates clicking like castanets as they nip at a stray, curled fibril my shade annoyed into twisting.

I crouch—knees popping dryly inside my trousers, no worse a sound than the floor makes—and set the brass foot of my primary resonator against the husk. 

My gloves have the index and middle fingertips cut out, the skin underneath rubbed silver-black with dry graphite so I don’t drag static across my instruments, and waxed heavy around the nail-beds where the thirty-four-degree air likes to split the quick. When I pull the spring-hammer on the cart, a hard rubber mallet drops once—*thock*—against the root-plate.

To someone standing twenty yards away, nothing happens. The porous floor drinks the hit instantly. There is no echo, no boom.

To me, with a cured heartwood rod clamped lightly between my gloved knuckles and pressed hard against the mastoid bone just behind my ear-seal, the whole South Sheet rings like a dry gourd. 

*Hnnnnn—tck-tck—hmmmm.*

I close my eyes behind my smoked-quartz lenses. The glare vanishes, turning into a dull, reddish warmth against my eyelids, and the valley arranges itself entirely by friction. Half a mile west, Pneumatic Mill Four goes *shhh-thump, shhh-thump*, pushing heavy carbon down the main trunk. High overhead—maybe two thousand feet up—a drift of cinder-moths cuts across a thermal, and I don’t hear them, of course, not directly, but I feel the microscopic flutter of the light dimming for half a second on the skin of my forehead where the spore-wax is thin. And down beneath my boots, forty feet deep in the blind structural dark of the root-plate, the rubber mallet’s pulse travels outward through ten million braided ochre tubes, hits a dense knot of old iron-rich limestone the plant hasn't finished digesting yet, and bounces back flat by a quarter-tone.

Good bone. Solid tension all the way to the vent-wall. 

I open my eyes, take two paces right—shadow sliding off the skinks, who scatter clicking—and mark the coordinate on a slate of roughened aluminum with a stick of soft copper. Copper lines on grey metal; nothing here is ink, nothing is wet, nothing smears unless you rub your thumb across a fresh static charge.

What I love—what keeps me out on the unbuilt sheets long after my zeolite cartridge has gone warm and soggy-heavy inside my brass muzzle—is that nothing in this basin is dead, and yet nothing is messy about living. 

My mother was a Founder, a botanist brought down from some foggy coastal institute she refused to talk about once she realized she was never going back up. When I was twelve, before she died, she showed me a textbook she’d smuggled down in a vacuum-tinned cylinder. The paper itself had had to be baked bone-dry in an airlock before the wardens let her keep it, so the pages cracked like thin biscuit when she turned them, and the color plates of *temperate rainforests* had faded to strange, jaundiced greens and purples. She pointed a bare, slightly arthritic finger at a picture of a fallen nurse-log covered in wet moss, bracket fungi, beetles, and shiny black slugs, and inside our sealed amber-cell her bare voice went thick and wobbly. *Look how generous it was,* she whispered—her wet plosives sounding terribly loud in our curved aluminum kitchen. *Everything eating everything else. Nothing wasted.*

I remember staring at the glossy, cracked page and feeling my stomach tighten with a polite, helpless dread. It looked like an infection. It looked like a damp-strike nobody had come running with lances to cauterize.

Out here on the South Sheet, nothing rots to feed the next thing; it simply holds the next thing up.

"Clerk!" 

Not a shout—nobody shouts—just a crisp, mid-range woodwind note carrying flat and clean across eighty yards of open cream floor, stripped of breathiness by a mica reed.

I look up. Coming down the slight rise from the transit viaduct is Joric, one of the Husk-Born damp-wardens on the noon-to-eight circuit. He is twenty-two, six feet tall, and walks with that eerie, gliding, flat-hipped grace the valley-bred kids all have—the *basin-rock* built right into his stride so even when he’s moving forward, he drifts slightly diagonally across the grain of the floor, never shading the same seam line for two steps running. On his left shoulder rides his whisper-hound, Crimp, a long, snowy-white noodle of a beast with blind pink eye-seams and translucent ear-sails the size of rhubarb leaves, veined with pale blue cartilage.

At thirty yards, Joric’s right hand flicks up from his coat hem in rigger-cant, copper rings flashing white under the arc-lamps: *Foundry-line. Subtle-sour. You-hear?*

I keep my mouth shut behind my muzzle—my cartridge is three hours old, and talking wastes the dry-back—and flick back with my graphite-blackened fingertips: *Dry-below. Forty-feet-true. Where-sour?*

We meet beside my stilt-cart. Before he even stops moving, he angles his left forearm out; I tap my copper wrist-cuff to his. *Snap.* A tiny violet needle jumps between the braided threads with an ozone *pip* I can smell right through my zeolite filter, sharp and clean as cold pennies. Only then do I reach into my tool-pouch and pass him the spare acoustic rod.

Up close, above the spun-brass curve of his muzzle, Joric’s cheekbones are waxed smooth as candle-tallow, without a single crease from windburn or squinting. Beneath his smoked side-shields, I know his eyes are grey and enormous. Meanwhile, Crimp leans half her narrow, weasel-muscled length out of his wicker shoulder-cradle to investigate my collar. She doesn't sniff—sniffing is a wet-world habit, drawing damp air over delicate mucus—instead, her dry, velvety split-nose quivers silently while those huge, papery ear-sails cup forward toward my chest, listening to the steady, muffled *lub-dub* of my wet heart inside its ribs, and the faint, crisp *tick* of cooling brass on my coat buttons.

"Not rot," Joric says. His voice through the mica reed sounds like a small, dry harmonium, pleasant and completely flat. He points two long fingers upward, not at the floor, but forty feet up the flank of a solitary, hexagonal relay-pylon standing a hundred yards off—the only vertical thing in half a mile. "Witch-burl. High eaves. Spark-mites bridged the third stay-wire."

I squint up through my dark quartz lenses. Sure enough, right where a shining copper guide-wire clips into the pale ochre corner of the pylon, a strange, frothy excrescence of rogue lattice has bloomed out into empty air over the last few days—delicate, disorganized lacework looking like dried sea-foam or blown bone, robbing current from the main rib. Beneath it, starved of voltage, two yards of structural seam look chalky and thin.

"Kids missed it on Tuesday sweep," I say, feeling the mica reed buzz faintly against the dry tip of my nose. Two paces left; Joric shifts with me automatically, half a beat behind, like we’re dancing to a metronome neither of us has to count. "Hollow?"

"Crimp hissed twice," he says proudly, reaching up a gloved knuckle to stroke the base of the hound’s skull; she flattens one ear-sail in dry bliss, scattering three tiny flakes of white dander into the cold air. "Static-beetles moved into the burl already. Ten, maybe twelve. If they chew down into the un-cured pith before the wind turns, they’ll bleed sap-gas."

Not wet sap—the husk has no liquid blood—but raw, unpolymerized carbon-ester pockets that smell like scorched vinegar and leave brittle, grey blisters in a beam.

"Give me ten minutes," I sign, saving my breath now, and unclip my lightweight fiberglass testing-pole from the spider-cart. 

This is the quiet seam of the town where I live best: not heroic like the wire-riggers swinging fifty yards out over the central trench with tuning forks in their teeth, and not saintly like the lamp-gleaners baking under the great quartz suns miles overhead while moth-velvet snows around their gondolas. Just a middle-aged surveyor standing in thirty-five-degree artificial noon with a twenty-two-year-old boy and a blind white predator, telescoping a twenty-foot dry-pole up against a living tower to tap its ribs and see how badly the mites have confused its geometry.

Halfway through my third tap—*tick, tick, hmmmm*—my eardrums suddenly pull outward.

A tiny, soft *pop* inside my head, right behind the eyes.

On Joric’s shoulder, Crimp whips her narrow head due north. Both veined ear-sails flare wide and stiff as drying parchment, turning translucent pink against the overhead glare. A quarter-mile away, along the unfinished cornice of the transit viaduct, three tiny dark figures in grey coats pause mid-climb and hook their elbows casually through brass stay-loops. Out on the smooth cream plain around us, twenty dinner-plate cinder-moths that were drifting lazily over my cart suddenly drop six feet, fold their heavy grey velvet wings into triangles, and hook their dry tarsal claws into the iron grounding-rail embedded by my boots, lining up nose-to-tail like grey shingles.

Five miles away at the throat of the valley, the magnetic louvers have just unlocked.

We don't speak. Nobody speaks during the turn; it’s bad luck, or maybe just bad acoustics. I plant the rubber foot of my pole between two grounding-strips, clip my cart’s tether-hook to my belt, and take one step sideways so my shadow falls across dead brass instead of living floor. Beside me, Joric tucks his chin down against his moth-felt collar, resting two fingers lightly over Crimp’s nose so the dry gust won’t chap her nostrils.

Three seconds of dead, bright library-hush. You can hear a single wire-skink ten yards away go *click-click-click* into a conduit slot.

Then the basin inhales the other way.

It hits my waxed forehead like clean, freezing bedsheets pulled straight off a line. The temperature doesn't drop, not really, it just sharpens—smelling fiercely of deep-earth carbon, static, cedar-dust, and five miles of hot copper wire. Around us, the entire sparse metropolis answers at once. The hexagonal pylon above us flexes half an inch toward the west with a long, dry, papery *shhhhhhhhh*, like ten thousand architectural blueprints being unrolled together in a vast room. Out across the bright valley, every brass and copper guide-wire strung between the solitary mesas catches the cross-draft at the exact same second and hums—a low, cold, shimmering chord in G-sharp minor that comes right up through the brass bristles in my boot-heels and vibrates behind my kneecaps. 

From the witch-burl forty feet above us, a brief, silent flurry of white spore-wax breaks loose, swirling glittering and weightless through the cold noon glare like dry snow that will never melt, never wet a sleeve, never turn to slush.

Inside my warm brass muzzle, I let out one slow breath, feeling the toasted zeolite beads catch my small, secret human dampness and keep it safe for me—then drink the cold, clean, singing air back in.

Before the chord has finished dying out of the overhead wires, the cinder-moths unhook their claws and lift silently back toward the lamps; Crimp folds her ear-sails down; Joric taps two fingers to his collarbone—*good-tone*—and glides off down the plaza; and I take two slow paces out of my own dark stencil, set the wood against my skull, and listen to the floor grow.
