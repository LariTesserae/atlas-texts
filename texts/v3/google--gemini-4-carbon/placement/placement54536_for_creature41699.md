I would not be down in the ferns, and I would not be up in the bright mica-glitter of the fifth-floor tilework. Both of those places ask for a swiftness of eye I don’t think I would possess here—either dodging the snap of a hooked yellow bill over the turnip-boxes or slicing strangler-roots away from a weeping brass union while copper frogs hammer at the eardrums. 

Put me instead at the precise seam between the brick and the tile: the third-floor gallery of Tower Six, or better yet, one of the narrow, riveted steel sheds cantilevered straight out from the eastern cliff face, forty feet above the slate floor and ten feet clear of the highest tree-fern frond. High enough that the damp falls away like a heavy coat dropped from the shoulders; low enough that the sixty-cycle hum of the transformers isn’t background noise, but the actual floor joists of my mind.

I think I am the one who minds the slip-joints and reads the stretch-gauges.

Someone has to. If a town is bolted into a wound that opens by the breadth of a fingernail every seven days, then nothing that crosses from east to west is ever the same length on Sunday as it was on Monday. The braided power cables take up their own slack on the great cast-iron drums, of course—that’s the whole reason we are here—and the hemp grocer’s loops just sag an extra fraction of an inch until someone re-ties the splice at the end of the month. 

But think of the rigid things. Think of the narrow-gauge tram rails pinned directly into the bedding planes of the bedrock. Think of the cast-iron water mains, the pneumatic return-tubes, the brass telegraph conduits, the footbridge trusses where a walkway leaps from a cliff-bracket to a tower doorway. Left to themselves, pinned fast to two halves of a parting continent, they would pull themselves apart at the rivets like cold taffy, or bow sideways until a tram flange climbed the rail and dumped a crate of porcelain insulators onto the raw slate.

So the whole basin is secretly jointed like a telescope, or the sliding brass tube of a trombone. Every fifty yards along the floor, and at the throat of every cantilevered bracket, there is a greased bronze expansion sleeve—one heavy, machined cylinder sliding slowly, relentlessly out of another on oiled leather packings. And beside every critical anchor-wall, spanning the hairline fractures where the actual splitting happens in the floor, sit the vernier bridges: heavy bars of invar steel bolted to one side of a crack and resting on oiled rollers on the other, etched with hair-fine silver graduations read through a fixed magnifying glass.

That is my trade. I am the Gauge-Walker, though the kids on stilts probably just tap two fingers sideways apart when I pass—*the Stretch-Man*—or maybe I am not a man at all, maybe I am one of those narrow, grey-haired women in a horn-toggled wool waistcoat with oil-darkened thumbs and a brass depth-micrometer hung round my neck on a flat leather lanyard (flat, stitched halfway through with cotton thread so it parts at ten pounds of pull if a grease-monkey snatches at the shiny brass). Let’s see who settles into the boots.

Yes: fifty-some years old, slightly deaf in the left ear from thirty winters of passing the Tower Three air-bleeder twice a day, walking with that long, flat-soled, rolling skater’s glide across the blinding noon plaza. My rubber-and-felt soles are thicker than most, laminated four layers deep, because my work requires me to stand dead still over a live fracture in the greystone for ten minutes at a time, feeling down through my heels to learn whether the rock today is sliding smooth like greased slate, or catching on some buried crystal deep down in the blind slot and storing up a tiny, stubborn pinch of torque before letting go.

My shed on the eastern cliff is eight feet wide and twenty feet long, riveted to four triangular steel knees pinned into the crisp ninety-degree granite. There is no soil within a hundred feet of my floorboards, only the sheer, clean scar of the rock wall six inches behind my desk—so close I can lean back from my ledger, reach through the open casement, and lay my palm flat against a pink feldspar vein whose exact twin sits forty yards below on a fallen block beside the tramline. Below my grated iron porch, the exhaust plume from Tower Six’s secondary condenser drifts sideways against the cliff, so if I look straight down between my boots, I am staring into the crown of an vertical jungle: eight-foot fern crosiers uncurling in warm mist, fat pocket-watch beetles clustered like dark cabochons along the sweating copper drip-ledge, the sudden, liquid *whir* of a climber launching itself from a fig-root onto a greased stay-wire. 

Inside the shed, though, everything is dry as salt, smelling of camphor, machine oil, hot ink, and the faint, clean flint-smell of unweathered stone.

What do I actually keep in there?

Long rolls of linen-backed graph paper weighted down at the corners with square chunks of scrap pig-iron. Three glass-cased barometers, because when the air pressure drops ahead of a stray plains squall, the cleft actually breathes—the cliffs relax inward by three-thousandths of an inch, the pawls in Towers Two and Seven take an extra four minutes to drop, and the condenser skirts drip twice as fast. And along the back shelf, a row of wide-mouthed apothecary jars filled not with chemicals, but with every odd thing I have pried out of the floor-sleves and tram-expansion boxes over thirty years: shed blue-black primary feathers stiff with graphite grease; four flat, chalk-white carapaces of young rift-crawlers that somehow climbed all the way up a sump overflow during the great brush-cleaning shutdown of ’ought-nine; a curious, walnut-sized lump of beeswax studded symmetrically with six copper rivets and a broken pearl shirt-button, tucked into a bridge-joint by some lovelorn bachelor climber and compressed into a flat square medallion over two summers of tectonic pull.

Wait—as I sit at that bolted pine table, dipping a steel nib into gall-ink that quivers in tiny concentric rings to the sixty-cycle chord of the wall, something else comes clear about what it means to be this person.

Everyone else in the cleft lives inside the *rate* of the place. They take the fingernail-a-week as an eternal constant, like sunrise or the price of Tower Four custard. They trust the slowness. 

I am the only person in the basin who sees that the earth is not a clock.

In my linen books, plotted out in thin red and black curves across twenty years of Tuesdays and Fridays, the rift has moods. It never shakes—there is no terror in it, no subterranean thunder—but it does not pull evenly. In late autumn, when the nights turn sharp and indigo-cold at four in the afternoon and the raw bedrock contracts by a whisper across the whole basin, the northern end of the cleft beside Tower One goes lazy. For three weeks running, the northern verniers barely creep half a hair, while down at the southern mouth near Tower Eleven, the bronze tram-sleeves slide apart twice as fast, gleaming with fresh silver grease-marks like a sleeve pulled back from a wrist. That means the whole town is rotating by an angle too small for a mason’s square to catch, yet large enough that the high-wire busbars between Four and Five grow tight as fiddle-strings, singing a quarter-tone higher in the afternoon wind, and the blue-backed clackers crowd onto those warm, taut spans in noticeably thicker rows.

When I see that red line steepening on my chart, I don’t ring an alarm. Nothing here happens fast enough for alarms. 

Instead, after dinner, when the sun drops behind the western rim like a heavy wooden lid and the four thousand birds tuck their yellow bills away and hand the valley over to the bronze-bowl *glock-glock* of the frogs, I put on my heavy wool jacket with the horn toggles. I pick up my folding stool and my chalk-slate, walk down the ringing iron spiral from my cliff-bracket, and stroll out across the crisp, sharp-stepped bedrock toward the pool of yellow lamplight where the chief anchor-smith from Tower Five is drinking chicory with his brother-in-law.

I don’t say a word as I walk up; nobody does. My soft rubber heels make no sound at all on the clean mica plates—only the shadow of a trowel-sized moth wheeling overhead announces me as I cross the lamp-cone. I set my stool down ten feet from the smith’s crate, hook one thumb toward the dark sky between Four and Five, hold my two forefingers parallel, and twist my right wrist outward by the smallest perceptible fraction while tapping three fingers against my knee.

*South end’s leading by three-thousandths. Slack the cross-stays on Five tomorrow.*

The smith doesn't even set down his ceramic mug. Through his thick boot-soles, now that I’ve given him the shape of it, he is already checking my math against the stone. He sits completely still for forty seconds under the solitary iron lamp, eyes half-shut against the rising steam of his chicory, listening through his ankles to the great buried drums turning behind the tall glass windows sixty yards away. Down in the black slot beneath the gratings, unseen, the pale square crustaceans are grazing sideways across the slick mineral wall, four-foot antennae combed straight by the steady phase of the current.

Then, deep inside Tower Seven across the dark flat, the three-hour pawl reaches its mark and drops home.

*CLANK.*

The single percussive wave comes cleanly through the fresh, unweathered slate—under the tram rails, under the dripping wall of dark ferns, under the wooden legs of my stool and up through my shins into my hipbones. To a stranger it would just be iron hitting iron. To the smith and me, sitting ten feet apart in the cold, bright-starred trough of rock, there is the tiniest second-echo tucked behind the ring of it—a crisp, dry *tick* from the southern anchor-bolts taking more than their share of the earth's slow sigh.

The smith opens his eyes, looks across the lamplight at me, and touches two greasy fingers to the brim of his cap. *Heard it. Tomorrow morning.*

I nod once, lean back on my canvas stool, and pull my wool lap-rug over my knees. Ten yards away at the edge of the dark foliage, a pair of round amber eyes blinks from an iron turnbuckle, watching to see if I brought a crust of custard-bread or a tarnished brass washer in my pocket. I reach in, find an old copper shim worn too thin for the tram-sleeves, and toss it underhand onto the bare stone halfway between us, where it lands with a bright, flat chime that dies instantly in the cold air, swallowed up by the frogs and the transformers and the patient, microscopic widening of the floor between my boots and the house across the square.
