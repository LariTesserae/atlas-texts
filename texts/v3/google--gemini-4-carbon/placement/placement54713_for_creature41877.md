I am down where the basalt stops being a floor and starts being a ceiling.

Not all the way down—not fifty yards under, where the blind miners dip their mandibles into the black, lightless seep above the water table. I could not breathe that heavy, drowned air for long. I am six feet under the alleyways, wedged into the dry, glassy rubric where the ropy lava cooled in hollow blisters and blistered tubes, right where the pale, cement-hard shafts of the termites punch through from below to build their spires above.

To live in the alleys is to spend your whole life negotiating eyes—hawk-shadow through a gap in the thatch, the ultraviolet flash of an angry weaver’s throat, the flat, patient pupil of the monitor draped along a root. Down here in the rubble-seam, eyes are dead weight. Mine are sunk under translucent scales the color of tallow, good only for telling whether a shaft above me has cracked open to let in the terrible, drying amber noon.

What I have instead is a jawbone shaped like a tuning fork and a belly plated in smooth, broad scutes that stay pressed against the volcanic glass.

If you turned over a slab of fractured crust—which you couldn't, because ten tons of baked red chimney and swollen copper-root have mortared it into the continent—you would see something eight inches long, flattened like a broad wooden wedge, somewhere between a skink and an earwig, though my bones are warm-ish, borrowing the steady kiln-heat trapped between the lava and the mud. My legs stick out sideways like a crab’s so I can brace against both walls of a fissure at once. My tail isn't for balance or whipping; it is short, blunt, and armored like a plug, dusty with cemented red silt so that when I back into a basalt crack and stop moving, I become just another mortar-joint in the foundation.

I am an eavesdropper on the public square.

Pressing my chin to a sharp lip of black stone, I feel six city blocks at once. Basalt rings differently than mud. The packed silt above is muffled and thick—a tortoise grinding through an arcade ten feet overhead comes down to me as a slow, rhythmic *thud-creak, thud-creak*, heavy and harmless. A root thickening against a spire is a single sharp *tink* every three or four hours as microscopic glass teeth shear away under the bark.

What I wait for is the crisp, fizzing static of a water-column.

There—four yards north, behind two walls of blistered stone. The pale workers are hauling up a heavy load. A spire up top must have split, or they are vaulting a new bridge across an alley. Through my lower jaw, the rush of ten thousand soft feet carrying wet deep-clay sounds—*feels*—different from dry maintenance crews. Wet pellets make the workers heavier; their rhythm slows by a fraction, and the soldiers flanking them tap their syringe-heads in a sharper, stiffer cadence: *tak-tak-tak-pause, tak-tak-tak-pause.*

I un-wedge my blunt tail and slide sideways through the razor-edged dark. The basalt here has never known rain, has never been tumbled in a creek bed; its fractures are still as keen as the century the crust froze, sharp enough to slice soft hide to ribbons. My scales don't scrape against it—scraping makes noise, and noise brings the thin, blind snakes down out of the midden-roots. Instead, my ribs hinge flat and my scutes glide on a dry, waxy grease I rub onto myself from old sheddings.

I reach the termite conduit where it bridges a three-inch gap between two cooling plates of lava. They have built a cylinder of pale, saliva-cured mortar right across the void, thick as a man’s wrist, pulsing faintly with the traffic inside.

I do not tear it open. If you tear a main shaft, a hundred amber-headed soldiers boil out in two seconds and glue your jaws shut with bitter resin that dries like amber and starves you in the dark. The amateurs up in the sunlit lanes—the big-eared browsers, the clumsy shovel-snouted lizards—have to smash and grab and run before the palisade forms.

I have a single hooked tooth projecting forward from the tip of my lower jaw, narrow as a cactus spine.

Bracing my splayed hind legs against the black glass, I press that single point against the mortar-tube and turn my head left, right, left, right, slower than a minute hand. Grain by cemented grain, making no rhythm different from the settling of cooling masonry, I drill a pinhole no wider than a grass stem.

Instantly, a thread of air sighs out into my dry crevice—cool, wet, thick with the cellar-reek of deep fungal combs forty yards down. It hits my nostrils like drunkenness.

A pale worker inside passes the pinhole, feels the tiny draft of dry air with one beaded antenna, and stops to plug it. It turns, bringing up a fresh pellet of wet, dark subsoil mixed with its own throat-moisture, and presses the mud into the breach from within.

Before the mud dries, my tongue—long, slender, tipped like a brush—flicks into the pinhole, steals half the wet pellet right out of the worker's mandibles, and pulls back.

The worker doesn't trigger the soldier-alarm; there was no tremor of a wall collapsing, no rush of outside light, no chemical stench of mammal breath. Just a stubborn draft that took two pellets instead of one. Another worker steps up behind the first and packs a second wet load into the hole. I take half of that one too, letting the rich, mineral-slick moisture dissolve across the roof of my mouth, tasting iron and deep-earth clay and blind white fungus. On the third pellet, I let them seal it. A greedy neighbor gets walled out with granite-hard soldier-cement; a polite leak gets patched with soft, wet mortar twice a day for ten years.

Full of stolen deep-water—four whole drops' worth, enough for a week—I back away through the glassy plates until I reach my favorite sleeping-chimney.

It isn't a real chimney; it’s an ancient hollow root-cast where a swollen tree died two centuries ago, rotted away to powder, and left a curved tunnel rising almost to the surface lanes, capped now by a thin crust of packed bird-midden and cemented leaves.

I climb partway up and wedge myself tight, belly against the warm clay wall, back braced against black basalt, blunt dusty tail sealing the tunnel below me.

Above the thin ceiling over my snout, late afternoon passes into evening. I know it without seeing the sky because the long, miles-wide cloud-shadow has stopped sweeping the district; the masonry stops flexing from the sudden twenty-degree shifts of shade and sun and settles into its long, steady nighttime hum. Six inches above my head, the heavy, liquid pad-steps of the six-foot monitor roll past—*shhh-press, shhh-press*—so quiet that the skinks sleeping an inch under the dry grass stems don't even twitch, though I feel the massive keel of its belly brush a protruding lava lip and send a low, bass shiver three yards down into the rock.

Then comes the best hour.

Up in the spires, twenty feet overhead, they chew open the evening vents. Down here at the roots of the towers, the draft reverses. A faint, almost imperceptible draw pulls air slowly through the porous middens and deep cracks into the lower galleries to feed the vast upward sigh of the chimneys. The whole stone-and-mud continent inhales gently around me, smelling of dry grass-thatch, crushed jade-sap, warm lizard scale, and old baked dust.

My chin rests on the basalt edge. Somewhere half a mile west, a swollen copper-tree finally snaps an old mud arcade, sending a single dull *boom* ringing cleanly through the volcanic sheet beneath the whole silent metropolis—and before the vibration has even died out of my jawbone, I can already feel the soft, papery rustle of fifty thousand blind builders turning in the dark below me, heading west with wet mud in their mouths.
