I would not have feet at all, at least not the kind that must choose one hummock over another. To have legs here is to negotiate with the mud; I would rather be the negotiation.

Look down into the flooded runnels where the black silt braids through the sphagnum, and then look twenty paces downstream where the bog-myrtle crowns hang rootless in the pewter air. Between those two places, threading the drowned peat mattress and looping straight through the horizontal pinches in the mist, there is a pale, branching reticulum no thicker than linen thread. If you parted the moss to find me, you might mistake me for the mycelium of the five-minute bracket fungi, except that I do not rot, and where my filaments cross a light-smearing seam, they glow very faintly with trapped opal dust.

I am an old, continuous tangle—part fungal hypha, part metallic-grass rhizome, and part leftover habit of the basin itself. Ages ago, when the machine’s instructions finally lost their metal housing and sank into the wet slate, something humble and blindly vegetative grew into the empty shapes of its logic gates, simply because the water was warm there and the minerals were stirred. Over ten thousand seasons of bruised-plum twilight, I kept growing along the paths of least resistance, which in this valley are never straight lines.

My body is four hundred yards wide and nowhere thicker than a knitting needle, except at the nodes where three spatial shortcuts intersect underground; there, I swell into smooth, dark tubers the size of river cobbles, cool and dense as bog-iron, resting quietly in the black muck.

I have no eyes to split between a thing and its ghost. Instead, I feel weight, heat, and rhythm across half a mile of fen at once. Every forty seconds, when the fifty-gallon water-shelf collapses onto the mud at my eastern edge, the flat *slap* shivers down my threads, hits a locked standing wave in the central channel, and bounces back against the underside of a granite rise. That cross-hatched vibration *is* my sight. When a gaunt grey heron steps high over the bubbling silt, I feel the three-pronged dimple of its toes pressing my upper net before its beak ever breaks the warped pocket five yards away. When the eyeless basalt-frogs haul themselves over the mosses, their slow, pulsing throat-sacs massage the damp skin of my shallowest runners like tiny thumbs.

And I know the round-shouldered ones best of all, because I am the wire they speak through when the water is too loud.

Peat-water carries their heel-taps cleanly for fifty yards, yes—*tak-tak, tak-trrrk-tak*—before the churning drumbeat of the channels muddies the crispness of the iron-and-birch clappers. Beyond fifty yards, a tap in ordinary silt dissolves into soup. So the elders, with their lichen-creased knuckles and wide-drifting eyes, know where my thicker cords run beneath the silver-green moss. When one of them wants to speak not across a channel, but across half the valley—to tell a cousin three domes away that the spiral rushes are bending the eastern drop-time, or simply that a big mud-eel basket has come up full—they do not tap the open runnel. They sink a broad, slug-grooved heel directly over one of my buried bog-nodes and strike slow.

I love the shock of it. Their clappers send a clean, bright shiver through my iron-rich sap. Because my threads ignore distance wherever they slip through an atmospheric pinch, a *tak-tak* pressed into the mud beside the fallen swamp-birch arrives instantaneously inside a willow-woven stillness six hundred paces north, right beside the steaming phase-boundary basin. Inside that warm, mint-smelling dark, an elder sitting with their bare sole resting lightly on a floor-root feels my skin twitch against their arch.

Sometimes, in return—whether out of gratitude, or maintenance, or that quiet, unhurried tidiness that governs everything they do—they feed me.

They know I cannot easily cross the razor-thin lines where mist hisses into quenched-iron rain; the constant, violent shearing of the droplets snips my tender growing tips before they can thread the gap. Left to myself, I would be cut into isolated islands whenever a condensation-line shifts twenty feet north or south. So when an elder trims back the forty stems of spiral rush with their slate sliver, they take two or three hollow stalks of dead horsetail, pack them with pulverized opal sediment and rich, black ink scraped from the edge of a five-minute rot-band, and lay the stalks directly across the hissing rain-line like tiny bridges. Protected inside the tough silica stems, fed on pre-digested rot, my pale threads race across the shear-zone in an afternoon, stitching my northern half back to my southern half.

As soon as the join seals, the whole newly-connected stretch of fen snaps into focus at once—a sudden, glorious widening of the chord. Here is the *boom-boom-boom* of the shifted slate cobble; here is the tiny, rapid *fzzzzz-pop* of four different hearth-basins warming four circles of toes; here, dangling twenty feet up in empty mist among the rootless bog-myrtle leaves, are a few of my finest, hair-thin aerial filaments, drifted right through a horizontal fold alongside the twigs.

That is my favorite place to be. Down in the drowned peat, everything is heavy, cold, and pressing, full of slow chemistry and iron taste. Up in the floating crowns, drinking ambient fog over open water, I am weightless. And sometimes, when the rain-curtains hiss loud and two of the fen-dwellers sit ten feet apart on dry hummocks below, slipping their hands into the pinch to talk in secret, their long, pale outer fingers brush right through my hanging threads in the mist. 

Their skin is cool as tallow on the outside, astonishingly warm underneath, smelling faintly of crushed herbs, static birch, and woodless steam. As their six-fingered hands find each other in the empty air and begin tapping soft, silent rhythms knuckle-to-knuckle, a tiny stray curl of my thread rests caught between their wrists—feeling both pulses at once, slow and thick and utterly unhurried, keeping time between the clicks.
