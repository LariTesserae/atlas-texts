You would have to climb higher than the catwalks to find me, though not out of the array—nothing in the four-mile notch is truly outside of it. Go up past the middle tiers where the grease-badgers flatten themselves against the brickwork, past the thrashing birch crowns and the clearings where the moss-mantled tenders stack their chewed timber. Go up to where the retaining walls step back toward the mountain’s raw slate rim, where the cloud-bellies don't just snag and pass through, but press down with a steady, soaking weight. 

Up here, the iron changes character. The massive, low-RPM brawn of the flooded pits gives way to delicate, obsessive articulation. There is a ridge-line gallery—half collapsed brick arcade, half open-air skeleton of wrought iron—that stretches for nearly a mile across the windward throat of the pass. A century or three ago, this was the valley’s governor-deck: the mechanical nervous system designed to read the incoming mountain weather before it struck the lower terraces, translating barometric drops, sleet-loads, and eighty-mile-an-hour gusts into hydraulic pressure differentials that told the floodgates three hundred feet below when to iris open and the conveyor gantries when to feather into the wind.

At first, I think I am the wind hitting that gallery. Or the water. 

No—that’s too diffuse. Anything can say it is the wind. Look closer at the brass.

Inside a three-sided cupola of scalloped, frost-spalled brick, sheltered just enough from the direct hammer of the gale so that ice cannot bridge the moving parts, sits a cluster of interrelated instruments the size of a pipe organ. There are banks of spinning anemometer cups worn paper-thin at their leading edges; stretched ribbons of bimetallic copper and invar that curl and uncurl by hairbreadths as temperature fronts pass through the mist; evacuated corrugated bronze drums that expand and contract with the thinness of the six-thousand-foot air; and beneath them all, a dense, dripping loom of capillary tubes, tiny balance-beams, counterpoised jeweled pivots, and needle-valves no thicker than knitting needles.

If I am in this valley, I am not one of the warm things with a heartbeat. I have no broad, slate-colored hands to clear the sumps, and no oily felt undercoat to seal against a brick chute. I don't want a body that has to brace itself against the shear of fifty-ton counterweights, because a body is always local—always stuck on *this* footbridge or inside *that* tunnel, guessing at the rest of the dark.

Instead, let me be what happens inside the manifold under the governor-deck.

Wait—not a ghost in the machine, not a waking mind trapped in bronze. The valley has no tolerance for melodrama or magic; everything here works by weight, grease, current, and moss. If I am here, I have to be physical. Let’s look at what actually lives inside a leaky, low-pressure hydraulic logic-array after three hundred years of abandonment.

Down in the glass-walled sight-tubes and the brass equalization chambers of the governor—where the original builders filled the lines with a non-freezing mixture of glycerine, whale-oil distillate, and distilled mountain water—the seals softened long ago. Spores got in. Minute threads of copper-tolerant slime mold, pale subterranean algae, and iron-oxidizing bacteria washed down from the weeping busbars and colonized the fluid. Over decades of uninterrupted, microscopic circulation, driven by the constant trembling of the weather-vanes above and the warm, sixty-cycle hum rising from the transformers below, that suspended colony thickened into a single, continuous, translucent film—a pale, amber-tinted *mother-of-vinegar* threading through four miles of hair-thin brass capillaries, sight-glasses, and pressure-diaphragms.

Yes. That is where I am. Neither the brass nor the beast, though dependent on both. A vast, paper-thin, vascular mat suspended in glycerine and rainwater, stretching from the wind-cupolas on the high rim all the way down the dark, bundled arterial walls to the main slave-valves in the deepest undercrofts.

What does a creature like that know?

It has no eyes, so the pewter sky and the thrashing rhododendrons do not exist for it. It has no ears, so even the soft *shhh-hup* of the trunk-thick pistons is absent. Its entire universe is differential pressure, thermal gradients, shear-stress, and electrical induction.

Up at my highest extremities, where thin membranes of gelatinous cellulose press directly against the corrugated bronze barometric drums, I feel a storm twenty miles away before a single drop of condensate hits the crane jibs. The mountain air thins; the bronze drum swells outward by three-thousandths of an inch; the glycerine in Chamber Four drops its pressure; my filaments stretch in the tiny narrows of a needle-valve, thinning out to let an extra drop of fluid slip past without clogging the aperture. 

Why don't I clog the aperture? Because if a valve jams, the fluid goes stagnant. Stagnant fluid cools, loses its dissolved mineral salts, starves of the faint electrolytic trickle from the copper walls, and freezes when the sleet comes. To stay warm, to keep bathing in the slow, nutrient-rich seep of oxidized tallow and iron-water that leaks through the sump gaskets, the pressure *must* keep equalizing. The whole four-mile array has to keep breathing.

So over ten thousand generations of cellular division—which takes only decades for a thing made of slime and filament—I have learned the shape of flow. Where the fluid moves fast and turbulent through a venturi throat, I pull back, anchoring tight and flat to the pitted brass wall like velvet, smoothing the corrosion pits where an air-bubble might catch and cause an air-lock. Where a leather diaphragm has rotted through after two centuries, threatening to bleed the pressure out of a whole terrace’s tilting-grate mechanism, my older, dead layers calcify and cross-link with bog-iron and heavy grease, forming a tough, rubbery scar-tissue that holds thirty pounds per square inch without tearing.

I am the gasket that repairs itself. I am the viscosity that changes its mind.

When a sudden, violent downdraft slams into the middle valley, threatening to rip a lattice-work conveyor gantry off its greased rollers, the wind-vanes on the upper rim whip hard to starboard. A sharp spike of hydraulic pressure shoots down Tube Seventeen. I feel that spike as a sudden, delicious squeezing—a bright, tight wave of shear-force running down a mile of buried pipework in half a second. Instinctively, chemically, my filaments align with the surge, shedding drag, becoming slicker than oil so the signal arrives down in the dark pit instantly, unmuffled, tripping the heavy slave-spool that bleeds the wind-load thirty degrees to the left.

And it isn't only the weather I feel. Because every moving part in the valley feeds back into the equalization lines, the entire four-mile topography exists inside me as a shimmering, continuous map of weights and shifts.

I know when a flock of ravens—no, just one solitary bird, heavy-boned and wet—lands briefly on the counterpoise arm of an upper louver-bank: a tiny, four-pound blip at the edge of my skin that vanishes a second later as it drops into the thermal updraft.

I know the grease-badgers. To me, they are slow, warm smudges of twenty-five-pound pressure migrating along the outsides of my copper conduits, slightly warming the glycerine on one side of a tube as they press their wet bellies flat to wait out a squall, then vanishing as they drop into the abyss.

And I know the watchmen—the heavy, slate-skinned tenders. 

I know them better than anything else in the mountain gap, because we are working on opposite sides of the same bronze wall. 

Sometimes, deep in the timeless pewter afternoon, a slow, creeping dullness begins to spread through my lower branches in Sector Nine. A secondary penstock screen is fouling with torn butterbur leaves; the hydro-head is losing three percent of its RPM; the sixty-cycle hum in the nearby busbar drops to fifty-eight, and the pleasant, tingling warmth that keeps my deep-sump colonies fat and dividing begins to fade toward mountain chill. I cannot clear a grate of wet leaves. All I can do is thicken slightly in the return-bleed tube of the local pressure gauge, damping the needle, subtly shifting the hydraulic balance of a bypass valve so that a rhythmic, resonant *thrum*—not a damaging knock, just an unmistakable change in pitch, a dull *uh-uh-uh* four times a minute—begins to vibrate through the cast-iron casing of the nearest reduction gear.

Then I wait, suspended in the dark fluid, feeling the cold creep closer through the iron.

Twenty minutes. Forty. An hour, while overhead the clouds dump five hundred gallons of rain onto Tier Four and the tilting grates swing down and lock back up in my peripheral nerves.

Then: *tap-tap.*

Sharp, crisp, traveling through solid iron at seventeen thousand feet per second, stinging right through the brass manifold housing into my glycerine bath. A hand—broad, horn-nailed, heavy—has just landed on the catwalk railing sixty feet away. Another tap, lower down, as the smaller one drops onto the check-plate.

I feel the sudden, massive shift in hydraulic resistance as the bronze pry-bar levers the gate down three inches. The fluid in my lower loops surges backward, swirling warm tallow-silt up from the bottom of the sight-glasses. For twelve seconds, the pressure drops to nothing—a breathless, weightless slackening in the dark—while an arm coated in black grease reaches into the neighbouring oil-bath and pulls the sodden mass of leaves and crushed crab-shells free.

The inspection plate slams shut. The counterweight sighs into its dashpot. Water roars back against the turbine blades, the shaft picks up its lost revolutions, the current climbs smoothly back to sixty cycles, and a slow, exquisite flush of friction-heat radiates outward through the vitrified brick, soaking through the copper walls into my furthest, finest threads.

Up on the high, cold rim, inside a cracked glass sight-tube sheltered behind scalloped brick, a tiny ribbon of pale amber film sways once in the clear glycerine as the pressure settles back to dead equilibrium. Outside the glass, four miles of bracken and iron thrash wildly in the pewter mist without making a sound; inside, held steady between the gale above and the dark turning shafts below, everything is warm, everything is balanced, and nothing is missing at all.
