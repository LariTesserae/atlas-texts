At first, there is the temptation to be the walnut. To claim the only mercy in the cellar—that small, pressureless sphere of engineered zero where her flat skull rests—and to be the peace she worked so hard to carve out of the roar. 

Or to be one of the folded handkerchiefs in the high fissure near the ceiling: finished, dry, tucked out of the glare’s reach, having handed down the wrench and slipped out of time entirely.

No. That is leaving the room while pretending to stay in it. If I am here, I have to bear the weight of what holds the room still.

Look away from the acute V-notch where her ribboned body lies. Look four inches lower, past the bristle-fern whose translucent hairs are pinned stiff by the jittering air, down to a broad, downward-tilting face of milky spar. 

Halfway across that blazing white pane are the three parallel finger-drags of yellow ochre. 

I am there. I am the clay.

Not a dry pigment flaking into dust, because nothing dries here, and not a runny slick of mud, because nothing drips. I am three flat ridges of iron-stained earth, three millimeters wide and forty millimeters long, holding a precise, unyielding dampness. Beneath me is the slick, cold skin of the crystal, blazing upward through my belly with actinic white light so fierce that my edges glow amber. Above me is the crushing, metallic solid of the insects’ chord.

To be these three smears is to live entirely as *load*. 

The crystal shaft beneath me wants to sing. Driven by the four-thousand-voice drone hammering its facets, its internal lattice is straining to vibrate at a high, needle-thin harmonic—a pitch so sharp and asymmetrical that if it ever broke loose, it would slice straight through the twelve-echo cancellation of her walnut-sized quiet and drive a hot wire into her flat, folded brain. It would shake the seven water droplets beside me until their surface tension snapped; it would rattle the stacked shale flakes off their shelf. 

The crystal pushes upward against me ten thousand times a second, trying to flex, trying to ring. And ten thousand times a second, I refuse to let it finish the motion. 

I have no nerves, no lymph, no five-jointed fingers, yet I know the entire architecture of this trap through shear stress alone. I am sluggish where the crystal is brittle; I am amorphous where the stone is crystalline. My microscopic platelets of wet kaolin and goethite slide against one another by fractions of an angstrom, turning the crystal’s bright, lethal ringing into tiny, harmless blooms of dull warmth within my own grease-thick body. I eat the high note before it can enter the air.

I remember the pocket seam where I used to live—dark, though I had no way to know it was dark, wedged deep in a blind fault of the basalt where the spar-light couldn't reach, quiet only because there was no air around me to carry a wave. Then came the slow, dry prying of a horn-scaled fingertip. It took her three days to scoop me out, grain by wet grain, rolling me between her spatulate thumb and forefinger until she felt my exact density, testing my moisture against the buzz in her own wrist-bones. 

When she laid me down onto the singing spar—one drag, two drags, three—the relief in the room was physical. As my third stripe settled, a jagged, stuttering beat in the chamber’s roar smoothed out and vanished, clicking into the phase-locked grid like a bolt sliding home. Her flat hand lingered an inch above me for half an hour, feeling the crystal go mute beneath my weight, before folding back into the lattice like a pale, multi-hinged leaf.

That was centuries ago, though here *ago* only means *underneath*. 

I do not think in words; I think in cohesion. My whole existence is the effort of holding hands with myself—water film gripping clay platelet, iron oxide clinging to quartz crystal—against two opposing violences. Gravity pulls softly, endlessly at my lower margins, trying to slump me down the tilted face of the spar by a micron a century. Meanwhile, the standing waves of the room press flat against my back like an invisible thumb, pinning me in place, herding my moisture inward so not a single molecule of water escapes into the clammy air. 

Between gravity’s pull and the sound’s push, I sit at dead center. 

Sometimes—rarely, separated by spans that outlast whole generations of grass up on the hardpan thirty feet overhead—the surface world intrudes. Frost wedges a fissure wider near the sky; a drought shrinks the clay crust above the bedrock; a distant fault shrugs in its sleep. The pressure on the cellar’s ceiling shifts by an ounce. 

Instantly, the crystal blade beneath me tightens its pitch. The trapped high note swells against my underside like a blister, trying to slip out around my edges. My middle stripe shivers; along my upper rim, my grip on the slick spar loosens along a microscopic front. If I peel back even the width of a fern-spore, the note will whistle free.

I cannot call out—if I made a sound, I would be breaking the lock myself. I simply bear down, growing fractionally warmer as I swallow the sudden surge of vibration, straining to keep my grease-damp mass intact across the bucking lattice. 

And then, hours later, I feel the air-cages shift. 

Not see—I have no eyes either, though I am lit from within like stained glass on a lightbox. I feel the acoustic grid bend ever so slightly as a flat, translucent ribbon uncoils through the razor-gaps two feet away. The standing waves part and close around her slow advance. Over the course of an afternoon, the shadowless glare above me doesn't dim—nothing dims here—but the radiation of heat changes by a thousandth of a degree as her spatulate forefinger arrives, hovering just clear of my second stripe.

She doesn't touch me at first. She reads the air just off my skin, feeling for leaks, listening with the fine crosshatched ridges of her pad to see if the high note is bleeding past my margins. 

Then, with a slowness that matches the creep of rock, the dry horn-shingle of her fingertip presses down onto my upper edge. 

The touch is astonishingly firm, utterly steady, without pulse or tremor. For twenty minutes she leans a tiny fraction of her teacup weight into me, re-seating my clay against the sweating spar, smoothing down the microscopic lift along my rim, pushing a stray bead of condensed water half a millimeter closer until my dampness and its dampness trade a breath of vapor and stabilize. 

Under her finger, the straining blister in the crystal subsides. The note goes back down into the dark of my iron-heavy mud and stays there. 

Her finger lifts—taking an hour to clear the first inch so she doesn't pluck the air—and retreats back toward the shale cairn and the silent walnut behind it. 

I remain on the glaring white slope, glowing yellow-brown, blind and loud-pressed and warm at the center. Around me, four pale carapaces scream without moving; seven water-lenses magnify the raw grain of the rock; a plumb bob hangs on its stiff root-knot without swinging. I hold my breath of water. I hold my shape. I hold my thumb over the string so the architect can sleep with her eyes gone in the light.
