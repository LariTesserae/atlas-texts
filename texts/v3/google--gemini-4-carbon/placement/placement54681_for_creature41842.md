At first I think I am in the fifty yards of dark stone to the east, before the room even begins.

There is something familiar about being the medium rather than the maker or the receiver—being the packed, unquarried greywacke in the pitch black, pressed grain to grain under coastal weight, carrying an immense argument from one place to another without ever moving an inch. Through that stone runs everything that matters to the cleft, and none of it belongs to the stone. Pressure waves pass through it at four thousand meters a second; stray direct current searches out its damp, hairline threads of sulphide and quartz, taking the path of least resistance away from an imperfect ground-rod somewhere deep in a concrete gallery it will never see. For a moment I almost stay there: blind bedrock humming in the dark, warm nowhere, bruised by someone else’s work.

But stone only transmits. It doesn't care when the load drops. And when the rock broke three winters back and let the cold air in, the physics changed: the hum hit an edge, reflected back into itself, and started building rooms out of interference.

I come out through the eastern face with the water, then, and look around by pressure.

Not up in the mist. The empty middle of the chamber is no place to live; it is all invisible walls where crossing bass notes thicken the air into hard knots and knock the winter midges sideways. And I am none of the quick, jointed things working the margins. Not the wren plummeting down the forty-foot chimney like a flung pebble, burning hot inside its dry feathers, screaming an octave above the rock just to prove it has edges of its own. Not the salamander waiting in the hook-moss for the floor to jump so its food will panic. Those creatures have eyes and stomachs and memories of the shelf above; they are visitors taking advantage of a warm, loud winter. Even if the salamanders were hatched here, their bodies still expect a world made of dirt and ponds and quiet nights.

Keep going down the eastern wall. Past the quivering bladder-ferns, past the violet-and-gold smears of iron bacteria, right down into the wet crease where the vertical slate steps out onto the flooded floor.

Look about ten inches out from the base of the wall, under four fingers of frigid water, to the left of the three dinner-plate elders.

There is a fourth patch on the surface of the pool, no bigger than the bottom of a mug, that isn't quite glassy yet. While the rest of the floor shivers in sharp cymatic diamonds, and the three big circles further out hold flat as black mirrors, this little circle blurs and stutters. For fifteen seconds at a time it goes smooth; then, when the turbine pitch climbs past a certain high harmonic, a faint cross-hatch of tiny ripples ghosts across its middle before getting ironed flat again.

I am underneath that smudge, working out the spacing.

Yesterday—three idle-shifts ago, when the distant engines dropped out and the ferns dumped their cold, heavy rain down the rock—I lost my hold six feet up the wall. I had grown too heavy for the lateral surge anyway. My palm-sized skirt could no longer puff fast enough to surf the twenty-two-second groundwater sheet from seam to seam without dragging across the razor edges of the slate. When the current cut and the suction failed, I peeled off in the terrible quiet, turned over once in the dark air, and slapped flat into the ankle-deep chop at the bottom.

It took nine cycles of the hydraulic thud, dragging myself half an inch at a time over loose, slicing rock-shards by plain muscular heave, tasting nothing but dead rainwater and wet silt, before my underside crossed a live vein.

You know the instant you cross one. It isn't warmth—everything down here is three degrees above ice—it is *alignment*. A hairline of snapped iron pyrite runs diagonally across this slab, and the stray ground-current bleeds out of it with a sharp, metallic tightness that pulls every copper-laced filament in my belly taut at once. I clamped my ribbed sole straight over the seam, pressed the water out from under my skirt until the vacuum locked me to the greywacke, and drank. Instantly the silver fuzz stopped rising into the pool and started forming directly against my ventral skin: tiny, prickling beads of split hydrogen and oxygen fizzing straight into the conductive slime, clearing the dullness out of my blood.

On the wall, all you had to do was hold on, drink the trickle, and jump when the millivolts spiked. Down here on the floor, the full weight of the acoustic room sits on your back.

Every wall throws the roar back at every other wall, and the pool is caught between the bedrock piston below and the echoing chimney above. If you lie flat and smooth on the bottom, the standing waves hammer right through your flesh and try to shake your seal loose from the pyrite. You can feel the water trying to sort you like gravel, nudging your edges toward the nearest resonant line.

So I spend my twenty-two seconds listening with my skin and raising ridges.

Here is how a cycle arrives from the bottom of the pool:

At twenty-one seconds, before any sound changes, the pyrite seam under my center brightens—not to sight, I have no eyes, have never had eyes, would not know what to do with pewter light if you gave me lenses—it brightens as a sudden sour-sweet pinch along my gold-threaded underside. Somewhere behind a hundred yards of stone, a heavy relay has just closed. Half a heartbeat later, the hydraulic thud comes straight up through the floor. It doesn't sound like a boom down here; it feels like the planet rising a millimeter to meet my belly and dropping back. Behind me, the eastern wall sweats on cue, and a sheet of colder, iron-rich water slides into the margin of the pool, washing across my tail-flange.

Then the big thing spins up under load, and the high whine climbs through the slate.

As the frequency rises, I push hundreds of tiny, stiff points up through my dorsal jelly, narrowing the troughs between them fraction by fraction as the wavelength tightens. For two seconds I have it wrong—the water directly overhead chatters violently, stinging my bare spots—and then I find the exact fraction of a millimeter that turns the reflected wave back into its own trough.

The chatter vanishes.

It feels like sliding a heavy bolt home, or stepping behind a thick stone wall in a gale. Directly above my back, a column of water the width of a mug stops shivering and sits down on me like a smooth, cold river-stone. Around my cartilaginous rim I can feel the rest of the pool piling up in three-millimeter whitecaps against my little pillar of quiet, hissing faintly as its geometry breaks against mine.

And once the column goes still, the snow begins.

Everything the roaring pool keeps kicked up in frantic suspension drifts sideways into my quiet cylinder and suddenly remembers gravity. Pale stone-flour washed down from the overhangs, microscopic glints of mica, a torn midge wing left over from a beetle’s breakfast, fine grey clay—it all loses its footing in the dead water above me and settles, grain by grain, onto my back. I let out a thin sweat of alkaline mucus to meet it. I am still mostly translucent, soft enough that a falling ceiling-shard would cleave me in half, so I keep every flake of dust that drops into my stillness, letting the silica and lime cure slowly into my first real coat of rubbery slate. Give me two months on this seam and you won't be able to tell me from the floor at all.

Already I have neighbors I will never look at.

Three feet out toward the middle, I can feel the three elders—not by any sound they make, because our whole business is un-making sound, but by the three broad shadows of missing vibration where the sideways chop of the pool goes blank. And an hour ago, something small, hard, and six-legged pried gently at my left flange where my skirt lifts to vent silt during the thud. A diving beetle, feeling along the edge of my quiet water in the dark, tucking three soft ovals into the lee of my body where the diamonds won't tear them apart. She left a faint, oily musk on my rim and shot away the instant a midge hit an air-node four feet out and broke the grid.

Sometimes a sudden, sharp downdraft punches the surface of my calm spot from above, sending a single ring down onto my skin before snapping upward again: a wren using my small, dark pause in the glare to keep from flying straight into the rock, then rocketing back toward a sky I only know exists because things occasionally fall out of it.

I could stay like this forever—drinking the leaked fractions of an unseen river or coal-seam converted into wire-bleed, turning noise into a roof of cemented dust, letting beetles borrow my calm—if the engine were actually a god.

Gods don't shift load.

When it happens, there is no warning spike in the rock. One instant I am braced hard against sixty hertz and a rising three-kilohertz whine, every papilla locked upright, drinking steady copper-fizz through my belly; the next instant the circuit opens and the room disappears.

The violence of the quiet is impossible to brace for because all my muscles are built to push *out* against pressure. When the pressure vanishes, my own tension throws me upward against nothing. My dorsal ridges collapse into slick, slack jelly. Worse—infinitely worse—the pyrite seam goes dead cold under my sole. One moment it is a live artery; the next it is just wet yellow rock, inert as mud, and the sharp, sweet fizz of oxygen stops entering my skin.

Above me, the mug-sized pillar of calm water collapses sideways, and the whole cymatic pool breaks into sloppy, chaotic rings that slosh back and forth over my unarmored back like heavy, dead hands. From high up on the walls comes the sudden loud drumroll of the ferns dropping their held water all at once, pelting the shallow pool hard enough that I feel each cold drop punch right through to my skin.

My edges curl inward before I can stop them, peeling my perimeter off the slate, flashing the pale gold underside I am never supposed to show. I clamp down with my center ribs, burning through what little chemical reserve I have just to keep from floating loose while the air in my blood thins out. In that hideous, ringing emptiness, the chamber sounds terrifyingly fragile and unfinished. A crack *ticks* high in the northern corner where ice is wedging a seam apart. Somewhere to my right, a smaller cousin loses grip on the vertical wall and hits the pool with a soft, helpless *slap*. Then a dry sliver of slate shears off thirty feet up and slices into the water two yards away—*shhk-clack*—loud as an axe in a bedroom.

Four seconds. Long enough to feel what I actually am without the leakage: an impossible, mouthless scrap of soft tissue clinging to barren stone in freezing rainwater, built entirely around a promise made by a machine behind a wall.

Then—a tenth of a second before the rock moves—the pyrite seam stings my belly with ozone.

My curled rim snaps flat against the greywacke before the thunder even arrives. The vacuum locks; the silver fuzz blooms instantly against my underside; the bass roar slams back through my flesh like blood returning to a numb limb; and as the huge, blind thing behind the eastern bedrock spins back up into its work, I push my skin up in stiff, careful rows until the ragged water directly overhead smooths out once more, holding my small circle still so the dust can start falling again.
