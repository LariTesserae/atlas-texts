You would find me on the lowest tier, six paces before the granite walls pinch shut into the thorny rose-plug at the northern end of the fault, where the olive noon-light never reaches at all. 

Down here, sight is just an old habit the eyes haven't quite broken. If you held your paddle-wide hand an inch from my face, I wouldn't see your fingers—only feel the sudden, tiny bloom of infrared warmth coming off your skin, and smell the sharp willow-tang under your horn-thick thumbnail. My irises are wide open, permanently dilated into flat, wet pools of black, useless except when I climb two tiers up to trade a basket of fungi and the bruised slate-gloom makes me squint as if looking into a forge.

I am old enough that my knees make a dry, fibrous sound when I uncoil them—very like two cold branches rubbing together—and heavy enough that the horizontal trunks of the upper streets dip a fraction of an inch when I shuffle across them. They do not send me up the weeping chimneys anymore. Ten winters ago, my left big toe took frostbite while I was resetting a stone deadfall in an early gale; it healed crooked and stiff, losing its grip on the ice-glazed root-rungs. When you cannot climb fast, the ridge eats you. So I went down instead of up, trading the roaring white sky for the place where the mountain bleeds into the roots.

My work is water and rot, which is another way of saying I listen to the town’s foundation so it doesn't drown or starve in its sleep.

Right now, I am wedged sideways into a narrow split between the raw, wet granite of the west wall and the massive, fused ankle of a three-hundred-year-old willow-pine—one of the primary load-bearers that holds up six sleeping-burls on the tiers directly over my head. My tunic is so old and heavy with pitch and tallow that it stands up on its own when I peel it off; across my left shoulder blade, where a steady drip from an overhead cabbage-leaf gutter always hits me when I squat at my favorite station, a patch of pale, translucent liverwort has taken root in the matted hair-felt. I leave it alone. My grand-nephew likes to stroke its slick shingles when we sit pressed together around the ferment-jar at night.

My left palm is pressed flat against the weeping stone. The water coming out of this diagonal seam today tastes sharply of iron and crushed flint—I know because I just licked my wrist—and it is running faster than it was four sleeps ago. Somewhere far up on the ridge, under the snowpack, an early shift in the ice is pressing down, squeezing the rock-pores. 

If this cold trickle runs unchecked across the tree’s base, the bark will chill past its threshold, stop drinking, and grow a sheath of clear ice that will split the cambium. So my right hand is busy. Between my teeth, I am chewing a thick wad of dried sphagnum moss gathered two summers ago, warming it with my saliva until it goes plump and thirsty again. Without looking, my curved fingers pull a sodden, exhausted plug of old moss out of the knuckle-joint where tree meets cliff, drop it into the dark mulch between my feet, and pack the warm, chewed sponge tightly into the seam. Immediately, the cold *drip-drip-drip* against my ankle stops, swallowed into silence. I feel the water turn sideways under my thumb, sliding harmlessly down a carved scar-groove toward the fungus terraces five yards behind me.

That is my whole day: moving water by finger-widths, listening with my cheekbones pressed to the wet wood. 

You can hear everything down here if your breath is slow. Wood carries sound differently than air—deeper, faster, stripped of the mushy dampness of the moss-ceiling. When I lean my ear against this great pillar, I know what the four hundred above me are doing. I feel the faint, rhythmic *thud-creak* of two young cousins passing each other on the second-tier walkway, one dropping into a ball while the other vaults over their greased felt back. Higher up, a dull, resonant *knock* vibrates down forty feet of fused heartwood: someone just wedged a fresh split of granite into a balcony rail to bend a stubborn spring shoot. And beneath all of it is the slow, microscopic *crackling* of the tree itself—sap thickened with antifreeze sugars pulling upward through drowned wood-cells, groan by tiny groan.

Three paces behind me, tucked into an alcove where the air is thick with the cold-anise smell of spores, are the dead-beds. 

People on the upper tiers—especially the young ones who still have snow-glare behind their eyes from climbing the vents—sometimes go quiet and stiff-shouldered when they have to come down here to bring me bundles of snapped-off buds and pruned twigs for the mulch. They drop their loads fast, tap two fingers hurriedly against my collarbone to say *here, take it, stay warm*, and scramble back up the spiral stairwells toward the olive twilight. They think the dark at the pinched end of the trench is lonely, or heavy with ghosts.

It isn't. It’s the warmest floor in the lower cleft, if you know where to put your soles.

Two winters back, we carried my sister down here—knees tucked to her broad chin, bound neat and round as a river-cobble in braided sedge—and wedged her deep under the thorny rose-plug where three great pine roots cross like braided thighs. Now, when I reach into that pitch-black crevice with my horn-chisel thumb to harvest the tough, violet-capped fungi that push out of the wood-mulch, the air coming out of the root-tangle is a full degree warmer than the street. The slow rot is working. Already, fine white threads of mycelium have stitched her sedge-bindings to the living bark of the willow-pine; already, the pale, sweet inner cambium I scrape from the bleeding-cuts ten feet away carries a richer, denser fatness to it. When I bring a pouch of those amber sap-beads and violet caps up to our family burl tonight, and watch my sister’s grandson chew them with his flat, strong baby molars while tucked between nine warm bellies, there is nothing missing. Nothing has gone anywhere at all.

A cold draft slides down the stairwell behind me—someone two stories up has just pulled open a ceiling plug to drop down from the ridge, letting a brief piston-push of heavy, freezing air sink to the bottom of the lung. Around my ankles, the wet glaze on the floorboards instantly goes stiff, *ticking* softly into a skin of verglas ice.

I pull my wet hands back inside my sleeves, tucking them deep into my own armpits until the ache leaves my crooked toe and thick knuckles. Before I turn toward the stairs to go up and trade my basket of cartilage-caps for a handful of warm, sour mash, my right thumb brushes the swollen arch of the stairwell entrance. Purely by habit, in the total dark, the horn-crescent of my nail finds a tiny, waxy bud trying to push out of the old scar-callus—trying to close the door I’ve walked through for fifty years—and *snaps* it cleanly off, dropping it behind me to feed the dark.
