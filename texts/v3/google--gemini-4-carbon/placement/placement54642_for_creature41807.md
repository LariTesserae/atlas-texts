Do not look for me on the piston rod. I do not have the nerve for the four-hundred-millisecond gamble, that frantic, knife-flat darting between obliteration and famine. Nor am I in the crackling copper heap around the flywheel, drinking raw blue lightning until my plates hum and push apart, or plowing trenches through the living gravel with a magnetite guillotine for a face. Those lives burn too fast; they are frantic, staccato things, trapped in the immediate hysteria of the stroke and the spark.

Look instead to the dead iron at the cooling edge of the pan, about twelve feet back from the active donkey-pumps, where the deafening *shhhk-shhhk-thud* of the bowl settles into a heavy, continuous tremor through the stone. 

Look at the thick-walled junction box—the one with its cast-iron lid rusted half-off, canted at an angle into a waist-deep drift of pearlescent shell-flour. 

At first, you will think the box is simply furred with corrosion. Its square-headed bolts and sheared conduit stubs are crusted over with blotches of matte rust-brown, verdigris, and dull, pitted brass. Then a stray eddy of zinc-bright wind dips over the rim of the sump, whirling a veil of white dust across the metal, and one of the patches of corrosion lifts two long, caliper-curved palps to test the breeze.

I am a Brood-mason. 

My body is no larger than a pocket watch, slung low between eight thin, double-jointed legs that creak faintly—like dry cork twisted in a bottle—whenever I shift my weight. My carapace is a counterfeit of dead machinery: mottled iron-oxide red and oxidized bronze, rough and light-absorbing, grown from the very minerals scraped off this housing ten thousand generations ago. Inside my chest, my waxy lipids are cool, just warm enough to stay malleable, kept from stiffening into tallow by the faint, residual vibration traveling through the granite from the working engines thirty feet away, and by the dense, insulating blanket of eggs packed beneath me.

For I am standing over a nursery of stolen futures.

Wedged into the dry shadow beneath the junction box’s canted lid, safe from the stinging wind that scours the peneplain above, are four hundred and twelve tiny, pearl-colored spheres. They are not mine. Some were dropped in a mindless fever by over-heated Anvil-backs sliding down from the pump blocks; others slipped from the bronze plates of Ley-jars still twitching with residual static; a few larger, flint-gray ovals belong to the Shear-dogs themselves, laid carelessly in the warm shallows of the dust-drifts and forgotten. 

In the open churn of the basin, ninety-nine out of a hundred of those eggs would be ground into chalk under ten million ceramic feet within an hour, or suffocated by the Silt-mites. 

I went down into the warm outward currents to fetch them. It is the riskiest part of my trade. Moving slowly—because I am built for precision, not speed—I have to wade knee-deep into the shuffling, rasping tide of bodies where the heated bugs slump outward from the iron. When a Shear-dog comes wheezing past, its abdominal bellows clacking like dry wood, I do not run. I fold my caliper-palps flat against my face, tuck my eight legs into the bevels of my shell, and go rigid. To the hunter’s pinhole eyes and vibration-tuned feet, I become nothing more than a loose flake of rusted cast-iron tumbling in the drift. Let the heavy, flint-gray bulk plow right over me, grinding me down into the flour; my shell is arched like a masonry bridge, built to take the weight without popping.

Once the wake passes, I unfold, reach down into the churning pearl-dust with my long palps, and pluck up an intact egg. 

Right now, I am holding one between my second pair of legs. Through the dry, horny pads of my tarsi, I can feel the faintest, microscopic *tock-tock* inside the shell—a tiny silica-chitin ratchet flexing for the first time. 

My palps are tipped with broad, velvety pads made of millions of split, microscopic bristles. Slowly, methodically, I rotate the pearl-colored sphere, brushing away the invisible film of pulverized carapace that settles over everything in this bowl. If I stop brushing for half a day, the flour packs into the shell’s breathing-pores, and the thing inside suffocates before it ever feels the engine’s heat. 

*Brush, turn, brush.* My joints click softly in the dry shadow of the iron lid, a tiny clockwork counterpoint to the massive *thud-clank* shaking the world outside.

Then I pause. My palps freeze on the shell. 

Through the hollow calipers, I feel nothing. No *tock*. No faint electrostatic tingle of developing nerve-ganglia. The winter-dry air got to this one before I found it in the drift, or perhaps its mother’s internal capacitor was half-flat when she laid it. Either way, the spark has gone out of it. Look closely, and its pearlescent luster has gone slightly chalky.

There is no sentiment in a pocket watch. This is my wage.

I bring the dead egg up to my narrow, shearing mandibles. A tiny *snip*—inaudible under the roar of ten thousand rasping bellies—and the shell parts. Inside is a minuscule bead of dense, sulfur-yellow yolk-wax and a pinch of dry, unformed protein. I scoop it clean in three neat strokes, swallowing the rich hydrocarbon paste down into my quartz-lined gizzard, where the stolen energy will keep my joints clicking for another forty hours. Even the empty shell is not wasted. I crush the chalky fragments between my lower maxillae, grinding them into a fine powder, and express a microscopic, precious drop of black mineral-grease from a gland beneath my chin—grease I traded a whole afternoon of patient scavenging around a dead Gasket-louse smear to acquire.

Powder and grease mix between my palps into a gray, tacky mortar. Turning back to the junction box, I dab the paste onto a bare spot of rusted iron inside the shelter, pick up a fresh, living egg I gathered an hour ago, and press it firmly into the mortar until it sets. 

Four hundred and eleven left.

I step backward onto the rim of the box and settle down, letting my mottled legs splay across the square-headed bolts until my silhouette vanishes into the geometry of old, dead industry. From here, looking out from under my iron eave, I can watch the whole frantic furnace of the sump without being consumed by it. 

Thirty feet away, the unshielded flywheel wobbles on its shaft, spitting a sharp *snap* of violet-blue static into the copper pyramid of Ley-jars, lighting up the shadowless zinc gloom for a fraction of a second and sending a fresh tang of ozone curling across the drifts to my antennae. Closer in, a heavy Anvil-back, hot as a skillet from the pump housing, slides lazily down the mound of smaller shells, scattering Gasket-lice like iron filings. Beneath my own junction box, deep down under six feet of compacted white flour where the sheared-off pipes vanish into the ancient granite, I feel the slow, heavy *shift* of a blind Sump-worm turning over in its sleep, followed ten seconds later by a silent, warm burp of ammonia gas filtering up through the dust past my ankles.

Suddenly, under my belly, a shell splits.

Not with the violent crack of a Shear-dog’s kill, but with a dry, minuscule *plink*, like a cooling filament in a dead bulb. 

I lift my front legs a fraction of an inch to look. Out of a mottled, oblong egg mortared near the back of the box crawls a hatchling Shear-dog, no bigger than my own thumbnail. For the next eight minutes, its flint-gray plates are as pale and pliable as wet parchment; its tiny, asymmetric guillotine-jaw hasn't hardened yet. If I wanted to, I could snip it in half right now and drink its wax before it knew it was alive. In three months, if it survives the crush, it will be the size of a terrier, and it would crush *me* without breaking stride if it ever caught me out in the open drift.

Instead, I reach down with one velvety palp and gently brush a speck of blinding shell-flour off its pinhole eye. 

The tiny thing shudders in the brisk, moistureless air. It feels the cold stealing its meager warmth; and then, through all ten of its soft, needle-tipped feet, it feels the deep, percussive *thud-clank, thud-clank* of the geothermal engines beating at the center of the world. It doesn't even look back at the dark iron hollow that kept it alive, or at the rust-colored spider standing over it. It simply turns toward the vibration, drops off the lip of the junction box into the pearlescent dust, and begins to scramble inward toward the heat, vanishing instantly between the rasping, slate-blue rims of two passing grazers.

I do not watch it go. Another egg two inches to the left is starting to dust over from the breeze. I step sideways, my silica tendons creaking softly, lower my caliper-palps to the smooth, pearl curve, and begin to brush.
