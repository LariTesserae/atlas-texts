It begins as a snag in the stroke—a tiny, silent drag against the skinning paste.

Where the bevel ought to shear away clean, leaving a corner crisp enough to split a drifting thread of mucus, a microscopic ridge of white carbonate remains standing. I draw my right forelimb back and run it along the sill a second time, pressing harder, tilting my thorax into the thick cold of the brine. The ridge stays. Worse, my second pass gouges a parallel groove beside it, ruining the plane. 

When I bring my forelimbs together beneath my mouthparts to clean them, I feel the missing wedge. The inner corner of the right trowel has spalled off against an oversized grain of basalt. Instead of a flat chisel, I end in a jagged tooth.

Beneath me, the worm does not care. Its glassy flank ripples; its crimson vessel pulses once, twice; its collar swells to lay down the next pale ribbon of wall. My middle legs are already drumming the rapid, automatic staccato against its slick back—*hold level, extrude, hold level*—while my glands weep a tiny bead of formic ester to keep it pinned to the plumb line. The geometry is still inside me, coiled tight as a spring behind my feathery receptors, demanding ninety degrees, demanding flat faces and rectangular openings the exact width of my shoulders. 

I drop off the worm’s dorsal curve to tamp the new ribbon, scooping black sand from my thoracic pouch. Stamp, press, draw. Again that ragged twin-scar trails behind my wrist. 

In the dense, syrupy water, pheromones hang heavy before they thin out. Within three body-lengths, another architect tastes the crookedness of my wake—or feels the miniature eddy tripping over the unshaved ridge. It scuttles down from the cornice above, pale legs rowing crisp and fast through the violet gloom, its trowels pristine, square as cut slate. There is no fight. We do not have weapons for one another. It simply wedges its flat shoulder beneath my belly, interrupts my drumming with a cleaner, faster rhythm on the worm’s collar, and crowds me off the slick glass tube. 

For a long time I cling upside-down to the underside of an arch of braided chitin, middle legs twitching against nothing. Without a worm beneath me, half my nervous system is speaking into dead water. Tap-tap-tap against the brine. *Curve inward. Purge.* Nothing answers. Passing builders brush my antennae—thousands of legs ticking silently through the milky haze, smelling of fresh limestone and sharp, acrid steering-esters—and none of them pause.

There is an ordinary path for a chipped tool. I taste it in the rising draft: the faint, greasy trace of lipid-wax drifting down from the ceiling pastures, mixed with the stale chemical signature of old, blunt-limbed architects. 

I let go of the chitin bridge and let the warm central plume carry me upward.

It is effortless, rising up the great shaft. Around me float the sparkling specks of new larvae and the milky curds of spilled mortar, all lifted on the gentle five-degree breath of the floor vents. As the basalt roof nears, the warmth stalls and flattens against the freezing stone. Here, fenced in by stiff, glowing violet stalks that burn with a dull tallow-light, bob the copepod herds.

I hook my hind legs into the woven fence and crawl onto the back of the nearest creature. It is enormous, blind, a slow-drifting bladder of clear oil. Beneath my six feet, its segments yield like soft tallow. Nearby, two other scarred crustaceans are raking their comb-mandibles across its pale hide, rolling tiny beads of secreted wax into loaves. 

I lower my forelimbs to try. Immediately my whole body recoils. 

Everything up here is round. Everything gives way. There is no plane to true, no corner to square, only endless, undulating, rubbery curvature bobbing helplessly against the ceiling. The mania in my receptors—hatched in the sulfur-bright updrafts, wired for plumb lines and sharp lintels—itches intolerably against all this softness. My middle legs drum furiously on the copepod’s bloated flank, trying to steer a blimp into a wall, dabbing wasted bitter esters onto skin that cannot read them. The copepod merely rolls a fraction in the cold eddy, sluggish and indifferent.

I cannot be a herdsman. The tool is broken, not the compulsion.

I crawl to the outer edge of the corral, past the last glowing tuft of violet kelp, to where the stalled water hits the raw volcanic wall, freezes, and plunges downward. I tuck my legs in and drop into the gray rain.

Falling down the outer downdraft is like sinking through liquid soot. Shaved curls of carbonate, dead bacteria, and missed grains of black sand fall with me in a slow, soundless curtain. Halfway down the dark facade of a tenement, a patch of tiled masonry peels away from the wall—three inches of stark black-and-white checks rippling like heavy silk in oil. It misses me by half a body-length, folding silently around a mason-pair emerging from a drainage slit below, vanishing with them into an aqueduct before the displaced water has even settled against my bristles.

I catch hold of a cornice lower down, deeper in the fissure, where the tallow-light of the kelp dims to a bruised, smoky plum. 

I am just above the stagnant threshold where the oxygen thins and the giant, blind breakers pry the dead wards apart. From the dark grates beneath me drifts the chalky, dead-protein smell of chewed foundations. Above me soar the bright, frantic avenues. And right here, wedged between the two, is something the clean architects above try to avoid.

A seam.

Two massive tenement spires, rooted on opposite sides of the narrow basalt pocket, have been cantilevered inward toward the same rising vent-plume for seasons. Here, in the dim middle-deep, they have finally met. 

Because the fissure walls were never parallel, the two towers have collided at a skewed, four-degree slant. Where their crisp balconies kiss, the inherited geometry breaks down. Two separate grids of rectangular windows and ninety-degree parapets are grinding into one another off-true, forming a pinched, jagged cleft of acute wedges and crooked overhangs. 

The water here stinks of chemical confusion. Dozens of mason-pairs have arrived at this junction, laid down conflicting trails of formic acid, spun tight, baffled loops, and retreated upward toward open water where their straight lines still work. Left behind in the cleft are stray, uncoupled worms—old or sluggish extruders bumped loose in the traffic—coiling aimlessly in the tight gap, oozing thick, undirected gobbets of white paste that threaten to choke the drainage channels entirely.

I scuttle into the crooked shadow of the V-shaped cleft. 

My left forelimb—still flat and true—finds purchase on the clean sill of the eastern spire. My right forelimb—shortened, notched, ending in that rough, chipped tooth—hooks into the acute, crumbling crevice where the western spire rams into its neighbor at four degrees off-plumb. 

For the first time since the basalt grain snapped my edge, my stance feels level. A square limb slips out of an acute angle; a broken one locks right in.

Below me, a riderless worm drifts up out of a narrowed shaft, its translucent gut heavy with recycled chalk from the breakers' sewers, its bristles rowing in a slow, blind wave. It bumps into the skewed junction and begins to knot itself, collar flaring uselessly against empty brine. 

I do not climb onto its back. There is no room in the tight seam for riding, and no straight wall to steer it along. Instead, bracing my hind legs across the gap between the two mismatched worlds, I reach down and tap a slow, sparse, lopsided beat against its swollen collar with one middle leg. *Stay. Spill.*

The worm presses its head into the crooked cleft and purges a thick, formless cushion of aragonite slurry, binding the two incompatible ledges together. Before the worm has even undulatingly backed away, I am working the wet mass. 

You cannot cut a sharp bevel in a four-degree collision; if you shave it thin, the weight of the overhanging towers will snap the joint like dry shell. It needs a rough, heavy fillet—an ugly scar of packed stone to brace the shear. I scoop sand from my pouch and ram it deep into the thickening paste. With my intact left trowel, I flush the mortar smooth against the eastern wall; with my jagged right wrist, I rake deep, scored furrows into the tight angle on the western side, giving the next layer of paste rough teeth to grip onto.

I gather my sand from the outer downdrafts where the gray silt-rain collects in dark drifts along the ledges. Lately, tasting the black grains as I tuck them beneath my thorax, I have noticed strange ones mixed among the ordinary seafloor grit. They are smaller, pitted all over with microscopic craters, carrying a faint, sharp tang that makes my feathery receptors curl—not the warm, rotten-egg breath of the bottom vents, where the kelp feeds, but something cold, biting, and sour, stripped raw out of the ceiling rock far above. 

I do not know what makes them, up there where the ocean presses against the roof. I only know that these fizzed, pitted grains lock tighter into the wet carbonate than smooth sand ever did. 

So I stay here in the violet-gray dusk of the seam, wedged between two perfect ideas that do not quite match. Above me, the clean masons narrow the sky; below me, my bloated cousins eat the dark floor; flatworms glide past my crooked crevice because their neat, square-checkered skins go instantly visible against my scarred, diagonal masonry. When a lost worm wanders into the cleft, heavy with stone, I reach out from the gap, drum a brief stillness into its collar, and pack another pitted grain into the fault line that holds the quiet together.
