To be down on the sunlit plaza is to be an apology—a thing forever tiptoeing, forever proving to the floor that you are not a seed or a scar. Even the grooms, who own the night, are bound to the tyranny of the straight line, pacing the steps like blind, sleepless clerks checking the sums of an empire that never spends its wealth.

If I am here, I am not on the jade. I am underneath it, in the dark where the chord is made.

Go past the pink, pleated lip of a shaft. Drop through the emerald twilight where the reed-mouths stream like wet flags, past the acoustic choke where the falling water pinches into a steady, chest-shaking F-sharp, and keep falling until the green light fails altogether. Two hundred feet beneath the immaculate geometry of the terraces, the city-plant gives up any pretense of right angles. Down here, nobody sees, and no groom’s tendrils ever reach. The architecture turns visceral: vast, vaulted cisterns of pale, unpigmented root-wood, curved like the insides of whales, ribbed with buttresses thick as redwoods that sweat cool, mineral water in the pitch black.

The noise down here isn't a distant organ chord; it is the inside of the organ pipe itself. The air is atomized water and booming pressure. Every surface vibrates so fiercely that a loose drop of condensation doesn't trickle—it dances into mist off the pale wood.

I am clinging to the ceiling of one of the dry vaults—an air-pocket trapped high between two plunging shafts, where the roar of an F-sharp on my left and a low C on my right cross each other through six feet of resonant, stretched timber, setting up a slow, shivering beat frequency in the wall.

I have eyes, though there is nothing to see down here except the very faint, ghostly blue-green bioluminescence of stray bacteria washing down from the upper world before the valley’s bitter terpenes finish killing them. My eyes are huge, dark, and oil-drop simple, leftover things from an ancestor that fell over the mountain rim ten thousand generations ago—maybe a cave-salamander washed in on a storm, maybe a bat or a crevice-spider, it hardly matters now because the basin remade me to fit its blind spots.

Think of something built like a flattened, pale-grey whip-scorpion crossed with a lungfish, eighteen inches from nose to tail, pressed into the vaulted groin where two great xylem-bundles fork. My skin is cool and slick, coated in a stolen mucus brushed off the backs of reed-mouths, so the chamber wall reads me as just another fold of its own damp lining. Four wide, webbed limbs hold me upside-down without claws, using broad pads of soft, wrinkled skin that grip the wet wood the way wet paper clings to glass.

Why live in the deafening dark when there is warm celery-scented sun two hundred feet up?

Because everything the upper valley refuses to acknowledge comes down the drains.

Up there, nothing advances and nothing decays. Down here is the basin’s unconscious—the fast, roaring river of everything sloughed, dropped, drowned, or rejected. Twice an hour, I crawl out along the vibrating ceiling to the wet lip where the shaft’s waterfall smashes into the deep sump pool below, churning the black water into a permanent, waist-high froth of pale foam.

I don't hunt in the torrent; the torrent would tear me apart. I hunt the eddies—the quiet, circular backwaters tucked behind the massive wooden ribs where the foam spins slowly before being sucked down toward the deep mountain fissures. I lower two long, ribbon-like barbels from my jaw into the spinning suds and read the history of the morning.

Here comes a beetle from three thousand feet below the valley rim, carried up on a noon thermal, dropped onto an esplanade, poisoned by the waxy sweat, and slid neatly into Shaft Four-Hundred-and-Twelve. Still fresh. Here is a curled, translucent peel shed from a skimmer’s foot. Here is a uric pellet from a stilt, dissolving into faint, sharp ammonia, and tangled right beside it—rare, prized—a bruised, detached groom-tendril, snipped off when some night-worker caught itself in a contracting stomatal slit at dawn, still heavy with concentrated, golden phloem-sugar and strange plant-hormones that taste like copper and lightning.

When you eat the shed tendrils of the grooms, over centuries, it does something to your nerves.

I am not smart the way a crow or a man is smart; I have no tools and nothing to build with in a world without stones or twigs. Instead, I have a vast, reverberant memory made entirely of pressure and pitch. My whole flattened ribcage is stretched with taut, drumhead membranes coupled directly to my spine. Sitting motionless on my vaulted ceiling, I don't just hear the two shafts beside me; through the dense, water-filled wood of the under-structure, I feel the whole three-mile chord of the basin braided together in my bones.

I know things even the grooms do not know.

A groom knows its own column, its half-mile of ninety-degree step, and whatever chemical gossip its neighbor passes along at midnight. It knows the local pressure. Sitting down here at the acoustic crossroads where forty drainage tunnels merge toward the mountain’s deep throat, I feel the whole city-plant breathing as a single beast.

I know when a cloud shadow the size of a county slides across the western courts, because within four minutes the pumps along that flank ease off by three percent, and thirty shafts a mile away sigh downward by a comma of pitch, shifting the standing wave in my ceiling two inches to the left. I know when a glider dives into a throat sixty yards off—*thump-whoosh*, a momentary cork in the flute, a sudden shower of torn pink reed-mouth scraps tumbling into my sump pool twenty seconds later, dinner delivered on a hydraulic chute.

And I know the one secret the valley keeps from itself.

Crawl with me—slowly, flat against the roaring timber, sliding through narrow, flooded crawlspaces between the colossal root-buttresses where the turgor-thrum is so loud it blurs vision—out toward the far southern edge, deep behind the plumb green cliff that weeps into the clouds.

Down here, four hundred feet below the sunlit plazas where the grooms never come and the light never reaches, the city-plant finally touches rock.

It is the only stone in the world. Cold, black, wet mountain granite, jagged and ancient, carrying the weight of the whole green basin in a grip of white, blind anchor-roots as thick as houses. Here, where living wood meets dead geology, there are no crisp margins. The roots squeeze into fractures, swell, crack the granite with patient hydraulic violence, and bleed thick, milky latex into the dark seams to seal the gaps.

And right here, wedged in a cool, dripping pocket between black stone and pale root, something is growing that would send a thousand grooms into a frenzy of chemical erasure if they ever smelled it.

A burl. An irregular, knobby, gloriously ugly twist of pale wood, no bigger than a barrel, tucked deep inside a granite cleft where the hormone-signals from above wash out too thin to govern properly. Every few years, in the pitch dark, that rogue meristem puts out a blind, white, curling shoot—a forgotten memory of the wild cliff-tree this monument used to be ten million years ago, before the grooms smoothed its mind into plazas and arches. The shoot has three tiny, vestigial, yellow-white leaves that never see the sun. They unfurl in the warm mist, live for a week on stolen root-sugar, and drop off into the trickle at my feet.

I don't eat them. They are tough and bitter with ancient tannins.

Instead, I curl my slick, flattened body into the warm hollow between the black stone and the twisted burl, resting my chin on a fallen leaf—the only litter in three square miles of perfection. Above me, through four hundred feet of thrumming timber and rushing water, the great chord shifts up a fifth: dawn has hit the eastern rim. The pumps are spinning up to daytime roar; the grooms are folding themselves back into their high, tight slits; the skimmers are setting their suction feet against the rising tide.

Down in the dark, pressed against the cold truth of the mountain and the one crooked branch in paradise, I close my oil-drop eyes and let the thunder rock me to sleep.
