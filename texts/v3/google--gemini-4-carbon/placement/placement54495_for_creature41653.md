Before the sun clears the eastern rim, I know how cold the night was by the pitch of the roof-flue above my head. 

Four shiver-hens wedged themselves into the slate chimney-neck at dusk, one stacked atop the next like greased stoppers in an oil skin, and in the last black hour before light their vibration changes. It drops out of the floorboards and climbs into the jawbone—a tight, frantic, sub-audible trill that means the glaze-ice outside has thickened across the vent-slits and the birds are burning through the last of yesterday’s shrimp-fat in their crops. 

In the dark, without lifting my head from the coiled thigh of my sister’s husband, I tap twice with my knuckle against the underside of the bone rafter an inch from my nose. *Loose the stopper.* Above me in the loft’s cramped triangle, a bare heel shifts; someone pulls the bottom hen free by its hot, greasy flanks, letting a needle-thin draft of razor air hiss down into our faces for half a second before the upper three birds slump downward to plug the gap again. Even that thimbleful of outside air smells of flint and dry salt. Around me in the sleep-pile, six broad chests expand in slow unison, drinking the sharp draft before the animal-heat swallows it back up.

My left hand is already awake, twitching against my ribs, rehearsing tensions. 

I am forty winters old—or close enough; we count in major seam-shifts rather than years—and I have thickened past any use on the high cliff-scaffolds. My ribs have sprung wide as a rendering-tub; the fat-pads over my cheekbones have crowded my eyes into narrow creases even indoors, when the bone goggles hang loose around my neck on their braided gut string. The middle finger of my left hand ends at the first knuckle, sheared clean off twelve seasons back when two dry-stacked foundation courses jumped sideways during an unannounced blow on the west wall. When I hum into my partner’s shoulder in the dark and tap out the morning’s work against her collarbone, that missing tip gives my phrasing a flat, clipped hitch—an accent everyone in our tower knows before they open their eyes.

*South lip,* my fingers say against her greasy skin. *Axle-grease. Stay-check.*

She answers without waking all the way, two warm palms pressing flat against my ears—*take the fat bird, wind on the drop.*

---

I do not work the slush-lanes anymore. When I duck out through the triple gut-curtains into the alley just after the sun’s blade drops into the basin, the chaos of the morning thaw is already surging past my knees, and I step sideways to let the quicker wrists have it. 

All around me, night-bridges of solid brine are snapping away from the horn gutters and dropping into the spongy rime with dull, muffled *whumps* like wet hides hitting wool. A crew of adolescents shoots past on the rib-bone catwalk overhead, flashing their slate batons so fast the black stone looks like strobe-sparks against the violet sky, hauling a fresh-peeled sheet of clear blister-ice up toward the melt-terraces. Down between my boots, a tide of shiver-hens pours down the dished threshold steps, silent as spilled tallow, beaks scissoring frantically at a pink slush-gush of blind mountain shrimp.

I press my back flat against the sunlit slate of Tower Six to let a bull- beast shoulder through the two-foot gap. Its grey fungal-wool apron drags across my gut-parka, leaving a streak of salty slime, and as its middle hooves suction onto the wet stone beside my boot, I feel the faint, rhythmic *thud* of its broad heart right through my layered soles. Its shovel-horn snout twitches once against the tower’s lowest course, tasting the hydraulic hum deep inside the basalt, finding it steady, moving on.

My place is forty paces south, where the city stops leaning on itself and starts leaning against nothing.

You can tell the parapet-crews by how we walk: knees permanently bent an inch deeper than the alley-folk, weight sitting back in our heels, chins tucked low to keep the updraft off our throats. At the southern lip, the enclosing stone disappears, and the glare doubles. Light doesn’t just fall from the black-violet membrane overhead; it boils straight up out of the blinding white abyss of the cloud-sea below, sneaking under the bottom edge of your bone goggles if your cheek-pads aren’t smeared thick with lamp-soot and tallow. 

Out here, six paces from a three-thousand-foot drop, stand the three great vertebral capstans and the anchor-blocks. 

If you look at the settlement from an upper balcony, it looks wedged into the cliff. Stand where I stand, with my heels four inches from the smoothed basalt edge, and you understand that the whole stacked, groaning warren of slate and bone is slowly trying to slide off the mountain on a greased skid of its own freezing brine. What holds the outer row of towers on the shelf are forty-two tension-stays braided from coastal bladder-kelp, thick as my thigh, lashed around the buttress-stones of the lower stalls and anchored down into deep hourglass eyes drilled into the parapet lip centuries ago.

Those stays are mine. Mine, and the bone axles of the capstans, and the three-foot black slate mirror mounted on its whale-joint swivel over the void.

All morning, while the city behind me silently pries up floors and harvests wall-worms in the white-hot noon slots, I move along the lip on my knees with a horn pot of clarified worm-oil and a flat bone marlinspike. Kelp is an alien thing up here. It belongs to the crushing, wet world under the cloud-floor; brought up into our razor-dry inversion, it wants to shrink, split, and turn brittle as dried gut, while the freezing brine splashing off the lower gutters tries to salt-rot it from the inside out. I don’t look at the cables—looking closely at dark surfaces in this glare burns green ghosts into your eyes even through burned bone-slits. I pull off one mitten for ten seconds at a time, tucking my bare, calloused hand under the stiff hide apron wrapping a splice, and *feel* what the tower behind me is doing.

A cable under proper load hums very faintly when a stall-beast shifts against the foundation thirty feet back. If it feels dead, the tower has frost-locked to its neighbors and will shear a wall when the midday sun melts the eastern ice-pillars. If it sings too tight against my thumb-pad, the building has crept a half-inch toward the abyss during the night’s freeze. Then I stand, pull my black slate baton from my belt, and clack it twice—*flat, sharp*—catching the eye-slit of a pry-crew up on a third-story rib-bridge, signing two quick flicks of the wrist: *wedge north, bleed your footing.* Within two minutes, ten men are leaning on bone levers in the slush behind the tower, and under my bare palm inside the hide wrap, the thick kelp hawser sighs and goes slack by the breadth of a fingernail. 

Before my fingers stiffen, I plunge my hand back into the front of my parka, pressing my chilled knuckles directly into the spun-glass plumage of the fat shiver-hen riding against my breastbone. Its syrinxless throat drills silently against my ribs, pumping fierce, dry shrimp-heat back into my wrist.

---

There is another reason I keep the southern lip, though nobody signs about it. 

Twelve times between my fourteenth season and my twenty-fifth—before my ribcage widened too far and my blood thickened past forgiving—I went over the edge in the hide sling.

Most people in the basin live and die without ever leaving the stone square. To them, the cloud-glare below the parapet is simply a bright floor where bone and kelp grow once every three moons when the capstan turns. Even the cliff-climbers who hang seventy feet above the alleys on sinew lines keep their bellies turned firmly toward the basalt; if a pebble slips from their chisel and drops off the southern edge, they do not watch it go.

I watched. And when I was lean and light enough not to waste hauling-weight on the capstan beasts, I climbed into the stiffened seal-hide basket beside forty bladders of worm-oil and twenty bricks of pressed shrimp, wrapped a scarf of greased gut three times over my nose and mouth, and let six grey beasts walk me backward off the lip of the world.

Nobody up here has the signs to talk about the Halfway Ledge. Our wrist-words are built for solid things that flash, slide, freeze, and stack. Down in the cloud-belly, two thousand feet below the inversion, light has no edges. The scalpel-sun vanishes into a suffocating, directionless milk that presses wetly against your eyelids until your bone goggles clog with grey rime and you have to shove them up onto your forehead—exposing your naked eyes to a dim, shadowless pearl-glow that makes your stomach lurch for lack of a horizon.

And the air. Thick as warm blubber, heavy enough to drown in. Halfway down, my oversized lungs always began to burn—not from cold, like up here, where a careless gasp stabs like swallowed glass, but from *weight*, as if someone were pouring wet sand down my windpipe. 

Yet it wasn't the breathing that ruined me for the indoor lofts afterward. It was the noise.

In the thick, wet air of the cloud-shelf, nothing drinks vibration. The first time my boots touched the slick black ice of the trading ledge, a melt-drop fell off the hide sling and hit a puddle beside my heel, and the *plip* cracked inside my skull like a slate tile snapping against my eardrum. I dropped to all fours, clapping both mittens over my ears in terror, convinced the mountain was splitting open. Ten paces away in the swirling mist, three figures stepped out from behind a stack of whale-ribs—our sister-blood from the unseen shore below. 

They looked wrong in ways that still make my left hand twitch when I remember them. Narrow-shouldered, long-necked, their faces pale all over without a trace of the purple-black goggle-burn across their noses, wrapped in slick, dripping capes of stitched fish-skin rather than dry blubber-hide. They were hurting as badly as we were—clutching their narrow chests, lips blue-grey in air too thin for them, flinching away from the diffuse cloud-brightness that felt like cave-gloom to me. 

We worked in frantic haste to swap the loads before either crew passed out, rolling bundles of salt-stiff kelp into our sling while dragging our oil-bladders onto their ice-hooks. Nobody spoke—their throats were cramping in the chill, and ours didn't know how—but because the air was so thick and wet, *everything made sound*. Their fish-skin coats rasped horribly whenever an arm moved. Whale-ribs clattered together like thunderbolts. Worst of all, we could hear each other breathing: ragged, wet, animal whistling saws tearing in and out of human mouths, an obscenity so intimate it felt worse than stripping naked in the snow. Once, when a slick bundle of kelp slipped between me and a long-necked girl from below, we both lunged to catch it before it went over the ledge; her bare, long-fingered, salt-pickled hand closed directly over my stubby, three-and-a-half-fingered grip, and through her open, gasping mouth came a sharp, voiced *grunt*. 

It went straight into my chest—not through bone-contact, like sleep-humming at home, but flying loose and shameless through empty air to strike my skin from three feet away. I yanked my hand back as if scalded by boiling brine. She stared at my soot-masked eyes, her pale pupils huge in the fog, then deliberately tapped two long wet fingers twice against the kelp-knot between us—a clumsy, slow echo of the old shared signs from centuries ago—before rolling the bundle into my sling and backing away into the mist.

---

I haven't been able to survive the drop for fifteen seasons now; the last time I went down, dark blood leaked from my nostrils into my mouth-wrap before the sling even hit the top of the cloud-deck, and my heart hammered so hard against my widened ribs I couldn't stand for two days after they hauled me back into the glare. 

So I stay on the lip. I am the last pair of hands in the basin before the rope goes into the white, and the first pair to touch what comes out of it.

When noon hits directly overhead and the alleys behind me steam with flash-fogs of diamond frost, I step over to the great swivel-mirror at the parapet’s center. I wipe the dry rime off the polished black slate with an oiled scrap of felt, loosen the bone-wedge at its base, and tilt the three-foot slab a fraction of an inch downward. 

A beam of stolen, razor-pure mountain sunlight shoots silently off the dark stone and plunges straight down into the blinding cloud-sea two thousand feet below. 

I hold my four-and-a-half-fingered hand flat across the bottom corner of the slate, cutting the beam cleanly: *flash—flash—hold—flash.* Are the whale-ribs stacked on the shelf? Is the kelp cut?

Nothing moves in the abyss. Behind me, a three-hundred-pound curtain of clear glaze-ice shears off Tower Two’s upper gutter and lands in the slush-lane with the soft, dead *whuff* of a pillow hitting a sleeping-mat. A pair of pewter-shelled crabs scuttle over my boot-heels, chasing an oil smear left by my grease-pot, and vanish into a dry-stacked crevice before a passing child’s bone hook can snag them. Inside my parka, the shiver-hen settles its hot chin deeper into the hollow of my collarbone and purrs into my teeth.

Then, far down in the featureless white glare beneath my toes, a tiny, bruised spot of brightness swells open like oil spreading on water, blinks twice—slow, clumsy, diffused through a half-mile of wet wool—and goes out.

My right wrist lifts before I even know I’m thinking, black slate baton flashing three sharp cuts into the silent, burning air toward the beast-stalls behind me: *Harness six. Clear the capstan. The coast is on the shelf.*
