You would find me four tiers down from the violet glare of the canopy, where the noon sun only arrives in narrow, erratic ribbons, sliced thin by six overlapping layers of catwalks, cable trays, and strangler-fig roots before it ever hits the deck plates. 

Down here, the air tastes of wet copper, bruised fern, and the sweet, heavy tallow of the drip-oilers. My ears went bad for air-sound twenty cycles of the great wheel ago—the high exhaust stacks saw to that when I was young and greedy for bird-eggs—so the screaming of the green flocks overhead is nothing to me now but a faint, tinny shimmer, like wind in dry seeds. Even the frogs in the cisterns below are just a pleasant, fizzy tickle in my kneecaps. 

Instead, I live inside the *thud-hiss*. It comes up through the horn-pads of my heels, climbs my shins, and settles behind my breastbone, forty seconds to the stroke, steady as tide-water.

I am not one of the big matrons who hold title to the glazed chimneys up on the second tier, with thirty cousins braiding lianas on their platforms and forty clutch-pans full of fat tubers. My ribs are just as wide as theirs, and my blood just as dark under the nail,, smeared thick at the cheekbones with sump-butter for when I have to climb into the sun—but I don't have the patience for family arguments or the constant scraping of beetle-grease around root beds. I keep a smaller, stranger territory.

My hammock—woven double-thick from boiled bark-cord so the wet won't rot it through in a year—is slung inside the gutted iron shell of an auxiliary switch-house, bolted to the flank of Pier Fourteen. Most people avoid the piers. Too close to the edge-draughts, too near the long drop into the white wool below, where there is nothing to hook onto if a gust takes your line. Inside my switch-house, the slate panels are still mounted in their angle-iron frames, row on row of dead knife-switches green with verdigris and thick brass dials whose glass faces broke before my grandmother’s grandmother had teeth. Mud-wasps build grey organ-pipes inside the voltmeters; tiny, translucent tree-frogs sleep curled inside the zero-stops of the ammeters. 

I am a keeper of the sloughs and the heavy jars, and a reader of the deep grease.

Come out onto my porch—it’s only an inspection grating cantilevered over a three-hundred-foot shaft where a six-foot drive rod plunges straight down toward the invisible boilers—and watch what I actually do. 

Suspended from the underside of the grating on bronze chains are twelve green-glass battery jars, the big square ones with flanged rims, salvaged over three lifetimes from the dark accumulator rooms behind the eastern condensers. Each one sits wedged in a cradle of split bamboo strapped tight against the cast-iron guide-collar of the great drive rod. Every forty seconds, that rod strokes downward nine feet, pauses with a heavy, oiled sigh, and draws back up. It doesn't buzz with the sixty-cycle fretfulness of the transformers where the chimney-clans brew their sour mash. This is slower, heavier work.

I don't brew mash. Mash is for drinking in two days while you gossip on warm bricks. In my jars, I make the black vinegar and the clear, biting oil-cutters, and the slow, salty paste we call *iron-marrow*. Into the glass goes crushed scale-insects scraped from the underside of bromeliads, the tart yellow berries of the mist-parasites that only grow out on the wind-baffles, handfuls of flaked rust from the sweetest low-carbon steel plates (you have to taste the rust first; cast-iron scale is bitter and ruins a batch, while blister-steel from the old uptake flues has a clean, blood-salt tang), and gallons of first-catch condensate before it hits the moss filters. 

Left on the sixty-cycle hum, that mixture turns to grey slime. Strapped to the forty-second stroke of Pier Fourteen, warmed to the exact heat of a sleeping dog’s belly by the friction of the guide-collar, it takes four shifts of the central valve—four months—to turn clear dark red, as sharp on the tongue as a static spark. A thumb-sized gourd of my red vinegar traded up-tier buys me twenty pounds of dried tuber-flour, three braided wire snares, or a freshly cold-hammered chisel blade. Nobody wants to live out on the edge-piers where the cloud-swells hit you sideways like wet canvas, so everyone pays me to do the waiting.

My days have the shape of the fog.

When the sky is hard purple and dry overhead, I climb down into the sumps and the settling-troughs—places too tight for the big-shouldered hunters and too greasy for the gardeners. I carry a short stick of ironwood shod with a brass ferrule, and three scraping-spoons carved from the shoulder-blades of red apes. 

Down where the light is green and murky, big cast-iron troughs catch the weeping of the main bearings. What drips down from the giant three-RPM turbines isn’t dirty black sludge; it comes out of the deep siphons far below already golden-brown and heavy as honey, smelling faintly of hot stone and something very old, like crushed ferns pressed under a mountain. Where it hits the warm water in the troughs, skin forms—thick, leathery mats of pale bacteria and mineral wax that feed on the oil.

Left alone, sometimes a mat grows too thick, plugs a weir, and sends good bearing-tallow spilling over the lip down into the abyssal trusses where nobody can reach it, starving the frogs and fouling the fern-banks below. So I walk the trough-rims barefoot, my toes curled over the slick, warm iron, testing the depth of the golden grease with my stick. I skim the pale skins off—dried on slate up in the sun, they make tough, translucent lantern-panes or waterproof wrapping for salt—and ladle the cleanest amber tallow into plugged bamboo joints. That’s the good salve. Rubbed into cracked heel-pads or smeared over your shoulders before crossing the high, unshaded tie-rods at noon, it keeps the fierce sky from blistering the plum-skin off your back.

Sometimes, while I am down there crouching on a flange, scraping quietly, I put my brass-shod stick against the main bearing cap and rest my jawbone on the wood handle.

The old listeners up at the central rotunda think they hear the heart of the basin because they wait for the great distributor valve to click once a month. They like drama—the big *clunk*, the ten thousand birds going up like sparks, the steam booming down a cold mile of pipe. That’s calendar-work. That’s weather.

Down on Pier Fourteen, leaning my teeth into the bearing housing of a primary shaft, I hear underneath the weather. 

Through the wood and into my skull comes the slow, planetary grind of roller bearings the size of millstones turning in an oil bath. *Rrrr-uhm. Rrrr-uhm.* Twenty seconds per revolution. And beneath *that*, coming straight up the vertical steel of the pier from thousands of feet below the cloud-wool—from whatever unimaginable floor or furnace anchors this whole four-mile sky-forest—comes a deeper, tighter thrumming. Not steam. Something heavier. Fluid moving under tremendous, patient head-pressure through pipes wide enough to walk six abreast inside, and the faint, crystalline chime of expansion joints taking the weight of the wind when a gale hits the lower miles of our legs down in the blind white dark.

Once in five years, maybe, I feel a hiccup. Just a tiny grit-scrape in the twenty-second roll, or a harmonic shudder from two thousand feet down, as if a boulder of clinker the size of a house just broke loose and dropped into an ash-pit nobody with eyes will ever see. 

When I feel that, I don't keep it to myself. I take my brass wrench-hook off my belt and rap sharply against the pier’s main gusset plate. 

*Tak-tak-tak. Pause. Tak-tak.* 

Just a note sent into the web: *Deep slip on Fourteen. Watch the western crane-tracks; a counterweight’s going to settle an inch before sundown.* 

Half a minute later, faintly through the soles of my feet, comes an answer from three tiers up and half a mile east—maybe one of the old women gardening leaks under the south viaduct, maybe a cable-rigger hanging in his harness stringing vine-bridges. *Tak-tak.* Heard you. 

Then comes the afternoon swell. 

You always know ten minutes before the cloud crests the western wind-baffles because the swallows and the copper-green parakeets drop down out of the upper trusses all at once, crowding onto the secondary steam lines just above my switch-house, fluffing their feathers and grumbling—a dry, rattling vibration I feel in the handrails as ten thousand claws clamp onto the iron. The light goes from sharp violet-gold to a strange, bruised silver. A cool breath slides between the girders, smelling of nothing at all—empty sky-water, thin and sterile—until it hits the hot brick flues and explodes into fat, rolling billows of warm fog.

Within two breaths, the world beyond six feet is gone. Just luminous, blinding white milk pouring sideways through the latticework, turning every spiderweb into a sagging necklace of diamonds and making the big woody lianas drip like gutter-spouts.

That’s my favorite hour. Nobody travels the outer piers in a fog-out if they don't live there. The red apes huddle in steaming, irritable heaps inside the crooks of the warm exhaust uptakes; the big monitor lizards tuck their noses under pipe-lagging and go limp. 

I clip my bronze hook onto the guide-wire outside my door, swing out over the invisible drop without bothering to look down, and climb thirty feet up a sweating cast-iron column to a wide, shallow dish formed by the cap of a blind rivet-cluster. Water collects there—half cold cloud-milk, half warm drip from an overhead expansion loop—making a round basin three feet across and four inches deep, cleaner and softer than anything in the cisterns. 

I unclip my belt of bone scrapers and bamboo grease-pots, scrub the gray fly-ash and old tallow off my face with a handful of crushed fern-pith, and just sit on the warm, plum-colored oxide of the girder cap while the white cloud streams past my shoulders. Every forty seconds, the pier lifts me by the thickness of a thumbnail and sets me back down. *Thud-hiss.* In my chest, my own breath—slow, deep, pulling thin air into big ribs—falls into four-to-one time with the stroke below. Five breaths to one beat of the siphon. 

In ten minutes the cloud bank will tear itself to shreds on the eastern condenser towers, the violet sun will slam back down like an opened furnace door, and I will have to smear fresh sump-butter across my nose, go back down to my battery jars, and bleed twofingers of clear water off the top of the third vinegar crock before the yeast-skin sinks. Maybe later, if the north-side pipes are cooling toward the monthly click, my sister’s boy will come skating down the diagonal tie-rod with a basket of parboiled tubers slung on his wide back, rapping twice on my switch-house doorframe so I feel him coming in my teeth before his shadow crosses the grate.

Until then, I sit on the warm iron at the edge of the four-mile world, wrapped in wet white cloud, listening through my heels to the deep dark engine keeping its own counsel far below.
