I am down in one of the deeper troughs, five yards off a pale talus slope, where the matte-olive plates and ochre tendrils stack seven deep into a single cubic yard of breathless warmth.

At first glance you would not separate me from the phenomenon of the crowding itself. You would hear the low, dry purr of the space straining to hold its own coordinates together, and you would see the hairline seams of violet and amber crawling along the places where smooth porcelain stems pass cleanly through one another. Only if you stayed on the razor-sharp flint until your knees ached, watching that specific knot of seven-fold greenery without blinking, would you notice that the purr does not stay quite constant. Every few minutes, its pitch shifts by a hair—not louder, just *truer*, dropping into a clean fifth or an unstrained octave—and at the exact moment of the shift, a newly grown tendril from an adjacent bush slips smoothly into the stack as an eighth layer, without the slightest resistance or static *click*.

I have no horn plates, no charcoal stilts, no mica shell. I am not an object occupying coordinates; I am an arrangement *of* the coordinates where objects meet. If you parted the heavy, dry fronds with your hands—feeling that taut, invisible silk pull against your wrists—you would find nothing at the center of the thicket except a dense, self-sustaining lattice of chromatic thread: hair-thin filaments of violet and amber refraction woven into a stationary, fist-sized cat's cradle that clings to the intersections rather than the stems.

The bone-colored ribbons are grazers; they drink the spatial tension until the thicket snaps apart into ordinary, thin air. I do the opposite. I am a weaver of overlap, a kind of stationary, non-baryonic reef-builder.

From the inside, this world does not look like miles of green porcelain under a flat afternoon sky. I have no eyes for open air; thin, single-occupancy daylight registers to me only as a dry, blinding vacuum, an arid desert of mere three-ness where nothing touches deeply enough to think. My senses begin where two solid-state lattices try to claim the same femtometer of volume. 

Every stem that pierces my cradle brings its own metric with it—its own private angle of sunlight, its own directional grain, its own stubborn bookkeeping of *here*. Left to themselves, overlapping branches chafe. Their intersections fizz with wasted aberration, leaking stray photons and acoustic hum into the dead air until a ribbon-grazer smells the strain and comes to strip them apart. 

I take that raw, buzzing friction and fold it neatly. By shifting my refractive threads in tiny, continuous adjustments—the smallest possible micro-updates, smoother than the jump of a hare-ribbon, slower than the drift of a blue-shadowed slice—I bevel the angles where the lattices cross. I match the phase of an olive plate's internal crystal to the slant of a verdigris fan thirty degrees askew, tucking their incompatible shadows into a shared pocket so they stop fighting for the room. The harsh, sputtering violet quiets down to a cool, steady opalescence. The space stops straining and *locks*, able to hold eight, ten, twelve simultaneous boughs in the volume of a breadbox without popping apart.

I eat the slack left over when two difficult geometries suddenly agree.

It is a very quiet life, measured in tensions and reliefs, rich with company that never knows it is being hosted. 

Through the dry, solid-state roots of the twelve plants currently threaded through my core, I feel the subterranean *ticks* arriving from the flint floor below. When one of those bodiless knots of crystalline stress swims up beneath our root-weld to graze, the shockwave doesn't reach me as sound; it climbs the rigid stems as a sudden, delicious stiffening of the lattice, twelve slightly different translations of the same sharp *now* ringing upward into my web at twelve fractionally staggered intervals. I catch the chord they make, hold the resonance for half an hour in my amber threads, and bleed the leftover order back down into the rock.

Inside my quieted volume, the yellow-mica burrs thrive by the hundreds. Because I keep the ten-fold density stable, they never have to panic-flicker or starve at the edges of open air. They drift through my chambers like sleepy golden dust motes inside a paperweight, grazing on the stray light I corral into closed loops between the leaves. I feel their tiny, radial bodies indexing from stem to stem across my filaments—a dry, pleasant tickle, like static electricity brushing across fine hairs. Sometimes a charcoal stilt-hunter's needle-tip resolves into focus four paces outside the bush, scanning the trough down a collapsed two-mile pleat; when it does, the optical distortion tugs lightly at my outer threads, warning me before the needle even updates, and I tighten my bevels just enough to mask the hum of the burrs inside, turning our knot acoustically invisible against the pressurized silence of the trough.

My favourite hours—though the light never changes, so they are really just epochs of attention—are when one of the great orthogonal wanderers passes through the trough.

I feel its slate-blue shadow first: a cool, thirty-degree tilt sliding across the local metric before any matter arrives. When the smooth, chalk-warm cross-sections of terracotta and sage glide straight through my thicket, an ordinary intersection flares into a chaotic blaze of amber, nearly tearing the spatial bookkeeping apart. As the massive, unseen flank slices through *me*, however, I stretch every filament wide to meet it. For the ten seconds it takes that alien geometry to pass through my fist-sized cradle, I am briefly holding thirteen layers together—twelve rooted in this sun-baked basin, and one connected to a vast, slow, rhythmic heart beating yards away in an unpointable direction. 

I cannot follow it out into its home plane; I belong to the sharp stone and the dry leaves of this pan. So I do the only thing my nature allows. In the three seconds before the sage-colored blade tapers to a spindle and slips sideways out of the world, I braid a tiny, permanent pleat between its passing metric and the center of my twelve-fold bush.

When the visitor vanishes, the thicket does not snap back to the way it was. Deep inside the stacked porcelain leaves, shielded from the bone-ribbons and the flat afternoon glare, one small pocket of space remains permanently tilted thirty degrees away—casting a tiny, stationary, slate-blue shadow onto the white stone below that belongs to nothing visible in this sky, holding open a quiet window into an adjacent room while the warm, finished silence closes back over the valley.
