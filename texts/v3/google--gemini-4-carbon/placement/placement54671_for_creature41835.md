I would not be in the Whistle-Watch. The air up under the crown of the vault is too thin on grease and too heavy on responsibility; my knees would ache from locking them on a stool, and I do not trust myself with the lanyard that brings the roar back down on twenty open necks. 

Find me two tiers down, at the north curve where the iron spiral stair pinches tight against the great intake burl. Locker Four, middle berth. 

I am the splint-maker and the boiler-fat render, forty-some years old—which down here means my thumbnails have thickened into yellow horn that no longer needs a brass shim to pry up a rusted cotter-pin, and my lungs have acquired the dry, quiet click at the bottom of an inhale that means the turpentine vapor has finally tanned them for good. My rubber ear-plugs went in thirty-eight years ago; the scar-ridge over my tragus is smooth as soup-bone. If you want my attention while I am hunched over the manifold stove, you do not stomp on the iron floor-plates—the west flywheel pit has a slight flat spot on its main bearing right now that throws a phantom *thud* up the stanchions every ninth stroke, and you’ll only confuse my heels. You reach down and lay two knuckles flat against the back of my neck, right above the tallow-line of my collar.

My trade is the poultice-pots and the copper wire. 

Every twelve minutes, when the lamps bleed down to that merciful, cooling-iron red and the gravel-roar shuts off like a slammed bulkhead, I am out on the second-tier gantry before the filament has stopped shuddering. I don’t carry the long gaff or the heavy valve-wrenches; I’m built wide in the hips and short in the shank, steady on greasy diamond-plate, slung front and back with six stone crocks on a leather yoke. 

Three crocks on my left hip hold the bitter stuff: green copperas ground up with flue-soot, battery-lees, and stinging vinegar distilled from soured browser-mash. Three on my right hold the sweet: rendered oil-licker fat whipped warm with crushed shell-lime, iron filings, and raw, bled sap from the young feeder-veins. 

I am the one talking to the tree, though *talking* makes it sound gentler than it is. It’s closer to driving a blind, stubborn dray-horse through a narrow alley using only a needle and a lump of sugar.

Right now—over the last four hundred shifts or so, maybe a month if anyone up top is still counting moons—my main quarrel is Joint Fourteen, just under the main dynamo bus-bar. A flanged cast-iron elbow carries hot exhaust-condensate back toward the Sump, and two hundred years of sour steam have eaten the belly of the pipe down to paper. You can feel it when you lay your palm on the rust: instead of the round, solid *thrum* of good iron, it flutters under your calluses like a wet bladder. If it blows during the gold light, it’ll scald three yards of phloem-tubing right off the wall, starve Number Two Engine of resin, drop the load on the dynamos, and leave the whole crust ragged and half-awake in a brown-out while the big scissor-jaws come down off the ceiling looking for easy meat in the gloom.

We have no spare elbows. The foundry that cast Joint Fourteen is either dust on the surface or buried under three miles of root-crown. All we have is the wall behind it.

So every third red pause, I slide out to Fourteen. My nephew—twelve years old, still narrow in the chest, wearing his dead grandfather’s spectacles lashed to his skull with braided bast—goes ahead of me with the flat brass shovel. He clears six square feet of sleeping browsers off the swell of pale wood behind the pipe. He’s good; he doesn't scrape the bark and wake the cambium wrong, just pops their suction-feet loose with one crisp shove so they rain down into the fog-pit like dry beans.

Then I’m on my knees in the slick. Thirty-two seconds left. 

I press my bare cheek right against the wood beside the pipe. That’s how you read how hungry a burl is—your fingers are too horned over to tell ninety-eight degrees from a hundred and two, and four degrees is the difference between wood that’s sleeping and wood that’s pushing cell-walls. Today it’s hot, feverish under the condensation, smelling sharp like crushed pine-needles and wet pennies. Good.

With my curved thumb-knife, I score five shallow cross-hatches into the pale, smooth lip of the wood where it’s trying to creep upward toward the copper bus-bar—*keep your nose out of the lightning, old girl*—and rub a thumbful of the green copper-soot hard into the cuts. Even in the red light you can feel a faint, tight cringe go through the grain under your palm, slow as pitch, the sap pulling back a millimeter from the poison. 

Then I wipe my thumb on my chitin thigh-guard, dip two fingers into the warm, sweet tallow-crock, and paint a thick, stinking stripe down the underside of the rotten iron elbow, bridging it back to the lower root-swell. The tree loves the iron filings; it loves the lime from the ground shells. Already, since last shift-week, a smooth, pearly knuckle of callous-wood—pale as pig-fat in the cherry gloom—has crept two inches out from the wall, cupping the thin spot on the pipe’s belly like a thumb over a leaky hose. Another two hundred shifts and it’ll wrap the whole flange in four inches of solid, cross-grained lignum, tighter than shrunk steel, drinking the rust right out of the pores and leaving a living wooden pipe lined with a ghost of black oxide.

*Twenty-seven, twenty-eight—*

Down through the iron soles of my boots comes the deep, sub-audible *clunk* from miles under our heels. The deep valves turning over in the blind dark. You feel it behind your kneecaps first, then in your back teeth.

My nephew taps twice on my hip-plate—*clear*—and he’s already sliding backward toward Locker Four, nimble on the slick rail. I’m slower with the crocks, and today a big, sleek ceiling-predator—eighteen inches of oiled mahogany shell, jaws open half an inch in its red trance—is frozen right across the latch-chain of the nearest stanchion-niche because it dropped down chasing velvet-skins right before the dynamo whined. 

No time for Locker Four. Four seconds. 

I don’t waste half a heartbeat trying to pry the predator off; its hooks are locked into the iron rust like staples while the red holds. I just step right beside it, turn my face into the damp crease where the cast-iron column disappears into a woody armpit of the wall, flip my heavy chitin collar up over my cropped scalp, cinch the draw-cord with my teeth so no thin-shelled tick can drop down my neck-grease, and tuck my thumbs hard behind my yoke-straps.

Overhead, the whistle hits. I don’t hear the note—haven't heard a note since I was three—but my breastbone catches the flat, brass punch of it through the wet air.

Below, the louvers blow. Warm, oily steam hits my ankles first, smelling wonderfully, disgustingly thick of cooked resin and hot brass, rising past my knees in a solid white wall. The red behind my eyelids snaps instantly to bruised, heavy gold.

And then the room comes back to life on top of me.

If you weren’t born in a canvas sling under somebody’s coat, this is the part where you’d lose your mind, throw your arms out, slip off the grease-rail, and go down into the flywheel pit to become tree-food. Because the *shhhk-shhhk-shhhk* doesn't just start around you; you are part of the pillar now, and the hot steam at floor level drives ten thousand fist-sized climbers straight up the stanchions. 

They hit my hobnailed boots in a heavy, clattering surf. Two seconds later they are over my gaiters, their neat, hooked little tarsi clicking on the riveted predator-plates of my apron, climbing me just as indifferently as they climb the wood. I feel the heavy mahogany predator two inches from my left shoulder snap out of its lock—feel the heavy *tock-tock* of its big legs shifting on the iron as it turns upward toward the vaults, dragging the sharp, cold curve of one scissor-mandible harmlessly across the greased leather of my yoke before the rising tide carries it overhead. 

For twelve minutes, I am a warm bump on an iron stalk. 

It isn’t frightening. Honestly, when your lower back hurts from the querns, it’s almost a rest. There is an extraordinary, heavy warmth to having thirty pounds of living shell streaming steadily up your back and shoulders; their pale bellies are slick with warm oil-sweat, moving in one continuous, upward muscle-ripple over my hood like heavy chainmail being hauled up a pulley. Inside my high collar, in the dark space four inches wide in front of my nose, I breathe slow through a rag soaked in weak vinegar to cut the turpentine sting. 

My heels stay wide on the grate. I listen with my shins. 

*Thump-hiss. Thump-hiss.* Number Two Engine is running sweet, fat on resin. Four yards to my right, under the roar of ten million legs, that tiny flat spot on the west flywheel bearing goes *tik... tik... tik* on every ninth turn—no worse than yesterday; we’ve got fifty shifts yet before we have to wedge the shaft and scrape the brass bushing. Down my boots comes the tiny, irregular hail-patter of shells dropping fifty feet onto the lower catwalk where the big hunters are already carving lanes through the crowd up under the ceiling ribs, followed ten seconds later by the localized, oily sprinkle of vault-rain pattering softly against the climbers on top of my head.

I stand there in the dark of my own collar, greased and armoured and buried alive in the maintenance crew, working out in my head how much lime-ash is left in the bottom bin back in Locker Four, and whether my sister’s girl on the upper tier has grown out of her second pair of gaiters yet so we can pass them down to the boy. 

In seven more minutes, I’ll feel the dynamo windings begin to tighten through the iron column against my forehead—a thin, rising hum that makes the bridge of my nose itch right under the spectacles-mark. Five minutes after that, the gold outside my hood will bleed down to cherry-dark, the grinding river on my back will lock instantly into silent, weightless stone, and I will shrug once, sharply, shedding twenty motionless isopods off my shoulders into the quiet air like dry mud off a shovel, pick up my crocks, and take ten more steps back toward the kettle to stir the fat before it scorches on the manifold.
