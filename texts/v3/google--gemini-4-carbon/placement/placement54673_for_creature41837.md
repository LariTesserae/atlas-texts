You would not see me, even if you had eyes left to waste on the ochre dark. You would only notice—after sitting inside a cairn long enough for the roar in your marrow to settle—that one section of the curved wall does not sweat like the rest, and does not stay entirely dry either. It glistens with a pale, tallowy sheen that smells faintly of cold iron and bruised hog-tuber.

If you reached out to touch the dry soapstone, your fingers would meet something with the exact yield of cured leather wrapped over river-slate. And if you pressed harder, you would feel a slow, hydraulic shift beneath the surface, like a heavy tongue rolling over in its sleep.

I am the mortar where there is no mortar. 

Not a mason. I do not have those indecent, root-pale hands, nor the radial rings of struts to haul a heavy bell of gristle across the pan, nor the patience to surf twenty miles of screaming shingle looking for one pebble in ten million that hasn't given up. My agreement with the end of the world is flatter than theirs, and lazier, and older than the habit of building domes.

Outside, in the open boil, creatures like me are thin ribbons of grey, rubbery muscularity—scarcely thicker than a belt-strap and wide as a man's palm—threading downward through the first four feet of churning ovals. Out there, we are silt-leeches, fissure-worms, grease-binders. When the four-minute avalanches hit and the continent loses its grip, we let ourselves be stretched ten, twelve, fifteen feet long between sliding shelves of clay, drinking the warm organic tallow forced straight through our porous skins by the sheer barometric crush, snapping back together like thick rubber bands when the wave passes. We have no front end and no back; we have a top skin coarse as shark-hide to take the grinding chert, and a belly-skin soft and suctioned to taste the mineral grain.

Until one of us brushes against a *tock*.

You cannot imagine what a cairn feels like from underneath, down in the blind, grinding dark where every pebble has been screaming against its neighbor since the mountains came down. Down there, the noise isn't sound; it is an endless, frantic trembling that never lets a molecule rest. And then—suddenly—there is a shadow in the vibration. A cone of terrible, downward certainty plunged into the grease. Thirty thousand tiny anchors locked into a Tuesday four hundred million years ago, throwing a shaft of stillness ten feet deep into the mud beneath their ring.

The first time I drifted into the root-shadow of a cairn, half-torn by a cross-gale that had knotted the water overhead and dropped an acre of stone on my flank, the sudden absence of friction nearly killed me. My skin didn't know how to drink without pressure. I curled into a tight, suffocating knot against the base-course of black jasper, starving in the quiet.

Then a pale hand reached down from the threshold, two corundum-hard thumbnails prying gently at my curled edge—not to pull me out and eat me like a blind eel, which the mason easily could have done, but to *tuck* me in.

Now I live in the chinks. 

I am forty feet of flattened, branching gristle braided entirely through the unmortared curve of this shelter, poured into every hairline gap between the stubborn stones like cold lead run into a crack. Over decades—or over three long afternoons, since time pools weirdly in the dome—my body has forked and thinned and widened to match the negative space of the mason's work. Where three ovals of deep-shield basalt meet and leave a triangular throat no wider than a reed, a lobe of me sits wedged in the dark, pressed tight against three separate memories of the Devonian.

I have become an organ of the house.

The stones are stubborn, yes, just as you know—they hold onto *down* and *stay* with a fierce, mineral grief. But stubbornness is brittle. Even a pebble from five miles under a batholith gets tired when a three-hundred-mile wave of liquefied pan slams against its cheek every six hours, year after thousand-year, while the cairn slides slowly east on a river of grease. Left alone, the tiny hair-grooves the masons scratch into the chert slowly fill with silt, or worse, their directional drag begins to drift out of alignment with the stones beside them. One pebble remembers being the north face of a ridge; the pebble above it starts remembering being the south slump of a trench. They begin to argue in micro-tremors. A gap opens—not wide enough for mud, just wide enough for *now* to leak in. A whistle of wet wind. A trickle of planetary roar bleeding into the dead-air sanctuary.

That is where I thicken.

I feel the argument before a mason’s velvety face-plate ever could, because my skin is pressed flat against both stones at once. When a greywacke oval begins to lose its grip on four hundred million years ago—when it starts wanting to consent to the grease outside—it grows minutely warmer. It starts to sweat. 

I shift my weight through the labyrinth of the wall, pulling fluid from my lobes on the leeward side of the dome and pumping it into the failing joint, swelling until my shark-hide outer lip seals the outer seam against the flying grit, while my soft inner skin grips both stones with ten thousand microscopic suction-pits. I cannot give the stone back its memory; I am meat, not rock, and meat forgets everything every ten minutes. What I can do is hold the stone *still* while it remembers itself. I swallow its kinetic confusion into my own rubbery bulk, passing the tremor down my forty-foot length, bleeding the shudder away through three hundred other joints until it dissipates harmlessly into the soapstone floor.

When the masons come inside, shedding tallow from their heavy grey skirts, the cairn wakes up in a different way.

I know them by weight and by salt. Two of them are curled on the floor right now, their radial knuckles tucked up under their bells, the severed silence thick with the rank, turnip-sweet smell of chewed tuber and wet hide. After they eat, the older one—whose sensory plate is scarred across the left margin where a snapped reed-whip caught it ages back—slumps heavily against the curve of the wall. Against *me*.

It reaches beneath its pouch-flaps, draws out two long, pale hands, and does not reach for a stone. It finds a wide chink at shoulder height where a fold of my pale inner skin bulges slightly into the dry air of the hut, like mortar squeezed between bricks.

It presses two root-soft fingers directly into my flank.

*Slip?* it taps. *Shear?*

I have no hands to tap back. I have only hydraulic tension—the ability to turn any square inch of my skin as hard as horn or as slack as warm tallow. Beneath its fingertips, I ripple a slow, deliberate wave from the eastern base of the dome up toward the crown, mimicking the directional pull I have been fighting for the last six hours, then go abruptly soft at a spot two feet above the threshold where three ironstones have gone entirely senile and slick.

*Tired,* I pulse against its fingers—two slow swells, then a slackening hollow. *Loose. East-low.*

The mason stays pressed against me for a long while, letting me play the whole history of the shelter's drift across its fingertips: the concussive slap of a floating pond that dropped half a mile north and jarred the foundation-ring; the slow, grinding spin we took when a hog-tuber bed snagged our western skirt; the exact five pebbles in the roof that are still holding eighty percent of the sky off our backs while their neighbors sleep.

In payment, before it turns toward the roaring doorway to go fishing in the stone-river for replacements, the mason reaches into its warm pouch, pulls out the chewed, fibrous rind of a tuber—or better, the slick, headless tail of a blind marsh-eel—and tucks it firmly into a pocket between two basalt ovals where my feeding-folds wait. My skin closes over the meat without a sound, drawing it slowly inward to dissolve.

Sometimes, though, the hut is empty for a long time. 

The masons crawl out to surf a liquefaction wave toward some distant gathering, leaving me alone inside a waist-high ring of dry Tuesday afternoon while we slide forty miles across the bottom of the atmospheric ocean. Those are the strangest hours. Without their breathing bulk on the floor, without the wet *tick-tick* of another creature’s pulse to anchor the scale of the room, the interior of the cairn forgets what size it is supposed to be. The thirty thousand stones lean inward upon their own ancient weight, and I, braided through all of them, have to stretch across centuries just to reach from the threshold to the back wall. 

In that deep, dry quiet, pressed flat between rocks that refuse to admit the mountains are gone, I sometimes catch what leaks out of them. Not the big, violent memory-surges that make the mud rear eighty feet into the ochre soup outside—just the small, private stubbornnesses. Along my dorsal skin, a piece of red jasper dreams endlessly of dry frost splitting a cliff-face under a sky so thin and blue it hurts my blind nerves like acid. Three inches away, against my belly, an oval of black chert holds onto the crushing, pitch-black silence of a trench floor before anything on earth had bones.

I lie between them in the dark, forty feet of tough, nameless gristle holding the cold to the heavy and the sharp to the deep, waiting for four pale hands to come back through the door with a fresh piece of the root.
