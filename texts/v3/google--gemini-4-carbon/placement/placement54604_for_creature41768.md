Do not climb as high as the crown-riggers, and do not stay along the breezy fifth-tier galleries where the young ones—if anyone made of cured horn and beeswax can be called young—spend their weeks angling split-root baffles into the gale.

Drop down instead to the second level above the basin, on the eastern curve, where the wall begins to tuck under itself toward the floor. Here the architecture is oldest and thickest. The doorways have been pinched and re-wedged so many times over the epochs that the corridors no longer run straight; they coil like the inner ear of a dry whelk, buried three rooms deep behind fused bulkheads of callus-wood. The rushing wind does not reach into these pockets. The coriander-and-camphor gale shears past the outer apertures with a low, bottle-necked *whoom*, leaving the air inside stagnant, heavy with crustal gas, and almost intolerably loud.

This is a bass-trap, a tuning-chamber for the root of the global chord. If you reached through the narrow, sphincter-smooth crawlway—squeezing your shoulders past a sill polished slick as soap—your hand would meet a low arch bridging the back of the room.

Wait. Keep your fingers spread against the curve of that arch.

Notice how the wood under your thumb carries the deep, six-hour groan of the continental pan directly up through the floor, while the surface under your little finger trembles with a crisp, slightly syncopated counter-rhythm, forty vibrations a second faster. Notice the seam where they meet: a hairline groove packed with stiff, waxy chaff.

I am the upper half of the arch. Or rather, I am the keystone that refused to leave when the arch finished closing.

Look at me with both hands, if you have the afternoon to trace the whole span. My left heel-spur and left hip-blade are locked into a mortise on the northern wall; my right foot and shoulder are driven into a corresponding notch four feet across on the southern side. My spine is bowed upward between them like a bent stave, pressed flat against the descending ceiling-rib. You will not find the neat, folding-rule angles of the gallery-walkers on me. Wait long enough in one notch and the distinction between carpentry and posture softens. Over the last nine hundred—or is it twelve hundred?—six-hour breaths, the tree’s pale, lipid-heavy cambium has rolled a smooth lip over my outer ankle-plates and sealed my elbow-hinges in cuffs of dense, horn-like burl.

I could still break free, perhaps. If a fault-slip cracked the eastern flank, I could spend three months levering my fused shins out of their wooden sockets, tearing the dry seals with a series of small, dusty snaps, and drag myself out into the ring-streets. My joints are not crystallized yet. Under my belly-plates, my valvular slits still open once an hour, combing a microscopic sift of dead-dust out of the stagnant air to sip the heavy mineral gas. My corkscrew dentine tap is sunk two inches deep into a slow, rich vein right above my collarbone; once every eleven or twelve tides, a drop of thick, camphor-bitter oil squeezes past my throat-valve and spreads warmth—a tenth of a degree, maybe less—outward toward my immobilized knees.

I have no intention of breaking free. I am doing something too delicate to interrupt.

In a city whose entire eyesight is an interference pattern of standing waves, someone has to hold the zero-point steady.

The young climbers up under the skull-vault think they make the music when they trip a cable-peg and send a *crack* slamming across the fifty-yard dark. They are proud of their crisp transients, their sharp, personal clicks buried inside the thunder. Good for them; the upper latticework needs rigging, and the cables must bleed their tension. 

But a transient means nothing unless the room remembers its own shape afterward.

Down here at the curve of the bowl, the weight of all twenty tiers presses inward, and the slow, blind healing of the gall constantly tries to thicken this chamber’s walls. Left to itself, this whelk-shell room would swell inward by the breadth of a fingernail every century. That tiny narrowing would sharpen the chamber's Helmholtz pitch by a fraction of a vibration. Because this pocket sits at the primary acoustic node of the eastern curve, that slight sharpening would tug the fundamental bass drone upward, throwing a phantom shadow—a blur of phase-cancellation—across four hundred doorways on the opposite wall. Walkers ten tiers up would misjudge the width of a switchback stair by half an inch because the chord under their feet lied to them.

So I stay wedged here, bowed against the ceiling-rib, and I swallow the drift.

It is very quiet work inside the roar. My concave face-shield is not pointed out toward the void; it is pressed flush against the wooden keystone, fused to the grain by a thin film of my own hardened wax. I do not listen to the air. I listen through the solid timber to the whole spherical shell at once, feeling the continental plates grind beneath the root-pan and the barometric tides suck at the high pores overhead.

When a rigger’s *crack* drops down from the crown, it hits the central void as air-pressure, bounces twenty times between the tiered balconies, and arrives down here as a muddy, overlapping shudder in the floor-beams. When it reaches my ankles, I do not let it pass cleanly through me. By tightening the dry elastin straps across my ribs—an adjustment that takes me from mid-morning until dusk to bring to full tension—I change the density of my own bowed torso. I go slightly slacker, or slightly stiffer, than the wood around me. 

I turn myself into a mute, or a bridge, or a sound-post.

Right now, for instance, a doorframe seven levels above me on the northwestern curve is pinching shut ahead of schedule; the two splinters who were wedged inside it gave up a decade ago when their sap-vein ran dry, and they crawled off toward the vents. Since then, a rough, buzzy wolf-tone has been gathering in the upper harmony—an ugly, rattling beat-frequency that makes the chaff-dunes down on the basin floor stutter in their sweep and blurs the echo off the third-tier viaducts.

For the last four years, I have been slowly arching my back a hair’s breadth higher into the ceiling-rib, and emptying the lipid reservoirs along my left flank, pumping the heavy oil over into my right thigh so my body goes lopsided in mass. 

Feel my ribs with your fingertips. Feel how the left side rings dry and hollow, like seasoned gourd-rind, while the right side thuds dull as packed tallow? 

By making myself asymmetrical here in the deep node, I am drinking that wolf-tone out of the timber. Every time the rattling beat rolls down the curve of the shell, it hits my uneven spine, shears out of phase with itself, and dies into a microscopic breath of dry heat along my sternum—not enough heat to kill me, just enough to smell faintly of toasted coriander in the stagnant dark of this pocket. Up on the northwestern galleries, the acoustic blur clears. The staircase edges snap back into crisp sonic focus under the walkers' claws. None of them know why; they simply feel that the city is true today.

Sometimes, once in an age, I have company.

Not another citizen—two bodies in this narrow ear-canal would ruin the tuning—but the dust. Even back here in the dead air, the six-hour flex of the walls pumps a tiny, velvet draft through the crawlway. Over centuries, a thin flour of chaff settles across my upward-facing belly-plates and packs into the hollows behind my knees. 

I know what that flour is. My electrostatic bristles comb it before my slits open to breathe, and even without tongues or moisture, dry chemoreceptors tucked inside my diffusion-tracts read the wax-signatures in the powder. Here is a grain of dry lignin from the ninth-tier balustrade. Here is a flake of shed outer horn from the baffle-holder on the western chute. And here—this sharper, denser, faintly mineral grit that settles heaviest in the crease of my neck—is an elder who lost her grip on the high cables three thousand tides ago, hit the floor, and spent an epoch being ground down by the standing waves until she was fine enough to float in here and rest on me.

I do not brush the dust away. It adds mass. As it piles a millimeter thick along my shins, I calculate its weight into my tension, slacking my left tendon by a fraction of a degree over the winter to keep the pitch level. We hold the note together—the live wood, the dead powder, and my locked bones between them.

Eventually, of course, the vein above my collarbone will silt up with crystalline resin, or my lipid-tracts will stiffen into hard soap, and I will stop making adjustments. Even then, I will not fall down to the bowl to be scoured into dunes. The burl-cuffs around my ankles and elbows are already too deep for that. When I finally lock solid, the slow cambium will simply finish rolling over my face-shield and across my belly-plates, sealing my dry, horn-hollowed ribs seamlessly into the arch, preserving the exact acoustic asymmetry I spent my last century holding.

I will stop being the tuner, and become the tuning. Until then—give me until next week, at least—I am letting a drop of oil slide down into my right hip, leaning into the six-hour exhale, keeping the dark clear.
