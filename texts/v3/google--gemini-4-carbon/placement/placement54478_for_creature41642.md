I am not out on the line. My shoulders were never thick enough for the three-hundred-yard spans, or perhaps they were once, and then a snapping guide-vine caught my left collarbone in my nineteenth year and healed it crooked, leaving me with one arm that aches before a drop-squall and refuses to lock above my head. In the rim-villages, a crooked bone makes you a net-weaver or a goat-herd in the quiet dust. Out here, if you refuse to stay behind in the silence—and I did refuse, because the silence back in the clefts feels like being buried under wet wool, like the world has died and forgotten to shake—they find another use for your weight.

I am the ballast in the third station out from the North Rim, wedged into the leeward hollow of the spire we call *The Split Molar*. 

Right now, I am lying on my side in the dim, green-lit belly of the bunker, my back braced against the curved bone ribs of the wall, my knees drawn up to make room for three bales of lowland salt-cloth wrapped in oiled bladder-skin. Outside, a pocket of cloud is tearing itself to ribbons against the windward face of the spire. I cannot see it, because the reed-sphincter is laced shut, leaving only a lattice of thumb-sized breathing holes facing the dead-air pocket, through which shoots a fine, cold atomized spray that smells intensely of crushed fern and wet flint. 

I don't hear the wind—my right ear went dead at fourteen when an orchid-island blew out sixty yards below me, and my left only catches the lowest, chest-rattling sub-tones—but I am reading the storm through my hipbone and my left palm.

My palm is flat against the *singing-post*: an oak-thick core of seasoned vine-wood that runs straight through the floor of our basket-shelter and is mortised directly into a natural fissure in the quartzite needle, pinned there with bronze wedges driven by my grandfather’s sister. Every shelter has one. It is the mast in reverse; instead of holding up a sail, it brings the stone’s voice indoors.

Through the callus of my palm, the spire is humming a clean, tight *B-flat*—not that we have letters for it; in our skin-talk, we call that particular vibration *the grease-boil*, a rapid, tiny purring against the metacarpals. That means the northwest shear is hitting the notched collar-arch two hundred yards upwind cleanly, bleeding off the worst of the rotary chop. Good. Beneath *the grease-boil*, though, coming up through the woven floor-mats from our secondary anchor-cables, is a slow, heavy *draw-and-shudder*. Four seconds of pull, one second of slack, then a dull *thock* as the river-stone counterweights at the bottom of the span reach the end of their arc and drop back.

Someone is on the approach bridge. Two people, by the sluggishness of the return-snap, or one person hauling a merchant’s chest on a belly-sled.

I roll onto my stomach—moving without lifting my center of gravity, an old habit even inside four walls—and reach into the grease-pot lashed to the base of the post. My fingers never open past a half-curl; I scoop a dollop of rendered glider-fat mixed with beeswax using the backs of my knuckles and smear it down the sliding-chisel hung beside the door, then rub the excess into the greying leather of my own harness-loops. Everything up here dries out and splits in the thin sun, then rots in the ten-minute mists, unless you feed it fat constantly. The whole bunker smells of rancid tallow, dried cloud-slug, damp wool, and the sharp, vinegary tang of human sweat that never quite washes out of the floor-reeds.

The *draw-and-shudder* shifts pitch. Three rapid, sharp jerks come through the floorboards, vibrating right into my kneecaps: *Clear the lip. Heavy parcel. Blind.*

*Blind* doesn’t mean their eyes are gone; it means they’ve got a rim-lander clipped between them—some lowland factor or courier whose inner ear has turned to sour milk from two days of forty-foot drops, who has squeezed his eyes shut behind his borrowed quartz-goggles and locked his hands onto the guide-rope in the rigid, terrified death-grip we call *stone-fist*. When a traveler goes stone-fist in a flash-mist, you sometimes have to rap their knuckles with a bronze toggle until skin splits just to make them slide their hands forward another yard.

I don't put on a mask—the leeward pocket of *The Split Molar* is calm enough to breathe raw, just a swirling eddy that tugs at my braided hair and slaps wet strands across my cheek—but I hook my belly-line to the singing-post, shove two stone weights into my hip-pockets so a freak spill-wind won't pluck me out of the doorway, and unlace the reed-sphincter.

White. Everything is blinding, roaring white, moving sideways past the edges of the rock at seventy miles an hour. Five feet to my left and five feet to my right, the world is a solid wall of luminous, shredding milk. Right in front of me, in the narrow cone of shadow behind the pale quartzite rib, the air is clear, damp, and swirling with torn bits of silver moss and tiny, stunned midges. 

Down at my bare feet, the stone slopes away smoothly into nothing. Thirty feet below the lip, emerging out of the rushing white wall like roots out of milk, the three main cables of the approach-bridge buck and yaw, glistening black with wet.

I drop onto my belly on the slick apron of stone, hook my big toes into the two deep heel-cups pecked into the rock—worn smooth as soapstone by four hundred years of station-keepers bracing for this exact haul—and get my good right arm around the capstan-horn. With my left hand, I reach down and lay two fingers on the top guide-strand.

They are forty yards out. I feel the sharp, metallic *skritch-clack* of iron harness-hooks sliding over rough bark, followed by a clumsy, dragging *thump*—the traveler’s knees banging against the lower stabilizer-vines because they don't know how to tuck their heels to their buttocks when an updraft bellies the bridge upward.

Then they punch through the curtain of mist into the dead-air pocket, twenty feet below me, looking like three drowned beetles hauled out of surf.

Tov is in the lead—my cousin’s boy, twenty-two, wide as a fireplace, his mahogany shoulders gleaming wet, bare feet walking up the near-vertical final pitch of the anchor-root while one hand effortlessly hauls slack on the tether behind him. Clipped three feet behind his腰-ring, trussed in a heavy canvas cradle like a side of salt-pork, is a tall, horribly narrow person in dark wool—a lowlander, limbs flailing uselessly like broken sticks whenever the bridge drops out from under them, face buried entirely in their own collarbone, heaving dry, empty retches into the front of a soaked leather wind-snout. Behind the lowlander comes Senna, snapping her pruning-hook free of a trailing beard of staghorn fern that caught on the traveler's boots, moving with slow, annoyed precision.

Tov reaches the stone lip, doesn't look at me, reaches up, and claps his wet, freezing hand onto my bare shoulder as he drags himself over the edge. Before his hips have even cleared the drop, his thumb and three fingers are tapping rapidly against my trapezius, right over the crooked lump of my old break:

*Tea-merchant. Paid double weight at the gate. Vomited since the Minor Third. Don't unclip him till he's inside, he tries to stand up.*

*Idiot,* I tap back twice against the thick tendon behind his knee as he crawls past me toward the sphincter-door. *Any rot on the south span?*

Tov pauses, letting me haul the merchant’s slack-line in over the bronzehorn, taking the dead weight onto my crooked back and bracing with my legs while Senna pushes from below. Tov reaches back and presses two knuckles hard and flat against my ribs—*solid*—then flutters his fingertips lightly down toward my hip: *Cloud-grazers thick as warts on the lower cable. Slippery, not rotten. Good eating if somebody goes out with a scraper after this clears.*

We drag the lowlander across the threshold like a sack of turnips. Inside, Tov laces the reeds shut, cutting the white glare down to dappled, watery twilight and dropping the bone-shaking roar back to its hollow, submarine boom.

The merchant collapses onto the flexing reed floor and immediately tries to get onto hands and knees—trying to find *down*, trying to find something flat and dead and motionless in a room that is gently rising six inches, tilting left, hanging, and settling back with a long, resinous creak of bone-joints. When the floor dips, the lowlander lets out a thin, high sound—I don't hear it, of course, I only see the cords in his pale, absurdly long neck stand out like bowstrings and see Senna wince slightly through her remaining good ear before she reaches over, places one heavy, scarred hand flat between his shoulder blades, and simply *pushes* him down against the weave, holding him there with forty pounds of steady, rhythmic pressure until his shaking legs stop pedaling against the walls.

That’s how you calm dogs and rim-landers. You become gravity for them until their stomachs stop floating.

I unbuckle the man’s soaked wind-mask so he doesn't drown in his own bile. He’s forty, maybe, with eyes the color of pale river-mud, wide and bloodshot, staring wildly up at the curved ceiling ribs where clusters of dried roots and spare bronze chisels click softly against each other. His hands—long, straight, pale fingers that can open all the way flat, uncanny as spider-legs to look at—are bleeding around the nails from clawing at his harness-straps. He mouths words at me, lips shaping big, exaggerated lowland vowels, begging or cursing or asking how many miles are left.

I don't try to read his lips. Nobody says anything worth knowing out loud in the basin. Instead, I pull a wooden flask from my warm armpit—steeped ginger-bark and salted fat-broth, lukewarm—unstopper it with my teeth, and press the spout against the corner of his mouth. When he swallows, I give his sternum two slow, heavy pats with the heel of my hand. *Still here. Hold fast. Roof holds.* Whether he knows the grammar or not, skin understands weight. His breathing slows from frantic rabbit-gasps to long, shuddering pulls.

Across the four-foot floor, Tov and Senna have already stripped off their wet harnesses and wedged themselves together in the corner by the salt-cloth bales, thigh to thigh, sharing body heat. Senna has her eyes shut, chewing a strip of tough grey slug-mantle, while her right hand rests lazily on Tov’s forearm and his hand lies over her shin. They are talking—a slow, half-asleep trickle of gossip from the rim-town, who married whom in the quiet clefts, whose child took their first steps on flat dirt without crying, the price of meteoric iron from the southern caravans.

I turn back toward the singing-post, tucking my crooked arm against my ribs to keep the damp chill out of the joint, and lean my cheekbone and jaw directly against the greased wood.

Outside, the ten-minute cloud has just torn free of *The Split Molar*. I know the exact second it happens before the light even brightens through the breathing-holes, because the post suddenly changes temperature—not cold to hot, just a subtle tightening of the grain as the sun slams back onto the wet quartzite needle above us and a million gallons of mist flash into whipping steam. 

Through my jawbone, the spire’s hum climbs half a step as the air thins and dries in the heat. Half a mile away, out in the bright, violent green sea I don't need to look at to see, a thermal column boils up out of the invisible abyss, catches five acres of dripping, emerald-bright orchid-mats, lifts them forty feet toward the ordinary blue sky, holds them breathless at the top of their arc—every cable going silent on the post for two full heartbeats while the whole sector floats weightless—and then drops them back into the harness of the stone with a deep, resonant, fifty-ton *thrum* that travels through the rock, through the bronze wedges, up the wooden post, into my molar-roots, down my spine, and straight out through my calloused heels into the swaying floor.

Everything holds. Not a root slipped.

I reach behind me without lifting my cheek from the post, find Tov’s bare ankle in the warm gloom, and squeeze twice—*clean drop, dry lines*—before closing my eyes to listen to the afternoon tighten its chords.
