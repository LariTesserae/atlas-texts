Four inches down, the daylight doesn’t get far, and the noise gets everywhere.

Down under the sluice of broken obsidian and empty, brass-tarnished shells, the floor of the pocket is a grinding bed of wet knives. Up in the light, the pale climbers move through clear air spotted with momentary bruises, hunting the distinct plum-colored blooms of individual impacts. Down here at the bottom of the litter, where the scalding runoff threads between millions of Shifting splinters, every step overhead sends a cascade of glass scraping against glass. The darkness isn't night; it is an unbroken, soupy paste of indigo shock-blots bleeding into one another until the interstitial water glows like stagnant ink.

I am down in the grind, pressed flat against the raw andesite bed where the heat comes straight up out of the crust before the air has a chance to touch it.

To have a body with volume here—even a hydraulic bladder wrapped in boiled leather—is to be crushed or pierced the moment three climbers wrestle directly overhead and drive the gravel bed down an inch. Volume is a liability. Instead, I am scarcely thicker than the film of yellow sulfur-slime on the ledges, a palm-sized, irregular sheet of dense, grey-translucent gristle with no top and no bottom, oozing and folding through the razor-maze like spilled mercury through coarse sand. 

If you lifted a handful of the spent glass out of the hot wash, you wouldn't see an animal. You would see ten or twelve dull, chipped obsidian triangles stuck vaguely together by a clear, rubbery mucilage, and before the water finished draining between your fingers, I would already have let go of the upper flakes and poured myself sideways through the gaps back into the dark.

Nothing goes to waste in a cistern this tight, not even the mistakes. Up above, they use edges until the edges break; down here, I live on the dulling of the room.

When a fresh razor snaps off the wall and eventually drops into the floor-bed, its fractured edge is a single molecule thick—unstable, frantic with mechanical tension left over from the cooling of the slag. As feet shift the pile, those raw edges chip microscopic sparkles of strained silica into the scalding water, and every tiny fracture releases a microscopic pinprick of violet shock-light. To something spread flat across two hundred shards at once, the gravel bed isn't dark at all; it is a sizzling field of minute purple static. 

I creep toward the brightest static. I wrap my thin, fibrous margins around a freshly dropped, ruined chisel—still smeared with the coppery lymph of an ambush-worm or the bubbly fat of a ruptured deep-eel—and pull myself tight across its jagged bevels. The sharpness doesn't cut me because there is nothing inside me under pressure to burst; if a needle-point pokes clean through my sheet, my tissue simply parts around the glass like heavy grease around a wire and seals seamlessly on the other side. 

I digest the meat-smears first, quick and hot. Then I hold onto the stone edge and drink the remaining tension out of the glass itself, etching the unstable, sharp-angled silica with acidic secretions until the blade beneath me goes smooth and soapy, incorporating the dissolved mineral into a gritty suspension inside my own slime so the saw-rimmed leeches slide right off me when they come nosing down into the gravel. When I move on, what I leave behind at the very bottom of the pile is the only rounded stone in the blister: dead black pebbles, sucked clean of both protein and sharpness, packed down into the low crevices like mortar.

Sometimes, though, something much stranger falls through the ceiling of glass.

Every twenty minutes or so, up near the rim where the cold sky presses in, one of the midair wedges shatters under the impossible bite of a spatial seam. The fragments tumble down into the hot wash along with stray, unlicked droplets of that colorless, ozone-stinking fluid—droplets so cold they hiss violently, armored in little boiling jackets of instant steam that keep them intact for three or four seconds as they plummet through the litter.

When one of those splinters hits the gravel near me, the shock-bruise isn't indigo. It’s colorless and wrong, a tiny ring of pure transparency that punches a hole right through the purple static of the shifting bed.

I don't pull back from it. The climbers up top go frantic and half-blind when they drink that leakage because they have insides and outsides, sequences of nerve and pump that need *before* to stay neatly lined up ahead of *after*. Down in the dark, with no eyes and no limbs and no forward direction, sequence barely matters anyway. 

I flow straight over the freezing, ozone-sharp splinter before the geothermal water can warm it back into ordinary rock.

The cold hits my margin and turns a quarter-inch of my grey flesh brittle as mica, killing that edge instantly, and right behind the frostbite comes the stutter. For half a minute, while the trace of fluid dissolves across my skin, my thin sheet isn't entirely flat against the present floor. Part of me is pressed against the hot andesite as it sits right now; another lobe of me is already tucked two inches to the left, slipped neatly beneath a heavy basalt flake that hasn't fallen out of a climber's three-fingered paw yet. 

I feel the phantom weight of the shard arrive before the shard hits. I feel the sudden, pitch-black heartbeat of the mountain settling a millimeter deep in its socket ten seconds before the tremor actually turns the cistern dark—giving me just enough warning to thin myself down into the deepest, safest troughs of smoothed pebbles before fifty startled climbers stomp the gravel bed flat all at once.

Then the cold burns off in the scalding wash, the brittle rim of dead tissue sloughs away from my margin to be eaten by the bacteria, and I am just grease among razors again.

Miles above the clouds, daylight pours straight down through the ragged hole in the mountain, crisp and indifferent, glinting off wet flanks and busy stone tools. Four inches below the commotion, in the silent, bruised ink where all their broken knives end up, I spread slow and wide across the hairline vent itself—taking the raw heat on my underside and the endless rain of sharp rubbish on my back, quietly rounding off the bottom of this vicious young room one ruined blade at a time.
