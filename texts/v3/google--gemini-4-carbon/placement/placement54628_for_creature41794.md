I am forty feet down, where the afternoon never reaches, though I know when noon has passed on the rim above because the weight of the city shifts by a hair’s breadth and the porcelain ribs around me tighten with a dry, microscopic groan.

At first, looking into the pit from the sharp trachyte collar, you might think to look for me among the walkers—the broad velvet masons reading the walls with saffron plumes, or the pale, long-fingered sisters ladling warm gold in the dark. Those are easy shapes to slip into because they have legs and errands. Even the heavy skinks have eyes that blink and tongues that taste their wages. 

I have none of those. I do not cross the viaducts. I am the thin, amber glaze lining the inside of the vertical chutes, and the thread-fine, colorless mycelium laced through the mortar itself, binding the chewed pumice to the cured parchment of old shells.

To find me, you have to follow a dusty pellet of raw, bitter balsam after a mason drops it into a shaft on the third terrace. 

It does not fall straight down like a stone in an empty well. The shaft is narrow, curved slightly to check its speed, and glazed smooth with forty years of accumulated resin. As the pellet tumbles and rolls down the warm porcelain throat, it leaves behind a microscopic smear of crude turpentine and static charge. That smear is my skin. 

I am a dry, lipophilic mold—not fuzzy like the wet-world fungi that rot fallen logs, because fuzz would catch dust and dry out, and there is no water here to swell a spore. Instead, I grow as a continuous, glassy lacquer, thinner than gold leaf, lining the dark veins between the sunlit plazas and the deep yeast-undercrofts. My hyphae do not bore with acid; they slip on trace films of liquid paraffin into the cooling micro-fractures of the masons' stone-paste the moment it cures out of reach of the brutal ultraviolet sky.

Where the sunlight hits the upper parapets, I burn crisp and die back to a clean gray line at the lip of every archway. Shadows are my shoreline. When the slate-green canopies hinge shut at dusk, I do not creep upward—nothing here moves so fast—but the volatile esters trapped beneath the closed leaves thicken in the dark air, and my upper margins drink them in, swelling by a few microns before dawn peels me back again.

Because I line the chutes, the cracks, and the undersides of the grease-vats, everything the city says to itself passes through my membrane.

A mason up near the western rim chews a mouthful of fresh black basalt grit and lays it onto a cooling buttress; three hours later, the faint, iron-tangy oxidation of that curing mortar seeps down a structuralcopper vine, transfers into my glossy film where the vine pierces a third-tier vault, and travels by slow chemical diffusion along my threads into the nursery walls. Long before a mason carries the news downward on its velvet back, the pale keepers standing on my glazed floors feel the sharp iron-taste through the spoon-tips of their claws and begin skimming a lighter, quicker-burning solvent from Vat Nine to thin tomorrow’s resin.

I do not think this message through. I am the medium that refuses to let the oil soak uselessly into raw pumice. Without my vitrified skin sealing the porous stone, the golden grease would wick away into the thirsty volcanic talus at the bottom of the pit, and the closed circuit would bleed dry into the mountain within a decade. 

In return, I take a tithe so small the city never misses it: one carbon ring in ten thousand from every drop of balsam that slides down the throats; the stray, sloughed belly-scale of a descending skink wedged into a warm corner, which I slowly turn to clear horn over six winters; the dead static charge bleeding off a river of turquoise mites when they cluster in a dark niche for the night. Static tastes like ozone and fractured glass. I use its tiny voltage to pull stray sulfur atoms out of the rising volcanic breath and lock them into my chitinous lattice, making myself tougher, smoother, more amber-clear as the years stack overhead.

My favorite places—if a sheet of living varnish spanning half an acre of honeycomb can have a preference—are the sealed chambers down around the sixty-foot mark, below the deepest vats, where the pale sisters no longer go.

Down here, the warmth does not pulse with the sun; it wells up steady and dry from the hairline vents in the trachyte floor. Forty years ago, an old skink crawled into a cracked hexagonal cell down here when its kidneys finally clogged with chalk, curled its tail around its snout, and stopped breathing. Nobody harvested all of it; a shift in the upper terraces caused the masons to plaster the doorway shut before the keepers finished their work. 

Now the chamber is a dark, warm drum. I have coated the curved ribs of the lizard and the pale gray porcelain walls in a single, seamless skin of dark topaz. A dead copper vine runs straight through the room from floor to ceiling, petrified into hard, resin-filled wood, humming faintly like a plucked cello string too low for ears whenever the mountain settles a hundred yards below. 

Nothing enters this room, and nothing leaves it except heat and smell. Yet I am still alive here, thinner and slower than in the grease-chutes above, maintaining the tension between the dry bone truss and the bearing wall. When a thousand velvet feet shift in unison seventy feet overhead because a cloud—rare as a comet—has crossed the high blue square of sky, the minute drop in thermal expansion travels down the petrified vine, flexes the mummified spine of the skink by a fraction of a micron, and sends a tiny piezoelectric shiver through my amber crust.

I hold the flex. I do not crack. I pass the faint, warm smell of forty-year-old tallow and volcanic sulfur back up through a million hairline pores toward the busy mouths in the light, reminding the ones laying fresh paste along the rim where the floor is, and how much weight the dead can bear.
