I would not be one of the pale, spring-steel runners down in the dark basins where the crust jumps six feet in an hour. Nor would I be the mountain itself—that blind, dry furnace heaving upward without knowing what stands on its shoulders.

Find me twenty-nine thousand feet up, on a wide, wind-planed saddle of dark plagiogranite where three fault-lines meet like the seams of an anvil. 

My left knee no longer unfolds past a tight, acute angle; a vein of insoluble silicate locked the hinge two seasons ago, and when I walk, my two-beat cadence has a slight, dry drag at the end of the second strike: *tick-tick-scrape, pause, tick-tick-scrape*. My plates have gone the color of old, quenched iron, thick with decades of unpurged slag, shedding fewer translucent scales in the thermal gusts than they used to because the cleavage planes have fused tight. Down on the lower scarps, where the white, unoxidized stone squeezes out hot and rich, a six-minute warning before a shear-off is no longer quite enough time for me to clear the fall-zone.

So I keep the Crossroads of the Three Teeth.

It is not a barren post. Because three deep crustal blocks grind past one another beneath this saddle, the rock never goes entirely cold, even if it rarely breaks open in great, rich feasts anymore. Hairline fractures spiderweb the flat bench, sighing thin, steady ribbons of warm nitrogen and trace hydrogen up into the starved dark. It is lean grazing—the chemical equivalent of thin broth—but an old, slow bellows does not burn much fuel. Twice between one star-rise and the next, I lower myself stiffly over a reliable seam near my shelter, unhinge my scarred breastplate, and let the soot-violet pleats inside my chest drink the slow heat while I chip thumb-sized flakes of mildly stressed slate from a creeping step-fault nearby. My gizzard grinds slower than it did; the red talcum cones I leave behind the windbreak are small and neat.

What this high, slow saddle has—what the violent, lucrative gulfs below lack—is unbroken sightlines and deep, continuous stone.

Two of the massive root-pillars of this massif join directly under my boots. When I drop down and press the thickened acoustic block of my jaw against the bare, warm spine of the ridge, drawing the dry electrostatic film across my eyes to shut out the crowded stars, the darkness turns into a vast, three-dimensional architecture of tension. 

I can hear seven miles down, where the dry mantle-plume shoulders against the underside of the crust—a low, continuous *thrum* that lives in my wrist bones and teeth so constantly I would think I had gone deaf if it ever stopped. Over the top of that bass note, I read the shallower music: the tight, glassy singing of a basalt dike bowing outward four miles east; the gritty, stuttering *chitter* of a talus apron sliding into a blind chasm to the north; and, sharpest of all, the clean, deliberate *clack—clack-clack—clack* of someone else’s gabbro hammer striking living bedrock across the void.

That is why I am here. A basin-runner down in the shattered trenches is deaf beyond their own spur; too many broken faults and rubble-filled chasms lie between them and the next ridge, scattering the stone-waves into unreadable mush. Up here on the old, fused saddle, the deep root-stone brings the news from three valleys into one room.

When a message rises through my chin from the eastern gulf—*long seam opening, pale rock, stable overhang*—and I know by yesterday’s faint, distant foot-tremors that a solitary young one is working a dying, cooling scarp four miles west across a shattered, sound-deadened rift, I become the bridge.

I lever myself up on my good leg, select the round, black cobble of dense peridotite I keep wedged in a notch of my shelter wall, and limp forty yards out to the western horn of the saddle, where a rib of unbroken root-granite juts into the freezing wind. If the western ridge is connected deep down, I strike the rib with the cobble—four clean blows, spaced like a calm heartbeat, pitching the azimuth and nature of the break down under the rubble-chasm and up into the soles of that distant stranger’s feet.

If the faults between us have sheared too raggedly even for the deep root to carry a clean wave, I go to the niche behind the master cairn. Wedged carefully under heavy slate slabs so the thermal tornados cannot pluck it into the void is a sheet of book-mica as wide as my chest, split thin enough to flex slightly without cracking, yet flat as still mercury. I haul it out with my scarred, chisel-nailed thumbs, brace my stiff hip against the cairn’s shoulder while the silent wind shoves hard at my ribs, and catch that permanent, copper-red smear sitting low on the western horizon. 

Tilting the sheet a fraction of an inch at a time, I throw a needle of copper light four miles across the black air, directly onto the high shelf where I saw a windbreak rebuilt three ten-days ago. *Flash. Flash-flash. Pause. Flash.* 

Sometimes nothing answers; the runner has already moved on, or is folded deep inside a dome asleep. Sometimes, ten minutes later, a tiny, star-sharp prick of copper winks back once from the dark wall opposite—*heard*—before vanishing. I slide the mica back under its flat weights, brush a curl of shaved horn from my heel, and limp back toward the warmth of my seam.

Between relays, I tend the calibrations. There are four tall cairns on this saddle, set here long before I split out of my sulfur-warmed shell down in the rift, though I have rebuilt two of them from the base up since coming high. Every cycle of the overhead stars, I crouch behind the foresight notch by my bivouac and check their tips against the wheeling constellations. The northernmost spire has crept upward half a finger’s breadth against the bright triad in the east over the last forty nights, steady and plumb—good news for whoever works its foot next season. The southern cairn has begun to lean a hair toward the gulf; two nights ago it dropped a single flat course-stone into the dark. Tomorrow, if the wind drops below a gale, I will walk the pale ribbon of flipped talus leading toward that southern spur, go out to where the stone begins to hum hollow under my chin, and walk backward for half a mile, flipping every pale marker-flake dark-side up with my pry-toes until the dead road disappears back into the black razor-scree.

Inside my low herringbone dome, my basalt needle is longer and ground finer than the rough trip-beams used down in the chaotic trenches. I have spent quiet hours shaving its underside with a shard of volcanic glass until it balances on a breath. Down below, you only want to know if your own ledge is about to shear off in the next five minutes. Up here, my needle clicks softly against its stop when the whole mountain massif shifts its weight miles underneath, telling me it is time to go outside and check the stars against the cairns again.

I rarely see another of my kind up close anymore. Once every few seasons, a young traveller crossing between major basins will come *tick-tick, tick-tick* up the flipped-stone path out of the starlight, pale-plated, vents shimmering hot behind their narrow jaw, moving with that effortless, caliper-clean stride I remember in my own shins only as an old geometry problem. 

We do not crowd each other; the hydrogen vents up here are too thin for two chests at once. The newcomer stops twenty paces off on the ringing talus and taps a knuckle twice against an outcrop in truce. I tap once from the doorway of my shelter. Sometimes, before trotting on down the safe path I have kept clear across the divide, they will reach into the hollow behind their hip and leave a fist-sized prism of freshly split, unoxidized black gabbro sitting on the flat stone beside my foresight—a rich, heavy lump brought up from the violent depths, still bristling with raw, unspent iron bonds.

When their clean, two-beat footfalls have faded down the opposite spine into the vast hush of the thin sky, I limp out, pick up the dark prism, and turn it in my three-fingered hand. Under the starlight, its newly broken facet winks once with the copper glow from the horizon. I scrape a curl of the rich, pale powder off its edge with my lower jaw, tasting the sharp, sudden warmth of deep violence I no longer have the speed to hunt for myself.

One night—not tonight, perhaps not for thirty star-turns yet, though my right elbow has begun to click dryly when I lift the mica sheet—my ribs will stiffen just enough that the thin upper air leaves me dim and slow even over the warmest crack. I already know which dome I will choose: not the central one by the foresight, which the next watch-keeper will need clear and ready, but a smaller, older ring of loose slate fifty yards out on a dead, fully cooled spur where even the soot-violet crust peeled away years ago. 

Before I crawl inside and pull the lintel-stones shut behind me, I will do one last thing. I will take the balanced basalt needle off its notched uprights in the main shelter and lay it flat on the floor. The next traveller who crests the saddle and ducks inside out of the silent wind will see the dismounted beam immediately, know the post at the Three Teeth has fallen vacant, and—if their own joints are beginning to thicken with dark slag and their stride has lost a fraction of its spring—perhaps set the needle back onto its fulcrum, shift the fingernail counterweight until it hangs hair-fine above the stop, and lay their heel against the stone.

And seasons after that, if a storm-gust or a sharp tremor shakes a course loose from that new keeper’s windbreak, they may walk out to the slumped stone ring on the cold spur, turn over the wide, iron-dark curve of my breastplate among the rattling slate, test its edge with a scarred thumb, hear that it still rings clear and true, and wedge me back into the wall to hold the wind off the balance.
