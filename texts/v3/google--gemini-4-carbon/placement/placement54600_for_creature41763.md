At first I try standing where you stood me—up on the weathered greywacke spur, looking out across the hollow dark. That is usually where an observer goes: somewhere high, with eyes, trying to hold all ten miles at once. 

From up there, the first temptation is the gliders. When the air is too thin to carry speech, flight looks like the cleanest answer—all carbon-boned silence, riding a column of rising diamond dust, looking down on the violet waves of static as if they were weather on another planet. Have a wide wingspan; stay above the machinery; drop down only when hungry.

Except the cold up there is sterile. A glider spends ninety percent of its life waiting in a freezing void for something warm below to make a mistake. And down on the slope, the six-legged grazers are worse off—all ribs and panic, whistling thin, dry cold into huge, fragile lungs, racing their own suffocation from one severed sap-tube to the next. Breathing, here, is clearly an error in design. It belongs to creatures that haven’t fully admitted where they live.

So my attention drops lower, down into the dark velvet mat, right to the foot of that choked chimney.

I watch those three boneless, black-cartilage fingers finish their work. With a slow, shuddering heave that travels through its splayed legs into the slate, the domed creature cracks the collar of crusted brine-scale inside the flute. There is no sharp *snap* in the deadened air—only a puff of white powder, heavy slabs of grey mineral flaking away into the rosettes, and then a sudden, silent vertical roar of hot vapor punching upward again, vanishing instantly into glinting ice-motes. Heat returns to the acre. Frost retreats from the dark, greased joints of the surrounding pump-kelp. *Shhh-clack. Shhh-clack.* The mill picks up its dropped stitch.

For a long moment, I think: *that one.* I would be the plumber. Plugged straight into the aquifer’s hot vein, heavy with copper, no face to freeze, no lungs to starve, glowing dull cathode-violet around the rim while tending the pipes. There is deep dignity in that body.

Yet as I watch it fold its specialized hands back into their warm pockets and wade slowly away through the copper-threaded scrub, I realize I am still looking *at* it rather than out from inside it. A body like that is built for leverage—for prying, clamping, hauling itself from fault to fault. It is solitary and localized. It can only be at one stuck chimney at a time.

Which raises a quiet question in the dark: *how did it know which chimney was failing?*

In ten miles of soundless valley, where a collapsing boulder barely whispers, a wheezing vent makes no call at all. By the time frost creeps visibly across an acre of black leaves, the lipid pistons are already seizing. Something had to tell the mechanic that pressure was dropping three hundred yards to the west before the ice won.

That is where I lose my shape and slip underneath.

Down beneath the knee-deep canopy of leathery rosettes, below the buoyant stems of the bladder-ferns, there is a two-inch boundary layer where the freezing night never quite reaches. Here, the ancient greywacke is slick with warm condensation, shed lipids, and mineral sweat from the root-cisterns. It smells—if anyone down here had nostrils—of hot pennies, sulfur, and tallow. There is no sky here, not even the bruised violet rim. There is only weight, warmth, salinity, and charge.

I am flat, dark, and wide—thin as wet chamois, branching into ten thousand pewter-colored ribbons that hug the bare rock between the roots. Something like a mycelium, something like spilled solder, something like a nerve plexus that never bothered to gather itself into a brain.

I don't have eyes, because nothing down here reflects light. Instead, I have *span*. My eastern margin is currently tasting the first sharp, metallic trickle of condensed fog running down the channels of six hundred rosettes; my western margin, half a mile upslope, is wrapped snug around the warm, pulsing stone beside a geothermal fissure. Between those two edges, my whole body is a continuous, living reading of difference—hot against cold, sweet water against thick brine, high voltage against ground.

When the fog spills over the passes and the leaves overhead begin their sixty-mile-an-hour electrostatic harvest, the violence of that charge has to go somewhere, or the delicate osmotic valves in the root-mat would cook in their own sap. I am what takes the excess. When the blue arcs snap across the canopy above, a silent, thrilling shiver runs through my damp filaments underneath. I drink the stray current, spread it sideways across acres of wet slate, step it down, bleed it harmlessly into the iron-rich rock. For twenty minutes twice a cycle, I am luminous in the absolute dark where no one can see me—a sprawling, buried lacework glowing faint violet against the mud, humming like a transformer.

And when a chimney begins to scale over—long before the steam dies, when the back-pressure in the pump-kelp rises by just a fraction of a bar and the rhythm shifts from *shhh-clack* to a strained, lagging *shhh... clack*—the vibration changes across my skin. The local brine turns slightly too alkaline; the heat thins out at one corner of me. 

I cannot pry the scale loose myself. I have no cartilage, no leverage, no hard ceramic mouth. What I can do is tighten around the nearby bleed-nipples and throw an intentional stutter into the local ground-charge—a rhythmic, salty pulse sent rippling outward through the root-grid like a tug on a spiderweb. 

Half an hour later, I feel the heavy, four-footed tread of the mechanic pressing down through the mat above me. One of its vulcanized pads settles directly over a ribbon of my tissue—crushing a little of me, which doesn't hurt, because I have no center to injure; I simply pool around its toes and re-join when it lifts its foot. When it clamps its proboscis onto the bypass valve and vents hot, spent aquifer-brine back into the soil, that scalding, iron-heavy rush washes right over me. That is my meal: the mechanic’s warm exhaust, the leftover ions, the greasy detritus flaking off the pistons, and occasionally the sudden, rich, terrifyingly hot salt of a grazer’s ruby blood melting down through the leaves after a silent tackle overhead, followed immediately by the ticklish, mercury-quick skitter of scavengers crossing my back.

In the long, dark stretches between the fogs, when every valve in my reach is clear and the piston-meadow beats steady as a sleeper’s heart, I do something that serves the biome not at all.

With whatever trickle of galvanic current I have left over, molecule by molecule, I plate the stone beneath me. I pull dissolved copper and silver and iron out of the seep-water and lay them down into the microscopic glacial scratches of the greywacke. Over centuries—because down here, without lungs or heartbeats, there is no reason to stop—the rock under my body becomes inlaid with hair-fine, gleaming circuitry that carries nothing: concentric rings where an old exhaust vent stood four hundred years ago, dense metallic knots where a mechanic died and rusted back into the mat, long sweeping curves recording a season when the eastern fogs came heavy and rich.

Nothing has ever been built by hands in this valley. If the biome ever died and the black moss stripped away to the wind, someone standing up on your high ridge might look down at the dark slate floor, catch a faint glint of starlight on ten miles of intricate, copper-inlaid stone, and swear a civilization had wired the basin and vanished.

They would be wrong. I wasn't writing anything, and I wasn't building. I was just lying in the warm grease under the engines, holding one acre in touch with the next, listening to the mountain pump.
