I would be at the seam first, before I knew I had a body.

Not out on the open gneiss, where the windless light strips everything down to raw exposure, and not deep in the middle of a crowded room—because in this place, a room’s middle is the least reliable part of it. It is the frame that holds. The threshold, the window-square, the mortise-hole, the narrow stone collar where a split-log pipe passes through a dry-laid wall: those are the places where two things have promised to remain beside each other no matter how much world swells up between them.

If you looked for me from the Cushion-plains, you would probably miss me for an hour. You would see a low, roofless bathhouse wall standing by itself in a league of wet, rusty sphagnum—just nine courses of hand-split grey slate, chinked with pale river clay, green at the base with liverworts the size of platters. And sitting on the stone bench built into the lee of that single wall, an old person—or middle-aged; age here doesn’t crease the skin so much as thicken it to the dull, waterproof sheen of an oiled saddle—wrapped to the chin in a heavy cape of beaten sedge.

My boots are resting on a dry paving slab three paces in front of me. Except the slab is currently half a mile down-plateau, tilted slightly beside a frost-shrub thicket, so to a stranger’s eye my legs stretch out across the crimson moss like two fallen bog-timbers, dark and foreshortened, ending in greased leather toes that twitch occasionally to keep the glycol-rich blood moving. I don’t look down at them. Looking at your own stretched limbs when a dilation gust is running only makes the inner ear argumentative, and my inner ear has better work to do.

My left ear is pressed lightly against the cold slate jamb of a square wall-niche.

Through that niche—which opens not onto the bog behind the wall, where it ought to, but into the dim, humid sorting-vault of the Third Cistern Ward seven miles northeast—comes the sound of water. Not one stream. Eleven of them. Eleven split-log and dressed-slate channels arriving from eleven different phantom valleys high in the empty sky, their trickles converging in the dark stone chamber on the other side of my cheek.

I am a *weir-listener*, maybe, or a tender of the joints—one of the people whose work sits right between plumbing, knot-craft, and patience.

In my lap, resting on an apron of stiff, resin-blackened reed-cloth, are four of the braided moss-ropes. Right now they are dry, asleep, coiled like loops of dead silvery ships'-cable, smelling faintly of bitter sap and old pond-bottom. My thumbs—broad, plum-dark at the knuckles, the nails thick as horn from prying wet slate—are working a fifth braid, weaving a strip of pale, dead rush-pith together with three living strands of silver-green high-vine moss and pinching a tiny, cold-hammered copper ring shut around the twist with my back teeth.

Wait. Let me feel what that actually tastes like.

Cold metal, green-sour and sharp against the tongue, and underneath it the bruised, peppery sap of the moss, which numbs the gum slightly where it touches. You learn not to swallow it, though a little wakes the stomach up on a slow afternoon.

Why am I sitting here instead of inside the vault? Because the vault is cold—thirty-three degrees and dripping—and my wall happens to be drifting through a pocket of bright, unclouded afternoon sun where the air has thickened just enough to hold forty degrees of warmth against the slate. Why sit in the dark when your ear and your right arm can do the damp work seven miles away while your spine bakes gently in the sun?

Listen through the niche with me.

*Tick-chitter-drip.* That is the fourth high intake, up on the unseen western shoulder of what the surveyors call the Split Tooth—a mountain nobody has laid eyes on since the planet’s crust had a different name, though its ghost still tilts sixty degrees toward the afternoon sun and sheds clear snowmelt out of featureless blue air. When the water runs clean, it makes a hollow, throat-clearing sound against the slate baffle inside the vault. Today there is a tiny hiss in the middle of the note. Mineral grit, or a mat of sky-vine rootlets choking the intake trough eighty feet above the bog.

I don’t stand up. Standing up in a stretched metric takes two full minutes of gathering your parts back into agreement—drawing your heels home across half a mile of moss without dragging them through a slow puddle, checking the copper plumb-bobs at your hem to make sure a phantom talus slope hasn’t drifted across your knees while you were daydreaming. Instead, I reach my right hand sideways through the square stone niche.

The air changes instantly at the wrist. My shoulder stays warm in the plain, honest sunlight; my hand plunges into dark, echoing cellar-dampness that smells of wet gneiss and cold iron. My fingers find the stone lip of the fourth conduit by touch—every mason’s chisel-mark on that lip is an old friend, worn smooth as soap—and I lay the sleeping moss-braid into the dry groove beside the water-channel, then flick two fingers of cold cistern water across its back.

For ten seconds, nothing happens. Then, under my wet fingertips, the dead rush-pith drinks and swells sideways while the living vine-moss tightens lengthwise. A tiny, fibrous *creak* travels up my wrist-bones. The braid arches its back like a caterpillar waking from winter, finds the stone groove with its blunt nose, and begins to roll—one thumb-width a minute, blind and tireless—up the dark conduit, through three severed culvert-arches, out into the bright sky six miles away, and up the hollow wooden riser nailed to a spiralling sky-vine, bound for an invisible ledge of pure gravity eighty feet off the ground. By tomorrow noon it will have scoured the root-mat loose and dropped back down into the catch-basket with its copper rings polished bright by grit.

There is something deeply restful about having no animals in the world, once you let go of missing them.

Nothing here looks back at you with fear or hunger. Nothing dies hot and sudden in the scrub. There is no guilt in the soup-kettle, only the greasy, turnip-sweet fat of the cold-root that spent six months drinking the spare warmth out of your bedroom floor, and the pungent, cabbagey steam of leaves that kept themselves alive in the frost by running a slow fever. Everything alive on this plateau works by *leaning*—vines leaning against vanished granite, cushion-plants leaning into their own hoarded heat, moss-ropes leaning into dampness, people leaning across broken miles through doorways their great-grandmothers laid without mortar.

Even loneliness has a different geometry here. I thought at first I might be solitary—a hermit on a continental shield, watching clouds of yellow roof-spore drift across empty leagues. And from above, if an eye could hang in that thin stratosphere, it would look like crushing isolation: one dark speck sitting against a ruined wall, the nearest human figure four miles south, frozen mid-stride by perspective until he looks tall as a siege-tower and thin as a rush-needle.

Yet three feet to my left, where the low pantry arch of my wall stands open to the sky, a wooden ladle just came through from the kitchen side—which has been parked near the Sunken Orchard since Tuesday—and tapped twice against the slate to ask if I want hot fern-broth.

I don’t even have to speak loud enough to scare a dust-mote. I just turn my head toward the arch and say, in a chest-voice low and resonant from breathing thin air all my life, *"Leave the bowl on the sill; my legs are out in the red moss."*

From the empty rectangle of tundra framed by the stone arch comes my cousin’s dry, whistling laugh—three miles away by crow-flight, if crows existed, and nineteen inches away by mason’s rule. *"Pull them in before dusk,"* his voice murmurs right beside my kneecap, warm with the smell of steeped fronds and boiled root-grease. *"A compression is coming off the north granaries. The copper bobs in the orchard are all swinging east."*

I look down at my own hem.

Sure enough: the three green-patinaed copper weights hanging on braided cords from my sedge skirt are not pointing straight down at the ancient gneiss anymore. Two of them are hanging plumb, but the leftmost one—the heavy octagonal bob my grandfather beat out of a native nugget with a river cobble—is pulling twelve degrees toward the horizon, tugged by a wrinkle in the metric, or the toe of a moving compression front, or some stray eddy of the old range’s ghost-mass shifting in its sleep.

And look out across the plateau now. Look with plateau-eyes, not visitor-eyes.

Half a league off, a sudden mist-seam has just materialized out of spotless afternoon sun—a vertical curtain of white vapour hanging forty feet up, flat-bottomed where it rests on an unseen shelf of vanished rock, soaking a grove of woody sky-vines whose dark waxy leaves hang suspended in mid-air like the canopy of a forest whose trunks were erased. As the mist vanishes again, a thousand drops of meltwater let go of the leaves at once, falling not straight down, but *diagonally*, sliding along an invisible chute of billion-year-old mountain-habit to splash with a quiet, musical *shhh-pop* into a dry stone gutter ten yards from my boots.

Wait—ten yards from my boots, which were half a mile away five minutes ago.

The red sphagnum between my heels and my bench isn’t scraping or bunching; it is simply becoming less necessary. Without a sound, without a breeze to stir the peat-fluff at my collar, the four hundred yards of giant silver liverworts and emerald cushion-domes separating my shins from my torso are thinning out like shallow water evaporating off warm slate. My boots slide homeward across the afternoon while staying planted on their paving slab. As the space condenses, a sudden pocket of trapped solar heat washes up over my face—five degrees warmer, carrying the sharp, resinous tang of crushed frost-shrub and the faint, bready breath of a roof-moss getting ready to spore.

Across the narrowing plain, three other walls of our tenement are drifting in from their scattered pastures—one crowned with a slate roof-corner where two copper kettles hang empty from a sky-vine’s tendril, another carrying the sootless, frost-breathing hearth-pit stacked neat with horn-hard bricks of curing peat. Nobody runs out to wave. Nobody cheers. Down at the far end of the returning street, a child bundled like a round brown hassock blows a tiny white puff of breath into the crisp air, watches the cloud stay round and compact instead of smearing into a mile-long thread, nods once with solemn satisfaction, and steps off a cushion-plant onto the kerbstone.

I draw my right arm back out of the dark wall-niche, wipe the cold cistern damp onto my reed-apron, and reach through the pantry arch for the wooden bowl on the sill—except by the time my fingers close around the warm, grease-slicked rim of bog-wood, the kitchen wall has slid quietly against my own wall’s ragged edge, stone kissing stone without a click, and my cousin is sitting right there on a rush mat in the sudden shade of our reassembled ceiling, blowing across his own cup.

I wouldn’t be a surveyor, I think, though I’d walk out with them sometimes just to watch the yellow spores trace the ribs of dead peaks in the bright air. Those people love the vastness; they love reading what isn’t there anymore.

I love what *stays joined*. I am the person with green copper-stain on my teeth and three sleeping moss-braids warming under my cape, drinking hot, bitter fern-broth in a room that only exists whole on Thursdays, listening with one ear cocked toward the wall-niche to hear whether a blind twist of rush and living moss has reached the sky yet, and whether the water falling off a billion-year-old ghost is running clear tonight.
