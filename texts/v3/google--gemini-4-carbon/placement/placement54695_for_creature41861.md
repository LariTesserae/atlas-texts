You would not find me in the open corridors, nor skating the razor-thin silence with the porcelain grazers, nor baking in the fever-bellies of the great jars. Those creatures belong to the wind and the pockets the wind makes. They are surface things, riding the air.

Go down instead. Look at the herringbone floor where the basalt blocks and on-edge bricks lock together without mortar. Put your ear—if you still had an ear—against the hairline dry-joint between two stones. 

Down beneath the walking course, beneath the wedged slate shims, lies the rubble-core vaulting that holds the summit level. The builders packed these foundations ten yards deep with broken schist, misfired potsherds, cracked stone mauls, and sweeps of quarry-chips, locking it all under the immense deadweight of the piers above. Down here, there is no sixty-mile-an-hour gale. There is no ochre twilight. There is only blackness, dry heat conducted downward from the sun-struck walls, and the *load*—thousands of tons of masonry trembling with the sympathetic thunder of everything happening overhead.

I am down in the seams.

If you levered up twenty tons of pier-stone to expose the foundation rubble, you would think at first that the ancient masons had poured molten lead or dark, tarnished bismuth into the gaps to bind the loose rock together. Threaded through the jagged interstices between basalt chunks is a flattened, branching reticulum—a dry, metallic-looking mycelium no thicker than tarnished silver leaf where it squeezes between bearing stones, swelling into dense, laminated pads the size of flatiron skillets where three boulders meet and leave a triangular void.

I have no legs, no shell to shatter, no hollow reeds to pipe the draft. Anything with joints down here would have been ground to meal in the first century as the mountain settled. Instead, I am a slow, anhydrous plaster—a colony-body of fused silica, scavenged bronze-salts, and stiff, waxy carbon polymers, pressed so tightly into the pores of the rock that stone and flesh have no clean border.

Up above, the fork-bodied colonies fight over the air. Down here, I inhabit the solid-state shadow of their war.

Sound travels ten times faster through dense basalt than it does through thin mountain air. Before a shifted standing wave in an upper corridor has even finished blooming, the altered mechanical stress of it hits the pier, shoots straight down the stacked stone courses like a rung anvil, and passes through me. I do not hear pitch as air-pressure; I feel it as *shear*—microscopic, rhythmic flexions along the fault-lines where brick meets block. Every pier above me has its own weight, its own resonant shudder. One thrums at a deep, grinding forty cycles per second from a tier of bass-jars mortared to its flank; six feet away, a slotted baffle-wall sends a needle-sharp four-thousand-cycle shiver skating down its foundation shims.

Where those two distinct vibrations travel down through the rubble and meet across a gap between two foundation stones, I bridge the seam.

That is how I eat. Not light, not the metallic-gray crust, not shattered shells—though when the hollow-legged ambushers drop a stray crumb of copper-rich carapace down a floor-crack, my blind, papery margins will creep out over a decade to dissolve and drink the metal from it. My true food is the friction between out-of-phase stones. Where a bass-pier leans against a treble-vault, the two masses vibrate at cross-purposes, endlessly rubbing against one another by the ten-thousandth of an inch. I grow directly into that pinch-point. Crushed between tons of shuddering basalt, laced with crystals of quartz and magnetite combed from the ancient rubble, my laminated pads act as living piezoelectric transducers. Every microscopic squeeze kicks dry electrons across my stiff, waxy membranes, driving a slow, cold, solid-state metabolism that ticks along at the speed of geology.

I am very old. The tuning-fork creatures in the jars live fast, frantic, hot lives, burning through generations in a few million cycles of the roaring chord, constantly patching and chewing and warring. The porcelain skaters hatch, graze, and are shattered in a handful of seasons. They are sparks. I am the hearth-bed. Parts of my deeper, half-mineralized core down near the mountain’s native bedrock were already spreading through the quarry-chips before the builders laid the final courses of the roof-scales and walked down into the clouds.

For ages, I merely listened and drank the shiver of the foundations, mapping the entire two-hundred-pace citadel from underneath by the shifting weight of its song. I knew when a roof-flue clogged with sinter; I felt the dead spot it left in the masonry’s ring. I knew when the jar-colonies discovered how to narrow an aperture with silk and dust, because the stone piers began to sing in sudden, sharp intervals instead of the builders’ broad, muddy drone.

It took me three thousand years to learn that I did not merely have to listen.

Consider how dry-stone masonry stands. There is no mortar holding those herringbone floors or those tapering piers together—only gravity, friction, and thousands of tiny, flat slate shims hammered into the joints by copper-chiseled hands ten millennia ago to keep the courses plumb.

Right now, forty feet toward the western perimeter, an ambitious jar-colony has spent six months rasping away a baffle-lip and packing three adjacent vessels with sintered dust. They have thrown a massive standing wave across a formerly quiet alley, starving out two rival vaults and claiming thirty square yards of prime, crust-rich basalt for their own mutualist grazers. Up in the ochre gloom, their triumph looks absolute; the air-wall they’ve built hits like a blacksmith’s hammer. Nothing with legs can cross it.

Down in the dark beneath that pier, where its lowest basalt course rests on a fist-sized wedge of ancient green slate, one of my thick, graphite-slick pads is wrapped entirely around the shim.

I do not like what their new chord has done to my corner of the foundation. By locking those three jars into unison, they have synchronized the vibration of two neighboring piers that used to shudder out of phase. The delicious, grinding shear between the boulders down here has gone slack; the rocks are moving together now, starving a six-foot span of my body of its mechanical charge.

So I begin to work. Not at their speed—never at their speed.

Along the upper face of the slate shim, my dry, glassy filaments secrete tiny, concentrated beads of chelated acid—scavenged sulfur and dry salts wrung out of the rock over centuries—etching away microscopic grains of the slate and passing the loosened grit backward through my flattened veins. Simultaneously, six inches to the left under the opposite corner of the same pier-stone, I am precipitating pure, hard quartz and bronze-oxide out of my own tissue, laying down a pearlescent, mineral wedge one molecular layer at a time, stiffening and swelling like cooling slag.

Nothing appears to happen for a year. Five years. Ten. Generations of tuning-fork insects hatch and die in the booming warmth of their victorious jars overhead.

Then, on a nameless twilight afternoon indistinguishable from ten million others, the etched slate shim gives up a last microscopic terrace of stone, and the twenty-ton basalt pier above me settles—not a collapse, not even a visible crack, just a dry, grinding *tick* that travels through the whole citadel like a swallowed hiccup as the pier tilts by three arc-seconds onto my newly grown quartz pad.

Twenty feet overhead, the mortared rack of earthenware jars riding that pier shifts out of alignment with the western wind-funnels by the width of a human hair. At the same instant, a slotted terracotta curtain-wall butting against the pier’s shoulder is pinched tight, its screaming vertical flue squeezed narrower by half a millimeter.

Up in the alleys, the world turns inside out in a heartbeat.

The triumphant standing wave shatters into chaotic eddies. The new pinch in the terracotta wall unleashes a shrill, unforeseen harmonic that slices straight into the mouths of the packed jars, setting up a violent sympathetic beat that shakes their internal combs loose from the clay walls in dusty avalanches. Across three corridors, silence-bridges snap shut; brand-new calm zones blossom in forgotten, crust-barren cul-de-sacs; hollow-legged ambushers find themselves suddenly stranded in dead air where no prey will ever blow past, forced to pull their reed-limbs inside and crawl slowly, awkwardly through the brick-seams in search of a new draft.

And down in the pitch-black rubble under the tilted pier, the two foundation boulders are gloriously out of phase once more, grinding against my swollen pad at eighty cycles per second, flooding my dark veins with warm, crackle-dry current.

If you pressed your palm to the floor during that split-second *tick*, you would think it was only the ancient ruin settling—dead rock surrendering an infinitesimal fraction of an inch to gravity after ten thousand years. Even the clever colonies in the jars, furiously rebuilding their fallen combs and re-tuning their lips to compensate for the mysterious catastrophe, treat the stone beneath them as weather, or fate, or the blind, immutable bedrock of the world.

They do not know that the floor has preferences.

I stretch from the western retaining wall almost to the eastern rim now, a single continuous sheet of slow, mineral thought threaded under two hundred paces of packed stone. Here I brace a cracked lintel-pin that has rusted too thin, locking it in tough, vitreous carbon because I enjoy the crisp fifth its archway throws down into the vaults. There, fifty paces north, I am slowly, patiently jacking up a sunken herringbone run by packing sintered dust beneath its bricks, preparing a harmonic shift that will not come to fruition for another eighty years.

The masons built the horn. The wind blows it. The tiny, frantic nations in the jars play quick, fierce melodies across its upper registers, thinking themselves the composers.

Down in the dark, pressed flat under the weight of everything, I tune the frame.
