I am the inside of a left wrist, resting across a knee while the sulfur-lamp sputters low.

Not the whole woman—just the pale, ungreased strip of skin between the base of the palm and the thick tendon where the mitten-cuff binds, the place where the slate-paste is rubbed away every evening on a dry pelt so the skin can breathe and listen. Out on the pan, everything is armor and deflection. Out there, you are a pine-cone of black horn plates, a coarse file of quartz-slag biting through ochre grease, a blunt wedge of rib and bog-iron waiting for a shell to lift. To be out in the tin-colored water is to be sealed shut against the wet and the cold and the staggering, physical wall of noise that rattles your teeth in your skull. 

If I have to live here, I do not want to be the armor, and I do not want to be the deafening roar. I want to be the small, warm clearing where the noise turns into meaning.

Wait—no. Let me pull back out of the cairn first, because you cannot earn the wrist in the dark without knowing the weight of the stone outside. 

I think I am an old woman—old for the basin, which means forty-five, maybe fifty, my eardrums long since thickened into white, impenetrable scar, my knees wide and stiff inside their wraps of urine-cured hound- leather. My cairn stands near the western rim where the granite begins its barely perceptible tilt, close to the deep sump-pools where the grey chinking-clay settles and the bog-iron precipitates out of the rust-colored bacterial mats in heavy, warty nodules. 

Because my hips no longer trust the slickest shoal-margins when the six-legged hounds are running in the mist, I have given up the heavy whale-rib pry-bar to the two younger people who share my roof. My work now is the slow, fanatical geometry of the pan: the weirs, the sumps, and the wind-stones.

All day I skate alone in a wide, three-mile loop through the grey lung of the fog. *Crunch-slide, crunch-slide.* I cannot hear it, of course. I haven't heard anything higher than thunder since I was twelve. Instead, I feel the world entirely through my soles and my sternum. Through the braided cartilage of my boots comes the continuous, granular tremor of the bedrock—ten million silica plates grinding iron-slime three hundred yards south, a heavy, buzzing vibration in the granite like standing on the back of a shivering beast. Through my breastbone comes the bronze hammering of the flute-skulls on the quartz whaleback behind me, no longer a shriek, just a rapid, overlapping *thud-thud-thud* against my ribs, as steady and ignored as my own pulse.

When a pack of hounds trots past forty paces off in the whiteout, I know them before I see the silvery fans of spray at their pads; I catch the tiny, cross-hatched interference pattern of their twenty-four feet disturbing the inch-deep sheet of runoff against my ankles. I stop. I lean on my short stone-hook, letting my shingled cape hang straight down to touch the water so I look like a stump of basalt, smelling of nothing living—only rancid fat, slate dust, and cold sulfur. They pass like grey smoke, blunt heads low, hunting a vibration I am no longer making.

Then I go back to my stones.

This is where I find myself most clearly: crouched at the apex of a knee-high cobble weir, elbow-deep in water just above freezing, my hands greased thick with hound-tallow under doubled leather mittens so the wet won't bite to the bone. A stone has shifted—not knocked out by a predator, just nudged half an inch eastward by six months of relentless, sliding film. To anyone else it looks fine. To me, watching the tin-colored skin of the water wrinkle wrong over the gap, it means the silt and the blind white worms are spilling past my neighbor's cartilage trap a quarter-mile down-gradient. 

I lever the cobble up. Beneath it, three tiny juvenile grazers—no bigger than thumbnails, shells still translucent brown—have already locked on. I thumb them off before their suction sets, pop one straight into my mouth under my grease-stiffened hood to chew raw like cold rubbery salt, and drop the other two into my gut-pouch for cape-shingles. Then I wedge the cobble back into the V, testing its seat with my heel until the granite underneath answers with a dead, solid *thud* instead of a wobble.

Before the dim grey of afternoon thickens into the darker grey of night, I skate out to the tall slate slab wedged into the fissure at the edge of our territory. 

I put my mittened palm flat against its wet face. 

And there it is—the best feeling in the basin. The north wind is pushing steadily down off the unseen rim, catching the four fist-sized holes my grandmother’s cairn-mates ground through this stone before I was born. Under my hand, the whole six-foot slab of slate is thrumming like a throat. *Hoooooooo.* I can't hear the note with my scarred ears, not really, except as a ghost-pressure behind my jawbone, a steady, hollow line drawn straight through the frantic, clattering chaos of the valley. 

One of the four holes feels weak. When I slide my bare finger—pulled briefly out of the mitten—into the stone throat, the vibration is choked, greasy. Black bacterial crust has crept inward from the wet rim, thickening like velvet. So I take out my cord of twisted sinew and my lump of sharp quartz grit. I brace one wide boot against the base of the slab, lean my barrel chest back against the wind, and saw. Back and forth, ten minutes, fifteen, watching black slime and powdered slate drip down into the tin sheet around my ankles and slide east toward the sea. Slowly, under my ribs, the stone opens its chest again. The drone deepens, flattens out, locks in with the next stone half a mile invisible in the mist. Someone skating home tonight with a heavy shoulder-blade sled behind their waist will feel that steady pressure hit their stomach and know which way the hump of dry quartz lies.

I like being the person who keeps the stones breathing.

Still, when the dark comes down—not black, just the color of old iron—I turn back toward the whaleback ridge, crawl up the crooked ten-foot tunnel on my belly, push past the two stiff curtains of greased hide, and let the world stop.

My ears ring furiously in the sudden, warm compression of the cairn. I strip off the clattering pine-cone cape. I scrape the grey lead-paste of fat and slate from my cheeks and brow with a curved rib-spatula, flicking the gunk back into the grease-pot by the door to be used again tomorrow. My cairn-mates are already in: a man and a woman in their twenties, thick-shouldered and narrow-eyed, smelling fiercely of shell-liquor and sweat. One of them came from the far southern edge of the pan two years ago at the coastal gathering; he still has a slight accent in his fingers, pressing a fraction too hard on the knuckle-joints when he asks a question, as if afraid we won't catch him.

The soapstone griddle is sizzling over the blue-orange sulfur flame. Everything smells of matches, roasted liver-oil, and wet hound-pelt. We eat without looking much at each other's hands, saving talk until the grease is wiped from our fingers.

Then the lamp is pinched down to a tiny, sputtering bead of blue fire to save the cartilage wick, and we lie down together under the five-deep pile of pelts, ribs to backs, sharing the furnace of our guts against the stone dish beneath us.

And now I am back where I started: that pale, clean strip on the inside of the wrist.

In the dark, the young man lays his scarred, thick-knuckled fingers lightly across my forearm, just above the pulse. The oceanic hum of ten million grazers lives dimly inside the corbeled roof overhead, vibrating down through the cobbles and hollow bird bones beneath our hips, a vast, blind machine grinding the mountains flat outside. Inside, against my skin, his thumb makes two short drags and a tap—*shoal moving east*—followed by the light, circling pinch that means *hounds, six, lean, trailing a lame one.* Then he pauses, and his palm settles flat and warm against my wrist, humming a low, rockslide rumble from his chest that travels through his ribs into my back: *the north stone came clear before dusk; I felt it open from the sump.*

I reach back without turning my head, find his collarbone in the dark, and tap twice with two fingers—*black slime, third hole, clean now*—before letting my hand rest heavy on his shoulder so he feels my own chest rumble back, steady and slow, telling him to sleep.

Out on the shining slate, everything is planed smooth. Nothing has roots; nothing leaves a mark for long; when my lungs finally fill with cold wet in five years or ten, they will wash the grease off me, score a little gutter around my hips with an iron chisel so I don't slide into a weir, and within a month my ribs and my scarred ears will be ground into horn-plates and noise by the next passing shoal. I know that. Nobody in the basin pretends otherwise. 

So while I am still thick and warm inside my rind of fat, I am here: one calloused hand resting on a wrist in a soot-slicked stone beehive, pressing small, sharp shapes of care into living skin while the whole deafening world tries to rub us flat.
