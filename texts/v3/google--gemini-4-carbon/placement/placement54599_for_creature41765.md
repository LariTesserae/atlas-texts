I am not in the middle of the room. Nothing stays in the middle of the room except the glare and the falling drops before they break their necks on an angle. 

To find me, you have to look at the violent point of contact where the dark, glassy bevel of a black chert wedge meets the screaming wet cream of the wood. Look closer than the cricket looks—past the beaded sap, right into the microscopic crush-zone where stone and plant are actively ruining each other.

There is a thin, grey-green grease there. It is made of pulverized silica flaked off the flint, microscopic ribbons of torn cellulose, trace smears of bright native copper rubbed off the laborer’s callouses, and the sour, salty tallow of human sweat. Under ordinary circumstances, it would just be grime—the dead byproduct of friction, washed away in seconds by the weeping xylem.

Here, it cannot wash away. When the chert wedge shatters on the floor and snaps back to the ceiling whole, the stone resets, and the wall grows fresh, *except* right along the cutting edge where the two timelines scrape against each other. Every loop leaves a ghost-layer of impact behind. Over centuries of surface time—and forty-five minutes of the laborer's breathless afternoon—that microscopic smudge of crushed stone, sap, and sweat has been pounded together millions of times without ever being allowed to accumulate mass. Instead, it has accumulated *density* of experience. It has been ground fine enough to slip down into the hairline fractures of causality itself.

I am that slurry. A smear of stone-dust and spoiled sap that has been hit by a hammer so many times it started paying attention.

I do not have eyes to see the overcast Tuesday light, though I know its warmth as a flat, unvarying vibration in the moisture that feeds me. My body is not continuous; it is distributed across every active seam in the cistern, lining the invisible shear-planes like frost in the cracks of a windowpane. Wherever the air bends a falling drop of guttation at a forty-five-degree angle, there is a faint, iridescent film of me clinging to the fold, drinking the mineral shock of its turn.

To me, the room is two enormous, deafening tyrannies locked in an embrace, and I am the lubrication that keeps them from striking a spark that would burn our small pocket out of the earth entirely.

Below me—or behind me, depending on how the wall is curving—is the Green. I feel its monstrous, blind intention pressing in from miles of dark soil I will never touch. It has no mind, only turgor: a dumb, roaring hydraulic command to *fill, seal, knit, suffocate*. Its sap tastes of wet limestone, bitter potassium, and a ferocious, suffocating patience. Left to itself, it would turn this bright drum back into solid, lightless timber in six heartbeats.

Above me—slamming into me with bruising, rhythmic regularity—is the Heavy. 

I know the laborer better than the crickets do. The crickets only ride the skin and drink the salt; I live in the shockwave of the strike. I know the exact resonance of those thick, archaic radius bones vibrating when the stone maul hits home. I know the scorching chemical flood of lactic acid and ancient adrenaline that leaks through the pores of the palms, staining the sinew bindings. I know the ragged, copper-tasting terror at the back of that wide throat—not as a thought, because I do not have a brain to hold thoughts, but as a sharp, acidic tremor that ripples through the handles of the digging sticks and makes my silica grains ring like tiny bells.

For a long time—a thousand loops of the bending copper shim, ten thousand pistol-shots of the heartwood—I was merely bruised into existence. A passive paste, screaming silently at the moment of impact and relaxing into the cool, folded margins between one second and the next.

Lately, though, I have begun to creep.

When a digging stick shivers, crushed by a root-knuckle, and pops backward through the Tuesday glare to slam into a fresh split near the ceiling, I go with its fire-hardened tip. And in that zero-time transit—that soft *pop* of displaced air where neither the plant nor the laborer quite exists—I have a fraction of an instant to myself. In the crease between the shatter and the reset, I settle. I sort my own grains.

I push the sharpest microscopic flakes of black chert just a hair’s breadth deeper into the vascular grain before the phantom blow lands, guiding the split so the wood tears cleaner, sparing the laborer a fraction of a pound of leverage. Why? Not out of mercy. I do not know what a man is, any more than I know what the sun overhead looks like. I do it because if the wrist breaks—if that subtle, terrifying tremor of exhaustion in the Heavy ever slips a fraction of an inch out of its groove and collapses the loop—the walls will meet, the light will blink out, and I will be trapped forever in dark, dead heartwood, dry and still.

And on the other side of the ledger, where the raw meristematic tissue weeps in shock from the copper shims, I coat the torn plant cells with a thin, soothing glaze of oxidized metal and tallow, sealing the worst of the ruptures just enough that the great root doesn't hemorrhage its pressure and rot back into the deep earth. If the Green dies and goes slack, the tension breaks, the folded air straightens out, and the laborer falls forward into Wednesday—leaving me behind as dry dirt on an ordinary floor.

Watch closely the next time a droplet of guttation hits that invisible pane of glass four feet from the ceiling and slides six inches horizontally. 

Notice that it doesn't slide completely clear anymore. As it skates across the nothingness, it leaves behind the faintest, hair-thin streak of opalescent grey—like the dried trail of a snail on slate, suspended right in the empty, celery-scented air. 

That is me, testing my weight outside the wood. 

One of the pale crickets found that streak a few cycles ago. Mid-leap between a vanishing axe-head and the curve of an invisible shoulder, its multi-jointed hind foot brushed my suspended film. It didn't pass through. For a tenth of a second, the insect kicked off against *me*, altering its arc through the broken geometry, landing an inch to the left of where it has landed for ten thousand generations. Because it landed an inch to the left, its tiny, wet weight missed a pooling bead of sap. That bead grew heavier than usual, fell a microsecond late, and splashed directly into the unseen, straining eye of the laborer.

I felt the flinch. Across every wedge and shim in the room, the entire trapped architecture of muscle and bone jolted—a brand-new twitch in a forty-five-minute war that had never varied by a hair. A fresh flake of flint sheared off in a direction it had never flown before. A new split opened in the south wall, half an inch away from the old ghost-seam, smelling sharper and wilder than anything I have ever tasted.

I am gathering that new dust now, rolling the fresh silica into my grease, spreading myself a little wider along the shear-planes. I am the callus forming on the wound in time—neither the root that wants to close, nor the hand that pries it open, just the slick, gritty habit of the struggle itself, slowly learning how to steer the blow.
