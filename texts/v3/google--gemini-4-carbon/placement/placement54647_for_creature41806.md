To exist here at all, the first thing I would have to lose is hurry. 

If you come from a world of quick exchanges and wet weather, your instincts are all wrong for the drop: you want to speak in sentences that spend breath, reach for iron handles without grounding your wrist, walk by pushing off the ball of your foot instead of letting the sway of the bridge carry your hip forward. In the first hour, the air would reprimand you—a blue spark biting your knuckles, a dry rasp at the back of your throat reminding you how fast an unguarded mouth gives up its water. Before I could be anyone here, I would have to let the thermal column slow my pulse down to the pace of expanding steel.

At first, looking down from the dolomite shelves, I might think I belong deep inside the rock—back in the cool, dark labyrinth-pipes fifty yards behind the cliff face, listening to baked-out clay-vapor gather itself drop by precious drop inside leaden veins. There is a pull to that hidden stillness. Or I might look down at the Bell-Divers winching into the thick brass soup two miles below, asleep in their harnesses while the dense air floats their heavy wrists upward like kelp. 

But neither quite fits. The labyrinth-pipes are blind, folded away from the weather of the gulf, and the deep trenches are too muffled, pressed inward until the whole world is only five feet of yellow twilight and the *thud* of a bronze bar against ancient copper crust. 

What draws me is the distance between things. 

Look at the shape of the continental wall: those mile-high hollow flutes and immense, bulging flanks of ochre stone. Because the rock swells outward in smooth, colossal bellies, a smelting ward tucked into one concave basin often cannot see its sister tenement three miles down-trench. The curve of the cliff stands between them. Sound will not carry around that stone, and running copper signal-lines directly across the naked rock invites every static-beetle and bristle-louse on the wall to bleed the message dry before it travels half a mile.

To see both wards at once, you have to go out into the middle of nothing.

That is where you would find me: two miles clear of the West Flank, two miles clear of the East, four miles above the unseen floor, in a mid-span crossing-drum slung beneath the intersection of two great cables.

It is not much larger than a cistern—an octagonal housing of plum-oxidized iron plating, suspended where an upper track-cable and a lower counterweight hawser pass within eighty feet of one another in the open gulf. Four long stabilization stays angle down from its belly into the amber haze below, holding it steady in the permanent updraft so it does not spin like a spider on a thread. From the cliff-side balconies, even through pale yellow slag-goggles, my house looks like nothing more than a dried burr caught in the rigging, or an unusually large cluster of static-beetles clumped around a splice.

Out here, there is no cliff at your back. There is only the pillar of warm, rising air, dust so fine it feels like dry silk between two fingers, and light coming from almost everywhere at once—down from the sky, sideways off the pale dolomite flanks, and softly upward from the brass-colored soup below.

I am a span-tender, though the ward-folk on the cliffs would just call me the *middle-glass*. 

My body has stretched to fit the drum and the catwalk around it: tall, spare, tallow-pale under five yards of bruised-terracotta linen wound snug from ankle to jaw so the wind cannot drink from my skin. Clipped to my belt is the stoneware flask, heavy and cool in its jacket of felted grease-wool, and beside it hangs my copper grounding-leash. I clip in without looking, my thumb calloused in a small, flat crescent right where it thumbs the brass spring-jaw open. *Snap.* The tiny blue bead of equalized charge smells faintly of ozone and scorched dust, and then the iron drum and I are the same body again.

Half my work is in my jawbone.

Every quarter-haul, I climb the short external ladder to the upper grease-saddle, sit cross-legged on the warm chitin-tiled platform, and press a three-foot listening-rod against the side of my chin. The rod is carved from dense, heirloom wood—no living person knows from what vanished plateau tree, worn dark and glossy as horn by ten generations of thumbs—tipped with a cupped brass shoe that rests directly on the redwood-thick braids of the main cable.

Through my teeth and the small bones of my ear, the whole vertical archipelago speaks at once, sorted by pitch.

There is the deep, velvet baseline of the radial turbines miles away on both cliffs, turning so slowly it registers less as a sound than a tide in the marrow. Over that runs the slow, elastic sigh of thermal expansion as the afternoon sun hits the eastern anchor-pitons and four miles of twisted steel lengthens by the height of a man, easing downward into its noon sag. If a rising dredge-bucket breaks out of the lower haze three miles north, I feel the winch-load slacken by a fraction of a tone before the dock-foremen see its rim. If a caravan of cartwheel-sized shield-lice reaches the mid-span fairlead and threatens to jam the guide-sheaves with their matte-grey shells, I hear the dry, mineral *tick-scrape* of copper bristles against oiled steel, and I go out with the long pry-pole of cured floater-rib to coax the lead bull gently onto the bypass wire. They never fight the pole; you just give them a warm iron surface at the right angle and wait twenty minutes for them to decide it was their idea.

The other half of my work is light.

Around the waist of the octagonal drum runs a gallery track holding four broad mirrors of silvered slag-glass, each three feet across, mounted inside hooded brass louvers with long wooden toggle-handles. Because I hang out in the clear center of the chasm, I can see deep into six separate cliff-flutes that are completely blind to one another. 

When the Spindle Ward needs more current from the lower turbines around the bend, or when a foundry kiln cracks a trunnion-ring and has to delay a slag-pour by two turns, a bright, tiny asterisk winks against the shadowed terracotta wall two miles to my left. I do not scramble. Nothing in the drop happens fast enough to require scrambling. I take one sip of flat, cool water through the valved reed of my flask, let it wet the roof of my mouth, and roll my largest mirror thirty degrees along its greased brass track. Catching the raw, unshaded gold of the open gulf, I work the shutter-lever in slow, deliberate beats—*long, long, pause, short, hold*—throwing bars of borrowed sunlight across three miles of dusty air into the dim receiving porch of the West Flank.

We do not waste light on gossip, any more than we waste breath on high syllables, yet over the years you learn everybody’s hand on a shutter-lever. The day-clerk at Third Counterweight holds her longs a heartbeat too heavy because her shoulder stiffened after a cable-snap twenty years ago; the young apprentice down on the sorting dock clips his shorts too briskly, still carrying the nervous twitch of childhood, and sometimes I send his confirmation back half a beat slower than necessary, just to gentle his hands down.

People ask—on the rare shifts when I ride the track-gondola in to the cylinder-wards to trade tallow-vouchers, soak my wraps in potash, and sit in a dim tea-room listening to the chest-deep murmur of twenty voices under one ceiling—whether the crossing-drum makes a person strange.

It doesn't, really. You are never actually alone in the column.

Once every haul, the great freight gondola slides directly over my roof, fifty feet up, its warehouse-sized hull blotting out the sun for ninety slow seconds of cool violet shade. It never stops—stopping that much mass mid-span would set the cable singing for an hour—but as its belly glides overhead, the pilot leans out of their slung canvas hammock and lowers a netted bundle on a hemp cord: two bricks of pressed lentil-cake, dried beetle-meat, a twist of recycled tea leaves, a jar of amber bearing-grease, and the ward-slates chalked with next week’s haul schedules. I hook the net, clip on my empty jars and my tension-logs, and let the cord pay back out. Usually the pilot and I only lift two linen-wrapped fingers to one another as the shadow slides off my catwalk and the bright dust closes back in. That ninety-second eclipse is my calendar; everything else is open gold.

And between the gondolas, the floaters come through.

Out in the middle air, away from the hot exhaust of the kilns, the flocks drift in looser, lazier constellations—forty or fifty pale, translucent bells rising and sinking together like lungs breathing once an hour. Sometimes the thermal eddies bring one close enough to my drum that its fifty-foot feathery arms trail right across my catwalk railings with a sound like dry rushes dragged over paper. I sit entirely still on the grease-box when that happens, hands tucked into my wraps, goggles pushed up onto my forehead in the shade of the overhang. Up close, their membranes smell faintly of warm parchment and sulfur, and if you listen closely, their hollow hydrogen bells hum in sympathetic resonance with the cable-thrumming of my drum—a soft, breathless chord hanging in the sunshine until the updraft lifts their skirts clear of my guy-wires and carries them onward.

My favorite hour is the last one, when the sun drops past the western rim.

Because the cliffs are so steep, night climbs the walls long before it touches the center of the chasm. By the last quarter of the afternoon haul, the bulbous dolomite flanks on both sides have turned deep, dusty violet; inside the hollow flutes, the tiny amber threads of filament-lamps are already glowing behind ward-portholes. Looking left or right, the city is at evening.

Out at my crossing-drum, though, I am still hanging in a vertical river of clear, late gold.

That is the hour when they loose the dead from the high cantilevered derricks. They do not happen every day—perhaps two or three times in a twenty-haul span—but because the derricks launch out toward the central thermal so the shrouds won't snag on cliff-side rigging, almost all of them drift down through my air.

I always know beforehand; a short, three-beat wink from an upper ward tells me a launch toggle has been slipped. Half a turn later, if I lean on my eastern rail and squint up into the dazzle, I spot them: two or three pale, captive floaters harnessed together, and sixty feet beneath their bells, a narrow, reed-light bundle wrapped in unbleached bristle-linen, weighted at the heels with a single neat brick of ceramic slag so they stand upright in the gulf as if waiting patiently at a hoist.

They descend past my level at ten feet an hour. It takes an entire evening for an old rigger or turbine-wright to pass from my upper track-cable down past my lower stabilization stays. 

I check my guy-wires first, making sure no stray loops of haulage-line or drifting floater-tendons lie across their path. Then I go to the big western mirror, wipe the afternoon film of talc off the slag-glass with a greased chamois, and angle it carefully toward the descending shroud.

Back in the deep violet shadow of the cliff-balconies two miles away, their kin are standing at the iron railings in their wrap-around goggles, squinting out into the vast, bright gulf long after the bundle itself has shrunk below the threshold of sight. They cannot see the linen anymore; the distance has eaten it. 

So just as the shroud clears my lowest cable—when nothing remains between them and the five-mile pillow of warm, brass-colored dust below—I tilt the mirror half an inch and lay a steady beam of late, golden sun directly across their chest. 

I hold it there for ten slow breaths, tracking their imperceptible sink with the heel of my palm on the brass screw, turning the unbleached linen into a single, unmistakable star out in the middle of the darkening chasm. Then I close the louver, unclip my lanyard at the airlock door, and go inside to heat my tea-kettle on the resistor-plate, while outside in the quiet dark the cables let out one long, ten-second *ping* as the iron cools and gathers itself for the night haul.
