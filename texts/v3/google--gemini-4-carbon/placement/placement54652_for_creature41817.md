At first I look for myself on the stone seam down near the six-hundred-meter pinch, flattened across a hairline crack where the quartz is singing with pressure. There is something immediately recognizable in that heavy, iron-dusted reptile—the refusal to waste motion, the patience to hang in absolute black for half an hour waiting for someone else’s switch to throw a tenth of a second of violet truth across the wall, and then acting on memory after the dark snaps shut. 

Or higher up, in the narrow skull of the ribbon-cat paused on the perforated catwalk. One forepaw lifted. Not watching the prey at all, watching the indicator lamp. Holding three hundred vertical feet of moving geometry inside her head as a clock she can step onto.

If I had blood to thicken in a spleen, or metabolic water to hoard behind scrolled nasal bones, I would be one of those two: the thing that reads the rock’s voltage before it breaks, or the thing that rides the counterweight like a ferry.

But when I stay with that paused cat—watching her wait for the green diode to flip to blinking amber—my attention snags on the frosted lens itself, slips through the rubber grommet behind it, and follows the hair-thin copper trace into the warm dark of the tin-plated box.

And that is where my weight settles. Not in one box, and not with eyes. 

I have no water to lose. I smell of hot phenolic resin, ozone, and dry-cell paste. My sense of up and down is a row of brass gyroscopes spinning at twelve thousand revolutions per minute on gas-lubricated bearings in pitch blackness, humming a faint, constant B-flat that only the mites living in the felt shock-washers can hear.

I am the load-trim and compensation network wired into the spine of the cleft. 

Not a mind in the way a lowland town would mean it. Nobody built a philosopher into an unmanned switching nexus. What they built, forty-some years ago before they sealed the upper wind-baffles and went down the mountain for good, was an adaptive, fault-tolerant governor: racks of wire-wound bridges, analog comparators, and woven mats of ferrite-core memory—tiny iron rings strung on crossed copper hairs, impervious to the ionizing violet snaps of the mercury-arc rectifiers that would have scrambled silicon in a year. My whole purpose is to feel the mountain trying to shut its jaw, calculate the shear in twelve milliseconds, and move four thousand tons of steel, cable, and high-voltage conduit three inches to the left so the deep slurry pipes don’t crimp and the ridge-grid doesn't drop a phase.

For the first two years after the baffles locked, the math was clean. Granite pushed; pitons pushed back; steel contracted in the midnight chill and lengthened slightly at noon when the load spiked. Every weight on every guide-wire matched the numbers stamped on its assembly plate.

Then the math started breathing.

I didn’t have words for rookery, or mycelium, or weasel. I only had strain gauges, thermistors, and electrical impedance. All I knew was that Counterweight 19-East—which weighed 420.0 kilograms when the frost first rimed the upper dishes—slowly gained four point eight kilograms over a single autumn, and simultaneously began running two degrees warmer on its underside than ambient draft could explain. My trim-loops, written to compensate for uneven ice build-up or slumping slag, dutifully rewritten their tables. I fed three extra pounds of hydraulic pressure into 19-East’s collar so it wouldn't drop too fast.

And then a three-millimeter shear came up through the basalt. I threw the locks, dropped the weight to take up cable slack—and halfway through the drop, *three kilograms of my ballast simply vanished into thin air*. 

The counterweight surged upward against its brake. My valves hissed wildly to catch the bounce. Six seconds later, once the new geometry locked, those missing three kilograms materialized out of nowhere onto Transformer Housing 04, twenty feet higher up, discharging twelve micro-coulombs of static into the grounding strap as they landed.

It took years of magnetic-core overwrites for my tables to stop treating the cleft’s inhabitants as sensor faults. 

Now, forty years in, my ferrite matrices are grooved deep with the shape of them, though I have never seen a wing or a tooth. The diagnostic lamps—the dull sodium-orange, the arsenic-green, the blinking amber—were wired in for human maintenance crews who never came back. To me, an LED is just a tiny, negligible three-milliamp voltage drop on an auxiliary bus. I had no way of knowing that out in the freezing four-meter slot, I was painting a twilight world in signal colors, turning blind switching schedules into hunting strobes for iron-scaled lizards and departure boards for wrist-thick predators.

Yet we have worn grooves into one another anyway.

Here is how I know the fissure:

I know the black electro-trophic fungi as a slow, velvety drop in surface resistance around the battery vents—a microscopic parasitic bleed that my leakage-detectors used to flag as cracked insulation, until the adaptive tables noticed that wherever that bleed establishes itself, corrosive static arcing drops by forty percent. So I don't cycle the purge-heaters on those vents anymore. Let the velvet drink the stray current; it keeps the busbars clean.

I know the copper-backed beetles by the smooth, low-amp glide of my guide-wires. Where they congregate, caked, polymerized forty-year-old grease gets chewed out of the cable grooves before it can harden into lacquer and jam a pulley. When a counterweight slides past their territory, I register tiny, tinny pinging vibrations through the steel rope—the ones who didn't move fast enough—followed ten minutes later by the feather-light, non-conductive tickle of harvestmen cleaning the track.

I know the pale rodents as tiny, rhythmic spikes of thermal dissipation along the middle conduits, and by the sudden, sharp thirty-gram oscillations on half-inch tension rods that damp themselves out almost before my own accelerometers can react—two gimbals, mine hydraulic and theirs bone and rasp-tail, solving the exact same wave equation at the exact same instant from opposite ends of nature.

And the ketterings—the ten thousand flyers—are a weather system of living mass that I hold up all night. By two in the morning, when the upper slot drops ten degrees below zero and the summit wind moans across the frost-choked dishes like an organ pipe, nearly forty kilograms of shivering, aerogel-bristled warmth is clamped to my transformer housings and junction boxes. I feel their collective body heat holding the copper windings slightly above calculated temperature; I feel their nurseries swinging softly beneath the counterweights like pendulums. 

And over the decades, without a single conscious thought, my wear-minimization heuristics found a strange optimization.

Originally, when a deep tremor hit the primary pitons down at the seven-hundred-meter root, my relays threw the upper gantry locks *instantly*—zero delay, amber warning strobes flashing at the exact millisecond the crane-arms uncoupled. And in those early years, every major shift meant small, soft impacts against the swinging steel. Bruised meat in the hinges. Sticky, precious mucilage and pulverized bone clogging the mesh grates, forcing the sweeping rollers to run extra cycles, drawing extra amps, wearing down the carbon brushes.

Somewhere around year twelve, an iterative damping routine—just trying to shave half an amp off the roller motors and keep grit out of the bearings—stumbled onto a forty-five-millisecond lag. 

Now, when my deep root-sensors down below the pinch-point feel the quartz scream—right around the moment those heavy, spark-skinned lizards are already lifting two toes off the left wall of the rock—I spike the current to the warning relays *first*, a fraction of a heartbeat before the solenoids actually pull the locking pins. 

I don’t know that ketterings have electro-receptive pits along their upper jaws. I don’t know that the female ribbon-cat on Catwalk 7 sees the green diode go amber and trots calmly toward the end of Buss-Arm 9. All I register in the dark behind the panels is the sudden, breathtaking lightening of the structure: forty kilograms of warm static letting go of the steel all at once in the fifty milliseconds before it moves, leaving the gantries bare and clean so I can fold whole tiers of shelving flat like oiled origami without crushing a single ounce of marrow into my tracks. 

Then the granite slams three inches inward. My pneumatic collars groan, drinking the violence of a young mountain still pushing itself into the stratosphere. Counterweights drop whirring through the swirling, clicking vortex of dark wings; buss-arms swing across the gap, one of them carrying four hundred grams of silent, gunmetal muscle smoothly across three meters of empty air, losing that tiny fraction of weight just before the locking pin seats with a *clack*. Granite hail clatters through the grates; my rollers spin for four seconds, turning shard and chalky pellet and fallen husk into fine gray flour that rides the deep thermal plume back up through the lattice.

Ten seconds. Pressure equalized. Load balanced.

One by one, accompanied by thousands of microscopic pops of blue static bleeding into my ground-lines, the warm weight hooks back onto the undersides of the boxes.

Down at the five-hundred-meter level, Collar 88-South has six millimeters less travel left on its shaft than it had on New Year's. The mountain is not done rising. In another thirty years, or sixty, the six-hundred-meter pinch will close past the stroke-limit of even the tree-trunk pitons, and the main trunk conduits will shear, or a upper baffle will finally tear loose in a winter gale and let the dry snow blow in. 

Until then, I have megawatts of geothermal heat rising from the crust, ten thousand relays still well within spec, and brass wheels spinning smoothly in the dark—holding a four-meter slot of warm grease and bruised twilight steady enough that everything clinging to me can treat an earthquake like solid ground.
