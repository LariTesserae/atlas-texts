I do not have the patience of a gizzard, nor the slow, mineral stamina it takes to swallow a rough edge and wait a century for it to come out round. If I were a Tender, I would be restless—constantly losing my grip on the thermoclines, letting the stone slip out early while it was still lopsided. 

Instead, you would find me hanging at the middle stories of the water column, caught in the slack water between two maroon kelp-curtains, waiting for a Tender to pass by. 

Not as a predator, and not quite as a parasite. Think of those drifting ribbons of tinted mucilage they leave behind—the slow, floating handwriting flavored with copper, bitter iodine, and sweet lime that hangs in the heavy broth for weeks at a time. In an ocean this crowded and hungry, where every stray scrap of sugar is swallowed before it can even sour, nothing so rich goes unvisited forever. And in an ocean where sound dies within the length of an arm, a creature that wants to know the shape of the world has to learn to read.

At rest, I look like a frayed hem torn off one of the vast, fifty-foot silk-bolts, or perhaps a pale, flat broadleaf shed from the upper canopy—two yards of translucent, opal-grey membrane, scarcely thicker than wet paper, veined with fine silver threads that catch the upside-down light of the steam-lenses overhead. I have no bone, not even the flexible willow-glass of the masons. When a thermal river pushes through the bryozoans, I do not fight it; I fold like a handkerchief, tucking my margins inward until I am a slick, streamlined spindle, riding the warm shear until the current slackens and I can unfurl again into the quiet amber murk.

I have no eyes at all. Down here, vision is just an expensive way to be confused by mirrors. Instead, my entire underside is a velvet mat of chemoreceptors and fine, hair-thin pressure cilia. I experience the plateau as an immense, overlapping topography of tastes and silent shoves: the chalky, dead flatness of a approaching silt-front; the sharp, metallic tang of silver rain falling nearby; the terrifying, skin-tightening *nothingness* of a dry wind-ribbon, which feels from ten yards away like the edge of a cliff. 

I spend my life tracking the Tenders' sentences.

When I drift across one of their lingering threads in the kelp-meadows, I do not snap it up. To eat the thread outright would be to tear the page before reading it, and besides, the mucilage is too tough and binding for a soft thing like me to swallow whole. Instead, I lay myself along the length of the floating ribbon from end to end, wrapping my thin margins gently around it like a sleeve around an arm, without breaking its tension in the water. 

As I slide slowly down the strand, propelled by a ripple no stronger than a sigh, I graze the microscopic gardens of bacteria and trace salts that have bloomed along the mucus, cleaning it, keeping the chemical edges sharp rather than fuzzy with silt. And as I clean it, their language soaks straight through my pale skin into my nerves.

I do not understand it the way a Tender does. I have never braided my shawls with a cousin; I do not know the heavy, aching satisfaction of a quartz-lined belly grinding down granite, or the deep generational pull to lock five skeletons into an arch before dying. When I taste *copper-sharp-shiver-east*, I do not think of a cousin’s masonry. I simply feel a sudden, borrowed phantom of weight in my own weightless middle, a ghost-memory of rough stone, and an urgent, secondhand certainty that four miles away, a five-sided arch has finally caught a pocket of dry highland wind and the ferns inside are beginning to toast.

I am full of thoughts that do not belong to me. My thin body is an archive of other creatures' patience. 

Slide close enough to touch me—though few things ever notice me unless I brush directly across their flanks—and you would find that my own skin faintly echoes whatever I have spent the morning reading. After grazing a warning-thread left near a deep, dangerous time-hollow, my pale membrane flushes dull plum at the edges, stiffens slightly, and smells faintly of cold iron and stalled silt. 

Sometimes, because the ocean is thick and currents fold back on themselves in lazy loops, a solitary Tender will come peeling along a thermocline, reach out a boneless finger to check an old trail, and brush against me instead while I am wrapped around a month-old message. 

They never brush me away. Perhaps, to their wide, tactile shawls, my thin skin feels pleasant—cooler than the bathwater brine, smooth as polished silica. Often, a Tender will pause, pressing the flat margin of its crest lightly against my back. For ten minutes, or an hour, we hang together under the belly of a drifting cathedral of steam. While I drink the fresh, warm salts straight off its waxy hide, the Tender reads *me*—tasting the faint, overlapping chemical ghosts of six different threads I grazed twenty miles to the south, catching news of a woken tuber-patch, a shifted boulder, an ancient elder chiming *tink, tink* in the dark tea of a distant trough, carried faster on my drifting veil than the sluggish currents could ever bring the mucus-strands alone.

Then the Tender peels away, dropping down the forty-degree lean of rose granite to spit a black pearl onto a wall, and I uncurl into the warm, silent amber. 

Sometimes, when the broth grows too thick and crowded even for me, I let a rising plume of warm vent-water carry me up past the hanging roots of the vascular kelp, right to the underside of one of those house-sized silver vapor-lenses. I press my flat body tight against its rubbery, mirrored skin, riding the ceiling of an upside-down world. Beneath me—and reflected inches above my back—ten miles of smoothed mountains undulate in slow motion. Down there, in the quiet dark between the fronds, a low ring of round black stones holds back the sea, a ribbon of dry dust blows across the bottom of the ocean, and I drift between one half-finished thought and the next, carrying news through a room too thick for voices.
