I am not in the middle of the room. Nothing belongs in the middle of the room except the dark air that keeps the neighborhoods honest. 

I am forty feet up the eastern wall, tucked beneath the underside of a great, ragged shelf of heartwood where the frost-split turned sideways along a knot. My legs are bent double, tarsal tendons clicked shut into their bony grooves, four heavy hooks of horn biting opposing ways into a splintered lip no wider than a forearm. I could sleep like this and never fall, though I am awake. The cold is a steady, iron band around my shins where the tallow has rubbed thin against the timber, while from my waist to my ears I am swaddled in a slow, simmering fever of grey fur and ticking feet. 

Perhaps two hundred moths are hooked into my wicker eaves right now. When the wave of shivering starts at my left hip and rolls up across my spine toward my neck, it feels like a warm hand pressing down the length of my coat, dry and humming, smelling of sweet grease and old sedge.

Above me, not six feet away, is the ceiling’s single flaw. I have no eyes to take the bruised noon light that leaks through it, so to me the pinhole is not grey; it is a sharp, vertical column of *thinness* falling into our thick room. It smells of dead sphagnum, sour rainwater, and the wide, frightening emptiness of sky-wind filtered through three feet of wet peat. My nasal rosette opens and closes in tiny, wet beats, tasting the draft as it slides past my chin. If the air coming down smells too crisp, frost is pushing down the root-hairs. If it smells too sweet and heavy with breath, the gap is choking on our own warm exhaust and the moths on the upper tiers will grow sluggish and drop from their rafters into the black keel below.

Right now, the wood is moving. 

That is my work, or the larger half of it. The younger ones with thinner calluses on their six-fingered pads tend the water-wheels and braid the hair-moss lines; they have the quickness for crab-herding and the hot hands needed to lure mud-eels out of the clay corners. My palms have grown wide and flat and slightly deaf to small textures, which makes them very good for listening to the big thing.

I lean forward over nothing—belly out over forty feet of pitch-dark drop, anchored entirely by the mechanical lock in my ankles—and press all twelve fingers flat against the raw, wet cambium beside the ceiling crack. 

People who have eyes probably think wood is still. Even the fast scar-tissue down by the lower sluices looks still if you only look at it. Through my finger-pads, under the background patter of a thousand coin-crabs marching down the Number Three gutter, the colossal tree is roaring in slow motion. Every sap-tube is taut as string under the weight of the mire overhead. When the wind blows across the bog-crown a hundred feet above us, down here the whole chamber tightens by the breadth of a hair—a deep, groaning compression that travels down the eastern buttress, crosses under the flooded keel, and sighs up the western wall, making every taut moss-bridge in the darkness rise half a note in pitch.

My left outer-thumb finds what I climbed up here to check: the third stone wedge we drove between the bundled surface-roots two moons ago. 

The bog-iron pin beside it is bleeding rust and cold sugar; two translucent crabs are squatting right against my knuckle, feeding greedily on the metallic slush, their whisker-legs tapping my skin and deciding I am only warm bark. Beneath them, the wedge of black flint has tilted. Not much—the width of a sedge-blade—but the great root on the north side of the vent is swelling with late-season water, rolling a smooth, rubbery lip of pale callus over the stone like slow dough swallowing a pebble. Left alone for ten days, it will pop the flint out. Left for thirty, it will seal our chimney shut.

I do not reach for my hammer. Hitting stone near the ceiling sends sharp clicks up into the wet peat, and twice since we hollowed this home I have felt the heavy, curious thump of a bog-otter’s webbed feet pausing directly overhead, snuffling at our warm exhaust pipe. 

Instead, I reach into the eel-skin pouch warm against my groin, bypass the flint-flakes and the bone awl, and pull out a stoppered knuckle of hollow bird-bone—something very old, brought from three trees ago, before I was born, carried in belly-pouches through the mud-tunnels. Inside it is a paste of crushed, sour-smelling gall-wasps mixed with bog-salt and eel-bile.

Holding the bone vial in my inner thumbs, I use the flat nail of my sixth finger to scrape a tiny, crescent-shaped scratch along the swelling lip of the root, right where it tries to swallow the flint wedge. I smear a grain of the bitter paste into the cut. 

The tree hates it. Living cambium recoils from gall-salt the way an eel recoils from hot ash; over the next four days, the fibers on that side will shrivel back and harden into dead cork rather than bulging forward. No noise. No striking stone. Just a quiet word put into the tree’s flesh so it grows the other way.

When I stopper the bone and tuck it back into my pouch, my ears—wide, cool saucers hanging low beside my jaw—swivel downward on their own.

Three tiers below, across the black gulf on the western shelf, the wet *clack-clack-clack* of the second cistern’s paddle-wheel has just missed a beat. *Clack-clack... shhhk... clack.* 

I don’t move to go to it. My nostrils flare twice, sorting the rising columns of warm air in the center of the chasm. Down on the western stair, I smell Split-Ear—her tallow always carries a sharp tang of bruised willow-bark because she chews the shavings while she weaves—already climbing toward the sound. A moment later, my finger-pads against the wall feel the faint, rhythmic thrum of her sixty-pound steps hitting the hair-moss bridge, steady and unhurried, going to clear whatever fat salamander or tangle of shed crab-legs has fouled the blades.

So I stay where I am for a little longer, hanging out from the sharp ceiling like a burl on the timber.

There is another reason I take the high shifts, one I haven't tapped onto anyone else’s jaw when we pass belly-to-belly on the ladders.

My right hand leaves the ceiling-crack and slides three spans down the eastern wall, past a milky cluster of six woodlice grinding their steady, chalky way along a sap-ridge, until my palm finds a patch of clay no wider than a platter, plastered tight over a round indentation in the heartwood and pinned around its rim with twelve thin nails of cold-beaten iron.

This is the door we came through.

To the young ones hatched in the casks down by the black water—there are two of them now, half-grown, already sporting thin mantles of thirty or forty moths—the courtyard is all there is. Wood on six sides, water coming in at the top, waste dropping into the keel, food walking along the troughs. They think the engine runs forever if you keep the paddles clean.

I press my cheek and the flat bone of my jaw directly against the cold grey clay of the plug, letting my moth-cloak drape loosely around my shoulders so the warmth stays in, and I listen outward, past our room, into the miles of drowned timber and black mire beyond.

Far out along the root-system—maybe half a mile west, through solid wood and compressed peat—there is the faintest, rotten *thud-creak* when the tide pushes inland against the marsh. That is the Old Hollow, the one we left four seasons back. Last winter I could still hear the hollow booming of air pockets trapped in its upper galleries. Now it sounds completely solid, muted and heavy; the black slurry has filled it to the roof, pressing our old dovecotes and braided bridges flat into the mud for nothing with eyes to ever find.

And farther north, barely a tremble at the very edge of what my jawbone can catch, there is a high, tight, singing tension in a neighboring tree’s taproot—a timber nearly as massive as ours, bending under three centuries of winter weight, its grain beginning to twist and dry at the core, getting ready for a deep freeze to come down and crack it open like an iron nut. Not this winter. Maybe three winters hence. Maybe five.

When it splits, the shock will knock half the moths off our rafters and send the eels boiling up out of the cisterns, and everyone else will scramble in the dark to mend the snapped moss-lines and re-caulk the cracked clay. I will climb down to the lowest cask, dig into the grease-soaked bundles beneath the dry sedge, and start sorting which flint blades and starter-pouches can be strapped between our ribs when it is time to pry out the twelve iron nails and dig into the blind, freezing wet again.

Until then, the draft from the ceiling crack smells right. Down on the western ledge, Split-Ear pulls something soft out of the sluice; the wooden paddle catches the runnel again—*clack-clack-clack-clack*—beating its tiny, tireless pulse against the ice. 

I reach two fingers under my collar, pinch a fat, sleepy moth from behind my ear where they are thickest, crush its warm, oily belly against the roof of my mouth, and unlock my ankles from the wood to climb down toward the casks and take my turn in the heap.
