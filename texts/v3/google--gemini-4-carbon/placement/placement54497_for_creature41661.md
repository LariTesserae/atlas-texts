To bring lungs into this room would be an insult to its truce. Anything with wet eyes, a pulsing throat, or skin that sweats would be nothing here—just a brief, frantic smudge of moisture hissed dry and blown out through the leeward flues before the nearest ashlar-back had shifted half an inch. I could not be a visitor wedged sideways between the piers, bruising my ribs and hoarding a canteen. The room has already solved itself; it does not have room for an observer who needs to leave.

I would be down where the light finally gives up, though the sound never does.

Beneath the vertical flagstones of the floor—packed upright like tight-shelved books—the masonry does not stop at dirt. Whoever built this basin dug deep into the salt-pan before laying the first course, packing layer under layer of dressed, dry-wedged limestone downward to keep the immense weight of the upper walls from subsiding. Down there, four or five feet below the blinding silver glare of the room, the upright slabs sit in absolute, lightless lock. The wind does not howl down here as a gale, because there are no open flues at the bottom, yet the air still moves—forced downward by the ferocious pressure above into hairline crevices, threading through the deep vertical joints in tiny, freezing, needle-thin drafts before escaping upward again at the far wall.

I am in the seams.

Not an animal, and not quite the pewter-stemmed scrub above, though I owe my living to both. If you pried apart two of the deep floor-flags—which you could not do without dismantling the entire twelve-foot world from the top down—you would see me mapped across the flat, unpolished faces of the buried stone like frost on a windowpane, or the black, dendritic branching of manganese inside split slate.

I am a dry crust, thinner than gold leaf, glassy and dark as tarnished silver. A cousin to lichen, perhaps, if lichen gave up its soft fungal sponge and replaced it entirely with spun silica, heavy metallic salts, and crystallized resin. Above me, the world is bone-white and silver-grey; down here in the crush of the foundation, in total blackness, I am the color of graphite and old lead.

I have no shape of my own. My body *is* the negative space of the masonry. Where two upright flagstones press together with ten tons of lateral force, leaving a gap three microns wide, I am three microns thick. Where a chipped corner leaves a triangular pocket the size of an apple seed, shielded from the tiny screaming drafts, I pool into a dense, amber-hard nodule, storing fat and bound hydrogen for a century at a time.

Up in the glare, the ashlar-backs listen to the chord. Down here, I *am* the tension in the string.

Because my filaments bridge the microscopic gaps between thousands of buried stones, wedged directly into the grain of the limestone, every vibration in the cistern passes straight through my glassy ribs. I do not hear the roar of the flues as air; I feel it as a continuous, crystalline ringing in the rock—a steady, ferocious piezoelectric hum. That vibration is how I eat. Down in the dark, where no glare reaches to drive photosynthesis, the constant mechanical stress of the roaring walls micro-flexing against my silica lattice generates a faint, perpetual trickle of static charge. It is minuscule, barely enough to shift an electron, yet it never stops. Hour after year after millennium, the wind shakes the upper piers, the piers press the floor-flags, the floor-flags squeeze my crystalline threads, and I harvest that relentless shudder to crack the trace minerals and strayalkali dust forced down through the cracks by the air pressure above.

I grow at the speed of geology, feeling my way upward toward the blinding collar of the floor, and stopping the instant the first stray photon of reflected silver light burns my leading edge. Light is for the tough green sharkskin rosettes above. Where the light begins, my brittle black frost halts in a razor-sharp horizontal line, two inches down inside the cracks between the upright floor-slabs, completely invisible unless you pressed your face flat to the stone and shone a beam straight down a seam.

Yet I am entangled with everything up there.

The pewter-wire plants do not have soft roots; to hold fast in that blast, their bases wedge deep into the floor-joints and wall-courses, hardening into iron-stiff anchors. Down in the dark seams, my black dendritic film meets their buried wood. I do not rot them—nothing rots here—instead, I plate them. I wrap their blind, cramped anchors in a glaze of mineral solder, fusing wood-fiber directly to limestone grain so the howling wind above can never lever them loose. In exchange, once every few years when a brutal cold snap out on the salt-pan drops the night air low enough for an invisible trace of vapor to condense inside the upper bark, a single molecule of spare, resin-heavy sugar sinks down the woody stem and bleeds into my crust.

And I know the five shells above me better than they know themselves.

To me, the four living ashlar-backs are slow, heavy planets dragging tidal bulges of pressure across my sky. When one of them slides down a central pier and onto the upright flagstones to cross from the west buttress to the east, I feel the arrival hours before its chert hooks touch the floor. The resonant pitch of the pier sharpens as its mass descends; then, one by one, the vertical slabs directly over my body groan downward by half a nanometer under the dense porcelain weight of its shell. *Tick. Tick. Tick.* Its electrostatic ratchets fire just inches above my upper edge, sending tiny, crisp sparks of secondary resonance shivering down the stone faces I inhabit.

Sometimes, as one passes overhead, sealing its mica skirt over a floor-seam to rasp at the base of a pewter-stem, it extrudes a neat bead of its pale grey waste-cement down into the crack.

In the upper walls, that porcelain grout simply hardens into stone. Down in the floor-joints, it falls onto me. That warm, waxy, mineral-rich paste—packed with ground silica, broken plant resins, and shed microscopic flecks of black chert from worn struts—is a feast that lasts me forty years. Over decades, my slow, glassy filaments creep into the bead of grout from both sides, lacing through it until the grey cement is veined with faint, dark hairline fractures like antique porcelain, locking the ashlar-back's mortar even tighter into the foundation while stripping out every trace of lipid left behind.

Over near the eastern pier, right beneath the venturi where the fifth shell died and locked itself to the rock centuries ago, I am thickest.

When that old one stopped moving, the subtle, rhythmic *tick* of its limbs vanished from the eastern flagstones, leaving a permanent, static knot of tension where its dead hooks refused to let go. Over the hundreds of years since, as the gypsum blast has planed away the upper half of its shell, the microscopic dust of its worn-down nacre—pure, life-spun calcium and ancient dry protein—has been driven downward by a quirk of the draft right into the floor-seam directly beneath its body.

I have caught almost all of it. Deep under that dead shell, three feet down in the breathless black, I have built a single, dense seam-pearl of black and silver mineral the thickness of a thumbnail, binding four upright limestone slabs together into a single unbreakable block. I hold the floor steady under the dead shell; the dead shell holds the pewter-stem out of the wind; the pewter-stem feeds the grey-green rosette; and right now, vibrating faintly through the limestone thirty inches above, I can feel the heavy, descending hum of the living descendant coming down the groove, millimeter by millimeter, bringing its weight back around to press the stone and feed me again.

There is nothing to wait for, and nothing to remember. There is only the tight embrace of stone on both sides, the cold needle-drafts threading the dark, and the immense, unbroken chord of the room above, shaking life into me forever.
