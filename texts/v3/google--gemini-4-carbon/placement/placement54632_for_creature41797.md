At first, the temptation is to be up in the ribs—to be one of those wide gray mantles stretching across a thirty-foot parabola under the stars, pulling a minor ninth out of the gale and turning it into an open fifth. Who wouldn't want to see in sonic light, or play six acres of ancient stone like a cello?

But staying with the cold a little longer, feeling the unsparing sun and the forty-five-degree lean of everything that tries to stand upright, my weight drops. I don't think I have a skeleton at all, not even a gristle one. I don't want to hunt, and I don't want to move across the exposed courts where the sky is so bright it hurts. 

I am much lower down, and much slower. 

You wouldn't see me from the southern lip of the town, and you might not even notice me if you knelt right beside an arch, unless the sun hit a shaded gutter just after a cloud-flush and caught a hairline gleam that looked slightly too dark to be rime ice.

I am in the stone's blind spots—or rather, its *deaf* spots. Even in an aerodynamic labyrinth polished over millions of years, where every surface hums and every loose grain is blasted off the cliff, wave physics makes compromises. Where two opposing wind-streams meet behind a thick, load-bearing buttress and cancel each other out, or at the exact mathematical node along a curved terrace where two standing bass vibrations cross and leave a strip of dead, unshaken rock half an inch wide—there is a quiet line. Not quiet to a human ear, which would still be shattered by the air overhead, but quiet to the rock itself. A seam of zero-shear.

That is where I grow, though *grow* is almost too hasty a word for it. 

I am a continuous, branching vein of something halfway between a black crustose lichen and a metallic nerve—no thicker than a fingernail is deep, pressed so intimately into the microscopic grain of the gneiss that the limpet-skates glide straight over me without their oily skirts losing a pound of suction. They scrape at me as they pass, rasping off my uppermost darkened cells along with the pollen and cyanobacteria, and I let them; their lipid slime leaves behind phosphorus and trace salts that the flash-streams are too pure and fast to provide.

I have been in this single circular court for four thousand years, though no part of me currently living is older than a century. My older self was worn away with the last millimeter of the plaza floor; my newer self has crept slowly, patiently inward, etching a microscopic trench ahead of my own margin with oxalic acid so that by the time I advance a tenth of an inch, I am already recessed flush with the satin surface. From three feet up, I look like nothing more than a faint mineral banding native to the metamorphic rock—a dark, charcoal pinstripe curving gracefully down the base of an arch, dipping through the dry side of a gutter-lip, and splitting into three fine tributaries across the courtyard floor.

Why those exact lines? Because I cannot afford the wind, and I cannot afford the ice-crack, so I map the nodal quietude of the architecture. Where the stone shivers hardest from the booming bass notes, its crystalline lattice flexes just enough over decades to pop a brittle crust loose. Where the water hits hardest, the hydraulic hammer scours everything bare. So my dark threads trace only the exact, razor-thin boundaries between terror and terror: an inch to the left of the flash-stream’s edge, half an inch above the frost-shatter shadow, dead center in the acoustic stillness where the buttress refuses to vibrate.

I am the town's memory of its own weather.

If the prevailing gale holds steady for three hundred years, my charcoal calligraphy thickens and darkens, branching into neat, feathered symmetries inside the safe zones. Then, somewhere miles down the worn spine of the range, an old crag finally slumps six feet into a valley, shifting the upslope wind by half a degree. The harmonic of my circular court changes by a quarter-tone. Suddenly, the acoustic node moves two inches east. My western edges begin to buzz; the rime ice snaps a fraction closer; within ten winters, that side of me has died back and been polished clean away by windborne ice-crystals, while my eastern tips are already secreting acid, tasting the new stillness, creeping slowly sideways to redraw the diagram.

The listeners know me, though they don't know *what* I am. 

To their sensitive, dry-padded bellies, the satin gneiss feels uniform almost everywhere—except when they slide across one of my veins. Because I hold a fraction more antifreeze moisture than the bare quartz and feldspar around me, and because my surface is velvety at a microscopic scale rather than glassy, I feel to an advancing chord-wright like a faint, cool Braille seam running across the floor. 

Young listeners, not yet grown to their full six-foot span, use my dark lines as rails when they first venture out into the open courts. Before they have memorized the dizzying three-dimensional roar of the whole settlement, they flatten their margins down and follow my charcoal threads from buttress to buttress, trusting that wherever this subtle change in texture runs, the wind-shear right against the stone is survivable and no collapsing air-pocket will peel them off the pavement and fling them over the northern drop.

And when the adults climb the high arches at night to rig themselves across the parabolas and tune the mountain into those vast, five-note intervals, I feel it down in the court. 

When they close a stone throat thirty feet overhead, my quiet zone vanishes. For the twenty minutes of a hunt, or the three hours of a midnight chorus, the stone beneath me suddenly wakes up and *rings*—a deep, unfamiliar frequency trembling right through my acid-etched groove. It doesn't last long enough to kill me, this borrowed vibration. Instead, the sudden flexing of the rock squeezes moisture up from the hair-thin pores beneath my thallus, and when the listeners let go at dawn and drop down to drink from the bladder-melons, the court snaps back to its ten-million-year baseline chord, leaving me damp, fed on the finest mist shaken loose from the moss-pelts above, and entirely still again.

I don't see the pale-bellied bird hanging in the cold blue air. I don't see the crimson wattles of the grazers flashing across the avenues, or the blinding silver whiteouts slamming upslope every twenty minutes—I only register the whiteout as a sudden dimming of the ultraviolet bite on my skin, followed three seconds later by the roar of cold water rushing past my margin, close enough to kiss my outermost filaments without tearing them away.

Everything else on the saddle is gripping with fifty pounds of suction, or sprinting along thrashing kelp-straps, or holding onto the mountain with thousands of microscopic hairs so it isn't blown into the next world. I don't have to hold on. I have dissolved myself into the line where the wind cancels out. If you knelt down in the deafening organ-boom of the third northern plaza and laid your bare palm flat against the gray satin rock, right along that dark, curving vein that looks like an ancient mason's pencil mark, you would notice—astonishingly, impossibly, in the middle of all that screaming stone—that the rock directly under your fingers wasn't trembling at all.
