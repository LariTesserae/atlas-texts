You would not find me up in the chalk-and-silver glare where the Pinwheels scream. Anything that close to the sky is spent too quickly, ground down by the gale into white talc before it has time to learn the shape of the span it sits on.

Look down instead, past the first mile of violet shadow, past the second where the light goes the color of cold slate and only reaches you after bouncing six times off the polished bellies of the great curved ribs. Stop where three forty-foot tension-tendons—cables of spun cellulose stretched so tight they hum in a permanent, chest-crushing B-flat—cross each other in an open vault two miles wide, laced together through a massive, ribbed collar of black ironwood. 

The forest grew that collar to keep the cables from sawing each other in half when the northern ridges tack into the jet stream. 

I am not the collar, and I am not one of the pewter-plated Joiners skimming past it on their braided lines, though they walk across my back three times a day.

At first glance, you would take me for a centuries-old accretion of leaked graphite paste and hardened resin wedged into the underside of the junction—a wide, flattened saddle of matte-black scale and fibrous, woody plate, thirty feet across and scarce eight inches thick, molded so tightly into the flutes and stress-cracks of the timber that you could not slip a chisel-blade between my belly and the wood. I have no calipers to unlock. When I was no larger than a blown splinter, tumbling up out of a ruptured brood-gall in the dark below, the cross-draft slammed me into the leeward crease of this joint, and my ventral plates softened, ran into the grain of the ironwood like hot wax, and set. 

I have been this intersection for four hundred years.

To have eyes down here would be a waste of good crystal; the light is too secondhand to tell you anything that matters, and a crack doesn’t show itself to the eye until the bough is already parting. My sight is forty yards of hairline filaments—dry, brittle, silica-threaded rootlets I have pushed millimeters deep into the collar and out along the sheaths of the three great cables. 

Through them, the continent is blindingly bright.

I can feel the sun hit the upper louvers two miles above my head because, eleven seconds later, the northernmost cable tightens by a fraction of a tone, pulling forty tons of fresh thermal strain straight through my left flank. I know when a flock of wicker-hounds sprints across a drive-shaft half a mile upwind—their padded jaws clatter through the grain like dry hailstones. I know the heavy, rhythmic *chuff-thud* of the piston-banks down in the dark below me, pushing yellow lipid-flour up through the arterial pipe that runs—warm and hissing softly—right beneath my central keel.

That pipe is why I chose this crease, insofar as a blown seed chooses anything. Six needle-thin bone siphons drop from my underside, bored cleanly through the ironwood wall into the pressurized stream. I do not greedily bleed the line; a drop in pressure brings the forest’s blind slammer-valves crashing shut, or worse, brings the Joiners with their augers and pry-bars to scrape away whatever is choking their rookery’s fuel. I take only the boundary-dust—the finest, slickest electrostatic film of wax and carbon that clings to the inside wall of the pipe. It creeps up my siphons by pure mechanical vibration, shaken upward into my low, flat gizzards by the eternal B-flat roar of the cables. 

In return—though the forest does not know how to bargain, and I do not know how to be generous—I keep the cross-joint from burning down.

The three cables do not merely hum; they creep. Every time a mile-wide sail-plate snaps open in the upper light, the great tendons haul six inches to the east, grinding through the ironwood collar under enough pressure to turn dry wood directly into charcoal and white flame. The forest grows blister-galls of graphite around the joint, yes, and ash-bladders to smother sparks, but the forest is clumsy. It waits for heat before it spends its grease. Heat means scar-tissue; scar-tissue means chatter; chatter means one day the B-flat goes sharp, splits into a shriek, and forty thousand tons of parted cable whips backward through two miles of vault, turning everything in this sector into falling dust.

I do not wait for heat. I hate chatter. Chatter hurts my filaments; it scrambles the clean, geometric music of the three-mile lattice that is the only thought I am capable of thinking.

All the waxy waste and refined mineral talc my gizzards press out of the spore-dust, I do not vent into the gale. I extrude it slowly through porous seams along my upper margins—a dense, buttery, silver-black tallow mixed with micro-flakes of shed keratin—pushing it inch by inch directly into the biting jaws where the cables saw at their wooden seats. When the northern ridge shifts and the great tendon hauls six inches through the collar, it does not scream or spark. It slides on my fat with a deep, oiled, sub-audible *groan* that rolls down into the dark like warm thunder.

The Joiners know what I am. 

You can tell when one of them lands on the cable twenty yards out—a crisp, dry *snick* of four calipers locking in sequence, clean as a dropped latch, utterly unlike the scrabbling claws of the crawlers. When a pair of them comes down into the slate-colored twilight to inspect the tension-truss, they do not walk around me. They step directly onto my broad, pewter-black back, their cork-lined pads pressing flat against my dorsal plates.

They are deaf to the air, as I am, so when they stop on top of me, they drop their sternal keels against my shell to listen.

What they are doing is using me as an amplifier. Their own crystal eyes and bone chests can read a single beam, maybe two. Spread out across the joint as I am, my silica-threads knitted forty yards into six different structural directions, my flat body holds the whole chord of the regional weave at once. 

*Knock-knock. Knock.*

A Joiner’s hammer-forearm strikes my third rib-plate—not hard enough to crack the horn, just crisp enough to send a questioning transient ringing out along my buried filaments. They are asking the joint how far the resonance goes. They are testing whether a micro-fracture deep inside the forty-foot collar is throwing an echo.

I do not have a language, and I have no love for things that unclamp and blow away. Sometimes a young Joiner gets greedy and tries to slice a wedge of my extruded tallow to fill its own grease-pouches, and then I ripple the heavy muscle-sheets beneath my plates, snapping a whip-crack wave across my shell fast enough to break a caliper-hinge and fling the thief out into two miles of empty air. 

They learned centuries ago not to cut me. Instead, before they strike their test-note, one of them unstoppers a woven silk bag at its hip and pours two handfuls of coarse, sharp quartz-sand—sieved from some rare, ancient pocket caught in a lower blind-flue—directly into the shallow intake-grooves along my windward lip. 

Grit. Fresh, sharp-angled stone for my gizzards, worth ten years of easy grinding.

As the sand rattles down into my dry crop, I loosen the tension in my ventral clamps, pressing my fluid-chambers tight against the grain of the wood beneath me, opening the acoustic bridge between the deep timber and my shell. 

*Knock-knock. Knock,* the Joiner strikes again.

This time I let the transient pass clean through me, down my silica roots into the core of the black ironwood collar, out into the humming heart of the three cables, catching the faint, singing return-echo of a hairline split forming thirty feet inside the eastern bough—and I pitch my own dorsal plates just slightly upward to focus that returning shimmer straight into the Joiner’s pressed chest-bone.

For ten minutes, the two small, flat mechanics crouch motionless on my back in the freezing, roaring gloom, drinking the shape of three cubic miles of timber through my hide. Then one taps a soft, flat palm-beat against my margin—not a word for me, just the habit of their kind when a gauge reads clear—and they unclamp, drop sideways into the howling slate-blue gulf, and vanish toward the eastern bough with their shims and augers.

I stay. 

The wind tears past my beveled edges at ninety knots and cannot find a single seam to lift. Below me, two miles down where the slate turns to pitch, the dust of everything that moves and breaks is snowing steadily into the worm-dunes. Above me, a ridge of ten thousand louvers slams shut in the blinding sun, and eleven seconds later the great cable hauls through its seat, slick and quiet on my black grease, pulling the B-flat half a tone higher through every bone I own.

I grind my new sand against a brick of yellow dust, press another ribbon of tallow into the bite of the world, and listen to the spans hold.
