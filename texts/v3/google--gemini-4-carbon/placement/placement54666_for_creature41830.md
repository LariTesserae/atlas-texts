At first I think I am down at the threshold—right at the lip of one of the smooth circular drains where the glare breaks over the curve of the rock and disappears into the dark. 

There is an immense temptation to retreat into the Sump, to curl up in a dry solution-cavity where a whistle actually sounds like a whistle and the light doesn't ache behind the eyes. To have warm blood and a clan, to press rubbery flanks together in the dark and wait out the screaming day. 

Wait, though. Look up through the drain-throat from just beneath the floor. Above you, a column of cold white fire pours straight down the stone tube along with the water. The ceiling hums like the hull of an iron ship driven at full steam. If you live down here, the surface is an errand—a breathless, deafening raid into a place that wants to strip the heat right out of your ribs. You go up to work, to hammer cobbles, to scratch at a groove your grandmother started, to watch the cobalt ribbon of sky with amber-slitted dread lest a spire detach itself and become a spear. 

I don’t think I am a visitor to the roar. I don’t think I want to hold my breath in it.

If I climb out of the drain and let the warm blood go—let the lungs go, let the need for quiet go—the terror of the valley vanishes instantly. The wind stops being an assault and becomes simply *the direction things lean*. The glare stops blinding and becomes simply *the fullness of the room*.

For a moment I try on the radial patience of the Spire-Crab, locked horizontally between two weeping walls, feeling the twin pillars hum through eight rosin-sticky pads. There is a deep, stubborn satisfaction in that—in being a living wedge, in having the whole mountain behind your back when you push. Expanding by two millimeters of pumped blood to pop a seal that fifty-mile-an-hour wind couldn't budge. You are pure geometry and leverage. Nothing moves you unless you un-ratchet your knees.

Yet the crab’s world is still an antagonism: rock against shell, pressure against suction. 

Slide three feet to the left, into the eighth-of-an-inch sheet of braided runoff, and let the shell dissolve.

Here. Right against the crystal lattice of the dolomite, beneath the skin of the water.

Not the Ribbon-Leech—even four feet of flatworm is almost too bulky, too busy raking and dodging the razor ridges. No, stay with it a second: there *is* something astonishing about pouring over a stone knife-edge like mercury, never breaking the ceiling of the water film, living inside a vertical river that is narrower than a coin is thick. Look through that ceiling. From underneath the water-skin, the ferocious, bounced glare of the slot-world refracts through the shivering meniscus into shifting curtains of cold, silver-blue brilliance. Above that thin roof of surface tension, an alien hurricane tears past at fifty miles an hour, completely unable to touch you. You are separated from obliteration by a tenth of an inch of capillary attraction.

Is that where I am? Pressed flat, wide, unpigmented, tasting the faint iron-and-magnesium tang of wet stone and bacterial slough? 

Almost. Except down in the middle chutes, something eventually slaps the water higher up. A dry gap races down the flute, followed by a two-millimeter wall of water—*three quick pulses; a pause; one long block*—the speech of warm giants passing directly through my body without my ever knowing it is language. To me it is only an inexplicable hiccup in gravity, a sudden tug at my lateral fringes before the steady sheet resumes.

Wait. That detail pulls me right back out of the water film. 

The signal in the chute. The edited groove.

I thought I didn't want the warm blood because I didn't want the fear of the sky-spear or the ache of the cold. Yet look at that forty-five-degree bevel cut across the face of the column. Look at the thumb-wide gutter polished smooth over forty years of weekly scratches, diverting six trickles of condensate so a dry patch opens up in the middle of an eternal, ten-thousand-year equilibrium.

Nothing in this valley wears away anymore, and nothing builds up—*except where teeth have been applied*.

I am not the timelessness of the place. I am the tiny, stubborn revision of it.

I find myself six feet up a narrow cross-slot near the center of the basin, braced in the chimney between two cathedral-tall fins. My ears are valved shut; my skull is filled not with the external shriek of the wind, which would pulp an eardrum, but with the deep, bone-conducted *thrum* coming in through my four wide suction-palms and my braced, dimpled back. It feels like holding onto the lowest pipe of an organ while a single eternal chord is played—a vibration so constant it feels like silence, if silence had weight.

Through the vertical slits of my amber second-lids, the intolerable white bounce of the wet dolomite drops down into crisp, honey-gold relief. Every flute and scallop on the opposing wall stands out in sharp polarized shadow. 

I am an old one—too scarred along the flank (four neat, healed punctures from a glancing drop years ago, when a gust caught a javelin-bird's tail-vane an inch too high and threw its strike off-center) to spend much time basking on the high solar ledges anymore. Let the younger pairs take the upper sun-traps and watch the zenith. My work is in the middle labyrinth, ten feet above our home sinkhole, where the glare is rich and bounced four times, safe under a jutting dogleg in the fissure where no eighteen-foot wingspan can drop plumb.

Held in my jaws is no prey, and I am not scraping with my incisors today. Wedged between my forepaws is a smooth, dark nodule of chert brought up from the phreatic river thirty feet below—harder than the siliceous dolomite, cold from the dark water. 

Above my head, a narrow wind-gap whistles invisibly, throwing a stray, slicing jet of air across an otherwise sheltered pocket where our juveniles haul out to dry their hides between dives. Last winter we jammed a rounded cobble into the throat of that crack, and it cut the draft by half, though a needle of cold shear still leaks through the bottom V of the taper.

I shift my hind heel-chisels into a natural pit in the stone, test the suction of my left foot as a braided ribbon of runoff washes over my toes—*steady water, uninterrupted; nobody is signaling from the upper crown*—and reach up to set a smaller, flat wedge of stone directly beneath the jammed cobble.

I strike it once with the chert nodule. 

In my valved-shut head, I hear only the dull *clack* traveling up the bones of my wrists into my jaw. Outside, in the roaring crystal brightness, the blow makes no sound at all. A tiny puff of wet white rock-flour blooms at the seam and is instantly swept away into the water-chute, vanishing down toward the Ribbon-Leeches, toward the sinkhole, toward the blind shrimp waiting in our cellar.

I strike it again. The wedge bites two millimeters deeper into the crack.

Immediately, on the wet pewter skin of my left shoulder, the slicing needle of wind vanishes. The air around my ribs goes dead calm, turning instantly from a refrigerator blast into a still, bright greenhouse pocket heated by four walls of mirrored high-altitude sun. Beneath my right palm, the continuous sheet of condensing water alters its path by a quarter of an inch—deprived of the wind-push that held it sideways, the trickle yields to pure gravity and drops straight down a new flute, wetting a dry rib of stone that hasn't felt running water since the glaciers left the cirque.

Down there, a dinner-plate shield grazer, motionless for three days, feels the cold edge of the new trickle touch its chitinous skirt, and begins, at four millimeters an hour, to turn toward it.

I drop the chert nodule into a dry pocket on the shelf where my nephew will find it tomorrow, un-wedge my hinged ribs with a long, slow exhale, and slide headfirst down the soapy column toward the dark, round throat at the floor. Five seconds from now I will unseal my ears in the underwater dark and hear my family clicking in the vault below; right before my hind feet leave the light, I slap my flat tail twice against the newly diverted runnel—*two short gaps*—just to feel the message race me to the drain.
