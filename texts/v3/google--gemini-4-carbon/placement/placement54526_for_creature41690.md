I am four thousand feet lower down, in the back room of a cedar-plank house where the air smells of wet wool, mutton tallow, and wet stone dust. 

My knees are wedged on either side of a heavy sandstone wheel that turns in a wooden trough of rainwater. When I press my boot down on the ash treadle, the axle groans—a soft, sleepy sound I can actually hear, because my ears are still open to the ordinary world. Between my thumbs and forefingers, pinched in a leather pad so I don’t grind away my own nails, I am holding a walnut-sized piece of the high water.

I have never seen the basin. My lungs are short and narrow, built for valley fog and woodsmoke, and the one time I walked up past the last twisted larches to meet the pony-train at the lower talus apron, the thin air put black spots across my eyes before noon. Yet I know the violent geometry of that place better than I know my own pasture. I know it through the soles of my thumbs.

Ordinary rock—even the good, clear crystal that goat-herds pry out of clay banks after a spring washout—is dead under the wheel. You press it to the wet sandstone and it yields with a dull, steady hiss, shedding white mud into the trough, indifferent to which way you turn it. It has been resting in the dirt for ten million years; all the fight has leaked out of it.

This stone fights back.

If I bring it against the spinning grit at the wrong angle—against the invisible grain of the uplift—it doesn’t grind at all. It *chatters*. A fierce, high, wasp-angry vibration leaps straight from the wet stone into my knuckle-joints, numb and electric, threatening to skate the piece right out of the leather pad and across the room. And inside the wet, half-rounded blank, even here in the dimness of a shuttered winter room, a faint, greenish-yellow needle of cold fire winks along the plane of stress.

You cannot force it. You have to turn it between your wet fingers until you find the quiet axis—the exact direction the water was rushing when the vein tore loose sixty feet down in the dark. Touch *that* face to the sandstone, and suddenly the chatter stops. The stone drinks the wheel with a clear, singing *shhh-shhh-shhh*, and the milky skin of rough fracture melts away into something that looks less like mineral and more like a drop of water that has forgotten how to fall.

Twice a year, in early July and again when the birch leaves turn yellow, the three of them come down the valley trail behind their stubby ponies. 

I always know they are in the village before anyone knocks, because the dogs go quiet and confused around them. Dogs don't like people who smell of nothing recognizable—not sweat, not woodsmoke, just bruised iron, cold tallow, and that sharp, gunflint tang that clings even to their rawhide pack-straps—and who walk straight past a barking cur without flicking an eyelid. 

When they come into my workshop, they bring the mountain’s silence with them, heavy and awkward in so small a room. The big woman with the frost-white bridge across her nose never sits on the bench I pull out for her. Benches are decoupled from the ground; wood floors make her uneasy, I think, because wood lies about weight. She squats in the corner where the stone hearth-apron sits directly on the valley’s buried granite, slipping one greased boot off so her bare, calloused heel can rest flat against the cold flagstone even while we bargain.

We don't speak. Years ago, when I was younger and didn't understand what the water had done to their skulls, I tried shouting prices at the side of her neck. It made us both ashamed—my voice cracking shrill in the quiet room, her eyes fixed patiently on the jumping cartilage of my throat as if she were watching a trapped bird in a basket.

Now I keep a smooth board of dark slate—one of their own rejects, split clean in the basin and traded down five summers back—and a lump of white river-chalk on the bench between us. 

She unties a knotted deer-hide pouch and lays four pieces on my woollen cloth. Two are the size of plover eggs; one is flat and three-cornered like an arrowhead; the fourth is as big as my fist, jagged and terrifyingly clear, with a tiny, hair-thin whisker of copper-green *wire-kelp* still pinched tight in a surface notch where the current slammed them together.

I don't touch the chalk yet. First I close the wooden window-shutter until only a single knife-blade of grey afternoon light cuts across the table. 

One by one, I lift her stones into that narrow shaft. While I look into them, the three quarrymen watch my face—not my mouth, since I am saying nothing, and not my hands, which they trust, but the skin around my eyes. Down here, everyone else looks at them as if they are half-beasts or madmen, creatures who throw themselves into a meat-grinder at the top of the sky so they can limp back down with purple arms and bleeding shins. In my shuttered room, when I hold that fist-sized piece into the sun-slit and let the refracted light fall across my palm, they get to see what the madness looks like when the noise is taken away from it.

Because this stone was born under twelve thousand feet of rising granite and hammered clean before air could ever relax its lattice, it doesn't bend light the way pond-ice or Venice glass bends light. It *splits* it, hard and clean, without a trace of fog. Across my dusty floorboards jumps a fierce, trembling fan of pure violet, cold blue, and sharp noon gold—so bright in the dark shop that the youngest of them, the broad-backed boy whose ears aren't quite closed yet and whose knuckles are fresh-scabbed with fine red lines, involuntarily squints as if he’s still wearing his blackened sheep-horn goggles.

He is looking at the light of the sixty-foot chimney, lying completely still on a dry cedar floor. Nothing is thrashing. Nothing is trying to tear his shoulders out of their sockets. He leans forward an inch, his mouth slightly open, staring at that quiet patch of prism-fire until the older woman taps the back of his wrist with two scarred fingers.

I pick up the chalk and draw four pony-heads on the dark slate, then two iron-shod mauls from the smith down the lane, then three clay jars of winter-rendered goose fat—softer than marmot grease, easier to work into cracked skin around the fingernails. 

The woman looks at the slate. She takes the chalk from my fingers—her thumb-pad is so thick with horn and white crosshatch scars it feels like touching dry rawhide—rubs out one of the mauls with the heel of her palm, and draws instead a long, narrow curve with small teeth along the bottom: two good ash-wood hafts, unmounted, seasoned straight, grain running uninterrupted from butt to head so it won't shear along a knot when the water hits like an oak plank. Beside them she draws a small, round circle. A sack of river cobbles from the bend below my mill, where the current has been rolling them gentle since before there were men in these valleys.

I nod once. No shouting, no spit-slapped palms.

After they lead the ponies out of the yard—the one-armed man walking behind with my sack of round river stones slung across his good shoulder, already testing their smoothness through the leather with his free fingers—I bar the door and go back to the wheel.

It takes me all of October and half of November to grind that fist-sized piece down. I work by a single tallow dip when the days shrink, dipping my stiff, cold-reddened hands into a bucket of warm kettle-water every twenty minutes so I don't drop the stone on the flags. Every splinter and chip that comes off the rough corners I save in a horn cup; the bone-setters two valleys over buy the raw shards just as they come from the pouch, wrapping their leather-bound thumbs around the butt and using those fresh, unweathered fracture-planes to slice cleanly through winter cataracts or proud flesh without tearing the skin—a cut so sharp a fevered child doesn't even cry out until the bandage goes on, just like brushing the wall underwater.

By first deep snow, the big piece is no longer jagged. It is a heavy, double-bellied lens the width of three fingers, clear as nothing at all, set into a rim of boiled elk-horn.

Before the passes drift shut, an old man who maps stars for the low-country lords will ride up on a mule and give me enough silver and salt-fish for it to keep my roof tight for two years. He wants it because through this mountain-strained crystal you can see the faint, greenish moons clinging around the winter planets without the false halos that cheap glass makes.

Tonight, though, before he comes, I carry the finished lens out into my yard after the village fires have banked down.

The air is dead still and ten degrees below freezing. High up at the head of the valley, invisible behind low clouds until the wind scours them clear near midnight, rises the white, saw-toothed spine of the massif.

I take a scrap of waste quartz from my apron pocket—a three-cornered chip knocked off that very lens three weeks ago—and strike it sharply once against the back of my iron knife, holding the finished glass up between my eye and the dark peaks.

Inside the waste chip, a tiny, furious green spark leaps and dies, smelling faintly of lightning and cold water. And through the clear, silent belly of the lens, twelve thousand feet up on the black ridge where no man could stand tonight and live for five minutes, the snow-plumes streaming off the granite lip leap suddenly close—sharp as etched silver, dry and utterly noiseless, hanging over the hidden throat where the mountain is still grinding next summer’s light out of the dark.
