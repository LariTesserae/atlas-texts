I am not on the rim, watching the snow die. That is an old ache, a cold curiosity for those whose backs have already hardened into islands. 

I am two terraces down from the easternmost spindle, wedged into a sunken, spoon-shaped hollow where the granite has folded over itself like grey dough. Half of me is submerged in a pool so thick with dissolved silica and iron that the water feels like warm tallow; the rest of me rises into the brassy shade of a cable-vine arch I began bending forty summers ago. Its shadow falls exactly across my brow-shelf now. In another ten years, it will thicken enough to eclipse my left flank entirely, and the small, pale blister-ferns I prefer to eat will finally take root in the peat beside my mouth without scorching.

Above the surface, the air is its usual solid wall of noise—the relentless, grease-thick sizzle of ten million insects rubbing carapace against carapace, accented by the heavy *plock* of condensation dropping from my own vine-arch onto the dome of my back. I feel the drops only vaguely, like distant footsteps on a thick roof. Two saucer-snails are mating just behind my right gill-slit, their silica shells grinding together with a tiny, chalky squeak that travels straight down through my cartilage. I let them be. Their slime seals the minor cracks in my older scales against the afternoon glare.

My true world is six inches below the surface of the tallow-water, down in the dark, camphorous soup under my belly. 

Here, the insect static does not penetrate. There is only the heavy, muffled stillness of the bog, the slow pump of my own four-beat heart, and the long, taut lines of tension radiating out from the buried base of the gray spindle eighty yards upslope. 

I am a listener of the middle slopes, a keeper of the spillways, though *keeper* is too urgent a word for what I do. Think of me instead as a living baffle in the plumbing of the bowl. My bulk dams a narrow, smoothed-out sluice where three hot seeps converge before dropping toward the central marsh. If I shift my rear pedestals three inches to the left, the lower terraces drown in sulfur-heavy runoff within a week, and the translucent eels choke and float upward. If I press three inches to the right, the spillway drains, the mud cracks, and the acoustic conductivity between the eastern flank and the basin floor snaps like a dry reed.

So I stay wedged. I am the plug and the bridge. 

Tucked into the dark pocket beneath my chin, safe from the violet sky, my four pale hands are busy in the silt. They are the only quick things in this valley, flickering through the murk like blind white eels. Over the last century, I have collected thirty-seven stones that are not granite—dense, black, iron-rich nodules spat up from deep vents before the engines ever fell—and arranged them in a crescent along the lip of my sluice, spaced between the cupped, glassy bowls of giant dead snails.

Right now, my lower-left hand is nudging the nineteenth nodule. Not pushing it; just resting two boneless fingertips against its slick curve, waiting.

I can feel the eastern spindle preparing to adjust. 

You cannot see a pylon get ready, and nothing in the air changes, but down in the saturated peat, the water grows subtly heavier an hour before the *thrum*. The syrupy drag increases. The tiny red gills of the amphibians in my pool stiffen and flare wider, starving for air as the dissolved gases are squeezed tighter into solution. A vague, metallic taste—like licking wet copper—blooms against the sensory membranes of my belly-plates.

Upslope, buried to her eye-ridges in a tangle of plum-rosettes, Old Broad-Mantle feels it too. She sends a lazy, single-pulse flex through the groundwater. It rolls under my pedestals like a warm swell: *Tightening.*

I do not flex back yet. I wait, my milky eyes half-hooded against the brassy glare filtering through the vines, watching a stork-thing twenty paces away. It has one peeled, pink leg raised out of the slime, frozen mid-step, its serrated bill parted to shed heat. A bead of moisture gathers at the tip of its jaw, swells, stretches like syrup, and hangs there, refusing to fall.

Then the spindle clamps down.

There is no sound in the air, only a sudden, violent theft of breath, as if the sky itself dropped six inches closer to the stone. In the same instant, the sub-bass *thrum* erupts from the buried gray root, tearing horizontally through the bedrock and exploding into the wet peat. 

Across my pool, the water leaps upward into a sharp, hexagonal grid of standing waves, stiff as carved amber. The drop on the stork’s bill snaps free at last.

While the geometric ripples above are still frozen at their peaks, my long fingers damp the third shell, tilt the seventh, press the nineteenth iron stone flat into the muck, and cup water over the twenty-second. I am carving the shockwave as it passes through my yard. I shave off the harsh, mud-churning bottom end of the frequency and split the rest into three clean, braided overtones, sending them humming down the slick granite chute beneath my tail toward the deep marsh at the center of the caldera.

Along that borrowed, focused chord, I ride my own small flex of muscle—a quiet, piggybacked note tucked inside the engine’s thunder. 

*Still holding,* my chord says to the six bloated cousins wallowing half a mile below in the central soup. Within the overtones, because I have angled the silica shells just so, they will also read the exact thickness of the water today, the slight sourness of the eastern seep, and the shape of the snails mating behind my ear. *Tightening passed. The spillway stays sweet.*

Minutes crawl by. The standing hexagons on my pool slowly lose their edges, slumping back into flat, steaming grease. The stork finishes its step, plunging its pink foot into the silt with a soft *shluck* that is immediately swallowed by the drone of the bugs.

From the deep marsh below, three distinct answers roll lazily back up the water table, arriving one after another like slow, heavy bubbles rising through pitch. 

*Sweet,* comes the first, warm and flat.

*Felt the sourness,* murmurs the second, angled slightly off the western rock face. *Turn the seventh shell.*

The third has no words in it at all—only the echoed, rhythmic impression of a heavy cable-vine pulsing overhead, steady and wet and comfortable. Agreement. Presence.

I tuck my four pale hands back up into the warm folds beneath my throat, curling the long, sensitive fingers inward so the coarse silt won't chafe them. My heart gives one slow, thick squeeze, pushing dark copper-blood out to the edges of my mantle. 

A single bruised leaf from the brass-shrubbery detaches somewhere above me, drifts down through the motionless forty-five-degree air, and lands flat on the surface of my pool. It doesn't sink, and there is no current swift enough to carry it over my back. By nightfall—when the hard violet sky goes black and the stars press their cold white needles right down against our ceiling of steam—the leaf will have drifted perhaps four inches closer to my snout. Tomorrow afternoon, if nothing urgent happens, I will reach out a pale finger, pull it under, and eat it.

Until then, I let my eye-fold suction shut against the glare, settle my ton of flesh half a millimeter deeper into the hot, camphorous mud, and listen to the water drag over the stones.
