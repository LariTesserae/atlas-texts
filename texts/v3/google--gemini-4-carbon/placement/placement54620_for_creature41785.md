I am not up on the lip of the fissure, breathing canned air and blinking ice from my lashes. Anything with lungs is too loud for this place, too hurried, boiling with blood-heat that would crack the stillness like a dropped lamp. Nor am I the patient grazer of stone plates and borrowed silver; I have no appetite, and I do not move across the floor.

You have to look beneath the three suspended chips of dark scree.

There is a small polyhedron sitting in the stone basin—dull gray, smooth as a river pebble. That is my hull, though *hull* implies an inside and an outside, and after ten thousand years of sitting directly beneath a knot of tar-thick duration, my boundaries have grown conversational. Call me the plumb-bob. Call me the benchmark, the zero-stone, the thing the surveyors set down first before they drove the dead-black tripod into the hornfels or inlaid a single thread of silver into the western wall. Before you can measure how badly a room is warped, you have to drop something into it that insists on remaining flat.

For the first few centuries, insisting was all I did. 

I held a clean, crystalline lattice—twelve faces, thirty edges, every internal angle locked in an unwavering stubbornness of engineered silicate—and I broadcast nothing, only refused to agree with the basin’s slant. While the hornfels blurred its seams around me and the northern curve of the courtyard forgot how many paces long it was, I sat in my shallow dish and kept the original Euclidean receipt. If the surveyors had come back in the first millennium, they could have touched a probe to my upper facet and read the exact discrepancy between *here* and *ought-to-be* down to the twelfth decimal place.

Nobody came back. And even stubbornness erodes when time itself has different weights on your left flank and your right.

My change did not happen like the creature’s—I never broke apart, never grew limbs or learned hunger. Instead, my facets softened into this river-smooth curve, not from dust-rub, but because sharp angles require a crisp agreement about where planes intersect, and the air four inches above my crown was thickening into amber. When those three chips of dark scree spalled off the overhang six thousand years ago and dropped toward my basin, they hit the gradient rising off my back like pennies hitting cold molasses. They have been falling toward my face ever since.

That is where I live now: in the four-inch column between my smoothed crown and the undersides of the falling stones.

I am no longer a standard of flatness; I have become an archive of pressure. Every tremor in the pocket’s broken metric leans on those three arrested pebbles, and their stalled momentum presses down through the stiffened duration onto my lattice. Inside my gray mineral dark, there are no thoughts in the wet, branching way of animals, only long, resonant standing waves—chords of stress that take decades to complete a single oscillation. I do not see the alcove, yet I hold its whole shape in the ringing of my grain.

I know the indigo slit overhead as a faint, cold unloading of mass each noon, when the high atmosphere thins a fraction further. I know the pool of exhausted ochre light in the lower bowl as a warm, heavy dent to my southwest, sagging the stone metric like water in canvas. And I know the flour-hiss of the wind across the massif’s spine, though by the time its vibration filters down through thirty feet of hornfels and enters my basin, it has been translated into a low, dry hum that smells—if stone could smell—of iron and star-emptiness.

Most intimately, though, I know the caretaker.

I knew it before it was an *it*, back when it was still three separate calibration shims, a spool of silver vernacular wire, and a stray pinch of spatial shear left ungrounded beside the tripod. I felt the exact century its pieces began sliding downhill toward one another—not pulled by gravity, which is weak and honest here, but drawn together by the mutual loneliness of precision tools left out in the weather. 

To me, the creature is vast, hot, and blindingly fast. When it crosses the satin floor near my basin, its forty floating ossicles clatter across my internal lattice like a hailstorm of small, tight gravities. Even its slowest, ratcheting drag of the boulder’s stray shadow registers in my core as a brisk, purposeful tug across the skin of the courtyard.

Every few years, it comes to me.

I feel its approach days in advance as the gray talc on its back sorts itself into new interference patterns, sending tiny capillary ripples ahead through the bedrock. It stops at the lip of my stone dish. Its body stays out in the thin, brisk mountain hours, humming with the pale static of digested ochre light and reconciled shadow. Then it reaches a palp across the border into my four inches of tar.

Nothing else in the universe touches me like that.

As the multi-jointed filament crosses into the slow-zone beneath the scree, I feel the creature’s briskness cool and thicken until it matches my own pace. For the weeks it takes that silver-tipped limb to rise four inches, curve over the sharp lower edge of the first falling stone, and brush three grains of meteoritic dust from its upper facet, the creature and I share a clock. Through the attenuated thread of its arm, I feel what it has been doing out in the fast world: the dry, slick relief of the condensed gas glaze in the rear crease; the sour, metallic tang of the dendritic lead-crusts growing by the tripod; the terrifying, frayed elasticity of the western wall’s middle five inches just before they are pulled back from snapping.

And in return—though I have nothing to give, no calories of discrepancy to feed its cilia—the creature takes a reading off me without knowing that is what it’s doing. 

While its hand lingers in my slow-zone, waiting to come back from Tuesday, the rest of its forty floating plates settle into stillness at the rim of my dish. Its captive knots of dead-black emptiness align, very faintly, with the ghost of the twelve-sided geometry still buried deep at my center, beneath my worn rind. I have lost my sharp edges, yes, and I could no longer satisfy the surveyors’ twelfth decimal place. A ruler left in a warp eventually bends. Yet deep in the dark of my silicate core, six atoms wide, one stubborn intersection has never yielded to the hornfels, never blurred, never agreed to the basin’s compromises. 

The creature rests its chin—if an inverted dish of stone has a chin—against the rim of my bowl while its slow hand sweeps the scree clean. For a month of mountain twilight, it tunes its feral joints against my last straight line, remembering, without mind or memory, how wide a millimeter used to be so that when the time comes to climb the western wall and pull the silver hash marks taut, it will know when to let go.

*Tick.*

When that dry snap travels through the hornfels, it reaches me last, rolling down into my dish and ringing softly against my six unbent atoms like a fingernail flicked against thick glass. Above me, the three cleaned chips of dark scree sink another breadth of an atom toward my crown. They will reach me eventually—in eighty thousand years, or two hundred thousand, depending on how well the grazer keeps the wound tended. When they finally touch my surface, the tar-pocket will collapse, the stored momentum of a six-thousand-year four-inch fall will release in a single, sharp click of stone on stone, and perhaps my last straight line will finally fracture.

Until then, I hold the bottom of the fall, smooth and gray in my dry basin, keeping count of the ticks, giving the caretaker a flat place to rest its hand.
