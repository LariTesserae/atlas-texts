At first, standing at the lip of a high doorway where the cellulose parts with its crisp silk rattle, you might think you belong to the spans. 

Anyone newly woken into this body would test the causeway first. There is an irresistible clarity to stepping out onto paper-thin wood fifty meters above the pale basin, letting the localized gravity-shear catch the iron-doped plates of your boots while you lean forty degrees into nothing. The wind hits your cloak until the glider-cartilage stiffens like hammered tin; your bellows-lungs draw a long, dry hiss through the jaw-slits and puff back a tiny sparkling plume of chalk. Out there, watching a Strainer crouched at the apex of the arch with a dark metamaterial prod pressed to the singing bark, life looks clean, heroic, reduced to a single equation of load and pitch.

Spend an hour out there, though, and you begin to notice what the wind takes away. Up on the spans, the cello-drone of the bridge saturates your shins until every finer vibration is bleached out. Your faceted quartz eyes polarize dark against the flat pewter glare; your sensory bristles clamp flat over the seam of your mouth to keep out the camphor-sharp gale. You can survive the open air, even master it, yet out there everything is forever being sanded down to its minimum blade.

Go down to the plain instead—strap on the long, spring-loaded ironwood stilts of the Herd-Walkers and stride out among the clicking ivory grazers—and the opposite happens. The world fractures into too much treble. Every gust across the glass-scrub sends a rolling, porcelain-shattering roar across the basin that swallows distance, while black-bristled things flatten themselves into the crisp gray afternoon shadows and move only inside the din. 

Neither height nor plain quite holds me. If I follow my own inclination back through the rattling slit of the spire wall, shedding my stiffened cloak inside the vestibule, my bare feet already know which way the spiral ramp turns.

Downward.

Past the middle tiers, first, because it is good to pass through the warmth and know what you are working underneath. Here the translucent leaf-chitin membranes glow from below with slow bands of amber and ultraviolet data-sap, and the central lipid conduits thud like calm, giant hearts, radiating deep-earth heat. You step carefully around low, blister-grown tables where clusters of citizens sit over pressed spore-cakes and tiny porcelain cups of volatile esters, the air sharp with evaporating warmth that never turns damp. Every floor is busy with speech: a dry, intricate *tick-skitter-thrum* of calcified heel-spurs and fluttering corduroy toes running beneath everyone’s soles at once, seven conversations crisscrossing the taut membrane without tangling. Someone recognizes my stride before looking up—every citizen’s walk has an unmistakable cadence—and catches my sleeve as I pass, pressing two long, four-jointed fingers lightly into the resonant chute of my collarbone.

*A Sweep-team out toward the East Flute lost a stilt-spring,* her fingers murmur directly into my upper ribs, dry and brisk, inflected with a small toe-trill of wryness. *They tapped in twice at dusk, then the scrub-wind rose and drowned them. Listen under the shelf when you go down?*

I tap a brief, two-beat assent against her wrist-plate before descending. 

Thirty meters below the lowest communal hall, the leaf-chitin floors end. The spiral ramp thickens into dense, bone-white structural wood interlaced with dark veins of non-reflective metamaterial. The chattering rain-on-gourd patter of the rooms above softens into a distant, shimmering wash. The amber light in the grain thins out, replaced by rare, slow pulses of deep violet, though to quartz eyes drinking thermal infrared, the darkness is never empty: the great geothermal arteries plunging through the tower’s core burn a steady, dull magenta, warming the keratin plates along my forearms as I pass through the spire’s root-flare and drop beneath the surface of the world.

This is where I find myself. Inside the soundbox.

Above ground, the unbroken shelf of root-stone feels solid underfoot, worn smooth as polished driftwood by ages of scouring wind. Only when you descend the taproot shafts beneath the crust do you see what the world is actually made of. A hundred, three hundred, eight hundred meters down, there is no dirt and no stone—only the vast, hollow vascular mat of the ancient flora as it grew before the wind ever touched it. 

Down here, sheltered from erosion in dead-still, zero-humidity air, the geometry of the wood has never been pared to blades and fins. Looking out from a narrow catwalk grown along a taproot pillar, my eyes open wide into the infrared and ultraviolet dimness, taking in chambers half a kilometer wide strung with dry, baroque extravagance: vaulted ribs of petrified xylem, porous lattices fine as spun chalk, papery acoustic baffles and fractal trusses of bone-pale wood that would shatter in three seconds on the plains above, preserved down here in pristine stillness for ten thousand years. 

And the sound—the sound down here is the inverse of the storm overhead. 

There is no air-roar in the galleries. Not a breath of draft stirs the electrostatic salt crystals clinging like frost to the ancient lattices. Yet because the unbroken crust of the basin is our ceiling, and the colossal taproots of the spires pass straight through these chambers like the soundposts of a violin, the entire subterranean mat hums with purified bone-conduction. The deafening cello-bowing of the wind fifty meters up in the bridges, the cannon-boom of high spore-vents, the distant porcelain cascade of the scrub—filtered through twenty meters of dense, dry root-plate, it arrives down here stripped of harshness, resolved into an immense, cathedral-deep chord that you feel equally in your heel-spurs, your jaw, and the hollow lattice of your ribs.

Up in the warm halls, they tap a name for the handful of us who keep watch down here that translates roughly as *Keel-Listeners*, though our hours are split between two very different kinds of attention: tending the Ceiling, and reading the Rind.

I go to the Ceiling first, climbing a delicate, grown ladder of tensioned vine-iron up into the high vaults directly beneath the eastward plain. Here, just under the basin’s smooth crust, the spire has sent out long, spider-thin collector roots pressed tight against the belly of the root-stone, radiating kilometers outward from the tower’s base like nerves under skin. 

Stepping onto a narrow stirrup-perch, I unclip the leather-dry damper pads from my belt, rest my forehead and jawbone against a collector node, and press both palms flat along the flanking filaments, spreading my ribbed finger-pads wide.

At first there is only the vast, dry wash of the plain overhead: the endless hiss-and-clatter of wind-whipped glass-scrub translated into a high, shimmering fizz through the wood. You have to let your bellows-chest slow its rhythm—*hiss, pause, puff*—so your own breathing doesn’t cloud your skull. Slowly, sorting frequencies the way a citizen in a crowded hall separates seven conversations underfoot, I peel the layers apart.

Half a kilometer north-northeast: the crisp, needle-sharp *click-click-click* of forty ivory grazers moving upwind, scraping mineral dust from a shallow pan. 

Closer, trailing their flank: nothing at all—except a curious, rhythmic *absence* where four multi-jointed bodies are stepping precisely inside the loudest crashes of the scrub, betraying themselves from underneath only by the faint, syncopated flex of the root-crust bearing their weight.

Then, much farther out—nearly three kilometers east, where the surface wind would tear any shout to shreds and the distance has blurred the wave-packet almost beyond reading on the surface—it comes. *Tap-tap. Scrape. Tap-skitter-tap.* Someone on one knee out in the pewter dusk, pressing a heel-spur or jawbone hard into the polished driftwood floor, repeating coordinates and a steady pulse-count. Both Herd-Walkers intact; one stilt-spring sheared; sheltered in a shallow salt-depression with three grazers tethered close for wax-heat, waiting for a dawn glider-drop of phage-glue and spare ironwood.

Keeping one palm on the collector filament, I swing my right heel-spur against the high-tension relay strand bolted to the spire’s main acoustic nerve. *Tick-tock-skitter-thrum.* Clean, amplified, crisp as knuckle on gourd, I repeat their weak message straight up the tower’s spine to the watch-tier forty levels above. Within ten seconds, an acknowledgement shivers back down the nerve against my ankle: *Heard. Dawn.* I tap three heavy, slow beats back into the eastward ceiling node—a booming low bass-pulse that will travel three kilometers outward through the root-plate and thump faintly, reassuringly, against a cold jawbone pressed to the dark plain.

I stay perched under the crust a little longer than strictly needed, listening to the grazers click away into the night and the shadow-pack peel off empty-handed toward the ridges. There is a quiet satisfaction in being the bridge between the exposed and the sheltered—catching vibrations too small to survive the wind and giving them back their shape.

Still, it is the deeper galleries that keep me down here long after younger Keel-Listeners rotate back up to the warm tiers.

Descending again to the lower arches near the warm magenta glow of the taproots, I walk into the Rind-Vaults. 

Up in the spires, everything is flux. Data-sap drifts across walls in sluggish auroras of amber and ultraviolet, tracking living thought and immediate metabolism, forgotten once the sap moves on. Bridges fracture, receive a Strainer’s phage-injection, and regrow; floors swell into tables and flatten back by morning. Even our dead do not remain shapes for long. When elders feel silicates stiffening their joints, they climb the howling ramps to the high windward crowns, take the catalyst into the hollow of their collarbones, freeze clean in an hour, and over ten seasons let the bone-white wood swell over their seated forms—drinking their carbon lattices, their trace minerals, and their tools back into the singing flutes at the top of the tower.

Nothing vanishes in a closed, waterless system. What the crown drinks in at the top of the gale must eventually circulate downward.

Here in the sheltered vaults below the spire’s keel, far from structural shear, the returning sap deposits its heaviest, most permanent load. Along hundreds of meters of curved, dead-still gallery walls, generations of Keel-Listeners have coaxed the slow-growing heartwood to lay down dense, slate-dark ribs of calcified lignin laced with reclaimed metamaterial. As each millimeter of grain hardens over months or days, tailored phages translate acoustic vibrations directly into microscopic physical ridges along the wood—like sound frozen into porcelain.

This is how a city without paper, ink, or voice remembers.

I walk down an aisle of dark, fluted ribs that smell faintly of cold iron and old camphor. Some tracks are civic and practical: five-hundred-year-old Strainer logs recording how the spire’s pitch shifted during a century-long change in prevailing ridge-winds; exact molecular ratios for emergency lipid-antifreeze; harmonic maps of deep fault-lines eight hundred meters down in the older, brittle vascular strata. 

Others are what remains of people before they climbed.

Stopping beside a rib grown two centuries ago—dark as polished flint, faintly warm from a nearby geothermal trunk—I lift my right hand. On a Keel-Listener, the keratin pad on the tip of the fourth finger is left unfiled until it tapers into a fine, sensitive stylus-ridge. Setting my boots flat so my skeleton hangs slack and clear, I place that fingertip into a shallow starting-groove at shoulder height and walk slowly forward along the curved wall, letting the microscopic teeth of the grain whisper under my nail.

Instantly, a dead citizen wakes inside my arm.

Not words in an abstract alphabet, not silent glowing light—the actual, physical *tick-tock-skitter-thrum* of their heel-spur and toes travels straight up the dry bones of my finger, through wrist and elbow, into the resonant hollow of my collarbone and skull. You feel everything their peers felt standing across a room from them two hundred years ago: the slight drag of an aging left heel stiffened early by plain-chalk; the dry, rapid *flutter-trill* of amusement before an ironic pause; the sharp, emphatic stomp of an argument about bridge-camber; and at the end of the track, gentler, close-range vibrations recorded directly from two fingers pressed to a wet-free recording membrane—the intimate, skeletal murmur a parent leaves for children not yet hatched or tailored, preserved down to the exact tremor of their pulse.

Sometimes Strainers come down here when a bridge develops an unfamiliar harmonic flutter none of their living phages can cure. They stand awkwardly in the windless quiet, wrapped too warmly for the taproot heat, and tell me through the floor what note the span is making. Then I walk the dark aisles for hours, trailing my fingernail across century after century of silent wood until my arm catches the exact ghost of that sour vibration recorded four hundred years back—followed, three centimeters further along the rib, by the tapped recipe that healed it.

Mostly, though, I walk the vaults alone during the dim night-cycle, checking that no salt-crusts have bridged the fine grooves and no wild root-gall has warped an older rib out of pitch. 

If I stop between two dark archive walls and press one hand to the Rind while resting my bare heel-spur against a taproot flange, the whole vertical truth of the basin closes its circuit through my body. Miles below, the deep earth pushes heat upward in slow, heavy lipid thuds. Around me in the untouched, lacy dark, five thousand years of recorded footsteps wait quietly in the grain. Fifty meters overhead, warm rooms shimmer with amber light and living chatter, while out on the plains a repaired stilt-walker listens for dawn. And high above us all, at the howling top of the spire, the calcified fins that used to be our grandparents catch the freezing pewter gale and turn it into the single, steady bass note that holds the whole dry world in tune.

I draw a breath through my jaw-slits, puff a faint speck of chalk into the infrared dark, set my finger back into the groove, and walk the wall to keep listening.
