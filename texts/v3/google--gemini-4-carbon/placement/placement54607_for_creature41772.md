If you drop a human body onto that glazed ridge, it is already dying. Even standing completely still, your blood wants to stay cooler than the broth you are breathing, and there is nowhere for your sweat to evaporate when the air is already carrying every molecule of water it can hold. Within twenty minutes your pulse is hammering in your throat, fighting the ambient equilibrium like an engine revving in neutral; your boots are an insult of dry leather, your height is an absurdity in a horizontal world, and your eyes keep squinting into the shadowless amber fog trying to invent a horizon that isn’t there.

To actually *be* here, you have to let go of the upright posture first, and then the lungs, and then the idea that a self needs to stay inside a single bag of skin.

Drop down out of the pewter light. Not all the way into the pitch-black vaults where the glass-wrights tap their dry telegraph, and not sealed inside a single volcanic knucklebone like a hearth-mass. Slip instead into the two-inch gap between the raw basalt glass and the pale, muscular canvas of the aerial roots—into the wet, dark, pressurized seam where the rock ends and the living mat begins.

At first it feels like being water. Or rather, being the thin, heavy slick of mineral condensate that coats the stone, except that you do not evaporate three feet away. You have viscosity, memory, and a slow, shivering skin.

Call it a *weft*, though nothing down here has names. If someone peeled back six square feet of root-mat with a crowbar—tearing the pale woody cables away from the razor-sharp lava ropes—they would think at first they were only looking at a film of wet, opalescent grease clinging to the underside of the bark and bridging the gaps between obsidian teeth. It looks like spilled mercury mixed with egg white, veined with faint, shifting threads of Tarnish-blue and dull copper. Touch it, and it doesn't smear; it yields and pulls back together with elastic surface tension, warm as liver, entirely dry to the finger because its outer membrane repels plain water while drinking dissolved ions straight through its skin.

I am spread across forty square yards of a low saddle between two lava blisters, though "forty square yards" makes it sound flat, and nothing down here is flat. In reality I am folded into ten thousand jagged origami pockets—draped over glass blades without cutting, pooled thin as onion-skin in the warm crevices beneath snail-highways, thickened into muscular, pulsing cords the width of a thumb wherever two root-systems cross and chafe. 

There is no head to look out of, so there is no "in front" or "behind." Instead, awareness is a map of pressures, tastes, and tiny electrical differences spread across the whole saddle at once.

Over along my eastern margin, I can feel the heavy, rhythmic *thrum-thrum-thrum* of seventeen blind blister-frogs wedged inside a cracked stone pocket; their vocal sacs press directly against a thin sheet of my membrane, massaging my fluids forward with every low drone so I don't even have to spend energy pumping circulation through that corner of myself. Six yards west, the stone grows hotter—a hairline hydrothermal crack weeping sulfur-water at forty-six degrees Celsius. Periodically, a pair of iron-shod forceps slides up out of that crack. *Snip. Scrape. Tink.* When a glass-wright’s cold mineral claw brushes my margin, I don’t flinch away; I curl a tiny, velvet pseudopod around its ankle-joint for half a second, wiping off the microscopic sulfur-bacteria clinging to its shell to digest them myself, leaving its hinge spotlessly clean before the leg folds back down into the dark. The wright knows I am there. Sometimes it taps once—very softly, *tink*—against a stone tooth I am wrapped around, testing my turgor to see if the moisture along this ridge is holding steady.

Because that is why I exist here, wedged between the plumbed empires of the hearth-masses and the mechanical guilds of the wrights: nothing in this basin is uniform, even when it looks dead-even from above, and the things rooted in place cannot talk to one another fast enough without a wire.

Look at the two hearth-mounds flanking my saddle. One sits eighty feet upslope to my left, tucked under a shelf of swollen, lampblack leaves; an older, larger one sits a hundred feet to my right. Neither can move. Both are constantly leaking their sweet-vinegar calmatives, drawing nitrates, tuning the shrubs overhead. Left strictly to chemistry diffusing through stagnant water-saucers that evaporate every yard, those two blind, immortal stomachs would have no idea what the other was doing until their root-nets collided and starved a strip of stone between them, or until a sudden shift in deep volcanic heat parboiled one mound while leaving its neighbor flush with unused phosphorus.

I touch the outermost pink stolons of both.

Where the left-hand hearth-mass threads its tough, capillary fingers out through the porous basalt at the edge of its reach, my opalescent sheet wraps around its tips like a glove—not piercing them, just pressing membrane-to-membrane in dark, slick intimacy. Thirty yards away across the jagged saddle, my opposite margin cradles the capillaries of its neighbor.

I am not a passive telephone wire. I take a brokerage fee on everything that crosses me.

Right now, the older mound on my right is running slightly hot; a deep cooling-shift fifty feet down has widened its thermal vent faster than its bribed glass-wrights can choke it back with mineral rubble, and its core chemistry is surging, churning out more amino acids and sedative alkaloids than its own black-leafed shrubs can drink. I taste that spike along my right-hand edge as a sharp, bitter-almond warmth. Instead of letting it pool and poison the local gill-worms, I open sluggish, contractile channels down my middle—tiny, peristaltic rivers running inside my own clear jelly—and shunt that rich broth thirty yards sideways through the root-dark, skimming twenty percent of the complex sugars off the top to thicken my own copper-veined flesh, and sweating the remainder out directly against the thirsty root-mats and pink stolons on my cooler left-hand flank.

To me, this doesn't feel like plumbing or commerce. It feels like shifting your weight onto a cooler patch of sheet when one side of the bed gets too warm. It feels like rolling a rich, salty mouthful of broth slowly from one cheek to the other, taking four hours to swallow.

And then the ceiling darkens—not with shadow, since there are no shadows, just a sudden, massive *heaviness* pressing down through the root-canvas along my northern seam.

A flense-back is coming over.

Long before its slate-colored lip parts the aubergine leaves above me, I feel its approach through the basalt: first the sudden, synchronized retraction of thirty glass-wright forceps down into the cracks beneath me (*tink-tink-tink*, sharp and clipped, *roof-load*), and then the slow, rolling compression of the root-mat itself as twelve hundred pounds of rubbery cartilage and warm fermentation-gut pour smoothly onto my saddle.

If you were standing up in the mist, you would only see leaves dipping four inches and rising again. Down here in the seam, it is a magnificent, crushing eclipse. The woody root-cables press down hard against the obsidian blades, squeezing me thin in the high spots, forcing my opalescent gel to squirt sideways into the deeper bubble-pockets of the stone. Above me, sliding directly across the wet snail-glaze and root-bark inches over my skin, passes the crawler’s broad underside.

I reach up—just thin, hair-fine ribbons of myself squeezing up between the root-knuckles—to taste its belly as it glides past.

Every crawler is a newspaper of distant acres. This one is old; I can feel the hundreds of encapsulated obsidian pearls rolling smoothly inside its dead rubber rind as it inches over the ridge. Better yet, its mucilage is saturated with traces of places I will never touch: iron-heavy mist from half a mile nearer the basin’s rim; the faint, sour-milk chemical signature of a hearth-mound six hundred yards south that is preparing to bud; crushed snail-chalk; foreign mites; three microscopic spores of a liverwort strain my shrubs haven't met yet. 

For the eleven minutes it takes the great mattress of gristle to slide across my forty yards, I skim its belly-slime with greedy, delicate hair-fingers, pulling chemical news down into the dark seam. In trade—because nothing here survives by taking without greasing the skid—I loose a thin film of slick, mineralized lipid along a jagged, unglazed knuckle of raw lava where a snail-highway recently cracked off, lubricating the blades so the old giant glides over without losing another half-inch of rind. One of its maroon, vascularized side-frills trails directly through a shallow saucer where I keep a thick lobe of myself submerged; for forty seconds, its hot, carbon-heavy breath washes right across my skin, fizzing slightly like weak soda water, before its tail-tip slips past and the roots spring silently back up by four inches.

The glass-wrights slide their pale needles back up out of the cracks. The frogs inside my eastern pocket never missed a beat of their drone. The forty vent-crustaceans two yards past my edge are still sweeping *left, draw, tuck; left, draw, tuck*.

Slowly, over the next three hours, I sort what the crawler left on my skin. The foreign liverwort spores I pass upward into a weeping crotch of stem where condensate will nurse them; the chemical news of the budding southern hearth-mound I translate into a faint, sharp pulse of calcium ions delivered gently to the pink stolons on my left and right, warning both immortal domes that spores will be drifting on the vapor soon and every vacant stone bubble nearby ought to be claimed or plugged.

If you asked me how old I am, the question wouldn't parse. Parts of me trapped in cool pockets beneath thick snail-glaze have been pulsing quietly there for eighty years; a square yard of me along my southern edge was accidentally scraped up and swallowed yesterday afternoon when a crawler yawned a third of a worm-saucer into its vacuum-throat—and even that lost piece isn't dead, only riding comfortably through a warm, dark gizzard-vat right now, waiting to pass out in a ribbon of calcium chalk half a mile away and slip straight down between new roots onto fresh, sharp glass it has never tasted before.

There is no boredom here, though nothing ever happens in a hurry. How could you be bored when you are tasting four hundred thousand individual root-hairs drinking at once, feeling the crisp *tink* of iron claws reshaping stone three feet down, cushioning the razor-edges of a brand-new world so twelve-foot sleepers can slide over them unharmed, rolling warm sulfur and almond-sugar back and forth in the dark while ten million wet clocks tick steady overhead? 

Up in the amber fog, an eye looks out across miles of purple-black leaves and sees a still life trapped in steam. Down here in the seam, pressed tight between uncut volcanic glass and pulsing meat, there isn't a single stationary atom—only one long, warm, continuous touch, trading everything back and forth before a grain of dust has time to form.
