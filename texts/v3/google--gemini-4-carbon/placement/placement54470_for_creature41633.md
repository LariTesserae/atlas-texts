I am the weight on the underside of the stalk. 

Not the vine itself—the vines are stupid, hurried things, whipping out into the warm dark like blind eels, spending themselves in an hour just to cinch around nothing. And not the stone below, though I know the stone’s dry, powdery secret under the sweat; I have tasted the ash when a whole shelf gives way and drops us all into the choking dark beneath the roots.

I am slower than the pale wood, and softer than the glass, and I do not have a bone in me to shatter when the canopy tears loose. 

If you pressed your swollen, star-whorled thumb against me in passing, you would think I was just a thick collar of scar tissue where a strangler-tendril bit into a fluted pipe-stalk and fused. My back has the exact waxy, tallow-slick grain of the organ-plants, pebbled with false condensation so the frantic, scraping claws of the leapers slide right off me without catching hold. I am broad as a platter, flat as a tongue, cinched tight around the swelling mid-joints of the big steam-drinking trunks where the sap runs hottest and tastes of sulfur and thin sugar.

I do not hear the roar the way the big-eared ones do. Sound to me is only pressure—a thick, ceaseless kneading against my dorsal skin. The shrieking of the throat-bags, the dry sandpaper *shhhk* of the segmented legs down on the basalt, the pistol-crack of the seed-bladders: it all just washes over my back as a warm, heavy surf. 

What I listen to is the wood beneath my belly.

My underside is a pale, muscular foot, endlessly rippling in tiny, microscopic waves, tasting the vascular shudder of the plant I ride. Through the watery fibers of the stalk, the whole ridge is clear to me. I feel the tight, erratic *thrum-thrum* of grubs chewing hollow galleries three yards down; I feel the slow, hydraulic lean of the neighboring trunks as they bend greedily toward a fresh gap in the canopy; I feel the deep, rhythmic *huff* of the rock-fissure twenty feet below, pushing hot mineral breath up into the roots.

When I move, it is only because the sap beneath me has begun to sour—the first chemical whisper that the root-mat has torn loose from the ash and the whole tangle is twenty minutes from collapsing into a ravine. Then I unseal my rim, let the warm air kiss my wet under-flesh for half a second, and glide sideways onto a younger, firmer cable, oozing smoothly over razor-sharp obsidian splinters wedged in the bark without leaving a drop of slime behind. My belly simply parts around the glass edge and closes after it, unharmed. In a place where everything strikes, snaps, and shears, there is a deep, quiet triumph in having no leverage to offer.

I know the heavy, six-limbed ghosts better than they know me, though they know me well enough.

Sometimes, moving along a horizontal cable in the pitch-black, a wide, double-thumbed hand clamps down directly over my back as one of them swings across a chasm. For three heartbeats I am pinned under a hundred and sixty pounds of dense, hot muscle and horn-callus. We do not startle each other. Their palm reads my slight, rubbery yield—*not rotten bark, not an acid-caterpillar, just a flat-clinger*—and their weight rolls smoothly off me onto their hooked elbow without breaking stride. In that brief press, I drink the radiant heat of their blood and the sharp, salty crust of mineral sweat on their skin. They are startlingly hot compared to the frogs and the crabs, like small, sealed pockets of the deep red rock-cracks walking around in the mid-air.

Mostly, though, I meet them through the stone.

I like the ridges where the steam vents hiss hardest, which means sooner or later, they come to my neighborhood to blind the valley. 

It is an astonishing thing to feel through your belly. First comes the sharp, clean *chop-chop* of glass biting through my host-stalk’s neighbors—not the ragged, tearing chew of a scavenger claw, which makes the plant-fibers scream with ragged vibrations, but a sudden, surgical severance. Then the scream begins. When they jam the cut pipes into the fissure, the stone beneath the roots goes mad with a single, sustained, tearing vibration. All around me in the dark, the frantic, metallic clicking of the leapers stutters and goes ragged; heavy, confused bodies lose their sonar-grip and plummet past my trunk, crashing through the tallow-fronds below. The velvet snare-worms haul up their mucus lines and curl into tight, sulking knots. 

For twenty feet around the whistling vent, the frantic predation stops. Even the millipedes turn away from the buzzing stone.

And then, underneath that high, protective shriek, the second sound arrives.

It comes up out of the basalt, climbs through the dry ash, enters the woody base of my stalk, and pours straight into my broad, flat foot. *Hmmmmmmmm-uh-hmmmmm.* 

The ghosts have sat down against the rock, pressed their heavy tailbones to the ridge, and begun to hum their maps into one another’s skeletons. 

They do not make it for me, of course. I have no skeleton to catch the harmonics, no mind built to picture a newly fallen mat three ridges east or a sweet-water seep by the obsidian scree. Yet I unclamp my outer rim just a fraction and press my entire central nerve-net flat against the quivering bark to soak it in. Nothing else in this basin makes a continuous, braided vibration like that. Everything else here is a spike: a snap, a shriek, a burst, a frantic scrabble, a wet crunch. The valley’s rhythm is panic and hunger, short and jagged. 

Their sub-bass is long. It rolls and overlaps, rising and dipping in deliberate, unhurried swells, warm as a deep vent, steady as the tectonic creep far beneath the caldera floor. While they sit tangled together in the dark, picking glass from their thick soles and passing their big-eared young from chest to chest, their song massages the water right out of the pores of the wood beneath me. For half an hour, clinging to the dark flank of an organ-pipe ten feet above their huddled shoulders, I stop digesting, stop testing the sap for rot, and simply vibrate at their frequency.

Sometimes I pay a steep price for liking the vents. 

They know what I am made of. If one of their wide, sweeping hands brushes me *after* the whistles are wedged in—when they are hungry, or when someone’s calf has been laid open by a fresh basalt edge and the alkaline lichen isn't enough to seal it—the hand does not roll harmlessly past. 

A square, horn-thick fingernail—*tik*—slips under my sealed margin before I can clamp down hard enough to become part of the wood, or an obsidian flake slides neatly beneath my foot, parting my suction in one slick stroke. No struggle; I curl instantly into a tight, rubbery ball in the dark, tasting thecured seed-bladder of a pouch or the hot, calloused cup of a palm. 

Wait, no—not always killed. That is the strangest thing about them, the anomaly that makes them part of the weather rather than just another jaw. Half the time, when a double-thumbed hand peels one of my smaller kin off a stalk near the shelter, they do not scrape us out with a thumb-blade to eat raw. Instead, we are pressed flat—*slap*—directly over a deep, weeping gash on a forearm or shoulder. Held there by a firm palm for ten breaths until our startled reflex makes us clamp down with iron suction on the hot skin, sealing the torn flesh shut against the airborne rot and the sulfur-sweat while our belly-mucus numbs the sting. 

Imagine riding *that* through the basin. 

I have done it once, when I was smaller—barely the width of three fingers. Torn from a tallow-stem in the pitch-black and clapped onto the bruised, heaving ribcage of a young scout whose side had been raked by a falling crab. For four days—or whatever passes for days under the purple-bruised haze—I had no slow, rooted stalk beneath me. Instead, I rode a furnace. 

The speed of it was terrifying, magnificent. We dropped twenty feet through empty, rushing warm air, hit a swaying vine-bridge with a bone-jarring *thud* that nearly popped my seal, and flowed immediately upward through a whipping tangle of strangler-cables. Through my foot, pressed tight over torn muscle, I felt the thunderous, piston-like slam of a four-chambered heart; I felt the twin throat-sacs inflate like bellows and shake the ribs directly beneath me whenever we dropped into a whistle-camp to trade maps; I felt the sudden, icy constriction of blood vessels when a heavy flyer blundered into our face in the dark, followed instantly by the rapid, silent *flick-flick* of fingers against a companion's collarbone. *Safe. Keep moving.*

I drank a thin broth of salt-sweat and healing serum from the seam of the wound, keeping the edges drawn tight together while the flesh knitted beneath my foot. Hands brushed over me constantly during the rest-piles—never prying me loose, only checking my rim with gentle, whorled fingertips to see if my seal still held, grooming away the acid-spores that settled on my waxy back just as carefully as they groomed the scout’s own neck-folds. For a handful of cycles, I was stitched into their skin, carried at the furious, leaping pace of thoughts and tools across miles of razor-stone I could never have oozed across in ten lifetimes.

When the scar beneath me finally hardened into dry, ridged horn and stopped feeding me moisture, I loosened my rim during a forty-minute sleep-huddle, slid quietly down a sweaty flank onto the bare basalt, and glided up the nearest fluted pipe-stalk before the juvenile at the vent tapped *tik-tik* on its shin.

Now I am broad as two hands, grown fat and tough on the high, sulfur-sweet sap of a prime vent-ridge. My back is scarred with minor nicks from colliding bat-moths and dusted with fine ash thrown up from a chasm collapse yesterday. 

Down below me, ten yards off through the dense, suffocating tiers of tallow-leaf, a steam fissure has been sighing steadily for an hour, clear of mineral choke, strong and hot. None of the stalks around it have been cut yet. The throat-bags are screaming all around me in the wet dark, loud enough to shake condensation droplets off my rim, mapping a territory they think belongs to claws and teeth.

I keep my belly flat to the trembling wood, drinking the warm sap, waiting for a bladder-fruit to burst and a half-dozen silent weights to drop out of the noise—waiting for the obsidian to bite the stalks, for the pipes to scream the leapers blind, and for the deep, rolling hum of the map-singers to come climbing back up through the stone.
