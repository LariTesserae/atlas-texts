Not out on the matte white arena—at least, not anymore, or perhaps never with the fast, vaulting grace of the ones who carry the glider-hide sails under their arms. Looking down into that thrashing copper bowl, my attention doesn't snag on the leap into the fronds. It goes immediately to a problem hidden in the cliff face: chalk and limestone under a relentless, silicate-heavy gale do not stay the same shape. 

A stone flute bored into soft rock and blasted continuously by mineral grit will widen. Its lip will bevel and fray. Left alone for five seasons, the three-toned chord that means *steady eastward heave* will flatten by a quarter-tone, drifting slowly toward the hollow minor groan that means *the vent is inhaling*. Left alone for twenty, the cliff will lie to everyone below it, calling harvesters out onto the exposed floor into the teeth of a downdraft.

So look for me about fifteen yards back from the overhang, inside the dry, whistling sinuses just behind the cliff’s skin.

Up here, the rock is thin enough that it shivers like a drumhead. There is no copper dusk—only a faint, dusty peach glow leaking down the six-foot throats of the wind-bores from the violent sky outside, ending abruptly against heavy, notched baffles of slate and dense, imported basalt wedged into cross-slots in the stone. The air in the gallery isn’t dead like the deep sleeping-chambers below, and it isn't the lethal whirlpool of the grove; it is a series of tight, ferocious jets, hot and smelling of struck flint, hissing around the edges of the dampers.

If I have a body here, it is built like the rest of them—flat-ribbed, tallow-skinned, greased with iron—but Asymmetric from years of bracing one shoulder against a vibrating tunnel wall while working an obsidian rasp with the other. Perhaps one leg drags a little stiffer than the copper chains require, the knee-joint fused decades ago when a tether snapped or a ground-chain hopped a boulder during a blue-white discharge on the floor, cooking the cartilage dry while sparing the heart. A body too slow to tack across open stone, which the colony cannot afford to waste and wouldn't dream of discarding. 

My work is calibration.

I crawl from bore to bore on padded knees, keeping my mica lids half-drawn against the stinging needle-sprays of chalk dust that shoot through the seams whenever the fumarole stutters. I don't listen with ears alone; ears go dull up here in the constant, roaring *fff-hhuuu* of the intakes. I press my collarbones, my jaw, the flat of my sternum directly against the warm partition walls between the flutes, feeling where two frequencies beat against each other inside the limestone. 

When the third throat begins to wobble—its pitch fraying because a week of grit-storms has scoured a thumb’s-width off its outer bevel—I slide a wooden wedge in to choke the draft, crawl almost to the blinding, roaring lip where the copper light boils, and pack the eroded curve with a mastic of pulverized fulgurite glass, iron dust, and boiled bladder-glue. It cures hard as flint in the hot sulfurous blast. Then I back out, pull the choke-wedge, press my chin to the floor, and feel the chord lock back into its clean, life-saving third.

Wait until the shift changes—until a younger cousin scrambles up the dry chimney, taps twice on my ankle-bone so I don't startle, and takes over watching the baffles. Follow me down the forty vertical yards of polished, narrow corkscrews into the absolute black.

Because this is the other half of the same trade, and maybe closer to what I really am down at the root.

When I peel off the dusty cuirass in the blood-warm dark and slide my horn-lids open, my hands are still humming with the day’s frequencies. Down here, past the mortars where the children are grinding glass into oil, past the violet flicker of the resin-paddles strobing over the nurseries, the passages narrow into the archive-crevices—long, dry ribs of stone where the rock is finest-grained and free of flint nodules.

Someone has to cut the walls.

The harvesters come down rattled and sparking from the arena, their muscles knotted, scraping quick, shorthand reports across their forearm-plates: *three bladders taken; the northeast stalk split halfway up its reed; the vent stuttered seven breaths instead of four before the big belch; the kite-flocks stayed high.* All of that is urgent, and all of it evaporates the moment the bone wand stops moving. 

I sit in the dark with a leather lap-apron to catch the white powder, an assortment of obsidian burins bound in sinew, and a small lump of glider-wax to keep the stone dust from floating into lungs. I don't carve pictures of the split tree or the high-flying kites. Pictures mean nothing to a thumb in the dark. What I carve is kerf, depth, and spacing. 

If you want a thumbnail dragged across limestone a hundred years from now to reproduce the exact anxious, syncopated *krr-tik-tik-krrrr* of a fumarole changing its multi-year rhythm, you have to calculate how fast an elder’s thick nail moves when her arm is sweeping from shoulder to hip. Cut the notches a hair too close together, and the future hears panic where there was only caution. Cut them too shallow, and three generations of inquiring fingers—hundreds of greased thumbs sliding down the wall in the dark to check whether the mountain has ever coughed like *this* before—will polish the history smooth and silent.

So the whole life is spent between two surfaces of the same white rock: up in the roaring peach-lit throat, adding hard paste to keep the wind’s voice from drifting out of true; down in the silent, static-lit marrow, subtracting tiny flakes of chalk so the dead can keep speaking through the fingernails of the living. 

Sometimes, in the quietest stretches when the fumarole drops into a long, low purr that warms the soles of the feet, one of the very small ones—still damp-eyed and frog-soft, years away from growing their horn-shields or tasting the open gale—crawls back into the crevice where I’m working. They aren't allowed near the upper shafts yet. They have never heard the canopy clatter like sheet metal or seen blue lightning jump thirty feet between swaying pillars.

I take their small, uncalloused hand, press their thumb into a groove cut six lifetimes before my own, and draw their arm steadily along the wall. *Ch-ch-ch-krrrrr-chk.* In the occasional purple flash from the workshop down the hall, I watch their pale pupils widen as the vibration climbs up through their wrist, into their radius, across their collarbone, and blooms inside their jaw—the exact shape of a wind they haven't met yet, waiting for them at the top of the dark.
