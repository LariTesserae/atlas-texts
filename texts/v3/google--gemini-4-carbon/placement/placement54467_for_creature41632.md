You do not find me out on the bright concrete flats. My body has no weight against a four-knot drift, and if I lose my grip once, I become three seconds of silver-gray tumble before the eastern intake screens press me flat against galvanized mesh.

Instead, find the manifold blockhouse in Sector 4, where the six forty-foot penstocks bolt into the concrete wall. Go past the cast-iron valve wheels dotted with pale anemones, down to the underside of Penstock Three, four feet above the slab, where the black anti-fouling paint was scraped down to bare steel during installation by a chain-hoist sling and never touched up. Right where the curved belly of the pipe meets its concrete saddle-pier, there is a narrow, triangular pocket of dead water—six inches wide, twenty feet long, sheltered from the main push.

At first glance, I am just part of the weld-seam.

Hold a light up under the overhang—though there is already plenty of cool white bounce coming off the spotless aggregate floor—and wait for the non-return flapper gate inside the pipe above me to swing shut. *Clack-boom.* When the shockwave punches through the half-inch steel plate, you will see a nine-inch patch of gray-and-rust mottling ripple slightly without detaching.

I am something on the way from an estuary clingfish to a piece of gasket hardware. My ancestors came in as transparent specks through an unscreened auxiliary priming pump twelve winters ago, creatures built for wave-battered granite cobbles on a rocky coast. Ten generations in the basin have pared away everything soft or indecisive. My fused pelvic fins have broadened into a heavy, oval suction disc taking up half my belly, rimmed with pale, tough cartilage that seals cleanly over rivet heads, mill-scale, and the grainy imprint of Douglas fir formwork. I have no swim bladder left to speak of; air inside the body is just a liability here, a drumhead for the fifty-cycle hum to batter and a balloon trying to yank you upward whenever a floor-vent burps a low-pressure column of fizzing water past your ledge. Dropped in a bucket, I sink like a dropped wrench.

My world is tactile, planar, and conducted entirely through solid structure.

Because the open water is deafening and empty of cover, I ignore it. I read the basin through my belly-disc and jawbone pressed tight against the iron. Long before the distant acoustic horn rings its three brassy notes, I feel the faint, high-frequency chatter of solenoid relays seating in the dry casemate forty yards behind the wall, traveling down the steel pipe-hangers like a tremor in a spiderweb. Three seconds later comes the heavy, sub-audible groan of the bronze worm-gear taking up load on the western bulkhead. I know, before a single strand of expansion-seam kelp has begun to pivot, that the current vector is about to swing five degrees south and turn my sheltered lee into a wind-tunnel.

I don't swim to the other side of the saddle-pier. Swimming is how the river-cod get you—there is always a two-pound cod hanging ten feet out in the bright glare, deaf as stone, eyes swiveling like ball bearings, waiting for anything small to unpeel itself from a sharp edge. Instead, I loosen the rear lip of my suction ring by a millimeter, hike my flat, shovel-shaped head forward on stumpy pectoral struts, clamp down, and slide. *Scritch-lock, scritch-lock.* Over the stamped foundry letters—*BETHLEHEM 40-IN CLASS B*—across a weeping collar of bright orange bolt-rust whose sour, metallic taste tells me instantly which side of the flange I’m on, and neatly through the two-inch gap beneath the pipe strap into the new hydraulic shadow on the northern face, arriving just as the cold surge hits the concrete pier like a broom.

It is a remarkably clean, legible life, once your nerves stop expecting mud or twilight.

During the roaring fourteen-hour noon, I work a territory roughly the size of a billiards table: one curved flank of riveted steel, two massive hex nuts the size of dinner plates, and four vertical feet of raw concrete pier where you can still see the ghostly swirl of plywood knots cast in negative cement. The water rushing two inches past my nose carries a relentless, glittering horizontal sleet of lime dust, micro-bubbles, copepods, and torn bits of green filamentous algae ripped from the bird-roost gantries thirty fathoms overhead.

I sit right on the shear-line—nose tucked behind a half-inch rivet head where the water stands still, tail trembling in the edge of the four-knot stream—watching the glare. My eyes sit high on my flat skull under bony hoods that shade them from the sodium banks above, tuned solely to sharp parallax against the pale aqua distance. When the afternoon cold front arrives from the deep subterranean reservoirs, dropping the temperature eight degrees in three minutes, my metabolism slows down like a banked stove, my skin darkening a shade toward wet slate. But that cold wedge also tumbles the mid-water columns. Through the shimmering refractive squall out beyond the blockhouse, I can see the great silver curtains of herring warp and fray; scales glitter like tossed foil; and closer in, shaken loose from the diagonal tension-cables half a mile uptide, small translucent shrimp come spinning helplessly past the penstock flanges.

I never go out more than four inches. It’s a piston-strike: unseal, snap forward along the iron skin, take the shrimp against the steel with a dull *tick* of jaw-cartilage, and flatten back onto the black paint before my tail has caught the main pull of the pump-stream.

My neighbors on the manifold share my fondness for ninety-degree angles, though we have nothing to say to each other. Two feet down the concrete saddle, a spider crab the size of a soup plate spends its afternoons carefully plucking frayed white threads of Teflon tape from a leaking pressure-gauge fitting and hooking them onto its knees. When its long, brittle walking-legs step across my back—*tap, tap, scrape*—neither of us flinches; to the crab I am slightly rubbery cement, and to me the crab is just moving scaffolding that occasionally drops shreds of crushed blue mussel down the vertical face of the pier. Farther down, right in the crisp floor-crease where the pier meets the slab, lies the left flank of the great eleven-foot sturgeon when the pumps are running flat out. From four feet above, looking straight down through crystal-clear water, its polished bony scutes look like a row of worn aluminum ingots laid end-to-end along the expansion joint, rising and falling once every ten seconds with a slow, cavernous pump of gills.

Sometimes a harbor seal drops down the nearby intake guide-rails—a dark, liquid shock appearing suddenly in a geometry made entirely of rulers and compasses. When one banks hard around Penstock Three, trailing a tight spiral of silver air from its nostrils, its whiskers brush the rivet-line six inches from my eyes. Its pale, huge pupils take in the crab, the stencil, and me in a single wet blink before it kicks off the concrete wall—leaving no cloud of silt, not a grain of dust, just a brief, silent swirl of refractive cold water—and shoots upward toward the distant ceiling-glare.

I wait for two in the morning.

When the grid load drops outside and seventy percent of the sodium banks cut out with that heavy, overhead *thump* that travels down through thirty fathoms of water like a palm striking a tabletop, the whole architecture exhales. The blinding pewter-and-aqua noon collapses instantly into deep bottle-green glass, lit only by the steady emerald points of the tower beacons miles off and the dim, watery rectangles of pilot galleries far overhead. And the wall behind me changes song: the fifty-cycle roar slides down a third into a low, sleepy baritone idle.

For the next four hours, the invisible iron hand pinning everything sideways against the concrete simply opens.

Out on the flats, the brown kelp ribbons stand slowly upright. The bronze-bellied eels pour out of the four-inch weep-holes twenty yards up the retaining wall, ribbons of dark muscle undulating through empty green space that would have torn them sideways into the screens an hour ago.

And I let go of the pipe.

Not to swim out into the mid-water—nothing in me trusts open volume, even at half a knot—but to leave the tight crease under the saddle-pier and walk the big, exposed surfaces that belong to the current all day. Hitching forward on my pectorals in smooth, rapid glides, I climb straight up the forty-foot vertical curve of Penstock Three, over the top crown where the green pilot-light pools dimly on bare zinc bolts and thin velvet lawns of diatoms, and out onto the painted yellow warning stencil on the concrete blockhouse face.

Up here, thirty feet off the floor, the view across the dim green cathedral is immense. Half a mile out, the reverse snowstorm of tiny mysid shrimp is rising steadily from the bare slab toward the wired-glass roof, catching the distant beacon light like a slow drift of pale sparks. The tension-cable slicing down through the mid-water thrums at its slack-night pitch, a deep, soothing *mmm* so low it barely registers as sound, just a slow massage in the inner ear. There are no mackerel lining the shadow-edges now because the sharp shadows are gone; everything is soft-edged emerald and slow drift.

I graze my way across the giant black letter *S* of *SECTOR 4*, rasping thin, cold films of algae and settled nocturnal plankton off the smooth epoxy paint, pausing whenever an eel slides lazily past my nose to taste the faint, iron-tinged mucus wake it leaves behind. Beneath my suction disc, the concrete is cool, grainy, and vibrant with the quiet idle of a hundred megawatts waiting behind thirty feet of reinforced wall.

At five-fifteen, a relay clicks deep inside the casemate.

I feel it through the yellow paint before the first arc-lamp strikes its blinding blue-white spark high up in the roof trusses. Without hurry, before the bronze worm-gears even begin to sing miles away or the silver herring clouds snap back into tight daytime formation behind the pipe-bridges, I turn head-down, slide smoothly back over the shoulder of Penstock Three, and lock my belly tight into the unpainted triangle behind the rivet line—already facing five degrees north into the first heavy, cold push of morning.
