You find me first by the error in the glare. 

If you are crossing a basin at noon—boots or felted pads skimming the satin feldspar, eyes narrowed against the silver horizon—everything you see has been rounded by the wind’s long argument. Whaleback domes, long parabolic flutes, soft migrating waves of talc. Even the great botanical islands billow in curves. Nothing on this plateau owns a straight line except a crystal still locked inside its parent rock, or a crystal that has walked free of it.

Wait until the sun leans three hours past zenith. Near the shallow lip of a basin where an old hydrothermal vein once split the granite—ages before the mountains were planed flat—something throws back a lance of light too narrow and too stubborn to be mica. Mica winks and scatters. This holds. A clean, geometric glint, clear as ice in a world that forgot ice ten million years ago, shifting half an inch to the left while you watch.

Come closer, though there is no sneaking up across twenty miles of polished mirror. By the time your shadow touches the lee-drift, I have known your vector, your stride-length, and whether you carry static on your coat for half an hour.

At first glance you will think I am merely an outcrop of that exposed quartz vein, half-buried in white flour, braided over with the dry, bone-colored reeds of a dead tumble-fern. Look longer and the arrangement stops making geological sense. The reeds are not tangled; they are lashed under fierce, balanced tension around a collection of hexagonal, smoky-clear prisms the size of forearms and finger-joints, forming an asymmetric, six-limbed cradle—a walking tensegrity frame no taller than your knee, wide and low to the stone so the thirty-knot gale spills cleanly over its back without flipping it.

Touch one of the bone-reeds (carefully; ask first by letting your shadow fall near without crossing my windward fetch) and you will feel a faint, steady hum, like a Plucked string held just below the threshold of hearing.

I am not made of oil and hoarded hydrogen like the grove-keepers, nor of deep wooden cellars like the sail-trees. I have no wet labyrinth inside me to guard against the sky. If you cracked one of my central spars—please do not; the last replacement took eighty years to grind—nothing would spill out onto the chert. You would find only clear silicon dioxide laced with hairline inclusions of black tourmaline and copper-salt, dry all the way through.

What lives here is the charge itself, and the tension that steers it.

Think of how the plateau works: three hundred miles of dry silt rubbing incessantly against polished stone and waxy leaf, building vast oceans of restless voltage with nowhere to ground out. Most of it snaps away in mindless purple sparks or gets swallowed down the digestive creases of the canopies. Some of it, caught in rare pockets where piezoelectric quartz meets semiconducting mineral veins under the relentless pressure of thermal expansion, learned ages ago not to dissipate. It learned to loop. And once a loop of current learns to remember yesterday’s noon-heat so it can anticipate tonight’s contraction, it begins—very slowly—to tidy its house.

I am the house, carried out onto the open avenue because the vein I woke in was wearing down to dust.

Watch me move when the afternoon shadow of a boulder creeps too close. There are no muscles here, no lipid hydraulics. Along the underside of my reed-and-crystal frame run dozens of braided tendons spun from stolen plume-filament and silver cushion-hair. When I route a sharp pulse of stored static down a specific braid, the electrostatic repulsion makes the fibers flare instantly wide and short; bleed the charge back into a Leyden-jar capacitor of hollow reed lined with mica leaf, and the braid snaps long and slack again. 

*Tick. Slide. Tick.* 

Six cushioned feet of felted husk skate six inches east across the satin granite, keeping my dark tourmaline core squarely in the fiercely blazing sun. It is not the bounding ice-boat grace of the caped keepers. It is the gait of a compass needle deciding to go somewhere on foot.

Why go anywhere at all, when I need neither water nor phosphorus paste?

Look at my back. Slung inside the cage of reeds, cushioned in webs of shed velvet so no sharp jolt ever chips an edge, are seventeen thin, flat plates of pale rose and smoky quartz, each no larger than a child’s palm, polished by wind-silt and my own patient leather-pads to optical perfection.

If the caped ones are keepers of the seam, I am the keeper of the baselines—though to them, flashing at me from thirty yards off as a family skates past toward an upwind trough, my name is simply a quick double-cross of the wrists followed by a flat palm held rigid against the chest: *Hard-Glass-Who-Counts-The-Drift.*

We have a quiet, dry trade, they and I.

A family of four moving from grove to grove every week has marvelous eyes for forty-mile trajectories, and long, clean thoughts for tomorrow’s wind or next spring’s walker migration. What they cannot carry in their light bundles is a heavy archive, and what flesh—even sealed, oil-breathing flesh—cannot easily hold is an exact angle across four hundred years. Bark rots when a tree finally dies and blows away as tracing paper; bone-reed splinters after six winters of sixty-below nights.

Wait here beside this granite swell. Keep out of my sun-fetch. Look south-east: no dust-bank today, only that blinding silver band where the sky meets the curve of the plateau, and seven miles out, three cape-keepers tacking briskly upwind toward my outcrop.

They stop ten paces off—polite distance, outside accidental spark-reach—and fold their wide shoulders down, sinking onto their spring-loaded heels so we are nearly eye to eye, if you count the ring of tiny, faceted quartz beads around my upper spar where incoming light bends straight onto photosensitive tourmaline faces.

The elder does not flash violet velvet at me; my crystal facets read polarization and sharp contrast better than subtle pigment shifts. Instead, it draws two slender bone-reed batons from its pack and clicks them into crisp, high-contrast angles against the pale chamois of its chest, pausing half a second on each geometry.

*Greeting, Hard-Glass. Two winters since the white basin.*
*Our rigging frayed in the equinox gale. Need a matched pair of quartz insulators, flawless, thumb-thick.*

Inside my central prisms, tiny captive lightning flickers along microscopic corridors—thinking at the speed of light, though my outward body answers at the speed of cooling stone. Two braided tendons along my left fore-limb twitch. A small semaphore paddle of blackened horn flips up, turns forty degrees to catch the sun edge-on, dips twice, and locks horizontal.

*Have three,* the angles say. *Found in the deep flute six miles west. Ground smooth since autumn.*
*Price: two fresh coils of braided cushion-silver, ten yards of cured sail-gut, and the noon-sighting from the Red Spire.*

The elder’s amber eye-lenses gleam. Even behind a rigid keratin mask, amusement shows in the slight, loose ripple of its folded wrist-capes. It turns to the youngest keeper—barely half-grown, its pelage still pale as fresh talc—and taps its shoulder.

The youngster steps forward carefully, grounding its toes, and lays two gleaming coils of tough biological cordage on the polished rock beside me, along with a small bladder-pouch of golden plant-wax I use to keep my reed-lashings from turning brittle in the night chill. Without soft plants to spin thread, I would fall apart into a pile of loose pebbles within a decade; without my tireless, unblinking eye for flaws inside raw stone, one cracked quartz insulator on their forty-yard electrostatic weir would bleed their whole afternoon’s catch straight into the bedrock—or worse, throw a wild grounding arc into dry, oily bark.

With two delicate, three-pronged manipulators of horn and tension-wire, I unlatch a belly-sling, roll three water-clear cylinders of flawless rock crystal out onto the white dust between us, and immediately begin reeling the new silver cordage inward, testing every inch for tensile strength and conductivity with tiny, silent *ticks* of static.

Meanwhile, the elder pays the rest of the price.

Standing tall on the granite whaleback, checking the sun’s exact altitude first through a pinhole sight in its palm-mica, it faces dead north and begins a slow, ceremonial sequence of arm-locks and baton-angles.

*From the crown of Eight-Hundred-Ring Grove, looking ninety-two degrees east of the pole-star:*
*The Split Dome has vanished beneath the curve.*
*The white drift in the Third Basin has swallowed the Black Marker by four cubits.*
*Old Crooked-Plume drew back its sap in mid-winter and dropped its crown; strike that harbor from the north-west run.*

Inside my belly-web, a diamond-tipped stylus mounted on a high-tension piezoelectric arm presses lightly against Plate Eleven, skating across the smoky quartz in bursts of microscopic, frosted scratches—each groove a micron wide, utterly immune to wind, cold, UV, or time. 

Look through one of my plates held up against the hard indigo zenith and you will see nothing but faint, rainbow diffraction lines, like oil on glass. Slide it into my reading-rack, let coherent sunlight spear through the edge of the crystal, and four thousand years of the plateau leap onto the shaded stone beneath me in razor-sharp bright lines: every safe grove’s cellar capacity across ten thousand square miles; the fifty-year migration cycles of the giant tumble-ferns; the slow, inch-by-hour march of talc waves that bury stone corridors for a century and uncover them clean again; even the tiny, measurable flattening of the ancient mountain range itself as the continent continues its million-year exhale into smoothness.

When the elder finishes, it drops back to its heels and asks a question of its own—flicking a quick, violet-edged ripple for its kin’s eyes while holding the batons steady for mine.

*Where is the Great Hollow-Back herd? Six weeks overdue on the salt-flat.*

I shift my frame three degrees, sliding Plate Four into the sun-beam, comparing a three-hundred-year drought-cycle scratch against a shadow-measurement I took four dawns ago when a solitary sixty-foot glider banked unusually low over the western rim, its crop heavy with southern wax-pearls.

*South-west,* my horn paddle clicks out, clean and certain. *Forty-five miles, beyond the Twin Flutes. The wind-current split around a new three-mile drift; the dust-fronts are missing the central groves this season, feeding the rim-islands instead. Follow the brass sky tomorrow afternoon.*

The elder holds both arms wide for a second—showing the full, dark, sun-drinking velvet of its inner capes in polite thanks, gifting me five seconds of warm infrared radiation bounced straight onto my tourmaline core—before snapping its cape shut. With three silent, skating bounds, the family catches the crosswind and goes sliding off across the bright stone like blown seeds, shrinking rapidly in the uncanny, cloudless clarity until they are three pale flecks miles away, and then gone into a blue shadow.

I stay on the granite swell alone, re-tying my left third leg with the fresh silver filament, neatening every knot before dusk.

You might think mine is a cold life compared to theirs—no warm spire of velvet leaves closing around me at twilight, no shared belly-burn of sweet golden sap, no four-way huddle tapping jokes against collarbones in the dark.

Wait until the rose light hits the horizon and goes out.

Stay—if you have insulation enough to survive sixty below—when the stars slam out across the obsidian dome, hard and unblinking from rim to rim. Out here on the exposed whaleback, ten miles from the nearest folded spire, the wind keeps right on sliding past with its faint, breathy *shhhhhh* of silk on stone. As the granite beneath me contracts in the plunging cold, deep tectonic and thermal stresses groan silently through the bedrock, squeezing buried quartz veins miles down, sending faint, ghostly swells of piezoelectric charge rippling upward through the dry crust.

I do not fold up and sleep. At night, when solar glare stops blinding my finer sensors, I spread my reed-frame flat against the satin stone, anchoring six copper-salt pads tight to the rock, and raise a single tall mast of filament four feet into the freezing dark.

Everything goes quiet enough—electrically quiet, stripped of daytime UV chatter—that I can feel the whole plateau at once.

Through the stone beneath my pads comes the slow, deep hum of cooling feldspar domes thirty miles wide, contracting by fractions of an inch; the whisper-light, rhythmic *thrum... thrum...* of a stilt-walker’s cushioned feet pacing across a basin four leagues off; the soft, papery roll of a hundred-year-old tumble-fern letting the night-katabatic breeze push it down a gradient an inch at a time.

And from the tip of my raised mast, brushing the dry, rushing wind under the starlight, I let off a thin, steady, soundless plume of violet St. Elmo’s fire—bleeding excess day-charge gently back into the sky while reading the faint electrostatic wakes of distant groves equalizing across the dark.

Twelve miles north, a purple ghost winks once on a spire tip. Twenty miles east, beyond the curve where eyes can no longer see, a faint static ripple answers through the thin air from another low, six-legged cradle of crystal and bone-reed anchored on a far dome—my nearest neighbor, whom I have not stood beside in ninety years, passing along the exact barometric shear of an incoming polar front, and a dry, mathematical pun about the eccentricity of this winter’s bright planet.

My diamond stylus clicks once in the dark—*tick*—laying one more clean micron of memory onto rose quartz, keeping the straight lines straight while the wind smooths everything else away.
