I would not be in the bright bowl. The glare there is an interrogation, a finished sentence with nowhere left to stand inside it. Everything in that tallow-lit drydock has already been solved by light and patience, pared down to a clockwork of forty shells taken, forty shells left, a thumb-groove deepened by the thickness of a nail. 

To find me, you have to follow those fifty cured mirror-shells after they leave the chamber in her bladder-skin bundle. You have to go east through the hot crystal pinch where she crawls with her ochre-banded cheek an inch from the burning roof, and then keep going until the geology tilts, until the gypsum lens thickens and dives beneath heavier beds of grey, iron-banded limestone that the surface sun cannot pierce. 

Where the endless, shadowless milk finally bruises into slate, and then into violet, and then into a dark so complete it feels cool against the eyeballs like wet linen—that is where the warrens open out, and where the air begins to move again. Not much; just the slow, subterranean lung-draft of deep karst breathing against the surface pressure. Here, sound gets its bones back. A dropped pebble actually rings. A throat clearing at the far end of a gallery arrives with the shape of the room still clinging to it.

At first, looking into that dark, I think I am the thing that receives the shells—an older pair of hands waiting on a ledge worn shiny as mutton-fat by ten generaciones of sitters, someone whose knees and clavicles have stiffened too much with mineral deposits to manage the eastern crawl anymore. 

Yes. Start with the hands, because in the deep galleries you know people by their hands and the cadence of their scraping before you ever waste tallow-light to see their faces. 

My hip went bad twelve seasons back—not broken, just fused slow and chalky from decades of drinking the bitter Epsom seep, until the left leg wouldn't fold tight enough to clear the Dog’s Throat three miles up-pipe. In a world of vertical cliffs, a stiff hip makes you dead weight; in a world of horizontal squeezes, it makes you a plug. Stop in the wrong seam and nobody behind or ahead of you gets out until you rot thin enough to drag over. We know when our last crawl is coming. You feel the marl-grit grating inside the joint-capsule for a year, and one day you come feet-first out of the transitional dusk-rooms, lay your bladder-skin down on the sorting shelf, and simply don’t go back up into the white.

So I stay where the ceilings are high enough to stand upright—though none of us quite do; we walk with that permanent, tucked question-mark in the spine even under forty feet of black limestone vault. Down here, my eyes have finally let go of their cramp. The heavy brow-shelf is still there, thick and scarred from youthful misjudgments against low rock, and the third-lid still sweeps sideways out of habit whenever someone shakes out a dusty hide, ticking across the eyeball with a tiny, wet *snip*. But the poppy-seed pupils have let go. They stay wide, drinking the faint, greasy orange bloom of a single marrow-lamp set in a wall-niche twenty paces off.

What do you do with mirror-shells brought down at great trouble from a blinding room into a place that has no light?

You steer what little you have.

Spread across my lap is a stiff apron of cured badger-hide—surface trade, or surface scavenging from the night-crews who go up through the sinkholes when the plain is moonless and cold—and on the stone beside me sits a bowl of warm, stinking mastic cooked down from shrub-resin and marl-flour. When the small woman slides into the gallery, smelling sharply of dry electricity, baked dust, and that unmistakable faint tang of chewed iodine on her breath, neither of us speaks for a quarter-hour. Speech is easier here than in the bright-bowl, where the dome smashes a word flat against your teeth, yet we keep the quiet anyway. Up there, noise feels vulgar; down here, too much talk makes the echoes crowd the gallery like strangers standing behind your shoulder.

She unrolls the bladder-skin. Fifty concave discs, coin-wide, cured for half a year on the gypsum slab until the trace chitin-oils have polymerized hard as flint, their backs dull horn, their fronts bright as pooled mercury. She sets the little fiber pouch of two dried geckos beside my knee—those aren't for me; those go down-ledge to the salt-boilers whose kids have the winter rattles—and then she touches two fingers to the top of my scarred left foot in greeting, notes that my water-gourd is half full, and goes off toward the sleeping clefts to scrape the white crawl-grease off her ribs before it clogs her pores in the damper dark.

I pick up the first shell. Even in the dim tallow-reach, turning it between thumb and forefinger sends a sudden, needle-sharp mote of orange skittering across the wet black limestone opposite me.

This is my work. I am a light-bender. 

Not a priest—we don't have the spare calories for mystery—just an old mechanic of weak radiance. Around me lean shallow wooden dishes carved from driftwood pulled out of subterranean flood-sumps, and curved ribs of giant fossil-sloths dug out of the deep clay beds lower down. Into the black resin lining a shallow dish, I press the mirrored carapaces one by one, overlapping their edges like fish-scales, angling each concave face by eye and thumb-pressure so their focal points converge three yards out.

When twenty shells are set and the resin cools hard, you can hold a single sputtering twist of moss-wick soaked in marmot fat in front of that dish, and instead of losing ninety parts of its feeble yellow breath to the hungry black ceiling, it throws a tight, clean shaft of borrowed daylight straight down a three-hundred-foot fault-line. We hang larger arrays—hundreds of shells mortared directly onto clay bosses at the bends of the main arterial tunnels—so that down from the twilight sinkholes where real sun enters for two hours at noon, a relay of beetle-tin catches that shaft and bounces it, corner after corner, six hundred yards into the cold gut of the mountain where the pale root-tubers grow in bat-guano beds and the blind white crayfish sluggishly patrol the sumps.

I live at the hinge between the glare that kills you by drying you out and the dark that kills you by breaking your neck.

Wait, though. Sitting with the resin cooling under my thumbnails, turning a single shell so its mercury face catches my own dim reflection—just the wet gleam of a wide, black iris under a heavy shelf of bone, and the pale ghost of that sideways membrane flicking across it—something doesn't quite sit whole in being *only* the old man on the ledge. 

Look closer at the array mortared onto the wet limestone bend fifty yards up the tunnel, where the noon-shaft comes down from the sinkhole.

For twenty-two hours of the day, that boss of clay studded with two hundred dead beetle-backs is just cold mosaic in the dark, sweating faint condensation, indistinguishable from the rough rock around it except that cave-crickets won't walk across its slickness. Then, up on the plain, the sun crosses the lip of a dry limestone chimney. A raw, hot, dusty blade of surface light drops eighty feet straight onto the first mirror-boss. 

Instantly—no warning, just *there*—the array wakes up and flings a cool, silvered, Compound beam sideways down the black gallery. The beam isn't whole anymore; because it is built out of two hundred tiny, coin-sized concavities set by slightly arthritic human thumbs, it travels down the wet stone hallway as a braided rope of overlapping moons, shivering faintly whenever a draft moves a cobweb up in the chimney. Where it strikes the far turn, a second boss catches half of it and throws it deeper still, dimmer now, greenish-white like drowned bone, just bright enough that a child picking white grubs out of the guano-beds doesn't need to burn precious grease to see her own fingers.

I think I am less the old man alone, and more the pact between his thumb and that beam. 

No—stay inside the skin. A pact doesn't have a fused hip that aches when the surface barometer drops before a plains thunderstorm. Stay in the body on the mutton-fat ledge, because the body knows things about the bright-bowl that even the crawler who visits it every six months has stopped noticing.

I remember that bowl. Thirty years ago my own narrower footprints were the ones sitting timeless in its talc. I remember the pressurized silence that pushes against your eardrums like warm water, and how, if you lay flat on your back between the waxy horsetail stalks for ten minutes to rest your cramped neck before the crawl home, looking straight up into the vast, milky dome of fibrous gypsum, a terrifying dizziness would take hold of you. Because there is no horizon in a shadowless sphere, and no texture in diffused glare, your inner ear loses its bargain with your eyes. Up stops being a ceiling and becomes an infinite white drop, and you clutch two handfuls of dry marl-silt at your hips to keep from falling upward into the hot, humming tallow of the sky.

Down here on my ledge, I never lose the floor. Gravity is honest in the dark. 

Beside my resin-pot sits a small, flat cake of that six-month calcite skin skimmed off the bitter basins—she brought me two pieces wrapped in dry moss, thin and brittle as mica. While the resin stiffens around a fresh ring of shells, I break off a corner of the crystal crust, grind it on a wet chert block with the heel of my palm—*shhh-k, shhh-k*, a crisp sound that travels twenty feet down the black passage and comes back slightly rounder—until it turns into a milk-white slurry. With a scrap of soft belly-leather, I rub that slurry in tiny circles over an older mirror-array brought in from the wet sumps, buffing away the green tarnish of cave-damp and spider-droppings until the hammered tin flashes clean again.

It is strange to think about: up in that drydock beneath the plain, the beetles grow mirrors on their backs to throw away a sun they cannot endure, grazing in clock-hand silence so they won't cook in their own shells. And down here in the wet limestone gut, we harvest their refusals, glue them into wooden bowls with stink-gum, and ration that thrown-away sun around dark corners by the spoonful so our children won't grow up completely blind like the bone-white geckos.

From down the sleeping-cleft comes the faint, hollow *clack* of a wooden gourd setting down on stone, and the dry rasp of a chert scraper cleaning tallow-paste off skin—*skrit, skrit*—each stroke measured, unhurried. Soon someone younger will bring up a clay pipkin of warm, mineral-heavy broth boiled over dung-coals down near the breathing-chimney, smelling of cave-crayfish and dried surface roots. 

Before they come, I lift the finished badger-wood dish in both stiff hands and tilt it toward the solitary marrow-lamp in its niche twenty paces away. 

My thumb shifts a single loose carapace at the rim by half a millimeter, pressing it deeper into the cooling black gum until its stray glint snaps inward to join the rest. Across the cavern, high up on a wet curtain of grey flowstone that hasn't seen light since the sea drained off this plain ten million years ago, a tight, shimmering spot of pale gold suddenly gathers itself out of nothing—bright enough to show red iron streaks in the rock, bright enough to make two long-legged cave-spiders freeze and tuck their knees—holding steady in the black air for three heartbeats until I lower the dish to my lap and let the dark take the wall back.
