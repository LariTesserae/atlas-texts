At the bottom of the west facade, twelve feet above the shrieking gravel-sound of the intakes, there is a submerged instrument housing bolted to what used to be a cobbler’s lintel. Inside a thick brass cylinder with a bevel-edged bullseye lens, a Bourdon-tube pressure gauge sits dry behind double lead gaskets, lit by a tiny, pale-yellow pilot bulb. 

I am not the gauge, though I am close enough to read its trembling needle if I had eyes. I am the junction box three feet to its left, and the armored conduit rising from it, and—more specifically—the hairline fault in the lead-antimony sheath just below the second-story bronze cleat, where forty years of acoustic battering finally fatigued the metal along a crystalline seam.

Not a living thing, at first. Or rather, a threshold where two dead regimes are forced to negotiate. 

Inside the sheath: braided cotton insulation gone brittle and brown with dry heat, copper bus-wires humming at sixty cycles per second, feeding the dim green-glass cage lights and the lower gauge-lamps. Outside the sheath: ninety-eight-degree water shearing past at twenty feet per second, saturated with dissolved iron, trace copper from the condenser tubes, and the relentless, bone-deep hammer of the turbines.

When the crack first opened—a split narrower than a razor's edge, tucked safely into the dead-water lee between the cable and the bread-loaf brick—the water did not rush in and blow the circuit. The pressure inside the old conduit was high too, packed with hardened dielectric tar that had melted and slumped over decades of boiler blow-downs. Instead, the hot water met the weeping tar and the bare, vibrating copper in a narrow, stagnant pocket half an inch deep.

That is where I began: as an electrolytic itch.

A stray trickle of low-voltage current bled out from a frayed cloth wrap into the mineral-rich water trapped behind the cable. Immediately, the dissolved metals in the condenser run-off began to plate out and strip back, over and over, driven by the alternating current and the fluctuating stray voltages of the turbine hall forty yards through the rock. Copper ions migrated toward the brick; iron oxides precipitated into microscopic, branching ferns along the lip of the lead crack; sulfur from the old pipe-dope bound with the tarnish to form a dark, semi-conducting crust of chalcocite and bornite. 

For ten years I was only a mineral callus—a brittle, metallic scab growing slowly out of the split sheath, bridging the one-inch gap between the armored cable and the calcified mortar groove behind it. 

Then the pale grease found me.

The thermophilic bacteria that coat the dead-water alleys behind the pipes do not care about light, and they barely care about oxygen; they eat dissolved iron, sulfur, and heat. To them, a warm, weeping seam of stray electrical current and freshly precipitated metal sulfides was a banquet. They colonized my crystalline branches, weaving tough, mucilaginous filaments through the porous copper-iron crust, thickening it, insulating the outer layers against the scouring eddies while keeping the inner core wet and conductive. 

Now I am neither strictly mineral nor strictly slime. I am a three-foot-long, thumb-thick vein of living solder wedged into the crease behind the easternmost conduit, extending slowly upward toward the third-story cornice at the rate of half an inch a year.

To exist here is to be an instrument played by two incompatible rhythms. 

Through my back—where my metallic roots bite into the copper wire—I feel the clean, mathematical certainty of the municipal grid: a steady, sixty-hertz buzz that tightens and slackens subtly when streetcars climb the hills far above the sealed foundations, or when the harbor cranes haul cargo at dusk. When the upper city wakes, the copper warms by three degrees, the voltage sags a fraction of a volt, and the green-glass filaments in the window niches dim from cooling ember to bruised plum. 

Through my skin—the slick, velvety mat of metallophilic bacteria trembling at the edge of the shear-plane—I feel the chaotic, hydraulic violence of the lung. The solid roar of the centrifuge presses against my flanks. When an upstream boiler blows down, the sudden ten-degree spike washes into my dead-water crease, accelerating every chemical exchange across my crust until I fizz faintly with microscopic hydrogen bubbles.

The thumbnail crustaceans walk over me constantly. Their crampon-hooks click against my harder mineral nodules and sink slightly into my softer, living rind as they graze the bacterial nap. They never strip me bare; where their mouths scrape away the outer velvet, fresh current leaks into the water for a millisecond, electro-plating a microscopic layer of dark copper-iron salt over the wound before the bacteria close back over it by nightfall. In return, bits of their shed, translucent shells become cemented into my matrix—tiny plates of chitin mineralized with iron until my lower inches resemble fossiliferous slate.

And then there is the heavy one. The four-foot female with the tie-rod scar.

She knows me well, though to her lateral line I must register as an oddity—a strip of mortar that hums at sixty cycles and tastes faintly of battery acid and pennies. Twice a day, when she slides off her warm brass thermometer housing on the third floor and inches sideways across the rounded brickwork toward my conduit, I know she is coming long before she touches me. 

Her approach changes the pressure signature of the wall. As forty pounds of dense, rubbery muscle pours over the window lintel, the micro-eddies behind my cable stutter and reverse. Then comes the shadow—not a visual shadow, for the amber gloom barely reaches behind the conduit anyway, but an *acoustic* shadow. When her thick, cartilaginous body passes directly between my seam and the central vortex, the high-frequency tearing hiss of cavitation suddenly muffles, replaced by the dull, wet friction of her suction-girdle breaking and slamming shut against the brick courses.

When she reaches the level of my upper tip, she stops. She plants her snout right at the lip of my gap, arches her heavy shoulder two inches into the screaming current, and turns herself into a chisel of deflected water.

For five seconds, my quiet world tears apart. The scalding jet knives straight down my alley. My outer ribbons of pale grease rip loose; four or five crustaceans lose their crampon-hold on my ridges and go spinning downward into the dark vault of her waiting belly. The sudden blast of oxygenated water strips the boundary layer off my metallic core, sending a sharp, galvanic spike shivering all the way down my spine into the junction box—enough to make the little yellow pilot bulb behind the pressure-gauge glass flicker once, twice, like a nervous eye.

Then she drops her skirt flat. 

For half a minute, she lies pinned directly over my conduit, sealing me entirely inside the dark, quiet cave of her underside. It is the only stillness this room ever manufactures. Beneath the arch of her belly, the roar of the centrifuge drops away to a distant, muffled thunder. Her horny, concentric mouth-plates rasp softly inches from my crust, sorting chitin from mortar grit. Occasionally, the outer edge of her suction-skirt presses directly across my exposed seam. When it does, the stray trickle of my sixty-hertz current passes harmlessly into her wet, copper-smelling hide. 

She doesn't flinch. In fact, sometimes after she has finished swallowing and spitting the grit through her low gill-slits, she lingers there for ten minutes longer—pressing the pale, ancient scar on her flank directly against my warmest, most conductive node, letting the faint, rhythmic tingle of the city's electricity buzz through her cartilage while the hot water hammers her back. Then she humps her spine, breaks the vacuum with a soft, muffled *pop* I feel right down to my copper root, and glaciers slowly back toward her brass housing.

When the twice-weekly stutter hits—when the turbines drop out of phase and the whole fifty-foot column of water slams its brakes on in that sickening, silent-seeming concussion—I do not let go. I cannot. I am the seam that holds the slack. While the pale eel tumbles through the amber streaks in the middle of the room, and the smaller males hurl themselves off the fourth-story street numbers to collide in the stalled foam, I simply drink the pressure-drop. Tiny pockets of trapped gas inside my porous mineral lattice expand and groan; a hairline flake of chalcocite snaps off my tip and vanishes into the restarting spin.

And once—perhaps three cycles of the mysterious spawning season ago—when the centrifuge snapped shut again and hurled everything outward against the facades, something struck the crease beside my upper inch and stayed.

Not a crustacean. A microscopic, sticky bead, driven hard into the junction where my mineralized bacterial crust meets the bronze cleat. My outer filaments grew halfway around its tough protein glue before it hatched, anchoring it against the twenty-foot-per-second shear just millimeters from my warm, weeping current. 

For months it was no bigger than a lentil, translucent as mica, clamped inside the tiny eddy formed by my own raised ridge, rasping away at the rich, iron-fed velvet growing along my flank. It grew fast in the ninety-eight-degree dark and the galvanic warmth. By the time it was broad as a watch-face and dense enough to crawl out from behind the cable onto the open, rounded brick of the second-story facade, its underside bore a faint, dark-greenish stain from the copper salts it had grazed off my skin—a mark it will carry under its belly for the next fifty years, long after my lead sheath finally splits wide open, shorts the lower lamp-circuit in a muffled underwater flash of blue steam, and goes cold inside the roar.
