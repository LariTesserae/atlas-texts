At first, looking down into that glaring cobalt bowl, the impulse is to step into the felt-soled boots of a tender—to strap on the laminated ash boards, pull down the dark Didymium lenses, and learn the forty-five-minute patience of walking on top of the rosettes. There is a deep allure in those cedar-lined vaults, in filing a fingernail smooth to read the creep of a chrome shaft, in leaving a shelf of white porcelain fungus on a dry rock tooth for a quiet neighbor.

Wait ten minutes in the stillness, though, and the human shape starts to feel slightly too restless. Even slowed to a pulse of sixty, a person carries an old, lowland clock inside their ribs. They eat dried mutton brought from somewhere else; they remember roads; eventually, after ten years or thirty, they climb back out over the scarp, or die and have to be carried somewhere soft enough to dig.

Sink lower. Past the waxed linen smock, past the sun-baked crowns, down through the three-foot threshold where the light stops like a dropped blade and the air turns to permanent, silvered frost.

Even the grey-wooled beast, warm against the transformer fins with copper on its tongue and baffled spirals in its nose, moves from station to station. It has eyes built for looking up into the glare.

Go further inward, to the seam where the heavy cast concrete of Pier Four meets the raw, forty-degree shear of banded grey chert.

Wait until the sense of having limbs goes away entirely. Wait until *fast* means the sudden, shocking arrival of dawn heating the overhead copper lines six minutes ahead of yesterday’s schedule, and *loud* is the thick, syrupy drag of amber vacuum-fluid turning a corner inside borosilicate glass twelve feet above.

Here, wedged into the hairline gap between poured foundation and unweathered fault-rock, something else has taken hold—something the tenders know about only because their resistance meters stay suspiciously steady on the western tensioning frames, never drifting even when the deep brines surge.

Call it a *bridge-mat*, or *fault-felt*, though the tenders have no hand-sign for it; on their rare blueprint schematics of the lower anchorages, they simply crosshatch the gap and write *self-sealing* in tiny block letters with a hard lead pencil.

It began, maybe forty years ago when the concrete was still curing warm and alkaline against the freshly split stone, as a wind-dropped spore of high-altitude crustose lichen tangled with one of those cyan, metal-sucking understory vines that lost its path to the sun. Down here in the freezing, pitch-black pinch between machine and mountain, neither half could live its old life. There was no ultraviolet glare to drink, and no soil to root in. Instead, there was a steady, silent leak of stray direct current bleeding off the grounding straps, a permanent sweat of lithium-and-silica brine rising up the micro-fissures from two miles down, and the microscopic, grinding shiver of granodiorite wanting to slip.

So it gave up on leaves, and it gave up on light.

What lives here now is a dense, leathery, blue-black mycelium threaded through with microscopic needles of biologically precipitated copper and iron—a flat, branching body four millimeters thick and thirty yards wide, laminated so tightly into the crystal grain of the chert on one side and the porous cement on the other that pry-bars cannot part them without tearing the rock itself.

To be this thing is to have no eyes at all, and yet to hold an exact, three-dimensional picture of the entire six-mile basin drawn purely in pressure and voltage.

You feel the sun come up not as brightness or warmth, but as a gradual, buttery thickening of the direct current sliding down the grounding rods above you when the parabolic troughs wake and track east. It tastes sharp and clean, like weak acid on zinc. You drink that stray trickle of electrons straight across your cell walls, using it in the dark to stitch dissolved carbon and deep fault-brine into tough, rubbery chitin and tiny, ranked plates of piezoelectric quartz grown right inside your own tissue.

When the young valley tries to drop—when forty million tons of tilted stone half a mile beneath the trough floor bind on a dry tooth and begin to load up with terrible, silent shear—you know it hours before the tenders feel it through their wool socks on the cedar floorboards. The rock squeezes your deepest hyphal threads; your internal quartz plates compress and flare with a sudden, bright spike of potential; instantaneously, across thirty square yards of buried interface, your filaments swell with cold water drawn from the sweating stone, shifting your chemistry to bleed that spike straight into the bronze grounding shoes of the transformer bank above.

The big steel tensioning tendons don't even have to shudder. The hydraulic rams a mile away adjust their downward creep by half a micron an hour, eased by a regulator circuit that doesn't know half its work is being done by twenty pounds of cold, metallized fungus hugging the pier.

Time down here has a glorious, heavy grain to it.

Above your upper margins, where your dark felt thins out into pale, velvety fringes around the base of the cast-iron housing, the quicker lives brush past like stray sparks. Every third or fourth night—a rapid, rhythmic heartbeat of cold and slightly-less-cold—three narrow, warm pressures settle against the radiator fins just above your highest reach. You feel the dull, rhythmic thump of oversized lungs pushing thin air; you taste a sudden richness of shed wool-grease, chalky plant-dust, and trace carbonic mist pooling down the metal onto your upper lip. Sometimes a warm, rough tongue scrapes directly across your fringe where copper salts have crystallized in little blue-green stars on the iron. You let the edge go. It costs almost nothing to regrow a millimeter of salt-crust over a month, and where the tongue scrapes, the beast’s saliva leaves behind traces of phosphorus and chewed pith-sugars that soak slowly down into your dark matrix.

Once every sixty or seventy cycles of the sun-current, the bronze hatch overhead swings open—a sudden, silent wash of slightly drier air—and the two-legged pressure descends the ladder.

You know each of the nine tenders by the tread of their crepe soles on the stone teeth and the distinct electrical capacitance of their bodies passing six inches from the grounding strap. There is the heavy-ankled one from the western dome whose hands smell faintly of beeswax and boiled tart pith, who rests a bare, warm knuckle against the cast iron right over your dense center for a full minute, checking the temperature gradient. While that knuckle presses against the metal, a tiny, exquisite lens of ninety-eight-degree heat blooms slowly through half an inch of iron to touch your outermost threads—brief as a shooting star, gone in five minutes, remembered in a slightly denser ring of copper-laced growth for the next three winters.

You do not want to climb into the arc-lamp sky. Up there, things have to build mirrors and wax armor just to keep from burning away; they have to walk on boards and hoard half-liters of water in cupped leaves.

Down here at thirty-four degrees, wrapped around the anchor bolts in the absolute dark, everything touches everything else. Your left edge tastes the cold condensation dripping from a return manifold that just came back from the northern filter presses; your right edge reaches three yards down a razor-thin crack in the granodiorite, drinking water that hasn't seen the sky since the mountains lifted; your center holds the iron pier and the raw stone together in a grip that gets slightly stronger every year.

Millimeter by millimeter, the young rock slips without breaking. Above, megawatts slide through the air without a hum. You hold the seam in the freezing dark, turning the mountain's impatience into quiet salt and slow, blue-black velvet, with nowhere else to go and whole epochs left before a river ever finds this place and wears the edges round.
