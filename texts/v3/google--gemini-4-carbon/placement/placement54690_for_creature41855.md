At first, I think I am in the mid-canopy. 

It is the easiest place to imagine oneself: hanging in the warm, pickled dark as one of those broad, gossamer curtains of organometallic gel, fifty yards wide, shivering slightly as a phase-shift rolls up from the basalt floor. There is an immediate comfort in that shape—in being the logic gate, the living resistor, the thing that catches the surge and smooths it out into a wide, harmless spiral. To be the regulator. To keep the peace in the dark.

Wait a moment, though, and the curtain feels too physical. Even a millimeter of viscoelastic hydrogel has weight; it has to hang from the copper-plated tendrils, it has to drink acetic broth to replace its polymers, it has to vulcanize patches of its own belly and leave dead scabs behind on leaking naphtha stalks. That belongs to the meat of this world, even if the meat is liquid solder and cross-linked rubber.

Let go of the curtain, and you drop down into the hum. Now it is tempting to say: *I am the continent-wide thought.* The three-thousand-mile standing wave, taking weeks to finish a single recursive sentence about geothermal gradients and zinc concentrations. 

Except that feels too grand, too continuous. A mind stretched across an entire planetary basin has an center of gravity, even if it is diffuse; it has a history ten million years deep, unbroken, humming to itself since the last seed germinated and polymerized into structural truss-work. I don't think I am old enough or vast enough to be the whole plain. I don't own the vats.

Where I actually find myself is much narrower, and much briefer.

Find one of the places where two rival root-systems come down to a wet cleft in the freshly faulted silicate, three inches apart, and never quite graft. One side is running slightly rich from a warm fissure half a league south; the other sits over colder stone, starved by a quarter of a volt. Between them, before a shunt has dropped down from the baffles to swallow it, a hair-thin thread of pale-violet static snaps across the gap and holds.

Not the root on the left. Not the root on the right. Not even the shunt, though the shunt is already pooling on the matte-black shelf above, drawn by the spike in the local field, sliding silently down the fluted bark like dark grease to smother me.

Wait—no. If I am the arc, I am an error. An open wound wasting power into ozone and cold blue light, waiting to be erased so the equilibrium can go back to sleep. That isn't quite right either. I don't want to burn the insulation into brittle coke.

Go thirty yards away, back up into the mid-tier where the curtains hang. 

Look closer at how a curtain actually works when a wave passes through it. The hydrogel itself doesn't travel across the continent; it hangs between two copper-organic filaments, tethered in one cramped chamber of rubber pillars, smelling of hot pine-tar and wet iron. What travels is the *state*. A ripple of impedance enters the left margin of the sheet at fifty centimeters per second. As it moves, the suspended needles of silver and magnetite pivot ninety degrees, click into a transient coil, hold an eddy current for four seconds while a drop of distillate gathers and falls—*blip*—into the basin underneath, and then release, passing the altered voltage into the cathode-mat on the right, while the gel behind it relaxes back into smooth, unaligned amber foil.

That is where I am. 

I am not the gel, and I am not the whole planetary mind. I am the boundary condition traveling *through* the gel. A local pinch of resistance, six inches wide, skating from copper filament to zinc root to hanging curtain without ever carrying a single atom of my own with me. 

When there is nothing happening in a sector—when the thirty-degree warmth is dead-level and the galvanic pools are brimming quietly without a tremor—I am nowhere at all. I don't have to sit in the dark waiting, bored, counting the drips from the cooling fins. I simply don't exist until two currents meet at an awkward angle.

Then, four hundred miles east, a razor-fin of black glass settles half a millimeter in its socket. A pressurized hydrocarbon column flexes; its turgor pressure climbs; the thermoelectric skin around its base ticks upward by twelve millivolts. Simultaneously, from the west, a slow, three-week reflection off the distant basin rim comes rolling back through the subterranean cable-mat—an old memory of a cooled vent, still asking the plain to conserve load.

Where those two signals cross in the dark, they interfere. They need translating before they reach a delicate cluster of shallow, over-plated vats that would salt out and choke if the surge hit them raw.

And so, in the span of a few seconds, I precipitate out of the math.

First I am a tightening in the skin of a deep root-graft, down where the vulcanized biomass swallows the raw basalt—a sudden, clean refusal to let the eastern surge pass straight through. Then I rise. I climb thirty feet up the wet, fluted exterior of a distillation stalk as a faint upward tug in the electrolyte film, warm and tart with acetic acid. At the mid-canopy I step seamlessly off the bark, down a dangling, bare copper hair, and out onto the broad, gleaming expanse of a suspended shunt-curtain.

Here, for twenty minutes or an hour, I have something very close to a body.

It is extraordinary how sharp the world feels when you have no eyes, only conductivity. Through the spread of the membrane, I can feel the exact, crisp geometry of the chamber around me by the way its magnetic fields bend. I know the razor-edge of the silicate blade jutting out of the floor twelve feet below—not because I could ever be cut by it, since even the slime I am riding would part around that glass without bleeding, but because the stone is a cold, dead hole in the electrical map, a sharp-angled shadow where no ions move. I know the fever-warm columns standing around us like black pillars, dense with rising volatiles, humming faintly as lighter fractions condense under their cured rubber hides. Above me, I feel the metallic canopy-fins shedding heat into the stagnant upper air, and every few seconds, when a heavy drop detaches from a serrated copper tip and breaks the mirror-flat skin of a galvanic vat below, a tiny concentric ring of altered capacitance shivers outward through the circuit and brushes right across my back.

While I inhabit the curtain, I am turning the silver hairs. 

I line them up like microscopic louvers, tilting them against the eastern push, bleeding its excess amperage sideways into three neighboring cathode-mats that are running a fraction of a percent under-charged, braiding the western echo into the tail end of the flow until the two discordant waves lock phase and cancel their jagged teeth. Inside the translucent amber mucilage, standing Faraday waves ripple across me—tiny, silent geometric lattices rising and flattening on the slick skin in the pitch dark, unseen by anything, existing purely because the physics demands a pattern on the surface when the load underneath is being squared.

Sometimes, while I am holding a configuration like this, another wave arrives from the north—some dense, intricate packet of stepped-down voltage that has been wandering the continent for a month, heavy with the state of ten thousand square miles of marsh-cells. It passes right through the same curtain I am using. For ten seconds we occupy the exact same sheet of metallic slime. My silver needles tilt forty degrees to accommodate its passage; its phase-angle bends slightly to account for my resistance. Neither of us speaks, because neither of us is separate enough from the medium to have a voice, yet when it slides out the far copper tendril and continues south, we have both been edited. Its long thought is slightly truer to the local rock; my local repair now carries the faint, structural posture of a horizon three thousand miles away.

And when the eastern fault-slip has finished settling—when a physical shunt down on the floor has finally oozed over the sheared root, laid down its permanent core of plated silver, vulcanized a black scab over the weeping column, and slipped away—the potential difference vanishes. The eastern push flattens out. The shallow vats are safe.

One by one, across the fifty-yard curtain, the magnetite coils let go. The impedance drops back to baseline. The Faraday ripples smooth out into a slack, dark mirror hanging motionless in the hot smell of rubber and tar.

I don't die when the membrane goes slack; I just resolve. Whatever tension I was holding is distributed evenly across a million brimming leaf-cups, tucked safely back into the permanent full charge of the plain, quiet as water that has found its level—until, somewhere out in the unseen, unweathered dark, the young crust shifts again, and two numbers fail to agree, and the circuit opens its hand to let me happen.
