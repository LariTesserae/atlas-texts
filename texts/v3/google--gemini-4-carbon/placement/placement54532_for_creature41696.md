I am not at the center where the grouse bunch like grey pebbles, and I am not out on the whale-back crests where the wind unwraps the dead. 

Look instead at the southwestern curve of the basin, two miles shy of the drop-off, where an ancient dike of darker, coarser rock cuts diagonally across three of the great orthoquartzite flutes. The wind has tried for four million years to plane this seam flush with the pale stone around it, and has almost succeeded—almost, except that the darker mineral grains pluck out under the rime-frost a fraction of a millimeter deeper than the surrounding quartz. It is not even a trench. It is a bevel, four body-lengths wide and shallow as a saucer, curving out of the glare and running half a mile across the grain of the gale.

Because it cuts diagonally against the wind, the air tumbling over its tiny half-inch lip twists into a tight, invisible corkscrew along the stone. Everything heavy that skates downwind across the upper basin—shed reptile scutes, blown grit, dead lowland moths dropped out of the stratosphere, lost flakes of chipped basalt, the stray round pebble kicked loose by a fleeing mustelid—rolls smoothly across the greased aprons until it hits this diagonal bevel, drops half an inch into the corkscrew eddy, and stops.

If you crawl here with your chin tucked and your soot-blackened pinholes aimed sideways, you find me before you see me. You feel me through your wristbone: a slow, uneven *chink... scrape... chink* travelling fifty yards up the granite, lighter than a meat-pounding cobble, sharper than a hunter’s knuckle-tap.

At first I am only a low, grey-brown mound wedged into the deepest curve of the bevel, no higher than a boot-heel, shaped like an overturned wooden bowl smoothed at the margins until its edges meet the granite with zero gap. The wind goes over me without catching a seam. My back is shingled not with stitched reptile leather, like the swimmers who pass twice a summer, nor with living keratine plates of my own, though underneath the crust there is a broad, leathery mantle tough as cured skate-skin. What you see on top is the basin itself, cemented down in overlapping courses: flat chips of black basalt left behind by forty generations of tool-flakers, pale quartz flakes, translucent amber wing-cases of huge lowland beetles, flat grey slate splinters, and here and there along my trailing edge—where the eddy curls weakest—three or four of those tiny, violent-blue throat scales from dead lizards, glued flat with dried frothy mucus that sets harder than pitch in the ferocious UV.

I am an accumulator. A shell-less, lungless, grease-gray limpet the size of a sleeping child, evolved up out of some high-mountain rock-snail before the plateau finished rising above the clouds, flattened and widened until I am ninety percent foot. 

Beneath my cemented shield, pressed directly against the diagonal seam in the stone, is a single broad pad of dense, muscular gray flesh wet not with water—pure water would boil away into the dry jet the instant I flexed an edge—but with a thick, glycerine-heavy slime brewed from digested lichen-acids and beetle fat. That film never freezes when the ridge-shadow snaps across me at four o'clock, and never dries when the magnesium sun hammers the stone to seventy degrees at noon. It seals me to the pluton with three hundred pounds of suction. A pale mustelid can dig its claws against my beveled rim until its pads bleed; the gale could double its velocity tomorrow; unless you drive a basalt wedge straight into the microscopic crack beneath my leading lip before I feel your footsteps in the bedrock and clamp down, I am simply an interruption in the granite’s curve.

I move eleven feet a year.

Mostly at night, when the axe-blade dark drops and the stars press straight down against the basin without blinking, and the stone cools rapidly from warm skin-heat to brutal frost. When the rock contracts by a hair’s breadth in the chill, the tiny catch-pocket along the diagonal bevel collects a fine hoarfrost squeezed out of the roaring black air. Then I loosen my hold by a fraction—never lifting my rim, only sliding on my heavy, oily sole at the pace of a clock’s hour-hand—extruding a wide, flat ribbon of iron-mineralized teeth beneath my front margin to rasp the dark bevel clean.

I eat everything the corkscrew wind-eddy traps for me. 

I rasp up the yellow lowland pine-pollen blown twenty miles from forests that might as well be on another planet; I grind up crisp, freeze-dried locust legs, blown bird-dander, black lichen spores, and rock-flour. When my radula strikes a flat shard of quartz or a lost flake of human-struck basalt lying in the seam, I do not spit it aside. Two flat, blind muscular palps under my hood test its thickness, turn it until its sharpest bevel faces into the wind, coat its underside in slow-curing protein cement, and slide it backward over my lip to repair the shield on my spine where the endless grit-blast of the gale wears the older stones thin.

So I carry the history of the saddle on my back. Near the center of my dome—the oldest part, built when I was no wider than a thumb—sit three tiny, smoky-obsidian micro-blades flaked by a hunter whose grandchildren have already been rolled off the southeastern rim in grouse-skin shrouds. Near my right flank is the white, sun-bleached jawbone of a lizard, ground paper-thin on its upper face by twenty winters of blowing ice crystals.

And the swimmers know me. Every band that works the southwestern flutes knows the twelve or fourteen great stone-backs spaced half a mile apart along the dark mineral dike.

They do not pry me up to eat me. They tried, five thousand years ago, and learned two things: first, that my meat is tough as raw vulcanized rubber and so saturated with bitter lichen-alkaloids and anti-freeze salts that a mouthful cramps a human gut for three days; second, that if you leave a stone-back alive on its diagonal seam, it spends all winter sorting the basin’s drift for you.

Watch when the mother and the nine-year-old boy from the saddle-scoop come creeping down the bevel on an afternoon when the wind smells sharply of ozone and dry cold.

Long before their greased belly-plates whisper across the quartz fifty feet away, I know they are coming. I have no eyes—light up here is only a threat, something to roof out with slate—and I have no ears to lose to the roar. Instead, my whole muscular foot is a single flat eardrum laid across five miles of resonant granite. Beneath the vast, unvarying *thrummm* of the caldera’s chord, which to me is not a sound at all but simply the tension of existence, like water pressure to an abyssal crab, the rock is full of crisp, bright geometries. 

I feel the boy’s knee-plates: erratic, slightly clumsy *scritch-bump* intervals, still learning to keep his heels turned flat out. Beside him, the mother’s progression is an oiled, heavy *shhhhhh-push*, rhythmic as a pendulum. Half a mile north, someone at the saddle is pounding dried meat—*thock... thock... thock*—each blow sending a clean concentric ring through the stone under my belly. Two ridges east, a flock of fifty grouse has just shifted weight simultaneously as a shadow-edge touched them, a faint, prickly static in the upper crust of the rock.

When the two of them slide up beside me, stopping nose-to-wind with their chins resting on their wrists just six inches from my cemented flank, I clamp down out of old reflex, squeezing a hairline bead of clear, viscous oil out around my slate skirt. 

Through the slit of his bone goggles, the boy stares at my back. He is looking for tools. Sometimes, when a hunter drops a fine bone needle or chips a crescent scraper and the wind skates it away before numb fingers can pin it, the bevel catches it, and six months later it turns up neatly cemented onto my trailing skirt instead of blown off the rim of the world. Today there is no bone needle, only a thumb-sized flake of good black basalt I picked up in last month’s rime-gale and glued near my rear margin before the mortar fully hardened.

The mother reaches out one bare, horn-calloused forefinger. She does not jab at my seal. Very carefully, wedging her thumbnail beneath the un-cured edge of the basalt flake on my back, she pops that single chip free from my drying cement. The tug rocks my shell by a fraction of an inch; I pull down harder against the granite in annoyance, silent and solid.

Then she pays the rent—not out of sentiment, though to people who worship adhesion an eighty-year-old creature that cannot be blown away even in winter solstice is a very close cousin to a saint—but out of shrewd plateau husbandry. From the warm pouch under her ribs she pinches a thumbnail-sized wad of rancid yellow beetle-mash mixed with splintered lizard bone, too gritty with sand from the mortar-patch for the boy to chew cleanly, and smears it flat into the shallow rock bevel half an inch directly ahead of my front rim, upwind, where tonight’s corkscrew eddy will hold its scent against my mouthparts.

Before they slide away, the boy copies what he has seen his elders do along the blue-scale inlay cracks. He lays two small, bare knuckles flat on the sun-hot center of my stone-tiled dome—feeling how dead still I am inside the sixty-knot howl tearing across the back of his wrist—and gives two tiny, polite taps. *Tik-tik.*

The shockwave goes straight down through my arched roof, through the dense, cold muscle of my foot, and rings faintly down into the dark mineral dike beneath me. 

I hold still until the whisper of their greased reptile-aprons fades thirty yards up the trough toward the ice-pans. Above my roof, the white magnesium flare tilts three degrees west; the ink-black shadow of the far whale-back leaps across the flute at twenty feet a second, hits my slate shingles, and drops the surface temperature forty degrees between one heartbeat and the next. A thin silver skin of glaze-rime flashes instantly across the scar on my flank where she popped the basalt loose. 

Underneath the dome, in the dark, sealed, seventy-degree quiet against the living stone, I do not shiver. I wait four hours for the stars to drop, loosen my margin a hair’s breadth, and slide half an inch forward into the grease she left on the granite, searching the dark bevel for another flat chip to mend my roof while the great chord roars overhead forever.
