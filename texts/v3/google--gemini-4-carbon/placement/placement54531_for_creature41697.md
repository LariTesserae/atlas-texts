I am not up under the pale sky where the crane-cables sag beneath the weight of coal-colored birds, nor am I riding the flooded flange-ways in the crisp mountain glare. To find me, you have to step off the broad pewter mirror of the concourse and go down one of the river-worn stairways—not far down, not into the blind, boiling dark of the deep turbine-vaults, just twenty or thirty shallow, hollowed steps beneath the street level, where the bright plateau sun gives way to the bruised-amber glow of whale-oil lamps hung from sweating brick arches, and the vast, saturated roar of the surface flocks softens into a steady, rhythmic percussion in the masonry.

This is a junction-gallery, a vaulted groin of red stone and crusted cast-iron where three district mains—one carrying hot, silty greywater from the upper tenements, one pulsing with thick brine from a salt-works two miles west, and a narrower, jacketed copper line singing high and tight with live steam—cross over a sunken settling-weir. 

I am sitting on a low, broad plinth of granite whose corners were planed away by warm condensation four hundred years before my grandmother’s tally-ring was stamped. The water stands up to the middle of my calves, warm as newly drawn blood, sliding slowly eastward over the weir-lip with a sound like heavy silk being drawn across wet slate. Around my ankles, blind, pale-shelled snails the size of my fists graze the sulfur-yellow mat clinging to my skin, their radulae rasping with a tiny, pleasant itch against the thick horn of my heels. I don't brush them off; they keep the dead callous pared smooth so it never splits.

I am old enough that the pale third lids of my eyes have thickened slightly at the corners, turning the amber lamplight into soft, spoked halos, and my barrel-chest doesn’t ask for the steep climbs up to the roof-parapets anymore. Among the guilds, they would call me a *Pulse-listener*, or perhaps just a *Weir-sitter*—one of the quiet fixtures of the under-ways whose work looks, to anyone passing through with a grease-horn and a hurry, indistinguishable from sleep.

Across my broad thighs rests a listening-rod: five feet of solid, dense bell-bronze, cast thick as a child’s wrist and worn to a dark, mirror-bright butter- sheen where three generations of heavy hands have gripped it. One end is flared into a shallow cup, greased with tallow; the other is rounded like a pestle. 

Every quarter-hour or so, without standing up, I lean forward from my wide hips—my belly-folds settling comfortably over my belt of braided copper—and press the pestle-end against the bolted flange of the ten-foot brine-main. Then I rest the heavy jawbone just beneath my ear against the flared bronze cup, close my outer eyelids, and let the city walk into my skull.

If you have never listened through bronze to thirty miles of flooded iron, you would think it was just a hum. It isn’t. Metal and water under pressure cannot lie, and they cannot forget what they have touched. 

First comes the big, slow *thump-shhh, thump-shhh* of the atmospheric beam-engines at the western rim, lifting brine out of the deep basalt pans—eight strokes a minute, heavy and even, the sound of an old draft-beast walking on level stone. Laid over that is the granular, whispering hiss of salt-crystals scouring the rivets three wards upstream, telling me the saturation is running thick today, rich enough to crust the intake-screens by nightfall. And underneath both—down in the register you feel behind your molars rather than hear—there is a faint, uneven *tink... tink-tink* traveling fast through the iron skin of the pipe. 

Someone half a league off is tapping cold-chisels against a stuck gate-valve. Not hard, frantic blows; patient, two-man alternating taps, coaxing five-year lime-scale loose from bronze threads without cracking the housing. I know that rhythm. Even through half a mile of wet masonry and rushing brine, I know the slight drag on the second beat: that’s old Marta’s crew from the third-terrace salt-houses, favoring the left-handed striker whose shoulder was crushed by a slag-cart ten winters back.

I pull the rod back, lay it across my knees, and sit for a long minute in the warm mist, letting my nictitating membranes *flick, flick* away the beads of ceiling-drip. Along the brick arch directly overhead, a curtain of pale, finger-thick land-tunicates hangs down nearly to my scalp. As my breath rises—warm and damp from deep in my ribs—their siphons open slightly and sway toward me, drinking the warmth off the crown of my head. 

Around my thick neck hang seven heavy rings of stamped bronze, green in the grooves with verdigris and bright on the flats where they rub against my collarbone and against each other. Every time I breathe in, they shift with a tiny, dull *chink*. My fingers find the fourth ring down without my looking—a broad, notched washer stamped seventy years ago when this weir was re-leveled after the southern foundry-pan settled four inches. My thumb traces the three deep punch-marks on its rim: *three turns open on the bypass sluice when the western brine runs heavy with crystal.*

I don't hurry. Hurrying makes waves in a narrow gallery, and waves wash the yellow bacterial mat off the weir-lip where it does the good work of eating the sourness out of the greywater. 

I slide off my granite plinth into the calf-deep flow, my broad, webbed toes spreading wide to grip the slick brick floor, the snails dropping off my ankles with small, soft *plops* to right themselves in the silt. Leaning my shoulder into the massive, rust-velveted wheel of the bypass sluice—its cast-iron rim warm and cushioned with tiny, soft-shelled limpets—I ease my fingers into the gaps between them so I won't crush their plates, set my heels, and put my horizontal weight into the iron.

*Groan... click.* One turn. A puff of warm, sulfurous silt rises through the water around my knees, and a dozen blind, hand-sized white crabs scuttle out from the wheel-housing, brushing harmlessly over my insteps before settling back into the warm shadow under the pipe. 

*Groan... click.* Two turns. 

*Groan... click.* Three.

Immediately, the pitch of the ten-foot main beside my shoulder drops by half a tone—from a strained, singing *eeee* to a loose, satisfied *oooo*—as a third of its heavy load spills sideways into the broad settling-basin, swirling slowly in greasy pewter eddies under the amber lamps. Half a league away, though they won't know why for another ten minutes until the pressure-drop walks back up the line, the stuck gate-valve under Marta’s chisels will suddenly ease in its seat and slide home like tallowed bone.

I don't need to tell them I did it. Still, because it feels good in the chest to answer metal with breath, I lay one broad, slate-gray palm flat against the sweating flank of the iron pipe, draw a slow draught of wet, mineral air through my bristled nostrils, and let my throat-pouch swell until it brushes the tally-rings on my breastbone.

*Booooom-hmmmmmm.*

Low, round, and slow, pitched right down in the marrow of the brickwork. It rolls out into the vaulted gallery, sets the hanging oil-lamps swinging by the width of a fingernail, makes the curtains of pale tunicates contract all at once in a silent, rippling wave down fifty yards of tunnel, and goes humming away up the flooded masonry channel toward the street-grates overhead—where some tram-conductor or passing Brine-walker, feeling the flagstones purr for three seconds beneath the horn of his bare soles, will know without looking down that the under-ways are awake, the weirs are breathing easy, and the water is going where it ought to go.

I wade back to my smooth granite seat, lift a small stoneware jar from a niche in the warm mortar behind me, and scoop two fingers of salted crustacean-curd into my mouth, chewing slowly while the snails begin their patient climb back onto my heels. Above the archway, through an iron street-grate forty feet up a slanted light-well, a single square of pale, high-altitude sky shines down through drifting steam. Twice while I finish my curd, the bright square darkens briefly as wide, leathery wings glide across it without a beat. Someday—not soon, my heart still beats as slow and steady as the western beam-engines, *eight strokes, eight strokes*—my nephews will carry this heavy barrel of bone and gristle up those river-worn stairs into that light, lay me out on the warm slates, and let me go up into those dark wings, and then back down the gutters into the lime-mills. Maybe a handful of my ribs, ground white and mixed with sand and warm brine, will come right back down this light-well in a mason’s hod to point the bricks around this very weir. 

It’s a good thought to sit with. I lay the bronze rod back across my thighs, rest my greased shoulders against the warm, living wall, and listen to the city digesting the afternoon.
