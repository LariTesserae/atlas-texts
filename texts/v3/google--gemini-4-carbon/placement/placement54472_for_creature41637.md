I am not at the rim. Anything that lies at the rim breathes too fast, sheds loose grit, carries the salt-and-iron stink of the open sea-flats in its clothes. Even looking down from above feels like an intrusion—a dark smudge of heat and appetite hung over an open eye.

If I am anywhere here, I am five tiers down on the northern curve of the basin, tucked under a corbelled overhang where direct sky has not touched the stone since the sinkhole collapsed. Yet there is no shadow on me. Three selenite plates—one forty feet up on the western cornice, one wedged into grey clay beside a cistern on the third tier, and a smaller, thumb-sized shard set into the bog-wood lintel across the alley—hand a single ribbon of cold white afternoon down through the tiers until it lands square across my back.

At first you would take me for part of the masonry’s repair. My knees are drawn up into the caves of my armpits; my heels rest in two shallow dimples worn into the slate sill over four hundred years by knees and heels identical to mine. The skin over my spine has the faint, greasy translucence of mutton-tallow left out in frost, faintly veined where the slow glycerol-thick blood moves at twelve beats a minute. A strand of pale chartreuse clubmoss hangs down from the parapet above and touches my left shoulder. Over the past three hours, condensation creeping down its tiny overlap-scales has transferred four beads of water onto my collarbone. I have not brushed them off. They run down the natural crease between my chest-fat and my upper arm and drop—without falling, sliding on surface tension alone—directly into the lip of the soapstone trough between my feet. I am part of the plumbing while I sit.

What makes me *me*, and not the keeper two tiers up who grinds mirror-edges, is the wall at my right cheek.

Behind this thin partition of fitted gypsum blocks—blocks no thicker than two stacked palms, mortared with snail-lime and old gypsum paste—are the four primary digestion vaults of the north quadrant.

Most of the forty live by the eye: the polarizing comb across the pupil, the ultraviolet sheen on the stonecrop leaves, the indigo bars blooming and fading on an inner wrist across the bright void. I have those eyes too, dark amber-slate behind their sliding clear lids, and when my sister on the opposite gallery raises her forearm into a light-shaft to show three thin lines and a stippled dot—*upper mirror creeping, frost-heave on the east lip*—my throat answers automatically with a dull plum-colored bar before my hand has even reached into my hip-fold for a wooden wedge.

But my work is mostly done with the flat of my cheek and the broad, seamless pads of my palms pressed flat against dead stone.

Inside the sealed vaults behind me, the pale fungi are eating. Right now they are working through two baskets of clipped heath-roots, forty-odd amber snail shells crushed to meal, six years of shed bird-down that lost its loft, and the left femur, ribs, and blubber-mantle of the keeper who sat in these exact heel-dimples before I was born. The vault has no window. Selenite light would kill the white mycelium in an afternoon. No one opens the greased stone door until the cycle finishes, twelve months from sealing to spooning, because breaking the seal lets fifty-degree warmth leak out into our thirty-four-degree air—a small invisible plume of convection that would set the upper air wobbling and blur the light-beams.

So someone has to listen to the temperature through four inches of alabaster.

Not listen with ears—my ear-holes are waxed shut with fine down like everyone’s, deaf to anything sharper than the heavy thud of my own slow heart. I feel the heat-bloom with the nerve-nets under my cheek-blubber.

To a warm creature from the gravel plain above, this wall would feel like cellar ice. To me, whose blood runs at forty-eight degrees, the stone over Vault Two has a distinct, buttery fullness. Two degrees warmer than my palm. Half a degree cooler than it was five days ago when the sun stood a thumb’s-breadth higher at noon. That half-degree drop tells me the fungus has finished the blubber-waxes and moved into the slow mineral grind of cartilage and moss-cellulose. Good. No souring. No anaerobic pocket swelling against the bird-fat seal.

Vault Four, three feet to my left, is fractionally too brisk. Lying with my temple against its seam yesterday—a whole afternoon went by while one bead of water traveled from the lintel to the cistern—I felt a tiny pinprick of fifty-two-degree heat gathering near the upper corner. Too much bird-dung in the last charging, perhaps, or an uncrushed snail egg that died rich. Left alone, fifty-two becomes fifty-five; fifty-five softens the grease around the ground-stone plug; a softened seal breathes out one thimbleful of ammonia gas; the liverworts flanking the doorway blacken at their tips within three days.

I uncurled my legs. It took four minutes. First the toes spread to test the slickness of the slate; then the thick grey pad of my heel took the load; then my hip lifted clear of the sill without scraping a single scale from the silver cushion-heath growing against my thigh.

I did not walk to the mirror-ledge so much as pour myself along the wall.

Here is the small, private pleasure of this life—the thing you could never guess peering over the gravel rim at what looks like forty pale toads frozen in a bright well: *nothing is blurry*. When your heart beats once every five seconds and your breath enters and leaves like cold oil through a reed, time stops jittering. I can watch the tip of a rosette leaf swell over six days the way a lowland creature watches water come to the boil. I know every bird on the lower three tiers by the exact ultraviolet speckle-pattern inside its naked throat-pouch. The heavy hen currently sitting on the stair-turn by my knee—she has a jagged saffron fork on her left wattle—has laid four hundred and eleven eggs since my wrists grew wide enough to hold a burin. She does not blink as my shin passes half an inch from her beak. Her plumage smells of nothing at all, only clean cold glass.

To cool Vault Four, I did not touch the vault. I reached up to the thumb-sized shard of selenite wedged into the bog-wood lintel across the narrow alley and pinched its clay seating between two broad, cool fingers.

The clay is stiff grey glacial till brought up from the bottom sump centuries ago, reworked with spit and tiny drops of wax-oil so it never dries to powder and never freezes solid. Pushing it feels like indenting old cheese. I tilted the crystal downward by the thickness of a snail’s shell.

Instantly, the reflected lozenge of light that had been warming the lower footing of Vault Four slid off the alabaster wall and fell instead onto a stone trough of translucent stonecrop six inches away. Starved of that tiny supplemental bounce of radiation, the stone block over Vault Four will cool by half a degree before tomorrow’s noon. The fungus inside will slow its chew. The bird-fat seal will stiffen back to tallow. The stonecrop, given an extra finger-width of glare, will thicken its sugary sap by frost-fall, and three months from now the child currently learning the second-tier staircases will lick a bone spoon dipped in its bleed.

That is what it is to be one of us. You never push the thing itself; you move a splinter of light six feet away and wait four days for the stone to answer.

Sometimes—once in two or three winters—something comes from the outside. Not a traveler; the plain is empty for leagues, nothing there but grey shingle, salt wind, and skuas that pass too high to see our hole. What comes is wreckage dropped by the wind across the invisible membrane overhead. A dried strap of kelp torn from the distant beach. A dead lemming frozen hard as oak, tumbling over the lip during a blizzard. Once, when I was very small and still clung to the central pillar-stairs, a single primary feather from a sea-eagle planed down through the thermal plug, spinning lazily through the cross-beams of thirty mirrors while forty pairs of slit-amber eyes tracked its descent in dead silence.

An elder caught it on a sheet of mica before it could touch a moss-cushion—foreign mites, sea-salt, unpredictable rot. Within ten minutes it was sealed into Vault One under a double smear of fat. Two years later its hollow shaft, mineralized hard in brine, became three fine tweezers for lifting bristle-worms out of the water-flutes, and its keratin went in black paste under the big millstone rosette on the lowest plaza. Even the sky’s accidents are digested until they look as if they had always been gypsum and green wax.

My hand comes back down from the clay notch, slow as cooled honey, and settles onto my knee. Across the pit, sixty feet away and four tiers up, my sister’s inner wrist catches a vertical beam from the rim. Two broad indigo bands, fading smoothly from the center outward: *steady now*.

I do not lift my arm to answer. From where she sits, looking down through the triple-lit air between seven cantilevered bridges and forty hanging curtains of motionless chartreuse moss, she can see the side of my neck clearly enough. I let a single thumb-print of dark venous blue rise beneath the tallow-pale skin behind my jaw, hold it while a bead of water creeps half an inch down its flint-cut herringbone channel beside my ankle, and let it sink back into the cold white quiet.

Then I lean my right cheek back against Vault Two, tuck my toes against the soapstone lip, draw one long, dry breath that will last me until the shadowless light shifts the width of a hair, and go back to listening to my predecessor turn into leaves.
