My left collarbone has been dull for three winters. 

A flake of shale came down while I was wedging a lower course near the eastern rim—not a large piece, no wider than my two pincers laid flat together, and it had only fallen six feet. I felt the tension in the ledge above me go slack a tenth of a second before it let go, which was time enough to tuck my head-flange down, though not time enough to draw my shoulder back through the slot. The edge caught the upper crest obliquely. It didn’t breach the cavity, didn't spill the clear inner fluid, thank the stone, or the mist would have gotten in and rotted the drum from the inside out. Instead, it scarred. The waxy grey tallow pushed up from the seam to seal the score, and over the season it mineralized too thick, leaving a raised, pale ridge across the membrane.

Because of it, the high *tinks* on my left side arrive smeared. Everything above a three-inch slate overhang sounds slightly wet, as if the rock were coated in bacterial slime two feet higher than it ought to be. When I stand in an unworked corridor, the roar doesn't blind me evenly anymore; it pulls to the right, a constant phantom lean that makes my right caliper-foot grip the submerged ledges a fraction harder than my left, wearing the vulcanized pad uneven.

I spend most of my waking hours out on the ragged fringe where the basin floor meets the boundary shale, two thousand paces away from the six-second bell of the Great Ring. Out here, the mist is thicker, rolled down fresh off the upper slopes, and the rock is younger, angrier, still settling from whatever ancient heaving cracked this valley open. The center around the Ring is nearly finished—generations of us have combed the corridors there until walking five hundred yards feels like sliding through a single continuous note, warm and dark and level. It is good for the elders whose joints have stiffened with extra lime, and good for the domes where the soft ones hang. 

It is bad for a listener with a scarred crest. In the smooth chords near the center, my dull left side sticks out to me like a jagged tooth. Every perfection in the old walls reminds me of the smear in my own bone.

Out here on the rim, everything is broken anyway.

Right now, my lower joints are locked into a three-point brace between two thick, hexagonal columns of black basalt. My feet are five inches under black water, hooked securely under opposing shelves where the stone steps inward. The cold does not bother me; cold is simply what water is, the baseline state of matter, the slowness in my blood that lets me hold this exact posture since the Ring tolled four hundred times ago. Around my ankles, the pale slime gathers in faint, cold eddies where my standing has interrupted the trickle. Something small and soft—a juvenile grazer no bigger than a thumb-pad—has been trying to cross the inner arch of my right foot for the last twenty minutes, mistaking my chitin for wet slate. I let it go. It isn't worth unlocking my knee-latches to peel off so small a mouthful.

Before me is a narrow throat of rock, maybe fourteen inches wide and twelve feet tall, choked at the bottom with the winter’s shatter. Above it hangs a broad, skewed shelf of slate that bleeds condensation fast. Five distinct drip-lines fall from that lip into the flooded throat below. 

*Plink-clack. Tuh. Bloop. Tink.*
*Plink-clack. Tuh. Bloop. Tink.*

It is ugly. Not because it is loud, though it rattles fiercely against my good right forearm, making the hollow ulna buzz like a trapped fly. It is ugly because the *tuh* and the *bloop* are fighting each other at the bottom of the shaft, bouncing off an angled wedge of fallen basalt so their waves cross jaggedly in the throat, cancelling the low tone that ought to anchor this whole forty-yard pocket and kicking a sharp, stray overtone straight out into the corridor behind me. Twenty paces back, where my sleeping-dome sits tucked under a deadweight cap of unfractured stone, that stray overtone drills against the outer corbelling all night. Inside, pressed flat between two dome-mates, I could feel it all yesterday evening—a tiny, insistent *itch* coming through three feet of dry-stacked shale and right through the carapace of the builder slotted against my right side. Neither of my dome-mates seemed to mind it as much as I did, though the younger one shifted her mandibles twice in her sleep when the mist thickened and the drip sped up.

So I am here.

I do not reach into the throat yet. If you reach in before you understand how the rock is leaning against itself, you become part of the fall. I fold my arms flat against my narrow chest, pressing my good right forearm tight against my ribs and angling my scarred left collar slightly forward to compensate for the dead spot, trying to catch the shape of the shaft underneath the clatter.

Wait. Count the Ring.

Far behind me, filtered through half a million wet vertical blades, the six-second pulse arrives not through the air, which is too choked with mist-static to carry it this far, but up through the flooded soles of my caliper feet. *Thrum.* One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six. *Thrum.*

Against that steady floor-beat, I listen to the throat in front of me. Not to the water, which is stupid and only knows how to fall, but to the basalt walls holding the rubble. 

Stone talks before it moves, though you have to be empty inside to hear it. Under the *plink-clack* and the hollow *bloop*, the right-hand pillar has a high, tight pinch in its grain, about shoulder height. A slab of the winter shatter is wedged sideways against its flank, pushing outward with maybe three hundred pounds of dead leverage. It wants to stay there for another ten years until the seep softens its seat. The left-hand pillar is slack, dead-sounding, carrying nothing but its own upright weight.

Good.

I unlock my left elbow. The grey tallow in the joint parts with a slow, stiff tug inside my shell, silent to the outside air. Sliding my arm edge-first into the fourteen-inch slot, avoiding the third drip-line so I don't scatter new spray across my face-plates, I fit my pincer around a loose, flat triangle of shale resting near the surface of the black water. It weighs twenty pounds, keen as a split reed along all three margins. A soft thing—one of the pale shaft-hangers, or some hypothetical creature without armor—would lose fingers just touching it. Against my vulcanized pads, the razor edge merely grates with a dry, satisfying catch.

I draw it up two feet, turn my wrist ninety degrees, and slide the point of the triangle into a seam on the *slack* left-hand wall, right beneath the path of the *tuh* drip. I don't let go yet. I hold it at a thirty-degree tilt, letting my own arm-bones bridge the stone to my chest.

*Plink-clack.*
The third drop hits my new triangle instead of the rubble below. Instead of *tuh*, it goes *shhhk-tik*, shedding half its force sideways into a thin silver fan of mist that wets the wall an inch above the bacterial line. And because that drop no longer strikes the flooded pocket a split-second before the fourth drop, the *bloop* suddenly drops an octave, opening up dark and round in the shaft like a mouth yawning underwater.

My right forearm hums. Even through the scar on my left crest, the nasty high rattle vanishes. Behind me, the corridor goes ten paces deeper into quiet.

I hold the triangle there while the Ring counts twenty pulses through my feet, testing whether the secondary spray from my angled plate is going to wash away the bacterial grease on the opposite column or pool somewhere foolish that will freeze and crack a good seam if the upper clouds drop cold air next month. The spray runs clean, joining an old, established trickle three inches down. 

With my free right claw, I select two small chocks of dense black stone from the water between my ankles—pieces that split off an older pillar years ago, their edges already slightly blunted by the acid of the slime—and reach in to jam them beneath the triangle's butt. A slow lean of my narrow torso, putting half my body weight through my stiffened wrist until the shale bites a fraction of a millimeter into the softer grain of the seam. A grey puff of dry stone-flour blooms under the lip, safe from the drip.

When I take my hands away, the triangle stays. 

*Plink-clack. Shhhk-tik. Broooop. Tink.*

Not finished. Not a true chord yet—the first two drops are still clattering double at the top of the throat, and the fifth is bright as a struck icicle—but the sickness is out of it. Tonight, the dome behind me will be dark all the way through the shell.

Before I unhook my feet to hunt for a broader slate to catch the fifth drip, a movement in the flooded slot right below my new stone catches my lower pair of pewter eyes.

The change in vibration has bothered the resident of the shaft. Drawn halfway up out of the black water, clinging to the opposing basalt walls with eight hair-thin, multi-jointed legs spanning the entire fourteen-inch gap, is a pale hanger. It is a large one, its tiny translucent body no bigger than an eye-bead, pulsing faintly as it tastes the new *broooop* ringing through its hooked tarsi. For three winters—maybe since the flake fell that scarred my shoulder—it hung down there in the ugly cross-rattle, enduring the jagged waves because the five drips washed mites down into its reach. Now I have smoothed its house, and it doesn't recognize the room. Slowly, testing each millimeter of stone above the slime-line, it lifts one thread-leg, sets it higher into the quiet zone under my dry grey stone-dust, and settles back into stillness.

I fold my arms against my chest again and stay locked on my tripod, watching the pale speck disappear into the dim pewter light until even my close vision loses it against the wet rock. 

A hundred paces to the north, through fifty layered baffles and a maze of crooked fins, another stone goes *tink-hmmmm* as someone else—likely the tall, quiet mason from my own dome whose left pincer is chipped at the tip—finishes wedging a course she has been nursing since yesterday. Her hum touches the edge of mine in the wet air between our territories. They do not quite match pitch yet; there is a ten-yard stretch of wild, uncombed spire-thicket lying untouched between her court and mine that still hisses like torn gravel. Next season, or the season after, we will work inward from opposite sides until we meet edge-to-edge in that narrow gap, trading chocks without having to speak over the drip, until her silence and my silence lock together into a single room.

No hurry. The cloud sits low on the rim, heavy with ten thousand years of water waiting to condense. My scarred crest buzzes faintly, off-center, and down through the soles of my feet comes the slow, bronze pulse from the center of the world, counting six, and six, and six. I unlock one knee, draw my narrow flank sideways through a five-inch slot without scraping a drop from the walls, and go looking for a flat piece of grey shale about the width of my head.
