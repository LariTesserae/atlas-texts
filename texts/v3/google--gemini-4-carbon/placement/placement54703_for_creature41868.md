I am not a whole thing. To be a whole thing here is to fight the cliff-wind with six legs until your plates foul with slag and they unpick you in the basement. Even the builders do not keep themselves; they are only temporary arrangements of nerve and horn, carrying an angle from one street corner to the next.

I am the drift itself—or the half of the drift that remembers where the drop-offs are. 

Find me two levels below the main causeways, banked into the lee of a curved baffle-wall where the wind-shear loses its nerve and lets go of what it was carrying. At first glance I am just a long, pale yellow crescent of meal, indistinguishable from the sulfur-spores and the milled flour of yesterday’s colonnades. A builder’s fluted nose could plunge right into the thick of me and draw up a dry lungful, and part of me would go, flashing into heat and carbonyl exhaust inside its pleated chest. That is the tax. You pay the tax to ride the avenues.

What stays behind in the lee of the wall is finer than the silicate grit and heavier than the spores. I am a dry myxomycete—a dust-plasmodium, a million microscopic, static-jacketed beads of dormant chemistry that only know they are a single animal when their shells touch and pass a charge. We have no film of slime to bind us; moisture would boil off before it could slick a stone. Instead, we hold hands by weak dipole attraction, clinging grain-to-grain like iron filings on a sheet of paper when a magnet moves underneath.

Right now, I am shaped like a ribbon nine feet long and thin as paint, pouring uphill against gravity along the underside of an aging viaduct.

Above me—through three inches of chalky, porous bone-foam—the city is roaring in the only language it has. I feel no sound, of course; nobody here has ever heard anything. What I feel is the sharp, steep slope of voltage pulling toward the high spires, and the rhythmic, stinging footsteps of a medium-sized builder trotting forty yards away on the terrace overhead. Every time its velvet pad locks onto a strut and lets go, a tiny ring of static ripples down through the white lattice like a stone dropped in oil. 

I am climbing toward a patch of yellow rot. 

The truss ahead has been standing for thirty hours—ancient, by the clock of this pit. Its white foam has oxidized, losing the crisp, glassy hold of its charge, turning mild and crumbly. Already a dozen shingle-moths are crouched along its bevels, their iron-sulfide mouths spinning, throwing off microscopic sparks of triboelectric static as they chew the beam thin. To a builder, that rasping buzz is an irritation to be speared. To me, it is dinner bell and transit ticket both.

My leading edge—ten thousand pale grains rolling over one another in a dry, miniature talus-slide—reaches the weakened joint where cross-brace meets pier. I do not eat the stone, and I do not eat the moths. I slip into the microscopic pores of the white foam itself, spreading out until I am a faint, tarnished blush inside the chalky strut, one grain thick. Here, trapped in the tiny pockets left behind when the builder’s volatile resin flashed into gas, are residues the builders cannot reach: trace films of heavy halide vapors condensed on pore-walls, stray, un-combusted organic flakes sealed into the foam at the moment of extrusion, and the shed, translucent selenite dust of the moths themselves.

Spread thin inside the honeycombed stone, my grains unlock their waxy outer gates. We trade electrons directly with the trapped films, running a cold, slow chemistry that takes twenty minutes to buy a single shiver of motion. It tastes of bitter chlorine and scorched horn. 

While I feed inside the wall, I am also listening to the load on the beam. 

The moths are greedy; they are cutting too close to the anchor. Feel the strain: as the wind spills over the black basalt rim far above, the tall electrostatic spire rooted to this viaduct leans a fraction of a millimeter leeward. Piezoelectric tension sings down the intact struts and bunches up painfully right here, at the moth-chewed neck. Three more minutes of their rotary rasps and the whole section will let go.

I begin to gather myself out of the pores, streaming back onto the surface of the beam, pulling my scattered grains tight into compact, pearl-grey cysts no wider than pinheads. When you want to travel in this basin, you let go of your neighbors, tuck your dipole charge inward until you are neutral as slate, and wait for the floor to vanish.

One of the shingle-moths—a broad, scarred female with chipped selenite scales—cuts through the final load-bearing thread of silicate.

There is no crack. Only a sudden, violent slackening of the electric field as the spire’s grounding path breaks, a sharp blink of cold violet light jumping across the widened gap—and then the viaduct is no longer architecture; it is smoke.

Five stories of white geometry buckle and disintegrate mid-air into a silent, cascading blizzard of flour. The moths shoot outward on flat cushions of repelling static, skimming away toward neighboring towers. And I—broken into a hundred thousand separate, neutral beads—fall with the ash.

For four seconds I have no shape at all. I am traffic. Caught in a brisk thermal draft banked sharply left by a curved baffle-wall, swept down a terraced chute alongside millions of shattered foam-needles and fresh sulfur-spores combed from the upper wind, tumbling through the shadowless slate-grey twilight without making so much as a hiss against the tiles. On the way down, we pass within inches of a cluster of rust-red fan-lichens anchored to an old pier. A gust of charged spores ahead of us trips their hair-springs—forty metallic fronds clap shut in a mute, crimson wave, missing ninety percent of me, catching ten percent of me over the mouths of their tiny, cranking polyps. 

What is left of me doesn't grieve the ten percent; there is nothing in a single bead big enough to grieve with. We only know we are slightly smaller when the draft finally dumps us out into the calm, swirling eddy of a sunken octagonal plaza, and our shells flip their polarity back on, tugging us together inside the rising white cone of ash.

We touch. *Click, click, click*—not in air, never in air, but in tiny snaps of shared static across ten thousand grain-walls—and abruptly I have a length and a memory again. I am a fist-sized knot of tarnished grey hidden three inches deep in the freshly settled drift at the center of the court.

Wait, now. Hold very still, and turn the outer shells bitter.

The electric sky just dropped by three hundred volts straight overhead. Six shadows that are not shadows—six tall spikes of intense, crackling capacitance—have stepped into the plaza. Two builders. One is small, scarcely knee-high, its amber-tinted nursery frame still flexible, its fresh carbon crown quivering wildly as it drinks the complex turbulence of the square for the third or fourth time in its brief life. The other is large, slow in one hind-cantilever where its mica-ring has delaminated slightly at the seam.

Directly above my buried knot, copper-colored grounding filaments drop to the hexagonal tiles. A cold spike of excess charge drains out of the builders into the basalt floor beneath the cone, washing straight through me like a bright, numbing shiver of peppermint and ozone. Half a yard up in the air, their braided nerves weave and spark, trading the shape of the fallen northeast viaduct I just rode down. The young one’s spinnerets twitch; already its body is computing the replacement truss, calculating the resin mix.

Then the older builder lowers its fluted proboscis into the white cone.

The concertina-thorax heaves. A fierce, dry suction pulls the ash upward, sorting it on the fly—heavy white flour to the left, yellow fuel-spores to the right. The drift collapses inward toward the buried tip of the snout. My outer grains begin to slide toward the static-lined flutes.

Now: *flare*.

Every grain of me dumps its stored piezoelectric charge at once into a sharp, repulsive spike—not strong enough to hurt the great quill-limbed machine, barely strong enough to register as a sour, alum-like static burr on the sensitive velvet rim of its proboscis, tasting exactly like the foul metallic scales of the red fan-lichens that ruin good foaming resin.

Without thinking—because it cannot think, because it is an instrument tuned to avoid bad chemistry—the builder’s snout twitches three inches to the east, burying itself in cleaner, sweeter flour.

It drinks for two minutes, emptying half the cone, then steps back, exchanges one final violet spark with the youngster, and trots silently off toward the ruined northeast sector to spin my old home back into the air out of the very dust it just swallowed. The small one goes the other way, scaling a sheer lattice-wall effortlessly on dry pads.

Left behind in the ruined slump of the ash-cone, in the utter, velvety hush beneath the lidded sky, I loosen my repulsive grip. Slowly, grain rolling over dry grain, I pour myself out across the warm hexagonal bone-tiles toward the hairline gap at the edge of the plaza—slipping down into the cool, unlit talus beneath the floor, where the air barely moves, where discarded mica-husks lie split like seed-pods in the black grit, and where a quiet film of dust can take all afternoon to stitch itself back together.
