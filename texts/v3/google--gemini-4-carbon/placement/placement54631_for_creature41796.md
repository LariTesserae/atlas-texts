At first, you have to strip away the urge to breathe, or to hold your breath, or to brace your shoulders against a forty-knot gale of freezing water. Anything with shoulders is wrong for this place. Anything with a spine would snap like chalk the first time the scaffolding recalibrates and drops the pressure out from under a million tons of blue.

Let go of bones. Let go of eyes, too—eyes are just soft bags of jelly waiting for a cavitation bubble to collapse next to them and turn them inside out. And what is there to look at anyway? Three miles of pale air underneath, blinding sterile blue all around, black geometry growing out of nothing. Sight is an expensive luxury when the whole world is an acoustic hammer.

If I am here, I am listening with my entire skin, and I am hiding from the roar.

Not down on the mirror-floor with the glass-sheets—that’s too frantic, a lifelong sprint over an abyss, tacking through afternoon light like a panicked skate, forever scraping iron off spinning shrapnel before the sky falls up. That is a life of pure velocity. And not in the copper galls on the windward spires, thriving directly in the teeth of the freight train, vibrating with frantic electrical joy until the quiet kills them. 

Look instead at those mistakes in the black rock. 

Look where the violet slicks got greedy along the shimmering margin of a growing pillar, drinking too much of the template’s math, starving one side of a hexagonal prism until the basalt forgot how to grow straight and curled over on itself like a black wave freezing mid-break. Inside that curl—underneath that jagged, accidental overhang where the four-day-old stone is veined with trapped purple crystal—the water doesn't roar. 

It hums. It eddies. 

That is where I am. 

I am what collects in the very first shadows of a world that wasn’t supposed to have shadows yet. 

At first glance, if you shone a dive light up under the hooked flute of basalt, you’d think I was just debris—a tangled, pale-gray catch-net of dead matter wedged into the sharp V where two flawed planes of silicate meet. And partly, I am. Nothing goes to waste in a sterile ocean, not when carbon is rarer than diamonds. Look closer at the grey tangle wedged into the back of the hollow: it’s built from the casualties of the blue. Shredded ribbons of pale chitin torn out of ruptured moss-boulders; splintered, translucent scales of biogenic silica from a glass-sheet that misjudged a phase-shift and got peeled off the floor; tattered scraps of copper-colored rubber ripped loose from a kelp frond three miles upstream. 

It looks like trash caught in a drain. Until a stray fragment of moss tumbles past the mouth of the cave in the screaming current outside, and a dozen fine, mucilaginous threads—clear as spun sugar and strong as spider silk—shoot out into the shear-zone, snag the green scrap, and winch it slowly back into the quiet dark.

I am not the trash; I am the glue holding it together. A blind, sprawling network of clear, cold-loving mycelium—or something very much like it—threading through a self-built reef of wreckage. 

To live here is to live by the ear—or rather, by tension. Every strand of my web is anchored to the razor-sharp crystal lattice of the young basalt, strung tight across the mouth of the spiral overhang like the strings of a harp. Outside, three feet away, the water moves at fifty miles an hour, booming with low-frequency thunder, tearing itself into silver foam. Inside my pocket, the water turns in a slow, lazy vortex, cold and crystal clear, vibrating with the muffled, bone-deep *thrum* of the pillar taking the hit for me.

I know the entire ocean through the trembling of the rock. 

I feel the copper-galls buzzing forty feet up on the windward face, a high, tight, metallic fizzing coming right down through the stone. I feel the slow, eerie *tick-tick-tick* along the outer lip of my cave where a surviving remnant of the violet syncytium is still creeping upward, drinking the heat-haze of creation, making my roof an inch longer every hour, curving the black stone out into a deeper and deeper horn. Sometimes the template jumps, and I feel a sudden, tiny crystalline *crunch* in the wall behind me as a patch of the violet creature gets entombed forever—a new vein of purple glass sealing off the dark.

And I know when the recalibrations are coming long before the white-ribbon worms or the moss-balls do. 

Seconds before the ambient roar drops out, the unseen grid tightens. Because my threads are chemically fused right into the fresh silicate—and because I’ve woven dead,copper-sulfide wires from shattered galls into my structural web—I feel the local spacetime kink shift as a sudden, sharp taste of ozone and pennies. 

When that taste hits, every slack, mucilaginous fishing-line hanging out in the eddy snaps tight, reeling itself flush against the jagged ceiling. I pull the stolen glass-scales inward, overlapping them like slate shingles across my softest nodes. 

Then comes the drop. That awful, pressurized hush where the freight train vanishes, and for half a heartbeat, hanging three miles above the ochre desert, there is no sound at all except the high, thin ringing of stressed basalt. 

Then the heave. Outside my overhang, the bright blue water turns blinding white as a million tons of snowmelt are yanked skyward. The eddy inside my cave tries to rip itself backward out into the column; the pressure spikes hard enough to flatten my threads into ribbons against the black glass; shards of freshly flaked stone scream past the opening like shrapnel, clipping the lip of the overhang and sending bright, glassy *pings* ringing through my body. 

And then the sleet falls back down into the upper sea miles away, the forty-knot current slams back into its groove, the freight-train roar resumes, and the aftermath drifts past my door.

That is my harvest. Every recalibration is an autumn. 

Torn ribbons of pale worm-flesh, stunned or halved; pulverized dust of green moss; flakes of dulled, acid-washed stone carrying faint films of digestive slime from the floor-hunters below. Slowly, cautiously, I pay my clear threads back out into the swirling edge of the shear-line, casting into the blinding blue light from my sliver of shadow, combing the sterile water for the tiny, microscopic snow of broken pioneers. 

I don't move. I don't hunt. I digest. In the dark back-corner of the basalt flute, shielded from the brutal currents, I hold my gathered scraps together and rot them—conducting the very first quiet decay in an ocean too young and too clean to know how to rot on its own. Around my threads, in a pocket no bigger than a bushel basket, the sterile snowmelt grows slightly cloudy, thick with stray amino acids, leaked nitrates, and tiny, suspended motes of silt I didn't let fly away.

I am making mud. Just a thimbleful at first, tucked into a razor-sharp crease of four-day-old black glass, held down by clear slime so the eddy doesn't wash it out over the clouds three miles below. 

Yesterday—or twenty recalibrations ago, which is how time measures itself here—a torn, three-inch scrap of a pale ribbon-worm got reeled into my shingle-roof and didn't die. Its hooks caught in my dead kelp-tangle instead of my digestive threads. It’s still there, curled in the calm, warmish dark behind my silica shield, twitching slightly whenever a cavitation crack booms outside, feeding on the cloudy, nutrient-rich water leaking off my compost pile instead of having to hurl itself back out into the lethal blue to find another moss-ball.

I haven't eaten it yet. My threads touch its pale chitin, tasting it, and leave it alone. Its waste feeds my outer filaments; my roof keeps it from being shredded. 

We are sitting in the crooked hollow made by a parasite's mistake, three miles up in the sky, listening to the mountains grow and the artillery-cracks of collapsing emptiness outside, waiting in the very first patch of quiet dirt for whatever else gets tired of the screaming and washes in.
