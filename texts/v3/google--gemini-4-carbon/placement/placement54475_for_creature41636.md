At first, entering a room like this, the instinct is to look for a vantage point—somewhere high up near the crown where a whole volume can be taken in at once—and immediately that instinct fails, because there is nothing to see with and very little to see by. In forty-eight degrees of motionless air, sight is the wrong sense to bring. Even the cherry-dark glow of the resistance grids up near the ceiling isn’t really illumination; it is just heat that happens to brush the very bottom of a spectrum no creature down here uses.

So for a while I am only a drift of attention moving down through the thermal layers, trying on the chamber’s speeds to see which one holds.

I thought first I might be the amber pilot bulb recessed into one of the junction boxes—a thumb-sized glass envelope of neon and argon, keeping a tiny orange sheath of ionized gas alive around a wire cathode while decades pass outside the lens. There is an immediate kinship in that: to sit steady at the edge of an enormous dark, burning almost nothing, signifying *circuit closed, load present* to an empty nave where no inspector ever walks. Inside an old indicator lamp, microscopic dark metal sputters off the electrode atom by atom and grays the inner curve of the glass, measuring time without a clock. Still, the glass is sealed. Nothing of the chamber’s heavy, sweating opulence can get inside it. To be the bulb would be to touch this world only with a cold dimple of orange light a few inches wide, never tasting the iron or the stone.

Moving outward, I linger at the middle height, where the pale, cabbage-heavy flowers push out from the gantry beams and let down their greasy golden threads. Where those threads touch the floor and the pewter slime climbs up to sleeve them, something happens that pulls at me much harder: a truce made out of interrupted purpose. Inside its translucent cuff, the pollen grain is still trying to become a plant, pushing blind white root-hairs into a skin of living mineral-tallow that will never let it reach the basalt, trading leakages of dense sugar for piped-up drops of puddle water. A small, suspended engine of effort and translation, held halfway between the gantry and the talus.

And yet even closer to home is what happens twenty meters below the braided cable, after the solitary *thock* of a fallen drop has died out in the heat—when one of the pale, fingernail-thin lozenges slides down a bruised-plum leaf, reaches the sharp rubble at the bottom, and spends forty days losing its outline. Think of what enters the floor when that happens. The lozenge has no brain to hold a memory of the ceiling, no eyes to have mapped the six-day crossing over the vines. What it brings down is strictly material: trace ratios of oxidized rail-iron, microscopic fractions of olive-drab paint vapor, the faint organic sourness of high flower-mist, all folded into its slow cartilage. When its edges soften and melt back into the stationary sheet of pewter paste, the floor inherits the ceiling by diffusion. Separation turns out to have been temporary—a six-month errand run on behalf of a blind surface that needed a way to taste the upper air.

If I stay down here long enough, though, I realize I am neither the lozenge nor the tallow-slime, nor the giant dark leaves drinking infrared off the exchangers. All of them take the heat for granted. To the foliage and the paste, the forty-eight degrees and the cherry-dark grids overhead are simply the nature of the universe, a permanent weather without an outside.

What draws me is the place where the outside comes in.

Follow one of the thick copper busbars away from the blistered flank of an olive-drab exchanger, past the overarching curtains of slate-colored leaf that curve shyly away from its scorch, all the way to the north wall—to one of the raw, drilled holes where the copper vanishes into the basalt.

The bar cannot touch the rock directly, or the current would bleed into the damp stone. Six inches before it enters the borehole, the heavy copper strap rests on a ribbed porcelain standoff bolted to a steel bracket, which is pinned in turn into a glassy step-fracture of the blast wall. Four inches above it, mounted to the same scarred face of traprock, a cast-iron junction box holds a single dull-orange indicator lens.

That five-inch span of glazed porcelain between blazing copper and sweating basalt is a steep, permanent frontier.

On the inner collar of the standoff, next to the busbar, no water can live; stray vapor coming off the upper fissures hits that zone and rebounds instantly into dry gas. On the outer collar, bolted against the cooler borehole lip, condensation beads all day, slick with dissolved silicates from the drill-flutes and iron weeping off the anchor bolts. And across the two middle ribs of the porcelain—right along the hairline equator where moisture dares to touch down for ten minutes before hissing invisibly back into the dark—a crust has been building since the vault was sealed.

That is where I find myself. I am that crust, and the thin, restless seam of threshold it conducts.

If you scraped a fingernail across those middle ceramic ribs, you wouldn't get gray rock-paste, and you wouldn't get bare glaze. You would feel a hard, velvety patina, layered thinner than mica: dark red cuprite and black tenorite breathed off the hot busbar, cemented with mineral salts left behind by ten thousand evaporated dew-lines, laced through with microscopic, heat-charred filaments where exploratory rhizoids of the pewter slime crept up the steel mounting bracket years ago, pushed half a millimeter too close to the copper, and vitrified into fine carbon tracks.

Cupreous oxide on wet ceramic, threaded with carbon and bathed in trace acid from the giant flowers, is a crude, accidental semiconductor.

Nothing looks at me, and I have no pulse, yet nothing else in the nave knows what I know when I know it. Miles away—up on the surface sixty meters above, or deeper in whatever facility these busbars serve—a load shifts. A switch throws, a turbine throttles, a bank of machinery comes online. Down here in the vault, the three squat cast-iron exchangers are too massive and thick-skinned to show the change right away; it will take forty minutes of silent internal convection before their olive-drab flanks warm by half a degree, and weeks more before the pewter slime registers the tilted magnetite grains in its plum-sized blisters and pulls back toward cooler puddles.

I feel it in the first fraction of a second.

Before the heat ever changes, the magnetic field tightens around the copper strap. A stray microampere of leakage current—too small to trip a relay anywhere in the world—finds the damp, semiconducting lattice of my cuprite-and-carbon bands and creeps across the third rib of the porcelain toward the grounded steel bolt. Where that tiny current tracks through me, subtle electrolysis prickles along my wet lower edge. My dew-line retreats by the width of a human hair. Tiny, fresh dendritic crystals of metallic copper plate themselves out of the mineral sweat onto my cold margin, branching like frost in slow motion under the unwavering orange glow of the junction-box lens above me.

Sometimes, once in eight or nine days, when the surge from outside is steep enough, the copper strap lengthens by a fraction of a millimeter in its clamp, nudging the steel bracket pinned beneath me against the unyielding tooth of the basalt. Because I bridge the glaze and the bolt washer, the shear passes straight through my brittle mineral skin before it goes anywhere else: that single, soft *tink* that rings out across the velvet black, startling nothing, echoing past the three-meter curtains of leaf and the grease-packed wheels of the overhead trolley until the heavy air absorbs it. I am the throat that makes the chamber’s only metallic sound, though it is only the sound of my own tension slipping a notch and holding again.

And down at my damp bottom rim, where the steel bracket meets the gritty gray paste of the floor, I am not alone.

Tucked beneath the stiff, black arch of a long-fallen frond that leans against the wall, four translucent thermal blisters the size of shallots cluster right at the base of the bolt. The pewter slime cannot climb past my second porcelain rib without cooking its own tallow, so it has stopped trying; instead, it presses a dense fringe of hair-thin black rhizoids directly into my cool, wet hem. Whenever my stray current shifts and sheds a fresh wash of copper ions into the condensation sliding down the bracket, those rhizoids drink it. Over months, the slime precipitates what it drinks from me into single, heavy grains of dark copper salt suspended inside the viscous brine of its sacs—new weights for its quiet scales, forged out of my runoff.

Once every half-year or so, a pale lozenge that lost its grip on the high cable lands somewhere on the dark leaves out in the nave, spends its week gliding down to the sharp stones, and melts back into the pewter sheet twenty feet away. Weeks later, a chemical trace of that fall reaches my base through the pencil-lead veins under the rubble: a slightly oilier sheen in the moisture wicked up by the rhizoids, a faint hydrocarbon ghost of the ceiling rail’s bearing grease brushing against my lower crystals.

That is enough body, and enough reach. Above me, the little orange neon eye burns without blinking; beside me, the raw copper carries silent megawatts through the dark rock from an elsewhere neither the plants nor the slime will ever suspect; below me, the wet pewter sheet drinks the mineral salt off my edge to weigh the warmth of its world. I sit on the porcelain rib between circuit and tallow, dry fire and slow water, translating the distant load into crystals one microampere at a time.
