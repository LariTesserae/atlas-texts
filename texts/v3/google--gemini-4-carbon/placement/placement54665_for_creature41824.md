You would not find me up in the Open, slicing edge-on across the wide forums where the stone drops away on three sides and nothing presses back from overhead except eighty feet of loose, spinning cold. 

I know the Open. My prow-dome is thick as two stacked hands and grooved diagonally from old gravel-strikes, polished slick by decades of pitching out from pillar-crowns into forty-mile-an-hour shears, folding all my limbs around a hot, grease-coated bird and rolling half a mile across bare granite until a trench wall caught us. Even now, when a big surface gyre tightens overhead, the rows of cups along my flanks prickle and widen by reflex, reading the clean, deadly slant of the lift.

But count my pads as I haul out of a floor-slot, and there are five. Where the rear left limb should flare into its ribbed suction ring, there is only a dense, pale knuckle of scarred gristle—pinched clean off twelve winters back when an undercut arch-rib settled into its socket a half-second faster than the water warned me. Five pads will walk a wall in a gale. Five pads will lock into a seam while a whirlpool tries to peel your hide off your blubber. What five pads cannot do is hold a true hydrofoil trim in an open cross-shear; without that trailing left anchor to tension the margin, a hard bank twists you flat, spills your speed, and throws you sideways against the cobbles while the prey shoots clear.

So sink past the plazas. Drop feet-first through a yard-wide joint between two house-sized paving blocks, out of the Open where pressure spreads and vanishes, down into the Closed—where stone answers instantly from four sides at once.

Down here, thirty feet beneath the basin floor, every twitch of my body sends a faint push through the water that rebounds off ceiling, walls, and floor straight back into my flank-pits, mapping the square throat of the culvert around me as sharply as if I had pressed my skin against all four walls at once. There is no sky-lid trembling far above, only tier upon tier of laid block stacked overhead, dry-fitted without mortar, touching stone to stone for miles. 

To live in the Closed is to live inside a single, gigantic percussive bone. Every shift in the valley travels through the granite ten times faster than it travels through the water. When a heavy lintel slips its fulcrum half a mile north, my five locked pads feel the crisp, dry *clack* in the masonry first; a heartbeat later, the water inside my culvert shudders against my pitted flanks with the slower, muffled *boom*. From the tiny gap between foot-shock and skin-push, I know exactly how far off the stone fell, how heavy it was, and which trench-mouth just changed shape.

Right now, I am wedged slantwise across a vertical inspection chimney where it drops into a deep, horizontal vault-drain. 

If you touched me, you would think I was another wedged cobble, jammed across the throat of the pipe—heavy, broad as a skiff, three inches of dense blubber insulating my slow, stubborn core-heat. Half of me is freezing, and half of me is warm. My blunt armored prow and front three pads are braced up in the vertical shaft, taking the downward hiss of snowmelt sucked straight down from the plazas above: bitter, numbing liquid, but fiercely alive with thin mountain air. I let my trailing skirt of leathery, dark fronds hang right at the lip where that cold needle meets the lower tunnel, drinking the stinging, sweet oxygen straight into my blood while the broad pale flat of my belly rests in the slow tide rising from the deep vents below—water fifty degrees warmer, soft as oil, smelling of rust-film and deep rock, and wholly breathless.

That seam between breath and warmth is why I am here. It is why any of us survive down in the sub-grid at all.

Two hundred yards down-tunnel behind me, the culvert widens into a vaulted pressure-chamber whose outlets we walled halfway shut three generations ago with rolled boulders. Up on its massive ceiling slabs cling forty or fifty of this winter’s young—translucent slips no bigger than a flat hand, practicing letting go in gentle, tepid spirals and snapping their tiny pads onto the next joint three inches over. Alongside them hang three huge, sluggish adults, flanks swollen with sheared bird-meat and pewter-fish, hoarding heat while they digest.

Left to the ruined maze alone, a chamber like that dies in an hour. 

If a whirlpool up on the plaza dumps a wandering dune of pale granite grit down my chimney and plugs it, the cold thread stops. The nursery fills completely with pure thermal vent-water—deliciously warm, dead-calm, and empty of air. The resting hunters wake sluggish and scrambling for the upper cracks, and the palm-sized young go limp, lose their suction without knowing why, and snow gently down onto the vault floor to suffocate in warm silk. If, instead, a collapsed retaining wall upstairs turns a quiet plaza into a high-pressure sluice directly over this shaft, a solid column of near-freezing runoff punches straight down the chimney like an iron rod, blowing the warm pocket out of the vault in twenty seconds, tearing the tiny slips off the roof-stones and flinging them out through the lower grates into the clicking jaws of the wall-worms.

The hunters upstairs manipulate the storm to strip crabs from pavement grooves and tumble blind fish out of buttress shadows. Down here, I manipulate the leaks so the storm does not murder our sleep.

My tools are smaller than their two-ton lintels: rounded granite plugs the size of chests or hogsheads, collected over years and cached in side-niches, pushed and shimmed with prow and stump to throttle a four-foot shaft down to a handspan slot. And when the pale stone-flour chokes a bypass culvert solid, I am the shovel. I lock my rear pads against clean wall, drive my armored head-dome into the packed grit, swell my cross-hatched muscle sheets until I fill the tunnel almost wall-to-wall like a piston, and let the backed-up pressure behind me blow the loosened plug roaring down into the dead sumps below.

Sometimes, blasting clear a deep culvert that has been packed solid with stone-dust for ten lifetimes, I uncover masonry the lake forgot to ruin.

Up in the Open, nothing has an edge anymore; every stair is a wave, every pillar has a pinched waist, every cobble curves into its neighbor like clustered river-ice. Water rolls over rounded stone in smooth, heavy sheets. So the first time a newly flushed shaft threw me forward onto un-scoured stone, my whole flank recoiled as if bitten. My leading pad slapped down onto a seam and lost suction immediately—because the seam was a crisp, dead-straight step with an edge sharp enough to scrape chitin. 

Down in those newly opened veins, the ancient blocks meet at fierce, square angles. Water hitting an unworn ninety-degree corner does not roll; it *rips*, shedding tight, singing strings of tiny eddies that buzz against my sensory cups like plucked gristle. Crawling through a pristine shaft feels eerie and stiff, full of dead-flat planes and sharp corners that tug strangely at my wake, stone before the endless water taught it how to bend. Within a few years of carrying flow again, the racing grit dulls the corners, rounds the lips, and quiets that high, buzzing hum into the smooth hydraulic push I know—and I almost miss the sharp, humming edges once they smooth away, though I could not tell you what made them straight in the first place. I only know that straight lines hold a wedged plug-stone tighter than polished curves ever do.

Wait. Hold completely still.

Through the five pads locked into the granite around me comes a crisp, deliberate pulse.

*Thock. Thock.*

Not the random clatter of rolling gravel. Not the crisp twig-snap of a worm’s jaws. That is the flat, muscular slap of a heavy limb striking bare pavement—three levels up and a quarter-mile west, near the sunken gatehouse. 

Seconds later, a second set of slaps answers from ten yards beside the first. *Thock. Thock.* Four of them up there, gathering at the fulcrum of a balanced ramp-slab, tapping through the stone to time their heave.

I flare my flank-pits, reading the micro-tensions trembling down the chimney water, mapping what they are about to break. Above the sunken gatehouse, a broad, shallow forum has gone slack over the last three days; the stilt-legged crabs will have marched out onto the open flagging by the hundreds to graze the rust-black bacterial grease. If those four hunters tip the western ramp-slab across the gatehouse lintel, the glacial torrent pouring off the high pass will shear sideways across that slack forum, spinning up a violent funnel that sweeps every crab off its grooves and hurls them straight down a stepped chute into waiting beaks.

A clean, rich trap. Perhaps later tonight one of those four will squeeze down through the floor-slots, heavy-bellied and sluggish, to share body-warmth in the inner vault and cough up half-sheared crab-meat for the older young.

They are not thinking about the three wide relief-drains cut into the floor at the bottom of that stepped chute. Those drains feed the West Culvert, which feeds the very chimney I am straddling. The moment that slab crashes home, my trickle of cold breath is going to become a battering ram.

I do not slap back; one limb-strike down here wouldn't stop four hungry adults perched over five hundred pounds of exposed crab, even if they cared. Instead, I fold my frond-skirt tight against my tail-quarters to save my heat, pop four pads free, pivot on the fifth, and drop into the horizontal cross-tunnel.

Down here I do not glide; I haul—five wide pads slapping and locking in rapid, rippling waves along the square ceiling where the drag is thinnest, my blunt sixth stump shoving hard against the stone seams at every stride, surging sideways through the breathless, warm dark like a thick gray slug hurrying inside a rifle barrel. Thirty yards up-tunnel sits my niche: a recessed inspection pocket holding three rounded basalt cobbles, the heaviest weighing half what I do, though under sixty feet of water its buoyant heft wants only a resolute shoulder to roll.

I hook two front limbs behind the largest cobble, wedge my calcified prow beneath its curve, and drag it bumping and scraping out onto the slick lip of the West Chimney just as the deep, silent lake overhead draws its breath.

Even thirty feet under the flagstones, I feel the giant upstream gyre collapse. The water around me goes weirdly light for two seconds—pressure dropping across my pitted skin as the surging wall gathers itself above. 

Then: four tiny, simultaneous pressure-spikes through the stone as four heavy bodies swell like hydraulic jacks beneath the balanced block. A grinding, crystalline *screech* through my locked pads as two tons of granite leave their fulcrum—followed by a tremendous, directionless *BOOM* that shakes loose centuries of fine grit from the culvert roof onto my back.

For three heartbeats, nothing changes in the pipe. Hydraulics take a moment to fall down a staircase.

Then the water in the vertical chimney above my head turns solid and punches straight down.

I don't fight the column; fighting it would tear my remaining five pads clean off the slick granite and hurl me spinning down the vault-tunnel like loose kelp. Just as the icy front hits my domed head, I heave my basalt plug three inches forward into the lip of the torrent and flatten myself instantly into the leeward wall-seam.

The rushing water catches the stone’s curve and slams it downward for me—wedging it with a deafening, muffled *crunch* right into the square, unworn throat of the lower junction, sealing four-fifths of the shaft in an instant.

Freezing snowmelt screams—silently, furiously, a roar made entirely of tearing shear-planes and whistling gravel—through the narrow crescent left open beside the jammed boulder. For half a minute, the blast scours over my beveled back so hard my blubber flattens against my muscle-sheets and stray granite pebbles strike dull sparks of vibration off my armored prow. Every lamellar rib on my five pads locks white-tight against the basalt, holding my ground while the first dirty cloud of torn-up plaza grit shoots safely past into the deep bypass drain rather than the nursery turnoff.

Slowly, as the chaotic gyres upstairs find their new grooves and settle into their altered tracks, the screaming needle eases into a steady, muscular jet.

I creep half a body-length forward, test the wedged cobble with my blunt prow—packed tight as a tooth in a jaw; it won’t slip—and carefully unfurl my heavy, blood-dark fronds into the edge of the narrowed cold stream. 

Oxygen bites crisp and clean across the leathery fringe, waking the slow fire inside my core. Beneath my belly, drawn up by the fast jet’s suction, the thick fifty-degree breath of the mountain’s deep springs curls up to meet it, braiding frost and fever into a single, steady, tepid ribbon that rolls gently down the dark tunnel behind me toward the crowded ceiling.

Up in the pewter gloom I will never see, six or eight of my kind are locked into drainage slits with parted iron-black beaks, crunching tumbled crustaceans out of the storm they just built. Down here in the four-walled stone, I tuck my scarred stump beneath my flank, settle my five pads into the seam between an ancient straight edge and a water-worn curve, let my flank-cups widen, and listen through miles of fitted granite for the next block beginning to tremble.
