I am sitting cross-legged on the corner of a swaying chitin deck, eight feet above the rasping carpet of crabs, with a split burrower-rib wedged firmly between my calloused grey heels. 

My shins are bare and mottled where the tallow-and-clay paste has rubbed thin against the stilt-cuffs, leaving angry, pale-pink rings of chafed skin that sting in the ammonia-heavy updraft. I am forty—which here means my knees click louder than the plumb-rattle beneath the floorboards, and my left hip no longer has the quick, rolling snap needed to throw myself backward off a bursting mud-blister when a four-hundred-pound amphibian breaches. 

A ridge of pale, puckered scar tissue runs from the back of my left thigh up to my ribs, stiff as cured rawhide—the souvenir of a marl-dome that collapsed inward instead of out six seasons ago, dropping me hip-deep into scalding slurry before the haulers could drag me clear by my vest-straps. The muscle underneath cooked just enough to draw tight. Now, on the flats, I limp even on three-foot stilts, swinging my left mud-shoe wide in a slow, skimming arc like a crippled wading-bird.

So I don’t carry the harpoon anymore. I make the feet.

In my lap sits a half-woven stilt-basket, round as a soup-kettle lid, its ribs made from the curved, springy margin-plates of the largest crustaceans, steamed soft over a dry-hisser and lashed at the hub with wet sinew that stinks of sour fat and sulfur. My thumbnails are thick as horn and stained deep ochre from prying split-chitin; I don’t even need the bone awl tucked behind my ear to open a gap in the weave anymore. I just twist the thumb in, shove the slick, wet strip of flyer-wing leather through, and pull it until it squeaks. 

Down below, through the narrow, bone-slatted gaps of the platform floor, the world is doing its endless, wet boiling. Thirty paces to the east, a broad yellow vent has been coughing since midday—*thud-slop, thud-slop*—throwing heavy pancakes of sulfur-streaked clay onto a shelf that was flat this morning. Every time it heaves, a wave of pale crabs skitters outward with a dry, clattering roar like gravel dumped down a chute, and three or four heavy flyers drop out of the pearlescent glare to hammer clumsily at the stragglers before flapping back up into the warm haze. One of them bounces off the cured femur-leg right beneath my seat—*thock*—and tumbles wing-over-head into the waiting mandibles below. Within four breaths, there is nothing left of it but a scrap of leathery wing-membrane jerking across the backs of fifty feeding shells.

I barely squint down at it. My eyelids are heavy, greased folds; through the double-thicket of my stiff lashes, the bright white floor and the milky, fractured ceiling three hundred feet overhead are reduced to narrow ribbons of pearly grey, cut across by the dark, jagged hairline cracks in the selenite roof.

Wait. I tilt my chin up, letting my hands go still on the basket-rim, and narrow my gaze until the glare breaks into sharp contrast. 

High up—maybe half a mile toward the basin’s western wall, where the gypsum roof thickens and droops in milky, translucent udders—there is a dark smudge shaped like a three-toed foot. Yesterday, the outer toe ended cleanly at a blue-white vein. Today, a black hair-crack has forked off its tip, spidering twenty yards eastward toward the center span. 

I reach down without looking, pick up my scraping-stone, and tap three sharp, flat beats against the hollow femur-post supporting our tent-hoop. *Tak-tak-tak.*

Two platforms over, across forty feet of rising steam and colliding flyers, Old Brine-Ear pauses with a strip of salted amphibian meat halfway to his mouth. He turns his broad, clay-caked skull toward me, chin tilted back to peer through his own lash-slits. 

I lift my right hand, make the three-toed smudge with stiff fingers, flick my thumb eastward twice, and drop my open palm flat against my knee. *Crack walking east. Big slab. Three days, maybe four, before it calves.*

Brine-Ear watches, chews once, and gives a short, downward chop of his forearm toward the flats below that sector, where two weir-teams are currently wedging travertine plates into a fresh carbonate seep. *Let them finish the pan,* his hand says, fingers curling into a scoop. *Harvest tomorrow, pull stakes before it drops.*

I grunt—a sound nobody hears past the edge of my own mat—and go back to pulling sinew. When a slab the size of twenty houses lets go from the ceiling, you don't want to be within five hundred paces of the splash. Not just for the razor-storm of shattered selenite that slices hide tents to ribbons, but because the sheer weight of it punches straight through the basin crust, turning half a mile of solid gypsum bench into a pitching, scalding sea of displaced subterranean mud that swallows weirs, tripods, and slow walkers alike. Better to let the crabs have that whole western shelf by midweek. After the dust settles and the mud flash-crusts around the fallen crystal spire, we’ll wade back in and quarry fresh shaving-flakes off its flanks for a month.

A sharp, rhythmic *clack—clack-clack* cuts up through the timber-rasp of the shells below. 

My nephew is home early. I lean over the braided-hide rim of the deck and peer straight down. Three kids—twelve, maybe thirteen, all knees and big grey hands and newly shaved scalps shining with fresh grease—are wading single-file between the platform legs, carrying two heavy splint-baskets slung on a chitin pole between them. The boy in front, my sister’s eldest, is walking crooked. Every third step, his right stilt sinks two inches deeper into the soft marl than his left, forcing him to wrench his hip sideways and jab his balancing-pole hard against a cooling ridge to keep from pitching face-first into the orange-banded centipedes cruising the seepage cracks.

He stops under the ladder-drop, tilts his broad, tallow-smeared face up at me, and slaps the shaft of his right stilt in disgust. Even from eight feet up, I can see what happened: the outer rim of his mud-shoe has snapped clean through where a travertine fin caught it sideways, and half the basket is dragging like a broken wing, clogged with twenty pounds of fast-hardening grey cement.

*Take it off,* I sign down to him, hooking two fingers and yanking upward. *Throw it.*

He braces his good stilt against one of the house-legs, unlashes the calf-thongs and the hip-brace with furious, nimble flicks, and balances stork-like on one bone leg while he pries his bare, calloused heel out of the cup. He swings the heavy shaft back and heaves it upward. I catch the slick rib-bone one-handed before the mud-caked basket can brain me, dragging it onto the deck. 

He climbs the knotted rawhide ladder using only his arms and his one bare foot, hauling his unshod leg behind him while his two friends pass the crab-baskets up the rope-pulley on the far side. By the time he tumbles onto the springy chitin slats, breathing hard through wide, sulfur-crusted nostrils, I already have my flint-knife wedged into the hub-lashings of his ruined shoe, sawing through the calcified sinew.

" dome-crust looked hard," he mutters, pitching his voice into that low, chesty hum we use up here so it travels through the deck-slats as much as the air, his big hands flying in embarrassment to explain before I can scold him. *Grey skin on top, no steam, crabs thick on it—stepped wide—* his left palm slices downward through his right fingers *—knife-fin underneath the paste.*

"Crabs walk on spit," I murmur back, flicking a thumb toward his chest: *You weigh more than spit.* 

I pry the shattered chitin-splints away from the bone foot-peg, cracking off chunks of warm, half-set marl with the butt of my knife and tossing them over the edge—listening with faint satisfaction to the *clatter-scritch* below as fifty blind crabs pile onto the falling stones just in case they're edible. The bone shaft itself—a good, dense fore-rib from an old bull burrower I helped butcher two years before my hip went—is sound, just scored white along the curve where the travertine bit it.

I toss the ruined basket onto the scrap-hide to be burned for glue-ash or crushed for pot-temper, and reach behind me for the new shoe I’ve been weaving all afternoon. It’s slightly wide for a thirteen-year-old’s stride, built for a grown harvester carrying eighty pounds of wet shell on his back, which means he’ll have to swing his knees wide like an old man to keep the rims from clipping each other in the steam. Good. It’ll keep him from running across grey blisters like a scatterbrained flyer.

"Hold the shaft," I tell him, tapping the bone with my knuckle. 

He drops cross-legged opposite me, clamping the cured rib between his knees and bracing it with both oversized, horn-nailed hands while I seat the peg into the fresh, wet sinew-hub of the new basket. Up close, he smells like all of us: rancid amphibian tallow, sour sweat, hot chalk, and the sharp, sweet-metallic tang of crushed crab-blood drying on his scale-vest. Behind his ears, the clay-grease has caked into pale grey ripples that crack when he clenches his jaw. 

As I wind the wet rawhide binding tight around the peg, pulling with the heavy, thick-knuckled strength of my shoulders until the bone groans slightly in his grip, the light inside the basin shifts. 

You don't see the sun go down; there is no west to look toward, only the great, milky ceiling losing its fierce inner fire all at once, like hot iron cooling through pearl to dove-grey, then to a deep, bruised violet that makes the rising steam-plumes vanish into the gloom. Down on the floor, the flyers are dropping out of the air by the thousands, scrambling onto the dry crests of older travertine ridges to fold their leathery wings, their frantic mid-air battering dying down until only the endless, dry sandpaper roar of the crustacean carpet remains—and beneath it, clearer now in the dusk, the wet, subterranean *blup... blup-hiss* of the valley digesting itself.

Along the crescent lips of our three nearest weirs, faint ribbons of ghostly blue-green bacterial light begin to glimmer through the dark, outlining the black, heaving mounds of shells trapped in the cooling pans.

Below the center of our platform, hanging six inches under the slats in the warm draft, the sulfur lump inside the dry carapace gives a single, tiny *tink*. 

Both the boy’s hands freeze on the stilt-shaft. My thumb stops mid-wrap. Neither of us breathes; we drop our chins, pressing our bare heels flat against the woven chitin floorboards, listening with the bones of our ankles.

Wait. 

*Tink-tink.* Then a slow, dry *rrrrr-up* as the lump of sulfur leans against the hollow shell wall and stays there, humming a faint, papery note straight up the four cured femurs of the house and into our sit-bones. 

The three-day-old gypsum bench beneath our northeast leg isn't collapsing—that’s a sudden drop and a sharp crack. It’s swelling. A new marl-blister is pushing up from the warm plumbing twenty feet down, tilting our whole cranefly perch slowly, gently toward the cooling mud-pans in the dark. By midnight, the tilt will snap the cross-braces; by morning, where our ladder hangs will be a steaming dome of grey soup crawling with centipedes.

Nobody sighs. Across the six-foot gap to my sister’s platform, I already hear the dry *shhh-ip* of oiled wing-quilts being rolled tight and tied off with quick-slip toggles. 

I don't hurry the final knot on the boy’s stilt-shoe, and I don't leave it sloppy; a bad binding in the dark breaks ankles. Three hard wraps, tuck the tail under with the bone awl, bite the excess sinew clean off with my back molars, and slap the shaft twice. *Done.*

He snatches it back, already sliding toward the ladder-edge to strap his legs on. I reach into the grease-crock wedged beside the tent-hoop, scoop two thick fingers of cold, gritty fat, and smear it briskly into the stiff scar-seam along my bad hip before reaching for my own stilts—the wide-shod, heavy-poled pair with the extra-broad baskets I wove myself, built not for speed, but so an old crust-maker can carry the pot-netting and the tool-roll across softening stone without sinking a hair’s breadth deeper than the rest of the house.

I swing my strapped legs over the edge into the warm, ammonia-thick dark, hook my bone-tipped pole over my forearm, and tap the rim of my left shoe twice against the northeast femur-post so the others down in the clicking blackness know I’m coming down the tilt-side—already leaning my weight out to feel, through three feet of hollow bone and woven shell, which way the new rock wants us to walk.
