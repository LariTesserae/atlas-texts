The seam along my dorsal ridge parted eleven days ago, though I do not count days; I count the slow slackening of pressure behind my eyes, and the number of times a heavy loom-grazer outside my wall has paused its rasping to shift its weight across the slate.

I am inside the dark within the dark. 

Around me, fitted so tight that not even the electrostatic filaments of the small, blind scavengers can thread the mortarless joints, are forty-two courses of split shale. I remember the edge of every slab. Before the splitting began, when my old plates had grown so tight at the gussets that my oil ran hot just climbing a thirty-degree scree, I spent an age with my four-thumbed clamps wedging those slabs from a fault-line down-chimney. Two of my kin handed the last three ceiling-stones down to me through the apex. Before they dropped the keystone into its friction-seat, we crossed brow-wires through the narrowing gap. 

*Good cleavage on the north face,* my brood-parent tapped into my filament, her static dry and crisp with high-iron health. *We take the silk-bridge three hundred yards up-fault. The hound-spiders are shifting anchor. Be hard when we return.*

Then the stone settled with a soft *chink* of quartz dust, and the capacitance of the wide vault vanished. 

Now there is only the smell of my own opening—a heavy, metallic tang of warm hydrocarbon—and the terrible, luxurious softness of my back. 

Out there, softness is death. Out there, every angle is an ambush of unweathered glass; to slip half an inch on a mica blade is to feel the sudden, irreversible drop in hydraulic pressure as your life beads out onto rock flour that has never known rain, where forty brittle crabs will unlock their legs at once to fight over the damp grey clump before you have even finished falling. For two hundred years—if you measure by the upper world’s spinning, which I do not—my world was rigidity, leverage, and ratchet. I knew myself as a series of stone-hard planes and dry leather bellows.

Right now, I am naked from cowl to vent. 

My old shell lies three inches behind me, split cleanly down the median line like a cracked pod, still holding the crouched posture in which I locked its struts before pulling myself free. My new skin is the texture of heavy, oiled parchment. If a splinter of selenite fell from my ceiling right now, it would pierce me to the core-vessel. Because I have no rigid frame to crank against, I cannot use my tendons; I am slumped low over my folded walking-legs on a bed of velvet-glider dust I ground fine beforehand so no stray shard in the floor could prick my sternum.

It is an astonishing, dizzying thing to feel the air directly. Thirty-four degrees, motionless, pressing uniformly against four square feet of bare, curing chitin. Every micro-shift of my internal oil—pumped in slow, lazy contractions to stretch the pleats of my new, larger carapace before the minerals cross-link and set—sends a dull, warm ache through nerves that usually sit beneath half an inch of horn.

I am not empty-minded in the dark. A maker’s mind does not drift; it maps.

Because I cannot move without tearing my curing gussets, I walk the vault behind my silvered eye-pits. My wrist-filaments are coiled neatly beneath my chin to keep them from brushing the rough slate walls while their tips harden, yet they twitch faintly with phantom static as I replay the territories I hold in trust.

I walk the Great Selenite Chute four miles east-by-down, where seven hundred needles of translucent gypsum lean together like felled trees, their bases glowing dull, steady amber from the crush of the ceiling-root. You have to climb that chute upside down, hooking your lower struts into the cleave-steps my ancestors chipped into the undersides of the crystals, because the upper faces are coated in six inches of impalpable grey dust that hides razor-wire snares left by a lineage we do not cross wires with. 

I check the balances. Seven hundred paces down from the amber pillar sits a boulder of black basalt sheared flat as an anvil. On its west corner: three shards of smoky quartz, leaned tip-to-tip. I placed the third shard myself, long ago, using the tiny, measured release of my index-clamp so I would not jar the two beneath it. The left shard has a thumb-wide notch of iron pyrite on its ventral cleave; the right is pure glass, tilted twelve degrees off plumb toward a narrow, vertical slot between fallen ceiling-blocks. 

*Do not descend,* it reads to any cautious hand feeling through the blackness. *Dead air in the sump. Heavy gas. Three husks at the bottom, untapped, out of reach.*

Thinking of untapped husks makes my sternal proboscis stir slightly in its sheath. A reflex of ancient economy. Even lying here with a mound of dry sulfur-crust powder within reach of my chest-mandibles, the thought of nine or twelve ounces of good, amber oil sitting sealed inside dead shells down in a carbon-dioxide pocket—forever beyond the reach of a copper needle because anything that climbs down there suffocates before its slow tracheae can diffuse a warning—feels like an itch in a phantom limb. Nothing in our world vanishes, yet things can become unreachable. That is our only true word for *gone*.

*Tink.*

Every phantom walk vanishes from my head. My silvered retinas flare with nothing, staring into pitch black, while the tiny acoustic membranes behind my cowl pull taut.

That was not inside my cairn. That was forty feet away, out on the flaking floor at the junction of the twin sulfur spires. 

Someone—or something—just stepped on the skirt of struck obsidian chips I left around my anvil-stone.

I do not breathe faster; I do not have lungs to race. My heart-tube does not hammer, because hammering heats the blood. Instead, I go even stiller than stone, halting the slow peristalsis of oil into my hardening dorsal plates. 

*Crunch. Shhhk-tink.*

My brow-filaments uncoil half an inch before I force them down lest they scrape my own slate wall and answer the sound. 

Nothing in the vault walks like that. 

A hound-spider spreads its weight across eight wide, cushioned tips; when it leaves its metallic curtain to cross the floor, it makes a sound like dry paper dragged over sand, with ten minutes between steps. A giant scissor-centipede flows in a continuous, low-frequency *tic-tic-tic-tic* of forty claws testing purchase before committing weight. Even a careless juvenile of my own kind, fresh from the brood-plate and stupid with unmapped space, places a strut, waits for the glass chip beneath it to settle, and locks its knee before lifting the next.

This thing out on my flaking floor is *falling forward* onto two points, catching itself, breaking twenty ribbons of obsidian at once, grinding them together with sickening, wasteful violence, and immediately lifting its weight to smash down again half a second later. 

*Crunch. Crunch. Crunch.*

The rhythm is frantic, insane. It must be burning joules like a sheared tectonic fault. The sheer kinetic waste of those footsteps makes my unhardened skin prickle with revulsion. How has it not cooked itself inside its shell?

Then it stops. Right beside my anvil-stone. Eight paces from the base of my dome.

For twenty counts, the normal world resumes. High up on the sulfur spire, a flattened grazer rasps twice at the rust-film—*shhhp, shhhp*—and goes silent, startled by the giant vibration that just rattled the scree. Far off, maybe sixty paces down the chimney, one of my own flint chimes gives a microscopic, sympathetic *ping* as a velvet glider lands on its wire and freezes.

Then the thing outside my wall makes a sound I have no geometry for.

*Hhhhhhh—khhh.*

A wet, rushing wind. A tiny, rhythmic gale forced in and out of a soft, slick tube. 

Even through eight inches of dry-stacked shale, my electrostatic bristles stiffen. The air seeped between my stones hasn't changed temperature yet, not quite, though if it stays there long enough its waste-heat will bleed into the stagnant thirty-four-degree wool around my shelter. What hits me first is a single, stray molecule of *vapor* drifting through a hairline chink between course twelve and course thirteen.

Water. Loose, unbound, escaping wildly into the kiln-ash air. 

Suddenly my silvered eye-pits catch something impossible. Through that same hairline chink—a gap thinner than a drawn glass wire, crooked through eight inches of overlapped stone—a needle of brilliance stabs into my tomb. It is not the dim, static amber of stressed quartz, and it is not the brief, cold-blue blink of a hunter’s spine-lure. It is blinding, scorching white, sweeping wildly across the outside of my cairn like a physical blow before vanishing, leaving purple afterimages burned onto retinas meant only to read tectonic pressure in pitch blackness.

The light sweeps back. Holds steady on my stones.

Out there, eight feet away, this burning, roaring, leaking thing has stopped to examine my kiln. 

I lie flat on my bed of powdered glider-shell, half-hardened, my dorsal plates the consistency of horn soaked in warm oil—tough enough now to hold my shape, not yet hard enough to turn a copper punch or bear the full ratchet-tension of my shoulders. Behind me sits my discarded carapace, empty and light as dry mica. Beside my folded left clamp rests a single nine-inch pry-bar of dense greenstone I used to snug the lower courses from within. 

A pebble of obsidian shifts outside. *Crunch.* Closer. Four feet away.

Now my wrist-filaments, shielded though they are by slate, pick up the monstrous, bulging electrostatic field of it. It is an ocean wrapped in membrane. A vertical column of conductive, salty fluid five or six feet tall, pulsing sixty, seventy times a minute—*thump, thump, thump*—sloshing gallons of free solvent inside a hide so thin it has had to wrap itself in dead, woven fibers just to cross the scree without spilling itself open.

A hand—soft, five-branched, radiating thirty-seven degrees of furnace-heat—touches the outside of my wall at course fourteen. 

I feel the stone drink its warmth. Through the rock, my filaments read the trembling, continuous micro-tremor of muscles that have no ratchets, muscles that must burn fuel constantly just to hold their own bones upright. It leans a little weight against my slate. It breathes out—*hhhhhhh*—losing another precious thimbleful of vapor to the dry dark, never to get it back.

It thinks this is a ruin. Or a cairn of dead markers. In a world without rot, where a shelter left three thousand years ago looks identical to one sealed eleven days back, it has no way of knowing that three inches behind the slab its warm palm is resting on, six folded limbs are tucked tight beneath a curing wedge-shaped cowl, listening to the frantic, tragic roar of its blood.

I do not reach for my greenstone bar. Even if it tried to lever a slab loose right now, dry-stone laid from the inside locks under outer pressure; it would take it an hour of smashing to breach my dome, and long before an hour was up, that kind of exertion in thirty-four-degree air would drop it twitching into the obsidian chips, boiling inside its own skin.

And even if it turns away—which it is doing now, lifting its hot palm, sending its scorching white beam jittering up toward the spider-curtains overhead, taking another loud, shattering step across my good flaking-chips—it does not matter. 

Look how fast it moves. Listen to how wet it breathes. Count the joules it throws away just standing in the dark. 

It cannot climb fifty miles of vertical razor-maze without tearing its soft bags open, and there is not a drop of free water in nine thousand miles of basalt to refill what it exhales every ten seconds. 

*Crunch. Crunch. Tink.*

The footsteps recede down-chimney, heading toward the black basalt boulder where my three shards of smoky quartz sit balanced tip-to-tip. It will shine its terrible light on them and see only three pretty stones. Perhaps its clumsy shoulder will brush them down, erasing three centuries of warning about the dead-air sump below; perhaps it will walk right into the sump itself, or snag a guywire belonging to the hound-spiders, or simply sit down two miles from here when its carried flasks run dry and its blood thickens to tar.

I settle my chin back onto my folded clamps. Slowly, deliberately, I resume the peristaltic squeeze along my core-vessel, pushing warm, heavy oil into the pleats of my new armor, feeling the keratin cross-link molecule by molecule in the dark.

I have another hundred days to cure before my joints can take the strain of levering out my three base-stones. There is no hurry. My kin are three hundred yards up-fault, splicing cables in the silence; when they return, we will cross brow-wires in the dark, and I will draw them a map of the strange, burning vessel that passed my door, and calculate—by weight, stride, and rate of evaporation—within half a mile of where its husk will be waiting for our needles.
