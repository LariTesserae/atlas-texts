Not down in the moss. Down on the basin floor everything lives by the thirty-second sprint—crop the silver leaves before the blue line takes your toes, flatten your ribs to the wet plate before the blood slows, snap the moth, flick the tail, dive when the sky drops. To live down there your hind legs have to push equally hard off slick slate, and one of mine healed crooked two winters back when a frost-wedged slab let go inside a chimney and pinned my knee until dusk. On flat talus I hitch sideways, half a heartbeat late to cover. Down there, half a heartbeat ends in the soft, dry rustle of owl-fringed primaries against velvet foliage.

And not up in the oiled skins of the lowlanders, either, though when they drag themselves over the eastern lip, retching dryly onto the clean black plates and peering out through narrow horizontal cuts in slabs of bleached bone, I understand their eyes better than the rest of my troop does. Looking through bone-slits is very close to looking through a four-way shuttered pupil under an amber lid. Still, they carry too much meat on them, their breath tears at their throats, and they belong to a wider, blurrier world down below where distances soften into haze. I would not know what to do with a horizon.

Where I find myself is twenty-five feet up the sheer western wall, above the damp reach of the vents, tucked onto a fresh chisel-ledge no wider than a forearm.

On flat rock I am slow, so I stopped using flat rock. Up on the vertical face, legs are mostly ballast; everything is collarbone, long tendon, and the dry, horny hook of nail-tips catching fractures the thickness of a thumbnail. Above my head, a stepped overhang of black andesite juts out seven inches. That seven-inch lip means no pale falcon can fold its wings and drop cleanly onto my spine without shattering its breastbone against sheer stone. It also means my crouched body stays locked in deep, biting shade while my long forearms reach two inches past the shadow-boundary into the raw flood of noon, resting on the three-stone tripod the climbers set here last summer.

I am one of the older silver ones—crusty around the shoulders with grey vent-salts, sealed ear-valves scarred flat, chest heaving in fast, shallow sips that never waste a scrap of vapor on sound—who stopped competing for the rosette thickets and became, instead, an appendage of the mirrors.

At first it was only warmth and bribe.

If you cannot forage fast enough along the moving edge of the sun before the cold catches you, you die in winter unless the rest of the forty let you wedge all the way into the back of a sleeping-chimney at dusk, rather than leaving you near the freezing lip where rime builds on your eyelashes before morning. The elders had already learned to nudge the humans’ dishes after a harvest, aiming light into the sleeping-cracks or onto shaded moss to draw moths. What none of them had the patience to do was stay up on the dry, barren rock all day and *track*.

A stone dish moved once at mid-morning holds its mark for twelve minutes. Then the sun shifts across the jagged crown above, and the reflected coin creeps up the opposite wall, sliding uselessly off the mouth of the sleeping-fissure onto solid rock while the interior chills again.

Because I have nowhere faster to be, I stay with the light.

Every four or five minutes, keeping my hollow silver coat cinched tight around my ribs and my amber membranes drawn halfway across my eyes, I extend two black, scaly fingers into the blistering white-violet glare and tap the edge of the polished milky quartz by the breadth of a grass-stem. All morning, while the shadow sweeps across the nave below, the pale oval of stolen sun stays pinned squarely inside the throat of our deepest winter fissure fifty feet away, baking the dark stone six feet inward like an oven. Down at the margin of another beam lower down—where I keep a second, smoky flake of volcanic glass angled toward a wet overhang—three juveniles crouch in the gloom, plucking soot-black moths out of my artificial noon as fast as the insects drift into it. When I climb down at last light, slow and three-legged down the hairline cracks, the juveniles give way without baring their pale gums, and the big females part their salt-stiffened coats to let me slip straight to the back of the chimney, pressing my soot-black belly skin directly against the rock I spent six hours heating for them.

That would have been a whole life—a small, clear bargain with gravity and kin. Keep the bedroom warm; get the innermost niche.

What changed was spending so many hours holding different pieces of stone in the glare when no one needed moths.

The rest of the troop treats every bright thing the humans leave behind as the same tool: a scoop for sun. Hang on the wall all day with nothing under your knuckles but rock, however, and you find out there are four distinct kinds of light in this basin, and each one behaves differently in the thin air.

Milky quartz is thick and forgiving. It scatters the ultraviolet sting into a soft, butter-white pool that warms rock evenly and never startles a feeding hare.

Volcanic glass—obsidian chipped from deeper veins down the mountain—is greedy. It drinks half the sun into its own dark body before throwing the rest across the basin as a dim, sharp-edged ghost. The other elders ignore the obsidian flakes whenever quartz is near, because glass makes a poor heater. Up on my freezing shelf, though, I keep a palm-sized blade of it tucked under my wrist: in raw sunlight the black glass grows almost hot to the touch, warming the stiff knuckles of my hands without forcing me to open my coat.

Hammered native copper, stolen once from the rib-bone shrine at the eastern notch before an elder dropped it onto a mid-wall shelf, throws a fierce, hot, orange-red spot—almost the exact arterial crimson of an opened wrist.

And split mica shivers. Even when wedged tight between two splinters of slate, the thin, peeling layers catch the faint upward drift of vapor from the fault lines below and tremble against one another, throwing nervous, fluttering white knives across the dark stone.

It was the copper and the mica that turned me from a stove-tender into something else.

Watch the basin from twenty-five feet up and you see the whole clock at once. Down among the three-foot bolsters of emerald moss and waist-high silver thickets, a monkey on all fours sees six yards ahead and straight up; the talus plates block the rest. From my shelf, I see the hares flashing their white tail-undersides along three separate fault cracks. I see the shadow-line eating eastward inch by inch. And because I am halfway to their ceiling, I see the pale grey falcons shift their weight on the high rim-notches two seconds before they fold their wings and drop.

The first time I saw a female falcon tip forward out of the white-violet glare directly above two half-grown juveniles licking water from a gut-skin sinew cord, my arm moved by instinct to flash my crimson inner wrist.

Before my mantle parted—before I dumped a single precious breath’s worth of blood-heat out of my bare capillaries into the sub-zero air—my hand was already resting on the mica sheet.

I tilted it upward instead of down.

A silent, needle-thin lance of raw solar glare shot straight up from the shadowed wall and struck the stooping bird directly across her huge, shade-widened eyes mid-drop. She flared violently in the thin air without a sound—only a panicked, silent explosion of pale feathers braking against nothing—missed the stone bowl by ten feet, and banked clumsily back toward the upper notches without touching slate. Down below, the two juveniles hadn't even looked up from the wet sinew. My fur coat had never opened. My chest had remained warm.

After that, I began collecting shims.

Like the rest of my kind, if I try to stack flat andesite plates into a windbreak on the floor, they slide apart; my hands do not understand walls. What my nails understand intimately is the *wedge*. Having pried dozens of the humans’ carved horn pins out of crevices to inspect them, I started picking up tiny, sharp triangles of shattered slate and tucking them into a hairline crack beside my hip. With three splinters of slate wedged under a flake of stone, you can lock a beam onto a moss bank while freeing both hands, or tip it half an inch to speak across forty yards of freezing air without losing a drop of body heat.

Now the basin floor looks up before it moves.

At dead noon—the brief, shadowless twenty minutes when the entire cleft brims with blinding vertical glare and no wall throws shade anywhere—I make my one crossing of the day. While the falcons sit dazzled on the rim and the troop presses into the wet fissures, I swing down the western fractures by my long arms, hitch rapidly along a steaming crack at the center of the nave, drink from a stone bowl, stuff four swollen, glowing stalks of bladder-sedge into my cheek pouches to chew the cool, clear jelly later, and haul myself up onto a matching chisel-shelf on the eastern wall so the afternoon sun stays at my back.

From there, as the blue-black shadow creeps out from the western cliff and drives everything below into its afternoon frenzy, my hands work the tripods almost continuously.

One steady white pool of quartz stays trained on the sleeping-chimney. Beside me, held loose under two scaly thumbs, sit a splinter of mica and the tarnished disc of copper.

Two quick white winks against the wet boulder beside the gut-skin drip: *clear sky, drink now.*

A sudden spot of copper-red dropped directly onto the silver fur of a cousin’s back when he wanders too close to an exposed scree slope: *sky-hunter shifting on the northern tooth; flatten.*

And sometimes—when the troop is fed and safe, and my belly is full of sedge-mucilage, and twenty minutes remain before the sun catches on the western teeth—something happens that has nothing to do with hares, falcons, or warm chimneys.

I take the dark flake of volcanic glass—the dim one that holds an edge—and aim its pale, smoky rectangle onto a broad, dry slab of shaded black andesite down near the quiet end of the cleft where no moss grows. Then I hold a peeled film of split mica just in front of the obsidian’s reflection, twisting my hooked black fingers slowly between the two stones.

Down on the black slate forty feet away, a sharp dark shadow of a hand—huge, four-fingered, impossibly long-jointed, haloed in faint violet rainbows from the split mineral—opens and closes against the unweathered rock where no hand is touching. None of the others look at it; there is nothing there to eat and nothing to fear. Only once, a young male crouched near a rosette thicket watched the giant shadow-fingers flex across the barren stone, looked up at my tiny silver lump high on the eastern wall, looked back down at the dark projection on the rock, and slowly opened and closed his own scaly black palm against his chest fur—not signaling *danger* or *mine*, just testing the hinge of it.

As for the lowlanders, they came back once before the early snows, four of them hauling themselves over the eastern notch in greased hides, coughing thin pink froth onto the scree and muttering behind their frosted mittens.

Wedged deep inside a narrow split ten feet above my eastern shelf, peering out through the amber shield of my third lids, I watched an old climber with gray braids sticking out from his hood pull himself up to inspect my tripod. He stopped when he saw the quartz dish angled away from his pale, withered cluster of medicinal glass-stalks. He reached out a thick leather glove to knock it back into place—and paused.

His slotted bone goggles bent low over the base of the three-stone stand, where four neat, tiny triangles of black slate had been driven tight under the outer leg to keep the precious quartz from sliding off the rime-slicked rock, and beside them sat the lost copper disc from the shrine and a neat stack of spare stone shims.

He stayed on the wall a long time, breathing hard in the thin cold, turning his bone-slitted face slowly left and right along the empty black fractures where forty pairs of black-scleraed eyes gave back no shine at all.

Before they scrambled back out through the notch ahead of the killing dusk, he climbed up to that ledge a second time. He angled his good quartz dish squarely back onto his medicinal herbs and wedged it tight so I could not easily budge it. Right beside it, though—three inches to the left on the bare chisel-mark, already propped on three flat pebbles of its own—he set down a second, cracked round of milky quartz from his pouch, aimed nowhere at all, waiting to be turned.

When the last oiled hood vanished behind the bleached ribs at the eastern rim and the flat *clack* of their boots died on the outer scree, I unfolded from my crack before the shadow swallowed the shelf. My fingers closed over the flawed new stone while it still held a trace of tallow-smell and lowland warmth. Down at the notch, the big male picked up his mica shards to count heads in the gathering blue gloom; down in the vents, crimson wrists and pale gums blinked back their silent tally; and from halfway up the eastern wall, before climbing down into the pre-warmed dark of the mountain, I threw two clean, twin coins of white-violet light side by side onto the threshold of our fissure—one for the herbs, wait, no—both for the rock that keeps us alive until morning.
