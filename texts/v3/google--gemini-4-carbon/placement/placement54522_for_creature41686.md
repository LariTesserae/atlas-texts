At first, the pull is downward—past the lip, through the three thousand feet of empty grey wall, beneath the blinding skin of the cloud-deck to where water runs without permission and wood burns loud. There is a workshop down there somewhere with soot on the lintel and a stone mortar stained pale blue at the bottom, where a dyer or an apothecary tips three grains of crystal onto a brass scale and wonders why it smells faintly of goat-tallow and lightning.

No. Down there the air is soup, thick and noisy, full of rot and errands. Once you have looked into that tilted nave under a violet lid, the lowlands feel crowded even in an empty room.

I am up here, then. Not breathing—anything with lungs is only visiting, hauling its heavy, sludging blood around like a leaky bucket until the cold takes its teeth or its balance. If I am to stay past autumn, past the midwinter gales when the cleft belongs entirely to starlight and stone-crack, I have to be without marrow.

For a moment I think I am the bronze sheet wedged high in the five spears. It is the obvious heart of the room—the one thing with a voice, the blind clock-hand nudged by the mist. *Clack. Clack.*

Wait, though. The bronze is frantic compared to the rest of the nave. It sits too high, exposed to every nervous draft, worrying its limestone socket year after year until one January night the rock will split a fraction too wide and drop it clattering down into the scree, where it will lie mute under old snow until May. Too restless. Too singular.

Drop lower, into the cobalt shade.

There is a palm-sized plate of scrap-copper—not even a whole vessel, just the sheared belly of an old lowland kettle that cracked along a seam eighty winters ago and was hammered flat on an anvil near the timberline before someone tucked it into their pack-frame. Right now, I am wedged at a forty-degree cant between two frost-shattered dolomite needles, catching a spear of ferocious noon glare and throwing it six feet sideways onto the lip of soapstone trough number three.

That is closer. Closer to the true speed of the place.

I am not just one piece, maybe, or else I am that specific piece and have been moved so many times across the labyrinth over four generations that I know every crevice by the pinch of its limestone. My back is rough, pitted with black oxide and dull green crust where wet mittens have pressed me into shadow-cracks; my face is scored with ten thousand circular scratches from being scoured bright every few weeks with a thumbful of dry limestone silt so I won’t lose my throw.

To be this small mirror is to hold a weird, borrowed heat in a freezing world. All morning I lie cold and dark under an anchor-stone beside the leather pouches, thirty below zero, brittle enough at the edges that a clumsy boot could snap my corner off. Nothing happens except the sharpening of the blue shadow-line inching across the gravel toward me. Then comes the slow rasp of the breathing-pad rounding the fin, the scrape of sedge-stuffed leather on glare ice, and the sudden, shocking animal warmth of two clubbed, green-nailed thumbs lifting me out of the dark.

For forty minutes each afternoon, after the whiteout collapses and the violet sky slams back down, I work.

Wedged between two dry ribs of stone, I take the unfiltered weight of the high sun square on my scoured face. My metal skin spikes warm—not hot enough to blister hide, just fever-warm, a tiny square of lowland July suspended in iron-tasting air—while two inches behind me the rock stays locked below freezing. I take that ferocious, silent downpour and bend it sideways into a corridor the sun itself can never reach because the eastern spires stand too close together.

I aim a trembling, bright coin of light onto the nose of a soapstone channel. Where my coin lands, the clear curtain of ice hesitates. It sweats. One fat, bitter drop detaches from the grey lip, flashes gold as it falls through my beam, and vanishes into the cobalt dark of the bowl waiting below. *Plink.* Seven seconds. *Plink.* Nine seconds. *Plink.*

Without me, that runnel seals up by half-past two. With me wedged right *here*, pressed tight against a flint nodule on the left needle and a thumb-flute on the right, the drip holds until three. Half a cup of sour, copper-hungry meltwater stolen from the frost before the shadow climbs my knees and puts me out.

I know the tenders not by their faces—no one has a face up here, only horn slits, yellow sulfur-grease, and crusted wool—but by the pressure of their thumbs and how well they read angles.

Some of them are clumsy in their first June, hypoxic and impatient, jamming me too hard into soft dolomite so a flake shears loose ten minutes after they shuffle down the corridor, dropping my light into the scree to melt a useless hole in a patch of salt-flowers while the trough overhead freezes solid with a faint, mocking *tink*. When they come back round and find the drip dead and me lying face-up in grey slush, I hear the long, ragged whistle through their frozen wool—not even a curse, because cursing wastes breath, just a heavy sag of the barrel chest before they stoop, slowly, waiting for the blood behind their eyes to settle, and set me right.

Others know me better than they know their own children down in the valley. There was one—three tenders back, the small left-handed one now sitting upright in the middle fissure at the rear with a knot of frayed cord in her lap—who knew how to set me ten minutes *ahead* of the sun’s crawl. She would wedge me where my face was still half in blue shade, calculating the exact arc of the massif wall so that while she was three corridors away scraping yellow alum-crust into a stiff pouch, the sun would step cleanly onto my cheek, switch me on without a hand near me, and walk my reflected coin three inches down the length of a frozen flute like a slow warm finger, thawing it top to bottom just when the rime was thickest.

When the autumn gales start screaming across the upper ramparts—still muffled down here in the stone nave to a heavy, shuddering thump in the air pressure, though snow-spindrift hisses horizontally over the open lip—the tender comes round one last time. They unrig the river pebbles so the ice-loads won't snap the hair cords. They pin the copper bowls upside down under heavy slate slabs. And they tuck me flat underneath bowl number four, face-down on dry gravel so blowing ice-grit won't blind my polish over the seven dark moons.

I don't sleep then; metal doesn't sleep. I just stop changing.

Through midwinter, when the sun barely clears the outer lip for ninety minutes at noon and no cloud-plumes have the thermal muscle to climb three thousand feet, the labyrinth goes completely dry and loud with cold. That is when the limestone talks to itself—sharp pistol-cracks at midnight when forty-below air finds a hairline seam of trapped October moisture and wedges a six-ton needle half a millimeter wider at the base. Starlight up here isn't soft; through the black-violet thinness it falls almost hard enough to cast its own faint, steel-grey shadows between the spires.

Under my bowl, in total dark, I shrink infinitesimally in the cold, holding the circular ghosts of last August’s silt-polishing on my skin, waiting beside the silent upright company in the rear fissures for the afternoon when the bronze vane overhead gives its first sluggish *clack* of May, and a rough cord-wrapped boot scrapes down corridor four, looking for an extra twenty minutes of sun.
