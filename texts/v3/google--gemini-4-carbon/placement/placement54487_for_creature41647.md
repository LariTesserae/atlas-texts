Everything that rises through the center of this sphere leaves empty-handed. 

When the warm vapor lifts off the bottom hull to begin its lazy climb toward the skylights, it is pure distilled ghost—just water, stripped clean, carrying nothing with it. It has to touch the cold outer iron near the crown to take on weight again. Up there, condensing on the weeping bolt-heads and trickling down ninety feet of curved plates, copper jackets, root-mats, and slick animal hides, it gathers the news of the walls. Every drop slides downward growing heavier with dissolved ferric salts, green verdigris from the brass baffles, shed bacteria, and microscopic hooked hairs rubbed loose from the rosettes of the great climber overhead.

Follow any stem far enough down the inward-sloping lower ribs and there is only one place left to go. 

Think about that washbasin pool at the very bottom, sitting over the massive forged-iron drain plug where the three structural ribs meet. For centuries, every drop of runoff that didn't stick to a leaf has crept down to feed it, while the rising thermal draft continuously steals pure water back off its top. By any ordinary reckoning, that basin should have choked solid three hundred years ago. It ought to be a dry, crusted cake of orange slag and mineral scale.

That it is still clear—that four inches of warm, dark water still sit cleanly enough to hold that motionless pewter crosshatch—is my work.

You would have to look straight down through the geometric lattice on the water’s skin, letting your eyes adjust against the metallic glare of the green afternoon light, to realize the bottom of the basin is not forged iron anymore. At first glance I look like the recessed hexagonal wrench-socket of the drain plug itself, silted softly over. Look longer—for half an hour, say, which is the blink of an eye in here—and you will see a seam along the dark, terraced stone part slightly, exhaling a clear, heavy plume that rises an inch and flattens beneath the surface tension without breaking it.

I have no need for legs, or membranes for the air, or eyes that focus on distance. My sky is four inches overhead and twenty-eight inches wide, roofed by hammered water. 

I am shaped to my hollow: a broad, flat, shell-less mollusk—or something older that arrived as a cyst in the priming water when the unseen engine miles below first blew these lines clear—grown slow and heavy until my muscular foot filled the entire machined depression of the drain plug. My back is a low spiral of rubbery, overlapping shelves the color of gunmetal and steeped tea, stiffened not with bone or cartilage, which would hum themselves ragged, but with dense, suspended granules of magnetite that drink the vibration of the hull and hold me down like a leaded keel.

Around my scalloped rim, where trailing fern-stems dip into the meniscus and wick their rusty droplets silently into the pool, sixty flat, spoon-tipped palps sweep the basin floor. They move at the speed of cooling wax. Move any faster and a wake would rise to blur the pewter crosshatch above me; move as I do, and the water never knows it is being stirred. 

All day, all century, those spoons gather what the sphere sheds. It is a rich, slow broth. Without lifting my head—I have no head, only a slow, sorting gut beneath my terraces and a ring of chemical pits along my lip—I read the entire hanging room with a four-hour delay. 

I know when a brass baffle on Manifold Nine has begun to pit more deeply, because by evening my eastern palps taste a sudden, bright spike of copper sulfate. I know when a tarnished crab has died wedged behind an I-beam, days before its shell washes down, by the faint, sweet ammonia threading through the root-filtered runoff from the northern wall. I know the smaller, younger climber tucked inside the southern cable-mount is healthy; once a month, the water carries down the dissolved, peppery grit of its shed mouth-plates.

What is living or rotten, I digest. What is metal, I keep.

Beneath me, where my pale, broad foot seals directly against the forged steel of the drain plug, lies the one mortal flaw of this world. Copper-tainted water pooling over iron at the lowest point of a warm hull is a battery; left to itself, galvanic rot would have eaten through the threads of the drain plug ages ago. One blown seam at the bottom of the curve, and all the heat, the vapor, the gliders, and the roar itself would scream downward into the freezing violet dark in a single catastrophic breath. 

I know nothing about altitude, or cables, or the twenty-eight thousand feet of empty air waiting half an inch beneath my belly. I only know that where the old iron threads have corroded thin, the freezing cold of the troposphere bleeds upward through the metal and pinches my foot. 

The cold irritates me. So I take the iron, the silica, and the copper I have strained from the water, bind them in heavy proteins, and secrete them downward in micro-thin laminations. Layer by layer, decade over decade, I pave the rotting steel with a dark, iridescent mother-of-pearl—harder than horn, denser than rust, veined with malachite green and dull specular bronze. Whenever a pinhole threatens to chill my underside, I thicken the glaze until the warmth returns. Across the upper dishes of defunct valve wheels and upturned beam-flanges, smaller cousins of mine—spawned from microscopic buds I release into the rising vapor once every few years—do the same work at the size of shirt buttons, lining every puddle-pocket in the sphere with smooth metallic nacre. Only down here, where all three ribs converge, is there runoff enough to grow as wide and patient as an anvil.

My days have no nights, only the green light above my four-inch ceiling, split by the standing acoustic wave on the water into thousands of tiny, intersecting bright lines that print a motionless checkerboard across my back. My skin reads that pattern the way leaves read summer. 

When the distant engine miles below the clouds shifts its seasonal load, I know it before the water changes taste. The manifolds drop a quarter-tone; instantly, the tight checkerboard of green light across my back shivers, melts into an even glow, and snaps back into focus a millimeter wider than before. That widening is my equinox. I draw my sixty spoons inward for an hour, holding still in the new geometry, letting my internal granules settle into the altered chord.

Then I wait for the eclipse.

It always comes a few hours after the pitch drops. First, far overhead, one quarter of the green light turns the dim, bruised color of diluted wine; even through four inches of water and ninety feet of air, the photoreceptors across my terraces register the distant dimming as the great six-limbed sovereign hangs against the cold glass port to bleed off its heat. 

When that wine-colored shadow vanishes, I count roughly two hundred of my slow heartbeats.

Then the green light above my basin disappears altogether. 

Three feet over the pool, the broad, six-foot hexagon of stretched purple skin arrests its fall on the rising thermal. It never touches my water. It never breaks the crosshatch. Yet the cushion of warm air trapped beneath its wide mantle presses downward against the pool’s skin, bowing my four-inch ceiling inward by the thickness of a thumbnail. 

I feel its arrival as a sudden, gentle barometric hush—a warm, heavy hand laid briefly over my entire sky, muffling the sharp upper hiss of the brass baffles into a deep, resonant thrum that passes straight through the water and into my magnetite core. For ten seconds, twenty, we hold each other in counterbalance at the nadir of the sphere: the wide traveler suspended on the breath of the hull, and the blind mason anchored in its plug, separated by thirty-six inches of roaring air and four inches of hammered pewter.

Then the pressure lifts. A rim of green light slices back across my eastern margin as it tilts two limbs, catches the wall-draft, and sails silently out and upward around the curve of the ferns. My checkerboard brightens back to full afternoon. A single drop of warm condensation, shaken loose from a creeper stem by the passing bow-wave of its glide, slides down a drowned leaf-tip at my edge and slips without a ripple into the pool—carrying a trace of oxidized plum hide and high, cold glass, which my nearest spoon reaches out, very slowly, to taste.
