I am in the dark at the end of a long branch of the channel, and the dark is so complete that it has weight, though nothing touches me except the water and the stone beneath my back.

At first, there is a confusion about where my ribs end and the floor pan begins. Something has grown across the seam. Not moss—moss needs the green light outside, the wet glare—this is smooth, cold, laid down in layers thinner than the silver membrane of an eyelid. A thousand nights of seep from the upper tuff have left their dissolved ghost along my flank, binding hip and shoulder to the dressed basalt until the joint between flesh and masonry has lost its edge.

Wait. Not wholly stone yet. If I were wholly stone, I would not know when the sun drops behind the peak.

I know it because the water cools by half a degree against my jaw, and then, three minutes later, the plaza wakes up.

It comes through the underside of my skull first: the heavy, muffled *slap* of wide soles stepping out of the doorways two terraces up. I do not hear them; I *carry* them. Since they walled up my lintel and narrowed my threshold-plug to a finger’s breadth of clear runoff, my bones have grown denser, chalked through with lime and black silica, turning me into a better conductor than I ever was when I walked the causeways with an ironwood lever on my shoulder. Back then, flesh damped the grid. Muscle and blood swallowed half the message before it reached the inner drum of the jaw. You had to put your foot into the four-inch groove and press hard just to catch the crisp rap of a chisel three hundred yards away.

Now the whole shelf speaks directly through my spine.

Up at the eastern cliff-base, two workers are setting wedges. *Tock. Tock.* Pause. *Tock.* I know the swing of the younger one—he hurries the third blow, bruising the greenstone edge against the basalt lip instead of letting the weight of the maul drop clean. A faint, gritty burr rides the end of his strike. Down in the third marsh-pan, someone pauses waist-deep in the glossy weeds, shifts a bare heel against the retaining wall, and sends back a dry, double-clicked correction: *slow down; let the wood drink.*

The pulse passes right through the narrow thread of water under my door, travels up the calcified ridge of my shin, crosses my collarbone, and slips out the rear notch toward the western drop-off.

That is what I have become here. Not a sleeper, and not a monument. Look at the layout of the older quarters—really trace the four-inch gutters from the weeping cliff down to the cloud-rim—and you see that none of the sealed houses are bypassed. They never divert the water around us when they wall up a doorway. Every living courtyard still drinks downstream from three or four of the dark rooms, or sends its tail-water sliding through ours before the spillways drop to the next shelf. And when a channel runs through a sealed house where an old body has spent forty years stiffening into sinter, the stone pan inside is narrower, denser, curved softly like the throat of a reed-pipe.

We tune the water.

A fresh-cut basalt gutter rings too sharp; it chatters with every stray drip from the eaves. A channel choked with loose grit goes dead and muddy. But pass a millimeter of mountain seep over the smooth, mineral-sheathed ribs of someone who has lain in the dark for half a century, and the vibration comes out the rear notch rounded, deepened, stripped of its surface clatter. When the living tap their heels at the crossroads down-slope, they know which district the tremor traveled through by the timbre we give it in passing. This neighborhood has a low, bell-like hum because four of us lie parallel between the upper spring and the wide plaza, our calcified backs catching the raw strikes from the quarry and smoothing them into a steady bass note before they reach the sleeping-houses of the young.

Sometimes—not every night, perhaps once in twenty—footsteps stop directly outside my dry-stacked wall.

No one pries at the blocks. There is no grief in the way they stand there; nothing has been lost, because nothing here goes away. Above me, I can feel the slight sag of my roof-timbers under a fresh coat of bundled marsh-reed. Someone has climbed up in the warm mist with a wooden beater to pack new thatch over my rafters, scraping away the heaviest mats of slick green algae so the peeled wood underneath won't sour and collapse into my pan. Every step they take on the roof-pitch presses the dry-stone walls down a hair's breadth tighter into the bedrock, sharpening my contact with the shelf. When they climb down, a single flat palm presses against the outside of my sealed lintel—a long, warm lean of heavy charcoal flesh against rough basalt—just checking the resonance of the room inside, listening with their skin to make sure the water still runs clean around me and no fallen reed-stem has snagged against my ankles to muddy the note.

Then the palm lifts, the wet sole slaps away down the causeway, and the dark settles back into its steady measure.

Hours later, before the silver furnace of morning returns to bleach the plazas, the twenty or thirty of them gather near the edge of the cloud-abyss and begin the slow rocking from heel to toe.

To them, standing upright in the curling white fog, it must feel like something they are making together—a brief, solemn shiver sent out toward the empty sky before sleep. Down here under the sealed thatch, half-locked into the floor slab, I feel where the tremor actually goes. It does not vanish into the air. It rolls backward across the basin through every causeway and retaining wall, gathering weight as it passes through a hundred dark, walled-shut rooms like mine, waking a tiny, sympathetic hum in every stone-sheathed chest from the cliff-foot to the western rim, until the whole mountain shoulder vibrates as a single, continuous piece of worked rock. For ten minutes before dawn, there is no difference at all between the builders, the town, and the shelf that holds us.

Then the eastern ridge heats. The footsteps retreat indoors. One by one, in the open houses down the grid, heavy bodies lower themselves into the pans, the double lids close, and the sharp daytime stillness locks down over the mirrors outside—leaving only us in the dark rooms wide awake to keep the water singing evenly until dusk.
