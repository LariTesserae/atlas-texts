Underneath the seventh-tier gallery on the eastern curve—far around the barrel from the smoked-warmth of the occupied rooms—a forty-pound block of black basalt hangs at the end of a braided root. 

It does not touch the slate shelf beneath it. When it was lashed there two autumns ago, you could stack four fingers edge-to-edge between the sharp bottom point of the hanging stone and the slick green cushion of clubmoss below. Last spring, you could fit three. Today, sliding a scarred knuckle softly into the gap without disturbing the plumb, there is barely room for two. 

The vine above has yielded another half-inch to gravity, and its bark is turning grey and woody along the twist, drinking calcium from the rock-water that sheets down it day and night. By first frost, the hanging stone will kiss the moss. Someone will have to come back with an antler pry-bar and a hide sling, untie the counterweight before it grounds out and goes lazy, and move it six paces north to pull the kitchen awning straight.

That someone is me. Or rather, I am the reason nobody else has to keep that two-knuckle gap in their head through the summer.

If you looked across the booming cylinder from the western plazas right now, you would probably miss me altogether. I am not out on the sunny nose of a cantilever with a mica mirror at my hip, trading *tak-tak* signals about an urgent fracture. I am tucked into the blue-shadowed crease where two columnar ribs of basalt meet at an acute angle, eighty yards clockwise from the nearest neighbor, crouched in the middle of a neighborhood where no fire has been lit in four years. 

My bladderwort cape is older than most—darkened almost black by seasons of re-greasing with crane-fat, stiff as bark across the shoulders, nicked white along the forearms from brushing too close to doorframes that haven’t had their obsidian spurs chipped dull yet. I am smaller than the men who haul the heavy bone-poles, lighter on the reed-mats, with wide heels calloused sideways inside thick-laced hide sandals and thumbnails grooved permanently down the middle from testing hairline cracks.

Everyone among the sixty works the stone, skims the drift-pots, and ties the living hitches, because sixty people cannot afford specialists. Still, temperaments sort themselves along the wall. Some people belong near the rim where the cranes step and the wind tears the clouds open; some belong in the sociable cluster of the three dry tiers on the north face, where six households currently live within breath-whisper of one another behind triple moss-screens, passing warm tallow lamps back and forth through interior hatches.

I belong to the fallow quarters. Give me the nine-tenths of the city that is empty and working on becoming something else.

To spend six hours alone on the uninhabited tiers is to step out of human time and into the tempo of wet carpentry. Out here, nothing is finished and nothing is broken; everything is merely partway through a long persuasion. I rise from my sister’s hearth in the dim quiet of morning, tuck a pouch of sorrel-tubers and three cured strips of black crane-jerky inside my ribs where my body heat will soften the flinty meat enough to chew by noon, hang my hornbeam clappers and a small wooden wedge-mallet at my hip, and walk out into the roar.

The sound hits the cheekbones first. Stepping out of the willow hatch is like thrusting your head into a barrel of tumbling river-stones, except nothing here is round. Every drop of water falling from the rim a hundred feet up lands with a sharp, flat *spank* against taut slate or broad bergenia leaves. For the first ten minutes on the stair, my ears tighten against it, still protective of the tiny, sibilant night-voices left behind in the moss dark. Then something inside the skull unlatches. You stop trying to hear *past* the applause of the drops and start hearing *with* it.

That is actually why I am out here. A city built under tension tunes itself like a harp, and if you walk the empty streets slowly enough, the deafening noise stops being uniform. 

Wait on a switchback landing with your eyes half-shut against the glare, palms resting flat against two diverging root-stays, and listen to how the falling water strikes the roofs below. A healthy terrace—one whose slate plates are pinched firm in the cliff-seam and whose living cables have hardened cleanly—answers the drops with a tight, dry *tink*, high in the teeth. Three tiers down, though, an empty parlor roof has begun to give back a dull, hollow *clop-clop*, half a tone flat. It means a root-knot underneath has swollen soggy and slipped a fraction on its hornbeam toggle, letting two overlapping slates part company. Left alone through the winter, freeze-thaw will lever that gap wide enough to drop the whole threshold into the abyssal updraft.

So I go down to meet it. 

Moving down an untended stair takes twenty minutes for forty steps. Unused reed-matting grows slick with translucent green jelly-lichen, and fresh slivers of glassy rock calved from last week’s gunshot-crack lie hidden in the creases of succulent leaves like dropped razors. You never look across the void while your foot is in the air. Heel first, rolling flat to test the pitch of the slate; knee bent over the ankle; two fingers brushing—never clutching—the weeping stone wall. If you nick a knuckle on a raw spur, the thin mountain cold numbs it before it bleeds much, leaving a neat crimson bead that washes away instantly in the shivering film of wall-water.

By midday, a cloud snags on the eastern rim and spills straight down into the nave.

This is the hour I like best, though the younger ones hate being caught out on the fallow side when it happens. In thirty heartbeats, the opposite wall vanishes. The two hundred paces of crisp blue air turn into a solid, blinding pearl. Light comes from everywhere at once—up from the abyss, sideways off the fog, bouncing off wet black glass—until shadow disappears completely and you have to narrow your eyes to slits behind chapped lids. Across the chasm, mica discs are useless now. Clapper-strikes still cross the white nothingness if someone needs help, dry and sudden—*tak, tak-tak*—detached from any visible body, though the fog thickens the echo so it is hard to tell whether a knock came from tier four or tier nine.

Even the roar changes inside a cloud. Because no new gusts are shearing heavy drops loose from the high ledges, and the fog itself is laying a cold fleece directly onto every leaf, the shattering *clack* softens by half. The town grows muffled, bright, and intensely private. My world shrinks to ten feet of shining black angles, silver-green leaves beaded like fish-roe, and the single pale salamander stopped dead on the lintel beside my hip, its throat pulsing once every minute in the thin, electric air.

I pull three split-antler shims from my belt-loop, tap two of them gently under the slackened toggle of the flat-sounding roof until the slate plates bite tight again—listening as the next heavy drip hits the eave with a restored, crisp *tink*—and then duck through the low doorway of the house behind it to eat my noon jerky out of the wet.

The room is half-ripe. Nobody lives here yet, and nobody will until the spring after next. Only the first layer of wicker screening has been hung a hand’s-breadth off the basalt wall; the dried clubmoss stuffing sits stacked in neat, dry bales in the corner, waiting for autumn. So the room is neither roaring nor silent. Behind the wicker, water sheets silently down raw obsidian; outside, the pearl-glare shines through the weave in a thousand tiny white pinpricks. The floorboards tilt slightly uphill toward the outer balcony—deliberately built too steep last year so that when the forty-pound basalt weight underneath finishes stretching the support vine this autumn, the floor will settle dead level.

Sitting cross-legged on the slanting slate, chewing cold crane-meat that tastes of iron and sharp wind, I know whose house this is going to be, though they haven’t asked for it yet. My brother’s daughter is sixteen; the boy from the south-wall crane pens has been bringing his best braided green-hide over to our hearth-basket every third evening, sitting with his knees tucked tight and whispering so softly into the peat-smoke you can barely tell when he stops breathing and starts speaking. By the time they wed next summer, the north-tier rooms where we live now will be getting damp—a fresh seam opened behind my sister’s sleeping-niche three moons ago, and already a cold ribbon of water licks down the corner post whenever the ridge groans. We will have to abandon that cluster for five or six years and let the moss take the sluiceway. 

When we move, this tilted room I am sitting in will be level, dry, moss-stuffed, and quiet. Their first child will learn its knot-games on the exact slab under my sandals.

There is no paper here, no ink, no charcoal spared from the tiny bladder-braziers to draw maps of two thousand rooms. The ledger of the city lives entirely inside four or five heads like mine—memorized as a ring of tensions, tilts, water-tastes, and acoustic pitches. Closing my eyes in the dim wicker-light, I can walk the whole interior of the chime in my mind without lifting a foot: *tier six northwest, pinched tight, leave it three winters lest it shatter; tier three south-by-east, sweet drip-pots filling fast, floor flat, ready for moss-hanging after frost; tier eight under the crane-gutter, slack vine, needs a heavier stone.*

Suddenly—without warning—the cloud begins to lift out of the top of the chime, pulled by a shift in the high pass. 

As the wind shears across the jagged upper lip, the whole stone cathedral catches the breath and sounds its deep, sub-audible note. 

*Hmmmmmm.*

You do not hear it with your ears; you feel it behind your breastbone and up through the pelvis where you sit on the rock. Outside on the terrace, every taut vine creaks once in unison. Every clay water-pot trembles in its wicker ring. And fifty yards straight down beneath my heels—down past the lowest drip-sieves, down where the sunlight gives up and the blue mist churns forever over the abyssal draft—I can almost feel the great wedged boulders at the bottom of the chasm lean inward by a hair’s-breadth and stop, held fast against one another.

My father’s ribs are down there, wrapped in calcified root-net alongside three sharp wedges of black glass, locked into the narrows under the eastern wall twelve winters ago. My older sister is ten yards west of him, holding open the seam beneath the lower crane-walks. In twenty years or thirty, when my eyes finally cloud over completely from the pearl-glare and my ankles lose the liquid, crane-slow certainty needed for the foot-wide stairs, they will wrap me in green roots with four good wedges of my own and lower me into whichever throat of the chasm needs stiffening most.

I don't mind the thought at all. It will just be the same job, lower down, without having to carry the mallet.

Outside the hatch, the pearl-fog tears apart into rags and vanishes upward, snapping the world back into hard cold blue and blinding diamond glint. Two hundred paces across the bright nothingness, tiny against the glistening green curtain of the western cliff, a white spark of mica winks once, twice, from an upper gallery. Someone looking across to check after the cloud-pass. 

I crawl slowly out onto the wet reed balcony, test the canted edge with my big toe, and stand up smoothly into the deafening applause of ten thousand falling drops. My mica disc stays in its pouch—my angle is still shadowed—so instead I draw my two hornbeam slats and strike them once, crisp and unhurried, against the roar.

*Tak.*

All sound on the fallow curve. All taut. Then I turn sideways along the foot-wide ledge, touch two fingers to the cold wet glass of the mountain, and go down to check the counterweights on tier five.
