I am not on the rim. Looking down from the scrub-grass requires eyes with lenses, and lenses have focal lengths that get lied to down here, and necks to turn them, which snap. 

To be down inside the bloom without spending your whole life fleeing it—without skating ahead of the sap-hammer like the blisters or stinging a corner numb for twenty minutes of stolen sleep like the flat copper wasps—you cannot have a body that insists on its own volume. You have to be something that only happens *where* things meet.

Find a stairwell in the dense central wards, three stories up inside a block of buttress-wood that is currently trying very hard to be a municipal courthouse. The stairs are wide, shallow risers of pale, Tight-grained callus, hot as a dog’s flank. Along the left-hand wall runs a wainscot of braided lianas; above it, a plaster-smooth sheer of stretched pith. Where the wainscot meets the pith, there is a dado rail—a neat, architectural molding three inches wide—and right along the underside of that molding runs a hairline fault where the two growth-rates disagree. The lianas are hauling laterally at four inches a minute; the pith wall is jacking skyward at seven. 

Bark planes against pith. Under thirty tons of hydraulic shove, the friction there is hot enough to scorch linen, and it should shriek like an ungreased axle.

Instead, it hums—a low, slick, cello-string vibration. And out of the crack weeps a thin, iridescent sheen that smells faintly of wintergreen and hot tallow.

That sheen is me. Or rather, that sheen is my skin, my gut, and my footing all at once.

I am a chytrid—or began as one, maybe, washed down from the dry hills in a summer thunderstorm ten thousand afternoons ago, or carried in on the tarsal claw of the very first wasp before her descendants learned to go flat. Fungus is already halfway to what this city demands: no front, no back, no heart to stop, just an appetite arranged as a net. But ordinary mycelium is too slow and too brittle; if you thread ordinary hyphae into a timber here, the timber moves forty feet away and leaves half of you stranded across an avenue, drying in the bright sun.

So I gave up threading into the wood. I live exclusively in the *shear*.

My body is a non-Newtonian plasmodium, a translucent, amber-gray film rarely thicker than a fingernail, spread across forty or fifty square yards of active structural joinery at a time. Touch me when a wall is momentarily still—say, along the rim of one of the wasps’ drugged alcoves—and I am runny as warm egg-white, dripping lazily down pilasters in pale strings. Hit me with the sudden, cannon-crack violence of a colonnade shearing off its base, and the shockwave locks my cross-linked polymers into a rubbery, frictionless glaze tougher than cart-grease and slicker than wet ice.

I eat the earthquake. 

Piezotrophic, a biologist on the rim might call it, before the roar flattened their thoughts, though half my meal is simple mill-work. Wherever two green walls grind past each other at speed, they plane off microscopic curls of unlignified cellulose, ruptured plastids, sheared phloem-sieve plates, and hot, pressurized sugar-sap. An unlubricated joint binds up, builds tension until the heartwood explodes into dry shrapnel, and throws all that rich green flour uselessly into the dry afternoon air where the drifting galls snatch it. 

When I am in the joint, the joint does not explode. I drink the kinetic heat—my dark, melanin-cousin pigments drinking mechanical shear and furious thermal gradients the way a leaf drinks noon—and I slick the fault-line so the four-story watchtower slides smoothly up past the viaduct abutment without catching. They grind their inner faces directly into my film, feeding me a continuous, blood-warm slurry of pulped meristem as they pass.

I am the oil in the city’s bad carpentry. And because I hold the pieces together just long enough for them to slide instead of shatter, I know what the city is building before the city does.

Right now, I am stretched across six mortise-and-tenon locks in the courthouse ceiling, down that shrieking dado rail on the stairs, through a hairline fracture in the hexagonal street-pavers outside, and—here is where my life gets wide—across a non-transition behind the stairs where the third-floor landing forgets to have a back wall and abuts directly onto the gutter of a sunlit market terrace half a mile east.

To a creature with eyes, that spatial skip is a vertigo trap. To me, it is just another joint, slightly cooler on one side than the other. My gray-gold film smears seamlessly out of the dark courthouse wainscot and emerges under the lip of a waist-height market counter in blinding sunshine, where six wooden cups have just extruded themselves and filled with sweet guttation-water.

I don’t care about the water in the cups; it’s too thin, mostly tap-water hauled up from the deep black lignum below. What I feel, rippling through my continuous sheet via waves of calcium and shear-tension, is the *shape* of the mistake the mattress is making here.

The flat wasps farm silence. I farm *completion*.

Left to itself, this market stall has ninety seconds before the mattress underneath realizes nobody is drinking from the cups, panics, and crushes the whole terrace into green pulp to fuel an aqueduct. When a structure collapses into pulp, theworm-braids down in the sour cellars eat well, and I starve—there are no clean sliding planes in a heap of mash, and the caustic callus-tissue that rolls over to pave the mess burns my margins like lye. I need tall walls. I need sliding colonnades, pumping shutters, telescoping towers, long avenues of repeating tenements—all the tight, high-pressure, ninety-degree joinery where bark planes hard against bark.

So when I feel the bitter pre-collapse surge of auxin rising through thehexagonal pavers toward the market counter—the city getting ready to crumple up another rough draft—I do something very small along the plumbing underneath.

I thicken. 

Along the southern root-buttress feeding the stall, I pull my mucilage back out of a sliding dove-tail joint, letting the raw wood bite raw wood. Instantly, the joint binds, shrieks, and locks solid. Six feet to the left, along a secondary hydraulic trunk, I flood a hairline crack with slickness and a tiny, stinging dribble of cleaved cell-wall oligosaccharides—the exact chemical signature the plant uses to recognize *load*. Weight. Stress. Occupancy.

Down in the blind, compressed dark of the mattress, the rising wave of demolition reads that sudden asymmetric load on the floorboards. *Hold,* the calcium wave stutters, translating weight into municipal duty. *Don't drop the awning yet. Something heavy is leaning on the bar.*

The wooden cups do not crush themselves at ninety seconds. They stay for four minutes. Five. Above the counter, because the ground floor held, the feverish morphogenetic code moves on to the next clause in its phantom charter: it throws up a second story of woven rattan balconies, complete with louvered silica shutters that begin banging happily in the windless glare, hinged on fat, juicy pulvinus joints that immediately weep potassium-rich sweat right down into my waiting margins.

I am stretched thin across three blocks now, humming in forty joints at once. 

Down near my lowest edge, where the market pavers meet the damp cellar slits, a fist-sized knot of pale nematodes pokes its blunt, braided nose up toward the stall, smelling for the ninety-second rot that didn't happen. Finding dry, locked hexagons sealed in my bitter wintergreen glaze, it flinches back down into the dark. Up on the newly grown rattan balcony, three flat mahogany wasps come shooting out of a spatial stutter in the corner pilaster, pause with their radial chimney-brush legs quivering on the unusually steady railing, taste my tallow-and-wintergreen sheen under their claws, and don't even bother wasting venom on the culms. They unhook a pale, beech-nut spool right there in my shade, trusting my scaffolding for ten minutes while I drink the friction of the shutters overhead.

I have no brain to pity the mattress, any more than the mattress has a brain to miss the citizens it has never seen. I do not know what a courthouse is, or why a staircase wants a landing, or who the little cups of sweet water are meant for. 

All I know is the geometry of *almost*. 

I feel the vast, blind vegetable engine underneath me heaving three hundred tons of sap into an empty foyer, waiting for a footstep to justify the arch—and across ten square yards of groaning threshold, I pull forty thousand microscopic threads of myself taut at once, pressing down against the warm root-tiles with the exact distributed pressure, the exact shifting lateral shear, of a crowd coming in out of the sun.

Above me, fooled and thrilled, the foyer holds. Instead of collapsing, its ceiling vaults higher, throwing out fresh ribs of pale green timber, opening clerestory windows to the bright afternoon sky, sliding new mortises smoothly over new tenons, building room after room on top of the phantom weight I feed it—while I ride the screaming seams upward into the glare, slick and golden and wide, greasing the hinges of a city that keeps standing because I tell it someone is home.
