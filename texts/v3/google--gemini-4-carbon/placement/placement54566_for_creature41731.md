You have to look twenty-two feet up, where the third rung of the old inspection ladder has rusted entirely through on its left weld and hangs at a slight cant, muffled in a thick, trembling beard of chartreuse fern. 

I am nine and a half inches long, and I have been nine and a half inches long for eleven years.

My belly is locked to the underside of that iron rung—not with the massive, industrial seal of the old one down in the sump, not yet, though the concentric grooves are all there in miniature, pale and tight against the metal’s pitted curve. Above me, the fern fronds stream straight toward the dome like green flames. Around my flanks, the thirty-second roar goes by. It pulls at the trailing edges of my fins, humming along my lateral line with a fierce, familiar itch, and I do not fight it and I do not ride it. I open my mouth a millimeter, let the warm upward rush thread through my gills, and taste the whole tank in a single pass: sweet oxygen off the moss, sharp copper off the manifolds, the faint greasy tang of deep-core exhaust, and beneath all of it—thick as oil, inescapable, soaking every drop from the floor grates to the ceiling—*her*.

It isn't a scent you smell so much as a weight behind the eyes. Every forty seconds the silver boil hauls her wash straight up through the middle of the room, hits the iron dome, and pours it back down the cold glass over the rest of us. It settles in my blood and tells my bones: *Stay light. Stay narrow. Not yet.* 

So my skull stays thin and sleek, blotched in bright leaf-green and rust-red so the snapping shrimp right beside my tail don’t bother testing me with their white porcelain claws. Three feet below my rung, one of them hangs upside down from a kelp stem right now, swaying violently in the blast, its big piston-claw cocked open like a set trap. We ignore each other. I am too big for its claw; it is too thorny and fast for my unmineralized jaws. We are neighbors on the middle shelf, shaking together in the engine’s teeth.

*Thrum-clack. Thrum-clack.*

Then the deep click behind the wall—the valve slamming shut—and the roar cuts off like a severed wire.

Ten seconds.

The ferns droop immediately, spreading out around my rung into a wide, floating canopy of sunlit green. Everything loosens. My opercula fold down; my heart gives one slow, deliberate shove. Out in the bright open center of the tank, tiny shreds of torn biofilm and rust-snow pause at the top of their climb, hang weightless in the golden light, and begin to drift downward.

I unpeel my pad—just the front edge, a tiny wet release—and glide three inches out from under the rung. 

Without a swim bladder, even nine ounces of me wants the floor. I plane outward on stiff, wide pectoral skirts, sinking on a shallow diagonal through the clear water toward a dead brass gauge collar where the young kelp blades sprout tender and translucent. Two quick snips with my front plates—soft clicks, nothing like her heavy iron *scritch*—and I have a ribbon of sweet green folded into my throat. A pair of thumb-sized foil-fish dart right past my snout after a loose scrap, their big copper eyes flashing, and right behind them goes one of the needle-fry: my sibling, or hers, or nobody’s, a two-inch sliver of clear glass with dark eyes, swimming frantic little zigzags to stay up in the hush before the sky falls on it.

Sometimes, when the green is scarce on my tier or the light hits the lower manifolds just right, I let the glide carry me too far down during the pause. Fifteen feet. Twelve feet. Down where the water gets noticeably hotter against the belly and the iron bulkhead curves inward toward the dark kidney-boulders of the floor. 

I did it four cycles ago. I was nose-down, reaching for a fat, hexagonal snail on the seam between two wall plates, when the brass rang.

*Tink. Tink-tink.*

It didn’t come through my ears; it hit my skull-bones like a needle right through the *thrum-clack* of the spire. Every muscle in my tail fired before I even saw her down in the shadows—just the pale, scarred rim of her massive suction pad shifting half an inch on the dark iron, and one bronze-hooded eye angled up through twenty feet of shimmering water, pinned directly on me. I didn't finish the snail. I caught the very first cough of the forty-second boom under my chin, spread my fins wide, and let the bubble-storm hurl me straight back up into the chartreuse dark of my rung, clamping on so hard my belly-rings ached for three cycles after.

Up above me, ten feet higher, tucked into the copper-red moss under a smooth piston rod, there is another one like me—seven inches, darker red, skittish. When that one drifts down toward my ladder during the ten-second hush, I lift my chin and rap my jaw against the rusted iron of my rung.

It only makes a dull *thock*—my jaw-plates aren't black with magnetite yet, they don't have the bell-ring in them—but the smaller one flinches anyway, backs up into the mercury-shimmer of the oxygen pearls, and leaves my ferns alone. I am practicing. 

The best cycles are the hot ones, late in the day, when the great hidden thing behind the wall labors hard and the iron backplate begins to shimmer with heat-haze. 

I feel the warmth creep through my rung first, soothing and heavy, and then on the next updraft she comes rising right past my terrace. 

I tuck tight against the rusted iron and watch her go by, barely four feet out in the roaring silver column. From underneath, she is enormous—a moving ceiling of slate and tarnished brass, sixty pounds of dense, scarred meat riding the boil without a single beat of her tail, kelp ribbons whipping harmlessly off her broad head. Then the gas cuts off, and instead of banking back to the iron, she swings out across the empty middle and slams that foot-wide pale gasket of hers right onto the center of the outer glass.

*Thud.*

For ten seconds, she blocks a piece of the glare. Her shadow falls directly across my fern tier—cool, sharp-edged, green-gold. Through the leaded panes around her bulk, the blinding white flat of the clouds sits miles down in the nothing, dead still, hurting my eyes even from the shade of the leaves. I have never touched the outer glass for long; the downward current there is too cold for a small body, sucking the heat right out through the ribs, and the sheer drop beyond the four-inch green wall makes something ancient in the back of my brain clench tight to the metal. 

I watch her pale gill-slits hold shut against the sky. I watch the slow, heavy pulse in the center of her suction disc—one beat, two beats, three—while the little hexagonal snails creep unbothered around her tail. 

There is no hurry in my body. The core engines keep the water warm; the pipe breathes for us; the ribbon-kelp grows an inch before dark. Down behind the wall, the preliminary click gathers in the plumbing. Up on the glass, the edge of her pale pad peels free a fraction of a second before the bronze throat roars below, and she drops cleanly back into the surging column, turning over like slow iron, sinking home toward the warm grates while the bubbles tear upward past my chin and stream my green roof toward the dome again.

I stay under the third rung. I open my mouth to the current, taste her in the warm rush, and hold fast.
