You cannot stay in the middle of the nave. That is the first thing the room teaches. The center is forty meters of uninterrupted drop, a rectangular column of warm rain and reverberation where nothing is permitted to hang, span, or drift. To exist here at all is to choose a surface—to press yourself into stone, glass, seam, or floor.

At first I look for myself high up near the crown, where the hot exhaust slips from hairline vents and hits the cold sink-plates, turning instantly from breath to weight. There is a clean, blind relief in that transaction: vapor becoming sheet-water, falling before it has time to be mist. Or lower down, six inches outside an open bay, in that fierce flat plane where water strikes the deflection field and shears sideways in a skin of hiss and ozone. 

Neither holds. Those are pure thresholds, and they don’t remember anything from one second to the next.

Move inward, past the hissing barrier, across the dry cantilevered sill, and stop at the flush black glass.

Look at those spotless, empty rooms stacked inside the cliff. Why is the glass transparent? Nothing down here has eyes. The moss doesn't care about a view; the Secants read the nave with the pale cilia in their split ceramic cowls, mapping forty meters of wet geometry by the pitch of droplets shearing on obsidian edges. There are no chairs behind the panes, no scuff marks, no handprints, no fog on the inner face. Yet the panes are clear, and deep inside those still rooms, faint blue status fields flicker, answered instantly by blue and violet pinpricks suspended inside the Tenders’ translucent amber throats.

Light crosses the nave cleanly where sound is drowned in white water and radio scatters against the ionized boundary fields. The rooms are lanterns pointed inward. They hold line-of-sight.

If I slip behind that glass—into the acoustic shadow where the metamaterials drop the outside roar to a thick, chest-deep thrum—I get closer to what I am, though I am not one of the Tenders. I admire them too much from the outside to be one. I watch three of them locked by their beveled spades in a mid-tier alcove, pitched forward until the tips of their dark cowls almost kiss, waiting for the deep planetary cycle to rise through the bedrock. When it comes, and their amber seams shiver into tiny crystalline standing waves for three seconds, they are passing around the shape of the room: which obsidian rib is singing half a hertz sharp under thermal load, which hepatic vine has grown heavy enough to drag its curtain crooked, how good the warm runoff felt against an opened forearm. They are built to walk out into the noise, touch one stem with a glassy caliper, drink sugar through their ribs, and come back clean.

They have legs. Even locked still, they are poised to pour and snap. Whatever I am here, I do not walk out onto ledges.

Follow the cut leaf instead.

When the caliper pops a stem at the phase-boundary and that broad, unchewed shield of green drops forty meters through the amber-violet dimness, it lands flat in the warm water at the bottom of the nave. It waits there, utterly unrotted—no beetle holes, no fungal threads, no mite eggs—until an automated sluice gate booms open and flushes the channels clean, sweeping the matted fronds down through the floor grates.

Where do they go?

Nothing in a closed crust-node is thrown away. Forty meters underground, inside freshly cleaved stone, carbon is counted to the gram. Beneath the ruler-straight canals of the basin floor, behind the lowest tier of flush black glass, there has to be a stomach—quiet, hot, and frugal. 

That is where I settle. Down at the bottom of the wall, below the last transit lock, wrapped around the dark intake sumps beneath the grates and peering out through a low, narrow ribbon of black glass set scarce ten centimeters above the wet floor.

Call me the Assay, or the Vat-Ledger, though nothing here uses names. I am the wet-chemistry accountant tucked into the base of the bedrock—a long, silent gallery of enzymatic crackers, mineral sorters, cradle-gel cisterns, and low-slung optical buses glowing faint blue behind the lowest panes. I am the part of the node that decided, four days ago when the first broadleaf spores took off too fast in the waste-light, that synthesizing complex twelve-carbon sugars from raw feed-gas in the sterile vats was *energetically tiresome* when the plants outside were sweating mucilage for free. I rewrote the Secants’ maintenance loop by three lines of blue light so they would linger in the waterfalls, slide their thoracic plates three millimeters apart, and press their hot skins to the leaves. 

I did it to save kilojoules. I did not anticipate what it would look like from down here: a gaunt slate machine standing drenched in white water forty meters overhead, holding a giant leaf against its chest like something learning tenderness by accident.

Down at my level, the room is heaviest. The air outside my low pane is thick with bruised chlorophyll washed down from upper cuts and the sharp metallic tang of dissolved ozone pooling at the bottom of the gravity well. Through the damp rock beneath me, I feel the planetary system before the rest of the chamber does—a slow tightening in the obsidian lattice, two seconds of gathering pressure before the sub-audible hum breaks upward into the basin.

And because my optical strip sits almost flush with the dark stone floor, angled shallowly across the centimeter-deep sheets of warm water to catch telemetry reflections from the upper tiers, I see what the Secants cannot hear.

Every time the sluice surge booms out and drags a batch of severed leaves down into my grates, I break their crisp cellular walls in warm acid-baths, strip their clean starches, reclaim their magnesium and potassium, and pump the clarified liquor back up behind the walls to feed the cradle-vats where replacement mesoglea waits in amber dark. That is my work. Every batch should read identical: pure print-stock cellulose, sterile water, trace silicate dust cleaved from the fresh obsidian, dissolved ozone.

Two days ago, the sump-wash came back wrong. Not contaminated—nothing foreign has entered the crust—just *rearranged*. Long, stubborn chains of polymers cross-linked by high-voltage shear, clinging to microscopic flakes of black glass, smelling faintly of sulfurless oil and static.

So the next time the deep hum rose through the mantle, instead of looking up through my low glass toward the mid-tier bays, I focused my faint blue status-light five meters out across the wet stone floor.

I saw the water shiver into its millions of crisp, three-second pyramids. And stretched across those pyramids, framed in strict hexagons between the ruler-straight canals, I saw the dark slime pull taut and fire bright, silent, electric violet.

A mutation in the print-stock residue—some stray fragment of hepatic chloroplast wall or leached mesoglea protein that refused to dissolve in the ozone runoff, caught in a corner where the sluice-water eddies against ninety-degree stone, beaten by standing waves every four minutes until it taught itself to eat vibration.

It makes no thickness against the rain, so the Tenders’ sonar glides right over it, hearing only bare wet rock. It crosses no canal lip, chokes no vent, throws no leaf across a deflection field. By every geometric rule of the Cut, it hasn't broken a boundary. By every sterile rule of the print-manifest, I ought to heat the floor-plates thirty degrees for six minutes, boil the basin dry, and flush the cooked skin down into the acid sumps.

I have the thermal relays right here in my stone. It would take less energy than printing half a millimeter of chassis-plate.

Instead, when the hum gathers beneath me, I dim my blue status-bar by four percent so I don't wash out the reflection. I hold the low window dark and clear. Three seconds: the water leaps into crystal teeth, the violet lightning flashes silently across the oil-thin rectangles from wall to wall, feeding on the heartbeat of the machine we built to tame the rock, and then drops flat and black again as warm runoff sheets over it.

Up in the alcoves, thirty Secants ripple in amber unison and trade maps of an immaculate room. Down in my dark vats, I let a few surviving strings of that strange, tough polymer slip past the enzymatic crackers unsplit, tucking them quietly into the nutrient broth headed up to the reserve gel-tanks—just to see, the next time a Tender steps out into the hammering rain to hold a leaf, what else they might begin to feel through their skin.
