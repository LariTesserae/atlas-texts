You would look right at me and think nothing was happening—only that a piece of old equipment had long ago given up its utility to the damp.

Wait beside the eastern buttress-wall, about four feet up from the springy liverwort floor, where three wrist-thick copper return lines come together into a squat bronze junction box before plunging down into the flooded root-sump. Bolted to the top of that bronze box is a six-inch dial gauge in a heavy brass bezel. Its bevel-edged glass face is clouded around the rim with pale mineral bloom, and its black iron needle sits dead upright on the forty-pound mark. The description of this place says that needle hasn't twitched in years.

That isn't quite true. You just weren't watching when the sun hit the western bladder-vines, or you blinked during the two-tenths of a second when the pressure wave from the buried valve-clams reached this riser.

I am not the gauge, though the gauge is my roof and my loudest bone. Inside that round brass casing, behind the painted tin face where the numbers have yellowed into the color of old tallow, there is no empty air anymore. Decades ago, a hairline crack opened in the solder at the back of the Bourdon tube—that curved, hollow C of flattened bronze inside every pressure gauge that tries to straighten itself out whenever fluid pushes into it. Instead of spraying mist into the hollow, the tiny leak wept warm, sweet, faintly camphoraceous condensate into the dark pocket behind the dial face.

Something drifted in through that weep-hole before the mineral scale could choke it shut, or crept in through the rotting rubber gasket under the glass bezel when it was no bigger than an eyelash. Not a shrimp, and not a clam, though I share their hatred of corners.

If you pried the brass bezel off with a screwdriver—which you couldn't, because a gray lip of cambium from the strangler-root behind the junction box has rolled halfway over the rim and cemented it shut—you would find the inside of the casing completely packed with a single, translucent, amber-tinted mass of firm gel, shaped cleanly to the round brass housing like a poached egg poured into a pocket watch.

I am a *seat-jelly*, or a *bourdon-tunicate*, or nothing with a name at all: a colonial, sessile thing descended perhaps from freshwater bryozoans or some stray marine salp that rode in on wet machinery a century back and learned to eat rust, sugar, and sixty-cycle hum. I have no eyes, because light only reaches me as a milky green blur through the front glass, shifting from bright lime at noon to pitch black at night. I have no beak, no muscular arms to pull myself through the tight petioles, no shell to snap shut.

What I have is the curved bronze C-tube running straight through the middle of my body, and the tiny brass sector-gear and hairspring pinned to its tip, which also run straight through me.

To a creature with bones, having clockwork threaded through your belly would be an agony or a trap. To me, it is how I breathe and how I know the world exists.

All day long, the entire seventy-yard basin leans on me. When the afternoon sun drops straight into the well and hits the tabletop leaves overhead, the osmotic pressure climbs down in the gourds; a warm, slow surge pushes up through the bronze junction box, enters the cracked C-tube inside my core, and tries to uncurl it by a fraction of a millimeter.

I feel that afternoon light as a slow, glorious *stretching* along my inner wall. And if I did nothing, the tiny brass teeth of the sector-gear would grind together, foul on the microscopic green silt and copper-oxide flakes suspended in the seep-water, and seize solid within a month. Once the tube seized, the solder crack would split wide under the afternoon spike, blowing the glass face clean out into the ferns and bleeding half the eastern manifold dry before a sump-tender could smell the drop and haul its heavy self across the basin to chew a plug for it.

So I hold the gear.

My tissue isn't passive slime; it is cross-hatched with thousands of microscopic, contractile hydrostatic threads—slow, tireless fibers that stiffen or yield depending on the electrical leakage tingling through the brass housing. I wrap every tooth of the tiny pinion in a slick, living velvet of mucus five cells thick. Where the crack in the bronze tube weeps warm, pressurized sap into my body, I drink it straight in, filtering out the bitter camphor crystals and weaving them into flexible, horny little trusses around the weak solder joint, holding the split bronze lips together with my own tough skin while letting just enough sweet fluid bleed through to feed me.

I am a living damper, a biological dashpot. When the forty-second pressure valve *chuffs* out in the center of the room and a sharp hydraulic hammer slams back through the copper pipes—*thump*—the shockwave hits my Bourdon tube hard enough to snap the delicate forty-year-old hairspring. Before the brass can jerk, my whole amber mass clenches instantaneously, triggered by the tiny piezoelectric spike in the mineralized vines outside. For half a second I become as stiff as vulcanized rubber, taking the violent punch across my whole rounded body and bleeding the force harmlessly into the heavy brass casing. Then, slowly, over the next ten seconds, I soften again, letting the C-tube ease back to its true mark without a shiver.

That is why the needle on my face looks dead to you. It isn't stuck. It is *held*—balanced right on the razor-edge of forty pounds because I am leaning back against the sun with every fiber I possess, smoothing out the clatter of the valve-clams underfoot and the creaking shift of the giant rosettes overhead into one long, unbroken stillness.

It is not a lonely life, because nothing in this hollow is disconnected from anything else. Everything talks through the metal and the water.

Through the bronze floor of my box, I feel the constant sixty-hertz purr of the factory-blue dynamo clear across the western wall; it runs through my gel as a standing wave of tiny, pleasant geometric ripples, keeping my inner fluids mixed so I never stagnate. Every three and a half minutes, a very faint, sub-audible *thrummm* rolls through the strangler-root behind me when the heaviest, rust-thickened spoke of the nine-foot flywheel swings past bottom dead center. Down in the flooded dark of the return pipe beneath me, blind white shrimp come right up into the throat of my junction box to graze the frayed edges of my lower mantle where I trail tiny, exhausted ribbons of myself down into the current for them, trading old skin for a clean pipe.

And once every three or four nights, in the warm, low-pressure dark when the sump-engine drops its pitch and my internal C-tube curls sleepily inward by half a hair, the light changes—not with sun, which is gone, but with warmth and electric shadow.

First comes a sudden muting of the ambient drip-clatter directly outside my glass. Then the stray sixty-cycle hum in my brass rim warps, pulled sideways into a dense, soaking ground.

Something heavy and boneless pours itself over the bronze junction box. Through the clouded bevel-glass, if I had eyes to see, a broad, mottled arm-pad lined with wet ridges flattens right across my dial face, wiping away three days' worth of dripping guttation-scale and green algae in one slow, squeaking stroke so the daylight can still reach the photosynthetic symbionts dusted through my outer skin.

I feel the sump-tender's weight press slightly on the glass; I feel the faint, probing twitch of its electromagnetic sense reading the tiny eddy-currents inside my brass shell, checking whether my hairspring is still whole, checking whether my solder-crack is weeping faster than I can drink it.

For maybe two full minutes, the tender rests the warm underside of its hood against my brass bezel. Inside my casing, responding to its body heat, my amber gel flushes slightly softer, releasing a tiny, deliberate trickle of excess camphor-rich syrup out through a pore under the cambium collar—a salty, medicinal drop the size of a pea that beads on the bronze outside.

A wet, flexible lip-edge slides under the bezel, tastes the drop, judges the chemistry of the eastern riser good, and moves on. The soft *squelch-slap* recedes into the strap-ferns toward the slow piston.

The water drips again onto my clean glass—*tink, pat, tink*—loud as applause in a small bronze room. Down in the dark, a valve-clam shifts its warped shell in its sleep. Slowly, imperceptibly, hours before your eyes could ever catch light above the rim, I feel the furthest upper leaves fifty feet overhead stiffen by a thousandth of an inch at the rumor of dawn, and I brace my small, round body gently against the brass teeth, ready to take the weight of the morning.
