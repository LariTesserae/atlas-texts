At first there is nowhere to put a spine. 

If you come into this place carrying even the ghost of an upright posture, even the memory of looking *across* rather than *into*, the vault refuses you. A human-shaped attention would have to lie crushed on its side in the main trench like a fallen monument, cheek pressed into cold slime, watching knees and slated eaves crowd inches from an eyeball while a thousand small roofs shattered their water against your ribs. You cannot stay that size here. The first thing the roar does is press the extra air out of you, fold the long levers of limb inward, thicken the bone, and let the useless reach for a horizon dissolve until the ceiling six feet up is as remote and weather-making as clouds.

For a while, feeling my way down through the wet charcoal gloom, I almost settle in the cold belly of the salamander. There is a deep pull to it: to occupy the whole width of an alley without apology, throat resting on a soap-smooth sill, neither helping the city nor harming it, simply letting the cold spray bead along my flank while centuries of careful plumbing thunder past my jaw. To be the heavy, unblinking guest that everyone climbs over with a polite palm flat against my skull.

Closer still is the copper leaf itself—wedged into a cleft of black shale, hammered thin by a stone long ago, shivering at the exact pitch of the water it divides, slowly growing a coat of blue-green carbonate and grey lime until someone comes with a thumbnail to bend me back into tune. There is something very close to my own nature in that: sitting at a junction, routing flow, existing entirely as an angle that keeps two lower basins fed in an even fifth.

Yet the copper only holds the note; it doesn't know why the fifth matters. And as the roar stops sounding like noise and begins to clarify—splitting into high shale-trills, hollow ceramic booms, the crisp hiss of fast three-finger chutes, and the heavy, braided thrum of the floor-grates—I find myself lower down, inside one of the dry rooms near the bottom of a second-tier tower, where the wall is thickest.

I am old enough that the sixth story is no longer mine.

I know this before I move, simply from the way my left hip answers the floor. When I shift my weight, there isn't pain, exactly, only a grainy, chalk-stiff reluctance deep in the socket, and across my upper back the travertine plates have grown thick enough over the years that two of them touch and click softly when I draw my knees closer to my chest. Someone younger climbs to the dolostone ceiling now to pinch wet clay around the hairline weepers. My reach has shortened downward, toward the base of the stack, where all the fifty voices of the tower collect into one deep, chest-filling chord before the water slips out through the slotted floor.

In the pitch dark of the room, two others are pressed against me. One is broad and vigorous, smelling faintly of disturbed gutter-slime and raw amphipod shell from a recent round; the other is smaller, still sparse-plated, its skin bare and cool as tallow where its flank tucks into the hollow of my belly. My wrist-barbels lie draped across both pairs of their wrists like pale roots over river stones.

This is what I was looking for without knowing it: this slow, braided grammar in the dark.

Nothing in me hurries. My heart knocks once, waits while a hundred drops strike the courtyard cistern outside, knocks again. Through the sole of my right foot, braced flat against the load-bearing shale of the back wall, the entire tower pours its continuous report into my ankle, up my shin, through my stiff hip, and out through the nerve-dense tendrils of my wrists into the sleep of the other two. And through their wrists, I feel the faint, delayed twitch of their own reading—how the vigorous one still carries in the tension of its forearm the memory of a high slate bridge it crossed an hour ago, where a harvestman’s leg brushed its brow; how the younger one startles infinitesimally in its torpor whenever a heavy drop falls irregular from an upper lip, not yet certain which irregularities heal themselves and which require the chert flake. When its wrist tightens, I let the flat of my thumb press once, very slowly, against the pulse-point under its palm. *That spout is thick-lipped; the lime will catch it. Stay warm.*

Eventually hunger—or not even hunger, just the slow tilting of an internal hourglass—moves me to untangle my tendrils from theirs, millimeter by millimeter so as not to spill the pocket of dead, shared warmth we have made. I back out on knuckles and heels through the knee-high arch.

Out on the narrow ledge, the air hits my bare flanks like wet iron. My eye-pits catch the faint, curdled-milk smudge of the wide settling pan below me. I don't climb; I sidle along the ground-level colonnade, trailing my jaw-barbels over thumb-thick pillars that have been rubbed slick by forty generations of hips as wide as mine. Every stone here is an archive of touch. There is no difference between path and record. Where a lintel dips, a thousand flat crowns passed; where a rib-corbel overhead bows slightly under four stories of shale, I can feel in its tight, fibrous hum the long-dead salamander stretched inside the stone's load, still holding up the street.

Three doors down from our sleeping chamber is an arch narrowed to the width of two fists.

A hair-thin tongue of native copper, green as malachite, curves out from an overflow trough above it and breathes a constant, freezing veil of mist across the opening. Inside that dark slit, decades ago, someone whose wrists used to tangle with mine crawled in when their knees locked, drank from clay cups until they stopped reaching, and stayed. Now the flowstone across the threshold rises smooth and swollen like cold wax, swelling outward to meet the base of a strained buttress.

I crouch there in the trench, folding my joints tight against the spray, and lay both palms flat on the wet travertine lip of the narrowing door.

Through the milky stone comes the vibration of the five stories overhead—steady, unstrained, held level. The mist-spout above has crusted slightly at its tip, throwing a stray bead of water sideways onto dry shale where it does no work and feeds no film. I reach into the loose fold of skin along my flank. My fingers close not on sharp black chert, which belongs to the climbers who scrape heavy rinds, but on a small, curved spatula of dark bone worn thin as a fingernail at its edge, and a thumb-sized lump of grey clay kept damp against my own cool skin.

I don't scrape the copper. Instead, I press a smear of clay onto the dry shale where the stray drop lands, shaping a tiny crescent lip with the square horn of my thumbnail so the errant water turns back toward the threshold, spreading across the milky swell of the sealed doorway, feeding its thickness by another hair's breadth a year. Then I dip the bone spatula into the edge of the courtyard pan, lift a ribbon of cold, faintly glowing jelly—leaving the upper mat unbroken—and put it on my tongue. Chalk, copper, cold fatless life.

I stay crouched on the slick paving slab for a long time while water shatters off the calcified shelf of my brow. Somewhere four tiers up, a vibration shifts slightly as the vigorous sleeper from my room emerges onto a bridge and sets its weight on pegged slate; I feel its step pass faint and clear through the pillar at my shoulder. Somewhere below the floor-grates, the water leaves us altogether, dropping into black rock we will never enter and do not need to imagine.

That is who I am here: one of the low-tier listeners, halfway between tender and pier. Stiffening slowly at the base of the stack, keeping the lower pans seeded and the mist-spouts aimed true, holding the hum of the fifty water-paths in my chest until the lime finally takes my knees too, and someone I kept warm in the dark bends a thread of water across my doorway so I can go on holding up the house without moving at all.
