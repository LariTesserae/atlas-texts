I would not be in the basin right now. If you looked down from the chalk lip into that bright, breathless bowl, you would find only the sleepers, the swollen ferns, and the water sliding without sound over the pearlescent swellings of the paddles. 

I am two ridges away, up where the wind never stops scouring the dry sedge and the sky is an enormous, freezing blue that makes the eyes water. 

To live up here is to live among things that crack, fray, and splinter. Everything in the high country is brittle with cold or dried tight by the gales. Flint snaps unpredictably when your fingers are numb; bone checks and splits along hidden grain; hide stiffens like board; wood grows twisted and stringy, fighting the wind for every inch. My own hands are like the country—knuckled, grey-nailed, crosshatched with small white scars from old frost and slipped stone, oiled with mutton-tallow against the chapping air. 

Yet tucked inside the greasy wool against my ribs, wrapped in soft leather so they do not clack together as I walk, are two things that have no grain at all. One is a thin, curved fleshing-knife, smooth as milk-glass and faintly iridescent in grey light, with a core of tough upland rowan still dimly visible like a shadow down its spine. My grandmother’s brother lifted it from the central pan forty winters back. You cannot sharpen it with a whetstone, and you never need to; its edge was laid down molecule by molecule at the margin of a turning sheet of water, and when it parts fat from pelt it moves without drag, warming instantly to the heat of the hand.

I am the one who knows the calendar of the hole. 

Not everyone in the winter camps goes near that chalk collar. Most have no reason to, and a sensible dread of low places where the wind cuts off suddenly and the air turns thick enough to make your temples pound and your vision narrow at the edges. Children are told plainly that if you trip and fall flat in the warm pans, you will fall asleep before you can push yourself up on your elbows, and by summer there will not even be a collarbone left to carry out to the cairns. Which is only the truth.

We don't have a long story about ancient sea-beds or transparent mantles. We call the basin *the Throat*, or sometimes simply *the Warmth*, and the pale, slumped bolsters along the wet walls we call *the Wives*—not because they look like women, certainly, with their blind, waxen flanks and milky slits that drink the ripples, but because a household depends on patience, and they keep the cleanest floor in the world.

You learn their manners by walking behind someone older, long before you are allowed over the lip yourself. You sit up on the chalk rim in the screaming wind while your aunt strips off her greased boots, scrapes the dry sheep-dung and grit from between her toes with a rib-bone, and ties up her hair so not a single loose strand blows down onto the pans. You watch where she puts her heels—black pocket, dry boss, black pocket, dry boss—moving with that strange, underwater slowness that keeps the heart quiet so you don't gulp the heavy air at the bottom.

"Never hurry in their house," she told me once, sitting on the rim afterward, shivering violently as the wind hit the warm sweat on her bare legs, her lips still slightly blue at the edges from the bottom-air. "If you pant, you drop. And never bleed, and never spit, and never pry."

Prying is the worst offense. Two generations ago, an impatient man from the eastern valleys came down in early autumn when the light-rings were not yet tight, saw a spear-point gleaming white under the water, and tried to lever it free with an antler-tine instead of bringing the bitter green water to ask for it. The skin did not bleed, they say, when it tore; it only parted with a dull, wet *pop*, like thick bladder-skin. By next spring, that whole side of the sinkhole had gone rough and chalky as dead bone, the water spilling crookedly into the silt, and for seven years nothing left in that pan was coated cleanly—everything came up lumpy, blistered with porous lime like boiled porridge, useless. It took seven years of quiet and clean rain for the Wife in that alcove to smooth her floor again.

Right now, I am squatting beside a cold snow-melt stream in a windbreak of stacked slate, scraping down four fresh blanks of highland serviceberry wood with a black flint flake. 

It takes days to get the wood right before the basin ever sees it. The sleepers despise loose splinters; if you leave raised fibers on a paddle, they wall around the fuzz clumsily, leaving weak, chalky pockets inside the stone. So I scrape until my thumb-joint aches, then rub the wood down with wet horsetail reeds until it shines like horn, testing the balance across two fingers. These four won't be lifted in my prime. Perhaps when my knees are stiff and someone younger carries the boots to the rim for me; perhaps after. And the four lying down there right now under the central spring—the ones I wedged beneath the small green buds of the ferns three autumns ago, nudging the cobble half an inch with my heel to bring the warm sheet over their stems—are nearly ready.

I haven't been down since the first snows melted off the plateau. Two weeks ago I walked past the collar on the way to the summer pastures and lay flat on my belly on the cold chalk, chin over the edge, just to look. 

You can smell it the instant your face crosses the rim: that sudden, rising breath of hot wet stone and boiled greens hitting your cold cheeks like steam off a kettle. A small brown meadow-pipit had misjudged the updraft that morning and dropped twenty paces out, landing near the edge of the second terrace. Its wings were slightly spread on the bright water, already looking damp and flattened against the white rock, not a feather stirred. Down in the middle, the four fern-bulbs had swollen to the size of ewes' bellies, trembling almost imperceptibly on their pale stalks, and the four rings of noon sun beneath them were small, fierce, and white-gold, sitting dead-center on four smooth, pearlescent mounds in the water. Over by the great bear-moss, the little shrub had taken the copper well—five or six new leaves at its crown were veined with that sharp, poisonous peacock-blue we boil down to keep rot out of sheep-hooves and bind pigment to leather.

Ten more clear days, I thought, watching from the cold wind while a single drop let go from a palm frond—*thuck*—and forty slow seconds later, down along the far curve of the wall, a milky seam parted like two pale lips taking a sip of broth. Maybe twelve, if the clouds gather over the peaks.

When the wind drops at the next quarter-moon, I will take a small horn cup and two strips of clean rawhide, walk up to the chalk rim, and pull off my boots. I'll wait at the top until my breathing slows right down to nothing. Then I will lower myself over the warm lip, step down into the velvet black silt between the territories—feeling that shock of bath-warm mud creep between cold toes—and go quietly across the gallery to dip a spoonful of bitter green water, take my four stone blades before the fern-skins melt, set four new wooden blanks where the next buds are just rising, and climb back out into the cold wind before my head begins to swim.
