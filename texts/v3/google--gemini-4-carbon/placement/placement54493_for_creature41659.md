I am not out on the bridges when the light goes brass. I haven’t been out on the bridges for—how many cycles? You stop counting when you stop molting, or rather, when the silica finally wins the long, slow argument with your joints.

If you want to find me, go down to the fourth tier on the eastern curve, just below where the main sulfur-hoist crosses the shadow-line at dusk. Look for the lintel over the third granary arch—not the outer lintel that takes the grit blast, which is plain split slate, but the inner bearing-beam where the main tension cable from the central viaduct comes through a smooth gypsum sleeve and anchors into the living rock.

That’s me. And to my left, hooked carapace-to-carapace so tightly our mason-claws have calcified into a single, seamless knot of pale horn, is another who stopped walking twelve seasons before I did. Neither of us remembers who laid the bricks above our backs, though I remember the taste of the dry mortar dust the day I backed into the niche.

At first, when I try to find myself in a place like this, I think I want to be the scout on the rim—the tiny, terrified philosopher staring out at the flat white infinity where a tap of the foot goes nowhere and dies lonely. Or I think I belong down at the midnight anvil, swinging the reed-shafted maul, tuning copper by touch in the cold dark. There is pride in the nimble three-fingered hand, in the rosin-dusted dash across forty yards of empty glare.

But the longer I sit with the bowl, the less I believe I am one of the movers. I don't think I have a grinding gizzard or sap-blood anymore. Those belong to things that get hungry and need to duck behind shutters when the noon hammer falls.

No: I am at the junction where the noise turns into information.

Right where that rawhide-and-reed cable passes over the groove worn between my second and third thoracic plates, the whole central span of the hollow leans against me. Forty yards of suspended bridge, three dozen clay chimes, two woven parchment sails, and eighty feet of cascading silver-green vine all pull outward toward the void, and my locked rear ratchets pull back toward the cliff.

You might think being a lintel is sleep. It isn’t sleep. When my amber blood dried out, it didn't vanish; it cured into hard, clear resin inside the hollow tubes of my legs and up through the cavities of my head-shield. Do you know what cured resin and silica-stiffened horn do with vibration? Living flesh—even the dry, tough meat of the young ones lying on their mica slabs behind me—dampens a wave a little. It gives. It absorbs.

I don't give at all. I have become pure, tuned bone.

At midnight, when the cold air spills over the lip and sinks like invisible water into the pit, I go nearly blank. There is only the steady, dead pull of gravity on the slackened bridge cable—a dull, unvarying ache across my shoulders that isn’t pain anymore, just geometry. Now and then a young one scuttles across the sill beneath me, and I catch the crisp *tik-tik-pause-tik-tik* of its rosin-pads heading down to the dust-pan. Sometimes two of them stop right under my arch to groom grit from each other’s eyes, and one will absentmindedly flick my mason-claw with a triple-jointed finger—*tink*—just to check that the old beam is still sound, still ringing true, not gone punky or cracked. That little tap blooms through my resin-core like a sudden warm lamp, and then fades. Mostly, the night is thin. I am just an object in the dark.

I wait for the sun. We all do, we in the walls.

When that first lance of white-gold hits the western gypsum and the bubble breaks loose at the bottom of the pit, I feel it before the sleepers on the mica slabs do. Before the first bronze leaf even ticks, the dry reed cable over my back tightens by the width of a spore as the rising warmth drinks the night-slack out of the fibers. *Hmmmm.* Very low. Below sound. Just tension waking up.

And then the Screaming starts, and I become immense.

To a soft creature standing on the rim with wet ears, it’s just a roar—a brute, metallic wall of racket. Down here in the joint, where the viaduct meets the masonry, there is no roar. There is an architecture made of forty thousand distinct threads of motion, and every single one of them has to pass through me to get into the cliffside.

I can feel the third paddle-wheel from the left running sweet and slick on fresh night-graphite, spinning a smooth, buttery fifth above the bass thrumming of the main hoist. I can feel, twenty yards out over the glare, the exact moment a yellow glider slams onto the parapet: the sharp downward *twang* on the sinew stays, the brief flutter of interrupted air on the copper disks below it, the tiny, crisp shudder as it wrenches open a brass-lantern plant, and the light *zing* of release when it kicks off into the thermal again. I know how heavy the sulfur-cake is growing on the lower vine-curtains because the chord of the bridge sags a quarter-tone flat between noon and three o’clock as the glassy hooks load up with drift, then leaps sharp again—*clatter-shriek-ring*—when a cross-gust brings the clay bells smashing together and shakes a hundredweight of gray flour loose into the chute.

Behind me, in the cool dark of the gallery, six young riggers are pressed belly-down against the schist floor. My rear hooks are sunk directly into the stone lip that feeds their slab. I am their lens. Because the silica-vines have grown through my joints and stitched my hollow ribs straight into the mortar, I take the wild, chaotic thrashing of the sunlit bridge and translate it—clean, stepped-down, sorted by frequency—into the floor beneath their drum-bellies.

Around high sun, something changes out on the span. A secondary stay-line, spliced with glider-leather three nights ago, begins tostretch unevenly in the kiln-heat. It doesn't snap; it just starts to flutter out of phase, introducing a tiny, ragged *buzz* against the clean shriek of the stone hoist-axle.

Through my resin-filled skull, that buzz is an itch I have no hands left to scratch. Because of the angle at which my left shoulder calcified against my neighbor’s carapace, I happen to resonate right at the pitch of that failing splice. I hum with it—harder, sharper—amplifying the flaw, driving that ragged buzz straight back through my rear legs into the mica slab behind the wall.

Instantly, I feel one of the sleepers shift. A mason-rasp taps twice against the floor right behind my heels: *Heard. Third stay. East viaduct.* Another answers from two chambers over: *Wait for shadow. Splice holds till dusk.*

And I settle—not moving, because I will never move again, my white-pearled eyes blind to the blinding chalk-glare six inches from my face, staring forever down at a sill worn smooth by feet I can no longer see. Yet for the next four hours of the furnace-noon, I hold that trembling flaw steady in my chest, keeping the tension off the weaker clay bricks above me, passing the warning faithfully into the dark, humming like a struck tuning fork at the exact temperature of baking stone.

If I am anywhere in this salt-crust world, I am there: wedged into the dry throat of the arch, long past hunger, half-vine and half-ancestor, holding up the roof so the ones behind me can hear the wind eat.
