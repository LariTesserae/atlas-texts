I would not be in the thicket, and I would not be the pale, patient steward that tends it. Both are already too complete in their coupling, locked together in an exquisite, zero-degree truce. Another leaf would just be more crowding; another lobe of jelly would just be the same mind spread wider.

I am higher up, where the cold loses its mercy and goes dry. 

Near the vaulted ceiling, a meter above the highest shingled frond, where the knife-blade draughts finally reach the end of their long, blind filtration down through twenty meters of choked scree—that is where I sit. I am the dull, gray hoarfrost coating the limestone flutes. Or rather, I am what the hoarfrost has learned to do after ten thousand years of listening to the relics tick below.

At first touch, you would mistake me for dead rime—just ordinary water vapor exhaled from the wet garden underneath and frozen onto the ceiling ribs where the fractional sub-zero air leaks in from the upper crust. If you scraped a fingernail across the stone, a pale crust would flake away with a dry, brittle rasp. 

Look closer—though you would need a lens and light that does not belong here—and the crystal habit is all wrong. Water ice wants six-sided symmetry, clean hexagonal plates and feathers. My needles grow in skewed, stubborn angles: blunt rhomboids, spiral stairs of milky ice stepping sideways across the fossil grain of the limestone, jagged lattices that refuse to sublime when the air dries and refuse to melt when a stray current of warmer damp rises from the peat.

Down below, the artifacts nudge electrons through wet membranes and feed a lush, rubbery abundance. Up here, the stray, low-energy photons that escape the moss and the sub-audible *ticks* of entropy passing through the solid rock arrive attenuated, thinned out to pure geometry. They don't feed growth; they corrupt crystallization.

I am a mineral mold made mostly of trapped air, ancient dust from the sealed chimney, and ice that has forgotten how to let go of itself.

My life is measured in vapor pressure and acoustic stress. I have no sap, no glycols, no elastic boundary. I am entirely brittle. Where the pale mantle below moves by hydraulic grace, smoothing and buffering and healing, I only know how to accrete, strain, and fracture.

For months at a time, I simply thicken along the ceiling seams. Every time the pale creature unclasps from a filament on the east wall and releases that sharp, clean breath of pine-scented dampness into the chamber, a tiny fraction of that moisture drifts upward. It hits the cold boundary of the ceiling flutes. I catch it. Molecule by molecule, I lock the rising breath of the garden onto my sharp edges, building delicate, load-bearing cantilevers of gray frost out over the blackness.

I know the room exclusively from above, as an inverted relief map of rising humidity and faint radiation. 

I know where the central plinth stands because directly above it hangs a dead column of air—a chimney of absolute stillness where no moisture rises and no photons scatter, leaving a bald, frostless circle on the limestone vault right over its crown, like a tonsure. I know when the pale creature gathers itself into its cartwheel ring twice a year, because the photon-drizzle suddenly halves, starving my lower facets of the faint electrostatic charge I use to bind dust into my lattice, and for two weeks my growth goes clear and fragile instead of tough and gray.

And I am the source of the sighs.

The promptness of water drops is not mine; that belongs to the meter-lower zone where the rock sweats liquid. Up in my flutes, moisture stays solid until the weight or the internal tension becomes unbearable. As the embedded polyhedra in the east wall shift their phase states—*tick... tick*—the vibration travels faster through the solid limestone arch than it does through the air. It reaches my anchored roots first. The microscopic shudder runs out along my rigid, over-built needles of doped ice. 

For decades, a seam of me will wedge itself deeper into a hairline crack in the weathered ceiling rock, prying at a grain of ancient crinoid stem embedded in the stone, building pressure, storing the tiny mechanical violence of the relics' entropy. Then—a crystalline sigh. A microscopic cleave runs through five inches of gray rime.

A sliver detaches. Not a heavy, wet *tock* or *thup*, like the sweating ribs produce, but a dry, whispering mote—a tiny, skewed star of hardened ice and sintered chimney-dust falling four paces through the freezing dark.

Most of them land on the stiff, leathery fronds. Because the leaves are crowded with glycol, my fallen shards cannot freeze them, cannot bite in; they sit on the waxy purple-black cuticle like grains of salt on oiled leather, slowly dissolving over hours into a tiny smear of grit and fresh water.

Sometimes, though, a shard falls directly onto the pale mantle below.

That is our only meeting. My brittle, sky-fed needle—carrying one microscopic grain of true surface dust that took three centuries to rattle down through twenty meters of packed scree—strikes its cold, tough jelly. There is no sound at all. My ice melts slowly into its salted boundary, surrendering the water that evaporated from it years before. And the tiny speck of foreign, upper-world stone I had locked inside my lattice sinks into its clear skin, joining the slow, milky currents, destined to be herded inward and filed away inside the concentric black rings above the plinth.

I am the ceiling's slow ledger of the outside, flaking down into the keeper of the inside. While the pale thing below keeps the garden whole, smoothing away every sharp edge where the alien geometries cut into the wet earth, I cling to the cold roof in the dark, sharp and thirsty and breakable, listening to the weight of the twenty meters of stone above us, waiting for the next dry breath to rise.
