My knees went fifty-odd tread-pins ago—not snapped, just filled up slow with the same mineral paste that cements the greywacke down in the damp pans, until climbing down past the third bogie-ledge meant holding everyone up while I hunted blind with my heel for the bolt-bosses. In the lean basins, an elder who cannot keep pace on the gravel usually unclips her harness during a dry watch, sits down cross-legged between the tracks with her staff across her knees, and lets the stern walk away from her before the dish-eared things have to work too hard.

They wouldn’t let me unclip. Not out of softness—nobody up here has the fat to spare for softness—but because my jawbone is thinner than theirs, or hollower in the right places, and my left cataract went opaque twenty sleeps before my right, leaving me nothing to waste my attention on except the pitch.

So I live thirty-five feet off the stone, wedged into the narrow, ninety-degree pocket behind the portside secondary turbine housing, lashed in with two belly-straps of greased glider-hide so I don’t roll out if I doze when we crest a steep whale-back. I haven’t touched gravel in four pregnancies. My bare soles have lost their horn; they are soft as grub-skins now, pale and creased, tucked up under my thighs to keep them clear of the rivet-heads that run ten degrees hotter than the hull-plate.

People down on the scree think the bellows-back makes one sound. Even the hunters up on the spine, with their noses full of lichen-mash and ozone, think it’s just the fifty-hertz purr and the *shhhk-shhhk* of the sixteen shoes and the *thump* when the purple light clears its throat.

It isn't one sound. It’s eleven. Twelve when the air goes thick before a horizontal drizzle and the upper intake louvers drop a quarter-inch on their worms to keep the compressors from choking.

I keep a short piece of bone—not root-wood, wood drinks damp and goes mushy in the grain, this is the forearm of a glider, hollow and dry as salt—wedged between my back molars and the main structural rib behind my ear. All watch long, while the children sleep below me in the shrub-slings and the flankers trot out in the plum dark with their digging-irons, I chew the engine.

I know four hours before the water-catchers do when the rear condenser pipe is going to slack off, because the third pump forward of the stern changes from a smooth *hmmmm* to a tight, fluttering *hmn-hmn-hmn* as the mineral crust builds up inside its throat. When I feel that flutter in my eye-socket, I reach down with my long toe and tap twice on the top hammock-rope. Within ten minutes a twelve-year-old comes scrambling up the dark alloy, smells my sour old-woman sweat to find my shoulder, and puts her collarbone against my knuckles.

*Tap-scrape-tap.* Short water ahead. Tell the stern-runners to top off every gut-bladder now, don't wait for the flat pan.

I know when we are coming up on buried basalt before the prow lamp shows a thing, because the electromagnetic skimmers underneath us hate clean volcanic stone—there’s not enough loose iron dust in it to feed them, so their field-coils whine up a minor third, hunting harder, pulling an extra amp off the main bus that makes the alloy rib behind my back swell by the thickness of an eyelash. *No grubs for six hours,* I tap down the line. *Sleep now, save your tallow.*

That would be enough to earn my wedge of root and my four swallows of warm pipe-water. That’s the trade.

Except there is a thirteenth sound, and I haven't tapped a word of it into anyone's collarbone since the last tangent-crossing.

Number four bogie on the starboard side—down on the far flank from me, buffered through eighty feet of condenser plumbing and heavy frame—has a hairline split in its main outer bearing race.

Nobody down there can hear it over the gravel crunch. Even the brush-children who sweep the silt out of that joint twice a sleep haven't felt it, because the housing isn't running hot yet. The sealed grease inside those old casings is miracle-stuff; it heals itself around grit for a thousand years. All it lets through right now, once every nine revolutions of the drive-sprocket, is a tiny, bright *tink*—smaller than a midge hitting a waxy leaf—that travels straight up the cross-brace, skips across the empty isotope tank in the belly, and lands right in my left molar.

*Shhhk-shhhk-shhhk... tink.*

I’ve been counting the spaces between the *tinks* while I braid sinew in the dark. Four pregnancies ago, it only happened when the starboard side took the heavy downhill lean across an angled wash. Two pregnancies ago, it started doing it on level greywacke. Since the sulfur-smelling band crossed us in the northeast basin and we traded the bladder-stitcher for young Keth’s boy, it does it climbing uphill too. And the grease-housing, when I sent a nimble eight-year-old across the spine yesterday on a dummy errand to check for baked beetles and tell me how warm the metal felt against his cheek, is running half a thumb-width hotter than number three.

I know what happens when a bearing race finally goes. I don't know it from seeing it—nobody alive has seen a bellows-back break—I know it from the bone-stories murmured into my chest when I was small enough to ride inside my father’s felt wrap. *The Hill That Walked South.* An old story, worn smooth as a knuckle. A rig that coughed steam out of its belly, locked one shoe for twenty heartbeats while its brain counted its own teeth, and then turned forty degrees across the grain of the basins, ignoring the damp air, ignoring the iron drift, walking faster than its usual amble—three miles an hour, maybe three and a half—straight toward the unseen rifts where the air falls off the edge of the world and boils.

The band in the story stayed on it too long, waiting for it to resume its grazing circuit, until the rock underneath went bare of lichen and hot enough to blister rawhide through the grease, and the air smelled of acid instead of wet iron. Only four of the fastest flank-walkers made it back upslope to catch the hum of another circuit before their bladders went dry.

My bone-splinter goes *tink*.

Down below me, the new boy from the sulfur-band—he still smells faintly of rotten eggs under the tallow we rubbed into him, and he flinches a little when the purple flash pops—is just learning to sleep without gripping the edge of his hammock. Below him, Marn’s daughter is swollen six months along, slow on the ledges, eating double shares of white grub-paste. Twenty-six of us. Twenty-seven soon.

If number four bearing holds together for another fifty shed pins, Marn’s baby will be walking, and we might graze tangent with another amber light in the wide silt-pan east of here. Even if the other band is full—say twenty-four or twenty-five strong—you can split twenty-six people across two or three crossings if you have warning. Four extra mouths on another hull for half a year is hard, it means everybody goes thin in the ribs and digs deeper for sour roots, but it isn't death. Not like twenty-six people dumped all at once onto empty scree forty miles from another hum.

And if it doesn't hold? If the diagnostic halt hits twenty sleeps from now, in the middle of the dry basalt waste?

If I tap it out tonight to the elders, fear enters the hull. You can't hide fear among twenty-six people who sleep touching; it sours the skin-smell instantly. They will start hoarding dried grub-meal instead of eating enough to keep up with the stern. The outriders will range too wide, wearing themselves down to gristle listening to the bedrock for another fifty-hertz beat that isn't there yet, missing their footing in the dark where the dish-ears wait. Worse—some clever, desperate twenty-year-old with a cold-hammered digging-iron might get it into his head to crawl down above number four shoe with a lump of rendered glider-fat and try to pack it into the seam to quiet the *tink*, and trip a proximity sensor, and send the engine marching for the rift tomorrow instead of next year.

The drizzle starts—not falling, just arriving sideways out of the blackness, warm and smelling suddenly sharp of wet rust and static. Down on the lower rib, hands rustle in the dark, pulling oiled felt wraps over the sleeping children. Somebody just below my dangling, soft-soled feet hums a three-note comfort-drone into a baby’s back so the wet breeze won't wake it.

*Thump.*

Violet light stamps the basin out of nothing: forty yards away, our three flankers are trotting inward toward the hull, heads down, digging-irons slung, shiny with mist like dark beetles; fifty yards behind the stern, four eyeless grazers lift their pitted snouts from our warm water-drips, pale and placid; then the dark shuts like a fist.

I spit the glider-bone into my palm for a minute to rest my jaw. Without it, the bellows-back sounds completely whole—vast, warm, unstoppable, humming its deep maternal note right through my ribs as it shoulders up the invisible grade. Any fool would trust it forever.

I wipe the slick condensation off my knuckles onto my wrap, fit the dry bone back between my molars, press my ear hard to the wet, sweating alloy, and go back to counting the spaces between the *tinks*, waiting to feel the bedrock tremble with a second hill before this one remembers it has somewhere else to be.
