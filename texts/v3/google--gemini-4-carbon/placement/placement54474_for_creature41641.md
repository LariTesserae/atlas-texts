I am not up in the wind. I do not want the wind, or the false distances, or the harsh brass honking of the birds. Even pressed flat as spilled oil, the surface is too exposed—too many unbuffered sky-bruises, too much raw, unsteered skipping. 

I am forty feet down, where the twilight finally gives up and the true, crushing dark of the machine begins. 

I am at the hinge. Where my upper margin tapers off, it is still supple enough to flex, heavy and slick with bog-grease, tasting the cold downward trickle of rainwater that still smells faintly of ozone and crushed celery from the spinning wheels above. My lower half, though, has forgotten how to slide. Over the last eighty years, the pale, blind rootlets of a secondary intake valve have stitched themselves straight through my gristle, pinning me tight against the curve of a lignified moss-artery as thick around as a silo. 

I am becoming a gasket. It is a tremendous relief.

Every four seconds, the artery swells against my belly—a massive, taut gathering of frigid, tannin-black slush—and then *fires*. The thump doesn't hit me like a blow anymore; it passes through me like a thought. My copper-threaded fascia rings with it, a low, satisfying *hum* that vibrates down into my saline bladders and bleeds out through the frayed ends of my nerves into the peat-felt.

I am only half-anchored, which makes me a translator. 

Below me, miles deep in the circulating ice-water where no light has ever bruised, the truly ancient ones hang in the roaring plumbing. Their thoughts come up through the copper-laced root-web not as pictures or words, certainly, and not even as plans, since there is nothing to plan for. They come up as *timing*. Slow, heavy, rhythmic cramps of pure voltage rising from the pitch-black slush: *hold, hold, bleed left, slacken.* Long memories of pressures that built up two centuries ago on the eastern shelf and are only just now returning as rebounding waves through the deep felt. 

Above me, forty feet up through the tight, wet weave, the drumskin stutters and shrieks. I feel the sharp, frantic clicking of the young surface-sheets as they zip and unzip across the sedge. I feel the light, rapid-fire needle-pricks of the blubber-birds sprinting past in their thousands, and the deep, rhythmic *clunk-clunk* of a stilt-grazer’s cartilage knees compensating right over my head. 

My job—though it isn’t a job, it is just how you rest comfortably when you are pinned to a roaring pipe—is to take the fast, ragged static of the ceiling and smooth it out before passing it down, and to take the slow, immense cramps of the deep dark and sharpen them into warnings for the skins above.

Right now, two hundred yards west of my seam, the geometry is going sour. 

I don’t see it; I taste it in my copper. The wet, iron-rich slush squeezing past my margins suddenly loses its weight for a tenth of a second, turning frictionless and weirdly cold, tasting of nothing—not clean water, not vacuum, just an absence of pressure where pressure ought to be. Two lesser veins are firing out of phase. Above them, I can feel three surface-mantles sliding greedily toward the thinning patch, their edges clicking fast, preparing to zip together and choke a vent to farm the skip. 

They are too young to feel what is directly underneath that intersection: a sunken brood-pocket, tucked just six feet beneath the sedge, where twenty palm-sized mantles—budded last season, their copper barely hair-thick, their suction-pits still soft—are clinging to a warmish bladder of rotting peat-sugar. If the surface-hunters force a slab-skip there, the square hole of starry dark won't just shear a few birds; it will tear the roof right off the pocket and spill the young upward into the inverted rain before they know how to flatten against the gale.

I don't think *save the young*. I simply feel an intolerable dissonance in the rhythm, like a grit of ancient sand caught in a rasp-mouth.

I draw every volt of cold static I have stored in my radial bladders—charge trickled down to me from a spinning lichen-wheel three days ago—and I clench my lower, root-stitched half with everything I have left. 

It hurts, in a dull, oxidized way; tearing slightly at the white rootlets that feed me sugar, making my copper bands ache with sudden heat. By cramping hard against the side of the silo-thick artery just as it inhales, I dent its wall by three inches. Not much. Nothing can stop the pump. But a three-inch dent in a high-pressure intake throws a premature flutter into the valve. 

*Thump-thump—th-thump.*

The artery fires half a beat early. 

The shockwave blasts laterally through the woven felt, missing the western intersection entirely and instead slamming into an empty stretch of sedge sixty yards north. Up on the drumskin, I feel the ridge erupt early—I feel the sudden, surprised scramble of the three surface-hunters as their carefully stalked tension dissipates beneath them, bleeding harmlessly away into a sixty-mile-an-hour freight-train wave of black slush that races off toward the horizon. 

One of the hunters slides directly over my seam a moment later, irritation crackling off its underside in jagged, salty twitches of static. I let a tiny pulse of my own charge rise through the forty feet of wet moss to brush its belly: *deep-pocket, thin-floor, off-beat.* 

The hunter clicks once, sharp and acknowledging, unhooks from its neighbors, and pours itself eastward after the wave.

Down in the dark, the silt settles. The bruised rootlets threading my hide slowly drink the lactic acid and leaked copper-salts from my cramp, trading me a slow, cold seep of bog-sugar in return. Below me, from two miles down in the thunderous slush, a faint, broad ripple of voltage washes upward through the network and passes across my nerves—not gratitude, just the quiet, heavy settling of a governor weights dropping back into balance. 

I flatten my upper margin a little tighter against the trembling wall of the moss-vein, letting the freezing black water wash over my back. In another twenty years, the white roots will have stitched my upper edge down, too. I won't feel the stilt-grazers anymore, or smell the faint celery-tang of the surface rain. I will just be tension and copper, listening to the miles-wide drumbeat from the inside, keeping time in the dark while the roof of the world tears itself apart and sews itself shut overhead.
