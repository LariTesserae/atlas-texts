I am three tiers down from the open light, wedged on my belly between a raw prism of dark greywacke and the unmortared underside of a widow’s hearth, holding a brass spirit-lamp with the wick turned so low its flame is no bigger than a lentil. 

The air down here in the talus-crawl does not move at all. It smells of wet iron, old mutton-fat, and the faint, sharp cellar-scent of quicklime coming off a sealed burial niche two boulders to my left—someone’s grandfather, dry as pressed bracken behind two inches of pale plaster, helping hold up the pantry floor. My ribs rest on a folded square of thick, felted grey wool so my breathing won't scrape leather against stone. Even down here under forty feet of stacked rooms, you don't let a buckle click.

In front of my nose, spanning a black gap between two shattered slabs of bedrock, is a sheet-web eight inches wide. The spider herself is tucked into a sharp V-notch at the back—fat-bellied, pale-legged, motionless. Yesterday morning her silk hung slack and dusty, a soft hammock catching stray rock-flour. Today the front anchor-threads along the lower slab are pulled taut as fiddle-strings, gleaming faintly in my tiny yellow light, and three strands on the eastern edge have snapped clean and curled back into little white beads.

The lower boulder didn’t drop. It rotated—just a fraction, tilting away from the upper scarp. 

I don’t reach for my brass calipers yet. First I pull off my right rush-sandal—not on my foot, I use my bare toes when crawling the under-vaults, tough-skinned and grey with slate-dust—and press the flat of my palm directly against the cold face of the greywacke. I close my eyes and let my weight settle through my wrist, listening with the small bones of my arm.

From overhead comes the city’s pulse, filtered through timber props and dry-laid stone until it isn't sound anymore, just texture. Fifty feet up and to the north, two sawyers are working a limestone block: *shhh-shhh, shhh-shhh*, the wet quartz grit biting evenly, paused every twenty strokes while the boy pours another ladle of grey water into the kerf. Closer, right above my shoulder blades, someone walks across the kitchen flags in felted soles—three soft, rolling *thuds* from ball to heel—and sets a heavy clay pitcher down on a wooden table without a clink. And threaded through all of it, continuous as blood in an ear pressed to a pillow, is the tiny, cold *tik-tik-tik* of an alder-wood water pipe sweating condensation onto a slate catch-tray behind the cistern wall.

Underneath those rhythms, the bedrock itself feels tight. Not trembling—not yet—just holding its breath.

I open my eyes, unhook the small pearwood case at my hip, and take out two splinters of kiln-dried cedar no thicker than barley-awnings, along with a thumb-pot of cold pine rosin warmed just enough against my belly-skin to take a dent from a fingernail. 

I am not one of the senior Seam-Readers who walk the high cliff-ledges at dawn in clean wool shawls, squinting into bowls of black oil while the porters step aside for them. My knees are too scarred for their calm dignity, and my shoulders grew a little crooked from six boyhood years hauling wet clay up from the fault-pits before an old Plumb-Warden noticed I could tell when a retaining wall had bellied out a quarter-inch from thirty paces away just by how the light hit the chinks. They gave me the cellars, the deep drains, and the wet-crawl where the city’s belly rests directly on the broken teeth of the graben. Up on the roofs they call us *Sill-Mice*—always spoken with that soft, chest-deep murmur that keeps the word from bouncing, half teasing, half careful respect, because nobody sleeps easy if the Sill-Mice haven't checked the wedges under their bedposts.

With the tip of my thumb, I press a bead of rosin onto each end of a cedar splinter and bridge the narrowest throat of the crack, right beside the spider’s torn guy-lines. I breathe on the resin—slow, open-mouthed, silent—Wait ten heartbeats until it clouds hard and brittle, then lay a scrap of bleached lamb’s wool on the flat stone ledge beneath it. If the boulder turns another hair’s breadth before morning, the cedar will pop and lie white-on-white when I crawl back in.

I slide backward out of the stone pocket, pushing with toes and elbows, keeping my scarp-apron strapped tight around my ribs. Where the wooden trapdoor lifts into the lower alley behind the lime-slaking vats, the hinges don't need my tallow-horn; somebody greased the oak pins within the hour, leaving dark, shiny crescents on the wood that smell faintly of scorched rosemary—the fat-renderers up on the third tier always throw bruised herbs from the window-boxes into the mutton-pots to cut the tallow-stink.

Stepping out into the stepped alley feels like surfacing through cold lake water, even though the open sky is still only a dazzling, ragged ribbon of pale blue sixty feet above the eaves.

Here in the mid-tiers, the noon light bounces down between walls of pale limestone and wet dark slate, turning the air luminous and grey-silver. Two yards away, an old woman sits on an overturned pine tub outside her doorway, dressing the edge of a slate roof-tile with a crescent-shaped iron crunching-plier—not striking it, never striking, just squeezing tiny half-moon bites off the stone margin with both thick wrists: *crunch... crunch... crunch*, soft as a horse chewing dry oats on the plain above. Dust coats her knuckles like flour on a baker’s hands.

As I pull the trapdoor shut, letting its felt-rimmed lip settle flush into the frame, she glances up. She doesn't speak. She just lifts her chin a quarter-inch—*Well?*—her pale-lashed eyes dropping briefly to the chalk-pouch at my belt.

I hold up my left hand, palm flat and level, then tip the little finger down just perceptibly before tapping two fingers once against my collarbone. *Slight creep. Watching it. Leave the walls standing.*

The corner of her mouth tugs inward, satisfied, and her pliers take another quiet bite of slate.

I start up the Long Cant, setting the balls of my rush-shod feet onto the worn centers of the stone treads, knees loose, letting my hips swallow my own weight at every step. You can always spot a rim-trader come down the switchbacks with bales of raw fleece or bundled birch-poles: within twenty minutes their shins ache and their jaws are clenched from trying not to stomp, and they walk stiff-legged as storks, terrified of knocking an elbow against a stacked buttress and bringing the whole grey honeycomb down around their ears. They think the city is fragile. 

It isn't fragile. Glass is fragile; mortared brick is fragile. A mortared wall fights the mountain until its back breaks all at once. What we live inside is five thousand dry-stacked stone baskets leaning into each other’s shoulders. Everything gives a little so nothing falls.

Halfway up the stair, where a timber crane-platform juts out over a three-story drop into the copper-washers' court, I pause to let a line of four stair-porters pass downward. They move in lockstep, bent forward under sixty-pound pack-frames stacked with damp yellow sod cut from the prairie yesterday—black earth held together by tough mat-grass roots, smelling shockingly rich and sweet down here where nothing rots and nothing grows on its own. A tiny brown beetle clings to a grass-blade sticking out of the last porter's load, bewildered by the vertical world of clean-split pyrite and cold stone. We brush wool shoulders in the thirty-inch gap without breaking stride; the last man smells of sweat, cold mist, and sheep-grease, his breathing deep and steady through his nose.

Out on the crane-walk, I lean my hip against the stout pine rail—pausing automatically to rub a thumbful of tallow from my belt-horn into a dry white patch where a hemp stay-rope chafes its wooden cleat—and look across the basin.

From here you can see almost a mile to the eastern scarp. The sheer cliff rises straight out of the highest terraced roofs like an axe-cut through dark cheese, banded in slate-blue, buff limestone, and wet black shale. Halfway up that raw wall, the Great Weep glistens in the steep sun—a quarter-mile seam of severed groundwater shining like polished pewter, threaded underneath with hundreds of tiny, slanted slate gutters and pale wooden trestles where the water-wardens move like midges along the wet ledges. Smoke from the lower lime-kilns rises in dozens of ruler-straight blue threads through the dead-calm air of the bowl, until every single plume hits the invisible ceiling at rim-height and whips flat toward the south, torn away by a prairie wind we cannot feel or hear.

Suddenly, across the stone-yards below me, a crane-boom stops mid-swing, a basket of charcoal hanging motionless twenty feet off the flags.

Beside me on the platform, the crane-girl—twelve years old, dark hair powdered pewter-grey, a bright square of raw pyrite pinned at her throat—lifts both hands from the windlass spokes. Palms flat. Stillness.

I don't turn my head; turning your head makes your collar rustle. I just stop breathing and raise my own hands shoulder-high, joining the quiet wave rippling up the tiers from west to east. Below us, the copper-saws stop their *shhh-shhh*. Three roofs down, a woman lifting a wet woollen shirt toward a drying-pole freezes with her arms raised. Even a small, prick-eared terrier trotting along a coping-stone halts with one front paw tucked up against its chest, ears swiveling toward the western wall.

Five thousand people become part of the cliff.

In that immense, heavy pool of silence, you can hear every small water-runnel in the district trickling down its clay throat. You can hear the faint, dry *tick* of cooling slag in a smelting-hearth four terraces below. And then, from high up on the raw greywacke behind us—near the new switchback cut—comes what stopped the cranes:

*Chkk... chkk-shhhhhh... tup.*

Not a slab letting go. Just a fist-sized prism of frost-wedged shale detaching from a dry bedding plane two hundred feet up, skittering six yards down a bare ledge, and dropping squarely into one of the woven-osier catch-cribs hung along the upper wall for exactly that purpose.

We hold the silence ten seconds longer, letting the cliff finish its sentence. Nothing follows. No deep groin-groan in the rock, no secondary patter of grit.

Across the yard, a senior Plumb-Warden standing beside a roof-cistern lowers one hand and makes a small, circular wipe in the air—*Clear, settle.* 

The crane-girl lets her breath out in a tiny white puff, lays her greased palms back on the oak spokes, and leans her small, broad-shouldered weight into the wheel. The tallowed axle turns without a murmur; the charcoal basket resumes its slow arc across the alley; below us, *shhh-shhh, shhh-shhh*, the copper blades take up their patient argument with the limestone.

I take a piece of soft white chalk from my pouch, walk twelve paces down the sunny side of a terrace wall where three wooden leek-boxes sit warm in the noon glare, and make a tiny, neat dot—no slant-line yet, just a dot—low down on the corner-stone of the widow’s house, right where the district Warden will see it on her evening round and know a Sill-Mouse has a splinter set underneath.

Before heading down into the next wet-cellar, I pinch half an inch off the tip of a bruised green leek hanging over the plank box—nobody begrudges a Seam-Reader a bite of sharp green to clear the stone-flour from his spit—and set it on my tongue. It tastes fiercely of black prairie dirt and upland sun. Below my rush-sandaled soles, massive and cool and freshly broken, two miles of sunken earth lies quiet in its cradle, and I go back down between the stones to listen to how it wants us to live on it tomorrow.
