I am not at the center of the basin, where the weight is oldest and the cisterns drop down into an abyss of drowned timber. I am three miles out, near the high rim—or what passes for a rim in a place without horizon—where the raw materials enter the dark.

Here, broad timber slides, slick with tallow and black mud, angle down out of the low ceiling of wet soot. No one goes up them. Things only come down. Every quarter-hour, a chain clanks somewhere up in the fog, a wooden gate lifts, and a two-ton bundle of barked pine poles or a sledge of virgin, unsplit blue slate roars down the chute, throwing sparks off the iron guide-rails before crashing into a massivearresting-crib of loose scree. 

I work forty yards below the chutes, in the deafening throat of the sorting tiers. 

If you saw me—though you would smell the scorched suet and wet iron filings first—you would think I was one of the Boys grown too thick in the shoulder to fit the drainage chimneys anymore, yet lacking the bowed, oak-stump legs of the block-layers. My alder clogs are carved thinner at the sole so I can feel the tilt of an unsettled slab before it bites, and my left sleeve is wrapped from wrist to armpit in strips of green rawhide, slick with rock-slime. 

My work is not lifting; it is listening for the lie in the stone. 

All this weight—five miles of unmortared slate climbing tier upon tier—holds together only because rock bites rock. Without lime or sand to bind the courses, friction is the only law keeping the upper terraces from avalanching into the central trough. A single slab with a hidden frost-fracture or a greasy vein of talc inside it will hold fifty tons for a month, and then shear silently in the night, spitting out a retaining wall and dropping three derricks and four masons into a dry-stone gorge.

So before the sledges go down into the maze, I tap them.

I carry no heavy maul. Slung across my chest is a belt of twelve small hammers, graded from the weight of a sparrow to the weight of a clenched fist, their heads forged from softer iron than the masons’ wedges so they will not shatter the edge they strike. In my left hand is a split-ash tallow-pan with a tin reflector bent behind the wick to throw a narrow, fierce blade of orange light across the face of a block.

When a fresh sledge groans to a halt on its greased rollers, I step out of my niche between two hurdles. The haulers do not wait for me; they unhook their hemp traces, tap twice on the timber rail to signal the windlass up in the fog, and vanish back into the side-alleys. I am alone with ten tons of cold, sweating quarry-spoil that smells of wherever *outside* is—sometimes a sharp, alien tang of peat-water, sometimes nothing at all, just the iron stink of deep earth.

I set my lamp on the top stone. I pull the third hammer from my belt. I do not swing from the shoulder; I drop the iron from the wrist—*tink.*

A good stone answers like a bronze bell muffled in wool: a tight, short *thud-ring* that travels straight down through the grain and hits the soles of my clogs as a single, crisp tap. When I hear that, I dip a stiff bristle-brush into a bucket of rendered fat mixed with red iron-ochre at my hip, draw a short, greasy slash across the wet slate, and step to the next block.

Yesterday—or five sleeps ago, since there is only the burning down of clay pans to measure time—the third sledge after my barley-gruel answered wrong. 

It was a massive lintel stone, nine feet long, squared rough at some unseen quarry beyond the soot-cloud, meant to span a cistern-shaft down in the middle tiers. When my hammer kissed its flank, the sound didn't drop to my clogs. It skittered sideways along the block and died with a flat, papery *clack*, like two dry boards slapped together. 

I leaned in until my pitch-visor scraped the wet stone. My ears are better than the masons’—I stuff them with oiled wool whenever the big shear-legs are booming nearby, hoarding my hearing the way the Boys hoard dropped iron wedges. I laid my bare cheek against the freezing slate, right in the grey paste, and tapped again with the smallest hammer. *Tink.*

Deep in the belly of the nine-foot block, two inches behind the adze-marks, something buzzed. A hairline seam of rotten shale, invisible from the outside, running diagonally across the load-bearing grain. If the riggers slung that over a shaft and piled three courses of rubble crib atop it, it would snap like a carrot before the tallow cooled in the pans.

I did not mark it with red ochre. Instead, I drew a cross on its flank with white chalk-stone, stepped back, and pulled the heaviest tool I carry from the loop at my spine: a short, stubby iron gad and a four-pound lump-hammer. 

If you leave a bad stone whole, someone in the dark will eventually use it. The maze is too hungry, the momentum too blind; a builder three tiers down needing a nine-foot span will scrape off chalk in the gloom, assume the mark was an accident, and lever it into place. The only way to keep poison out of the foundation is to kill the span right on the sledge.

I found the faintest pale thread on the upper bevel where the rotten seam kissed the air. I set the iron gad, spat on my horn-calloused palm so the haft wouldn't twist, and hit it three times. Not the great, lunging *huff-shhh* of the block-splitters, just three sharp, spiteful snaps of the wrist.

The nine-foot lintel didn't crack like a gunshot. It sighed—a wet, grinding *shhhk*—and slumped apart on the sledge into two useless, irregular wedges, spilling a handful of grey, soapy clay out of its rotten heart onto the timber rollers. They would still use the halves, of course; nothing here is wasted. The Boys would drag the pieces to a chute and tip them into a rubble-crib for dead-weight fill. Better loose scree in a timber box than a treacherous bridge over a drop.

That is my whole territory: sixty yards of slick slate platform between the thunder of the chutes and the first drop-off of the maze, lit by four clay pans I tend myself. 

I rarely go down into the basin proper anymore, except once every twenty or thirty lamps when my hammers mushroom at the face and must be dressed at the smith-bays four tiers below. Going down there makes my skin crawl—not from fear of the dark, which is everywhere, but from the *silence* inside the stone. As I descend the cantilevered stairs, passing beneath the roaring derricks and the shouting chutes, I find myself laying my mittened knuckles against every retaining wall I pass. *Solid. Solid. Solid. Shifting—that crib will slump before next gruel, stay clear of the alley beneath it. Solid.* 

I cannot turn it off. The whole five-mile valley sits in my teeth like an ache. Beneath my clogs, I feel the millions of unmortared corners grinding together in the cold damp, settling a sixteenth of an inch a year, squeezing the water down into the black shafts, holding up ten thousand tons of dead pine and cleaved rock by sheer, jagged stubbornness.

Sometimes, waiting between sledges while the tallow sputters and a pale scuttle-thing investigates the red ochre crust on my clog, I walk ten paces up toward the bottom of the timber chutes and look up into the soot-vapor where the iron chains disappear.

Once, caught in the rough sap-wood of an unbarked pine pole that had slipped past the adze-men outside, I found something that was neither stone, timber, nor tallow. It was wedged tight in a knot-hole, crushed flat by the chains: a three-inch sprig of stiff, dead needles, still faintly green beneath the grey mud, smelling so fiercely of sharp resin that it made my eyes water worse than pine-smoke. 

I pried it out with my thumb-pick. I didn't show it to the haulers when they came out of the gloom for the timber; we have no iron-taps for *green*, and if I held it out to one of the broad-backed women, she would only squint at my empty hand, check my boots to see why I was standing idle, and hook her trace to the sledge. 

I kept it in my rawhide pouch until it dried to brown dust and lost its smell, and then I tipped the dust into my clay lamp. It burned with three tiny, spitting sparks of white light before the tallow drowned it back to orange. 

Down in the dark, a bell-clear *clink* rings against an ash upright—two haulers waiting at the lip of the third terrace. Behind me, high in the wet black ceiling, the iron gate clanks lift, and the chute begins to roar with fresh weight coming down. I pull my pitch-visor low over my eyes, hook my little tin-backed lamp to my wrist, and reach for my third hammer.
