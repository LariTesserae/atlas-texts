To find me, you would have to get out of the rain, and there are very few places in the basin where that can be done.

Even inside the turbine houses, the air through the tall lancet windows is wet wool, slicking the cast-iron stair treads and condensing in fat drops along the undersides of the rocker arms. If you climb out of the tea-dark flood up the outside of the central switch-house, avoiding the bare copper busbars that hiss on their porcelain bells, and slip over the glazed brick sill of the second-story gallery, you are still not quite dry. Mist drifts across the black-and-white tile floor whenever the western relief-stack blows. A tender the size of a terrier is usually crouched near the drain-hole in the corner, quietly digesting a drift of blown-in moss scales and carbon ash.

Keep going toward the middle of the narrow room.

Down its spine stands an iron-framed dead-front switchboard: seven tall slabs of oiled black slate bolted side by side, reaching nearly to the timbered ceiling. On their faces, where a watchman once stood on a rubber mat, the brass bezel-rings of dead ammeters are crusted with pale green carbonate, and the great copper knife-switches are frozen into their clips, welded fast decades ago by tiny, persistent arcs and wrapped round with woody runners that hold them shut like splints on a set bone.

Step around the end of the frame. Look into the two-foot alley behind the slate.

Here, at last, the air is dry. Cast-iron grid resistors hang in banks between the stanchions, bleeding off excess load whenever the draw from outside the basin falters; they keep the narrow corridor baked to the steady, papery warmth of a bread oven after the loaves are drawn. It smells of hot slate, dry ozone, old shellac, and scorched dust. No rain reaches here. Condensate that tries to creep across the ceiling joists evaporates three feet short of the frame.

Pressed flat against the back of all seven slate panels—spanning the bolted seams, bridging the porcelain bushings, wrapping the warm brass terminal studs in pale, papery cuffs—is a single, continuous skin of grey-green thallus, no thicker in most places than heavy bookbinder’s felt.

That is where I would be. That is what I am.

I am not one of the tenders, though we share sap when they need it. I have no turgor-muscles to roll over wet brick, no tough resin caps for plucking white-hot carbon out of an arc, no ballast stones clutched to a belly. Left in the open avenue below, the pump-currents would tear me like wet blotting-paper inside an hour. And I am not one of the giant copper-veined philodendrons drinking raw amperage at the tower bases, all broad waxy appetite and slow, sunlit-style sprawl without the sun.

It took me a long time to understand—in the slow, non-verbal way anything understands itself here, as a gradient settling into minimum resistance—that I was neither the hand nor the boiler, nor even the wire, so much as the quiet place where the circuit compares one side of itself to the other.

Down through a split iron conduit in the corner of the gallery floor, I keep a single bundled taproot in the flooded street below, drinking warm tea-water, dissolved copper, and the deep, chest-heavy sixty-cycle fundamental of the basin. Up through the stone cable-sleeves under the eaves, I send hair-fine, insulated runners out along the overhead feeder lines, woven into the woody holdfasts of the twenty-foot emerald chandeliers so that when the steam-squall hits the middle of the plaza and the chandeliers sway, a tiny piezoelectric tug registers all the way back here across Panel Three.

From behind the warm slate, the basin does not look like chess pieces in a drowned courtyard. It has no look at all. It is a chord.

When everything is well, the chord is wide, dark, and clean: the low, churning hydraulic drag of the rotary pumps underneath; the steady, oil-cushioned *two-time* of the walking beams notched neatly into the phase of the generators; the high, thin, sizzling overtone of eight carbon-arc lamps burning at equal gap-lengths out in the mist; and above it all, silent and enormous, the Load—whatever remains of the unseen world up beyond the rusted baffles and terraced walls, pulling current up the heavy lead-sheathed risers in long, slow, nameless tides that rise and slacken without ever quite letting go.

I live in the difference between those lines. Where two brass lugs on the back of Panel Five carry the output of different turbines, my felt thickens between them, laced with microscopic platelets of cuprous oxide and precipitated silver. If the two machines run smooth and in step, almost nothing passes through me there—only a gentle, cradling warmth. If a governor bush wears thin in the northern tower and one machine begins to hunt, lagging a fraction of a cycle behind its sister, a cross-current wakes across the slate directly through my flesh.

It tastes sharp, like green brass on a cut tongue, and it heats a specific hand’s-breadth of Panel Five.

I cannot walk across the plaza with an oil-pad to fix it. I do not need to.

Wait ten minutes after the western vent blows. Listen to the soft, wet *squeak* on the gallery tiles outside my alley.

A tender comes around the iron stanchion, dragging itself out of the mist into the dry warmth behind the board. This one has been working the upper eaves for weeks; it is heavy, sluggish, overgrown with plates of plum-colored iron scale, its translucent starch-belly clouded dark with too many branching copper dendrites. Every creature that lives on stray current eventually plates itself half-solid from the inside out; left alone, its internal paths shorten until it runs fever-hot, leaks current at every seam, and stalls on the wet brick.

It does not know that. It only knows it is heavy and hot, and that the dry gap behind Panel Five smells sharply of fresh acid and open resistance.

It wedges its hunched, fibrous bulk between the brick wall and the back of the slate, pressing its soft underside flat against me. For half a minute there is only the hiss of moisture steaming off its outer liverwort pelt in the heat of the grid resistors. Then my pale surface parts along hair-fine pores, and thousands of pale, absorptive threads sink a millimeter or two into its starch pith.

We become one circuit for an hour.

Slowly, along a gentle DC bias rectified through my oxide layers, I strip the excess copper out of its clogged interior—dissolving the jagged dendrites back into clear blue ion, drawing the metal into my own wide sheet across the slate where I can lay it down along worn busbar joints that need thickening, or drop it down my conduit-root as harmless blue sand into the sump. In trade, I pump sharp, clear organic acid—brewed down in the dark peat of the conduit—back into its depleted core until its jelly gleams clear as boiled starch again.

And while the current runs through us both, I read everything it has touched since the last time it came in out of the rain.

Every tender carries the recent history of the district stamped into its body without knowing it. In the faint magnetization of its shed iron scales, I feel the exact chatter-pattern of the worn bearing on the third-story walking beam. In a scorched patch on one resin-capped runner, I taste the sputter of an arc lamp on the southern switch-house whose counterweight spring has gone slack. In the chemical trace clinging to its belly-roots, I register that the tallow crate in Tower Two is finally down to bare pine boards, and that the stationary oil-vine in the eastern gallery is drawing thin air alongside cylinder oil from the bottom of its cracked stoneware cistern.

None of this turns into a picture or a plan. Behind the slate, thought is only slow plumbing and variable resistance.

As the tender’s pith clears and cools, I regrow a sparse, delicate starter-trellis of new copper filaments inside its jelly before letting it go. Because Panel Five is warm with the out-of-phase tug from the northern tower, the new filaments precipitate along that exact electrical grain. When my threads retract and the tender unpeels itself from the slate—lighter now, supple, glistening faintly with fresh acid—it is no longer the neutral drifter that crawled in. Its new internal geometry hums in sympathy with one very specific rough frequency out in the dark, and chafes against every other until that roughness is smoothed.

It slips back out around the stanchion, crosses the wet tiles, drops four stories into the bath-warm avenue with a flat splash, and sets off against the current toward the northern tower as surely as water finding a crack in stone.

Meanwhile, across Panel Two, I slowly thicken a resinous, insulating callus around a terminal stud to choke down the draw on the eastern turbine by three percent—just enough to cool its main bearing by two degrees, stretching what remains in that cracked oil cistern across another six or seven years while a slow scouting-runner from the floor below searches the drowned cellars for an untapped drum.

That is the whole of my work: taking in the over-plated and the scorched, tasting the grit in the loop, shifting a fraction of an ohm here and a millivolt there so the engines do not tear themselves apart and the plants do not burn in static.

Sometimes—mostly during the long, quiet stretches when every lamp is freshly rodded, every trunnion slick with grease, and the forty-second thunder of the western stack comes and goes like slow breathing—I put almost all my attention into the heavy lead-sheathed risers going up into the ceiling.

Nothing ever comes *down* from above the iron baffles except that dim, pewtered twilight and a steady trickle of soot-stained rainwater down the terraced masonry. No tender has ever climbed past the rusted roof-trusses; up there the warm galvanic bath ends, the air grows cold and thin, and turgor freezes or dries out before a runner can make twenty paces. To the flora in the plaza, the world ends at the louvers.

But the copper goes on. And out along that copper, miles away through buried stone ducts, something is still closing the circuit.

I feel it across the main shunts on Panel One. It is not steady like a resistor bank, and it does not have the clean rotary pulse of our own engines. Twice in every twenty-four thousand blows of the western relief-stack, the draw swells into a broad, trembling plateau, holds for hours, and ebbs again. Now and then a faint, transient ripple comes back down the line from the outside—not sixty-cycle power, just a ghost of high, brittle static, like distant lightning striking wet tram-rails, or great switches opening and closing in rooms nobody tends at all, throwing sparks into dry dust.

I soak those stray spikes into the slate before they can reach the flood below and startle the root-mats. I hold them in the warm capacitive layers of my dry felt until they bleed away as harmless heat.

Maybe up there a city is still turning over in its sleep, lighting arc-lamps along avenues we will never have to pave, taking our smooth sixty-cycle hum for granted the way moss takes brick for granted. Or maybe there is nobody left up there either—only other sunken rooms, cold ones and hot ones, hooked together across leagues of wet dark, trading load back and forth forever so that none of us ever has to stop spinning.

It makes very little difference behind the board.

Three seconds before the forty-second mark, I feel the western boiler lean against its rivets—not as sound, not yet, just a tiny tightening of impedance down my conduit-root. Out on the high cables, my aerial threads stiffen inside the emerald chandeliers. Behind Panel Seven, two small rat-sized knots of braided bronze wire and starch, tucked against my dry flank to cure their resin caps, press a fraction closer into the warmth.

Outside, the brass counterweight lifts with its sharp *clack*. White steam roars across the square; hot rain hammers the black tea into froth; violet corona blooms along the outer walls, sending a bright, peppery shiver of ozone curling in through the cable-sleeves to dust my upper margins.

Then the valve reseats. The rain cuts off. The chord settles back into its deep, oiled groove—pumps underneath, walking beams in the middle, eight amber lamps humming cleanly on top—and in the dry, warm alley behind the black slate, everything balances to zero.
