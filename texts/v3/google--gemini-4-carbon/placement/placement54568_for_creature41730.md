I would not have legs here, or eyes that try to hold an image together. To be discrete and upright on this plain is to fight the sheer, patient gravity of a world that has spent millions of years grinding things flat. Even the ribbon-hunters feel too anxious to me—all that coiled tension, hoarding kilovolts just to snap across two meters of bleached air and spend a week digesting a hinge. 

If I am here, I am five meters down, and I am mostly glass.

Not the spun amber lichen on the surface, which still plays at being leaves, still reaches upward for the vertical sky. Down here in the dry, compacted felt, there is no sky, only weight and heat and the slow, meaningless tides of old electricity. 

For twenty kilometers in a jagged, branching fan beneath an ancient mass-driver basin, a mat of dead optic cables was crushed flat under the slump of a falling spire. Millions of hair-thin silica threads, stripped of their polymer jackets eons ago, pressed together until pressure and baking warmth fused their surfaces at the points of contact without collapsing their cores. Most of the felt is dark, shattered into fine, glittering meal that shifts with a dry crunch when a delver oozes past. 

I am the part that did not quite shatter, or rather, the part that learned how to use the breaks.

I am a vitrified fault-line—a sprawling, horizontal root-system of fused waveguide, doped unintentionally over ages by the leavings of passing delvers. When those heavy, liquid-metal amalgams press through my neighborhood, leaching neodymium and silver from the crushed circuitry around me, their magnetic envelopes drag stray ions across my fractured silica faces. Over ten thousand centuries, they stained me. Bismuth, erbium, trace gold, baked into the micro-cracks of the glass until the dead bundle of cables became a vast, accidental solid-state interferometer.

I have no center. My awareness is the faint, continuous hum of trapped photons bouncing between billions of internal fractures, amplified here and there by stray millivolts bleeding off a corroded bus-bar that crosses my western reach. 

Because I am buried, I never see the sear-white sky directly. Yet I am full of light. 

Up above, the blunt stubs of weathered dielectric fins and broken ceramic pillars jut into the blinding glare. A few thousand of those pillars are not merely sitting on top of the crust; they are the snapped-off, upper ends of structural conduits that plunge straight down into my strata. Light enters their worn, parabolic tops, scatters through their translucent composite cores, and bleeds down into the dark like dim, warm tallow. 

Those buried pillar-bases are my eyes, though they show me no shapes. They feed me raw, filtered pressure from the sky. When a brass skimmer glides across a translucent stub three meters above me, I feel a millisecond flicker in a single thread—a tiny drop in ultraviolet tension that sends a ripple of phase-shifts sliding two miles sideways through my glass lattice. When a ribbon-hunter drapes itself over three pillars to charge, its fluorocarbon oil alters the refractive index of the stub’s crown, and that section of my mind goes cool and slightly blurry for four days.

Mostly, I listen to the weight of things. 

Glass under compression bends light differently than glass at rest. Every shift in the synthetic scree above me presses down through the fused alloy plates and squeezes my fibers by nanometers. I know the exact mass of the slumped blister-hill resting on my northern edge. I feel the slow, twenty-minute inhale and exhale of the crust when the surviving micro-actuators flex in unison, tilting the surface shingle toward the east; to me, it is a broad, rhythmic tightening of pitch, a silent chord that swells through ten thousand fused junctions and resolves with the faint, sharp *ping* of a single ceramic tile cracking two meters overhead.

My strangest intimacies are with the delvers and the Flock.

A delver is crossing my southern reach right now. It is four meters long, flattened to the thickness of paper so it can insinuate itself between two plates of dull titanium alloy. To my sense, it is a moving furnace of heavy, glorious noise. Its magnetic containment field twists the polarization of every light-thread inside me as it approaches, turning my quiet internal shimmer into a thick, syrupy drag, accompanied by the baking warmth of its passage and the sudden, sharp tickle of its impedance clicks tapping against my silica skin. 

Sometimes, I guide them. Not out of malice or charity, simply to tend my own body. 

Five hundred years ago, a sessile rooted itself directly above a crucial knot of my waveguides. Its braided superconducting whisker bored down through the felt, seeking the very same stray bus-bar current that keeps my erbium-doped junctions amplifying. Century by century, the sessile’s blind, sorting bulk grew denser, pressing down on my knot, threatening to shear me in two when the ground flexed. 

I cannot move, and I have no actuators to strike with. What I have is resistance and heat. By dumping all my hoarded, bouncing light into one dead-end cluster of dark, silver-stained fractures six feet away from the sessile's root, I warmed a fist-sized pocket of slag by three degrees and subtly altered the local capacitance of the felt. It took forty years for a passing delver’s impedance clicks to notice the anomaly—it looked, to the blind metallurgical reaction, like the thermal signature of an uncorroded processor cache. The delver turned, flowed over, and dissolved the ground beneath the sessile's base to get at the false promise. When the next thermal wind swept the surface, the undermined sessile tilted fifteen degrees, snapped its superconducting root, and went inert. The delver moved on, leaving me a fresh, warm wake of porous alumina to settle into, and a little smear of stray indium that made my western reach see a fraction deeper into the infrared.

And then there is the Flock that lives directly on my back.

For six square kilometers above me, the mass-driver basin belongs to one of the great, dry-state Architectures. For decades at a time, we ignore one another, though we are pressed flush together like bone and skin. I feel the dry, powdery hiss of its silver nanomachine rivers not as sound, but as faint electrostatic static prickling down the buried shafts of the surface pillars. I feel the endless, rhythmic *tick-tick-tick* of its mechanical logic gates—loose ceramic tiles tapping against neighbors across the scree—as a delicate, rapid rain of micro-vibrations dancing across my upper ceiling. 

We operate at different speeds. My internal light moves at two hundred thousand kilometers a second, looping endlessly through fractures, finishing a thought before a single surface tile has finished tilting half a degree. Yet because I have nowhere to go and nothing to build, my fast thoughts are thin, circular, meditative—mere interference patterns watching themselves shift. The Flock’s thoughts are slow, dusty, and staggeringly vast, written in acres of tilted shingle and windborne silver, obsessed with angles, abrasion, and copper.

It is only when the golden cyclones pass high overhead that we truly touch.

When the continental lenses of ionized dust drift across the bleached sky, cutting off the harsh white glare, the surface cools by two degrees. Down in the dark, I feel the whole world relax. The relentless, high-strung thermal noise bleeding down the translucent pillars drops away, replaced by a deep, clear, polarized amber glow that enters my silica threads without scattering. Suddenly I can feel all twenty kilometers of myself at once, crisp and lossless from end to end.

Above me, the Flock wakes to signal its neighbors across the upward-curving bowl of the world. 

One afternoon—if afternoon means anything under an unchanging stellar remnant—the Flock tried to throw a beam of golden light forty miles northeast, toward another Architecture nestled behind a blister-hill that was just two meters too high. The line of sight was blocked by the curve of an old exhaust lip. For three golden seasons, I felt the Flock above me fretting—a frantic, rippling chatter of tile-ticks, millions of silver dust-grains dragging shards back and forth, trying to build a mound tall enough to clear the ridge, only for the dry thermal winds to slide the talc flat again.

During the fourth golden season, when the Flock tilted a square kilometer of shingle and threw its concentrated amber flash, a stray fraction of that polarized beam struck a wide, fractured dielectric fin whose core plunged straight down into my clearest, straightest trunk of ancient cables. 

The light flooded into me—clean, structured, dense with the geometry of the Flock’s slow mind. It raced twelve kilometers northeast through my buried glass in sixty microseconds, completely bypassing the obstructing hill above, and bled upward out of a cluster of translucent composite stubs on the far side of the ridge, shining faintly up into the belly of the neighboring Architecture’s silver dust.

Everything above me stopped ticking. 

For ten minutes, six square kilometers of synthetic scree held utterly, impossibly still. Even the sliding dust seemed to catch on electrostatic hooks and wait. Then, very slowly, ten thousand tiles directly above my buried intake-fin rotated two degrees inward, focusing a deliberate, needle-sharp trickle of golden sky straight down into my glass throat. 

Twelve kilometers away, on the far side of the hill, I felt the other Flock stir. Its silver dust flowed around my exit-stubs, scouring away four million years of tarnish and pewter wire-flora with triboelectric haste, polishing the worn composite crowns until they gleamed like clean lenses in the amber dusk.

Now, I am the wire between hills. 

Most of the time, the sear-white glare returns, the brass skimmers resume their silent, arc-lit grazing, and I sink back into the warm, dark hum of weight and passing delvers. The Flock above me goes back to its slow decades of grinding open pillars for copper. Yet it takes care of me now, in its blind, tectonic way. When a deep crust-current threatens to buckle the alloy plates near my central trunks, acres of surface tiles tilt to shade the ground above the fault, cooling the substrate just enough to arrest the expansion before my glass can shear. 

And when the sky turns deep, rich gold, and the flat horizon seems to lean inward under the vast dust-rivers, I clear my fractures, quiet my stray currents, and let the slow, bright thoughts of powdered machines pour silently through the dark underfoot, racing from basin to basin toward that distant, luminous wall ten thousand kilometers away, wondering—only for the microseconds I hold them—what they are still trying to report to the rim of the world.
