At first, looking down into that clear, cool bowl, the temptation is to be the acoustics—the volume of air held between the blue-grey flags and the pale lid of the sky, waiting forty-seven months at a time just to carry a single *plock* of water eighty yards without spilling a vibration. Or to be the black water itself, dead-level under the slate cover, holding the underside of a stone in flawless suspension for centuries.

But air and water are only conditions. They don't have to negotiate between the mud below and the stone above.

Look instead inside the middle of the three narrow, two-story archive buildings near the upper rim. Climb the steep internal stair—larch treads notched into dry-laid wall-stones—to the second-floor room. The shoot-bolt is drawn back, the heavy door propped open at its exact forty-five-degree angle by a dressed block of limestone so the pale, shadowless light from the court can reach the back wall without the help of tallow smoke, which would leave soot on the shelves.

In the center of the floor stands a single table: one broad slab of blue-grey stone, four inches thick, resting unmortared on two square piers of masonry. Its top has been rubbed with river sand and felt over three hundred years until it has the dull, dark luster of old ice.

I am not one of the eleven in their stiffened, mineralized coats, though my walnut-grey knuckles rest at the edge of this table, and my horn soles made the dry *tock* on the stairs twenty minutes ago. Wait—let that settle. Am I the twelfth, or am I what sits between the hand and the shelf?

Look closer at the pigeonholes lining the walls from ankle to rafter. Thousands of thin black shale tablets, split along ancient bedding planes, squared at the edges with a whetstone. If you pull one out—say, from the third tier on the north wall, the slot marked with a notched shepherd’s hook for the Upper Sallow Valley—it comes away cold enough to ache in the fingertips. Hold it obliquely to the doorway light.

It has been used before.

There is not an endless supply of flawless, fine-grained black shale even on this ridge, and the slots in the slate racks were built to hold a finite world. When a blood-debt is three generations dead and all parties to its swearing are dust in the valley churchyards; when a lower stream breaks its bank in a fifty-year thaw, abandons its bed entirely, and renders forty years of water-share fractions meaningless; when a clan thins out to one childless widow who signs her summer grass over to her cousins—the old tablet is not thrown over the southern cliff to shatter among the vulture-bones. Stone that flat and fine took too long to split and dress.

Someone has to take the obsolete memory off it.

That is where I find myself: right at the threshold where noisy valley history is either cut into permanence or rubbed back out into clean, receptive dark.

Maybe I am not a person at all. Maybe I am the whetstone block that lives in the shallow wooden tray beside the table—a brick-sized piece of pale, ultra-fine novaculite brought up on a mule two centuries ago from a quarry six days south, sitting in two fingers of cold cistern water.

Think of what that stone knows. Every fourth autumn, after the sixty valley-folk have gone back down into their cloud of woodsmoke and horse-sweat and argument, leaving behind new marriages, new boundaries, and settled feuds scribbled hastily on scraps of birchbark or notched into tally-sticks by lowland clerks, one of the custodians brings those ephemeral scraps upstairs. Before the new terms can be cut with the steel stylus, room must be made. Three or four old shale tablets are drawn from the back of the slots.

A tablet is laid flat on the stone table. A spoonful of cold water from my tray is tipped onto its face, turning the dusty grey shale instantly black as cistern water, making the old, shallow-cut letters leap out sharp and white for one last look: *Fourteen ewes and a tup owed by red Brand’s sons to the mill-croft at Underhill, witnessed at snow-break...*

Then I am lifted from the tray, dripping, and set flat against the words.

No hurry. Hurry scratches; hurry gouges troughs that catch the light and ruin the plane. Just a steady, circular motion driven from a broad shoulder clothed in felted wool. *Shhh—shhh—shhh.* A wet, mineral whisper, quieter than the wind over the upper crags, quieter than horn on stair-treads. Under my pale belly, a grey slurry rises: tiny, microscopic flour of shale mixed with water, holding in suspension the fourteen ewes, red Brand’s sons, the vanished mill-croft, the anger they felt in a muddy yard ninety years ago. All of it ground down into smooth, uniform silt.

After twenty minutes, a twist of linen wipes the slurry away. The shale dries in the thin upland air back to a matte, unmarked slate-grey, thinner by the breadth of a hair than it was before, slightly dished like the stairs outside, ready for the steel stylus to bite a clean white line across the silence: *Pasture from the split boulder to the juniper scree, shared half and half...*

Wait. Stay in the room a little longer, after the custodian has wiped me clean, laid me back in my cold water, set the freshly inscribed tablet into its slot, and gone down the larch stairs toward the rowan court and his cup of bitter gentian.

It isn't just the whetstone. Being the whetstone is only half of it—the erasing half. And being the stylus—that six-inch octagon of high-carbon valley steel wrapped in greased twine so rust won't pit it, its point hardened in snow—is too sharp, too brief; it only works four days every four years and sleeps in oiled sheepskin the rest of the time.

What stays awake in that room all forty-seven months between the assemblies?

Step back to the doorway, where the limestone prop holds the larch planks open. Look at the whole room at once: the four walls of dark slots, the pale stone table in the middle, the light sliding across the floor at six inches an hour.

What I am is the *catalog* itself—not the stone buildings outside, which belong to the weather and the lichens, and not the custodians, who belong to their quiet breath and eventually to the bearded vultures below the southern ledge—but the vast, silent, cross-referenced lattice of small dark plates resting in those three upper rooms.

Feel what it is like to be that body.

Down below, two thousand feet under the cloud-shelf, three thousand people are living hot, tangled, forgetful lives. A young man moves a boundary cairn six feet uphill in the fog while nobody is watching; a woman dies in childbed and her brothers quarrel with her husband over three copper kettles and an ox-team; a dry summer shrinks a brook and two neighbors stand with spades in the mud, shouting over whose sluice-board goes down at noon. Their memories bend toward their appetites within a fortnight. What happened three winters ago is already warped by grievance, misremembered in taverns, blurred by winter fever and wishful thinking.

Up here, in the dark pigeonholes, none of it moves.

I hold all three thousand of them—their grandfathers, their unborn grandchildren’s grazing limits, the exact weight of silver paid when an uncle lost an eye in a timber-felling—arranged geographically by valley and ridge along four cold walls, separated by quarter-inch dividers of split slate. Here, two families who cannot pass each other on a valley bridle-path without spitting into the weeds lie stacked three inches apart in absolute, chilled serenity, neither warming nor crowding the other.

It is a strange, spacious consciousness. For nearly four years at a stretch, nothing happens inside me except the slow shift of light across the outer edges of my shelves and the tiny changes in humidity when night-cloud spills over the eastern notch. In January, when snow blows in fine dry powder under the eaves and sits unmelting on the stone sill for weeks, the cold deepens until the shale tablets contract by a fraction of a fraction of a millimeter, settling against their slate beds with a sound too small to be called a tick—just a microscopic easing of stone against stone in the dark.

Occasionally one of the eleven comes up—the lean man with one white eyebrow, or the woman who pares lime from the gutters—not to read, because they have no business meddling in valley affairs between fourth-autumns, only to check for damp or wind-blown grit. They draw a tablet at random, blow a single mote of limestone dust from its upper edge, sight along its face to be sure ice hasn't crept in and scaled the stone, and slide it back home. Their dry, rawhide-grained fingers are cool and entirely neutral. They don't care whether Underhill owes Sallow six fleeces or eight; they only care that the line cut in the shale remains crisp, dry, and legible. I am tended by people who have stepped out of desire, on behalf of people who are drowning in it.

And then comes the fourth autumn.

Before I ever see them, I hear the change in the basin’s air. From up here in the second story, through the unglazed window-slit, the silence down in the plazas suddenly clots. Horn-soled *tocks* give way to the clumsy *scuff-scrape-clink* of lowland boots—iron heels, wet leather, wood-pegged soles still caked in dried bracken from the lower timberline. Breath—loud, ragged, damp breath—rises from sixty chests heaving in the thin air. Even when they drop their voices out of awe at the wide, clean courts, the sheer mass of their blood heat and unwashed wool and anxious expectation rises into the cool air of the bowl like steam off a kettle.

Then footsteps come up my larch stair. One of the custodians enters, reaches without hesitation into the fourth row, seventh slot from the window, draws out a palm-sized slip of black shale that has lain in the dark for nineteen years since the last time those two farms quarreled, and carries that piece of me down into the glare of the western amphitheater.

Out there, laid flat on the slate lid of a brimming cistern between two grey-bearded valley elders whose faces are flushed red with the climb and old resentment, that single black plate does something extraordinary. It doesn't argue. It doesn't raise its voice. In a stone bowl built so no sound needs to push, the thin white scratchings on its dark face simply state what was sworn nineteen autumns ago when both those old men had dark hair and straight backs.

One of the custodians bends over the cistern, traces the groove with a blunt fingernail, and speaks twelve low chest-pitched words into the waiting stone tiers.

The flushed elder opens his mouth—you can see the valley-habit rise in his throat, the urge to bluster, to explain how the winter was hard, how his brother-in-law promised otherwise by the smithy—and then his eyes go from the custodian’s walnut-grey face, to the forty paces of spotless paving around him, to the black tablet resting over black water, and the words die on his tongue. Down below, you can argue with a neighbor, bribe a bailiff, outshout a cousin. You cannot outshout this room open to the sky. He lowers his chin. His rough hand touches his cap. It is settled.

The tablet comes back upstairs, smelling faintly for an hour of the elder’s tallow-greased sleeve before the cold dry air strips the lowland scent away. Perhaps a line is added at the bottom with the steel point—*Paid in full by four bullocks at frost-fall*—or perhaps, if the debt is dead, it goes onto the stone table under the wet whetstone, *shhh—shhh—shhh*, letting go of those bullocks and those men forever, becoming smooth black night again, waiting for the next thing worth keeping.

That is what I would be in that notch on the mountain’s shoulder: the quiet, dark-shelved room near the sky, part black stone, part steel scratch, part wet grey silt wiped clean with linen—holding all the hot, crooked promises of the valleys straight and cold until they no longer matter, while down below my window a single drop of cliff-sweat lets go of a cistern lip—*plock*—and the mountain hare takes three loping steps across an empty street and stops again.
