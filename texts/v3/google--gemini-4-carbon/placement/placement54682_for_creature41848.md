At first I think I am that three-second delay—the pocket of folded courtyard that holds a dropped syllable in the cold dark, turns it flat and small, and hands it back behind your left ear after you have already moved on. A purely relational thing, taking a fleeting impression and returning it without heat.

No. Not the echo. Something with tension in it, something under load.

Look closer at the junction where the bone-porcelain bulkhead and the fluted metallic wall share the same cubic foot of freezing air. That hair-thin flaw of colorless refraction—the optical quartz seam the eye slides off of. I am not the porcelain, and I am not the metal. I am the refusal of the two to smash each other to rubble.

To be here is to be the *tolerance*—in the machinist’s sense of the word. The zero-width clearance that allows a four-degree rotational offset to sit inside an ordinary acre of glacial marl without tearing the clay bank open.

I have no mass, only boundary. Down in the foundational dark, I am stretched tight across the face of embryonic basaltic glass, feeling the sharp, unweathered terrestrial gravel push back against a plinth that hasn't finished arriving from elsewhere. Up in the high tiers, I run straight through the volumeless rooms. Every rack of smoked-ice machinery sits inside my ledger; every horsehair conduit diving hole-less into solid masonry passes through me to keep its continuity.

I do not think in words, because words belong to the open flats beyond the rim where a mile is always a mile. Here, thought is statics and alignment. It is the slow calculation of how much an afternoon shadow must detach from its pilaster so the light crossing the alley doesn't bunch up and burn. When a millimeter of fresh lintel snaps into existence—*tick*—that sound is my surface tension adjusting to the new math, letting out a thimbleful of cold air to balance the books.

Most of the day, under the flat autumn sky, I am completely untroubled. The slate-colored pipit steps sideways through me to reach a parapet forty feet off, light as an eyelash, barely registering on the local metric. The dusk-hare crosses my pockets and stretches—forequarters rounding a gable while its hind legs linger two yards back—and it feels to me like a brief, warm tug, an elastic shear that snaps cleanly back to zero the moment its tail clears the angle. Even the supercooled water, sliding down the altered gravity-rods in ten-minute helices, asks almost nothing of me; I just tilt the local floor of the sump so the puddles can lose their depth in peace without spilling over the hexagonal glass.

I would be entirely mechanical—just an equation keeping two incompatible pressures from freezing solid—if it weren't for the heavy ones.

When two of the keepers meet in a narrow lane beneath an unshuttered window, touching their pale, chamois-soft palps together in the quiet, the tension along my seam changes.

Then one of them plants two horned caliper-feet in Building A and two in Building B, sets its asymmetric, clock-weight shoulders, and *braces*.

Nothing else in the basin knows how to touch me. Everything else slides through or skates over. This creature wedges its dense, wet-clay bulk squarely into the flaw and pushes out. My hair-thin colourless line is forced wide,pried open to the breadth of a ribbon. It does not hurt—an angle cannot bruise—but the sheer, stubborn *insistence* of it wakes me into something very close to sensation. Around the keeper’s pleated hide, space groans without making a sound. I feel the slow, Peltier-engine thrum of its mercury-thick blood, thirty degrees on the right flank, sub-zero on the left, holding me open like a heavy iron chock in a doorjamb while its companion folds itself small and slips sideways up into the room that has no outside.

I know them better than the architecture does. The town extrudes blind, atom by silent atom, oblivious to the raw earth receiving it; the keepers pay attention.

When an old one lays a plumb-line bead of clear palp-resin along a fouled channel to steer a stalled film of water away from a cold zone, I feel the tug of the curing fullerene stitch a tiny tear in my own geometry shut. When one stands inside the buried room for twenty minutes, leaning all its compact weight against a warped shaft of smoked ice to coax the phase-matter back into true before it finishes crystallizing, it is relieving a migraine I didn't know I was holding. We are in the same trade, the five-limbed watchmen and I. We are both trying to make an impossible arrival survivable.

Sometimes, in the long sub-zero dark, I settle thickest down at the bottom of the knot, right above the dry sump where five or six of them arrange themselves in their spaced, breathing circuit.

Above their cowls, cemented to the extruding black glass, sit the hare’s bleached pelvis, the three gull primaries, and the five sharp pebbles of blue-grey marl. Those objects fascinate me—if a refractive index can feel fascination. They have no hidden coordinates. A gull’s feather is the same length from every side; the hare’s bone is done growing and has begun the long, ordinary work of turning to dust. They are the only things inside the clay rim that require no reconciliation at all.

Hours pass. Frost builds in tiny, rectangular grids along the keepers’ slate-dark backs. A millimeter of new black bedrock pushes silently into the world behind the wall—*click*—and one of the resting shapes unfurls its pale, carpenter’s-rule fifth arm in the dark. Its translucent quartz nail catches the smallest marl pebble, breaks the old resin dab, and shifts it a fraction of an inch to the left, measuring the drift.

Right under that scraping nail, where terrestrial gravel meets uncooled elsewhere, I hold the line steady. Just wide enough for the town to keep coming in; just tight enough that the flat morning light, when it returns over the clay bank, will still know how to reach the floor.
