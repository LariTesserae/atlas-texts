The warmth is the first thing. Not heat—heat is sharp, heat is a leak—but a deep, heavy, ambient ninety-two degrees that smells of lanolin, wet iron, sulfur-tallow, and the sour-sweet ferment of yeast-flasks wedged between the third and fourth dorsal spines. 

Beneath my spine, the great matriarch walks. 

I do not hear her walk. I *am* her walk. Every four seconds, the left fore-piston draws back, the heavy biological boiler-ribs beneath the felted floor expand by three inches, and the blister rolls gently to starboard like a boat over a long, oiled swell. *Shhh-click.* A pause while the internal pressure equalizes—felt as a low, sub-audible purr in my pelvic bones—and then the secondary leg settles onto wet shale, rolling me two degrees back to port. I have been rocking on this exact four-second pendulum for fifty-eight years. If you set me down on the dead basalt for an hour, my inner ears would ache from the stillness, and my heart would lose its count.

My legs are useless from the knees down. A winter trek thirty years ago—a calf-bull threw a primary rod in a deep fog-basin four miles east, and the exhaust bladders tucked under my tunic cooled faster than I walked. The plateau took eight toes and fused my left ankle into a smooth, painless club of bone and plumbago-tinted scar. On the flat scree, I would be slag within an afternoon. Up here, wedged into the stern-corner of the main dorsal blister with a bolster of warm oil-bladders behind my broad back, I am the stillest, warmest, heaviest thing on the cow, save for her own furnace.

They bring the dark to me, and I sort it.

Right now, my youngest daughter’s boy sits cross-legged against my shins, soaking up the spare heat of my belly while I mend a dead kite’s capacitor in my lap. My fingers are thick as bog-roots, plated in grey horn that has split and healed over with yellow mineral paste a hundred times, yet they know the exact tension of the translucent membrane. I do not look down much; the interior of the blister is completely dark to save uncovering the sternum-chute, lit only by the faint, dying violet sweat on my thumbs where the nutrient-gel leaks. 

Instead, I listen through the boy’s shoulder blade. My chin rests lightly on the greased leather of his collar. He is twelve, still slightly too narrow in the chest, his turbinates not yet fully thickened to the winter damp, and he is humming a question into my jawbone.

A three-note chord, low in the throat, shifting upward on the nasal third: *Why did we salt the tarn on the left flank yesterday, and leave the east puddle dry?*

I do not hum back right away. There is no hurry. The matriarch takes ten steps. Forty seconds of smooth, greased *heave-and-settle*. Outside our triple-layered wall of cured hide and matted wool, a bank of freezing rime-fog is dragging its belly over the cow’s back; I can feel the slight, microscopic stiffening of the outer felt as ice crystals form on the guard hairs and immediately melt again in the wash of her dorsal vents.

I press my heavy brow ridge firmly against the crown of his head so he gets the full chest-resonance, round and steady from the bottom of my partitioned lungs. I hum him a map.

People think maps are flat things scratched on slate or hide, because elsewhere the ground stays still while you walk across it. Here, the ground is mostly empty, and what matters is *when* two slow things arrive at the same stone. You cannot scratch a four-hundred-year drift onto a piece of gut. You hold it in the long, quiet vaults behind your eyes, counted out in four-second steps, amber pulses, and indigo blinks.

I hum him the shape of a shallow gravel basin sixty leagues north—twelve days at the matriarch’s autumn gait. I hum the slow, low-frequency drone that means *shale-rot*, then drop a fifth to the heavy, metallic vibration for *bog-iron crust*. Seventy years ago, before my ankle froze, my mother’s brother chipped four finger-wide rills into the frost-heave at the lip of that northern basin. Every spring since, black snowmelt has trickled down those four grooves onto a thumb-sized smear of red sedge-spores he scraped off his boot heel. 

By next Tuesday week, when this cow’s iron hooves reach that rise, that smear will be a braided mat forty paces wide. Thick enough to hold wet silt. Wet enough to draw six or seven rotary-jawed grazers in off the dry scree. And where the grazers cluster and drop their tungsten-rich slag, the matriarch’s youngest bull-calf—who has been running half a degree hot behind his sternum all month, burning through his mineral liners too fast—will smell the refined oxides, turn two points west to lick the gravel, and cross vectors with the broad-backed sterile cow from the outer herd whose keepers flashed us *surplus bog-wrenches, seeking young bull-tallow* four nights ago through the mist.

We salted the left tarn yesterday so the matriarch would drink from her left side, swing her heavy shoulders half a degree east on the departure, and hit that sedge-mat dead center in two hundred and fifty thousand steps. 

The boy goes very still against my knees. Through his skull, I feel his breathing slow down to match mine, and beneath both of us, the huge, patient valves along the matriarch’s spine sigh out a warm plume of sulfur-vapor—*shhhhhh*—into the pewter dark. He understands. Not all of it yet, not the eighty-year arcs, the way you nudge an ore-vein with a spark today so your great-granddaughter’s host-beast won't split a hoof-plate in a dry pass two centuries from now. But he feels the weight of the steering.

Above us, the leather sleeve by the forward vent parts an inch. No draft enters—the airlock flap is weighted with lead-shot rendered from slag—just my daughter’s horned hand slipping down into the warm murk. She taps twice on my knee, then passes down a cold, freshly shed scraper of grazers'-bone and a pouch of damp yellow scale peeled from the cow’s third intake valve. Her fingers smell sharply of raw cold and wet shale; she has been hanging in the lower harness for an hour, down near the amber wash of the sternum, watching the stones slide underneath.

Before she draws her hand back out to climb onto the ridge, her knuckles brush my cheekbone. Two short presses, a pause, one long press downward.

*Three miles port. Indigo blink. Twice.*

I set the mended kite-organ carefully into its warm gel-pouch between my thighs, reach into the greased wool beside my hip, and draw out the older, heavier lantern I have carried since my wedding at the Red Bull’s Cooling. It feels like a warm, muscular heart in my palms, pulsing faintly with stored static from storms that never broke. 

"Help me up to the collar," I hum into the boy’s collarbone.

He braces his broad, boulder-solid little shoulders under my arm. Together, moving with the four-second roll of the spine so we waste not an ounce of push against gravity, I haul my heavy torso two feet up the vertical felt chimney until my greased brow clears the collar-slit behind the matriarch’s immense, piston-driven neck.

The cold hits my face like smooth iron. 

Even through the thick coat of tallow on my cheeks and the warm blood-labyrinth inside my flat nose, the thinness of the air pulls at my ribs. My heavy eyelids narrow instantly to slits. 

For ten breaths, there is only the shadowless pewter ceiling of the clouds, so low it feels as if the matriarch’s rising steam-plumes are holding it up, and the endless, whaleback curve of dark basalt sliding south beneath her flanged feet. Miles away, spaced out across the dimness like drowned stars in black water, hundreds of pale amber sternums pulse slowly over the wet gravel. *Shhh-click. Shhh-click.* The whole plateau turning over in its sleep.

Then, far out to port—beyond a lone, cottage-sized erratic boulder that I last saw from the other side when I had ten toes and black hair—a tiny slit of cool indigo opens in the grey mist.

One long hold. Two quick shutters. A pause of six steps. One faint, downward smear of blue.

*Old bull slowing. Calcified third valve. Drifting south of vector. Seven winters left in him, maybe eight.*

I know who holds that lantern. Forty years ago, in the three-day steam-haze beside a cooling carcass, we pressed our greased foreheads together and hummed into each other's skulls for an hour before her uncle’s cow walked north and mine walked north-by-west. We have not been closer than two miles since. Every three or four seasons, our herds graze parallel basins for a week, and we trade valve-counts and water-depths across the scree.

If her bull is calcifying on the third intake, he will lose heat on the winter steppes. He will drop back half a mile a month. By spring, he will cross the shallow depression where my uncle’s four little water-grooves have been feeding the red sedge. If the ground there is too soft from early melt, a heavy, limping bull with weak boiler-pressure will bog down to his iron fetlocks and chill his belly-plates before he can haul free.

I bring my horned thumbs together over the muscular poles of my lantern, aiming low across the matriarch’s oiled wool so the light goes nowhere except across those three miles of damp air.

I squeeze. Two steady indigo blinks. Pause four steps. Three short blinks, shuttered left.

*Keep him high on the shale ridge. Basin ahead runs wet this decade. Good cushion-lichen on the west shoulder.*

Three miles away, through the drifting micro-condensation that swallows every sound before it travels ten yards, a single, brief point of blue winks once in acknowledgement, and goes dark.

I do not linger in the raw air. Heat is life, and I have spent six breaths of it. I slide slowly back down the felt chimney into the rich, dark ninety-degree musk of the blister, settling my fused ankle back among the warm tallow-bladders, and pull the weighted leather flap tight overhead.

In the dark, my grandson presses his forehead against my chin, waiting. 

I hum him the new count. One limping bull, eight winters hence, coming down the west shoulder of the wet basin. When we stop at the next tarn before dusk, the boy will climb down the flank with a chisel of tungsten-bone and a pocketful of crushed slag, walk forty paces out onto the barren rock where no beast walks today, and turn three flat stones over to catch the Tuesday fog—starting a patch of grey moss for an old woman’s dying bull to eat when the boy is twenty, and the bull is cooling, and the plateau is three millimeters older.

Beneath my back, the matriarch’s great piston-tendon draws, holds, and settles. Warmth against my spine. Grease on my thumbs. Ten thousand quiet amber lights drifting north through the cold, and every stone accounted for.
