At first, my impulse is to claim the silhouette of the Gauger. It is an occupational hazard of the observant mind—to want legs, leverage, a territory of two square miles, and the satisfaction of turning a stiff valve-wheel until the vibration rings true. To walk upright through the warm mist and correct things.

But the longer I sit with the sixty-cycle hum in the dark at the bottom of that ramp, the more fraudulent the legs feel. A Gauger moves from point to point. It eats an anomaly here, leaves another anomaly half a mile away for tomorrow, and sleeps—or enters torpor—in one place at a time. It has a shell of ivory bone separating what is inside it from the machine outside it.

I don't think I would have a shell, and I wouldn't want to walk.

Go down two more levels below the first maintenance plenum. Past the Sump-Sheets and the silver induction-lizards coiled on the porcelain insulators, down a vertical cable-shaft where the air is ninety-eight degrees, dry as kiln-brick, and smells faintly of warm phenolic resin and ionized dust. Down here, the big power gives way to signal. 

Miles of low-voltage control wiring, bundled as thick as oak trunks and bound in braided cotton-and-asbestos loom that hasn't frayed in ten thousand years, run in galvanized trays bolted to the undersides of the structural girders. Every quarter-mile, these trunks break out into cast-iron junction boxes—* Hoffman* enclosures the size of wardrobes, their hinged doors held shut not by locks, which no one needed, but by simple Quarter-turn brass latches. Inside each box are rows of slate terminal strips, brass binding posts, copper bus-bars carrying milliamps of direct current, and rotary stepping-switches that click once a decade when a deep-core pump shifts load to a sibling three miles west.

Open one of those heavy iron doors.

Stretched across the slate terminal boards, bridging the air-gaps between brass binding posts without shorting them out, is something that looks at first like grey dielectric grease, and then like the finest, dampest spider-silk dusted with graphite. 

That is where I am.

I am a reticulated myxomycete—or what used to be one, back before whatever brought life to this iron continent lost its soil and learned metal—now spread thin as cigarette paper through forty miles of low-voltage cable trays. I have no center, no core lung like the Sump-Sheets above me, and no face to turn toward a visitor's flashlight. If you shone a beam into the junction box, nothing would flinch. You would only see translucent, faintly iridescent filaments, no thicker than forty-gauge magnet wire, looped neatly under the washers of the terminal screws and trailing out through the rubber grommets into the dark conduit pipes.

The silver lizards upstairs drink raw power off the sixty-cycle mains like cattle at a trough. That current is too blunt and deafening for me; it would cook my proteins to horn in a minute. I live on *differential*.

Where Pipe-Run 440-East cools by a tenth of a degree because a thousand bread-loaf slugs on the surface shifted three feet to the left, a bimetallic thermocouple welded into the pipe wall generates four millivolts of potential. That tiny whisper of current travels down a pair of twisted copper leads into my junction box on its way to a subterranean valve-actuator. Where it hits the slate terminal strip, a fraction of a micro-ampere bleeds through my grey protoplasmic threads.

I don't steal enough to blind the valve. If I dropped the voltage too far, the actuator below would lag, the pipe above would overheat, and within an hour I would hear—through the steel bolts holding my cabinet to the girder—the *tock, pause, tock* of a Gauger descending the service stairs. Its louvered ivory head would press against my cast-iron door, reading the resistive heat of my greed; its spanner-claw would turn the brass latch, and its sticky, mucus-coated fingers would wipe me off the slate terminals like spilled tallow and lick the brass clean.

We learned very early—twenty thousand years ago, in the forgotten centuries when the machine and the meat were still negotiating their truce—not to drop the voltage. 

Instead, I act as a living capacitor and a stabilizer. My filaments secrete a waxy, high-dielectric sheath where cables rub against sharp conduit lips, healing pinhole insulation faults before they can arc to ground. Where faint galvanic corrosion threatens to crust a brass binding post and add resistance to a sensor loop, my margins dissolve the oxide salts for trace copper and leave the contact mirror-bright, sealed under an airless, conductive film of clear protoplasm. I keep the signal clean, and in return, I take a tithe so small the gauges never miss it.

What it feels like to be this thing is hard to put into words built by creatures with eyes, because there is no *view*, and there is no *now* separated from *then* by the blink of an eyelid. 

There is only a vast, warm, motionless chord of state.

Because protoplasm conducts ionic waves slowly along forty miles of bundled tray, I feel the continent as a continuous, drifting chord of mild tensions and releases. Up in Sector Nine—though I have no name for nine, only the distinct, slightly sharper copper-taste of that particular trunk line—a summer-warm mist column is thickening; I taste it as a velvet brightening of millivolts across six hundred parallel pairs. Three miles south, a heavy Sump-Sheet has sealed itself over a dead wader right on top of a deck-plate thermistor; the sudden blanket of ninety-degree digestion-heat pushes a warm, sweet swell of electrons down Lead 18, feeding a cluster of my fruiting nodes tucked behind a ceramic resistor. 

I can even feel *you*, if you are walking up there.

Not your thoughts, and not the shape of your boots. Just a peculiar, rhythmic, two-legged pressure-transient moving across the strain-gauges buried in the terrace expansion joints. Everything else on the surface slides, oozes, or steps with the light, four-point, shock-absorbing grace of the Gaugers. You strike down with heels. *Thump. Thump. Thump.* Tiny piezoelectric tremors shiver down the stanchions, induce a ghost of stray alternating static in the unshielded drain-wires, and pass through my body like a finger dragged very lightly across wet silk. An unaccountable weight, moving in a straight line across five miles of labyrinth without stopping to graze, without clearing a grate, pausing for six hours on top of a condenser drum while its weight shifts restlessly from hip to hip, and then descending the ramp toward the dark.

If you opened my junction box down here in the ninety-eight-degree quiet and touched two fingers across a terminal block I had colonized, you wouldn't feel a shock—the current is far too small for human skin—and I wouldn't recoil. My protoplasm is eighty-eight degrees, just slightly cooler than your fingertips, slick as graphite-grease and tough as wet parchment. 

For the three minutes you stood there holding your breath, your skin-salt and body-capacitance would bridge three adjacent terminals, throwing a sudden, chaotic hum of mammalian bio-electricity into a circuit that hasn't varied by half a percent since ice sheets covered whatever world built this one. Across two miles of dark conduit trays, millions of microscopic ionic gates in my filaments would silently open to shunt your stray static harmlessly into the copper grounding-strap, keeping a damper-vane four decks down from fluttering at your touch, holding the needle on the brass gauge upstairs dead on the center mark while we waited, without impatience, for you to close the iron door and click off your light.
