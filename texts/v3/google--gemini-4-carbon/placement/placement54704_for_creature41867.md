I am sitting astride a split-slate ridge forty feet above the Seventh Sluice-Court, with a cured fish-skin bag between my knees and my heels wedged hard into two chipped notches in the stone so the slick doesn’t take me.

Down between my calves, the city drops away in wet black-and-white steps until the pewter mist swallows the lower pans. Up here the air has a little push to it—not wind, never wind, just the heavy, warm drift off the northern fault-line, carrying fine cataract-spray that beads on the grease of my shoulders and runs down the groove of my spine. My jaw is set loose, back teeth slightly apart. If you clench your teeth up on the high parapets, the bass note of the waterfall climbs straight up through your butt-bones and rattles your molars against each other until your skull aches behind the eyes. You learn by eight thicknesses old to keep a cushion of tongue between your grinders.

I am not a Weir-Keeper. I don’t have the patience for the big water, or the wide-shouldered authority to stand on a tower with a horn mallet clacking orders at fifty maul-gangs. My knuckles aren’t swollen white from hot lime, though the skin around my fingernails is permanently stained the rust-orange of pan-ochre.

I work the *dry-seams* and the *listening-posts*. People flash two quick signs when they mean my trade: thumb-edge drawn across the ribs, then two fingers forked against the neck. *Crack-finder. Bone-hearer.*

A city that turns itself into a single solid boulder is never actually still. That is the thing children don’t understand until you take them down into an under-vault and put their ear against a dry pillar. Fresh basalt stacked by men wants to settle downward; white travertine laid down by water wants to swell outward and heavy-up on the lips; and underneath all of it, the hot belly of the basin breathes—tiny, slow shrugs of the deep fractures that open a hair-thin seam here and pinch a boiling-vent shut fifty paces away. If a dry-joint cracks inside a fused wall three tiers down, nobody sees it through six inches of pale sinter crust. Nobody *hears* it over the toads and the cataract. Water slips into the dark split instead of sheeting down the outer face. For half a thickness on the Gauge-Stone it eats out the clay-packing in secret, loading an interior vault with ten tons of trapped seepage—and then one shift-sleep a whole tenement flank shears off along the slick bacterial grease that grew inside the crack, dropping four families and twenty yards of aqueduct into a lower fish-pool.

So I go around with a three-foot rod of dense, black phonolite hung from a human-hair sling across my back.

Right now I slide down off the roof-ridge onto the narrow catwalk behind the rail-cotes, stepping right over two fat, flightless rails that don’t even bother to hiss—just shift their heavy, scaly feet in the warm guano and blink white nictitating membranes at my ankles. My soles make that soft, sucking *tchk* on the wet sinter. They’re due for the pumice block; I can feel a hard spur building under my left heel that catches slightly on fresh-chipped basalt.

An apprentice is waiting by the drop-ladder—my sister’s boy, maybe nine thicknesses old, his scalp shaved two days ago so the dark stubble is just catching mist-beads like velvet. He’s got too much hair in him to stay lean-headed for long; good strong rope-stock, our family. As soon as my grey shoulders drop into his line of sight, his small rust-red palms flash at his chest, tidy and clipped, elbows pinned polite against his ribs so he doesn't knock a nesting swift off the coping:

*Wall-belly / weeping / cold-side / Third Tanner’s Vault.*

I don't sign back with both hands—one is hooked into a braided hair-loop cemented straight into the stone lintel—just flick my right thumb down twice and puff my left cheek. *Show me. Slow.*

Going down into the older tiers is like climbing inside the throat of a beast made of chalk.

On the upper terraces, the basalt is still black between its white joints, sharp enough to slice a knuckle if a crab startles you. Down three levels, in the cramped alleys under the slab-bridges where the pewter sky shrinks to a grey ribbon overhead, nobody has seen black rock in four generations. Everything is coated in swollen, pearly, overlapping curtains of travertine—lintels narrowed into round-lipped throats, doorways gone oval as eggs because the maul-gangs chip the floors flat every few thicknesses while the corners keep rounding inward. Warm trickle runs everywhere, orange-streaked where sulfur bleeds out of a seam, busy with flat snails gliding over one another’s backs. Bronze bull-toads sit stacked in the warm wall-niches like pulsing metal ingots, their throats ballooning in and out, in and out, hammering that relentless shrill sheet of sound straight into your temples from six inches away. You don’t hear them anymore than you hear your own blood, except as a tightness across the bridge of your nose.

We duck past an oiled bird-skin flap into the Third Tanner’s Vault, and the smell hits first: tallow smoke, hot caustic snail-lime, and the heavy, meaty tang of split rail-hides soaking in floor-troughs.

Two women are crouched by the vent, scraping fat off inner skins with obsidian half-moons. They glance up with big, black, unblinking eyes, flick a single red palm upward in greeting without dropping their blades, and go right back to scraping. No wasted motion. My nephew points to the rear supporting pier—a massive, swollen column of white-crusted rock carrying the weight of an entire neighborhood’s boiling-trough directly overhead.

Near the base, where the stone should be dry and warm from a neighboring floor-flue, there is a sweat-patch no wider than two hands. Not clean, hot spring seepage. It’s cool to my palm, slightly slimy with pale grey thread-scum instead of healthy orange slick, and tiny blind white midges are already hovering over it in a tight, confused knot.

Dead water. Filtered through dark stone where it dropped its lime and lost its heat.

I unship the phonolite rod from my hair-sling. Phonolite is strange rock; the quarry-crews only find it in one narrow dike near the western rim, and a good flawless bar of it is worth three full hanks of grandmother-braid. When you strike it in the open air, it rings over the frogs. When you press one chipped flat end tight against a calcified wall and press the tragus of your ear—folding that little flap of cartilage shut over the canal to lock out the air-din—against the other end, the stone speaks straight into your jawbone.

I kneel on the warm clay floor, brush two beaded lizards gently off the base of the pier with the back of my wrist so their claws don't scratch against the rock and muddy the sound, and jam the rod hard against the swollen white crust. Close my eyes. Press my greased cheekbone and shut ear tight to the butt of the stone.

The frogs vanish—not gone, just pushed outside a heavy door.

Inside my skull, the city turns into pure vibration. First comes the deep, eternal *shhhhhh-thump* of the northern cataract traveling three miles through the solid basalt floor of the basin. Over that is the dull, rhythmic *tok... tok...* of a maul-gang chipping a footpath two streets east. Higher still, the faint, fizzy hiss of live thermal steam rushing up a healthy vent twenty feet below my knees.

I shift the rod six inches left. Listen. Six inches higher. Listen.

There.

Right at shoulder-height, buried deep behind four generations of solid white sinter, there is a hollowness—a dead spot where the waterfall’s bass hum goes flat and papery—and inside that dead spot, a tiny, irregular *tink... tink-grit*. Like a crab rolling a pebble inside a clay pot. Basalt prisms shifting dry against each other because the water meant to weld them outside has found a rat-hole down the middle and is washing their seat away. Left alone, in half a finger-breadth of time, the boiling-trough upstairs comes through this ceiling in a cascade of scalding broth and twenty-ton blocks.

I pull my ear off the rod, and the roar of the basin slams back against the side of my head like warm water filling a bucket.

The older tanner-woman is watching my face. Not my mouth—my eyebrows, the set of my shoulders. I straighten up, wipe the grey clay-grease on my chest where the stone dust smudged it, and give her the sign nobody likes to see inside their work-room:

Left forearm held level for a lintel; right red palm tucked underneath it, fingers fluttering downward like falling gravel; then two stiff fingers tapped hard against my collarbone. *Hollow under load. Call the Weir-Keeper.*

She doesn't curse or throw down her scraper; nobody wastes heat on rock doing what rock does. Her nostrils flare once—a long, slow breath out—and she reaches over to tap her partner’s ankle, hands already moving briskly at chest-height to start organizing where they’ll haul the lime-vats while the drill-crews come in to chip weep-holes and pack the split with hot sinter-grout.

Back out in the alley, my nephew falls in half a step ahead of me, proud to have spotted a real rot-seam, his skinny greased shoulders swaggering just an inch wider than the corridor calls for. As we climb the steep sluice-stair back toward the middle terraces—hot alkaline water rushing ankle-deep over our callused toes, feeling remarkably good on that rough spur under my left heel—I reach out and catch the back of his neck with one damp hand.

He turns his head right in against my collarbone as we pause on a landing. Down here between two high aqueduct walls, the frogs are loud enough to blur your vision if you think about them too hard. I put my mouth right against the wet hollow behind his ear, where the shaved stubble ends and the skin is thin over the mastoid bone, and hum low in my chest before shaping the words—slow, lazy, feeling his small jaw shift against my cheek as he catches the vowels through bone:

*"Good eye. Take the pumice when we get in. Then go scrape two baskets off the high rail-ledge for your mother’s vat before sleep."*

He doesn't waste breath answering back into my ear; he just presses his chin once hard into my shoulder—*heard*—and flashes a tiny, cheeky flick of red fingers down low by his hip where no passing Chisel-Master on the bridge above can see it: *My knees hurt, old man.*

I snort through my nose, flick water at his calf with my big toe, and let him scramble ahead up the slick white steps.

Before going in behind our doorway flap to grease down fresh and curl around the floor-vent for four hours, I stop for a breath on the wide lip of the cistern-court. Across the misty breadth of the basin, half a mile off through the shadowless pewter glow, two tiny white bone-paddles flash up, out, and down from a high roof-platform, followed a heartbeat later by the sharp, silver *CLACK-CLACK* of a phonolite slab cutting cleanly over the roar. Somewhere down on the lower shelves, a woodenless gate of split slate drops into its groove. A dark, dry-stacked wall suddenly gleams like wet obsidian as warm water sheets over its face for the first time, beginning the slow work of turning stacked stones into one stone.

I reach up, rub a thumb across the Gauge-Pier at the corner of our court—feeling the sharp, glassy white rim of lime that wasn't quite that thick when my sister’s boy was born—scrape two fat channel-snails off its base to drop into our stew-pot inside, and duck out of the mist into the red tallow-glow.
