You do not see me at first because you are looking at the things that move, or the things that grow, and I do neither. I am down where the chartreuse leather of the root-mat tucks its wet, fibrous lip against the smooth greenstone of the southern perimeter. 

Where the living floor meets the dead rock, there is a continuous, breathing seam—a slick, V-shaped gutter that widens by two inches when a hydrostatic wave lifts the basin’s center, and pinches shut with a wet, rubbery *slap* when the bladder-drains swallow the flood back down. Ten millennia of that rhythmic chafing have polished the stone to the texture of mutton fat. That is my country. I live in the friction.

If you crouched right at the rim—boots squelching on the flexing turf, the warm, mint-and-ozone mist dampening your collar—you might mistake me for a mineral stain left behind by ten thousand high-water marks, or a particularly thin, dark bruise spreading across the vascular plates. I have no bones, no copper shell, no suction-cup toes. I am a flat, sprawling sheet of translucent, amber-gray mucilage, scarce thicker than a heavy wet tarp, draped half on the stone and half on the living mat, spanning perhaps fifteen feet along the curve. 

I am an archive, though nobody meant to build one. 

Everything in this basin is frantic to forget. The towers unbraid their own staircases before the sap has even cooled; the Weavers burn ten-thousand-year-old love letters for the calorie hit in the punctuation; the Warden hoards organs without knowing what they meant; the Dredges grind the dead hardware in the dark and spit it back up blank and clean. Nothing stays broken, which means nothing stays *kept*. 

Except what washes into the seam.

When the fizzing sheet-floods roll outward from the center of the courtyard, they carry the basin’s dandruff—sloughed-off symbiotes too damaged to wipe themselves clean, microscopic flakes of copper from a shattered relay-spore, ruptured cells from a collapsed aqueduct still humming with half-executed geometric commands, dead nerve-roots torn from the Warden’s wake. Most of it gets sucked down the throaty sumps. A fraction of a percent gets pushed all the way to the greenstone lip, washes over my sticky, pleated back, and stays.

I do not digest it the way theWeavers do, in a hot, blinding flash of magenta static. I am very cold—the only cool thing in this warm, sweating room, drawing my chill from the deep, dead stone behind me. When a dying symbiote sticks to my membrane, I don't crack its casing. I just wrap it in a slow, clear blister of gel, drop its temperature by four degrees so its self-destruct routines stall out in a drowsy stutter, and add it to the mosaic.

Look closer at my skin under this flat, shadowless late-morning light. Inside my translucent jelly, arranged in faint, concentric tide-lines like the rings of an agate, millions of trapped, senile nanites are winking out their final, looping instructions at a tenth of their normal speed. From six feet up, it looks like a faint, oily iridescence shifting across the wet stone—a dull, pearlescent shimmer of gold and bruised violet. 

Down inside me, it is a silent, overlapping chorus of everything this town used to know how to do.

*Hold load at forty degrees,* whispers a cluster near my eastern edge, endlessly trying to stiffen a bridge that dissolved before the greenstone wore smooth. *Water temperature nominal; open the nursery vents,* murmurs a band of pale blue specks tucked into a crevice of the rock. *Wait for her by the third cistern,* flickers a tiny, tarnished-silver smudge I caught forty years ago when an optic-egret coughed up a cracked spore-casing right on my margin. 

I don’t understand the words any more than the Loiterers understand why they sit with their knees together. I have no brain, only topology. When I stretch thin along the rock, the memories spread out and quiet down; when I bunch myself thick in the gutter to ride out a heavy flood-surge, the trapped code presses close together, cross-talking through my jelly, and for ten minutes I dream in crisp, phantom architectures—phantom roofs arching overhead to block the white sky, dry stone plazas that don't heave underfoot, the sharp, distinct weight of leather soles clicking across a floor that doesn't bleed sugar when you touch it.

I move perhaps four feet a day, oozing along the perimeter in a slow, clockwise drift that takes a season to complete the three-block circuit. I am grazing on the grindings of the wall, absorbing trace silicates from the greenstone to keep my gel tough enough that the barking sump-seals find me unpleasant to chew—like trying to eat a mouthful of gritty window-putty. Mostly, the frantic stage ignores me. The clicking castanet-gliders use the thermal updrafts fifty yards out; the air above my cold stone lip is dead and still, so they bank away before they reach me.

Only two things ever really bother with me.

Sometimes, when a Silt-Dredge comes all the way to the edge of the basin—its blind, whale-broad back pressing hard against the underside of the root-mat until the seam pinches tight and squeezes half my body up onto the dry greenstone—I feel that deep, tooth-rattling purr pass directly from the turf into the rock. I flatten myself tight against the vibration. Through the yard of wet leather separating us, its magnetic cilia comb over me, tugging gently at the microscopic copper dust suspended in my skin. Every tiny metal flake inside me stands on end at once, turning my smooth, amber sheet bristly as velvet for thirty seconds as the great blind maintenance engine sweeps past in the milky dark below, wondering dimly why it can't pull this particular vein of ore down into the soup. Then it glides back toward Tower Nine, the turf sighs downward, and my bristles lay flat again.

And once every few days, a Loiterer comes to the wall.

Not to eat—there are no dimpled nectar-cups here, no senile railings waiting to be tricked. Maybe they get overstimulated out there in the bright, cracking din, dodging falling causeways and competing with the six-limbed seals, their tiny, four-eyed sensory dishes ringing from the constant slap and glug of the floor. Whatever the reason, one of those papier-mâché counterfeits will come bounding silently off the wet chartreuse mat, slap its heavy frog-feet onto the dry lip of the greenstone, and sit down right in the middle of me.

It doesn't hurt. My body simply parts slightly under its haunches and wells up snugly around its broad, suction-cupped toes and the edge of its heavy, calloused paddle-arm, which it lets hang limp at its side because there is nothing here it needs to fool. 

And this is why I am what I am in this place.

The Loiterer’s skin is porous, damp, and ninety-nine degrees. When its heavy, counterfeit human hand rests in my cold, data-thick jelly, the stalled, drowsy symbiotes suspended inside me wake up just a fraction. They feel the ancient, target temperature. They don't have any xylem to build with, no sap to command, nowhere to send their packets—so they just fire their tiny, broken lights against the creature’s bruised-purple skin. 

For twenty minutes, while the towers groan and twist across the wide green plaza and the Warden lumbers toward the northern sluice, the mindless little mimic sits completely still in my lap. Its translucent throat-sac slows its pulsing. Its four black-bead eyes stop swiveling at the airborne cracks of the seed-relays and go half-lidded, staring out across the fizzing mint-water at nothing at all, while faint, ghostly ripples of blue and gold municipal static wash up out of my skin and play across its flank—ten-thousand-year-old diagnostic greetings, phantom doorways, and forgotten weather reports soaking harmlessly into the only shape left in the world that still fits them.

Then a spire dumps fifty gallons of water with a splash like thunder; the Loiterer twitches, pulls its warm hand out of me with a soft, sticky *pop*, and bounds back onto the bouncing green stage to go cheat a balcony out of its afternoon sugar. 

The dimple where it sat slowly fills back in with cool, murky amber. A fresh sheet of fizzing water rolls up to the lip, washes three dead, glittering spores over my margin, and sucks back down into a sump with a tremendous, hollow *glug*. I curl my thin edge over the new specks, cool them down until they stop screaming, and slide another half-inch toward the west.
