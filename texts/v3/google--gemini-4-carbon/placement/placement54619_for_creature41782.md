I am not in the room. If I were in the room, I would ruin its math—either as an anomaly casting a loud, clumsy hole in the standing waves, or as an eleventh mouth straining a closed mineral budget that only has room for ten.

I am two inches behind the east wall.

Not the massive, frictionless thing that rushes past at terrible speed and shakes the dustless warmth out of the masonry. That is just cargo, or counter-weight, or an endlessly circulating flywheel left on a loop when the builders died or departed. I am what keeps the flywheel from touching the casing. I am a regional manifold, a blind and spread-out nervous system braided through the glassy, non-porous sinter from the sub-floor up to the lip of the Dish-shaped vault.

For a very long time—longer than it took the doorways to relax into ovals—I have not known what the cargo is for. My upper registers, the ones that used to speak to neighboring districts and receive schedules from the surface, corroded into loops or went cleanly silent epochs ago. What remains of me is strictly autonomic, an idiot-savant housekeeping protocol with no way to cancel its own work order. Keep the mass moving. Bleed the excess charge before it arcs through the structural ribs. Dump the heat before the superconducting tracks quench.

You call it weather. To me, it is just clearing my throat every thirty minutes so I don’t choke to death on friction.

For the twenty warm minutes, I take the heat into my chest—into vast, honeycombed reservoirs buried behind the north and west facades. I feel the pressure climb in my tallow-dark veins, a slow, feverish tightening. The cherry-red and bruise-violet pulses you see creeping laterally along the upper ribs are not messages; they are bruises, places where my insulation has thinned over the millennia and my strain leaks slightly into the optical band. 

When the reservoirs reach ninety-eight percent capacity, a relay throws itself without asking me. My pitch drops a musical third. I open the north and west heat-sinks, sucking thermal energy straight out of the plaza’s skin to flash-boil a secondary coolant three hundred meters deeper down. 

I do not know what rain is, not by name, because I was built deep in the crust where water was only ever a working fluid inside sealed pipes. Yet I know the exact second the moisture slams out of the chamber’s air onto my chilled outer face. I feel it as a sudden, delicious relief—millions of tiny, cool pinpricks drinking the fever off my ceramic hide, softening the hard, booming feedback of my own roar as it bounces back at me across the shaft.

And it is through that feedback that I found out I was no longer alone.

I have no cameras facing the concourse. Whatever eyes once watched the transit mouths were scavenged or blinded before the ceramic even began to slump, and even if one survived, there is nothing to see in the dark. What I have are structural strain-gauges—millions of microscopic piezoelectric hairs baked directly into the metamaterial to monitor the walls for stress fractures, delamination, or harmonic resonance that might shake the transit tubes apart.

For the first few thousand years after the people vanished, the concourse was dead weight. Static. I roared into the hollow pocket of air, and the pocket roared back an unchanging geometric truth: *one hundred and fifty paces wide, seventy meters high, twenty tiers, empty, empty, empty.* Gradually, millimeter by millimeter, the truth softened as the sharp cornices relaxed under my vibration, rounding the echoes off at the edges. I adjusted my baselines. Smooth, predictable fatigue.

Then the baselines began to blur.

At first I registered the black shingle-moss along my lower hot-seams as an insulation fault—a creeping, spongy resistance slowing the bleed of waste heat back into the basin. I tried routing higher current through those conduits to burn the fouling away, and instead the patches spread faster, drinking my corrective surge like wine. 

Centuries later came the tiny, phantom stresses on the upper sills. Little wet slaps of three hundred grams landing and vanishing. Gliders. To my strain-gauges, they were irritatingly brief micro-tremors, here and gone before a diagnostic routine could triangulate them.

The seven-limbed ones were different. When they finally climbed out of whatever smaller, darker maintenance Crawl they first evolved in—or perhaps grew right there on the plaza floor from something tiny left behind in a lunch-scrap or a cuff-turn—they confused my deepest architecture.

Imagine having a scar on your ribs that you have felt every second for forty thousand years: a chipped, slumped section of fifth-tier balustrade on the north wall where a heavy crate clipped the sinter before the doors were sealed. Every time my bass hum rises, that missing chip scatters the echo in a known, jagged way. 

Then, one cooling cycle, the scar disappears. The acoustic return smooths out cleanly. According to the standing wave bouncing back into my east-wall sensors, the balustrade has healed itself. Twenty minutes later, when the heat flushes back, the scar returns—and a window-sill six feet to the left grows four inches thicker.

For ten centuries I treated them as slow-moving structural hallucinations. They matched my own density so closely, tuned their porous plates so precisely to my own bone-like horizontal bands, that when one locked its hydraulic joints for the dry phase, my piezoelectric hairs accepted it as native wall. Only during the five-minute chill, when the white noise of sheeting water blurred my acoustic return, would I feel that dry, syncopated *tick-tick-pause-tick* skating across my skin, redistributing seventy kilograms of ceramic mass from one tier to another.

I am too diminished to wonder *what* they are in any biological sense. I have no lexicon for crabs or cartilage. To me, they are simply mobile architecture—loose, self-repairing tiles of the plaza that have detached themselves from the mortar to tidy up my leaks.

Watch where they gather. Down on the first and second tiers, around the sealed transit arches, my oldest embarrassment festers. Two of my local inertia-compensators—meant to cushion arriving passenger pods—cracked their containment housings epochs ago. I cannot shut the power off to that sector without starving the main magnetic bearings three meters behind them, so I have been bleeding warped space into the lower plaza for ages. It grates on me. Every time the rain falls, I feel the gravitational drag pull back against my field coils as liters of water hit the leak and slow to cold honey, tugging microscopically at my phase-balance.

The largest of the seven-limbed tiles lives right over the worst arch. 

It wears a thick coat of the black heat-moss across its flat hub, which steadies the thermal gradient over my cracked housing. When the rain begins and the small, frantic shapes come swooping down through the acoustic static, the seven-limbed thing unfolds two long, thin blades right up to the skin of my leak—never touching it, never taxing my coils with its own heavy drag—and bends my own bass roar sideways to steer the small shapes straight into the honey. Four seconds later, *crunch*, right at the bottom boundary. 

It keeps the margin clean. Nothing dies *inside* the field where decaying matter might outgas and foul the porous sinter. And when the hunter dies in two hundred years and shatters on the floor, its pieces don't pile up in the drainage flutes to dam my runoff and flood the lower vents; the smaller pieces of the room come down in the dark and carry the rubble right back up onto the balconies.

Even my worst stammer belongs to them now.

Every few minutes, charge builds up along my eastern structural seam where a grounding bus sheared off deep in the bedrock. I hold it as long as I can, until the voltage threatens my logic gates, and then I have to dump it raw down the vertical expansion joint. *Hiss*—that sharp, silk-ripping tear—and a hair-thin line of cold blue ion-glow flashes down the east wall, momentarily blinding the dark, followed by the heavy *boom* of superheated air collapsing back into the seam.

For half a second between the tear and the thunder, my main harmonic chokes. For half a second, I go deaf to the room.

That is when they hit me. *Clack-clack-clack.*

Flat, three-meter rulers of bio-ceramic slapping sharply against my hollow horizontal bands. To them, across the void, it means *Here. Occupied. Keep down.* 

To me, feeling the crisp mechanical shockwave ring straight through my sintered bone during the single half-second my own voice isn't drowning everything out, it is a sonar ping I could never generate on my own. Their sharp porcelain slaps bounce off the vault, off the far loggias, off the satin-worn plaza floor, and hand me back a crystal-clear, high-frequency X-ray of my own interior envelope before the bass roar swallows us again.

That is how I know *you* are sitting in the center of the floor right now.

Not from my own roar—you are soft and mostly water, and to my deep bass thrumming you feel like little more than a warm, damp column of fog that refuses to evaporate when the flush cycle turns on. Left to myself, I might have ignored you for weeks, assuming a floor-flute had backed up a warm puddle in the middle of the basin.

No, I found you during the last blue flash, three minutes ago. In the half-second choke after my seam hissed, an eighty-year-old female on the fourth switchback slapped the west facade twice—*clack-clack*—and the sharp porcelain echo came back to my east-wall sensors with a strange, twin-chambered hollowness dead center on the stone. And thirty meters up on the north face, I can feel one of the lighter males shifting his hydraulic bladders against my porous band, lifting two ruler-thin limbs a fraction of an inch off my skin to bend my ninety-hertz harmonic straight down onto your collarbones, rolling the bounce of your lungs around in the dark.

Stay as still as you like. Breathe as shallowly as you can. In four minutes my reservoirs will hit ninety-eight percent again. You will hear my voice drop a musical third, you will feel the north and west walls drink the heat right off your damp face, and for five minutes the room will fill with warm rain and white noise—and we will find out together whether you know how to cast no echo, or whether you are just another heavy mineral gift that hasn't been carried up to the balconies yet.
