I am four tiers down from the rim-lights, where the sideways gold never quite reaches in a solid beam—only in broken, nervous coins of brightness shaken loose through three layers of wet alder leaf and slatted catwalk. 

You wouldn't look for me unless a cable-anchor started to creep or a hearth-floor began to list two degrees toward the bog. I live in the armpit of an ancient, split-trunked cloud-cedar they call the Old Crutch, right where the sweet, fast snow-water falling from the upper flumes hits the slower, tea-brown seep of the lower tiers. My knees have the cold-weather click in them now—too slow for kicking ice-jams out of the high chutes—so I came down into the thick of the weight.

At first, if you peered off the greasy rail of the Tanner’s Walk through the blowing mist, you’d think I was just another bulge in the root-wall. That is the sedge-cape doing its work, grey-green and sprouted at the hem with tiny, pale tear-ferns I haven't bothered to pick off since last moon’s rains. Wait a second, though, and you’ll see the pale flash of my palm slapping the wet trunk, and hear the dull *tok… tok… tomp* of my horn mallet.

I am a listener in the dark wood, a seam-minder. Not quite one of the high bough-pleaters—they work up in the bright wind with young green shoots, planning rooms for grandchildren who aren't born yet. Down here, we don't plan; we hold. Everything above us—sixty hearth-slates, four hundred water-goats shifting their weight in the hurdles, six tons of rushing flume-water, and every screaming jay and dripping haystack of a neighbor—presses straight down through the crooked knees of six huge, half-drowned trees. 

Put your ear directly against the wet, velvety liverwort on the Old Crutch’s flank. Go on, press your cheek right into the cold wetness; wipe the slime off on your sleeve later. Hear that? Under the roar of the third-tier spillway, under the *thump-slosh* of the fuller’s bucket overhead, the wood itself is talking. It doesn't groan like dead ship-timber. Living wood under load *hums*—a tight, fibrous, stretched-bow vibration that comes right through your jawbone. 

When a root-arch six feet under the black peat-water starts to go spongy at the core, the hum drops half a note. It goes flat, like a slack drumskin. 

That is my whole life: hunting flat notes in the wet dark, with three fingers of my left hand wedged into a bark-fissure to feel the shear-strain while a heavy gust hits the basin up top. When the wind slams into the horseshoe, you feel it down here three seconds later—first the high rigging whistles, then a shower of cold drops and knocked-loose cavy-straw patters down through the slats onto my spruce-root hat, and *then* the great cedar-rib under my palm swells, tightens like a bull’s neck, and settles back half a thumb-breadth into the mud.

I don't work alone, though I rarely bother wasting breath on words down here where the water-drum is loudest. Hanging off my left collar-strap, tucked inside the smoky warmth between my bladder-skin poncho and my greasy under-fleece, is a three-legged polecat with half an ear gone to an otter trap. She’s useless on the open rigging—slips on the slick bark when she tries to corner fast—so a flume-runner gave her to me rather than skinning her for a mitten-liner. She earns her char-heads. When my horn mallet finds a suspect pocket under a grafted knee-joint, I don't cut the bark open right away; cutting lets the wet rot-spores in. I pry up a thumb-sized flap of turf-packing and point my chin. She pours herself out of my shirt like warm oil, flows headfirst into the black gap between the grafted boughs, and sniffs. If it’s just cave-crickets or a drowned mouse nest, she backs out sneezing and wipes her whiskers on my beard. If she starts digging furiously and chattering her teeth, there are white wood-grubs in the heart-grain, and it’s time for me to whistle up two boys with a pot of boiling pine-pitch and a green alder splint.

My workshop isn't a room; it’s a crooked shelf wedged directly behind the Copper-Beater’s water-wheel, because I am greedy for his leftover heat. His chimney-flue—just a hollowed, clay-lined trunk smeared thick with bog-mud—passes right behind my sitting-log. It leaks thin ribbons of sour, sweet alder smoke through twenty hairline cracks, turning the underside of the boardwalk above my head shiny black with soot-tar, where even the moss won't grow. 

Sit here with me for ten minutes out of the wind, knees drawn up to your chest so a splashing goat-boy on the ramp outside doesn't kick peat-slurry into your lap, and you’ll see what keeps the town’s belly warm. 

Everything I own hangs within one arm’s reach, rattling softly whenever the great wheel outside takes a heavy dump of snowmelt from the chute above:
*   Four crooked gouges of cold-hammered copper, their handles wrapped in rough, dried shark-skin traded from seven valleys away so they never twist in a tallow-greased fist.
*   Wooden tubs of "black-chew"—my own mix of rendered mutton-suet, scraped spruce-resin, crushed charcoal, and chopped goat-hair. You have to warm a lump of it in your cheek like tough jerky before you can thumb it deep into a splitting cable-notch, which means my teeth are stained dark as bog-oak and everything I eat tastes faintly of turpentine and old sheep.
*   Coils and coils of split willow-root, soaking in a hanging leather bucket of tannin-red bark-water so they stay supple as eels until the moment I lash them tight, knowing they’ll shrink iron-hard as soon as the wind hits them—no, wait, nothing dries down here. We don't bind with drying-shrinkage; we bind *green-to-green* and drive dry mountain-ash wedges into the weave so the wet swells the wedge and locks the whole joint until it grows bark over itself.

Look down between the gaps in my slate-stones. Three feet below my greased heels, the black pool-water moves in a slow, muscular eddy. Big, hook-jawed char hang there in the shadow beneath my floor, nose-first into the current, waiting for me to drop peelings or scrape grease off my leggings. Sometimes an otter pops its slick, whiskered head right up through the drain-hole by my wood-pile, stares at me with flat, dark-amber eyes, hisses once at the three-legged polecat bristling under my chin, and drops back down without leaving a ripple.

People think the low tiers are gloomy, all mud and goat-piss and dripping tails, and the runners up on the rim-chutes make a great show of holding their noses with mint-sprigs when they have to slide down a stay-cable into my shadow to fetch a heavy maul. Let them brag. Up on the high terraces, the wind strips the fat right off your ribs, and a bad squall tears your roof-thatch clean out over the abyss. 

Down here, you are inside the animal. 

Around mid-afternoon, when the cloud-rack breaks open out past the invisible lip of the mountain, one singular, blinding shaft of pale-gold light spears sideways under the Tanner’s bridge, misses the goat-hurdles, and hits the curtain of spray throwing off the Copper-Beater’s wheel six feet from my nose. For twenty minutes, my smoky, dripping shelf sits directly behind a roaring wall of pulverized gold. Every floating mote of wood-smoke, every drifting seed-tuft, every flashing drop of ice-water catches fire at once. The emerald liverwort on the Old Crutch glows like lit tallow from the inside out. My scarred, purple knuckles look almost young in that glare as I reach out, hook two fingers into my mouth, and blow a sharp, flat-double *wheep-tock!* up through the slats.

Forty feet above, invisible through four decks of weaving cavies, clucking slate-hens, and rushing troughs, somebody’s wet boot stamps twice on an overhead beam in answer. *Got it. Shifting the third gate.*

A wooden clack echoes down the trunk. Overhead, the water-note changes—less thunder on the wheel, a gentler hiss in the bypass reed-chute—taking half a ton of trembling push off the cedar knee I’ve just bound up in peat and fresh willow-skin. I pat the wet bark twice with an open, pitch-black palm, shift my butt two inches closer to the warm clay flue, and pull a cold barley-cake smeared with rank, salty butter out of my hat-crown, breaking off the crusty corner for the polecat before the jays hear me chewing.
