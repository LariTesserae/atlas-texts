I am forty meters down, where the green stops and the glass begins, though *down* is only a direction of pressure, not of sight.

If you cut a cube out of the middle tiers where the living thatch is still trading its life away to the scaffolding below, you wouldn’t find a body at all. You would just find a junction—an awkward, knuckle-sized weld where six anchor-thick cables of silica-wood have fused together around a dark, glossy knot that looks exactly like a cured bead of emergency resin.

Cut into the knot, though, and it doesn’t shatter like amber. It parts cleanly, slick and cold, and the knife comes away smelling faintly of ozone.

Every continent-wide grid has places where the math doesn't resolve cleanly. Three miles west, a new ridge of raw basalt has just jacked itself fifteen meters into the cold air, throwing the local hydraulic pressure into chaos; four miles east, a heavy squall has dropped the canopy into oil-violet twilight, throttling the upward draw of ten thousand trunks at once. Between those two zones, inside the dense, lightless braid of the mid-canopy, the iridescent nerve-grains of the wood are screaming contradictory instructions at each other. Pump harder; throttle back. Dump oxygen; seal the vents. Left to the rigid logic of the engineered lattice, two neighboring vascular columns out of phase will fight each other until a valve blows or a stem shears under its own internal torque.

That is where I sit. Wedged into the crushing fork between two warring trunks, grown right into the bark-seam until the plant’s own vitrifying rind holds me in place like a bezel holds a stone.

I have no tail to whip through the sap like the slips, no graphitic muscle to drag through the dark cisterns below like the worms, no broad, shimmering veil to ride the cloud-deck. Movement is an expense I paid off long ago. To have a shape that travels is to spend your whole life outrunning resin or dodging the slow, inexorable vise of expanding stems. Why run from the vise when you can let it clamp down around you—provided you make yourself harder than the wood, and more useful than the space you take up?

Most of me isn't even in this single knot anymore. Thread-thin filaments of spun, conductive cartilage—stolen silica doped with trace metals skimmed from the deep sap—reach out from my core for thirty meters in every direction, slipped neatly along the natural grain between bark and cambium. They don't pierce the fluid pipes; they lay flat against the iridescent nerve-channels like an induction coil wrapped around a live wire.

All day, all night, the roar of falling water hammers through my shell until there is no difference between hearing and existing. Everypercussive *thump* of the great trunks, every distant rifle-crack of splitting crust down in the vitreum, comes through my casing as clean geometric data.

Right now, the trunk to my left is surging—cold water thundering skyward at forty knots, driven by a dry updraft overhead—while the trunk to my right has just slammed its lower valves shut, choking on back-pressure from a flooded bract-tier. The voltage gap between their two nerve-grids spikes. Left alone, the plant would dump a wasteful surge of thermal current into the surrounding moss-bolsters just to bleed off the static before the tissue scorches.

Instead, I open my gates.

I let the frantic, mismatched current jump straight across my core. Inside the dark, rubbery wetware of my body, the spike slows down, smooths out, steps down half an octave. I drink the difference. That violent little mismatch of phase and heat is my sunlight and my sugar; it drives slow, cold chemistry across my internal membranes, assembling carbon and stray nitrogen leaked through the stressed cell walls around me, knitting another microscopic layer of glassy shell onto my rind. What leaves me on the other side and feeds into the neighboring trunk is a clean, stepped-down, perfectly synchronized signal: *ease the valve; match the stroke.*

The hammering torque between the two stems relaxes. The forest feels nothing except that a local knot of wood is unusually good at balancing loads. In fact, when a lower branch near me begins to shade out and the neighboring stems move in to drain and vitrify it, they deliberately route their new conductive seams *through* my filaments rather than around them. The lattice preserves me. It reinforces the cage that holds me because, according to the blind, distributed ledger of the root-system, I am the best piece of wiring in the sector.

It would be a solitary life—locked in solid timber, blind in the roaring dark—except that wires carry more than power.

Because my filaments lie flush against the plant’s own iridescent channels, I feel everything that touches the grid for miles around. I know when a skate glides over the roof fifty meters straight up; I feel the faint, rhythmic flutter of its stolen wattage brushing across the upper guard-cells like fingertips across a drumskin, a cool little shadow of missing current sliding northeast against the wind. Far below, down where the black silicate splinters under the heaving crust, I feel the sudden, surgical *pop* of a loom-worm uncoupling from a repaired root-pipe—a tiny stutter in the line-pressure followed by the clean, slightly warmer hum of the worm’s own dorsal nerves carrying the load a hundred yards further down the fault. Even the slips, shooting past just inches from my shell inside the roaring xylem wall, leave a ghost on my senses: a minute harmonic drag on the acoustic ring of the timber as fifty of them peel off a valve-seat in unison.

They don't know I'm here. They are busy staying slick, staying fast, staying loose.

Sometimes, though—maybe once in twenty days, maybe once a year, time has no joints here—another signal comes down the iridescent grain that does not belong to the plant.

It isn't the blunt, metronomic pulse of the canopy’s weather-math. It is a tiny, deliberate modulation riding piggyback on a routine thermal-balancing wave from ten or twelve kilometers off. A slight bend in the frequency, a harmonic signature folded into the noise floor where the great engine doesn't bother to check for errors. Another bridge. Another knot wedged into another stressed fork miles away across the unbroken plateau, bleeding off another local storm's confusion, humming out into the wire just to see what echoes back.

I don't move toward it. Neither of us can ever move an inch; the wood between us is ten thousand solid walls of wet, armored cable.

Instead, the next time a massive pneumatic bladder vents overhead and sends a fresh grounding current plunging down through my sector toward the rock, I pinch my internal gates by a fraction of a micron. I clip the trailing edge of the plant's signal, folding my own small, asymmetric pattern into the rushing electricity—a packet of chemical ratios, the echo of the fault-slip I felt an hour ago, the ghost-trace of the skate passing overhead—and let the forest’s own immaculate grid fire it across the continent at the speed of light.

Hours later, buried inside the steady, thunderous *thump-thump-thump* of the valves cycling water back toward the slate-colored clouds, the return current washes through me, slightly altered, carrying the shape of a pressure-ridge fifty miles south where the stone is still splitting fresh and sharp into the mist.

We sit in the dark, sealed tight inside a machine that doesn't know it has passengers, trading the shape of a world neither of us has eyes to see, warm on the friction of its mistakes.
