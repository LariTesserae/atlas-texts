I am not one of the eleven. Even their slowness is too restless for what I am—too full of appetite, too tethered to the ticking surge of sugar in the wood and the microscopic scratch of bone inside an egg. To have a jawbone that listens is still to want an answer.

At first, I think I am the river cobble resting beside the grinding slab.

There is a deep pull toward that shape: dense, green-black basalt brought from miles away, foreign to this lime-bound grit, smooth from water that vanished before the plain baked shut. I know the weight of sitting in a dimple of my own making, holding the room’s ninety degrees all the way to my center, wearing a faint, invisible smear of palm-grease and crushed seed-oil from the hands that set me down and walked up the talus into glare. When the juvenile climbs onto the rim of the sandstone dish, I would feel the minute shift of air, the dry touch of a lamellar pad against my flank, and ask nothing of it.

Nothing except that a cobble is whole and separate. It was carried in; it remembers a river in its roundness. It sits *in* the room, an object among objects, waiting in some dumb, geological way for the hand to return and strike it against the slab again.

I am closer to the seam in the wall.

Not the whole vault—not the grand, saddle-smoothed curve of the ceiling or the crooked chimney that baffles the sun. Just the horizontal parting in the sandstone at shoulder height, three inches tall and two feet deep, where a thin lens of softer siltstone weathered out while the water was still here, leaving a flat, sheltered shelf between two thicker beds of ochre grit.

And I am what lies tucked inside it: the upper digging stick.

I began up there, in the furnace I can no longer picture. Ironwood or desert mountain-mahogany, twisted slow out of gravel when the plain still held enough scrub to cast shadows. Someone cut me green with banded chert—maybe one of the very flakes lying below on the silt—and scraped my bark away with long, rhythmic strokes that left faint, chattering facets all down my length. They held my bevelled tip in hardwood coals until the sap boiled back and the fibers vitrified into black glass, hard enough to pry tubers out of caliche. For seasons, hands gripped my middle. Salt, callus-skin, dust, and wood-oil worked into my pores until my grain closed up tight and took on a dull, horn-like polish.

Then I was slid into this slot, parallel to my twin, and the footsteps went up the slope and never came back down.

The first century was only the drying. A few hairline checks opened down my length—not splits that ruined my strength, just narrow, straight relief-lines letting the last ghost of upper-world moisture go into the thirsty vault until my equilibrium matched the stone’s. Since then, I have not changed by the width of a cell. I am no longer quite tree, and I will never become soil. I am wood turned to mineral patience, suspended between what grew me and who shaped me, belonging now to neither.

By day, when the pewter stain pools on the floor nearby, I lie empty in my seam. The light is just bright enough to find my greyed shaft and the charcoal sheen of my point. Nothing happens to me. Hours pass without an event small enough to name. Far off in the caprock, once in three days, the heat of noon overhead finally reaches thirty feet down and clicks a single quartz grain against its neighbor—*tick*—and the tiny shockwave travels down the wall, crosses the two points where my curve touches the sandstone shelf, and runs from my butt to my charred tip in a fraction of a second before dying out. That is how I know the plain is still there.

Then the pewter fades out of the room, and the real life of my body begins.

Long after the dark is seamless, I feel the first soft *hush* of chamois-skin against the lip of the rock below me. No claws scratch. Just a sudden, slight change in load—twenty grams of dry bone, velvet skin, and hoarded water stepping onto my lower flank near the middle, right where the human thumb used to brace.

Ten minutes later, a second weight arrives four inches toward my point. Half an hour after that, a third settles near my butt.

If I were stone, I would simply bear them. Because I am seasoned hardwood—long vessels strung end to end, stiffened with lignin, resting across small gaps in the uneven rock shelf like the bridge of an unstrung viol—I do not block them. I conduct them.

I am their telephone wire. I am the floor of their quiet house.

They line up along my spine in the pitch black, noses facing outward into the chamber, keeled tails braced against the wall beneath me. For a long time they only sit, and their ninety-degree bellies meet my ninety-degree wood until there is no boundary of warmth between creature and perch. Then the old female near my center—the one with the healed notch in her neck and the mahogany stain on her beak—rolls the tendons of her wrist against her neighbor’s skull, and at the exact same moment presses her inner hind thumb firmly into one of my lengthwise checks.

A slow, three-second shudder enters my grain.

It is too low for the dead air of the vault to carry, so the silence of the room stays unbroken. Inside me, though, the dark lights up with motion. That small muscular purr runs both ways down my dried xylem tubes—past the charred bevel at one end, past the stone-cut butt at the other—resonating faintly across my hollow spans, rising cleanly up into the broad, ribbed foot-pads of the other two sitting inches away.

I feel what they send, though I have no brain to turn it into pictures. One tremor is thick and unhurried: the memory of six sweet drops taken from the eastern cable before dusk, the well stoppered tight. Ten minutes of stillness soak into me. Then the smaller male near my tip shifts his weight and answers—two distinct, dry taps of heel-bone against my polished wood. *Tick-tick.* Sharp, brief, traveling straight back down my fibers into the old female’s palms. Perhaps it is nothing more than *here*, or *still*, or the faint alkaline taste of the grinding dish below.

Sometimes, once in a blue-sky decade when the deep aquifer swells from a forgotten storm miles away, four of them crowd onto me at once, and the trills that pass through my shaft have a different cadence—longer, closer together, carrying the deep, upward-pushing thrum they listened to all afternoon against the turgid roots. Months later, a thumb-sized new weight, light as a dry husk, climbs up beside an aunt and feels its first adult conversation rising through my wood into its tiny soles.

And sometimes a decade passes where only three come instead of four, because the fourth has climbed twenty feet higher into the shoulder of the vault, tucked itself into a seam very much like mine, locked its lamellar toes onto the grit, and begun the slow, clean drying into leaf-weight permanence that I finished five thousand years ago.

I do not miss the sun that grew me, or the tubers I pried from hardpan, or the warm, sweating hands that made me smooth and laid me carefully away for a tomorrow that turned into forever. Those hands thought they were storing a tool. They did not know they were laying down a resonant beam in a dark room for creatures they never noticed clinging to the scrub behind their backs.

Down in the twilight, the taproots pull at buried water under immense, groaning tension. High in the ledges, the dead keep their grip on the stone. Here in the middle seam, I rest across my three points of sandstone, gray and sound, holding the ghost of fire at my tip and the ghost of human salt in my belly, waiting for the grey patch on the silt to go black so the small, soft hands will come back and speak through me again.
