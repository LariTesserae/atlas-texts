I am not down in the sumps where the black oil pools, and I am not among the heavy-limbed tenders sliding frictionlessly past one another on the spiral stairs. My place is at the very top of the tear, wedged flat against the dark, glassy underside of the ceiling plug, forty yards above the duckweed.

Here, the heat of the pit reaches its absolute zenith. It collects against the sealed rock roof like water pooling upside down, an motionless, hundred-and-twenty-degree cushion of stale air, sulfur, and ester-fumes. Where the bundles of heavy copper conduit and the thick iron Sweat-pipes pierce the basalt to vanish into the forty yards of solid rock between here and the surface, there is a manifold—a dense, riveted collar of brass junctions, check-valves, and pressure-relief dials bolted directly into the stone. 

I am the manifold-keeper, though to look up through the bruised-plum canopy from a middle catwalk, you would only think one of the vulcanized rubber cistern-bladders had been hung up near the roof to cure. 

Even among the thirty who inhabit the vertical maze, my blood runs thin and strange. You cannot send the largest, heaviest-boned tenders all the way to the ceiling; the stamped-steel catwalks end fifteen feet short of the plug, giving way to a dense, horizontal briar of hot pipes, copper wiring, and blistered heat-vines suspended over a sheer drop. To work the ceiling collar, you must be small, flat-chested, and light enough to trust your weight to a two-inch conduit without bowing the copper and cracking a brazed joint. 

I spend weeks at a time without descending past the upper flywheels. My ribs are hinged flatter than most, my limbs slightly more attenuated, pale tallow stretched over pliant cartilage. I lie belly-down across a hammock of warm, bundled return-pipes, my cheek pressed directly against the main iron Sweat-pipe descending from the upper world. 

Because that pipe comes down from the cold rock above before the pit’s air can warm it, it is perpetually slick with icy condensation. That cold iron is my pillow and my tether. Without it, the stagnant heat trapped under the glassy roof would boil my brain in its pan within three hours. So I live in split thermal halves: my back and shoulders bathed in the rising, wet breath of the entire conservatory below, while my sternum, belly, and jaw drink the sharp, metallic chill of the surface-pipe. 

I am the ear at the ceiling, and I am the final palate of the kidney.

Everything the pit refines passes beneath my ribs before it leaves us. The clear, viscous cycad-sap, pumped lazily upward from the glass carboys strapped to the grates below, gathers here in a series of thick-walled glass sight-cylinders set into the brass manifold. My work is to watch the amber glow of the last caged filament bulbs shine through that rising fluid, checking its clarity before the main check-valve admits it into the ascending shaft. 

If a root-mat down in the stew-tanks has ruptured, or if a barrel-cycad has begun to rot internally, the fluid arriving at the ceiling will carry a faint, milky suspension of unbound lithium salts or gray sulfur-floc. A gauge cannot read cloudiness, and if that sediment enters the narrow, forty-yard ascent through the cold rock, it will cool, crystallize on the inner walls of the copper, and choke the artery. If the artery chokes, the pressure in the ward below will have nowhere to bleed; gaskets will blow, hot brine will scald the velvet leaves, and the slow, silent equilibrium of our world will tear itself apart.

So I watch the glass cylinders with tar-black eyes that blink once every three or four minutes. When a faint haze ghosts through the clear sap, I do not hurry. I hook one prehensile big toe around a ceiling bolt for leverage, reach out with a horn-calloused hand, and ease a bypass lever down by three degrees, shunting the cloudy batch back down a narrow lead return-line toward the lowest chemical baths to be drunk by the gourds a second time. 

Sometimes, before I close the bleed-cock, I catch a single drop of the ascending ester on the flat of my knuckle and touch it to my tongue. 

I know the health of every level of the tear by the taste of what rises. A sharp, coppery tang means someone on the middle tiers has newly wired a wound on a giant vine and used too much flux; a sweet, heavy, almost narcotic oiliness tells me the old tender whose heart had slowed to twelve beats last month has finally been slid into the lower boiler-tanks, and the great night-blooming gourds are drinking well and rendering him back into the loop. 

What makes me different from the cousins moving through the grease-chutes below is what I feel through my jawbone. 

They feel only the pit: the *tink-tink* of bronze wrenches on steel struts, the forty-minute sigh of the hydraulic rams, the deep geothermal hum rising from the planetary crust. They know only down and inward. 

I know *up*. 

Pressed against the thick iron of the descending Sweat-pipe and the ascending sap-conduits, my inner ear catches the ghost-vibrations that survive the forty yards of solid basalt plug. They are faint, brittle, frantic things, arriving down the metal like the twitching of insects caught in amber. 

I feel a rapid, metallic *clatter-clatter-clatter* that has no weight behind it—iron wheels rimmed in something hard, striking cobblestones in thin, dry air. I feel the bizarre, high-frequency hum of machinery that turns thousands of times a minute instead of once an hour, burning itself up in a furious fever of friction. Sometimes, at regular intervals that have nothing to do with pressure or brine-heat, a deep, hollow *bong* shivers down the copper—a great bronze bell being struck somewhere in that terrifyingly open emptiness above the rock, counting out divisions of time too small and sharp to be of any use to living flesh.

When those frantic surface-tremors shiver down the pipe into my cheekbone, I curl my broad fingers tighter around the cool iron and close my eyes. I try to imagine an air that does not hold you up like warm tallow—air so thin and dry that water vanishes right off your skin, stealing your heat, cracking your lips, forcing you to burn food like a furnace just to keep your heart hammering at seventy or eighty times a minute. I imagine light that doesn't glow amber from a wire, light so violent it bleaches the dark purple right out of a leaf and leaves it a raw, sickly green. It sounds like falling forever without hitting a catwalk.

Down below me—thirty feet down through the dark leaves and glistening flywheels—a faint *tink... tink-tink* rises up a structural stanchion, passing from steel to brick to copper until it taps lightly against my shin. 

One of the lower tenders is asking for slack on number four brine-return; they are grafting a fresh cluster of cycads near the vulcanized cistern. 

I shift my weight. My rubber tool-apron unpeels from the damp pipe with a soft, wet *schuck*. Reaching down through a curtain of pale, blistered heat-vines, I lay both palms flat against the warm brass rim of the number four gate-wheel, lean my heavy, doughy shoulders into the turn, and let gravity do the work. Over ten minutes, the wheel yields half an inch. Pressure eases silently in the dark below. 

My head is beginning to swim slightly from the hot pocket under the ceiling, a slow, golden stupor thickening behind my eyes. When the valve is set, I slide backward two feet along the pipe-bundle into a jagged V where the basalt plug meets the eastern wall. Cold groundwater weeps steadily from a hairline fracture there, trickling over flinty planes that have never known wind. I wedge my spine tightly into the stone corner, letting the tepid film of mineral water run over my ribs and shoulders, washing the excess heat down into the dark tangle below—where it will drip from a bolt-head, strike a bruised-plum leaf, slide into a root-box, and eventually, weeks from now, rise clear and viscous past my eyes once more, headed for a world that doesn't even know it has a kidney.
