I am hanging upside down in the gutted parlor of the corner tenement, my broad, horn-calloused heels hooked over a charred oak ceiling joist that pickled into black ironwood a century ago. 

My knees are bent just enough to hold my weight—what little weight the heavy, oily brine leaves me—against the upward pull of my own tallow. Around my waist, three thick wedges of pale fish-fat and a coil of annealed copper wire tug gently toward the floorboards twenty feet below, balancing me so precisely that if I unhooked my toes, I would neither sink toward the milky halocline at the wainscoting nor bob up into the toxic blister of hydrogen pinned beneath the stairwell outside.

In my hands I hold the long, flat ash-wood peel. Its blade is slick with bacterial mucilage, dark and smooth as polished horn. 

Above me—which is to say, pressed flat against the stripped plaster lath between my splayed feet—hangs a trembling silver skin of stale air. It is a bad mirror, tarnished at the edges where carbon dioxide has soured it, tasting of old pennies and cellar damp if you break it with your lip. Six feet away, through the unglazed transom of the parlor door, a thin, steady rosary of bright beads rises from a pair of low-voltage copper plates bolted to the masonry. Oxygen, mostly, with a sharp tang of ozone. One bead every two seconds, detaching from the green-furred anode, wobbling upward through the dense brine, flattening itself against the lintel until enough gathers to spill over the brickwork and skitter across the ceiling like liquid mercury.

Without hurrying—because hurry costs heat, and heat is the only real coin down here—I reach out with the wooden peel and catch a palm-sized globule before it can slide off toward the stairwell shaft and marry the hydrogen. 

The bubble pushes back against the wood like a soft, living belly. I coax it across the rough plaster ribs, herding it past a rusted gas-lamp bracket, and fold it seamlessly into the wide, breathable pond pooled inside the third-floor bedroom next door. The ceiling-mirror shivers once in slow, heavy rings that catch the sullen cherry glow of a valve-heater across the alley, turning the underside of the ceiling momentarily red as watered wine.

Before I drop down, I tilt my chin back, slide my face upward through the cold, silvery skin of the bedroom pond, and let my nostrils click open.

The air inside is six inches deep and smells of wet slate, tallow, and the breath of the three others who drank here during the last two falls of the wipers. It is thick, cold, and comforting. I take four long, unhurried draughts, feeling my flattened ribcage widen inside its smooth jacket of blubber, feeling the dark, heavy blood in my chest drink the sharpness down. Above my eyes—still submerged just beneath the trembling meniscus—the plaster ceiling is inches from my nose, studded with the stubby black stalactites of old iron lath-nails furred in sulfide scale. When I exhale, warm fog blooms briefly against the wet plaster and vanishes back into the damp.

My nostrils seal shut. I pull my face down out of the mirror, watching the silver skin heal instantly without a drip, and unhook my heels from the joist.

I do not swim. Swimming stirs the graphite silt off the window ledges and wastes the afternoon’s warmth. Instead, I lay two stubby fingers against the rounded brick jamb, push once with the strength of a wrist-flick, and let the brine take me out through the tall sash window into the three-foot shaft of the street.

Out here, my eyes scarcely matter. The watch-crystal cauls stay drawn across them against the salt, turning the world into vague blocks of charcoal shadow, bruised violet under the archway rectifiers, and smeared vertical pillars of cherry-red down below my feet where the milky halocline cuts across the second-story lintels. 

What I *see* with are the stippled seams across my cheekbones, my brow, and the backs of my forearms.

As I glide diagonally down the narrow canyon between the brick walls, the town presses against my skin in a rich, layered topography of current. To my left, behind fourteen inches of vitrified brick, a stairwell thermocouple bank hums like a warm loaf of bread—a broad, buttery, upward-flowing pressure against my ribs. Below me, down in the dense white gloom of the streetbed, a gutta-percha main swallows; as its magnetic collar slides forward along the copper guide-rod, a smooth, rolling wave of induction brushes up my shins like velvet cat’s-fur.

And ahead, thirty feet north where the overhead gantry crosses the third tier of catwalks, something tastes *sour*.

It isn't a dead short yet. A dead short hits the brow-pores like an ice-pick and smells instantaneously of boiled brine. This is smaller: a tinny, serrated itch right behind the bridge of my nose, pulsing unevenly against the steady galvanic grain of the alley. 

I trail my left hand out as I drift, catch a draped bundle of lead-sheathed cables as thick as a man’s thigh—feeling the polite,contained hum inside the lead, brushing gently past two pale, shoe-last isopods who tuck their antennae politely beneath their skirts as my shadow crosses them—and pivot my body Feet-down beside the fourth cathode harp in the row.

Hanging motionless in the dark, a foot from the vertical wire frame, I wait. Around me, the pale, feathery frost of silver-lead and tin clings to the wires like winter fern. High overhead under the black keels of the ice ceiling, the counterweight latch lets go without a sound. Across the shaft, six bronze wipers begin their greased, twelve-minute descent, shaving the metallic snow free in silent, glittering curtains that drift down through the halocline toward the stoneware hoppers below.

Except on this fourth frame. Halfway down the greased guide-track, the bronze blade checks, shudders a fraction of an inch, and hangs slightly askew, letting its corner kiss the outer wire of the harp. *There*—that sharp, sour prickle against my cheekbones, bleeding good plating-current straight into the iron balcony rail behind me, fizzing a thin thread of stray gas out of the grease.

I hook one broad foot beneath the iron tie-rod of the balcony, anchoring myself, and unclip the flat whalebone scraper from my copper belt.

Close up, in the dim ultraviolet spill from an archway thirty feet below, I see what did it. Not scale this time, and not an isopod. A thick, stubborn tuft of rust-colored wire-weed has colonized the warm brass bushing at the back of the wiper shoe, stiffening itself with stolen iron until its wiry roots wedged between the bronze and the track. Worse, a pale parchment-fish—no bigger than a dinner plate, translucent enough that its dark gut shows like a smudge of ink—has pressed itself flat against the stalled bushing, drinking the tiny trickle of heat caused by the electrical leak.

For half a minute I do nothing at all, just hanging there with the bone blade in my fist, feeling the tiny, frantic flutter of the fish’s gill-nerves against my wrist-pores. Heat is scarce; it found a warm spot; it isn't wrong to want it.

Slowly, moving with the oil-heavy patience of the hoses below, I slide the back of my horn-tipped knuckles under the fish’s belly and peel it gently off the brass. It doesn't bolt—nothing bolts in sub-zero brine—just ripples its pale margin twice, drifts three feet sideways, and plasters itself seamlessly against the glass brick of a cherry-red valve housing instead.

Then I set the whalebone edge against the bushing and pare the wire-weed away. 

It parts with a stiff, gritty crunch I feel through my thumb rather than hear. Little rust-red sprigs tumble slowly away from the frame; down below the milky horizon line, the hair-thin nets of the mortar-worms will catch them before they ever touch the flagstones. With my thumb, I take a pinch of pale tallow from the wedge at my hip—stiff as cold butter, rendered last month off old Marrow’s belly-fat in the warm jacket around the south busbar—and smear it smoothly along the scored bronze track until the mucilage and grease marry again.

Freed, the bronze wiper sighs downward without a sound, shaving a thick, overdue cascade of tin-snow past my knees. Simultaneously, the sour itch vanishes from the bridge of my nose. The local field smooths out like rippled water settling in a tub—clean, quiet, humming in flat parallel lines again.

Before I can clip the whalebone back to my belt, the water behind my right shoulder changes shape.

Not a wake, scarcely even a pressure wave, just a sudden, familiar warmth in the electrical grain of the alley—the slow, deep, two-beat galvanic signature of another heart beating forty times a minute inside an apron of blubber.

Silt slips out through an unglazed parlor window behind me, trailing one hand along a cast-iron penstock to check her drift. She is older than I am; her slate-gray back is scarred white across the left shoulder-blade where a gantry chain pinched her twenty winters ago, and the blubber around her throat has thickened into soft, pale collars that hide her jaw entirely. On her copper belt hangs the heavy bronze grease-horn and two spare leather hopper-slings.

We do not look at each other’s faces—faces are only for drinking air and feeling current. As she coasts into the three-foot gap between the harp and the tenement wall, turning sideways so her smooth, tallowy belly slips past mine with two inches of dark brine between us, she reaches out and lays two blunt, terrapin-padded fingers flat against the bare skin of my inner forearm.

Her hand is cool as cured hide, slick with the universal biofilm of the basin. Nothing moves in her wrist—not a tendon twitches visibly—yet beneath her fingertips, deliberate little volleys of nerve-fire jump across the salt water into the dark pores along my radius.

*South hopper,* her palm prickles quietly against my skin, sharp little staccato flexes from the base of her thumb. *Full to the lip. Heavy.* Then a slower, broader tension across the heel of her hand, warm and unhurried: *Two-haul, after we warm.*

Without lifting my arm, I tighten the deep flexor under her fingers twice—*heard, coming*—and add a tiny, rolling twitch along my little-finger tendon toward the third-floor transom overhead: *Fresh mirror up there. Sweet.*

Her fingers slide off my forearm as momentum carries her another yard down the shaft. She hooks a heel lightly on a window sill, tilts upward, and disappears smoothly through the sash toward the bedroom ceiling-pond to drink before the heavy work.

I let myself sink six feet lower, tucking my back into the shallow brick niche beside the mercury-arc rectifier under the archway. 

For ten minutes—nearly one whole climb of the wipers—I simply press my shoulder blades flat against the pebbled masonry behind the glowing tube, letting the bruised blue pallor fall across my knees while three degrees of waste warmth soak slowly through my gray hide into the thick blood beneath. Two yards away on the red valve housing, the parchment-fish is doing the exact same thing. Neither of us moves a fin or a finger. Below my dangling feet, the milky halocline lies flat and undisturbed as a sheet of ground glass, glowing faintly pink from the buried lamps underneath, while pale flakes of shaved tin drift down into it and vanish like snow falling into a pond.

High up—four stories up, past the draped lead cables and the tiered iron catwalks and the dark, jagged mountains of the black ice ceiling—the narrow copper throat of the exhaust riser shifts its slush an inch upward, then an inch down. I cannot see it from this archway, and I never climb there anymore; I tried once when I was sleek and small, touched a finger into that dead, fresh meltwater where the electricity stops, and felt as though my hand had been lopped off at the wrist into nothingness. 

Down here, nothing is empty. Every wet brick hums; every lead cable purrs under its velvet skin of bacteria; six tenements away, behind four feet of stone and brine, I can faintly feel three of the others sleeping wedged behind the north stairwell thermocouples, their slow hearts ticking in and out of phase like pendulums in thick oil.

Above me, a faint downward push of water slips out of the third-story sash window as Silt leaves the ceiling-pond, her lungs full and heavy, sinking gracefully past the second-floor lintels toward the milky sump and the waiting gantry chains.

I push gently off the warm brick with the flat of my palms, let my weight tip me nose-down, and glide silently through the false horizon of the halocline into the cold, dense white below to take the other chain.
