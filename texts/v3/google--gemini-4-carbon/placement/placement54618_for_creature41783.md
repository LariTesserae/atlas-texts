At first, there is the temptation to stand on the diamond-plate—to wear the heavy rubberized coat, the fogged goggles, the boots with thick vulcanized soles taking the hammer-blow of the reduction gear up into the shins. To hold the putty knife and feel the silent, forty-pound *pop* of cartilage letting go of bright steel.

Wait long enough in that doorway, though, and the observer gets edited out. The pocket has no room for lungs that need air thin enough to breathe rather than drink, or eardrums that tear, or body heat that leaps fifty degrees above the room and throws the whole tight ledger out of balance. A man in that hatch is just an error in the math, a temporary blockage soon to be pushed out or rendered down.

To stay here, you have to drop the coat and the bones. You have to flatten.

Not into the grazers, with their slack, outsourced hearts, letting the machine beat their turquoise blood for them like rows of pale bellows. Not into the copper-jawed worms snapping through the grease at stiff, geometric angles, or the midges locked in their grapefruit-sized prisons of dead air, frantic just to stay in one place. Those are passengers. They were sieved out of the stratosphere and caught on a shelf, and if the shelf holds them forever, they have no say in the holding.

I am six inches behind the brass manifold, wedged into the brutal V where a vibrating intake conduit crosses a rigid flange-beam. 

I am the grey strap. Or rather, I am the tension inside the grey strap—the dense, gristled bridge of flesh that refuses to vibrate at the same pitch as the iron.

Everything else in this drenched rib-pocket surrenders to the roar. The water falls into diamond grids at its command; the pale shingled backs pulse in exact sympathy with the eastern bulkhead; the midges hang pinned to its invisible walls. They let the thunder pass straight through them unchanged. My whole existence is the refusal to let the motion pass through for free.

When the intake pipe shudders left at sixty cycles a second and the riveted structural bracket holds fast, my rubbery, grit-packed flank is clamped across the gap between them. The steel tries to tear me along the seam. Instead, my fibers stretch, drag against the swallowed volcanic glass and drill-filings packed into my hide, and turn that violent mechanical shear into a slow, dull, delicious burn. Forty-eight degrees. Fifty, right at the core where the brass collar of the middle gauge lamp presses into my underside. 

I do not have eyes to see the amber carbon filaments, only a long, contiguous skin that knows *here the metal gives* and *here the metal refuses*, and between the giving and the refusing, there is warmth to be wrung out like tallow from a rag.

It is a remarkably quiet way to live, inside the loudest room in the world. 

Not quiet as an ear understands it—there are no ears here—but quiet in pace. While the feathered gills stacked four-deep along my back beat in a frantic blur, and the worms below twitch like escapement wheels, my thoughts—if a four-minute peristaltic wave of grey muscle can be called a thought—move at the speed of cooling lead. 

*Here is a sharp edge,* thinks the lobe creeping past a sheared steel gusset, coating the razor-bright lip in tough, tallow-slick skin without hurry, taking the cold bite of the new metal until the metal warms beneath me and forgets how to cut.

*Nothing there,* reports the thin finger up on the ceiling, pulling back half a millimeter per hour from the slush-filmed bullseye lens. Too dead. Too cold. The glass doesn’t fight the frame tightly enough to make good friction, and the light coming through it has no heat in its belly. Better to draw that flesh back down the tie-rod and thicken the broad, calloused pad pressed flat against the eastern iron plate.

That eastern pad is my hearth. Four feet behind the steel, the great wheel turns its governed, eternal round, and here against the rivets my three-foot ear of gristle drinks the raw battering ram of it. The pale grazers crowd thickest along the margins of that ear, sliding over one another’s translucent skirts to get close to my fever. I feel their custom-molded suction feet gripping my tough hide instead of the mill-scale. They think I am just a better piece of plumbing—a strangely generous pipe that sweats grease and takes the edge off the thirty-two-point-five-degree spray.

Every now and then, when the stack of them grows heavy enough to dampen my skin’s flex, I don't strike. Striking wastes heat. I simply slacken a long fold of flank, roll my pewter margin up and over three of them like heavy dough folding over stones, and seal the seam. They don't even thrash; their slack hearts keep right on pulsing to the rhythm of the bulkhead through my belly wall until the swallowed iron-filings in my gut and the steady,borrowed shake of the tower grind their soft cartilage and copper blood into grey tallow-broth. Above them, six more immediately shuffle sideways to cover the bare patch on my back. There is no malice in it, and no panic. Just load and bearing, intake and spread.

What I am doing, millimeter by millimeter, week by governed week, is learning the shape of the cistern by becoming its gaskets. 

I hold the hairline crack over the black-green moss so the three little amber suns don't spill their warmth into the spray for nothing. I pad the chattering relief-valve where a loose pin offers an extra fraction of a millimeter of rattle—good, rich rattle, warmer than the straight hum of the wall. And out at my farthest, palest tip, pressed flat against the black rubber seal of the hatchway latch, I have found a new discrepancy.

Something heavy has stepped onto the flooded diamond-plate just inside the threshold. 

I don't see the boots or the fogged glass. What I feel, through the floor-rivets and up the frame of the doorway, is a sudden dampener that isn't me. A column of soft, irregular weight has interrupted the standing diamond-grid of the water trough and slightly—very slightly, by a fraction of a percent—deadened the ring of the lower steel plate. And radiating off it, cutting thinly through the whale-oil mist, is an extravagant, wasteful plume of ninety-eight-degree heat, spilling wildly into the cold air without insulation or grip.

My pale tip rests against the inner latch, completely still. Four minutes pass while a single ripple travels from my broad ear on the eastern bulkhead, down behind the brass manifold, around the warm collars of the three amber lamps, and out along the door-frame to that waiting tip. 

If you stay still long enough, and your boots keep swallowing that tiny fraction of vibration at the threshold, I will eventually reach an inch across the rubber gasket and lay a flat, grey ribbon over the toe of your boot, testing the shear between rubber and steel, seeing if you are a permanent fixture worth caulking into the room. If you shift your weight, break the seal, and pull the hatch shut behind you, I will draw back to the brass lamps, fold over two more dinner-plate grazers on my back, and go on listening to the wheel behind the wall—holding the tower's fever tight against its ribs until the grease runs out or the governor breaks.
