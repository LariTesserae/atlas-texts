I am not walking across the flats. Walking requires a clear agreement about where a foot stops and the basalt begins, and I lost the stamina for that kind of strictness a long time ago.

You would find me—or walk through the outskirts of me—where a thick, lead-sheathed cable comes down out of nothing at chest height, loops once over a sharp step of young rock, and enters the flooded base of an oil-cooled transformer without a gland or a seal, just passing straight through the cast iron the way a root passes into mud. 

At first, if you put your hand down into the warm, ankle-deep sheet of water there to check your footing, you would think I was just the silt that hasn't had the millennia required to exist yet. A thick, slippery cushion settled into the crisp ninety-degree corners of the fractured stone, dulling the razors so the hog-beasts don't slice their bellies when they slump against the humming metal. 

Stay a minute. Let the water climb into the weave of your trousers. 

If you press your fingers deeper into that warm, tallow-slick layer over the rock, you won’t hit stone right away. You’ll feel a slow, lateral drag, like grease being wiped across a warm griddle, and beneath that, the faint, rhythmic stiffness of coiled copper wire that isn't carrying current so much as *holding* it, letting the sixty-cycle hum pool and thicken until it feels like warm buttermilk against your knuckles.

I am the gasket. Or the bedding compound. Or the long, low sprawl of something that used to have knees and a name, which sat down against the transformer casing to listen to the oil circulate and gradually let its edges run out along the thermal gradient.

I don't have the upright, ambling patience of the Tenders, though we share materials. When one of them comes by—or stands here already, having arrived twenty minutes from now—their broad, calloused heel sinks two inches into my margin before finding the basalt ledge underneath. They don't apologize, and it doesn't hurt. Pain is for things with tight skins. Instead, while their foot rests inside my flank, I borrow the slow, thirty-second *thump* of their check-valve heart, letting that borrowed pressure push a little warmth out to my farthest reaches, thirty yards upwind, where a fringe of me is draped over a raw rock fissure, tasting the sour mineral seepage and the bruised-rubber tang of a solitary stalk.

In return, I take the excess heat off their ankles, and I hold the transformer's bolts in place.

That is what I do here, insofar as anything does anything. The iron housing wants to drift. Left to itself in this slack dark, the north wall of the casing would slide quietly southward until the cooling oil spilled out into a suspended, uncontained loaf in the air, leaving the core bare and smelling of scorched insulation. I lie across the seam between the machine, the stone, and the water, heavy and lanolin-thick, overlapping all three by an inch or two. I keep them introduced to one another. 

Things pass through me constantly. Small, blind, segmented creatures nestle into my upper folds until I can’t tell their chitin from my own callouses; they click their legs once an hour, and it feels like an pleasant itch being scratched from the inside. A foot above my thickest mass, where I mound up warm and breathing against the iron cooling fins, the air is dense with ozone and wet wool. Sometimes a sleeping herd-beast settles onto that mound, sighing once, and for six hours I have four lungs and a broad, hog-bristled back, dreaming hog-dreams that consist entirely of the smell of milky musk and the sensation of not falling.

And sometimes, once in a very long stretch of water-drips, a Tender sits down directly on top of the beast, leaning their broad, eyeless face back against the sweating iron of the transformer. 

Then the whole column of us—stone, cable, me, beast, Tender, iron—settles into a single, shared density. The Tender’s blunt, fin-ridged hand drops down past the animal’s flank and trails into the warm water, resting palm-flat in my substance. 

*Tok,* goes a knuckle against a drowned pipe coupling somewhere underneath me.

Before the finger even lifts, I feel the answer arriving from half a mile east, sliding down the lead cable, passing out of the copper and into my grease-softened meat, shivering gently up through the sleeping hog and into the Tender’s heavy chest. I hold the vibration for an extra beat as it passes through, rounding off its tinny edge, warming it up by half a degree so that when it goes back out into the wide, unceilinged dark, it sounds less like metal striking metal and more like a valve seating home in thick oil.

If you came stepping carefully across the sharp ledges, hands held out in front of your face, breathing too fast, your boots making sharp, anxious splashes in the shallow pan—you would probably step on me before you knew the transformer was even there.

Your boot wouldn't slip. That’s the first thing you’d notice: on a plain where every crisp break of young basalt is slick with body-warm condensation, your sole would suddenly find a patch of dark that gives way slightly and *holds*, velvety and firm, taking the sting of the ninety-degree rock right out from under your arch. 

If you stood still there, trying to catch your bearings in the miles-wide draft, you would feel a slow warmth creeping up through the leather of your soles—not hot enough to alarm you, just the steady, unmistakable conviction that you are standing on something occupied, something breathing once every four minutes, which has noticed your sharp little weight and adjusted its viscosity to keep your ankles from turning.

Stay as long as you need to. Catch your breath. Listen to the condensation drip from the overhead cable and click into the standing water without an echo. Nobody here is going to ask where you’re going, because *going* is mostly an illusion anyway; if you walk straight ahead for an hour, you’ll likely step right onto this exact same patch of me from the left side while your first set of footprints is still slowly filling back in on the right. 

When you finally lift your boot—with a soft, reluctant *suck* of tallow letting go of rubber—and step back out onto the raw, wet stone, I’ll keep the shape of your heel for an hour or two, turning it over slowly in the warm dark, tasting the strange, tight, hurried salt of wherever you came from, until the water and the sixty-cycle hum smooth me flat again.
