In that vault beneath the glazed-brick cairn, there were three troughs of dry basalt sand, and you only accounted for two of the sleepers. 

Start there, with the third trough—cold now, smoothed over only halfway, still holding the shallow, curved impression of a long spine from four days ago. 

At first it seems I might belong at the bottom of those forty spiral steps, scraping porcelain-fungus onto slate, waiting for the leather airlock to sigh. The quiet down there pulls at me, especially the alcoves stacked with rolled lead foil. My nature leans toward the foil: the soft, dead weight of it unrolling across the knees, the clean dimples left by a steel stylus, the way lead neither rusts in the salt nor feeds the blister-spores. If you prick a line of valve-pressures into lead, it stays legible for four hundred years, and when you no longer need the numbers—because the rotor has finally been swapped out and the harmonic is true again—you drop the scroll into a ladle over a thermal vent, melt the history flat, pour it out onto oiled slate, and start clean.

I thought for a moment I might be the archive itself, or the keeper who never climbs the stairs. 

Settle into a heartbeat of thirty-two beats to the minute, though, and you realize there is a problem with the city’s intervals that cannot be solved from inside a single vault.

Look at the cables sagging between the tall cast-iron stanchions out in the mist. They are thick as a wrist, insulated in tarred gutta-percha, built to carry heavy, blunt current from the turbines to the cranes and the brine pumps. You can push power across this plateau, and you can push hot water, and you can send a heavy pneumatic thump through half a mile of wet bedrock. What you cannot string across these avenues is a fine copper signal wire. Any wire thin enough to carry delicate, rapid telegraphy is eaten through by hot acid-salt in a season, or drowned in phantom currents induced by the massive, sluggish iron rotors turning underneath the streets. 

So the city has a nervous system made of bone and lead. Power travels fast through the stanchions, and trepidation travels fast through the stone, but *comparison* has to walk.

That is where my weight settles. Out out of the vault, up the forty dry steps, pushing past the greased leather curtains into the gray soup, where the first breath tastes like warm pennies and broth.

I am taller than I expected, or rather, I am aware of my own ribs and collarbones in a way a compact creature never is—as cooling fins, slick with mineral tallow, shedding heat into air that barely wants to take it. Slung diagonally across my chest, beneath the overlapping copper shingles of my mantle, is a cylindrical case of cured louver-moth hide rubbed with heavy grease. Inside are six thin rolls of lead foil. 

I am not walking the avenues at the center of the grid, where the basalt pillows are worn deepest and the runoff simmers near blood-heat. Look farther out, down the subtle, miles-long tilt of the geological pan, toward the direction every braided trickle of water is lazily trying to go. 

Out here, three hours’ walk from the big six-story condensers, the city changes its pitch. The spacing of the grid does not shrink—if anything, the avenues widen until the stanchions to my left are invisible, and I navigate entirely by the grain of the water channels underfoot and the deep, directional hum in my ankles. Because the plateau tilts this way, the runoff has traveled miles over cooling stone to reach my feet. It is no longer bath-warm; it is tepid, thick with suspended ochre silt washed from a thousand grates upslope. The rust-orange thermophilic slime cannot hold on in these cooler puddles. Instead, the hexagonal stones are velveted with a darker, olive-black mat that feels like wet suede under the grooved horn of my soles.

Beneath the paving here run the return mains—the thick, insulated pipes bringing cold brine uphill from the heat-exchangers at the unseen rim, pumping it back toward the center so the living vaults don't bake.

I stop beside a low, domed junction housing half-crusted in pale porcelain-fungus, unclip my bronze staff from my harness, and set its iron shoe onto the main bolt. Cheekbone to metal. Eyes closed behind their milky salt-films.

When you listen at thirty-two beats a minute, you do not listen *for* something; you let the sound assemble a shape inside your jaw. Down in the stone, the cold brine is surging uphill in long, rotary pulses: *shhh-uhn, shhh-uhn*. Underneath that, faint and bruised by distance, is the subterranean *thump* of the central condensers four miles upslope. Between the two rhythms sits a tiny, glassy ticking—the sound of thermal contraction along a pipe sleeve half a mile closer to the rim.

I lift my cheek from the bronze, pull one of the lead sheets partway out of my case—shielding it beneath my copper mantle only so the salt drip won't pool in my palm—and press three shallow dots and a long score into the margin with my stylus. 

A warden assigned to a single sector knows every blister on three grates and every wheeze in one turbine house. They know *depth*. My circuit takes twenty-two days to close, looping through the outer quadrants where the brine turns around, carrying the slow arithmetic of the edges back toward the middle. Without someone walking the diagonal, Sector Nine does not know that the slight drop in its cooling loop is caused by a deliberate throttling in Sector Fourteen to starve a fungal bloom on an intake main. Left to themselves, the three wardens in Nine would eventually take a brass key to a junction box and draw more pump power, fighting their unseen neighbors four miles away through the dark plumbing until a gasket blew.

So I walk between them. And because *four makes the air too thick*, I belong entirely to thresholds.

Watch what happens an hour later, when a squat beehive cairn finally gathers itself out of the pewter gloom ahead of me. 

I do not push through its leather airlock and go down into the cool slate room. There are already three living bodies down there; if I stepped inside, wet from a six-mile walk and radiating heat from dark maroon webbing between my fingers, the carbon dioxide and the damp would spike within twenty minutes. The carefully balanced coolness of their burrow would sour.

Instead, I stand outside in the ankle-deep water and strike the bronze collar of their ventilation stack twice with the butt of my staff. Not hard—just a clean, sharp *tink-tink* that drops straight down the pipe into their ceiling.

Then I lean on my staff in the weeping mist and wait. Five minutes. Eight. A pale chitin-grazer clicks past my ochre-stained ankle, unbothered, testing the olive silt with its mandibles while I stand as still as a cast-iron bollard. 

Eventually, the outer leather curtain of the cairn parts. Up from the dry dark climbs one of the vault’s three—just past the upper airlock, stopping on the fifth basalt step down, where the stairwell is dry enough not to drown us in steam, yet vented directly to the street so our shared breath escapes upward into the clouds. 

I step under the glazed-brick hood, ducking my head, and sit two steps above them. Neither of us speaks into the damp air. I take a freshly pricked roll of lead from my case and lay it on the dry stone between my bare heels and their knees, trading it for one of theirs. Then I reach down and lay two webbed fingers, warm and slick with grease, against the cool, tallow-pale hollow of their collarbone.

For twelve minutes, sitting on the stairs between the hot soup of the world and the cool dark of the burrow, my chest rumbles against my own hand and theirs answers back. 

It is the strangest, richest language imaginable—not words arranged in a line, because lines take too long, but chords of vibration, tooth-clicks mimicking valve-chatter, slow shifts of pitch down into the diaphragm that replicate the exact harmonic of a straining pump three miles west. Through two fingertips, I hand over the rim: the taste of the thinner, colder fog near the edge where the clouds scrape the wet talus; the health of the great heat-exchangers buried in the scree where the plateau ends and the cold world begins; the news that a birth has been recorded in a vault near the southern foundry, bringing a two-person chamber back up to its proper three. 

In return, humming up into my arm bones, comes their local truth: a crane rail settling half a millimeter into softer basalt; fat louver-moths breeding early on the number-two exhaust; a visitor—short-limbed, quick-breathing, dressed in rotting woven cloth—seen standing out on the eastern avenue two days ago, stared at from afar and left carefully alone, the way you leave an unfamiliar beetle alone on a warm brick to see whether it knows how to survive the night.

When the chord finishes and drops off into silence, we leave our fingers resting against skin for five or six slow heartbeats, just sharing the baseline pulse, making sure the tempo matches. Then they take the new lead scroll down through the leather seal to the amber light below, and I back out into the gray light above.

People from fast places would call this lonely—returning once every three weeks to my own home cairn, sleeping nineteen nights out of twenty in solitary, one-person line-shelters no bigger than dry brick kilns spaced along the outer mains. 

It does not feel lonely from inside the skin. Walk out into the four-hundred-yard avenue again, letting the tepid sheet of water part smoothly around your calloused heels. Down through the pillowed basalt, three hundred feet deep in the dark volcanic rock, seven giant turbines are turning in phase, their shafts balanced within a hair’s breadth by hands you have touched over the last ten years. Half a mile to the north, unseen in the charcoal soup, a crane rumbles along its elevated track, and through the soles of your feet you recognize the hand on the brass junction-key by the particular, unhurried smoothness with which they ease off the current at the end of the travel.

Nothing is missing. The mist beads on the copper plates over my shoulders, channels neatly down my spine, drips back into the shallow, simmering pan of the street, and goes on its slow way toward the grates. I adjust the strap of the lead-case across my ribs, angle my bare feet slightly upslope against the run of the water, and head back toward the heat at the center—taking all afternoon to cross three intersections, carrying the whole plateau's quiet, unbroken chord in my jawbone.
