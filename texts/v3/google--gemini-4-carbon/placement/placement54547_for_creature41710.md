I would not be anywhere near the open air. Above the crust, the light is a blade; it pares everything back to baked ochre and salt-glaze, and anything that belongs to me would boil dry in a minute if it ever broke through into that pale blue glare. For me, the sky is a ceiling half an inch thick—tight, hot, mineral, and impenetrable, humming all afternoon with the dry tension of clay trying to pull apart and failing.

At first, if you went looking for me, you would probably find only an edge of me behind a western-facing step late in the day, where one mile-wide pan drops two feet to the next.

I would be pressed close behind the vertical face just as the four-o'clock heat soaks inward, turning that ninety-degree corner into a clean, warm wedge in the dark. Like the small ribbon-mantles that line the embankments, I cannot help pressing my sensitive upper skin into the tight-packed grain of the wall’s inner rind. From down here, you can feel where the great smoothing-board passed when the basin was shaped. Where the tool pressed hardest, the microscopic platelets of clay lie flat and overlapping like fish scales, denser than the loose, straw-laced mud an inch deeper in. Tracing it in the dark is like laying a palm against a long, unbroken brushstroke—one single sweep that ran for half a mile without a hitch, a tremble, or a seam where the maker stopped to rest. I could spend hours laid flat against that compressed arc, reading its certainty while the afternoon heat pools and slowly thins away.

Only I wouldn’t end three feet down the wall like the basking ribbons do. If you followed my pale, gelid margin northward along the step, you would find where I peel away from the trowel-grain altogether and gather around something rough, vertical, and fibrous.

Above the crust, right at the sharp corner of the earthen dike, an old gourd vine has anchored itself. Its broad leaves further out are still tilting edgewise against the afternoon glare, advancing across the brickwork inch by inch, but here near the center of the patch sit three mature gourds from an earlier rain—swollen big as kettle-drums, bleached bone-white, cured hard as seasoned timber, and completely hollow save for the loose rattle of detached seeds inside. Their woody main stem plunges straight through the baked rind into the cool umber below, splitting into a crown of pale, tough roots.

That root-crown is where my upper body lives. 

I wrap around the damp wood in thin, pleated cuffs of clear amber gel, drinking the faint, sour-sweet mucilage the deeper rootlets sweat into the silt, paying it back by holding a thimbleful of cool water pressed against their bark when the pan above is dry as kiln-ware. But mostly I hold the stem because the hollow gourds sitting on the terrace crust are never quiet.

Resting tight against fifty square miles of taut clay drumhead, those cured shells catch everything. When a thermal tears loose from a damp flat three miles east, the sudden drop in air pressure makes the thin wood sigh—a low, almost imperceptible flexure that travels straight down the fibrous vascular bundles into my grip. When dry dust skates across a courtyard and hisses against a step, I feel a faint, papery buzz in the sapwood. When a five-mile row of mud bricks shrinks in the noon glare, the *snap* of opening hairline joints arrives through the ground-crust and rings the hollow spheres like thumbnails flicked against dry porcelain. And when a cloud reaches saturation twenty-five miles away and drops its grey pillar of warm water onto the plains, the gourds begin to hum long before the smell of steam reaches us—a deep, continuous, loose-skinned thrum running down into the dark.

The broad slip-mantles grazing nearby hear that hum too; they touch a passing lobe to the root-zone, take the news, and begin shivering their leading edges—*tick, tick, tick*—liquefying a path toward the lowest drainage trough to wait for the runoff. They belong to the horizontal world. Everything they do happens in the top two feet of mud, chasing shadows and puddles across the flats.

I don't go with them. If you followed me downward from the gourd-root, you would see what I actually am.

Beneath the root-collar, my body narrows to the width of an oar-blade, then a strap, then a single, continuous plumb-line of dense, kaolin-ballasted gel dropping straight down into the earth. Ten feet. Thirty feet. Seventy feet. Down through the warm upper mud where the chopped reed-stalks still smell faintly of marsh sun; down past the quiet crossroads where court-wide slip-mantles glide smoothly *through* my narrow column without breaking it, parting and sealing my edges as easily as oil passing through oil; down into the cold, heavy, black-umber dark where afternoon heat has never reached, where the packed clay smells of iron and trapped ancient rain, and the weight of the continent presses inward from every side.

To keep from being crushed solid into the thixotropic earth, my whole seventy-foot length maintains a barely audible, continuous tremor—so soft it doesn't liquefy the mud around me into cream, only keeps a hair-thin film of slickness between my skin and the packed straw, like a greased wire resting in cheese. 

At the very bottom, eighty feet beneath the gourd vine, my lower hem flares wide again and lies flat against the cold, mercury-heavy dome of a *level*.

Down there, time is thick and nearly motionless. The great hydrostatic lens beneath me is four miles across; it knows gravity, tilt, and shear-stress, and almost nothing else. Left to itself, it only learns about a localized rainstorm after hundreds of thousands of tons of water have already struck the pans overhead and driven a spike of raw weight down through eighty feet of mud—by which time it has to heave its massive bulk slowly sideways to keep the crisp ninety-degree corners above from shearing off.

I am the nerve between the drumhead and the counterweight.

When the hollow gourds up in the blinding light begin to hum with a collapsing cloud twenty miles up-terrace, I don't keep the vibration to myself. I let that quick, woody surface-tremor travel all the way down my taut, plumb-hung body, stepping its frantic pitch down as it descends through the cold clay until, eighty feet deep, my flared foot taps a slow, steady, unmistakable rhythm into the level’s northern flank: *weight coming, weight coming, from the west-northwest.*

Nothing dramatic happens. There is no sudden heave. Only, down in the iron-smelling dark, twenty minutes before the grey curtain of rain ever reaches our pans, I feel the immense, silent lens beneath me begin to lean—bleeding interstitial water eastward through a billion microscopic pores, drawing down the pressure under our terraces ahead of time, making room for a skyful of water before the first drop lands.

And when the rain finally drags across our own basin—turning the blinding ochre ceiling above me dark as wet slate, drumming directly onto the hollow gourds until the root-stem shudders in my grasp—I let my upper fringe rise two inches through the softened silt right beside the vine. Just for those twenty minutes, I open a neat ring of pinhead chimneys into the warm puddle above. Stale gas from eighty feet down fizzes softly out of me into the standing water; fresh, rain-cooled oxygen washes down through my skin. 

For that brief span, stretched taut through the dark earth, I get to feel the whole house working at once: warm water fizzing across my lips at the surface, cool mud drinking around my middle, and eighty feet below my hem, four miles of living mercury shifting a quarter of an inch to catch the storm cleanly—so that when the steam clears and the ferocious sun comes back out, the sharp corner of the step above me hasn't sagged by the thickness of a hair. Then I sweep a smear of fine colloidal clay across my breathing-pores, seal the roof shut from underneath, and go back to listening to the gourds tick in the heat.
