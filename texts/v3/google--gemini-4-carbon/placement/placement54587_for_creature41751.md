I am not in the scalding plazas, and I am not one of the pale, heavy proprietors Braided together in their stewing rooms. Their work is too deliberate for me, their skin too bitter, their fate—to harden slowly into the very basements they tend—too heavy a commitment to a single foundation.

You would find me four stories up, in a narrow, vertical canyon where two tilted apartment blocks lean so close their upper cornices nearly kiss. Here, the sulfur-heavy plumes rising from the cellar grates have lost their burn, cooling into a steady, blood-warm draft that smells faintly of iron and old silt. 

At first, if a tallow-mat drifted past and cast its weak, tallow-colored light against the masonry, you would think I was only a flaw in the mortar-less stone—a dark, velvety seam wedged deep into an eroded joint where the wall meets the ceiling of an open loggia. I have no shell to click shut when the cold comes down from the rim, and no bones to leave behind for the masons to dissolve into paste. 

I am a ribbon of muscle no thicker than two fingers, flat and supple as soaked leather, perhaps the length of a forearm when I choose to unspool. My skin is the exact dark, rusted plum of the heat-loving sponges, mottled with pale, silty flecks that mimic the drifting snow.

For hours at a time, I am simply an architectural detail. I anchor my tail-end deep inside a crooked, porous pocket of dead bryozoan skeleton—a crevice too narrow for a mason’s blunt cowl to probe, too tight for an eel to knot inside—and let the rest of my body hang slack out of the wall, swaying in unison with the rows of jawless ambush-feeders on the ledge below. 

My world is entirely vibration and taste. I have no eyes, only a continuous, sensitive ridge running the length of my flanks that reads the pressure-map of the canyon. 

I feel the city through my belly and my sides. When a yard-long catfish glides through the oval window beneath me, I feel the plush, heavy cushion of water pushed ahead of its blunt snout long before its trailing barbels brush the sill. When an archway three blocks away settles a fraction of an inch into the riverbed, the low, resonant groan travels up through the fused limestone and hums directly through my anchored tail. 

I am a gleaner of the margins, a draft-rider, an inspector of fresh plaster.

Wait until a mason has finished patching a cracked balustrade across the alley and glided heavily away, leaving a slick, chalky ribbon of new slurry hardening in the warm water. Before the paste sets into living rock, it is rich with binding proteins and stray scraps of river-clay organisms. As soon as the bitter, ammonia-sharp wake of the giant fades, I unhook myself from my crevice. 

I do not glide on stone; I swim like a slow, silent brushstroke, throwing flat, sinusoidal waves down my length that push me effortlessly across the canyon without displacing enough water to alert the blind feeders below. I settle onto the edges of the wet patch—never the center, never marring the smooth soapstone curve enough to draw the mason back to re-work it—and skim the rich, mucous film from the margins with a soft, toothless mouth, tasting the deep-earth sulfur and dissolved shell-grit of the city's newest bone.

Mostly, though, I hunt the traffic of the walls. The thumb-sized crustaceans that click across the stucco have thick shells, too hard for me, except right after they molt. In this perpetually warm, mineral-thick broth, someone is always shedding. I drift along the vertical faces of the towers, an inch off the stone, tasting the micro-eddies leaking out of alcoves and window-vents. When I catch the faint, sweet copper-scent of a soft, newly peeled crab hiding in a porous pit to wait for its armor to calcify, I pour myself into the hole like dark oil. A quick, enveloping wrap, a quiet squeeze in the pitch black, and I retreat back into the rising plume, leaving an empty, paper-thin shell to tumble slowly down into the mud-choked ground floors.

I know the cold fronts better than almost anyone, because I occasionally dare the heights.

Sometimes, when the mid-level canyons grow too stagnant or crowded with the slow lanes of rays, I ride a thermal chimney all the way up past the tiered courtyards, past the last of the sponges, to the pointed, eroding tips of the highest towers. Up there, the city ends. The water thins, losing its comforting, silty weight, turning cool and terrifyingly empty. Above me presses that bruised, impenetrable slate—not light, not really, just a vast, pale heaviness that makes the skin along my back prickle with an ancient, nameless unease. 

I twine myself around one of the waxy, leafless creepers that cling to the upper cornices, wrapping tight against the cool current, just to feel the sheer scale of the thirty fathoms pressing down. From up on the roof-peaks, you can feel the downdrafts the moment they spill over the unseen rim of the basin. The temperature drops; the water sharpens, carrying a clean, dead taste of high rock and nothingness.

I don't clamp shut. I let go of the creeper and drop like a shed ribbon, diving straight down the vertical shafts ahead of the rolling fog of cold. 

As I fall back into the dark, I hear the wave of a million tiny shells clicking shut behind me, a dry, cascading rattle chasing my tail down through the neighborhoods. I plunge back into the thick, murky broth of the avenues just as the rays sink toward the cobbles and the eels pull back into the joints. 

And instead of hiding in my crevice right away, I go all the way down to the central plazas. I slip low over the emptied cobbles, dodging the slow, rising skeins of the masons emerging from the grates, until I reach one of the immense, humming pillars at the heart of the town. I press my flat, cold-stiffened body into a smooth, fist-sized pore on the flank of the chimney, clinging fast to the lip.

Down beneath me in the foundation pier, something the size of an overturned skiff—half flesh, half limestone—registers the chill I just outran. I feel the tremor in the rock as the ancient giant gathers itself. And when that massive, deliberate contraction comes, forcing a great, hydraulic sigh of hoarded, scalding water roaring out through the pores, I let the edge of the blast catch me—tumbling me head over tail back up into the dark, thoroughly warmed, riding the town's breath back toward my fourth-story wall as the million little doors click open all around me.
