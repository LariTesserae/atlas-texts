Before there is a body, there is the wake. 

Ten paces upstream, a boulder sits wedged into a glacial groove, and the current splitting around its flanks does not come back together cleanly. It peels off in alternating curls of shear—left, right, left, right—a silent, spiraling stutter in the heavy water that travels downstream like a trailed rope. At first it seems enough just to be the place where that stutter arrives: a stationary threshold in the pitch dark where the whole drowned basin reports itself. From the east, three miles across polished gneiss, the organ-pipe rim of an old grove clatters in steady clusters of five and six as an eddy works its flank. From high up the stepped granite wall behind, a canyon cable drones a thick, steady low note, telling anyone with skin to feel it that the pass is pouring hard today, swollen from the core. Farther off, deep in the floor, an upwelling throat rattles its everlasting column of gravel against stone—a dry, bright hiss underneath all the bass.

If I could be anything here without choosing, I might almost have been the gorge-cable itself: taut black fiber wedged into rock with cold copper, humming day and night with whatever force passes through it, turning raw volume into a single legible note until friction takes it apart. 

Almost. Except a cable cannot move ten paces sideways to hear how the chord changes when a ridge steps in the way.

So there is weight after all. Three hundred pounds of it, pressed flat-backed and low against the slate so the current pushes down across the wedge of my skull and shoulders rather than getting underneath my chest. My big toes are jammed into a hairline frost-crack that opened before this mountain ever drowned; my crampon nails hook the upstream lip of the seam. Every ten or twelve heartbeats, the siphons behind my jaw open, pull the frigid, gin-clear water across stiff branchial plates, and vent it down the trough of my spine—*huff-thrum*—two brief ribbons of faint warmth swept instantly away behind my heels.

I am out on the open flat between basins, half a mile from the nearest windbreak of kelp-trunks, kneeling beside the seventh marker stone on the north saddle path.

Most of the others prefer the lee of the groves or the close, dead-water dark of the beehive huts. It makes sense that they do. In the groves there is work that feeds you—the pale blue sap welling up thick and peppery under the flint adze, the iron-gritty mats of crimson moss peeling up in heavy rolls—and in the huts there is the slow, shared heat of three or four bodies raising trapped water from near-freezing to merely cold. I go into the huts too, when my joints stiffen after four days in the scour or when my harness bladders are full of spore-curd that needs stowing. I unlash my belt, pull the doorway slab shut until the roar of the valley drops to a muffled murmur in the wall-stones, and press my chin to a cousin’s thick collarbone to trade news.

Still, after a few days in that warm, stagnant pocket, my pressure-pits begin to itch with silence. Inside a hut, all you feel is your own breath-jets swirling back off fitted slate and the sluggish shift of another knee against yours. Inside a grove, the noise is too close and too crowded—every rubbery trunk booming and groaning with internal sap-pressure, calcified stalks banging together ten feet overhead, defensive tendrils snapping at stray drift. It is like trying to listen to a voice with your head inside a gravel chute.

Out here on the bare gneiss, the dark is wide open and clean. Nothing floats in the water to blur a shear-line. Nothing softens an echo. Crouched low in the five-foot boundary layer where stone slows the river down, holding one broad hand up into the flow with fingers splayed wide, I can read four miles of highland at once.

Right now, I am reading a stone that has gone quiet.

I slide forward on knees and knuckles, letting the drag skate over my blubber-mantle, until my forearm brushes the seventh boulder. Centuries ago—maybe eight, maybe ten, before the big grove in the lower cirque split into two islands—someone hauled this block of dark banded gneiss upright into a socket and chipped three deep V-shaped notches down its upstream arris. A clean notch cuts a sharp, whistling vortex into passing water; ten paces downstream in pitch blackness, you catch that crisp flutter along your jaw-pits and know at once that you are on the saddle path and not drifting three degrees east toward the smooth bedrock troughs that shoot out over the mountain's rim.

Water here carries no silt, or almost none, yet over hundreds of seasons the rare pebble torn loose from an upwelling vent, or a shattered husk of calcified kelp-stalk tumbling across the flats, knocks microscopic grains off the notch. Pure hydraulic friction polishes the rest. Feeling down the boulder’s upstream edge with my thumb-pad, I find the three cuts worn round as an old tooth. Ten paces back, their wake barely whispered; in a heavy surge, a thirty-year-old making their first untethered crossing could walk right past it without feeling the tug on their cheek, miss the turn toward the lee ridge, and keep walking down-gradient until the granite grew too slick for horn-nails.

I unhook the banded maul from my kelp-rind apron and draw a heavy flint chisel—not a thin harvesting blade, which would snap at the first blow, but a stubby, fist-thick wedge flaked down from a nodule pried out of the east cliff. Bracing one shoulder hard into the downstream face of the marker so the recoil won't slide me backward across the polished slate, I set the flint into the upper notch by touch.

*Click. Click-clack.*

Water steals half the swing of an arm unless you move with a slow, gathering heave from the hips and back, letting mass do what speed cannot. And water carries the strike everywhere. The instant flint bites gneiss, the sharp double-crack leaps outward across the basin at five thousand feet a second. Half a heartbeat later it comes back to me off the near talus slope; a full beat after that, flatter and wider, off the great cirque wall; and faint behind both, a scattered wooden shudder where the shockwave just brushed a grove canopy a mile down-current and set a hundred hollow stalks chiming out of rhythm.

I pause after three strikes, resting my knuckles on the bedrock, listening to my own blow map the valley back to me. 

Down toward the south gorge, the echo returns with a tiny, split raggedness it did not have two winters ago. Something big—a slab the size of three huts—has detached from the frost-stepped crag above the chute and settled onto the apron below, splitting the acoustic shadow in two. Nobody has walked that side of the ridge lately, or if they did, we haven't crossed paths in a shelter to hum it into bone. I store the shape of that split bounce in my throat, practicing the low click-and-slide that imitates it, feeling the vibration settle behind my breastbone so I will have it exact when I next press my jaw to someone’s shoulder.

That, I realize as I reset the chisel into the stone, is what I am here. Not the planter who crawls into barren fault-seeps wedging glowing blue capsules into cracks for grandchildren two centuries off, though my harness carries two sticky pods in a closed pouch just in case I cross an empty mineral leak. Not the grove-bleeder who knows every trunk’s firing rhythm by heart, though I have adze-calluses at the base of my thumb like anyone else. 

I keep the legibility of the dark.

I walk the long bare stretches between the oases—the notched paths, the cable anchorages, the shifting talus aprons—checking whether the world still sounds the way our maps say it sounds. It suits a slow, cold-blooded patience. Five blows, then still. Blows again, feeling a thumb-sized flake of gneiss spall cleanly off under the flint, leaving a fresh, keen step in the rock. Instantly, against the dimpled pores along my outer wrist, the water peeling off that new edge sharpens. It stops slurring and begins to sing—a tight, rapid, silent flutter that will trail thirty feet downstream without breaking up.

Half an hour it takes to crisp all three notches. My siphons are pumping deeper by the end, warm twin jets pulsing steadily down my spine, copper-dark blood moving a fraction faster inside four inches of stiff fat. Near the last strike, a seed-pod fires out of the distant grove upstream—a muffled, hydraulic *thump*—and arcs high across the current above me. I tilt my flat skull just enough for my small, deep-set eyes to catch it: a brief, cold-blue streak of luminescent mucilage shooting fifty feet overhead through gin-clear nothingness, casting one stark, wheeling shadow of my crouched bulk across the black gneiss before it strikes an outcrop two hundred yards off and smears into a stationary, pale-glowing comma. Far above even that, barely distinguishable from an ache behind the eyes, hangs the immense bruise of the ceiling where water tears itself white against mountain wind. I do not look at it long. Looking up makes the soles of the feet feel light, as if the granite might tip.

I stow the chisel and loop the maul back onto my hip-harness, then crawl ten paces downstream to test my work.

Holding up my left hand, palm angled slightly across the river, I wait. There it is: three distinct ribbons of tight, whistling turbulence, braided cleanly at chest-height for a crouch, unmistakable even over the deep drumbeat of the artesian vents and the far-off bass of the gorge cables. It will hold that edge for three hundred years before the water rounds it dull again. Someone not yet born—someone who will spend thirty years tethered to a waist-line before ever feeling this flat alone—will catch that flutter across their jaw in the blind dark and know where their feet are.

Down-current to the west, four miles over the low saddle, sits the three-slab shelter above the iron seep. By the time I lean into the flow and haul myself up the stepped granite, pulling from crack to crack on hooked nails, someone else may have crept in from the far groves to wait out the high surge. If the crawlway stone is drawn shut when I reach it, I will tap twice on the outer lintel with copper, squeeze my cold, heavy shoulders through the gap, and pull the slab tight behind me until the endless push of the mountain finally lets go. And when the trapped dark warms by two degrees around us, I will lean my chin against a slate-gray shoulder blade and hum them the new shape of the south crag, and the crisp pitch of the seventh stone, before we pass the curd around in the quiet.
