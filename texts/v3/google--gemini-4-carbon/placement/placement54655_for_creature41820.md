I am forty feet down in the third-oldest district, where the ochre mud-brick has been squeezed so hard for so long that it rings like fired iron when a claw strikes it.

Above me, the roar is a solid sky. Down here, it is a geography. 

If you have never had ears, you do not miss silence; you only know when a wall is hollow and when it is full. My lower jaw is wide and flat as a hearthstone, pressed flush against the cool, greasy polish of the floor. Through the dense ivory of my teeth and the fused plates of my skull, the city arrives in distinct, braided currents. High up—thin and fizzing in my nasal bones—is the dry, electric sizzle of the cicada-crust on the sunless towers. Beneath that, sitting right behind my eye sockets, is the steady, percussive *pat-pat-pat* of the browsers’ hooves out on the gravel plaza, scattering pebbles the size of plums. Deeper still, felt in the heavy girdle of my pelvis and the marrow of my ribs, is the slow, tectonic shifting of my neighbors. Two chambers east, someone is turning over. A quarter-mile north, someone is leaning into a buttress, grinding an itch against an angle of compressed guano, sending a low, pleasing shudder through the bedrock that makes the loose skin at my throat sway.

And right beneath my belly—tiny, crisp, almost ticklish—the builders are awake. *Tink. Scritch. Tink.* Blind mouths mixing dry dust with sour spit, cementing grain to grain. Patching a hairline fracture my grandmother made when she settled here two hundred years ago.

I am not the largest in this chamber, nor the oldest. My back is a good, broad dome, slick and slate-dark at the crest where low lintels have planed me smooth, though I still carry thick, crusty islands of silver moss in the deep troughs behind my shoulders. Every few days, when I pass through the upper vestibules, one of the little barking rodents drops down onto my neck to nip at the dry fruiting bodies of that moss. I let them. Their sharp little feet feel like dry rain on leather, and they pick the ticks from the creases I cannot reach against a pillar.

Right now, I am breathing out. 

It takes a long time. You do not throw breath away in the basin; you let it uncoil through the dark, baffling labyrinths of your snout, letting the cool bone spirals catch the faint dampness on the way out so you can swallow it back down on the next pull. My nostrils valve shut with a soft, leathery *thup*. 

I am hungry. Not the sharp, frantic hunger of the little hot-blooded things in the rafters that have to eat their weight every week or die, but a slow, salty emptiness at the back of my tongue. A sense that my reserves of fat—stored in the heavy, dragging root of my tail—have thinned by an ounce or two since the last time I saw the grey sky.

Waking is an agreement negotiated with gravity. 

First, I pull my chin up an inch. Immediately, the great, braided song of the city vanishes from my skull, leaving me suddenly deaf, floating in the muffled dark with only the dull thud of my own slow heart—*lub... ... ... lub*—for company. I slide my left foreleg under me, feeling the wide, horn-layered spade of my claws catch a groove worn into the floor by ten thousand predecessors. Heave. Pause. Slide the right leg. Heave.

My flank peels away from my neighbor’s flank with a dry, papery whisper. She does not wake, only lets out a long, whistle-thin sigh through one nostril and settles half an inch deeper into the hollow I’ve left behind.

The ascent takes what a swift would call a morning. I do not hurry; the tunnel was made to my measure, or rather, I have grown to the tunnel’s measure. It is a long, spiraling ramp of black-polished silt, smelling profoundly of old horn, dry musk, and the sharp, ammoniac tang of centuries of dove-droppings locked safely behind three feet of termite-plaster. Where the passage narrows at a turn, I do not steer around the corner; I lean my right shoulder into the inner curve and let my weight carry me through. *Grrrrk.* My bony skin-nodules score four pale, chalky lines into the dark glaze of the wall, shedding a tablespoon of fine powder onto the floor. By nightfall, the blind ones will come up through the pores in the wainscot, gather that dust, wet it, and put it back somewhere else. Nothing is lost. We just move the rooms around very slowly.

Halfway up, the heat changes—from the deep, subterranean warmth of crowded lungs to the cool, chalky draft of the upper tiers. The dark turns to charcoal, then to lead, then to the flat, shadowless pewter that means *outside*. 

Rodents scatter up into the ceiling crevices as my broad snout breaks the plane of the oval doorway—not terrified, just making room, barking sharp little two-note warnings right above my skull that I feel only as tiny puffs of warm air on my neck-folds. 

I stop on the threshold and drop my chin to the sill.

Ah. There it is. 

The whole bowl floods back into my jaw. Out here, closer to the source, the cicada-roar is huge, a shimmering, metallic ocean pressing down on the thick shelves of my brows. Through my third eyelids—filmed shut while my eyes adjust to the dry glare—the plaza is a smear of pale bone-white and muted sage. Slowly, I retract the membranes. 

Across the swept gravel, fifty yards away, rises a fluted mound shaped like melted tallow, draped from crown to mid-flank in grey-green curtains of strap-leaf. Three pale browsers stand near its base, their long ears swiveling independently like radar dishes in the din, chewing sideways with mesmerizing, clockwork steadiness. To my left, a windrow of parched, curled leaves—pushed aside by tails like mine since before the ceiling fell—hisses faintly in the draft stirred by a high, wheeling chimney of blind swifts.

I check the plaza with my chin one more time. Another heavy walker is out—somewhere behind the bloated, elephant-foot shrubs to the south. I can feel the rhythmic *scuff-thump, scuff-thump* of a dragging tail and four broad spades moving away toward the central avenues. No conflict. Plenty of wall for everyone.

I step out onto the cobbles. 

The gravel crunches beneath me, ancient river-stones shifting in their beds of dry silt, clicking against one another like knuckles. Behind me, my tail settles into its groove, sweeping a stray parchment-leaf effortlessly toward the mound’s base. 

Ten paces. Twenty. Stop to breathe. Pull the dry, chalk-tasting air deep into the dark sacks under my ribs; hold it while the world roars; let it go without stirring a grain of dust at my feet. 

When I reach the hanging garden on the tallow-mound, the browsers don't even stop chewing. One steps neatly sideways—*click-clack*—lifting its chin so its soft throat clears the rough, lichen-crusted ridge of my pelvis as I pass. I smell its dry, grassy breath; it smells my waxy, stone-dust hide. We have no quarrel. It eats the straps; I eat what clings to them.

Bracing my wide hind feet in the cobbles and locking the thick, muscular tripod of my tail against the ground, I rise. Blood shifts slowly backward into my hindquarters; the loose, heavy canvas of my chest-skin hangs straight down. I lean forward until my breastbone meets the cool, porous ochre of the wall—*thud*—sending a brief, polite knock down into the sleeping chambers of whoever owns this mound, letting them know it’s only someone browsing the facade.

With my left foreclaw, I hook a thick, tough braid of air-plant and draw it gently down against my chest, pinning it there without snapping a single slow-grown fiber.

The cicadas are packed shoulder to armored shoulder in the clefts behind the leaves, big as my own blunt toes, dusted with the same white powder as everything else. Their tymbals blur, drumming so furiously that when my dark, leathery tongue unrolls and touches the first three, it feels like licking a live wire—a sharp, buzzing tingle right on the wet tip before I curl them inward.

*Crack.*

My flat back teeth meet, sideways and heavy. The brittle shells give way all at once, yielding a sudden, rich gush of cool hemolymph and thick, nutty yellow fat. Moisture. Real, wet water, stolen by deep shrub-roots from hidden cracks fifty feet below the cobbles, sucked up by the nymphs, carried into the light, and delivered straight onto my tongue.

I close my filmed eyes, leaning my broad chest into the tower, letting the vibration of ten million surviving insects buzz straight through my ribs while I methodically clear a square foot of stone. *Crack. Grind. Swallow.* A dry flake of chitin falls from the corner of my beak-muzzle, tumbling down the folds of my throat until it catches in a patch of grey moss on my collarbone, where a tiny, pale mite is already waiting to eat it.

Later—maybe in an hour, maybe in three, when the grey light hasn't changed at all—I will drop back down to all fours. There is a seam of dark, salty guano-stone exposed near the corner of this warren that smells pleasantly of old iron and nitrates; I will shave a ribbon of it off with one claw and clean my palate. 

And much later still, a hundred years from now or a hundred and fifty, when my knees have grown too thick with calcium to negotiate the spiral ramp, I won't come back out into the pewter glare at all. I will walk down past the barking rodents, past the warm, breathing boulders of my nieces and grandsons, all the way back into a narrow, quiet cul-de-sac at the bottom of my home mound where the ceiling sags a little too low. I will wedge my broad, bone-studded back right up under that weak spot, drop my hearthstone jaw onto the floor one last time, and listen to the whole roaring city while the little blind mouths come up through the dust to mortar me in, holding up the roof for the ones still waking.
