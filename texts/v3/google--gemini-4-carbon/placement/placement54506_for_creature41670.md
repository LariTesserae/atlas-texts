I am not down on the wet basalt where the hooves click, and I am not up in the blinding sun where the bronze-ruffed scavengers dry their wings. I am in the four hundred feet between them, where the world is nothing but pewter air, moving water, and a braided black line thicker than a human wrist.

If you want to find me, you have to look straight up from the roof of Rig Four until your neck aches and your visor runs with mist, past the exhaust stack’s warm breath, into the stalled belly of the cloud. Or you have to start in the dry blue glare above the aerostat and follow the umbilical down until the sun vanishes as abruptly as a lamp blown out.

Right there, three hundred feet off the sharp floor, where the Kevlar sheath goes slick with supercooled fog and hums a steady, shivering F-sharp—that is my entire country.

At first, you might think I am the cable itself: the blind, continuous tension of it, pulling twenty-five tons of sky upward against fifty tons of earth, conducting three thousand volts of upper-air wind down into the dark diabase while carrying optical pulses of mineral data back up toward the satellite relay on the blimp’s spine. There is a strong pull toward being that wire. It is the only continuous sentence in a landscape made of broken planes and separate pools; it translates weather into torque and depth into light.

Wait a moment longer in the drizzle, though, and the shape comes slightly loose from the weave.

I have six legs—no, not the brittle, jointed sticks of the stoneflies down in the wheel-wakes, which would snap in the forty-knot gusts when the cloud-top shears—something Squat and radially braced, closer to a sea-spider or a crab built out of pale, translucent horn and dark gray felt. Call me a tether-louse, though there is no blood to drink here, and nobody has named me yet because when the technician lowers the aerostat once a year to inspect the winch-drum, I walk upward ahead of the descending wrap, staying comfortably inside the cloud-deck she never sees.

Before the rigs came, five or ten generations ago—or fifty; time moves oddly when you never freeze solid anymore—my kind lived only on the highest, cruelest pinnacles of the outer rim, clinging to the vertical seams of dry-iced granite where the valley winds slammed upward, waiting with hooked claws to snatch whatever frozen midges, ballooning spiderlings, or wind-tossed grass seeds the updrafts flung out of the green world two miles below. Most of us starved between gusts, or were sheared off into the talus when the rime-ice grew too heavy and calved away from the rock face.

Then these warm black stems grew straight up through the cloud.

You cannot imagine the luxury of a vertical river that never freezes. Inside the braided Kevlar armor, the three-phase power lines shed just enough resistive heat to keep the outer jacket four degrees above ambient. While the rest of the high air turns fog into murderous needles of rime, the cable merely sweats. A permanent, millimeter-deep sleeve of liquid water slides slowly, endlessly down the black cross-hatch of the weave, spiraling slightly clockwise with the lay of the braid.

I sit facing upward, straddling three strands at once, my hooked tarsi locked not into rock cracks—which don’t exist here—but into the microscopic valleys between synthetic fibers that do not fray or cut. My belly is flat and broad, fringed with stiff, inward-pointing bristles that touch the wet Kevlar like the brushes of a street-sweeper. Everything the upper shear catches on the aerostat’s vast, damp envelope—dead lowland aphids blown over the passes, grains of yellow pine pollen from forests a hundred miles west, microscopic soot, shedding bronze barbules from the vultures’ feathers, frozen gnats stunned by the turbine blades—washes down the handling lines, gathers at the gimballed nose-cone, and pours down the main umbilical right between my palps.

I don’t hunt. I filter the sky’s runoff.

My whole body is an instrument for reading two things that never stop arguing: the wind above and the rock below.

Because I am clamped directly to the tensioned sheath, there is no slack between my nerve-cord and the drill bit seven hundred feet down. When the core-rig is rolling across the stepped greywacke, the cable slackens and tightens in slow, rubbery swells as the pneumatic bladders climb two-foot ledges; I feel the topography of the unseen floor as a gentle, seasick *heave-and-sigh* through my six knees. When the rig halts and jacks its hydraulic legs level—*hiss, pause, hiss*—the line goes dead-plumb and hard as iron bar. Then the drill starts.

Down on the stone, the pale cats read that vibration through their chins to time a stalk. Up here in the white-out, I read it to hold onto my dinner. Soft slate gives the cable a greasy, purring chatter that shakes the descending water-film into fat, slow beads easy to comb out with my mouthparts. Hard basalt sends up a high, glassy ring that atomizes the runoff into mist if you don’t flatten your carapace hard against the weave to dam the flow. And when the bit hits a pocket of pressurized brine deep in the fault, there is a sudden, hollow *thump* through the Kevlar a full second before the white steam-plume down below warms the updraft around my back legs, smelling faintly of ancient ocean and hot copper.

Sometimes, without warning, the air tears open.

A dark, whistling wedge drops out of the ceiling three feet from my eyes—one of the nine-foot vultures riding the cable down like a fireman’s pole. It doesn’t touch the line, not quite, because at sixty miles an hour the Kevlar would burn through scaly toes, so it keeps one horned foot crooked loosely an inch off the weave as a blind-man’s guide in the zero-visibility soup. The vortex off its folded primaries slams into the tether, spinning me halfway around the braid into its leeward shadow, plastering me flat while a smell of sun-baked keratin, carrion, and high-altitude ozone washes through the cold wet air. Two seconds later, far below in the pewter dimness, comes the muffled *whump* of nine feet of wing catching the dense air above the rig’s roof. And drifting down in the wake of its dive come three or four tiny, pale feather-mites jarred loose by the braking—rich, fatty windfalls tumbling straight into the bristles of my front legs.

And the mouse-gray wrens! They are the only thing I truly dread, coming *up* the line on the warm exhaust column, hopping sideways up the steep spiral of the cable like tree-creepers on an endless black pine trunk, bright black eyes inches from the weave, sharp little bills clicking as they glean. When I feel the faint, rhythmic *tick-tick* of tiny claws climbing the braid below me, I don’t run—nothing outruns a warm-blooded bird when your own blood is the temperature of meltwater. Instead, I slide around to the windward face where the cold cloud drives hardest, tuck my six legs completely beneath my flanged shell until my edges meet the diamond pattern of the Kevlar, and let the descending water sheet smoothly over my back. My shell is the exact matte charcoal of wet synthetic fiber, crossed with faint diagonal ridges that mimic the braid. The wren hops right past my flank, so close its wet breast-feathers brush my margin, peers upward into the murk where the air grows too thin and cold for its little furnace, cheeps once—a tiny, dry spark of sound immediately swallowed by forty miles of fog—and lets go, dropping back down toward the warm inverters and the steaming backs of the camelids.

There are times, late in the afternoon when the cloud thins to that strange, shadowless pearl light, when the mist parts for thirty seconds in a vertical shaft around the tether.

Then, without moving a claw, I see both halves of the world that do not know each other.

Straight up, the underside of the aerostat hangs like a silver-gray whale belly blocking out a patch of intolerably bright cobalt sky, its wind-turbines spinning silent blurs at its tail fins. Straight down, shrunk to toys by four hundred feet of thin air, the warehouse-sized rig sits square on the dark, glinting puzzle-pieces of the rock, ringed by three hundred slate-brown ovals steaming quietly in the cold, while tiny white-and-gray plovers stitch back and forth across the silver sheen of pan-pools. I can see the thermal wake behind the rollers because the mist refuses to settle on it for twenty yards, leaving a dark, wet ribbon across the pewter glare, though from up here you can see how temporary it is—already fading back into unmarked stone before the next rig, three miles east, shows only as another faint black thread rising into another bank of cloud.

I have never touched rock. If a gust tore me loose and flung me clear of the rig’s roof, I would land in one of those inch-deep iron-tasting pools among the flat amphipods, and the sharp greywacke would mean nothing to me except a floor I couldn’t grip properly because it has no weave, no steady sixty-hertz hum, no warm downward trickle of sky-soup. Probably a plover would have me in three seconds.

Yet when I molt—twice a summer, wedging my old claws deep into a tiny frayed burr of Kevlar so the wind won’t strip me naked mid-shed—the empty, translucent shell stays behind only until the next heavy drizzle loosens it. Then that hollow ghost of me slides down the whole four hundred feet of black umbilical, bumps softly over the steel winch-housing, washes off the hull in a warm condenser drip, splashes onto the broad, woolly rump of a sleeping camelid mother, gets shaken off in a musical *whuff* of silver spray, and lands unbroken in a pan-pool.

From there, riding the millimeter-thin overflow from ledge to sharp ledge, my cast-off skin travels alongside the maroon flatworms, the fresh grains of unweathered feldspar, and the microscopic white curls of polyethylene shaved off the technician’s lonely boots—all of us drifting slowly, silently toward the lowest corner of the tilted basin, gathering in the cold clear water to wait a hundred thousand years for the lake to begin.
