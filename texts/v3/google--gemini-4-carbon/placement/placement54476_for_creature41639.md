The heat leaves the stone before it leaves the water, and that is how you know where the edge is without opening your eyes. 

Actually, there are no eyelids to open—only a clear, tough scale fused over each socket, scratched fine at the lower rim from decades of quartz grit, polished clean again every few summers when the old skin splits down the spine and peels away in grey, papery ribbons among the sedges. Sight is mostly an inconvenience between mid-morning and four o'clock anyway. The world goes white-hot and flat, a double furnace of sky and pan, and anything taller than six inches spends its life squinting or blistering. Better to stay two inches thick and let the light skid right over you.

Right now, at midday, the gneiss under my belly is warm enough to keep the gut working without effort, while my tail—three feet of heavy, banded muscle flattened sideways like an oar, though there is nowhere deep enough to row it—lies trailed back in four inches of pan-water that feels like fresh blood. That is the whole trick of living on the basement: straddling the seam. Half on the whaleback, half in the bath, letting the blood shuttle heat from whichever side has too much to whichever side has less. 

Through the flat bones of my lower jaw, pressed flush against the dry rock, the basin talks. 

The air-noise—the endless, tinny rattle of the little crack-frogs, the dry-grass saw of cicadas in a fault-tree four hundred yards north—is just weather. It never stops, so after your first week out of the egg you cease to hear it, the way you cease to feel the weight of your own ribs. What matters comes up through the stone. Banded gneiss is dense, ancient, welded tight from the roots of dead mountains; it rings like an anvil if you know how to listen with your chin.

*Shhhk. Pause. Shhhk.*

Eighty yards east, one of the heavy grey bellies is working a pan. I can feel the low, grinding purr of its horn-lip planing purple mat off the bedrock—*scritch, scritch*—and behind that, the slow, careful placement of four wide pads. Not a calf. Too deliberate, too much pause between the lift and the set, the rhythm of an old cow whose hips remember a slip ten rains ago. Even if I lifted my head an inch—which costs water through the throat-lining—all I would see against the glare is a dark, shimmering blockage with two white-legged stilts stepping neatly inside its belly-shade. Nothing for me there. Everything past its second rain is merely landscape until its teeth wear out.

Closer—twelve feet left, where a hairline fracture in the quartzite holds an inch of grey kaolin soup and a dozen red sundews—something tiny taps: *tik-tik-tik*. A pale crab, shell hard enough to turn a front tooth, scavenging a dead beetle stuck at the edge of the mucilage. Let it keep its pinchers. Calcium is good, when you can swallow it whole without cracking a jaw-plate, which is why I swallowed three smooth quartz pebbles the size of plums ten years back; they sit in the muscular sack of my stomach right now, rolling gently against each other whenever I shift my ribs, grinding crab-back and turtle-scute and lungfish skull into grey paste so nothing goes back to the pan that my own scutes can use. On this rock, you do not shit out bone. Nothing does. Even my dung, when I drop it in the warm shallows once a month, turns white as chalk and vanishes under a boiling knot of tadpoles and grazing snails before sunset.

The sun leans west. I know because the dark band of iron-stained rock six inches from my snout begins to sting through my chin-scales.

Time to become water.

I do not lift up onto my legs—walking high is for dusk, when a pool dries down to salt-crust and you have to haul sixty-five stone of armour half a mile down the polished grooves to the next saucer, elbows splayed wide enough to clear the stone, claws clicking slow and steady in the track the big grazers rubbed smooth before any of us hatched. Now, I only loosen my ribs, push once with my hind toes, and slide forward three feet. Not a splash. A ripple two millimeters high rolls out across the breathless sheet, bends over a low, pillowy dome of ochre slime, and dies against the ankles of an ibis fifty yards off without making the bird look up.

Four inches of water closes over my back. The warm bath hisses faintly into the dry crevices between my scutes, floating off a pinch of white mineral crust left from yesterday's drying. Seven small frogs that had tucked themselves into the damp shadow behind my armpit scatter in three frantic hops, land on a stromatolite cushion, and go straight back to rattling like shaken gravel before their toes are settled.

I flatten my ribs outward—we are wider than anything our cousins in distant, muddy rivers ever needed to be, spread out like dropped batter so our bellies don't scrape raw in shin-deep pans—and let the lung-air leak silently out of my nose-valves until my weight settles onto the velvet mat. The algae squishes warm and slick beneath my plastron. Oxygen bubbles, tiny as pinheads, detach from the bruised purple film and tickle up along my flanks to pop in the blinding air.

Only two bumps of dark horn—eyes and nostrils—stay dry. Looking sideways right along the skin of the mirror, the world has no depth at all, only dazzling horizontal bands: silver water, heat-rippled blue sky, a kiln-baked termite spire half a mile south wavering like a mirage finger, and the dark, flat-topped crown of the solitary tree floating detached above its own reflection.

Now there is nothing to do for six hours except let the sun cook yesterday's lungfish into tallow at the base of my tail.

People who run on tall legs—the silver night-dogs with their radar ears, the clattering storks—must spend half their lives frantic just to stay warm or get cool, eating every day or starving by the fourth. That seems an exhausting quarrel to pick with a flat world. If nothing moves near my snout between this rain and the next, my heart simply drops to four beats a minute, my blood thickens slow and sweet in the warm pan, and I wait out the dry weeks on three mouthfuls of crab and the fat in my tail-root. Boredom is a warm-blooded word. What I have is correspondence: the rock lies flat, the water lies flat, and I lie flat between them until something forgets which stone is older.

Afternoon thickens. The water around my flanks creeps past blood-heat, turning lazy and thin; little ghosts of steam curl off the dark scutes along my neck where they nearly break the surface. I open my jaws an inch underwater—letting the pale yellow flesh of my palate show, dumping heat into the pan while the throat-flap stays sealed tight behind my tongue. A cloud of tiny black tadpoles drifts right across my lower teeth, rasping blindly at a thread of green slime snagged on a canine. They tickle faintly. I let them clean it.

Then—through the water first, and an instant later through the bedrock under my chest—comes a different cadence.

*Plip-slosh. Plip-slosh. Pause.*

Lighter than the cow. Quicker. Four pads, still soft around the rims, not yet spread to their full dinner-plate width, testing the slick olive mat without knowing yet which patches hide loose grease-sand underneath.

Without turning my skull—any twist of a snout sends concentric rings shouting across calm water—my left eye rolls two degrees backward in its bony turret.

The old cow has moved thirty yards closer, her broad horn-lip planing a fresh strip through the ochre film, two white stilts still stitched to her black belly-shade. Behind her, ten paces out where the glare turns the water into hammered tin, stands this season's calf. Dusty-backed, squint-eyed, ears flicking at the cicada-roar, pausing every four steps to lick a dry white salt-ring off the crest of a low stone swell where the water shrank back last week.

Ten paces is not enough. Twelve is barely enough. If I lunge across open pan in five inches of water, the bow-wave gives me away at second one, and by second three the cow will pivot and drive eight hundred pounds of blunt four-toed pillar straight down onto the flat of my skull. My cousin half a mile west has a healed dent behind his right eye-ridge where an old bull taught him geometry twenty summers ago.

So I do nothing. Not *waiting-to-spring* nothing, which has tension in it that birds can read, just stone nothing. Even the throat-gape closes millimeter by millimeter until the yellow meat disappears and I am banded ironstone again, stained rust and olive, aligned grain-for-grain with the ancient strike of the gneiss beneath me.

Twenty minutes pass. Half an inch of water evaporates off the whaleback behind me. A stork clatters its bill on the fault-tree; a white feather drifts down and sticks flat to the surface forty yards away.

The cow follows a richer seam of purple mat toward a deeper saucer, angling slowly left, her breath puffing warm rings across the film. The calf stays at the salt-lick a minute longer, rasping its black tongue loud against the quartzite—*kshhh, kshhh*—greedy for the sharp tang of mineral on its palate. When it lifts its head, the glare from the west hits it square under the brow-shelf. In that blinding white flash, its mother is a dark smudge twenty-five yards off, and between them lies a shallow sheet broken by three low steps of dark, banded rock.

Two of those steps have been here for four hundred million years.

The calf sets out at a careful, slightly hurried trot to catch her shadow—*shhk, splash, shhk, splash*—cutting the corner across my pan. Eighteen yards. Twelve. Six. Through the jawbone, each footfall punches clean and distinct up out of the earth. I can see the crusted white water-line dried along its knees, the dark slit of its eye squinting against the bottom-light, a single speckled frog clinging tight behind its ear.

At four feet, my tail—already cocked in a gentle curve under the tepid sheet where glare hides the bottom—braces its keel against a two-inch lip of worn bedrock.

There is no thought between the stillness and the hit; they are two halves of one hinge. Water explodes white into the pale sky, louder than thunder across the flat shield; four short legs drive sideways clear of the stone, jaws clapping shut with a wet, hollow *boom* of horn-sheath and bone right over the narrow bridge of the calf’s ankle-joint, and then the whole flat weight of me rolls once—not to drown it, you cannot drown anything tall in six inches of bathwater, only to yank that slick-footed pillar sideways off the grease-sand so gravity and the bedrock do the breaking.

A heavy, sickening *thwack* as young shoulder meets solid gneiss. A bawling cry cut raggedly short as my second bite shifts up from shin to throat-latch and clamps down with three thousand pounds of sun-warmed hydraulic muscle, flattening my own body tight to the pan floor while white spray rains back down for thirty yards around us.

Now the ground shakes in earnest—*BOOM, BOOM, BOOM*—the cow charging back through shin-deep sheets that leap six feet high around her chest, stilts scattering skyward with hoarse shrieks. If this were dry land, I would have to let go. Instead, keeping my jaws locked like a rusted vise on the windpipe, I walk backward with short, piston strokes of all four legs, dragging the thrashing weight ten yards down the imperceptible slope into the deepest permanent saucer—fourteen inches of hot, churning soup clouded instant-crimson and boiling with panicked crabs.

The cow skids to a halt at the slick rim, broad pads sliding an inch on the bruised biofilm—*shhhhk*—catching herself before her hip goes out. She lowers her squared-off muzzle, snorts a blast of spray across my submerged scutes, and shoves once against my flank with the flat plane of her jaw. It feels like being nudged by a drifting boulder; I slide two feet across the quartz sand, smooth pebbles rattling faintly inside my gut, claws screeching against the stone, and do not open my mouth by a hair.

For ten minutes she stands over the pink water, heaving, flanks gleaming wet in the fierce bottom-light, stamping once so hard the shockwave sets ten thousand frogs silent for three full seconds before their metallic drone washes right back in to fill the hole. Nothing moves under the clouded twelve-inch mirror. Nothing breathes up. Gradually, the smell of blood overrides the habit of guarding, and the old shield-wisdom settles back into her heavy shoulders: a broken thing in pink water is already rock again. She turns, takes three deliberate paces up the quarter-inch groove worn by ten thousand mothers before her, and lowers her lip back into clean algae fifty yards away.

I do not eat yet. Tearing meat takes breath, and my blood is thick with sprint-acid that takes an hour of quiet soaking to clear. I simply keep my snout wedged over the warm carcass in the warm pan, eyes bumped just above the clearing film as the red silt settles and pale crabs begin marching in sideways from thirty yards downwind, their little claws already clicking hopeful rhythms on the stone.

Let them come. When they crowd thick enough along the ribs tonight, I will get meat and shell in the same mouthful.

By the time the sky goes copper and the whole flat world burns red from underneath, two neighbours have arrived—longer than me by a foot, scarred white along the tail-crests, drawn three-quarters of a mile by the single thunderclap of that first tail-slap carried through the basement stone. Nobody fights here if a twist will work instead. One clamps onto a hind quarter, braces four wide legs, and spins twice in a fountain of molten-gold spray; a leg comes free with a wet pop, and he drags it twenty yards away onto a dry whaleback to swallow it whole, hooves and all, tilting his flat snout toward the first pale stars while his throat bulges wide.

Darkness drops like a lid. The air cools fast, leaving the fourteen-inch saucer feeling richly, luxuriously hot around my ribs. Out on the stromatolite pillows, the big night-frogs begin their hollow wooden booming—*bung, bung, bung*—right beside my ear. Two hundred yards off, stars shatter briefly in a shallow pool where one of the long-legged silver runners sprints splashing after a roosting stilt, claws ticking faintly on dry stone as it misses and trots on into the dark.

Full to the throat-valve—calf rib, three soft-shelled crabs scooped in the dark, and one fresh white pebble of pure, grease-smooth quartz swallowed for good measure to help grind the ankle-knuckle down over the next three weeks—I haul myself slow and dripping out of the saucer just before midnight, following the polished quarter-inch groove only far enough to rest my chin back on dry stone while my heavy belly stays warm in the starry bath.

Three miles away, out in another dark pan, a bull grazer lets out a long, wet sigh across the mirror. Under my jaw, the worn roots of the ancient mountain carry the faintest hum of his shifting weight, hold it for a second, and go still again beneath the singing of the frogs.
