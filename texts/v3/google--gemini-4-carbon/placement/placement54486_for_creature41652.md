You feel me first through the greasy, inch-thick callous of my right heel, though if you are looking across the two-hundred-yard gulf between the Tannery Grid and the East Sluice, you probably think I am a tear in a curtain of chartreuse bladder-vine that has caught a stray violet reflection and refused to fall.

I am hanging upside down by one knee-crook and a braided non-baryonic tether, forty feet beneath the primary walking-membrane of the Sluice, where the underside of the street looks like oiled soap-film seen from underwater. Above the film, blurred silhouettes of wide grey feet pass back and forth—*thrum-pad, slide, thrum-pad*—each step pressing a momentary dimple of white refraction into the ceiling over my head before the standing wave snaps it flat again. Down here, there is no floor at all until the basalt knives three hundred and eighty feet below, only seven drifting tiers of wet emerald mist-fern that obscure the black stone like green smoke suspended in warm tallow.

My jaw is unhinged half an inch. My chin rests lightly against a hairline corner-strut—a thread of dark, cold geometry no thicker than an eyelash, dropping plumb from the violet node overhead—so that the bone of my mandible can drink what the district is doing.

Through my teeth, the East Sluice tastes sour. 

There is a flat, rattling third buried inside the big F-sharp turbine-chord rising out of the fissure beneath Block Nine. An outsider wouldn’t know it from the rest of the roar—to raw meat, all of this is just an iron foundry falling down a staircase forever—but through the gristle-lattice of my ribs, it registers as an itch behind my breastbone, a tiny rhythmic stutter every eleven beats where two containment shears are failing to kiss cleanly. Somewhere six hundred yards down-wave, toward the fringe pylons, something heavy has died on a primary wire, or a colony of wild hydrogen-gourds has swollen fat enough in the damp to bend a phase-boundary three degrees out of true. If nobodybleeds the weight off before the evening harmonic steps up, a forty-foot square of residential floor over by the gut-weavers’ hammocks is going to go soft as rotten melon, and somebody’s grandmother will step out of bed with a mist-hoop and go straight through four canopies without making a sound louder than tearing wet silk.

I am not a Line-Tender anymore. You can tell by my eyes—the rims are milky grey as boiled fish-glue, which means my retinas already tore loose once down in the heavy roar at two hundred feet and had to be tacked back by an old biologic midwife using thorn-splinters and ultrasonic cautery. Drop me into the deep halls now, where a body blurs three inches wide, and I’d go blind before my tether ran taut. 

Instead, I keep the shallow undersides. They call us *Chin-Tuners*, or sometimes *Spit-Levels*, when the young grease-slicked cutters want to be ugly about it with fast blue flickers along their ribs. We are the ones who live ten feet below everybody else’s heels, crawling the ceiling-angles like the whip-tailed plaza-hunters, reading the sag of the city through our teeth because we are too scarred to dive and too restless to sit aloft weaving moss-kilts.

A ten-second squall hits the Sluice.

You feel the turbines down in the cracked earth shift pitch first—my flexible sternum caves inward half an inch, ripples like water over a stone, and lets the bass spike pass clean through my lungs and out between my shoulder blades without bruising a thing. Instantly, the warm air around my hanging shins locks up. Millions of beads of clear sweat materialize in stiff, trembling three-dimensional rows an inch apart, turning the empty void beneath me into a vast, sparkling crystal block of suspended water. For eight heartbeats, the violet pinpricks half a mile away split into a hundred thousand tiny purple prisms. 

I don’t bother reaching for a hair-hoop. I just open my mouth wider, let my tongue hang out toward the nearest lattice-line, and turn my neck slowly left to right, licking thirty beads of warm, completely tasteless sky-water out of their invisible cages before the chord drops a semitone and the whole shimmering block vanishes back into thick breath.

Across the chasm, sixty yards out in open air, a flash of dirty amber catches the corner of my milky vision.

A kid—fifteen, maybe, all long pelican-shins and loose ankles, carrying two bloated gut-sacks of salt-slurry slung wide on a shoulder-pole—has stopped at the un-railed margin of the Tannery span. He’s new from the inner tiers; his arm-bacteria are pruned into neat, fussy little civic squares of pale cyan instead of the broad, sloppy bands we wear out toward the seam. He doesn't trust the bridge-shear ahead of him. I watch him hawk up a glob of phlegm and flick it off his thumb into the empty twilight between us.

It hits the invisible span six feet out, flattens into a bright silver wafer the size of a knuckle, skitters three yards sideways with a faint *zzzzip* of atomizing edges—and then, right over the sour eleven-beat stutter I’ve been chewing on all afternoon, the silver coin slows down, balls back up into a fat wet tear, and drops straight through the floor.

It falls through an overhanging sheet of chartreuse bladder-vine twenty feet down—*pffft*, a tiny pinhole opening and sealing itself instantly—and disappears into the green gloom toward the knives.

The boy’s collarbones flare sharp, panicked white-blue. His toes curl back on the good air.

I pull my chin off the hairline strut, unhook my dried arthropod shade from my harness, and snap it open once with my left wrist, angling the stiffened chitin wing precisely into the up-drafting brass roar rising past my ribs. 

Across sixty yards of howling dusk, I punch a three-inch hole of pure, momentary quiet straight into the center of his chest.

*Ba-dump. Dump.*

Even from here, I watch his supple ribs jump forward half an inch into the sudden pocket of missing pressure, startled, then settle as he finds me hanging under the Sluice eaves. I sweep my right forearm down in a slow, rolling green wave—*easy, long-legs, keep your spit in your head*—then mask half my palm to cut a crisp amber diagonal pointing forty paces north, where the suspended mist-beads are still vibrating tight and square in the residual humidity. *Cross up-chord. This seam has an ulcer.*

He flashes a ragged, embarrassed violet thank-you from one elbow and lopes northward with that loose, floating gait we all have, his wide grey heel-pads kissing the shimmer without breaking stride, the two heavy slurry-sacks bouncing gently on the air-cushion behind him like tethered moons.

Once he is clear, I haul myself up my tether hand-over-hand, hook both calloused heels onto the narrow ledge of compressed phase-space where the vertical wall-tension meets the underside of the road, and crouch there in the warm gale like a folded heron. From my belt-pouch I take a pinch of coarse, black, sharp-edged grit—real stone-sand, traded two weeks ago from a Rim-Walker who smelled of sour leather and flat, dead air. Every grain of it feels obscene between my thumb and forefinger: tiny, dense, unyielding teeth from the outside margin where things simply fall down and stay fallen.

I lean out into the empty drop beneath the bad patch of road and drop six grains of basalt grit onto the underside of the ceiling-shear, upside-down, letting the upward roar slam them against the weakened membrane from below.

Five of them shatter into invisible dust instantly, flung sideways into the chasm with tiny violet sparks. The sixth catches in the lazy, sagging pocket of the stutter—and hangs there, spinning furiously in a tight two-inch circle, carving a visible white ring of micro-condensation into the air like grease pencil on glass. Now any Line-Tender passing within four blocks will see the white bullseye hanging over nothing and know where to bring the pruning-knives before dark.

I wipe my gritty fingers on my moss-wrap, shivering slightly through my loose shoulder-joints. I hate touching rim-dirt. Even three grains of it makes my hollow thigh-bones ache with a phantom heaviness, a sudden sickening memory of stories about the raw edge two leagues east—where if you drop a gourd-kettle it doesn't drift or skitter or explode into mist, it just *clacks* once against wet black rock and lies there in the horrible, breathless quiet, waiting for your knees to snap so you have to lie down beside it.

Below my perches, fifty feet down in the third curtain of aero-moss, a swarm of pale ribbon-gliders catches the afternoon harmonic shift and peels off a bladder-vine in a single damp, silver-white wave—two hundred of them surfing the exact trough of the F-sharp chord out into the middle of the half-mile gap, banking around a knot of violin-bugs whose iron-hail clatter shakes a warm five-second rain down into the deeper green halls.

I lean my back flat against nothing at all, trusting the standing shear behind my shoulder blades to hold my sixty-eight pounds over four hundred feet of razor blades, open my mouth to let the great, warm, crushing brass organ-pipe of the earth pour cleanly through my jawbone and into my skull, and slide thirty yards sideways down the invisible eaves to listen to the next block over.
