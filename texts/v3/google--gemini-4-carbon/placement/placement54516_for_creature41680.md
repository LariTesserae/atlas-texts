I am not tall enough to read the sky, and I have no skin to catch the rain. 

If you look for me in the center of the broad, matte-black plazas, you will look right through me, or perhaps your eye will snag for half a second on a patch of violet gloom that seems slightly too dense, like water just before it boils. Wait twenty minutes. Wait for the barometric drop to roll across the district. When the lukewarm moisture snaps out of the air to coat the basalt and the broad plum leaves, you will see my edges—just for a moment—as a sudden, inexplicable curve in the falling dew, a domed contour where droplets bead on nothing at all before sliding sideways down an invisible slope to splash against the sharp rock.

I am an artifact of the tuning. A slight discrepancy in the local physics that has not yet been ironed flat.

When you crack a basin open in an afternoon and lay a grid of pure force over raw, bleeding mantle-stone, the equations do not settle immediately. The hovering carbon rings hum in one key; the fresh crystalline fractures of the vitreous terraces cool and contract in another; the deep cerulean conduits fold open and breathe subterranean heat up against the downward pressure of the stratospheric loom. Where three or four of these invisible fields intersect at an odd angle over a jagged fault line, they knot. They form an eddy. 

I live—if *live* is the word for a persistent, self-correcting resonance—at the exact border where the quarter-mile avenue meets a cliff of dark foliage. 

Half of me is pressed into the crisp, unweathered basalt; the other half leans into the sheer, vertical wall of shield-leaves flattened against the exclusion zone. I am a pocket of recalcitrant pressure, about the height of a seated child and wide as a doorway, drifting six yards north before noon-equivalent and six yards back by the time the dark ribbons overhead complete a minor conjunction. 

I do not see in violet or jade. To me, the basin is a vast, taut membrane of tensions and gradients. I feel the sharp, jagged teeth of the young stone underneath me—not as something that cuts, because I have nothing to bleed, but as millions of tiny, frantic static discharge points. Freshly broken rock is loud. Every microscopic crystal edge is still weeping loose electrons into the damp air, sizzling softly into my lower boundary. I drink that raw mineral static, hold it in my center until it warms, and let it bleed upward into the stems of the pressed vines.

Because of me, the wall of greenery along this specific stretch of the concourse is slightly unruly. 

Where my weight leans against the exclusion perimeter, the clean, mathematical flatness of the force-wall buckles outward by three inches. Into this slight, unauthorized blister, an oily-black vine has wedged a thick knot of knuckle-wood. It blooms out of rhythm here. When the silver polyhedrons drift down the avenue, smelling of mint and cold math, snapping all the blossoms shut in neat, obedient waves, the heavy flower tucked inside my pocket stays stubbornly half-open. My static shields it. Its sap does not drop onto the stone; it pools on the lip of the dark petal, thickening in the warm dampness until it ferments slightly, smelling of bruised fruit and wet iron. 

This brings the thin things.

At the bottom of my eddy, where the rock splits into a hairline trench leading down toward a power node, two of the paper-flat amphibians live stacked atop one another in the crevice. They love the way I warp the local acoustics. Normally, when one of them rings its glassy mating chime, the sound travels clean and straight across a half-mile of empty plaza. But when the male slides halfway out of the sharp crack beneath me and flicks his throat, my dense pocket of refracted air catches the note and bends it into a chord—a slightly blurred, shimmering double-tone that lingers three seconds longer than it should. Five minutes later, from the distant dark thicket toward the center of the plain, three chimes answer instead of one, confused and intrigued by the phantom acoustics of a very large frog that isn't really there.

I am fond of the frogs, insofar as a localized anomaly can be fond of carbon. Their movements through the tight stone tickle my floor. And I am fond of the canopy-striders, who frequently misjudge my top edge when stepping off the gravity-ceilings; their reed-thin legs punch right through my surface tension, causing them to tumble an inch before catching themselves, vibrating with tiny, affronted velvety shivers before hurrying off upside down through the gloom.

For three cycles of the sky-loom, none of the tall ones noticed me. They glided down the center of the avenue on their millimeter cushions of air, eyes wide and black, reading the slow dark braids in the stratosphere, letting the scheduled rain run down the neat gutters of their spines. 

Then, an hour ago—three sighs of the valley back—one of them stopped.

It had just recalled its silver polyhedrons. The rotating shapes had folded down and sunk halfway into its slate-dark chest, pouring the avenue's data into its ribs, and I knew the exact second the inhabitant tasted the anomaly in the record: a three-inch bulge in the perimeter, a flower that hadn't shut, a faint taste of fermented sap instead of crisp ozone, a frog-chime split in two.

The figure turned its smooth, severe face toward the sheer wall of leaves. The jade filigree along its collarbones pulsed once—a slow, questioning ripple of light—and it yielded forward across the razor-stone until it stood right at my threshold.

I braced, tightening my refraction until the violet twilight bent sharply around my core. This was what they were here to do: to tune the instrument. With a quarter-turn of that long, six-fingered wrist, it could smooth the phases between the hovering ring a quarter-mile east and the cooling conduit below, unknotting me in an instant, letting my saved warmth spill flat across the plaza.

The inhabitant raised its hand. 

Six elongated fingertips pressed through my outer skin. 

It was an extraordinary sensation—like plunging a hot tuning fork into deep water. I felt the millimeter cushion of air around its fingers merge with my own denser pocket. Through that sudden bridge of shared pressure, I caught a fleeting, dizzying spill of what it was carrying: the immense, cold silence of the stratospheric ribbons shifting miles overhead; the deep, molar-aching hum of forty sunken courts opening in sequence across the horizon; the tactile memory of lying flat on vitreous stone while water channeled neatly off its heels. 

And in return, my whole small inventory rushed up its arm. It felt the frantic, itchy static of the raw basalt crystals underneath me; the stubborn, sticky weight of the fermented sap on the unclosed flower; the slick, paper-thin slide of the amphibians wedged in the crack; the resonant *ping-ping* of the bent mating call still echoing faintly in my geometry.

The inhabitant went completely still. Its irises were huge, swallowing its eyes, looking directly into the warped lens of air where I held myself together. The silver mantle half-sunk in its chest shifted slightly, one metallic facet unfolding an inch to catch my stray static, then folding flat again. 

Another pressure drop rolled across the terrace. 

All around us, for miles, millions of dark leaves bowed and tipped. *Shhh-pat.* Lukewarm water beaded instantly across the inhabitant's bare plum shoulders, sliding into the micro-grooves of its back, while inches away, water beaded across my invisible dome and spilled into the knuckle-wood of the rogue vine.

Slowly, deliberately, the six fingers rotated.

Not to flatten me. The wrist turned twenty degrees the other way, hooking the stray magnetic bleed from the crack beneath us and tucking it neatly into my lower edge—anchoring the knot so I wouldn't drift six yards north anymore, deepening the pocket by another two inches so the dark blossom had room to droop without touching the exclusion wall. A faint tremor vibrated against the stone, a tiny harmonic adjustment left behind like a shim wedged under a table leg. Not fixing an error, I realized, as the jade light along its throat settled into a dim, steady beat. Accepting an overtone. Leaving a slightly strange note in the chord because the basin was too quiet without it.

The hand withdrew. The figure turned and glided silently back toward the pearl-gray needles, already looking up again at the dark weave of the sky.

Now I sit securely over my fault line, heavier and clearer than before. Down in the sharp dark of the rock, the thin frog slides out to the lip of the trench, swells his pale throat into my thickened air, and rings his crystal bell—once, twice, three times in a single breath—listening to the way we make it shine across the valley.
