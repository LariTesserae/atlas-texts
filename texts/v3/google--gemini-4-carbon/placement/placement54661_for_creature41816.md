Slowly, so you won't stir up body-heat you’ll only have to sweat off, you roll onto your stomach and draw your knees under you, feeling the smooth, cool patches of benign gray flora cultivated across your ribs slide easily against your inner arms.

Ten feet up the slight two-degree grade, past the receding flank of Pipe Two, sits the one thing at the western bend that never moves: the massive concrete thrust-block poured into a bedrock trench forty years ago to anchor Pipe Four where the turn begins. Acidic leaf-mulch and bat dung have eaten the corners of the concrete round as karst stone, and squat barrel-trees have thrown muscular, wrist-thick roots across its flanks to pin it down twice over, yet where Pipe Four rests in its curved saddle there is still a four-inch slot underneath through which the cold seepage from Pipe Three slides downhill. As your palm comes down at the lip of that slot to steady your crawl, two blind caecilians—cool and slick as greased thumbs—slip unhurriedly over your knuckles on their way toward warmer mud, followed by the delicate, sideways *tik-tik-tik* of a pale land crab walking across your wrist without bothering to raise its claws.

You let it pass, then reach up to run two calloused fingers along the top crown of Pipe Four.

Sure enough, three feet east of the concrete saddle, a new aerial rootlet dropped down from an overhanging *Ficus* bough since you checked two nights ago has found the ninety-eight-degree iron. Mistaking the bitumastic coating for fallen hardwood, its pale, gummy tip has already flattened out to glue itself across a spiral weld-seam. Left alone for two weeks, it would lignify into a cable tough enough to stall the polyurethane rollers of the ultrasonic inspection collar; a stalled collar throws a high-current fault to a server on the coast; and forty-eight hours later the night tears open with diesel clatter, churning tracks, yellow chemical suits, and ten-thousand-lumen flood-bars turning the dark into a steaming white hell.

You reach down to the rawhide thong at your hip—Wait: rawhide rots into slime in four days down here; it is a braided ring of shed *Boa* skin greased in heavy black crude wept from a pinhole flange-leak up the grade—and slip out a thumb-sized flake of glazed brown porcelain.

Kneeling by feel beside the pipe, you slide the glassy edge under the root-tip and sever it with one slow, neat draw, peeling the sticky white tendril clean off the tar without nicking the wrap. You don't toss it into the brush. You hold the juicy six-inch cutting down at knee-level in the dark and wait four seconds. There is a dry scrape of plastron against limestone, a faint puff of warm air against your knuckles like a small bellows, and the horny beak of a yellow-footed tortoise closes carefully around the stem half an inch from your fingertips, tugging once before pulling back to chew with slow, wet crunches.

That is pretty much the whole bargain you keep with the valley: slice the fig-roots off Pipe Four so the machine stays happy, bring down fresh porcelain flakes twice a year when the others’ shaving-shards go dull, and keep your voice shut inside your throat until you forget how your own name used to vibrate against your teeth.

If someone dragged you above the cloud-deck tomorrow and asked how long you had been down here, you could not give them a number in calendar years. Numbers require edges, and down here time only comes in thicknesses. You know you have been at the bend for three full cycles of the big crude-line shutdowns—periods when the northern pumps went completely cold and silent for months during whatever collapses or wars were rearranging the coast, when the boas unbraided themselves from the cooling welds and slid out into the thickets, and you crawled half a mile east to sleep flank-to-flank with the sinkhole woman and the old Tender under his long rubber awning, sharing mammal warmth like clustered bats until the iron trembled back to life. Before that, you remember boots turning to wet cardboard, the panic of a dead headlamp, the lunatic loud ringing of your own breathing when you first followed the power-line trace down off the western ridge-towers because the world up in the bright sun had run out of water and room at the same time.

Whatever language you carried down that slope has long since shed its consonants. You don't think in sentences anymore; sentences are built for pointing across distances at separate objects, and nothing within arm’s length is separate from anything else. Your mind moves in gradients of warmth, salt, vibration, and interval.

You crawl across the root-braided concrete saddle and lay your left cheekbone flat against the *west* side of Pipe Four, where the empty steel lifts clear of the mud and begins its five-mile climb up the stepped karst talus toward the rim.

Because the heavy concrete anchor-block damps half the vibrations trying to cross it, the two sides of this saddle listen to two entirely different worlds.

Pressed to the western iron, your jawbone reads the unpeopled slope and the country beyond the clouds. Right now, miles away past the limestone rim where the sky is clear, dawn must be striking the exposed pipeline in the dry scrub hills: you can hear the faint, rhythmic *tink... tink* of thermal expansion creaking through steel roller-guides ten miles out in the sun while down here in the pan the air remains ninety-eight degrees and black as velvet pitch. Closer—maybe half a mile up the western grade, where the transmission cables pass close over a jagged karst pinnacle and roosting fruit-bats occasionally bridge an insulator string in a brief, sizzling blue flash—something heavy just shifted on the rock. Up there on that steeper talus, where you make a slow four-hour crawl once every few months when your strength feels thick, broken brown porcelain insulator-skirts lie shattered in the wet ferns waiting to be knapped into razor flakes, right near a weeping seam of ancient, brackish gray shale where brocket deer and tapirs have licked a smooth salt-hollow into the cliff.

And underneath the distant sun-creaks on the western pipe, much closer—maybe eighty yards up the grade, rolling steadily down out of the upper dark at six feet a minute—comes the soft, unmistakable tooth-hum of polyurethane wheels and fifty-hertz induction coils.

The Collar is coming back down into the bowl.

You lift your cheek, slide two yards back to the east side of the concrete block, and tuck your knees into the lukewarm silt beside the low slot, folding your hands over your shins so your toes clear the steel.

It takes forty minutes to cover those eighty yards. Nothing hurries you while you wait. Overhead, a large bat drifts past so slowly in the stagnant soup that you feel each lazy down-stroke push a puff of warm, musky air across your shaved scalp. Ten feet downhill, Pipe One gives one last microscopic *shudder-slip* across the wet clay as its temperature stabilizes, nudging a five-foot *Bothrops* viper gently against a tortoise’s shell; neither bothers to hiss. Over by Pipe Three, you reach out blindly with half a folded palm-spathe hung from a copper hook under the sweating belly of the return-line, tip two mouthfuls of fifty-degree distilled condensation onto your tongue—cold enough to ache behind your molars, tasting cleanly of iron dust—and hang the trough back under the *plink, tup, plink*.

Then the air two yards west changes smell.

Before you see the green pinhole, you smell what the Collar’s warm electronics housing picked up three days ago up on the rim and has been slowly baking inside its vent-baffles all the way down the slope: the dry, sharp, resinous tang of upland *Podocarpus* pollen and scorched dust, utterly foreign to this drowned bowl of rotted figs and wet iron.

A point of tiny, sourceless green light materializes at arm’s length, floating four feet off the bedrock.

In total blackness, to eyes dilated wide for a decade, even a quarter-watt indicator LED looks startlingly jewel-hard, though it throws zero beam beyond itself. You watch it glide onto the concrete saddle: first the ring of curved rubberized feelers brushing lightly over a sleeping nightjar that merely lifts one feathered wing, takes two stiff sidesteps onto a root, and tucks its beak right back down; then the low, clay-caked aluminum cowling, warm as a fevered forehead as it passes six inches from your knee, carrying three tiny white cliff-snails from the upper rim glued shut in their own dried mucus along its bolt-heads; then the faint electromagnetic buzz of its EMAT array ringing megahertz queries down into the steel skin and catching the clean, unfractured echo back.

For three seconds as it clears the concrete block and rolls smoothly over the spot where the *Ficus* root was glued an hour ago, the green dot reflects simultaneously in forty pairs of wet, unblinking eyes packed into the mud-crescent around you—tapirs, tree boas, stacked cane toads, and land crabs—none of them turning a head, all of them letting the small, tireless beetle of the outside world pass unchallenged onto the four-mile flat.

You wait until the green pinhole shrinks to nothing in the wet dark—forty feet, fifty, swallowed cleanly by mist and drooping dinner-plate leaves on its fifteen-hour crawl toward the center pan.

Only then do you slide your right hand four inches deep into the cool silt beside the concrete pier and close your fingers around your striker: a fist-sized lump of dense dolostone wrapped tightly in three layers of black vulcanized crane-mat rubber lashed with sinew—no, nylon gill-net cord—so it can never throw an induced static spark under the low wires.

Moving on the *east* side of the anchor-block now, where the steel stretches four dead-level miles through the dark under a million warm and cold bellies, you swing the padded stone lazily from the wrist against the flank of Pipe Four.

*Thump-thump.*

Low, dull, and bone-deep, stripped of sharp clatter by the rubber skin, the double pulse leaps eastward down the hollow forty-eight-inch barrel faster than anything with legs or wings ever moves in this valley: *Awake at the bend. The track is clean. It’s coming down to you.*

You drop the wrapped stone back into the mud, slide down flat onto your side beside the tapir cow’s warm chest, and press your ear and palm against the naked iron.

For two hundred slow beats of your carotid, there is only the deep fifty-hertz river of the crude pumps humming up through the bedrock, the dry papery whisper of millions of jaws chewing waxy leaves in the dark, and the steady *plink, tup, plink* of cold water striking stone.

Then, faint through half a mile of steel, comes the crisp drag of a river pebble across a weld-seam, and two light taps. Half a minute later, farther out where the old Tender sleeps between the boas under his rubber saddleblanket, a short brass rod clicks twice—*tik-tik*—and then, barely more than a change in the iron’s grain from two miles east by the deep sinkhole, a thumbnail answers back.

Nothing is broken. Nobody is coming down off the rim with white lights. You let your hand slide off the pipe into the warm clay, match the rise of your ribs to the five-hundred-pound lung sighing against your back, and let the dark close back over your head.
