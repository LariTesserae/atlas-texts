To find myself here, I have to leave the glare first. 

Even the pale-winged bird crossing four hundred feet up doesn't want the floor, and I wouldn't want the bird's thin, dry altitude either—watching an engine work from the rafters without ever touching the iron. If I belong to this basin, I belong to the ratio that runs it. Not the exhaust, and not the scraps cemented like rivets into the apron, and not the panicked split-second under the chisel-jaw.

I go down through the warm leather-mud, past the hairline cracks where the pumice-tunicates sit distilling their drop an hour, down into the dark where the noise stops being air and becomes purely weight.

Down twelve feet, twenty feet, where the compacted clay turns to fractured shale and then to the upper shelves of hot, weeping basalt. No light has ever arrived here, which is fine; the light above was only ever an flat, shadowless proof that nothing was changing. Down here, everything is tight, flooded, and loud in a way that has no echo. Sound doesn't travel across emptiness; it slams straight through solid rock and scalding brine, instantaneous and whole.

I am not an animal with legs and a neck. Legs are for crossing dead space between vents, and down here there is no dead space—every seam is part of the boiler. 

At first, you might mistake me for a vein of dense, rubbery bitumen or dark, native rubber forced into a fault line by pressure, branching through the hairline plumbing that connects three neighboring chimneys. Something between a creeping subterranean fungus, a blind colonial ribbon-worm, and the heavy, heat-cured gasket material they use on steam locomotives. My skin—if you can call a sheath of vulcanized, iron-crosslinked collagen *skin*—is tough enough to take ninety degrees of wet heat without cooking, slick with a dense mucilage that keeps dissolved lime and silica from crusting me solid into stone.

I live in the constriction throats. Right below the chambers where the groundwater flashes to steam.

Why there? Because the basin looks eternal from the top, an engine turning over at idle forever, *clack-hiss*, four seconds to the beat—but rock isn't actually a machine. Left entirely to itself, mineral water precipitates too fast. A throat chokes on its own travertine; a copper valve-seat grows a rough wart of malachite on one lip so the cap stops sealing tight; pressure leaks sideways, a hammer loses its compression, sputters out into a lukewarm seep, and dies. And when a chimney dies, the grease-mats dry to flaking soot, the arthropods wander out onto the leather-flats to desiccate, and the shingled hunter has to make that terrible five-year walk across the glare.

Nothing plans to prevent this. I certainly don't plan it. I only eat scale.

Stretched along the narrow stone conduits twenty feet beneath a hammering cone, anchored into porous pockets of basalt by thousands of stiff, microscopic bristles, my body feels the water coming in from the cold fractures further toward the bluffs, and feels the deep, bruised heat rising from the iron beds below. Where those two meet and flash, the rock wants to fur up like an old kettle. Fresh, crisp crusts of aragonite, gypsum, and iron-oxide drop out of solution with every pressure drop.

To me, that fresh precipitate—still warm, micro-porous, trapping whole constellations of deep-earth sulfur bacteria before it hardens into solid limestone—is pasture. 

I extrude slow, muscular flanges of dark tissue along the vibrating walls of the conduit, secreting weak organic chelators—little localized sweats of oxalic and sulfuric acid—just enough to keep the fresh crystal slush soft, rasping it smooth with microscopic rows of magnetite teeth not unlike a chiton’s radula, though spread across yards of blind, branching flank. I swallow the bacterial paste and void the dissolved minerals into the high-velocity up-rush right when the chamber blows, letting the chimney spit my chalk dust out into the sky or lay it down safely on the wide outside terraces where it won't choke the pipe.

I am the reason the four-second *clack-hiss* stays four seconds for three hundred years.

What is it like, being four yards of blind, leathery gut wedged into the throat of a natural steam-hammer?

It is entirely rhythmic, and completely devoid of waiting. Above ground, the shingled salamander waits fifty cycles for one mouthful; the beetles walk fifty yards for a shed skin. Down in the throat, the world happens all at once, every four seconds, through the whole length of me.

First comes the *draw*: the stone chamber above has just blown its cap, pressure drops momentarily, and cool, heavy groundwater surges inward through the basalt seams along my lower flanks. It feels sharp and metallic, carrying trace oxygen from miles away under the yellow bluffs. My lower filaments flare open into the seam, breathing hard while the water is liquid.

Then the *lock*: three yards above my highest reach, five hundred pounds of native copper drops back into its beveled stone seat. 

The shockwave doesn't hit my ears; it compresses my entire hydrostatic skeleton against the basalt wall in a single, glorious, bell-like thud. All through the rock around me, I can feel the dimmer, staggered thuds of the wider basin—the slow diesel-chugger four hundred yards west hitting half a beat late, a high-pitched limestone whistler half a mile south ticking fast like a cricket—all their tremors crossing through my body like ripples on a pond floor. That overlapping chord of vibrations tells me everything there is to know about the world's geography. Where a neighboring throat is narrowing and ringing sharper (good eating there; send a slow, blind runner through the wet shale over the next six years to tap into it). Where an old cone a mile east has gone silent and cold (pull the runners back, seal off that root, let the dead limb slough away).

Then comes the *boil*: for three point eight seconds, trapped under the seated copper cap, the water around my upper reaches superheats. The pressure climbs. Bubbles of pure steam tear loose from the hot basalt, roaring upward past my tough, slick hide. I pull my breathing filaments tight into their sheaths, flatten down into a tough, streamlined lining along the stone, and press my rasping pads hard against the throat walls while the heat peaks—because the hottest tenth of a second right before the blow is when the richest sulfur-coatings precipitate out of the water. 

A hairline shiver runs up the stone—the exact shiver the armored hunter up in the daylight is waiting for on its pale belly—and then the cap lifts, the water flashes violently into vapor, scouring past me with a screaming *hiss* that strips away all my shed mucus and dissolved grit, shooting it up into the glare where the horsehair polyps are already snapping open their tiny fans to catch my leavings.

And then the cold draw slides over my lower flanks again. *Draw, lock, boil, blow.* 

No eyes to ache in the colorless sky. No shell to dry out on the mudstone. When dusk turns the upper flats to tarnished brass and then to starlight, not a half-degree changes in my dark seam. I am twenty years old in one chimney, four hundred years old across six of them, slowly creeping through the wet, fractured dark beneath the foundry floor—keeping the valves ground smooth, tasting the deep iron, holding the beat steady from the inside.
