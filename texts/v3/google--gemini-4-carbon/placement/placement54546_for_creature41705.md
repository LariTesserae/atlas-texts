At first, entering the basin, the pull is not toward a body at all. It is toward the seam. 

For a long minute I think I might simply be the thin lead-sheathed teller-wire strapped to the underside of a braided bridge-bough, carrying low-voltage clicks across fifty yards of cold mist from a switchbox to a hopper-tendril. There is a profound temptation, in a place so quiet and continuous, to shed the trouble of having hands and become part of the instrumentation—to be a brass capillary tube threaded into sapwood, translating immense, dark vascular pressure into the steady tremble of a needle in the green. Nothing hurried, nothing argued, only load and resistance, temperature and draw.

Even the wood invites it: that slow, patient swallowing of bolt-heads and iron washers until foreign metal becomes native bone. 

Hold still in the courtyard while the afternoon fog thickens, though, and the difference makes itself felt. The wood only knows the pressure of the present hour. The iron collar only knows tension. Neither of them remembers that forty-one winters ago, during three weeks of dry east wind, this same root-plate slacked off by two pounds to the square inch and threw a hairline check into a turbine-mold fifty paces upslope. 

Something in me cannot stop comparing November against May, or wondering what a quarter-turn today will mean to a pair of hands not yet born. To do that, you cannot be the tree, and you cannot be the gauge. You have to stand slightly outside both, squinting at where they meet.

So I have cork and raw crepe under my heels after all.

***

You would not find me on the heavy scaffolds of the mold-workshops, leaning my shoulder into a three-foot ratchet bar—I haven’t the heft for it, though I know the stamped flange-number of every cast-iron corset in the parish. And I am not often up on the curled timber rim, except twice a year in the cool weeks of spring and autumn when the narrow-gauge railcars come grinding up from the low valleys.

I do go up then, standing back a little from the transfer-crane with my hands tucked into stiff ochre sleeves. I like watching the valley crews. They are not allowed off the timber-bolted unloading stage, and even there they have to pull thick felt overshoes over their hobnailed boots before touching a rope—a rule that makes them clumsy and cross. They talk too fast, their voices flat and bright in the open air under the clouds, smelling fiercely of coal-smoke, wet horse-blanket, and salted grease. They unload pig-iron, sack-grain, baled wool, and rock-salt—the salt double-casked in coopered oak and slung with extreme, ritualized paranoia over the courtyard to a lead-lined locker, because one burst barrel on the bare burl would burn a weeping ulcer into the town’s floor that fifty years wouldn’t heal. 

I watch them swing a finished iron-burl ship-knee onto a flatcar: six hundredweight of compressed, silica-dense wood dark as gunmetal, forty-four years in the corset, smooth as tallow. The valley foreman raps his knuckles against it, grins because it doesn’t ring hollow or metallic—it just eats the knock dead—signs a damp paper manifest, and waves the engine back down the grade. Within twenty minutes they are gone, and the rim settles back into wind and pine-vapor.

Walking back down the shallow terraces after the train leaves, feeling the long valve-roots shift gently beneath the soles of my boots, I always feel the relief of returning to my proper speed.

***

Follow the bundled teller-wires down to the third terrace, where three thick cable-boughs meet a squat, wide-hipped bole whose bark has grown in heavy slate-gray folds over its lower iron rings. Step high over the threshold lip—polished dark as ox-horn by five generations of wool cuffs brushing across it—and let the latched door ease shut against its felt weather-strip.

This is the Trim-Loft. It is warm enough inside to take your coat off at once.

Down on the lower level, the main return-pipes from two dynamo-towers pass through the floor ribs, radiating a steady eighty degrees and a faint, resinous hum that sits below hearing, more like a purr in the breastbone. Up the curving interior stair, in a vaulted circular gallery lit by four bullseye portholes and a pair of low-voltage yellow filaments, the whole parish comes together in miniature.

This is where I spend my days, and a good portion of my nights, in sleeveless slate wool and felt slippers, with a brass magnifier slung round my neck on waxed twine.

Along the inner heartwood wall—not hung on plaster, of course, but bracketed to swallowed iron studs grown straight out of the structural ribs—stands a curved bank of tall glass manometers filled with dyed wintergreen spirit, rising and falling by bare millimeters as the seven terraces breathe. Beside them, small electromagnetic repeaters click very softly, tallying the strokes of the courtyard flywheels and the draw of the overhead tendril-lines. You do not even look at the repeaters once your ear knows the room; you only look up when a rhythm drops half a beat behind its neighbor.

The rest of the gallery belongs to the racks.

Paper is scarce here, brought in on the railcars and saved mostly for valley manifests and patterns, and in the warm, humid breath of a living trunk, unbound paper wants to curl and fox anyway. For the basin’s real memory, we use thin, quarter-sawn slips of seasoned boxwood and pale birch-veneer, planed to a satin card, slotted by the thousands into honeycomb pigeonholes grafted directly out of the dry inner ribs of the room. 

Look at my hands and they have the same deep purplish-black tannate stain in the creases as any tender’s, though mine comes less from scale on a spanner and more from the ink-crock on my bench. We make the ink in a stoneware jar on the warm pipe-casing: iron filings swept from the machine-shop floor steeped for months in bitter bark-liquor and rainwater. It is true iron-gall. Brushed onto boxwood with a stiff quill or a split brass nib, it bites straight into the grain and turns blacker over twenty years than the day it went down.

Every iron corset in the workshops, every valve-root under the plazas, every residential trunk and suppression-collar in the needle-mats overhead has its stack of slips here. 

That is who I am: the trim-clerk—though the older cambials usually just call the post *keeping the draw*.

***

It is strange, quiet work, sitting in the middle of a conversation where half the speakers are dead and the other half aren't born yet.

When a pair of tenders tightens the tension-bolts on that cartwheel gear-mold by a quarter-inch, they don't choose the quarter-inch by whim. The afternoon before, I pull the wheel’s slip-bundle. The top slip was started nineteen springs ago by my predecessor’s teacher—a dry, minute hand with sharp downstrokes: *Collared off Manifold Six, north limb. Sap-head 14.2. Clean start, mild mist.* Beneath his lines are seven years of his entries, then eleven years of the woman who trained me, and at the bottom, three years of mine. 

Before tomorrow’s turn goes onto the schedule-slate, I check the manometer for Terrace Two. I check the repeater-count on the courtyard piston. If the east needle-mats have had three dry afternoons without fog, or if the children’s gallery in Bole Nine has had to open its sap-radiators wide against an early frost, the pressure under Workshop Four will be riding half a pound lean. Tighten an iron mold on a lean limb and the wood starves unevenly; ten years later, when they unbolt the iron, there will be a pale,chalky inclusion deep in the hub—*dead pith*, we call it—and forty years of slow hydraulic labor will be fit for nothing but chocks and counterweights.

So I dip the brass nib, write *Hold turn; mist thin; let her drink till Tuesday*, and hang a small stamped copper tag on the hook by the door for the morning runner.

Then there is the other half of the bench—the work I love best, when the afternoon mist finally drops into the basin, the louvers click shut high overhead, and the yellow lamps come on.

Whenever a finished piece of iron-burl is sawn free of its manifold-branch and hoisted toward the rim, the sawyers take a second cut half an inch lower, before sealing the living stump with hot pitch. That thin collar-wafer comes down to the Trim-Loft still smelling faintly of warm silica and bruised wintergreen.

It looks nothing like timber. It is heavier than slate, sink-in-water dense, dark olive-brown banded with black and rust-ochre where the iron corset bled mineral into the outer rind. You cannot shine a light through it any more than you could shine a lamp through river-flint. Instead, over three or four quiet evenings while a kettle mutters on the copper bus-bar beside my elbow, I grind one face of the wafer flat on wetted valley sandstone, then polish it with rottenstone and mutton-tallow until it takes a hard, glassy gloss like dark agate.

Under the low-voltage lamp, tilted slightly beneath my brass lens, the whole biography of the part leaps out in reflected light.

Every winter is a hairline thread of metallic gray; every wet coastal summer is a dense ribbon of watered silk. You can see the exact autumn twenty-eight years back when a bold apprentice over-torqued the top turnbuckle by a sixteenth of an inch—there, a tiny, rippled whorl where the vascular bundles clenched and healed around the bruise. I lay the polished wafer beside the thirty-year stack of boxwood slips for that mold and read them side by side: the town’s written intention against the tree’s compressed truth. 

If our notes say the grain drew even, and the stone-hard rings say it pinched on the north bolt, I write the discrepancy onto the master-slip for that manifold-branch. Next year, when the grafters set a new mold on the neighboring bough for my grandchildren to unbolt, they will shim the north collar two millimeters shy from the start.

***

Sometimes, an hour after dusk, the oval door swings inward and the older tender with the silver braid steps over the sill, bringing a small gust of cold, pine-sharp fog in on her oilskin shoulders.

She doesn’t speak right away. She hooks her boxwood listening-rod onto the peg by the bulkhead, steps out of her cork-soled boots on the mat, and stands warming her dark-stained knuckles over the copper return-pipe. Down along the seam where the warm pipe meets the calloused rib of the wall, two fat, pewter-colored scale-backs move slowly out of her shadow, antennae lifting slightly at the damp wool of her trousers before they resume grazing invisible mold from the bark.

On the wall, a repeater clicks. Five seconds pass; another clicks. 

I pour two small stoneware mugs of hot water darkened with a spoonful of roof-skeps honey—sharp, thin sweetness tasting of waxy needles and cold iron—and slide one across the burl-table.

"Number Three valve-root," she says at last, voice pitched low for the curved wood. "Down by the west hopper-stanchion."

I don't reach for the nib yet. I wait while she takes a swallow.

"Not cavitating," she says, looking up at the glass spirit-tubes, where the third column from the left is riding steady, perhaps half a hair sluggish on the rebound. "Just thickening a little in the throat. Like old resin settling in the turn."

I step in my felt slippers to the west-quarter honeycomb, slide out three smooth boxwood cards darkened by thumb-oil at the corners, and carry them back to the pool of yellow light. We stand shoulder to shoulder over the bench. Seventy-six years ago, in tiny, cramped tannate script: *West stanchion root sluggish after dry harvest. Slacked Silo Two tendril-load four days; cleared clean without lancing.*

She reads the dead clerk’s line, nods once—a movement barely larger than the tick of the brass gauge—and finishes her mug. No surgery in the root-plate, then; no bruising the courtyard. Just four days of letting the overhead hopper rest in its cradle while the tree washes its own throat clear.

When she has pulled her boots back on and stepped silently out into the fog, latching the heavy door behind her, the gallery settles back into its deep, humid quiet. 

I wipe the brass nib on a scrap of oiled rag, cap the ink-crock, and sit for a minute with my palms flat against the warm, curved rib of the workbench. Beneath the floorboards, beneath the return-pipes, deep down in the dark hydraulic vaults of the parish, that evening shift passes through the substrate—a long, muscular, sub-audible *thrum* rising up through the heartwood, into the table, into the bones of my wrists. Four hundred people turning down their lamps inside a living engine; a hundred iron molds holding their patient grip in the dark; six thousand boxwood slips waiting in the dry ribs around me, holding the place in the sentence until morning.
