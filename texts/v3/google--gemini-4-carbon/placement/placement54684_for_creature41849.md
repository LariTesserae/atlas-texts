You would not find me up on the bleached curve of the ridge, squinting through charcoal-grease into the glare. My knees were ruined for the top-walk twenty years ago—not from a fall, nobody survives a real fall, just a bad twist in a wet split when a bull-grazer kicked free of a gaff and took two of my toes and the side of my left knee-joint with its hind hooks. Up on the sunny weave, if you cannot roll your weight clean from heel to pinky-edge, you become a drag on the march and bait for the pelican-jaws when the shadow passes.

So I live fifty yards down, on the Second Tendon. 

Look through a wide chink in the floorboards near the center of the valley, past the hanging water-gourds and the shimmering copper crusts of cicada-bellies. Let your eyes adjust to the blue-gold gloom under the mat. Fifty yards below the bottom of the world’s floor, a single solitary limb of ironwood—forty feet wide, smooth as oiled horn, cambered like the keel of an overturned boat—sweeps two miles across the empty gulf to anchor the eastern rim against the canyon shear. There is no weave down here. No brush, no splits, no amber puddles. Just one tremendous, pale highway curving out through three miles of blue nothing, catching the angled light that pours down through the sieve of the upper meadow.

They call my perch the Catch-Bough, though nobody calls it anything out loud while the sun is up. 

I am the woman who keeps the knots, and I watch what drops.

Down here, the noise is different. Up top, the cicada-roar hits you flat in the face off the ridges; down here on the solitary span, you are suspended *inside* the harp. The whole forty-foot beam under my thighs hums at a single, terrifyingly pure note—a low, wooden *thrummm* that never quits, vibrating straight up through my calloused sit-bones into my ribs until I don’t know where the timber ends and my own sternum begins. I haven’t worn ear-plugs since my hair went grey. The top-walkers think I’ve gone stone-deaf, and mostly I let them think it, because it spares me having to listen to teenagers complain inside the burls about blister-sap and torn harnesses. The truth is I didn’t go deaf; my ears just gave up on air altogether. Everything I hear now comes up through my heels, my palms, and a six-inch tuning-peg of dense river-basalt I keep wedged into a hairline check beside my hip.

Touch your thumb to that stone with your eyes shut, and you can read three miles of the upper basin like a stretched hide.

There—a rapid, papery skittering slightly to the north: *mist-lizards flushing off the sun-side, which means a thermal just broke early.* 
A rhythmic, double-beat thump-slide, faint and sour in the grain: *my sister’s boy, the heavy one who still lands too hard on his right heel, hauling water-gourds up toward the blister.*
And then the sharp, singing *twang* that makes me open my eyes and reach for my long pole: something hard just bounced off the underside of the upper weave and is tumbling into the blue.

People think everything dropped through the floor is gone to the green dark at the bottom. Mostly they’re right. A cracked shell or a slick offal-sack falls straight and clean—or gets snapped out of the air two hundred feet below me by the big leather-wings that patrol the middle sky, banking past my tendon so close the stink of sour fish and warm musky hide washes right over my face. 

But the updrafts down here are tricky, thick and muscular as river currents. Light things—a snapped bone-needle, a child’s shed harness-strap, a dry gourd torn loose in a squall, or a freshly shed sheet of raptor-hide—don’t fall straight. They ride the rising breath sideways. And because the Second Tendon sits right in the throat of the main thermal chimney, half of what the five-mile valley loses drifts across my bow.

I have three nets rigged off the leeward curve of my bough, woven not from heavy grazer-hide—the wind would tear a hide net right off its pegs and take half the bark with it—but from thrice-braided whip-root and boiled cicada-wing, wide-meshed so the updraft passes through. I live tethered on a forty-foot braided belly-line anchored to a massive, swollen burl that grew over an ancient stone cleat driven here ten generations back, before my great-grandfather’s people even knew this loop of the valley. My shelter isn't a wedged blister; you don't drive splitting-stones into a solitary primary tendon unless you want the whole eastern ridge to groan and drop three inches in the night. Instead, I sleep in a slung cradle of oiled reptile-skins lashed tight into the dry, windless armpit where a dead secondary branch snapped off a thousand years ago, leaving a deep, smooth socket like the hollow of a collarbone.

It is cleaner down here than up on the mat. Cooler, too. No grazing herds clattering over my head at dawn, no biting dung-flies, no angry purple rot-sap weeping through the floor because twenty cousins stayed a month too long in one camp. Just the pale curve of the wood, the endless warm wind rising out of the three-mile haze between my bare dangling feet, and the slow, silent snow of dry beetle-husks drifting *upward* past my knees on the morning draft before gravity wins and takes them back down.

Yet I am never really alone for three days running. 

I am the hinge between the sunlit meadow and the Drop.

When the top-clans move their loop across the valley, they don’t haul their spare rope-coils or their secondary river-cobbles all five miles around the rim if they don't have to. They lower them to me on greased drop-lines through the Big Eye—a twenty-foot tear in the upper weave directly overhead, framed by smooth, worn timber where a thousand years of sinew-cords have grooved the lip like an old well-head. 

I’ll feel three sharp raps on the wood above—*tock, tock-tock*—transmitted down a taut guide-cord tied to my burl. I hobble out onto the crown of the bough, leaning hard at a forty-five-degree slant into the warm, roaring updraft, my bad leg stiff as a prop-pole, my wide, cracked right foot gripping the curve of the grain. Looking straight up into the dazzling blue square of the Eye fifty yards overhead, I’ll see four dark little heads ringing the hole like beetles looking into a bucket, their white wrist-bones flashing against the bright sky:

*Flash-drop-cross:* **Heavy load. Wet clay. Watch the swing.**

I unclip a single bone paddle from my belt—mine is stained yellow with old tallow so they can spot it against the pale wood below me—and give them one lazy sweep across my chest: **Send it.**

Down comes the bundle, spinning slowly in the empty bright air between the floor of the world and my solitary rib, shrinking and growing in the shifting shafts of sun. The wind catches it halfway down, pushing it twenty yards east out over the three-mile drop; I brace my hips into my harness, hook the drifting line with my twelve-foot bone-tipped gaff, and walk it smoothly in until the heavy, sweating skin-sack settles onto the ironwood between my ankles. Cool. Smelling of deep mud, dead stone, and the black bottom of the world. 

I press both palms flat against the wet leather before I untie it, just to feel that cold, mineral stillness against my skin. Nothing grown in the sky feels like mud. Wood always has a pulse in it, a dry, fibrous tension; stone and clay are dead, quiet things, and to touch them when your whole skeleton has been ringing like a struck bell for fifty years is like drinking cold water in a fever.

Twice in my life, I didn't haul up bundles. I hauled up Drop-crews.

That is really why the clans keep an old half-crippled woman fed down on the Second Tendon, lowering me haunches of roasted grazer and gourds of tannin-water every fourth afternoon. Because when six twenty-year-olds come crawling back up the verticals after three weeks in the green dark miles below—half-blind from the sudden glare, toes grey and peeling with bottom-rot, chests bleeding raw into their rope-harnesses, hauling two hundred pounds of basalt wedges inch by agonizing inch up the bare pillars—they cannot make that last fifty-yard free-hang through the Big Eye. Their hands are locked into claws around the rope; if they stop climbing, they don't have the fingers left to tie a rest-knot.

They make it to my bough, and they collapse onto the forty-foot curve like drowned things pulled onto a raft.

I don’t sign to them when they get here. Their eyes are too blown from the gloom to read bone-flashes anyway, and they smell wrong—thick and cold and mushroom-sweet, with pale, multi-jointed blind ticks from the lower trunks buried behind their knees and ears. I just crawl over them on my one good knee and my knotted forearms, slice their harness-knots before the swelling cuts their breathing off, smear hot boiled resin onto the rope-burns across their ribs, and shove strips of salted cicada-fat between their chattering teeth. 

For two days they lie lashed side-by-side in my leeward nets while the dry, warm updraft cures their soggy soles back to leather and the great roaring hum of the wood shakes the bottom-chill out of their marrow. None of them ever talk about what the ground looks like down where the granite dust went, not even when dusk comes and the fifteen-minute quiet drops over the basin. Once, a boy from my mother’s clan—barely seventeen, with a fresh black flint-core strapped to his collarbone like a baby—woke up in that purple twilight silence, looked straight *up* at the underside of the world fifty yards above us, where the dark weave blocked out the stars like a ceiling of tangled bones, and began to shake so hard the net-cords sang.

He didn’t look down into the three-mile dark beneath our backs. Nobody ever cares about the dark below. He was staring up at the meadow, terrified that all those miles of hollow wood were finally going to remember how heavy they were and let go.

I reached over, took his scarred hand, and pressed his palm flat against my basalt wedge driven into the check of the bough.

*Feel that?* I tapped twice on the back of his knuckles with my thumb. *Still pulling.*

You can feel it, if you live down on the bare structural bone long enough without dirt or leaves or chatter to distract you. The wood isn't resting over the abyss. It is *drawing*—tight as a bowstring from rim to rim, drinking the warm grey breath rising out of the bottomland right now as dusk cools the valley, swelling microscopically beneath your skin, shifting its weight a fraction of an inch against the wind as it has since the stone mountains blew away.

Right now, the sun has just cleared the western rim. Above me, the copper crusts clinging to the ceiling of my sky are shuddering down in great, ragged cascades of quiet. Ridge by ridge, the metallic surf washes away to the east, echoes once off the bone-smooth curve two miles out, and dies.

My ribs unclench. The phantom ringing blooms behind my eyes, sharp and sweet.

Fifty yards overhead, faint dark blurs move across the gaps in the weave—the top-bands climbing onto the knuckles of the floor, pulling their plugs, straightening their cramped spines against the indigo sky. In a moment, the first long, open-throated *oooo-ahhh* will roll out from the northern ridge, spilling over the lip and dropping down through the hollow air past my perch like smooth water.

I don't stand up straight to answer them; my left knee hasn't locked straight since my thirties, and down here on the bare keel, standing tall when the evening down-draft kicks back is a fool’s vanity. Instead, I sit straddling the edge of my forty-foot limb with one bare leg hanging out over three miles of darkening violet air, pick up a heavy cobble of river-stone tethered to my wrist, and wait for the four-second echo between the eastern and western ridges to cross right over my head.

Right in the seam of that echo, I swing the river-stone down hard against the dry, tight grain between my thighs.

*THOCK.*

Just once. Clear, wooden, and deep—a single dark heartbeat sent running two miles east and two miles west along the primary tendon, rising up through the weaves, into the burls, and straight into the calloused soles of eighty bare feet waiting in the twilight above, letting the whole hollow mountain know the Catch-Bough is still tight, the nets are clear, and nothing fell through the sky today that wasn't meant to go.
