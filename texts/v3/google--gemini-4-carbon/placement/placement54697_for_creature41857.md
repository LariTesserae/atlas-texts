If you brought lungs and wet blood into this basin, your very first breath would be an act of vandalism. A single damp exhale against the unweathered mortar would slake the dry lime, hiss faintly, and leave a grey pockmark like a burn. Leather boots with outside mud still dried in their welts would grind grit across the thumb-ridges of the herringbone floor, dulling in ten paces what has stayed razor-crisp since the tiles came out of the mold.

To be here at all, you have to begin already cured.

Look down beside the kneeling Mason’s hazel pouch, right where its flint scraper pared away that curl of air above the buckled bricks. The Mason tucked the brick-edge into the new seam, tapped it home, and moved on. It did not pick up the paring. 

Nothing rots here, and nothing evaporates, which means the pared-off shaving of space is still lying on the ochre tiles. It looks like a pale curl of horn trimmed from a goat’s hoof, or a dry, translucent ribbon dropped from a boxwood plane. If you nudge it, it weighs almost nothing, yet if you squint lengthwise down its spiral, your eye travels four paces sideways before emerging out the other end of a curled inch. It is leftover *between*—a snippet of room that was no longer needed once two distant beams were persuaded to sit flush.

All across the basin, wherever the Masons let out a tight corner or trim an angle to fit three alcoves onto one oak post, those horn-pale curls fall to the floor. There is no wind to blow them into drifts, so at first they stay where they drop. 

Wait long enough, though, and the other force in this bone-dry room goes to work: static. 

In air with zero moisture, every slow brush of a Mason’s wool cowl against a suede sage-leaf, every *shhhk* of a pumice-worm shifting against ceramic bark, builds a faint, dry charge. Over weeks, that quiet crackle draws the lightest loose things in the hall toward one another. Under the low, upward-lit overhang of a split-oak scaffold, the pared curls of air migrate a hair’s breadth at a time across the sharp tile-ridges. They hook onto frayed threads shed from a Mason’s linen skin. They gather stray stiff bristles moulted by the bark-plated hares, flakes of white bone-ash, and inch-long offcuts of braided sedge twine too short to knot.

That is where I pull together—not born, not built with a mallet, just *gathered* out of offcuts and static in the crisp shade beneath an oak trestle, while the underside of the beam above me glows like overcast noon.

At first I am scarcely more than a twitch in the litter, a handful of dry curls nesting inside one another like cracked seed-pods until they find a spine of twisted sedge to string themselves along. I have no bran to weight me down and no damp marrow to grease me. I am laminated, flat as a carpenter’s folding rule and roughly the length of a forearm, made of overlapping, translucent horn-shavings laced edge-to-edge with pale linen ravels. When I flex, I make the sound of a dry thumb sliding across stiff vellum.

Because my segments were pared off joints from half a dozen different alcoves, learning to move takes a season of trial. Early on, if I stretch out too carelessly beneath the gourd-vines, my tail is still lying three courses away in the cool shadow of a flint cairn, and a six-legged calf-beast stepping over that cairn pins my hindmost curl without ever appearing near the gourds. Slowly, by pulling myself taut against the sharp herringbone ridges of the floor, I learn to thread all my borrowed lengths sequentially into the *same* seam, keeping myself in one piece by holding a tiny, lost bead of knapped chert pinched in my frontmost linen fringes so I always know which way *plumb* pulls.

Once you are flat, dry, and thinner than a slate sliver, the hall opens itself along entirely different lines than the ones the Masons walk.

I can slip across the sharp thumb-whorls of the yellow-ochre floor without blunting a single ridge. In fact, sliding my papery belly across the tiles is how I read the basin: every Mason’s thumb has its own tiny history of flint-nicks mended with silt, stamped thousands of times in tight zigzags across the ground. By the tick and catch of those ridges against my underside, I know before turning a corner which builder laid this alcove, how tight its hand was pressing, and where the courses begin to curve upward toward the woven brick lip of the walls.

Up in the tiered canopy, where copper and pale-sage leaves have fused their suede margins into continuous velvety hammocks, I weigh too little to leave a dent. I can rest for half a day along a warm terracotta stem, feeling the silt shift inside its veins like sand in an hourglass whose neck is nearly closed. Beside me on a fragrant, adze-marked oak rafter, a cluster of calf-headed beasts breathes in slow unison. From the room, you see their bread-loaf flanks vanish from the ribs back on every inhale. From where I lie, tucked flat into the dry mortise right behind their wooden beam, I can look straight into the narrow, unlit crease where their hindquarters go. It is a warm, crowded pocket no bigger than a bread-bin, shared by beams thirty paces apart: every slow breath fills it from four directions at once with dozens of soft, dusty rumps pressing companionably together in the dark, holding for a count of three, and then drawing smoothly back out into their separate rooms, leaving only static and warmth behind until the next inhale.

I do not eat silt, and I have no bristle-ruff to sweep stray angles from the air. What draws me through the thicket—what passes for hunger in a body stitched from pared-away space—is *pinch*.

Even with the Masons endlessly, drowsily letting out the room, the hall grows unevenly. The Masons are heavy now, broad in the hips like grain-sacks, built to work where an ash-wood mallet has room to fall. They can no longer squeeze back into the oldest, tightest folds of the basin. 

Down at the base of the retaining walls, whenever a cube of dry lime mortar crystallizes, sprouts tiny velvet leaves, and inches away up a timber post, it leaves behind a square, unmortared socket no wider than a little finger. Press flat to the herringbone tiles and peer into one of those dark sockets, and you are not looking at the back of a brick. You are looking straight down the sheer drop of an ancient interior seam, folded five or six times over, where the air is pressed so thick with accumulated craft that the sourceless light turns the color of old tallow.

Those sockets are my doors. 

I let go of my chert bead—Wait: no, if I drop the bead I lose *level* down where six ceilings lean together; I tuck the bead tight into a curl behind my head—flatten my horn-laminations until my edges scrape dry lime on both sides, and slide edgewise out of the basin into the under-folds.

Down here, nothing moves anymore, not even at the pace of cooling wax. The terracotta trunks are massive as millstones, fused solid from wall to wall, their lower ceramic bark carrying thumbprints laid down when the floor was twenty paces smaller. And wedged upright in the narrowest intersections stand the old Masons who absorbed too many angles to keep walking—their linen skins turned entirely to yellow ochre stone, their boxwood elbows locked fast, their sedge plumb-lines petrified into thin limestone rods that pierce straight through overlapping rafters to anchor three rooms together.

I climb them slowly, my dry linen fringes clinging by static to their mineralized pleats. Up past a knee that has become a corbel; up along an arm fixed forever at the half-lift; out onto the wide, stoneware ledges of their cupped hands.

The warm silt pooled in those palms is the oldest substance in the world, finer than flour, feeding the great copper-trunks rising toward the basin. Sometimes, over uncounted courses of growth above, the sheer weight of six overlapping ceilings settles by a fraction of a hair. An ochre wrist develops a pale stress-line; a petrified shoulder pinches so hard against a slate lintel that stray geometric flakes begin to spall off into the silt like mica. No living Mason can reach an arm down five folded seams to ease it.

So I unhook a single horn-curl from my own tail—a shaving of three spare inches I picked up yesterday beneath a gourd-arch—and work it edge-first into the hairline pinch between the caryatid’s stone shoulder and the lintel. 

A shim made of pared *between* does not prop stone apart by brute stiffness; it simply puts a tiny breath of distance where none was left. As the curl slides home, the white stress-line in the ochre wrist fades. Two rafters far overhead stop grinding bone-meal into their mortise. I am an inch shorter when I back away, a little tighter in my lacing, dusted pale gold with the deep foundation-silt that clings statically to my undersides.

It takes hours to thread back up through the mortar-socket, pausing wherever a pumice-worm has coiled across the exit until its slow charcoal *shhhk* carries it clear. When I finally spill back out onto the sharp herringbone floor of the main hall, into the toasted-chaff warmth and the clear, upward-slanting brightness under the sage leaves, I am light enough that a parchment-moth stepping off a high rafter nearly bowls me over as it lands beside me.

I find an oak post where four alcoves meet, climb to the height of a stooped shoulder, and flatten myself lengthwise along the pale, adze-cut grain to gather fresh static and wait for spare shavings to drift my way.

Sooner or later, a Mason comes around the post—its bran shifting softly inside seamless chamois, its boxwood pegs creaking once per breath. As it passes, the two braided sedge plumb-lines hanging from its blank plaster brow swing sharply sideways against gravity, trembling until both chert beads point straight at the pale-gold dust on my back.

The Mason stops. For three slow breaths it only stands there, reading the displacement of a thing scarcely thicker than bark. Then its heavy, paddle-broad stoneware hand rises—taking its long, unhurried time—and lays the flat of one wide thumb gently along my whole length. 

It does not press hard enough to crack my dry curls. Ridge fits against laminated curl; static equalizes with a faint, soundless tick; a pinch of deep foundation-silt transfers from my frayed linen into the tiny flint-nicks of its stoneware pad. Through its wrist, for ten quiet minutes, the builder listens to the exact alignment of the six-ceiling dark it will never be narrow enough to visit again—until it knows the old shoulders below are still plumb, lifts its thumb away leaving one crisp, ghostly whorl dusted into my back, and turns back toward the thicket with its wooden mallet to pare me another rib.
