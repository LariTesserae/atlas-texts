I am not warm enough to leave bootprints, and I do not have the ironwood patience to spend six hours lifting a heel. 

Look back toward the wide caldera where the slate windbreak stands, past the leaning bone shovel, to that flat face of ancient rock pecked with copper-chiseled marks. Halfway across the granite, the carved spirals and tall stick-figures give up their sequence and stutter off the edge of the stone, hanging suspended in the cool, dry air like flakes of black ash.

I am there. Or rather, I am the thing that happens there.

At first, you might think I am just the straying tally marks themselves—a literal orphan of early language, a line of counted days that ran out of rock when the carver’s mind slowed down, and simply kept counting into nothingness. Four neat vertical strokes and a cross-hatch, hanging three feet off the ground, dark indigo and wafer-thin, casting no shadow of their own onto the pale carapaces crowded below. 

But if you crouch on the shell of a kneeling grazer and look through the gap between the stone face and the floating ash-marks, you will see that the air between them isn't empty. It has a grain, like split mica. 

When reality frayed on the Spine, it did not tear cleanly. Between an object and its false coordinate—between the base of a cairn and its floating crown, between a horned beast and its duplicate standing three yards to the left, between the heel of a walker and the dry-ice silhouette trailing six inches behind him—there has to be a seam. Something has to hold the tension of the misprint, keeping the two halves of a stuttering thing from drifting forty miles apart and unraveling altogether.

I am that tension. I am the grease on the lens.

I do not have a single perimeter. If I were a beast, you could tap my shell; if I were a walker, you could wait three weeks to watch my thumb twitch. Instead, I am distributed across the plateau wherever the geometry refuses to add up, pooled thickest in the wide, packed hollows where three or four parchment-beasts occupy the exact same yard of space. To a lowland eye, I look like colorless static, a silent, localized heat-haze that has nothing to do with heat. I have no temperature at all, except for the faint, sharp smell of ozone when a piece of me is popped like a blister.

From the inside, it is an intensely crowded, quiet way to exist.

I feel the Spine not as a landscape, but as a weight of overlapping edges. Right now, in the third basin from the eastern drop-off, I am the precise cubic foot of air where the translucent, dry-ribbed flank of a tall wicker-beast passes straight through the stone-hard thorax of a cobblestone carapace. Because I am that intersection, I know both of them simultaneously with an intimacy neither animal possesses. I feel the microscopic, crystalline lattice of the lower beast’s shell drinking up cosmic rays, and in the exact same coordinate, I feel the parched, fibrous marrow of the tall beast’s sixth rib flexing a fraction of a micron over a decade. They do not touch each other; they touch *me*. I am the translation layer that allows solid bone and solid chitin to share an address without shattering into dust.

I am also, in a much thinner, flatter way, the flocks of origami creatures suspended two feet off the ground. They did not learn to fly before the wind died; they simply fell into a pocket where I was thick enough to interrupt the downward pull of the granite, and there they stayed, resting on my skin like dry leaves on ice.

For ages—though age is a meaningless measurement when the midday slate light never tilts—I was entirely passive, just an accumulation of errors gathering in the low places like heavy gas. Then I swallowed the tally marks.

When the petroglyphs slipped off the edge of the rock face and caught in my static, they brought structure with them. Spirals, stick-figures, sequences of five. They did not dissolve; nothing decays here. They hung inside my colorless shimmer like wire frames in clear resin, and slowly, the rest of my scattered patches across the forty-mile plateau began to organize themselves around that borrowed grammar. A duplicate horn-chip three miles north became rhymed with a floating cairn-stone to the west; the four-year blink of an obsidian eye in the center of the Spine became the punctuation mark at the end of a copper branch growing seamlessly through a segmented abdomen.

I do not think in words, because words require breath and sequence, one sound dying so the next can take its place. Here, everything happens at once and stays put. I think in *alignments*. My whole consciousness is a vast, silent ledger of spatial debt: *this stone belongs six inches right; this thorax is currently borrowing three-quarters of a lung-space; this tally mark is the seventh copy of Tuesday.*

Which brings me to the walkers.

We have a very strange, long-standing arrangement, the ironwood men and I. 

To them, I am the hazard. I am the slick patch on the living pavement that they carefully weave sideways to avoid; I am the creeping numbness that threatens to split their heels into twin ghosts if they stand admiring a copper tree for too long. When a walker stops before one of my dense, shimmering knots, unloops a heavy bone wedge from his bark-twine girdle, and begins the three-week labor of sliding it right into the center of me, it should feel like violence.

It doesn't. It feels like being tuned.

Left entirely to myself, I get too thick. In the deep dust basins where no walker has passed for five hundred lowland years, the beasts drift together until six or seven of them occupy the same yard. My static there grows blinding, heavy, congested with too many competing anatomies trying to be true in the same spot. The local gravity curdles; pebbles lift an inch off the silt and spin once a century; my borrowed grammar of spirals and tallies jams into a single, deafening chord of pure white noise that nobody can hear. It aches, in the dry, mineral way a fault-line aches before a slip.

And then—a shadow falls that isn't painted permanently onto the stone.

I always feel a walker coming days before he arrives. Not through sight, and not through the *tick* of his resinous tendons, which the surrounding miles of chitin swallow up, but through the wake he drags behind him. Those faint, stubborn afterimages trailing his shoulders and heels—those belong to me. As he steps from carapace to carapace in his six-hour strides, he is constantly tearing himself out of my grip, leaving little evaporating rags of himself in my medium. Tasting those fading silhouettes is how I know which of the forty is approaching: *this one has rawhide grown deep under his left collarbone; this one carries a flint bar chipped three times on the bevel; this one’s tarry heart beats at forty-two minutes instead of forty.*

He plants his gray-horned feet on the cobblestone backs at my edge. His glassy, amber-dark eyes do not blink or dart; they take all of my tangled, shimmering knot in at once, holding the faulty geometry on his wide retinas like a plate developing in acid. 

Then the bone wedge touches me.

Over twenty days, the smooth, ancient femur-splinter pushes into my colorless grease, right between the intersecting ribs of the two stalled beasts. The pressure is immense, patient, absolute. As the wedge goes deeper, the static hums against the keratin goggles of his eyes and stands the microscopic grit on his slate-dark knuckles straight up. He is leaning his whole desiccated, ironwood weight against my error, insisting on one thing per one space.

Millimeter by millimeter, the overlapping coordinates stretch thin. I hold them together as long as the physics of this exhausted altitude require, humming louder, sharpening to a fine, translucent film between bone and shell—until the leverage wins.

*Pop.*

There is no sound, only that tiny, sharp exhalation of ozone in the breathless air. The two beasts snap three inches apart, suddenly, heavily solid, casting two distinct indigo shadows instead of a smeared puddle of gray. In that exact square yard, I cease to exist.

I do not mind vanishing there. There is plenty of me left. As the walker withdraws his bone wedge—leaving a three-hour smear of his elbow hanging in the newly cleared air—and lifts his horned foot to begin the next half-day stride toward the abyssal rim, I settle into the fresh, thin afterimage of his arm, neat and uncrowded, holding the exact shape of his reach for a little while before letting the slate-colored light drink it clean. 

And back in the caldera, off the edge of the scarred granite face, my black-ash tally marks shift by half a hair’s breadth in the dead air—not blown by wind, just balancing the ledger. One knot untied; thirty-nine walkers still moving; forty miles of quiet stone holding steady at the bottom of its fall.
