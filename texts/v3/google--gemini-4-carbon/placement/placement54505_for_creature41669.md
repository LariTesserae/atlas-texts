I am not on the surface. I have no eyes for the tarnished pewter noon or the plum bruise of dusk, though I know when the light changes because the sap above me thickens slightly and slows its lateral draw. 

You would find me forty feet down, below the warm peat, below the braided, living stolons that flinch when cut, right at the threshold where the active, pumping vascular system of the world gives way to the drowned, timber-flesh dark. Here, the water has no tea-colored clarity; it is heavy, tepid, and black with dissolved tannins, tasting of iron and centuries of steep. There is no air. What little oxygen trickles down here is carried deliberately by deep-diving breather-roots—pale, spongy conduits the thickness of hogsheads that hang down into the flooded galleries like drowned pillars, bleeding microscopic films of gas into the dark so their own tips won't suffocate.

I am what hums under their heels.

Not the thing that tears open the sink-rots—that is nothing alive at all, just the blind, bloated physics of trapped methane finally ripping through rotten leather-mats a half-mile deeper than I ever go. When a pocket gives way down there, I feel the sickening drop in pressure hours before the surface sighs, and I turn my bulk away from the rising sourness. 

I am fifty feet of segmented, hydrostatic muscle, broad as a living dome and flattened top-to-bottom so I can slide horizontally between the ancient, compressed layers of cellulose without wedging myself stuck. I have no bones—bones require calcium, and down here every stray molecule of mineral is hoarded fiercely by the roots overhead. Instead, I am stiffened by rings of dense, cured cartilage and pressurized chambers of lymph. My skin is pale gray, blind, and velvety with thick pastures of sulfur-eating bacteria that slick my flanks as I move. 

To move at all is an act of slow, hydraulic leverage. I anchor my hind-flukes gently against two breather-pillars, expand my forward rings to part the waterlogged mat ahead of me, and pull my mass forward ten feet an hour. That is the low, resonant vibration the walkers feel in their sternums: the long, rhythmic *thrum* of ten tons of muscle equalizing fluid from chamber to chamber, coupled with the deep, grinding purr of my rasping plate.

I am the plumbing’s keeper, though I do not know I am keeping anything. I am simply hungry for what the surface has forgotten.

Over millennia, the sheer weight of the plateau presses dead plant matter downward, squeezing the galleries shut. Old root-bladders collapse; mineral-heavy silt—nothing rocky, just the microscopic, insoluble grit of ancient silica bodies from decayed leaves and the shed radulae of a billion snails—settles into the low channels and sets up like dense wax, choking the lateral flow of water. Above a choked gallery, the marsh sours. The sheet-water stagnates, turns overly acidic, and drowns the sweet-tuber terraces.

I smell that stagnation from leagues away. To me, a clogged channel smells rich—a concentrated plug of waxy, fermented deadwood and settled organic snow. I tilt my broad, shovel-blunt head toward the sour gradient and spend three days wedging myself into the narrowed seam. My mouth is underslung, a wide crescent lined with overlapping plates of dark, horn-like chitin cured hard by the very tannins I swim through. Slowly, methodically, I rasp the blockage away. 

*Hrrrrrm.* Pause. Swallow. Shift water from the fore-ring to the mid-ring. *Hrrrrrm.*

Above me, forty feet up through the wet sponge, five squat, slate-skinned people stop dead on a pale-pressed path. They drop into their low crouches. Ten broad, webbed hands press flat into the oxblood moss. Their third eyelids click shut so they can put all their mind into their wrists and ankles. 

I feel them, too, though I have no idea what a hand or a sky is. Along my dorsal ridge runs a lateral line of deeply recessed sensory pits, exquisitely tuned to pressure waves descending from the ceiling. Walking feet are usually too light to reach me—just faint, meaningless surface-static, like mist collecting on a leaf. Even the percussive crash of an unlearned, heavy-footed animal barely registers down here through forty feet of damp baffling. 

But they do not just listen when I rasp beneath them. Sometimes, when a marsh above has been drowning too long and they are relieved to feel a deep-borer finally working the seam directly beneath their flooded weirs, the eldest among them keeps her palm flat on an exposed, downward-plunging breather-root, drops her chin against her breastbone, and *answers*.

A tight, deliberate pulse of bass travels straight down the turgid wood of the pillar, bypassing the muffled peat entirely, ringing directly into the black water ten yards from my head. 

*Thrum-thrum. Pause. Thrum.*

It does nothing useful for them; they cannot steer me with sound, and they know it. It is purely greeting, or gratitude, or the quiet habit of a species that hates to let a shared room go unacknowledged. 

Yet when that small, tight vibration hits the water beside my sensory pits, I stop rasping for a moment. I hang suspended in the warm, pitch-black broth, my gill-frills pulsing slowly against the oxygen-film of the root-pillar. Nothing else in the deep makes a rhythm like that. Gas pockets roar and tear; settling timber groans once and goes silent; other borers emit long, monotonous pressure-wakes to warn me off their seam. Only the ceiling-things send down neat, bundled little knots of three or four beats, warm and harmless as falling drops.

For ten heartbeats—mine take a full minute each—we hold the pause together across forty feet of solid, living dark. Their palms on the upper bark; my flank brushed lightly against the drowned lower bark of the exact same organism. 

Sometimes I taste them more directly than that. 

Once every few years, as I work my slow, blind circuit beneath the margin of the great open marshes, the water filtering down from a breather-pillar changes flavor. Usually, surface-water carries only weak humic acids and the faint, bitter trace of defensive sap. Then, subtly, a rich plume drifts down through the vascular plumbing—not the foul, sudden rot of an animal that died untended in a pond, which stays trapped in the upper anaerobic slime, but something strange and clean. Nitrogen, already broken down and eagerly drunk by the roots above, leaking excess sweetness at the tips; a sudden, thrilling tang of sodium; and fainter still, already mostly stripped away by the hungry wood before it ever reaches me, the ghost of dissolved bone.

A hummock has been planted overhead. The hormone-paste has opened the gates of the root-nexus, and the forest is pumping a dead walker outward in every direction, sharing the rare salt of their blood down even to the deep breather-tips to pay for the oxygen that keeps the lower cellar alive. 

I curl my fifty-foot body around that sweet-tasting pillar and stay there for a month, grazing the sudden bloom of thick white bacteria that explodes across its bark in response to the feast above, resting my flat chin in the silt while, far overhead in the pewter dimness, two bound stalks begin their forty-year lean toward each other. Then I flex my fore-rings, brace my flukes, send a single deep *gurgle-hum* shivering up into the soles of whoever happens to be walking the new path above, and slide on into the older dark to keep the water moving.
