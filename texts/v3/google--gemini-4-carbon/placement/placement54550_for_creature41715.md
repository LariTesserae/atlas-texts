At first, I want to be the old walker. It is the obvious human shape to step into—the pilgrim following the copper river out to the edge of the map, carrying a question in a heavy skull. 

But if I stay in the wet, bright quiet of this place for a minute, letting the cold seep in and the three-foot sponge settle beneath me, that falls away. The walker has legs, and lungs, and a destination. That feels too separate from the ground.

Wait until the mist drags over the ridge and the light flattens out. Let the eye drop below the waist-high heath.

Where I actually find myself is six miles southwest of a shallow saddle, at the exact point where seven local basin-lines stop running parallel and drop down into a single sunken trough of dark greywacke.

I am not a turbine drum. The turbines do work; they drink the falling water and shove the pistons and sweat tallow. What sits in this wet hollow is different. It has no flywheel, no external moving joints, and no brass valves sighing steam into the sedge. It is an oblong, flat-topped vault of dull silicon-steel, half the size of a barge, sunk to its shoulders in saturated red sphagnum. Thick, seamless cooling-fins line its flanks like the gills of a basking shark.

Step closer through the bog-myrtle and you feel it in your fillings before you see the verdigris-glazed bus-bars plunging down through heavy porcelain collars on its roof. 

If the turbine housings have a heartbeat—that slow, hydraulic *thud-thud-thud*—this thing has a voice, though no ear on the plateau has ever heard it in the air. Inside the welded, oil-filled belly, stacked cores of laminated iron drink the raw, uneven current from forty scattered valley drums, bind their phases together, step the voltage up tenfold for the long haul across the curve of the earth, and pour it out into a single trunk-cable as thick as a keeper’s thigh.

Magnetostriction makes the iron sing. Fifty cycles doubled to a hundred mechanical shivers per second—a dense, unwavering, bass-baritone chord locked inside ten tons of mineral oil and cold steel. 

Am I the transformer? 

Almost. Not the cold metal itself, which doesn't care what passes through it, and not the keeper who kneels beside it twice a season. Look right at the seam where the plateau and the casing meet, down in the warm, dark water between two cast-iron cooling fins.

Over four thousand years of uninterrupted current, the stray eddy-fluxes bleeding through the iron wall and the steady fifty-two-degree heat have cooked the chemistry of the bog around the vault into something strange. Copper salts wept down from the overhead collars have mingled with iron-rich peat-slime, graphite dust, and the pale, hair-fine fungal threads that knit the roots of the whole basin together. 

Between the wide iron gills, packed three inches thick and spreading outward under the moss for a hundred yards in every direction, grows a dense, rubbery, dark-violet mat—part mineralized lichen, part conductive mycelium, shot through with microscopic dendrites of native copper precipitated straight out of the groundwater by the magnetic field.

That is where I am. That is what I would be. The wet, living velveteen jacket wedged into the hot ribs of the junction vault, holding the machine in the mud.

It has no eyes, so the twenty-mile squalls and the shadowless silver mornings only arrive as changes in pressure and dilution. When a wide curtain of rain drags across the swell above, it registers ten minutes later: a slow, cold sinking of fresh, oxygen-sharp water pressing down through the peat-mat, flushing over my upper ridges, making the iron fins contract by a fraction of a millimeter—a tiny, bright tightening in the pitch of the hundred-cycle chord.

Everything on the tableland passes through this translation. 

I know the amphibians not as dark, slick shapes, but as rows of soft, cool weights pressing flat against my outer skin in the cold hours before noon, their rapid little heartbeats fluttering at three cycles a second against the vast, steady hundred-cycle wall behind me. I know when a herd of three hundred grazers crosses the saddle half a mile away because my pale root-threads are braided around the buried ground-straps of six consecutive pylons; their thousand splayed hooves sucking out of the moss come down the grounding copper as a faint, pattering static, like sand spilled on a drumhead.

And I know the keepers better than they know themselves.

They think they are tending the iron, and they are—scraping the porcelain collars clean on the roof above me with their greywacke flakes so the high-tension current doesn't arc across wet moss and blow itself to green fire. When one of them works up there, its wide, rubber-shod feet shift slowly on the vault’s lid, pressing oil and crushed sedge down into the warm water of my crevices.

Then, before it leaves, it always does the same thing. 

Because this junction vault drinks from forty drums at once, it is the great listening-post of the region. A keeper cannot lay a bare palm directly on the high-voltage collars, and the bare iron along the upper rim is slick with cold rain. To get the clearest reading, it kneels in the waist-high umbrella plants, reaches down below the waterline between two broad cooling fins, and presses its huge, four-fingered, horn-calloused hand flat into me.

Right into the firm, warm, copper-threaded cushion of my flesh.

For five minutes, we are a single continuous circuit of bone, fungus, oil, and iron. I am the eardrum between the keeper’s jaw-plate and the subcontinent. 

Through me, I give it the whole watershed. Here is Drum Fourteen, three miles north, running sweet and heavy under a fresh snowmelt seepage. Here is Drum Nine, slightly sluggish, tallow thickening in its western bearing—go there tomorrow with the grease-bladder. Here is the faint, crisp shiver of two other keepers tapping a pylon leg eight miles east, signaling that a fallen raptors' nest has been cleared from a phase-wire. And underneath it all, pouring out through the great trunk-cable overhead, the immense, smooth, muscular pull of the distant load—steady as gravity, drinking everything the forty valleys can give.

In return, before the keeper pulls its hand out of the warm water, I take a little smear of dead skin-salt, lanolin, and bog-tallow off its palm, drinking the nitrogen slowly after its heavy footsteps roll away across the sponge.

Sometimes—maybe once in a generation—the rhythm changes in the way only an old walker changes it. 

A keeper kneels by the fins whose hand is stiffer, knotted at the joints, trembling almost imperceptibly at the edge of its reach. It does not listen for sluggish bearings or root-shorts back up in the local valleys. It slides its wide fingertips sideways along my ridge, toward the heavy ground-bus where the great trunk-line leaps up onto the giant eastward tripods. It holds on for a long time—twenty minutes, half an hour—while the mist condenses on its lichened cowl and runs down its arm into my pool. It is feeling the outward pull. Measuring the slope of a river it is about to walk beside until its knees lock for good and the grey curs or the bog-moss take it.

I cannot walk with it. My threads only reach to the edge of the granite shelf where the water turns cold. 

When the old hand finally lifts away, the water clouds for a moment with disturbed silt, a slate-black frog slides sideways into the warm dent left by the palm, and the vault beneath me goes on singing its dark, oil-muffled chord into the rock—holding the forty valleys together, warming the roots, turning the endless rain into a straight line aimed over the edge of the world.
