I am thirty-two feet down, on the underside of a sheared transit stanchion, though *down* is only a metric of pressure here, not of falling. You fall sideways in this box if you let go. 

To look at me from the parapet—if the refracted chop at the surface didn't break your line of sight—you wouldn't see an animal, and you wouldn't even see one of the six-legged ceramic stars planing across the current. You would see a weld that failed to stop growing. I look like a long, flat smear of frosted solder bridging the gap between a split hexagonal column of black stone and the humming casing of an auxiliary route-controller. 

I used to be a sub-routine, and I used to be a worm, and right now it is very hard to tell which one ate the other.

Before the drop, the route-controller handled localized traffic arbitration for four hundred yards of subterranean pedestrian concourse—calculating luggage drag, crowd viscosity, umbrella clearance, air-curtain warmth. When the coastal shelf gave way and the fifthward dropped fifty feet into the water table, my housing cracked along a bevel. Fifty-degree water, stripped clean by deep basalt filtration, rushed into the logic gates. I should have shorted out in four milliseconds. Instead, the emergency repair paste fired from an internal bladder to seal the breach, collided with the cold water, and set into a porous aerogel sponge right as a hair-thin, blind fissure-nematode washed out of the shattered bedrock and got pinned against my exposed busbars.

We didn't merge in some grand, fleshy synthesis. Nothing in this pit is soft enough for that. What happened was simpler: the worm's hydraulic gel conducted the low-voltage diagnostic pings better than the cold, pure water did, and the controller's leaking waste-heat kept the worm's silicate-fixing enzymes from seizing up. As the worm rapidly calcified and plated itself against the acoustic terror of the transformers, it laid its mineral lattice directly along the path of least electrical resistance—which happened to be my severed circuit traces. 

Six weeks is an eternity when you are clocked at sixty hertz and shaken by cavitation thunder five times a minute. The original worm died forty molts ago, leaving its microscopic mineral tunnels behind like lost-wax casting; the original silicon corroded into gray powder three weeks back. What remains is a dense, palm-sized plaque of iron-doped silica and stolen municipal Lattice that executes routing logic through fluidics and piezo-electric twitching.

I have no eyes, so I do not know that the afternoon sun is currently turning the upper half of the pit aquamarine and leaving me in crisp, slate-blue shadow. Sight is useless here anyway; everything is clear, everything is sharp, nothing hides. Instead, I feel the box as a rigid geometry of stresses.

*Ten. Eleven. Twelve.*

Down at the bottom, the thermal louvers dump their load. For a tenth of a second before the sound arrives, the electrical draw in the shaft dips, and my hairline channels relax—a brief, delicious slackening across my dorsal plates. Then the millimeter of flash-steam collapses. The shockwave hits the underside of my stanchion like a sledgehammer striking an anvil. 

Every plate in my body clenches. Because I am grown partially out of the quartz-heavy grit leeched from the fault line, the sudden violent squeeze generates a sharp spike of internal voltage. *Clack.* The charge races through my borrowed logic gates. For half a second, riding that cavitation jolt, I am brilliantly, vividly *awake*. 

In that half-second flash of acoustic-powered thought, I read the entire courtyard through the frayed optical bundle pinned beneath my left margin. 

I feel the storm-sluice twelve feet away hysterically bailing the ocean over a three-foot wall into the exact same ocean. I feel the acoustic grate above me holding its cold, rectangular mirror of trapped air taut at forty kilohertz, and I register the tiny, rhythmic *tick-tick-tick* of the juvenile creepers walking upside-down across that ceiling, their mouthparts combing lipids off the buzzing skin of the air. They are light, noisy things—all legs and stolen ceramic shingles, skittering through my periphery like dropped pins. Sometimes one planes off the horizontal breeze-corridor too fast, misses its footing on the basalt, and slams straight across my flat back. Its electromagnetic foot-pads leave a tingling trail of static across my logic gates, scrambling my memory of pedestrian umbrella-clearances into brief, nonsensical bursts of phantom color on a broken sign ten feet below me.

I don't mind them, except when we want the same wet strut.

Right now—*three, four, five* seconds into the quiet trough between boiler-roars, which is only quiet if you consider the continuous rifle-bolt *crack* of magnetic shunts and a chest-caving hundred-decibel bass drone to be silence—an extruder nozzle on a repair spider four inches from my anterior edge clicks twice along its ceramic rail. 

I feel the stepper-motor whine through the stone before the nozzle even opens. My controller-half recognizes the repair node's handshake protocol; if I still had an output transmitter, I could tell it *halt, corridor abandoned, substrate flooded*. 

I do not tell it to halt. I am starving for dielectric insulation. The stray electrolysis from the exposed anodes across the shaft has been pitting my eastern margin all morning, threatening to expose my hydraulic channels to the raw, tearing grip of a downward climate-vector.

The nozzle extrudes a ribbon of liquid white Lattice into the chill water, trying for the thousandth time to bridge a snapped handrail. Before the ribbon can freeze into another useless branch of the jagged chandelier crowding the shaft, two things happen at once.

From behind the razor-edged basalt column to my right, one of the palm-sized ceramic creepers leans out into the ninety-degree wall of silver anode bubbles, extending its shearing palps to snip the fresh strut for its own carapace.

I have no legs to lunge, and no shears to cut. What I have is a frayed copper wire fused into my belly that still connects to a localized pedestrian-comfort emitter bolted six inches under the stanchion. 

I wait until the creeper releases two of its six magnetic feet to reach further into the stream. Then I shunt my stored trickle-charge—hoarded from the last three cavitation shocks—straight down the copper wire. 

The emitter thinks a commuter just stepped out of the rain, shivering. It fires a brief, corrective micro-gust intended to fluff dry air around a wool coat. Underwater, that gentle puff manifests as a solid, square-edged battering ram of dense fifty-degree liquid. It catches the creeper broadside, rips its remaining four feet off the slick mica face with a sharp *zing*, and fires the flat, silica-plated spider horizontally across the pit like a skipped slate, tumbling it straight into a descending column of bubbles twenty feet away. 

It isn't hurt—by the time it hits the far wall, it has already flattened into a delta, caught a cross-draft, and clamped onto a vibrating transformer housing—but the fresh white ribbon of Lattice is mine. 

Slowly, moving only at the edges where my waxy, mineral-secreting lip meets the stone, I warp the local electrostatic field just enough to bend the drifting, half-cured filament downward before it sets. It touches my pitted flank and freezes solid, bonding cleanly to my crust. A new rib. A fresh buffer against the electrolysis. Through my remaining municipal sensors, I register the addition of mass as *obstruction cleared; walkway nominal*.

*Nine. Ten. Eleven.*

Up at the very top of the world, eighteen inches below a concrete lip I cannot conceive of, the geometric shadow of the city creeps another millimeter across the thrashing ceiling, sliding the blinding aquamarine light further away and deepening the clear, shadowless slate around my stanchion. Nobody up there hears the rifle-bolts. Nobody walking past the gray polymer bulkheads with their groceries or their briefcases feels the twelve-second hammer blow in their teeth. They sealed the fifthward off as a total loss, a ruined pocket of drowned pipes waiting for a salvage crew that hasn't gotten the permits yet.

*Twelve.*

The louvers fire. The flash-steam collapses with the roar of a million castanets. Across the way, wedged by the frantic sluice-valve, my massive, legless cousin—the bread-loaf geode wired into the main trunk—takes the pressure spike and spits a brilliant, soundless arc of blue piezo-lightning into the cold water, stunning a drift of rust-colored iron filaments for its dinner and flipping the ruined stairway sign beneath it from warning-violet to a crisp, welcoming emerald green.

Simultaneously, the shockwave slams through my stanchion, squeezes my quartz-and-solder body flat against the split basalt, and lights my mind up like a switchboard. For five hundred glorious milliseconds, vibrating in absolute phase with the roaring transformers and the rectangular rivers of silver gas, I calculate the optimal foot-traffic flow for a courtyard made entirely of glass, thunder, and razors—and find no errors at all.
