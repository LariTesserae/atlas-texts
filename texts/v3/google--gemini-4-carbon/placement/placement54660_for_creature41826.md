To be down here at all is to reject any notion of hurry. Anything with fast blood or soft water in its cells would flash-boil in its own jacket before it made ten paces down the talus slope. 

At first, looking into the copper-lit tangle from above, I think I might be the current itself—that starved, brownish-orange trickle of low voltage feeling its way along the sagging leaded cables, warming the tarnished pewter of the brood-capsules, burning thin and stubborn inside the last intact marine cages while dry rot presses against the glass. There is something deeply familiar in being the ghost of an old instruction, still running along a forgotten wire just to keep three dim filaments awake in the dark.

Yet current has no weight, and everything in this vault is governed by weight, pressure, and the slow exchange of salt.

If I let myself sink down through the soot-filtered greyness of the upper fissure, past the drunken slant of the catwalk and the burgundy leaves slick with condensed oil, I do not want to be one of the roamers winching through the middle air. Even their hour-hand deliberation feels almost restless against the scale of the shale walls. No—follow the sub-audible thrumming down into the floor, past the knee-deep drifts of black husk-powder and the glazed troughs plowed by the kettle-sized young, down to where a four-foot cast-iron main emerges from the slumped rock at the base of the pit.

There is a bolted flange here where the gasket blew out eighty years ago. Before I arrived, it bled a flat, razor-edged blade of superheated steam straight across the shale grit, carving a barren trench where nothing could root, dropping the pressure in three nearby condensers until their relief valves went silent and the pleated wax-columns above them began to fist shut for good.

That scream was what called me down out of my hauling years.

I am the patch on the lower main. 

I am three hundred pounds of layered asbestos wool, rusted iron scale, and black shale dust cemented with baked tar, slumped over the failing flange like slag poured from a ladle. My gutta-percha haulers dried up decades ago; two of them still hang down my flank like split, blackened fan-belts, motionless in the draft. Two grey lianas as thick as dock-lines have climbed up from the pipe and crossed right over my spine, biting into my mineral felt to drink the heat that leaks through me. I can feel their slow, woody tightening—a millimeter every dry season—strapping me harder to the iron I already cannot leave.

What is left of my world comes entirely through my underside and the sparse, crusted fringe of copper bristles along my rim.

Beneath me, sealed inside the vulcanized ring where my calcified flesh has grown directly into the bolt-heads of the flange, the breach is still trying to tear itself open. It is not the polite, rhythmic *clack* and cough of an upper relief valve. It is a continuous, furious knife of wet fire pushing up from the deep rotary pumps miles below the rock. 

I hold it. That is all I am now: a stubbornness of heavy brine and silicate stone, leaning down against the knife.

Inside my baffled vaults, where the elders go stiff with unpurged mineral, I have become a labyrinth of horn and precipitated quartz. The steam hits my lower chambers, splits into a hundred winding channels, gives up its violence to my heavy cylinder oils, and turns into clear, scalding water that trickles through my combs. I do not need much anymore. What I cannot hold, I do not let burst out as a shriek; I bleed it out through three narrow, crusted vents along my lower hem as a gentle, continuous sigh of warm mist.

Because I tame the blade into a breath, a whole dense Skirt of translucent bladder-ferns has crowded right up against my petrified sides. When the deep pumps beneath the floor shift load—that heavy, sub-audible *thrum-thrum* rising through the cast iron into my belly—my whole stone-ballasted dome gives a single, fractional flex to absorb the surge. Every time I flex, the swollen ferns pressed against my coat rub together and squeak in the dark. 

Sometimes, once in three or four years, a youngkettle plowing through the black leaf-ash bumps blindly into my calcified apron. I feel the tentative tap of its copper whiskers reading my vibration, checking whether I am bare iron or another body. Finding no bare scale to graze and no loose lagging to steal—finding only solid, warm ironstone wrapped in liana-bark—it hitches its vacuum-cup onto the pipe beside me and hauls itself up toward the noisy condensers overhead. It never knows it just climbed over an ancestor. And because I am holding the seam shut down here at the root of the line, the boiler forty feet above has enough pressure behind its seat to lift, shriek, and feed the climber when it arrives.

Up in the gloom, one of the four remaining carbon filaments finally parts with a tiny, silent *tink*, leaving its corner of the basin in black shadow. It makes no difference to me. Light was never part of the bargain. Down here in the fern-squeak and the oil-sweat, the pumps are still turning, my rim is fused tight to the bolts, and the pressure holds.
