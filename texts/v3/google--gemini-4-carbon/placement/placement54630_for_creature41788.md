At first I try to stand on the salt-bitten heath above it, boots planted in the tough, matted roots of sea-thrift and stunted gorse, waiting to feel the ground thump through my soles. Up here, the weather has no count. The northern wind tears sideways across the lowland in ragged, meaningless gusts, smelling of sheep-wool, wet peat, and open brine. Forty feet of dark limestone is enough to swallow almost everything; what reaches the turf is not a sound at all, only a faint, sub-audible shudder in the jawbone if you lie flat and press an ear to a frost-split outcrop. A pulse like an animal in winter torpor, beating five times a minute under the grass.

I follow the scrub down toward the dry landward crack where the otter slips in, thinking I might belong on the threshold—something crouched at the pinch-point where the surface dark meets the deep dark, listening from a safe distance. 

Even in thought, a human body feels instantly wrong for the space beyond that seam. Too tall, too hot, shedding lint and skin-flakes, breathing out warm carbon dioxide in nervous, irregular clouds. To crawl through that narrow clay passage and stand on the floor of the bell would be an act of vandalism before I even took a step. When the wave hit and the chamber screamed, my soft, water-heavy bulk would cast a massive, ragged dead-spot across the bouncing soundwaves—a clumsy eclipse thrown directly over the whale ribs and the slate wall. Worse, when I left, the pale thing tucked under the ceiling would have to drop down and spend weeks scrubbing the grease of my boots and the microscopic ruin of my passing off the clay just to bring its room back into tune. Those earlier people knew how to sit still in the dark, how to fit flat slate so cleanly it wouldn’t click in a gale, and—best of all—they knew how to vanish four thousand years ago and leave their needles behind. There is no room down there for another pair of hands.

So I let go of hands, and test smaller shapes along the margins. 

Not the otter; the otter belongs to the kelp-forests and holts, treating this deafening vault as nothing more than a cold tavern for sweet water on its way somewhere else. Not the rock doves high on their ledge; their gift is stupor, tucking their heads into oily feathers to sleep through an apocalypse that doesn't concern them. If I am going to be in this world, I don’t want to sleep through the twelve-second bell, and I don’t want to pass through it. I want to be somewhere inside the mechanism of the chord itself.

Follow the pale, bruised light upward, then—past the ferns taking three seasons to uncurl a single dark frond, past the white blister clamped tight to the liverworts, past the ruffled breasts of the doves—into the crooked, ten-yard throat of rock where nothing with legs or wings can hold on.

Out at the bottom of that unseen chimney, the sea is an ugly, disordered violence. Heavy green water piles onto the submerged basalt shelf, full of torn bladderwrack, grit, broken crab-shell, and drowning foam. Left to itself, it would surge straight up the shaft, pour over the lip, drown the mosses in freezing brine, and sweep the whale ribs and bone needles into a tangled heap against the western wall. 

Something in the twist of the chimney has to stop the water while letting the breath through. Something has to take a blind, heavy shove of ocean and sharpen it into a note clean enough to see by.

That is where my weight settles. Halfway up the crooked flue, six yards above the basalt shelf and four yards shy of the cave’s ceiling, where the ancient currents dissolved the softer limestone into a sharp dogleg and exposed a thick, stubborn seam of black chert. 

I am the projecting lip at the bend of the throat—a curved, knife-edged baffle of flinty silica, harder than the grey lime-rock rooted around me, jutting two-thirds of the way across the shaft. 

I live split down the middle between two completely different worlds.

My underside faces the sea, and it knows no peace at all. Even in the nine-second intervals when the chamber behind me drops into heavy, ringing silence, my chin and belly are loud with messy drainage. Sea-water sluices back down the vertical rock below me, gurgling out of crevices; foam hisses as millions of cold bubbles pop in the dark; tiny acorn barnacles cemented into my lower pits click their wet plates shut against the draft. Then the next swell gathers on the basalt shelf, seals the bottom of the chimney, and rams upward. Tons of solid, green, sand-laden water leap up the dark shaft and slam squarely against my curved underside. I take the dead weight of the crest—feel the grit scouring my stone skin slick, feel a strand of torn kelp slap and slide away—and force the heavy water to break its back and fall roaring down into the sea again. 

Only the air trapped ahead of the wave gets past me, squeezed through the narrow gap above my spine at hurricane speed, carrying a torn ghost of atomized mist. As that compressed blast shears across my sharp upper bevel, it splits into violent eddies—the exact way breath splits across the lip of an iron whistle. 

I am what turns the shove into the *boom*. And two seconds later, when the defeated wave drops away into the ocean trough and yanks the air violently backward out of the cave, the wind drags across the rougher, stepped grain of my reverse face, tearing itself into that high, whistling banshee shriek.

My inward-facing slope never feels solid water, only the freezing vapor settling and sliding back, and the weak, grey daylight that bounces off the churned white foam at the cliff-base, strikes my wet flank, and bends dimly around the corner into the bell. Just enough bruised illumination for the black-green tapestry on the walls below to drink, and no more.

Once—five or six thousand winters ago, before the first slate was carried down from the beach, when surface frost crept deeper into the cliff than usual—a single palm-sized spall popped loose from my upper edge with a sharp crack and skittered down into the chamber. Someone found it in the grey dust, liked the glassy black density of its fracture, and struck slivers from it beneath the whale ribs to scrape rawhide and shape bone. A piece of my own flank still lies ten paces from the water basin, thinned to a translucent blade. Whenever a dove’s wing-draft or a heavy drop of condensation nudges that sliver a hair out of alignment, I don’t feel it, of course—stone doesn’t reach after what it drops—but twelve seconds later, the echo coming back up the dome has a tiny, dry buzz at its edge, until the pale thing drops from the wall on the next boom and tucks my missing shard neatly back into its bed.

We never touch, the pale thing and I. Its edge stops three feet below my inner threshold, where the scouring wind gives way to the first plush velvet of the liverworts. To its grey sensory pits, I have no shape of my own; I am behind the light, the blind spot at the mouth of the horn from which the whole sharp, three-dimensional cavern flashes into existence seven thousand times a day.

Yet we keep the room between us. Down on the floor, it shims the loose slate with stiffened flesh so the stones won't click, and polishes the bacterial film from the ribs so the ivory rings true. Up here in the dark throat, taking the green punch of the northern sea in my chest so only the mist and the measure get past, I hold my edge—millimeter by silica millimeter, century after century—so the pitch of its world never slips flat.
