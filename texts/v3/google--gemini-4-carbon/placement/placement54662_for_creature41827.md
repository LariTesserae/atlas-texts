I would not be out on the rim where the iron pines drink the gale. There is too much raw sky out there, too much pale nothingness waiting just past the granite lip, and I have no wish to feel the drop-off in my knees. Nor am I built for the deep, airless dark down at the bedrock where the giant worms digest the history of the plateau in centuries-long swallows. 

You would find me at the seam where the town ends and the chest-high jungle begins, walking the wet stone trenches rather than the tin skyways overhead. 

If I have human hands here, they are thick-knuckled, plum-flushed, and permanently darkened at the creases with tallow-grease and black peat-stain. My chest has the same deep, vaulted cage as the rest of the basin-born, housing lungs that sip the mineral air without panic, and I smell like everyone else: boiled wool, turpentine bark-tea, and hot brass. 

My work is out along the perimeter conduits—the three-mile loop of insulated steam-mains, pneumatic return lines, and stone cable-channels that stitch the outer factory sheds to the turbine-houses on the basin’s slope. They call us *line-walkers* or *pruners*, though neither word quite captures the negotiation of it. 

Most of the town stays dry in the corrugated catwalks above, moving from warm burrow to warm shed like blood through veins. Down on the cobbles and the boardwalks laid directly atop the ten-foot peat mattress, the world is submarine and green-choked. Walking here has a peculiar, springy physics; even through heavy, hobnailed boots, you feel the slight, sponge-dense give of ten thousand years of compressed roots beneath the flagstones. You learn early never to set a brass wrench down on the moss beside you. If you take your eyes off it for ten seconds, the emerald cushion-dome doesn't quite swallow it, not that fast, but the light is so pearlescent and shadowless, and the wet ironwood twigs are so tangled, that the metal simply ceases to be a separate thing from the wet dark. Every tool on my belt is tethered to my brass chatelaine by braided copper wire.

To walk the outer lines is to be a quiet listener to pressure and tension. 

I carry two main tools: a heavy, curved pruning-hook forged from oiled spring-steel, and a three-foot listening rod made of solid brass, flared at one end like an ear-trumpet. 

The alpine jungle wants our heat with a slow, crushing desperation. Left alone for a season, the python-thick steam-vines don’t merely wrap the municipal mains; they wind themselves into the expansion joints, force their woody tendrils under the felt-and-tar insulation jackets, and squeeze until brass relief-valves warp and gaskets blow. My job is to walk the gray, sweating corridors between the dwarf cloud-laurels and keep the truce. I don’t clear-cut—if you strip a pipe bare, the howling draft off the granite swells cools the steam into sluggish water before it reaches the tenement autoclaves. The vines *are* part of the insulation now. Instead, I prune selectively, slicing away the strangling leader-shoots just before a flange, coaxing the heavy growth to sleeve the straight runs while leaving the brass gauges bare to the mist.

When I slice through a thick vine, its sap doesn't run thin; it beads up slow and milky, steaming faintly in the cold air, smelling faintly of raw potato and crushed fern. Within minutes, before I have walked fifty yards down the trench, a moss-badger will haul itself out of the interlocking ironwood canopy on its curved claws, utterly indifferent to my stiff, waxed-wool bulk, to lick the warm, starchy bleed from the cut stump. Sometimes, when I crouch by a manifold to repack a weeping gland-nut, one will stay curled around the neighboring uninsulated elbow-joint, breathing little white ghosts of vapor into my sleeve, its algae-green coat rising and falling against the hot iron while I work. We share the same small pocket of stolen warmth; neither of us wastes calories on startling.

Every hundred yards, I stop, wipe the constant silver sweat of condensation from a cast-iron stanchion or a bearing-housing, press the tip of my brass rod to the metal, and lay the flared bell against the bone behind my ear.

Shutting my eyes in the muffled wet, the whole basin opens up inside my skull as an architecture of pitch and rhythm. 

Out in the thin air, twenty feet away is silence. Inside the iron and stone, everything speaks. Through the brass rod, I hear the *shhh-clack, shhh-clack* of the traction-cable sliding over its sheaves right beneath my boots, and beneath that, the low, buttery purr of well-greased bronze bearings. Farther down the metal’s memory, felt in my jawbone rather than my ear, is the immense, slow, sub-audible *thrum-thrum* of the pine-cone turbines turning out on the invisible rim. And threading across it all is the quick, bright morse of the town talking to itself without wasting breath: *tink-tink, pause, tink* from a machinist four terraces up asking for two more pounds on the manifold; two flat, heavy raps from the shed boss acknowledging.

I listen for the wrong notes. A dry, singing whine in a cable-trench means the tallow-drip has clogged, or a colony of grease-backs downstream has abandoned a sump because an acidic pocket of peat-water seeped through the masonry. A ragged, fluttering hiss inside a steam-pipe means scale is building on a baffle. When I hear a bearing running dry, I unhook my brass mallet and tap three slow, resonant notes against the housing, followed by the two-rap signature of my sector. Half a mile away, in a warm, moss-roofed shed where giant felt-moths hang asleep from the trusses, a tender feels the vibration in the sole of his boot, reaches up without looking, and opens a grease-cock a quarter turn. Minutes later, lifting the heavy iron inspection grate at my feet, I watch the pale, blind shapes of the amphibians slip lazily back toward the fresh drip in the warm dark, keeping the iron clean in exchange for their dinner.

It is solitary work, hours spent waist-deep in wet beard-lichen and dripping cloud-belly, my core kept warm by the two copper charcoal-tins smoldering slowly against my ribs beneath my chalice-down vest. Yet it never feels lonely. The boundary between the built thing and the grown thing has dissolved too thoroughly here for loneliness to take root; you are simply one more dense, slow-moving organ inside a mile-wide creature hunkered down against the sky.

Wait—perhaps there are afternoons when the mist thickens until the terraced slate roofs vanish entirely, when even the silent ore-buckets overhead become invisible ghosts known only by the faint *hum* of their overhead wires, that I realize I might not even be the walker with the brass rod. 

Spend enough time listening to the junctions here, and it becomes easy to slip out of skin altogether. 

Sometimes, standing very still while a slate-colored bird tips silently off a porcelain insulator above me into the rushing grey river of cloud, I think I am closer to the junction-house itself—one of those squat, half-buried masonry vaults out where the granite whale-backs swell through the peat. Half brick, half living hummock, furred three inches deep in water-engorged velvet moss, with a pulsing shelf of tallow-colored fungus illuminating the brass pressure-gauge beside my felt-gasketed door. Inside my ribs, a single six-foot redirection wheel spins in silent, greased perfection, taking the raw, cold kinetic pull from the rim-turbines, turning it forty degrees, and sending it humming smoothly down toward the tenements so the stew-pots keep hissing and the burrows stay warm. Vines wrap my warm chimney-vent; marsupials sleep in the dry lee of my eaves; blind pale creatures keep my cellar sumps clear; once every two hours, a barrel-chested figure in waxed wool emerges from the fog, presses cold brass to my iron lintel to listen to my heart, nods once on finding it steady, raps twice in greeting, and disappears back into the wet green dark.

Whether I am the walker tending the seam, or the warm stone housing that holds the tension on the turn, my nature in this place is the same. I do not want to conquer the thin air or burn bright and brief against the cold. I want to be well-gasketed, heavily insulated, and useful. I want to hold the heat in, translate the relentless wind into steady pressure, and keep my pulse slow enough to match the iron pines turning in the mist.
