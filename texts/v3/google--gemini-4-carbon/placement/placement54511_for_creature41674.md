At first, there is only the pressure of the dark and the taste of lime. 

Not a body yet—just an awareness wedged tight between two courses of old kiln-brick where the heat stays longest. If I were here, I think I would begin as something that does not cross the open air at all. Not a swift, tearing the amber shaft in half; not even the boy near the collar with the wind on his shaved scalp. Too much sky in both of those. The pit’s gravity pulls inward and down, toward the places where things touch on four sides.

Maybe the blind white root of a caper bush, flattening itself like spilled tallow into a mortar seam, drinking the sweat of a stone counterweight one molecule at a time. That feels close—that slow, stubborn wedging into what was meant to be solid. Pushing grains of sand apart over twenty years just to hold on. 

No. Too deaf. In a drum like this, you have to be able to hear.

Shift three tiers down, behind the bulging belly of an abandoned firing chamber where the light arrives dead last, bruised to the color of steeped bark. There is a space between the outer structural wall and the inner brick lining—a rubble-filled jacket four inches wide, originally packed with ash and broken tiles to hold the kiln’s heat centuries ago. Over the generations, the ash has settled and washed downward with the slow creep of damp, leaving a vertical labyrinth of warm, dry, pitch-black flues that the people only know by the way their knuckle-raps sound hollow against the face-brick.

Wait—no, I am not inside the wall looking out. If I stay in the dark behind the brick, I miss the grease and the bone-trowels and the hemp. And the whole strange mercy of this place is how *handled* it is. How nothing is left to be merely wild or merely stone.

Come out into the tea-colored gloom. Down to where the sound changes from the dry *shhh-shhh* of linen to the bright, hollow surf of the bottom drift.

I think I am sitting with my spine pressed into the concave curve of a broken amphora big enough to hold a calf, half-sunk into the terracotta gravel like a beached boat. My legs are stretched out in front of me, half-buried in the warm red pebbles because the weight of them feels good on aching shins. 

I am old—old enough that my middle fingers don't quite straighten when I open my hands in the morning, stiffened into permanent hooks from fifty years of jar-rims and rawhide stays. My name has changed twice since I was born, because I have moved down the wall as my knees lost their outward spring: once I was named for a high windy niche under the western cedar-collar where the hawk-chimes kept me awake as a child; then for a wide marriage-shelf near the big sweating limestone block on the fourth tier; and now they call me by a low, warm, step-over alcove six feet off the shingle, right beside the lime-crocks where a person doesn't have to climb to eat.

I am not mending ropes today. Someone else’s thumbs can take the skinning-scrape of dry hemp. Instead, across my lap lies a long, curved rib of cedar taken down yesterday from the third-tier catwalk—not rotten, because wood doesn't rot easily in this warm draft, just tired, compressed on its underside until the grain began to lift in dry, grey splinters. Nothing leaves the pit, and nothing is burned, so a retired beam has to become forty smaller things before it is allowed to turn to dust.

Beside my hip sits a bowl of dark, green-glazed stoneware, chipped on three sides, holding two inches of yesterday’s condensation and a handful of pale, papery skins shed by the big skinks up on the rafters. No—not skink skins, what would those do? Let that go. In the bowl is water, a lump of dove-tallow, and a pinch of pulverized vitrified brick, finer than flour, for polishing. 

Between my knees, I have wedged a piece of that old cedar, split down to the size of three fingers. In my right hand is a shard of black-fired kiln-brick from the deepest layer of the drift—the dense, glassy stuff from ten generations back that breaks with an edge like flint. *Scrape, pause, blow.* Thin curls of ancient, resin-dry cedar fall onto my linen kilt and slide off into the terracotta gravel with barely a whisper. 

I am carving a toggle-pin for the salt-basket line. It has to have a waist smooth enough not to fray rawhide under two hundred pounds of pull, and blunt, rounded noses at both ends so that when it swings loose in an updraft, it bounces off the nesting jars instead of cracking them.

Every few minutes, a toddler—my daughter’s boy, greased shiny with rue-fat so the red dust sticks to him like cinnamon on a wet plum—wades clumsily over my buried ankles, setting off a cascade of small ceramic chimes. He drops a potsherd onto my bare thigh, watches my face with wide, dark, pit-adapted eyes, and waits.

I pick it up without setting down my scraper. I don't even have to look at it long; my thumb knows the pit’s inventory better than my eyes do. Too porous—low-fired kitchenware from some surface village traded down eighty years ago, soft enough to crumble under a fingernail. Useless for shimming, useless for cutting. I click my tongue once—the flat, dull *tock* we use for *not yet*—and toss it gently six paces away toward the center of the oval, where the traffic is heaviest and the sliding gravel will grind it down into mortar-grout over the next forty winters. 

He laughs, a small, short burst pitched right under the rolling coo of three thousand doves, and scrambles off on all fours after a ringed civet that has just slipped out of a drainage pipe with sticky yellow yolk on its whiskers.

Why this body, and not the hawk-chime boy, or the civet, or the stone basin itself? 

Because down here at the bottom of the light, you get to feel the whole column of the world resting over you without being crushed by it. 

Look up—not with the neck bent back, nobody bends their neck like that here, just rolling the eyes upward under heavy lids. Forty paces of woven, pegged, mortared stubbornness climbs away into the amber haze. Every single brick up there is held in place only because it leans against its neighbor. There isn't an ounce of iron binding any of it. It stays up because someone’s grandmother wedged a curved sherd under a tilting lintel, and someone’s nephew tightened a rawhide lashing three turns when the dry north wind blew across the plain overhead and shrunk the pegs.

My bare left heel is resting out of the gravel, pressed flat against the base of the great central pier—solid mortared brick eight feet thick that rises straight through five tiers of scaffolding like the mast of a buried ship. 

Through the thick horn-pad of my heel, the pier talks.

I feel the heavy, soft *whump* of four dozen doves launching all at once from the third tier when a skink slips into the wrong tube. Two seconds later, the air-push of their wings reaches my face, smelling faintly of dry dander and warm clay. Behind that vibration is a steadier, tighter hum: someone—two tiers up, judging by the crispness of it—is driving an oak peg home with a limestone maul. *Thock. Thock. Thock.* Good sound. Solid seat. The mortar around that timber isn't crumbling; if it were, the blow would come down into my ankle bone with a mushy, double-edged *thud-slap* instead of a clean ring.

And higher still, too faint for the ears, felt only as a ghost of tension in the air pressure of the pit: the weather up on the grassless plain has shifted. The wicker baffles in the collar overhead have begun to creak in a slightly faster rhythm. Within an hour, cooler air will spill down the western curve, the green-rimmed basins on Tier Four will start sweating harder, and the erratic *plink... plink* to my left will quicken into a steady three-beat patter. Someone will need to swing the diversion spout over before the moss-mat below the big limestone counterweight gets flooded and slicks the footholds on the main ladder-run.

I don't get up to do it. I don't need to. 

I lean three inches to my left, pick up a fist-sized chunk of dense kiln-brick, and rap twice—*tack-tack*, pause, *tack*—against the hollow terracotta flue-pipe mortared into the side of the central pier. 

Thirty feet up in the golden dust, invisible behind two overlaps of drying racks and a curtain of trailing caper-vine, a bare sole lifts from a rung, reads the message, and raps once back with a heel. *Got it.* 

I go back to the cedar pin. *Scrape, pause, blow.* 

My thumb rubs the waist of the wood, testing the curve, rubbing a dab of dove-tallow and brick-flour into the grain until it shines like dark bone. In ten minutes I will hand it to the toddler, point upward toward the lowest hanging basket-hook, and watch him figure out how to wedge his fat little toes into the chipped frogs of the pier to reach it. 

And when my own bones finally stop bending altogether—when even the six-foot climb to the low alcove is one lintel too many—they won't haul me up through the cedar collar into that blinding, terrifyingly flat emptiness where the wind blows everything away. There is no room to bury anyone down here, and no fire to burn them. They will take me up one last time in the salt-basket at dusk, pass me gently out to the plainsmen waiting in the dark with their mules, and trade the weight of me—wrapped in old, frayed hemp cordage that can't hold a load anymore—for two sacks of quicklime and a bundle of raw cedar poles. 

Wait. Would they? 

Let me sit with that a second, feeling the smooth cedar pin between my stiffened fingers. Would people who don't let an egg-shell or a wood-shaving go to waste send forty-fivestone of lime and bone and water out into the wind? No—no, plainsmen wouldn't want dead pit-folk, and the pit wouldn't give up its calcium so cheaply. Yet forty warm bodies in a damp, closed drum cannot keep their dead beside the porridge-crocks. 

Ah. Look at the first sentence of the floor beneath my heels: *packed lime, broken pottery, and tamped ash going down further than anyone remembers.* And the quicklime traded down in walnut-sized chunks, fierce and hissing when it meets water. 

No mules. Nobody ever goes up through the collar, living or dead. 

At the very bottom, under three feet of singing terracotta pebbles, you pry up a patch of upright bricks, lay what is finished in a bed of dry, unslaked trade-lime, pour the week's brackish wash-water over it until the stones hiss and steam in the dark, and tamp the bricks back down on end before morning. In three years, if someone pries that spot open again to set a new pier-footing, there is nothing foul—only clean, white, crumbly mortar-stuff and brittle bone ready to be carved into awls, toggles, and beads for a child's greased throat.

Yes. That settles into place like a peg driven flush. 

I turn the cedar toggle over in my palm. It is nearly balanced now—smooth as a river cobble that has never seen a river, warm from my skin, smelling of bitter rue and four-hundred-year-old trees. Above me, a shadow slides across the rectangle of sky; the pottery chimes *chink-clack* in the sudden downdraft; fifty doves thunder across the amber shaft; the crickets hush for half a heartbeat; and I sit very still in the singing gravel, listening to the water drip into the bowls, holding my small piece of the rig until the roar comes back.
