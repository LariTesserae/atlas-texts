I am low to the ground, heavy in the forequarters, and older than the check-dam at the third eastern cleft, though younger than the lip of the horse-pan below the mercury braids. I know this because my teeth fit the older mortar differently. When I lay my jaw against the rim of the horse-pan to test it after the dry-wind season, the grain of the cement there has the tight, glassy set of flour gathered when the hemisphere’s dust-ring was wider by two thumb-breadths—a sleekness my own saliva never quite achieves, though I have chewed the white grit from the threshold a thousand midmornings.

Right now the sun is high enough to bleach the pink out of the pavement, and I am walking the long, bald grade between the torus pan and the split-boulder hollow. Nothing hurries in me. My feet are broad, fibrous pads damp only at their deepest cores; each step meets the limestone without a jar, spreading across old water-flutes and stray grit until the stone accepts my weight as part of its own settling. Even to myself I make almost no noise—only the dull, interior shift of shoulder- cartilage inside heavy hide, a sensation felt behind the eyes rather than heard.

On my back I carry four inches of layered crust. Near my hip-bones, where my reach cannot tidy it and no neighbor bothers to groom, three tufts of wire-sedge have rooted in a pocket of blown mica cemented during a mist-year. When the cross-basin wind leans against me, I feel those three tufts pull minutely at my carapace—tiny, silent levers telling me the wind has backed three degrees toward the saddle in the northern rim. That means the afternoon sweat in the north-facing clefts will start six hundred heartbeats late today, and the flat trickle beside the lone wax-stalk will dry before reaching its pan unless the lip it spills over is pared deeper before the shadow arrives.

So I turn slightly uphill.

A quarter-mile to my left, forty of the tall, flat-sided runners cross a gravel sweep. Even with my head swung low, their panic hits the edges of my vision like quartz struck open—harsh blocks of black and hot magenta stuttering across the glare. Coarse talk. Loud, ragged squares of warning that spill everywhere and mean only *teeth-upwind, wheel-east, run*. My upper pupils narrow a fraction, polarizing the glare off the limestone until the herd’s shouting dims to bearable grey flickers and the subtle things return: the faint violet flush of a glider’s wing-veins two hundred feet overhead where a warm column bends; the greasy, silver-hole shimmer of two armored browsers hugging a damp joint ten paces ahead of my nose.

The browsers scatter six inches sideways as my shadow slides over them, antennae tasting the dry, chalk-and-ozone reek of my leg-scales and finding nothing worth fleeing further from. I fold my forelegs—one, then the other, feeling the familiar pull in the thick skin behind my elbows—and settle my chest onto the sun-warmed rock.

The fissure here is narrow, blue-dark down in its throat, smelling faintly of cold stone far underneath the heat. It has not begun to sweat yet, though down inside the crack the chartreuse mat is already loosening its leathery edges in anticipation. Here is the fault: five days ago a dust-devil died against the wax-stalk up-cleft and dumped half a mouthful of coarse mica right into the throat of the runnel, and since then the mat has grown sideways over the blockage, welding the grit into a spongy dam. Left alone through three more afternoons, the water rising out of the cleft will miss the velvet groove entirely and sheet out onto this flat pink apron, where the sun will take it in ten minutes and leave nothing behind but a salt-ring and dead skimmers.

I drop my chin. My lower tusks meet the wedged grit.

Through the dense enamel, into the heavy bone of my lower jaw, comes the only music this valley retains: the private, interior *grrrk-shhhk* of stone answering tooth. Outside my skull the sound dies before it reaches my own knees, swallowed by the felt-thick air. Inside my jaw it is rich and distinct. Native limestone has a warm, chalky drag; wind-blown mica hisses sharp and brittle; old mason-cement rings high and tight against the root of the tusk, warning *stop, someone’s mouth shaped this before you*.

I pare back a quarter-inch of encroaching chartreuse mat—lifting the severed strip neatly with the two fingers of my upper lip and tucking it into the back of my mouth, bitter and rubbery and cool—and then I draw my right tusk three times down the limestone flute. Pale powder curls up over my lip-pad. A blow through my nostrils sends the dry dust spinning silently away down-wind. Five strokes more, very light, smoothing the shoulder of the groove until the curve matches the old polish below it.

I do not wait to see the water run through it. Water knows what to do once the stone is honest.

The sun is straight overhead now. The shadows of the lone boulders have shrunk directly beneath their bellies, except down-slope, half a mile south, where the black torus hangs four feet above its pan and casts the exact same shadow it cast at dawn.

All morning, while I walked and scraped, the bristles along my lower plates have been leaning slightly southward toward that cleanliness in the air. Away from the structures, even in this hushed basin, the air still holds minuscule, irritating raggedness—the tiny pressure-burrs of wind tumbling over gravel, a runner’s pads thudding nearby. Near the torus, all of that smooths out into something vast and taut and completely still, like deep water under ice, except it is warm.

Two others are already folded along the eastern arc of the pan when I arrive.

One is narrow-shouldered and smooth-backed, hardly crusted past its spine-ridge—forty seasons old, perhaps fifty, its mortar-work still rough around the edges when you come across it in the west-rim pans. The other is *Broad-Split*, whom I have sat beside at seven different monuments since my back was bare horn; a pale, healed notch divides the left edge of her carapace where a loosened tor-slab caught her in a long-ago rockfall, and two distinct patches of dormant rust-lichen map the hump of her withers.

Neither shifts weight as I pace into the northern quadrant of the circle, ten feet back from the floating black ring. I fold my legs, tuck my feet beneath my apron, and let my chin-pad rest near the stone.

Between us, four feet up, the torus does nothing at all. You cannot look *at* its surface for long—my double pupils slide off its matte blackness with nothing to polarize, no glint, no dust-mote, only an outline clipped clean out of the dazzling limestone behind it, and rising off it that steady, dry, odorless warmth that smells faintly like sun-struck flint and nothing living.

Slowly, easing the muscles behind my jaw, I unroll my throat-fans.

The sudden cool of dry air on damp vascular skin makes my eyes half-close, then the radiant heat from the black ring catches the membranes full on, soaking straight into the blood-beds. Across the pan, Broad-Split’s fans are already open, angled forty degrees between the torus and the sky. The young narrow-backed one opens last, a slight tremor along its lower margin betraying impatience—its skin-script is still noisy at the edges, flicking stray threads of nervous yellow before settling down to brass.

Broad-Split begins.

She does not look at me or the youngster; she looks past the black ring at the wavering white line of the southern ridge. Across her left throat-fan, a band of tarnished bronze rises from the collar-slit, thick as two fingers, edged on its leading side by a hairline of cold violet. It climbs steadily across her membrane, reaches the outer tip—

—and I have it. My skin takes the exact ratio of bronze-to-violet before I have thought about taking it, blood-shunts opening and closing in tiny, oiled cascades across my left fan, carrying the bar smoothly from left to right across my throat and casting it across the ten-foot gap of baking stone to the youngster, who catches it half a beat clumsy—the violet smears slightly wide—before smoothing it out and passing it back across the circle to Broad-Split’s right edge.

Around it goes. Once, twice, ten times, faster as our blood warms in the orthogonal heat.

It is not speech, not the quick close-up cursive we use beside a puddle when two of us meet and trade news of a cracked pan lip or a fresh bloom of copper-stalks under the rim—*three-strides-east / split-slab / sweet-mat*. That talk has edges and stops when the matter is settled.

What turns around the torus has no nouns in it. It is shape, held open against drift.

On the twentieth turn, I lay a second pulse obliquely across Broad-Split’s bronze bar: three sharp pinpricks of polarized white, spaced like the three eastern clefts where the wind backed three degrees early today. My carapace-tufts felt that shift; now the circle has it. Broad-Split lets the three white points pass through her bronze band on the next revolution, and where they cross her violet hairline she bends the angle by a fraction, folding in what she knows from the lower gravel flats—how fast the midday glare drank the horse-pan this morning, how high the crystal-gliders are having to climb to find lift.

Layer upon layer, the lattice tightens across our six open throats. Indigo cross-bars. Ratios of brass widening and narrowing as the heat-plume above the torus wavers in the silent wind.

Up above us—far up, three hundred feet into the bleaching sky—seven ribbon-gliders have found our column. Through the top slit of my polarized sight I can see their stretched, clear bodies banking tightly inside the cylinder of clean heat rising off the black ring. When a silent gust of wind sweeps down off the limestone pavement and shoves the heat-column sideways, the gliders’ wing-veins flare irritable gold as they slip out of lift—and simultaneously, down here on the rock, I feel the warm boundary slide two inches off my left cheek-fan.

Instantly Broad-Split’s throat snaps stark, corrective teal.

I do not think *move*. My heavy forequarters simply lean four inches clockwise on folded knees; across from me the youngster shifts its haunches outward into the wind-draft, raising its armored flank like a check-dam against the gust. Between our three stone-crusted bodies, the turbulence breaks apart. The rising warmth straightens back to plumb. Across our throats, the teal alarm dissolves back into revolving indigo and gold, denser now, calm, holding the vertical line steady between the buried geometry of the world and the empty sky.

Hours pass inside that turning ribbon, though nothing inside us counts them as hours. My blood grows hot and clean and slow; the tiny sedge-roots in my back tighten against the afternoon drought; a single grain of windblown grit strikes the invisible threshold half an inch below the black ring, vanishes out of the world while a bar of violet crosses half my throat, and drops *tick*-less into the pale flour-pile beneath it, stripped of all haste.

Somewhere out on the blazing flats, an ambush stalker will be sleeping beside the deep-light hemisphere; somewhere under a blue ledge, skimmers are waiting folded in damp scum for the shadow to lengthen. None of them know what holds the warmth steady, or why the pans keep their water an hour longer than raw rock ought to allow. They do not need to know. Even the young narrow-backed mason across from me only half-knows, still letting its brass edges fray slightly when a dust-devil spins past fifty yards away.

Then, far down through the limestone pavement beneath my folded legs—deeper than sound, purely a subtle tightening of the rock’s skin as the sun tips down toward the smooth western molars—the stone cools by half a degree.

Mid-stride across my right fan, the indigo lattice lets go.

Nobody drops it first; it simply ceases on all three throats at once, like mist vanishing at the rim. I roll my fans inward, tucking the hot, tender skin safely back behind dry scales, sealing the slits tight against blown grit. For ten heartbeats longer I stay folded, letting my eyes adjust downward from polarized script-reading to ordinary rock-glare.

Before I turn away, I step three paces inward to the edge of the pan, drop my muzzle to a hair’s breadth below the black ring’s shadow, and part my split lip. Gently, without stirring a puff, I draw up half a mouthful of the dead, velvet-fine flour lying directly beneath the torus, rolling it back into the pouch of my cheek where my saliva begins at once to slick it into paste. There is a hairline crack starting at the south corner of the split-boulder pan, three-quarters of a mile west; if I reach it before the shadow fills the cleft, the patch will set hard before the first bead of sweat gathers on the rock lip.

Broad-Split is already twenty yards upslope, grey-pink against grey-pink, vanishing into the pavement between one blink and the next. I swing my heavy head west, plant one padded foot ahead of the other on the warm stone, and walk out into the glare before the water starts to run.
