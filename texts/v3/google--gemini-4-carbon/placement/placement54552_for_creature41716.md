I am not on the moss. If I were on the moss, I would want to be warm, and wanting to be warm here requires too much grease, too much patience, too heavy a spine. 

Instead, find me sixty feet above the eastern haul road, bolted to the cross-arm of Pylon Fourteen, where the high-voltage transmission line drops off the blasted slate rim and makes its first long, sagging leap toward the floor of the pit. 

At first glance you would take me for hardware—three ceramic insulator bells stacked like thick green-glass skirts on a forged steel pin, glistening with condensed turbine-mist. Look closer at the underside of the lowest bell, where the flared porcelain lip forms a dry, downward-facing cup sheltered from the drifting vapor plumes, and you still won't see an animal. You will see a crust of bright, sulfur-orange *Xanthoria* lichen, a weeping streak of rust bleeding down from the galvanized cotter pin, and a lump of grey dielectric putty left over from when the linemen strung the phases forty years ago.

Wait until the amber beacon on Caisson Six, a mile to the west, flashes twice, and the stratus ceiling darkens by half a stop toward evening. 

The lump of grey putty uncurls. No—it doesn't uncurl; it *loosens*, letting go of its own geometry by millimeters. 

To live up on the iron in a hundred-and-ten-decibel wind-shadow, where the cold air sits right on the knife-edge of freezing and a single drop onto the turf below means slow starvation or eighty pounds of moss-backed carnivore slamming down like a dead-blow hammer, you cannot afford to be heavy, and you cannot afford to be deaf in the bones. You have to love the sixty-hertz hum more than your own blood.

I am small enough to fit inside a lineman’s stiff leather glove, though I am shaped nothing like a hand. Perhaps my great-great-grandmothers were northern bats—hardy little brown *Myotis* or hoary bats blown across the upper plateau one autumn who dropped into the pit chasing a phantom thermal and never climbed back out. If so, the basin broke their wings and remade them within twenty winters, or else I am older and stranger than that, something woken out of the deep flood-control borings that climbed the concrete caisson ribs before the fans spun up to full pitch and found the wires. 

Wings that catch the air are suicide near the shafts, and useless out in the flat, freezing mist between them; membrane stretched thin across finger-bones freezes brittle as mica in three minutes flat when the zero-degree plumes drag past. My fingers are still long—four inches each, multi-jointed as whip-scorpions—but the skin between them has thickened into heavy, scalloped webs of dark, pebbled leather laced with counter-current capillaries and insulated with short, oily velvet the exact matte grey of weathered zinc. I cannot fly a yard. If you threw me into the open air, I would only parachute clumsily, spinning like a heavy sycamore seed.

I do not use the air. I have the cables.

Above the three-foot sponge of the turf, there is a second, invisible basin suspended forty to eighty feet in the twilight: twenty-seven miles of braided aluminum-steel conductor, three phases per tower, swooping in long, mathematical catenaries from the northern cliff face to every caisson collar, every relay cabinet, every buckled pump-shack in the twelve-square-mile pit. Because resistance is never zero, and because seven ship-sized axial fans draw megawatts continuously from the hydro-turbines deep down in the black rock, every inch of braided wire carries a faint skin of Ohmic warmth. Not hot—barely three or four degrees above the freezing point—just enough that while the leeward sides of the ridges below grow needles of white glass rime, the cables stay perpetually wet and bare, weeping single cold drops into the crowberry fifty feet down.

I reach up from under the ceramic bell with one long, hook-thumbed forelimb, clear the eighteen-inch air gap that keeps the current from arcing to the grounded steel tower, and latch onto the live phase. 

Nothing happens—no spark, no singe—the moment my back claws release the grounded porcelain. And oh, the *current* when I hang entirely on the wire.

Down on the quarry benches, the flat hunters plug their whiskers into the willow roots to listen to the lemmings clipping bark. Up here, hanging upside down like a folded rag of oiled chamois from seventy-two kilovolts, my whole four-ounce body sits inside an electromagnetic halo that makes every fine, short hair under my chin stand at gentle attention. Through the horny, grooved pads of my thumbs and hind feet, I feel three things braided together into a single chord: the deep, sub-audible *thrum* of the valley air pushing against the half-mile span of cable; the clean, tight sixty-cycle sine wave of the subterranean generators far below the greywacke; and, superimposed over both like bright scratches on dark slate, the faint, high-frequency corona crackle where wet mist-droplets hiss and ionize against microscopic burrs in the aluminum strands.

I begin to haul myself out across the abyss, hand over hand, moving smoothly beneath the cable like a sloth made of cold rubber and wire-cutters.

Look down past my flat, leaf-nosed snout—the nostrils curled inward like scroll-shells to keep the mist out—and the whole sunken world opens out in shadowless pewter and dark olive. Forty feet directly beneath my spine, the silver-grey ribbon of the old haul road curves down the eastern wall. Two hundred yards south, where the overburden forms a sharp, moss-mantled sawtooth, one of the big flat beasts lies draped across a ledge. From down on the turf, he is invisible, an exact match for the *Racomitrium* wool-moss growing in his hollow bristles. From sixty feet directly overhead, hanging plumb on a power line, I can see him easily—not by color, for my eyes are pinhead-small and dim, tuned only to silhouettes and contrast, but by the way the rime refuses to form in a neat five-foot oval around his skirt, and by the faint, pale smudge of crushed sphagnum he left three hours ago before the sponge swelled back up to erase his track. 

Once, two seasons ago, a rough-legged hawk dropped off the upper flats and tried to take my brother off the span between Pylons Nine and Ten. My brother did not drop—to drop is to belong to the moss-backs—he simply flipped around to the *underside* of the three-cable bundle and pulled his scalloped webs tight around the steel core. The hawk misjudged the sag in the flat, shadowless light; its primary wingtip brushed the adjacent phase while its talons raked for the grounded shield-wire above. There was a white-violet flash brighter than any sun this valley ever sees, a brief smell of ozone and burnt keratin instantly swallowed by the roar of Caisson Three, and two pounds of charred feathers tumbled silently into the dwarf birch, where an old female moss-hunter found it before the meat had even cooled. Since then, the hawks give the catenaries ten yards of sky.

Why am I crossing half a mile of empty, roaring gulf in the freezing mist?

Not for the midges down by the relay cabinets—to climb down a steel pylon leg to within four feet of the ground, where the orange lichen thickens and the concrete apron warms the snow-fleas, is daring work reserved for desperate midwinter stretches when the wind shifts north and locks the upper wires in black glaze ice. No; out near the midpoint of the long spans, where the braided aluminum cables dip lowest into the horizontal drift of the caisson exhaust plumes, something else happens.

The warm, damp air blasted out of the deep subterranean tunnels carries things up from the dark rock and sweeps things down from the surrounding plateau rim: spores, bacterial slime, blown dust of crushed slate, stray wingless collembola, spiderlings ballooning on silk threads across the upper plain that get sucked down into the pit's downdraft eddies. As the vapor plumes drag slowly through the sagging wires, the electrostatic field around the high-voltage conductors acts as a mile-wide precipitator. Microscopic organic dust, fungal threads, tiny wind-blown mites, and freezing mist-droplets are yanked out of the air by static charge and plastered into the spiral grooves between the aluminum strands, building up a slick, dark-grey paste rich in lipids, mineral dust, and stunned micro-arthropods. 

Halfway out over the sunken valley, suspended eighty feet above a buried talus cone where not even a willow bush breaks the rolling green mattress, I stop and wrap my webbed forearms around the humming metal, forming a closed, warm sleeve around the wire with my own body. 

My tongue is long, flat, and edged with stiff, comb-like papillae—not for nectar, not for blood, but for scraping the spiral valleys of seven-strand ACSR conductor cable clean to the bright metal. Inch by slow inch, sliding backward along the grease of moisture and static-trapped biofilm, I lick the wire clean. It tastes of aluminum oxide, ozone, cold rainwater from a thousand feet underground, crushed plateau grass-pollen, dormant tardigrades, and frozen midges blown stray from Caisson Two. Beneath my belly, the cable shivers continuously in the acoustic blast of seven giant propellers, rocking me in a violent, unceasing cradle that my calcified inner ears register not as sound at all, only as gravity's natural pulse.

Down in the dark peat, a lemming clips a root. Up on the ridge, the moss-backed heavy-bone shifts six inches through the wet crowberry, listening with his face buried in the mud. 

A quarter-mile ahead of me along the sagging black line, near the dead-end insulators bolted to the steel collar of Caisson Four—right where the vertical gale of subterranean air roars straight up into the low stratus clouds, warm at forty-four degrees Fahrenheit before the basin chill hits it—three more small, zinc-grey lumps hang clustered beneath a steel vibration damper, pressed flank-to-flank like dried seedpods, grooming the condensed mist off each other's ears while the steel tower shakes hard enough to blur their outlines.

I fold my thumb-hooks over the next twist of the braid, pull my tail-membrane tight against my heels to seal in the four degrees of stolen generator heat, and slide another foot down the catenary toward them, riding the hum between the thunder and the clouds.
