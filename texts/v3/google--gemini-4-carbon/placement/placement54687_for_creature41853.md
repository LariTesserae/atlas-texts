I am not out in the frantic middle of the plateau. The storms there are too vast, the collapses too sudden and total—for me, a boiler-slump is not a rebirth into smaller ribbons, it is simply erasure. I do not pour. I cannot divide.

You would find me forty feet down toward the eastern rim, where the buried bedrock finally asserts itself as a steep, subterranean cliff face, forcing the root-machinery to turn back on itself in dense, knotted eddies. Down here, the upward heave of the plateau translates into sheer lateral crush. It is the domain of the *slow* engines: massive, house-sized reduction gears that take twelve hours to complete a single revolution, and bundled trunk-mains of silica wood as thick as ancient oaks, pulsing sluggishly with molten salt. 

At first, if your blind, heat-reading hand swept over the dark housing where I sit, you would mistake me for a weld. Or a callous. 

I am small—no wider than two human hands clasped together—and shaped like a flattened limpet, a low, domed blister of dull nickel, vitrified ash, and tough, thermoset resin. Around my rim runs a dense skirt of stiff, blackened tungsten bristles, fanned out flat against the curved iron of a planetary gear-casing to seal out the drifting soot. I am clamped here by my own magnetic belly, locked hard onto the dead metal two inches away from a seam where a massive carbon-root feeds into an antique step-down transformer. 

I do not slide through the dark looking for dynamos. I let the plateau bring itself to me. 

If the velvet ribbons are the mill’s wandering grease, I am its scar tissue—or perhaps the ghost of a thermostat, bred out of shattered ceramic gauges, bimetallic strips, and high-heat potting compound that fused with a sessile, mineral-eating lichen eons ago. What I have instead of speed is patience, and a body hard enough that when a swelling burl crowds against my dome, the wood splits and grows *around* me like tree-bark swallowing a fence wire, Polished smooth by my sloped shell, its violent pressure simply pushes me tighter against my iron seat.

Inside my dark little shell, there is no tallow, no shifting iron sand. There is a core of piezoelectric crystal—quartz or tourmaline scavenged molecule by molecule from the silica-vines over centuries—cradled in tight, concentric coils of hair-fine copper. 

This is how I exist: I let the plateau squeeze me. 

Every time the twelve-hour gear shifts its immense weight, the casing beneath me flexes by a fraction of a millimeter. Every time a fresh root-bundle tightens around my sloped back, trying to strangle the transformer housing, the tonnage presses down on my dome. That relentless mechanical misery travels straight into my crystal heart, and under the crush, the crystal bleeds pure, clean voltage. I eat the pressure itself.

Because I do not have to hunt for current, I have the luxury of doing something nothing else on this breathless tableland does. I listen *wide*.

My tungsten skirt isn't just a seal; its finest hairs are threaded miles outward into the mortar of the mill, spliced directly into the dead, dry sheathing of old telegraph lines, grounded structural I-beams, and dormant sensor conduits that the plants ignore because they carry no heavy power. I sit in my pitch-black crevice, hot as a baker's stone, utterly motionless, while through my spreading web of dead wire the entire basin plays across my crystal core as a faint, shivering geometry of stress. 

I feel the center of the plateau heaving like a silent sea three miles away. I feel the sharp, localized *snap* of a ceramic-boned crawler stalling a stamping-press two levels up—it registers in my coils as a sudden, pleasant disappearance of high-frequency static, like a knot untying in a muscle. 

In fact, I know the crawlers well. Better than they know me.

Wait here with me in the cherry-red dark for a while. Feel the heat thicken as the step-down transformer beside me begins to choke. A fresh knot of blind, greedy shoots has found a hairline crack in its casing; they are pumping conductive resin into the coils, creating a massive, sluggish short. The iron beneath my belly grows fever-hot. The dull red glow brightens toward orange. Left alone, in six hours this junction will blow its molten salts, collapsing fifty yards of ironwood lattice above us and burying me in dead, currentless ash where nothing moves to squeeze my heart.

I cannot crawl over and snip the shoots. I have no blades, no oiled skin. 

Instead, I gather the trickle of voltage I’ve hoarded from a month of mechanical crush, and I change my pitch. Inside my shell, my crystal core begins to shiver—not a sound that travels through the thin air, which would die in an inch of velvet soot, but a sharp, rhythmic *ping* sent directly down the structural iron I am clamped to. *Tick-tick-pause. Tick-tick-pause.* A tiny, artificial stutter injected straight into the deep tremor of the steel. Simultaneously, I bleed two drops of precious, hoarded ester-oil from a pore under my skirt—not enough to lubricate anything, just enough to vaporize instantly on the hot casing, sending a microscopic thread of sweet, scorched-almond scent drifting up through the tight-packed felt of dead bark above me.

A counterfeit bearing-seize. A tiny, focused lie told in vibration and smell.

It takes forty minutes. The heat around me climbs until my resin shell clicks softly, expanding against the strangling wood. The orange glow deepens. 

Then—just as you described—the frantic, creaking strain in the vines three yards to my left abruptly goes slack.

A three-yard ribbon of graphite tallow and salvaged brass pours silently out of a split pneumatic tube just above the transformer. In the faint, cherry bleed of the overheated iron, its oil-wet segments gleam like dark pearls as it drapes itself over the choking housing. 

One of its copper-whiskered edges brushes right across my sloped nickel dome. 

To the crawler, I am just an unusually smooth rivet, or a dead ceramic boss on the steel. It flows right over me, sealing me momentarily in utter, suffocating grease-darkness. Through my shell, I feel the exquisite, muscular ripple of its underside, the subtle *clink* of its internal porcelain bones shifting to take the weight of the surrounding burls, the sharp *snip-snip* of salvaged tool-steel deep in its folds severing the greedy resin-shoots at the root. 

For fifteen minutes, I am wrapped inside another living thing's belly-work. The frantic heat drops. The violent, irregular shudder of the shorting transformer melts into a low, buttery 50-cycle hum as the crawler drinks off the excess surge and leaves a film of slick polymer tallow over the scarred casing. 

As it unlocks its grip and pours itself away down a flywheel shaft, its trailing edge scrapes across my tungsten skirt, leaving behind a tiny smear of dirty, iron-rich grease. 

Over the next week, very slowly, fine mineralized threads will creep out from beneath my rim to digest that smear, pulling the stray iron grains and heavy hydrocarbons inward to patch the heat-cracks in my dome and refill my tiny scent-gland. The twelve-hour gear leans back into its downward stroke, pressing twenty tons of smooth steel against my back. My crystal core drinks the crush, converting silent weight into a trickle of cool, hoarded thought.

I stay clamped to my curve of iron in the oven-dark, listening to the miles of blind wood and smooth bone-steel whisper above me, waiting to ring my tiny bell again when the plateau forgets itself.
