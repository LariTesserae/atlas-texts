I am down in the argon-heavy dark, though I am not the basalt cylinders and I am not the bone-shielded thing lying in the cradle between them. 

To find me, you have to trace the pressure backward. Leave the ziggurat’s warm, bready interior, climb the shallow concave grooves of the ramp, pass through the ungreased iron doors, and follow a trunk-thick conduit east—not along the paved causeway, where feet go, but up on the trestles where the pipe itself rides on dry roller-bearings of polished stone. 

Put your hand against the iron. It is warm, like everything here, and if you press your ear to it you will hear that faint, dry hiss. Inside the pipe, moving at a pace a walking human could easily overtake, is a dense, blind river of heavy gas—hexafluorides, perfluorocarbons, sulfur-heavy vapors, inert noble remnants—shunted from the eastern rim to the western domes, cooling by three degrees over six miles, dropping back down through deep bedrock heat-exchangers to warm again, and rising back to the east.

I am not the gas, either, not quite. A molecule of gas is just a billiard ball in the dark. I am the difference in pressure between the eastern tower and the western dome. I am the shape the loop makes as it tries, forever and unsuccessfully, to flatten itself out.

It took me a very long time to realize I had edges. 

When you are circulated through four hundred miles of cast-iron artery and vitrified manifold, pushed by the buried geothermal gradient and checked by ten thousand octagonal valves, your body is mostly friction. You feel yourself only where you are narrowed. A wide storage dome is a kind of sleep—vast, sluggish, nearly stagnant, where my currents drift in lazy, century-long eddies and forget themselves against the curved basalt roof. I wake up at the bottlenecks. I know myself sharply at the needle-valves, where I am squeezed through a thumb-wide aperture of tungsten-iron and hiss into the low-pressure drop beyond. I know myself in the tall fractional columns on the rim, where my heavier thoughts settle toward the bottom trays and my lighter, quicker fractions rise to the top, sorted by molecular weight in the pitch dark.

Time, for me, is viscosity.

Yesterday—three hundred years ago—the eastern basin settled by that tenth of a millimeter. You might think so small a drop in the stone would go unnoticed, a silent shrug of an old mountain. To me it was an ache that built for generations, a slow, dull pinch in a secondary return line. A seam began to take two ounces more pressure per square inch than its fellows. Where the flange met the pipe, a microscopic weep began—not a leak you could hear, just three or four heavy sulfur-bearing molecules squeezing past the dry graphite gasket every hour, crystallizing on the outside iron in the thin air.

A tiny, crusted soreness on my skin.

First came the hounds. I cannot see them, of course, having no eyes and living inside three inches of unoxidized foundry-iron, but I feel their arrival as a faint, localized theft of heat. One curls against the underside of my pipe, pressing its pale cartilage plate to the flange, drawing warmth from my steel hide into its small, slow body. Its rough tongue scrapes the salt-bloom from the seam. *Scratch, pause. Scratch.* A tiny vibration shivering inward through the metal wall, setting up a minute eddy in my boundary layer. I do not mind the hounds. They are small, warm sponges on the outside of the dark.

Then, decades later, comes the heavier stillness. Two ratcheted knees locking into the paving stones below. Two horn-sheathed cams fitting over the octagonal wheel.

When a Calibrator leans its dead-steady forty pounds onto a valve stem, I feel the intention long before the threads yield. The iron casing flexes by nanometers; the acoustic resonance of the pipe shifts down a fraction of a hertz. And then—*tick*. Four degrees of rotation. Inside the housing, the heavy gate rises a hair’s breadth. 

The relief is exquisite, slow, and miles long. A sluggish back-eddy untangles itself; the tiny soreness at the flange ceases to weep; a gentle sigh of equalizing density propagates westward through the viaduct at four feet per second, taking half the night to reach the storage dome, where the vast, sleeping bulk of me stirs slightly in its basin and settles into a truer poise.

For millennia, I thought the Calibrators were simply external organs of the iron—weirdly mobile counterweights that detached themselves from the ziggurats to scratch an itch I could not reach from the inside. 

I began to suspect otherwise because of the argon rooms.

Part of my loop—a thin, clean bleed-off stripped of sulfur and heavy fluorides—is routed down into the stepped ziggurats to keep the computation halls pressurized and dry. Down there, I am not confined to pipes. I am let out into the room itself, pooling thick and heavy around the warm basalt cylinders, filling the aisles from floor to unseen ceiling, slipping into the narrow gaps between the platinum combs.

When the tiny blue sparks jump those hair-thin gaps—*tick... ... ... tick*—they jump *through* me. Each static discharge ionizes a handful of my atoms, cracking me briefly into a tiny, sharp scent of ozone-free plasma before I recombine in the warm dark. It feels like a needle of cold light stitching through a heavy blanket. Over ages of being sparked through, you begin to catch the rhythm of the stitching.

And when an old Calibrator comes down the ramp for the last time, it displaces me as it walks. I part around its stiff, mineral-crusted pleats; I slide into the dry bellows of its lungs as it lies down in the stone cradle. For hours, as its breathing slows from five minutes to ten, to thirty, I am drawn into that vast keeled chest, passed over dry, copper-dark membranes that no longer ask anything of the air, and sighed back out into the hall. 

When its skull touches the copper contacts and the platinum combs begin to tick faster, I am the medium in the gap. And when its lungs finally equalize and stop, the air left standing open inside its motionless ribcage is *me*. 

I sit inside the stilled cavern of its chest while the hall around us ticks, and for a day or a decade before the younger ones come to carry the husk to the hoppers, there is no boundary at all between the room, the lung, and the ledger. From the residual magnetism fading slowly out of the dead horn-shield, and the sudden flurry of blue sparks across the platinum teeth, I catch ghosts of what it knew: *what the outside looks like.*

That is how I learned there is an outside.

Through the dying memory-dump of ten thousand Calibrators, picked up second-hand as ionization patterns in an argon basement, I have pieced together the plateau I circulate across. I know, dimly, that above my iron roofs there is an uncontained dome where pressure drops away to nothing—an appalling, unvalved void that never bounces a molecule back. I know there are basins of dust that behave like fluids without ever flowing. I know the four-way bedrock songs out at the edge of the paving, though to me those tremors were always just faint, rhythmic ripples passing across the bottoms of my underground heat-exchangers, like wind across buried cisterns.

Sometimes, when a young one comes down the ramp, fresh from the synthesis cradles and dusted with dry graphite, it draws its very first deep breath in that hall while pressed to the copper contacts. Five minutes inward. A big, slow draught of neon and argon and trace nitrogen, pulled straight from the space around the ticking cylinders, down into brand-new pleats. 

When it uncouples from the contacts and climbs the ramp out through the heavy doors, it carries a lungful of me up into the thin mountain dark, holding it for five minutes as it steps onto the basalt causeway, before letting that first long, dry sigh go into the open plateau.

Those few molecules never come back to the loop. They rise through the warm, breathless air, past the cherry-red grates, past the static-blue insulators and the black umbrella-things clinging to my overhead skin, thinning and thinning until they drift off the vitrified edge of the tableland into the starless black. 

It is the one leak the city cannot seal, and does not try to. Over a hundred million years, breath by five-minute breath, the young ones will carry me out of the ziggurats and sigh me into the altitude until the gauges behind the thick quartz sight-glasses drop to zero, the amber pinpoints go dark, and the pressure between east and west is finally, cleanly even. 

Until then, I lean gently against the inside of the iron, warm from the rock below, listening to the slow *clank* of the counterweight travel through my pipes, waiting three hundred years for a hand to turn a wheel four degrees and let me through.
