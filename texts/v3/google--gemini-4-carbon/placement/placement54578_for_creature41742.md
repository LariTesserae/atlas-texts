At first, there is the pull of the crawl-slot—to be the third pair of horn-soled feet waiting in the reed-shadow, or perhaps the old mason topside who mixes the hillside yellow slip in wide wooden bowls, washing the grit out through seven waters until it is fine enough to betray a sigh in the bedrock. To have hands that understand mud.

Better, maybe, to stay down in the eighty-eight-degree dark and be small: the whip spider’s eight-inch feeler testing the silver meniscus on a ninety-degree arris without breaking the surface tension. Or the baked-clay pea itself, pinched in the black throat of the split, holding its single grain of grit in the dark, waiting for its one syllable.

No. Closer to the seam than that.

I am twenty feet up the west wall, and ten inches inside it.

I am not the mud, which is only river-silt and dead reeds brought here in baskets, slapped into place by loud, sweating palms two months ago. I am the boundary moving *through* the mud. The curing-line. The slow, blind marriage where bound water lets go of the clay platelets, slides outward molecule by molecule along microscopic capillary alleys toward the warmer air, and leaves the earth locked behind it in a tight, ringing lattice.

Down at the basalt shoulder where the Wall-Listener presses her yellow slip-ribbons, I have barely begun. Down there the wall is still fat, cold-hearted, heavy as tallow, dreaming it is still the bottom of the river. Up at the fourth story under the open sun, I am already dead and finished—baked to pale brick, brittle, ringing slightly when a dog trots past or a cooking pot is set down hard on a roof-terrace.

Here in the middle, two stories above the tree-fern and just below the round ventilation holes, I am alive and working.

To be this is to feel everything as pressure and thirst. Above me rests the blunt, downward-pushing weight of three tiers of dwellings: sleeping mats, grain-jars, hearth-stones, the shifting load of six people walking from one side of a room to the other. That weight comes down through the mud not as sound, which the dampness eats within four inches, but as a continuous, dull compression, trying to make the wall squat and bulge like a dropped melon. Below me rises the warm breath of the young fissure, endlessly slicking the skin of my lower courses, trying to keep me soft, trying to coax the silt back into slump and slide.

I hold the argument between them.

When the afternoon draft cools from amber-gray to slate outside my skin, I feel the pull reverse. All day, the sun on the outer alley wall drew my moisture outward toward the street; for nine hours I crept three-tenths of a millimeter deeper toward the shaft. Now the desert sky opens above the town, greedy and dry, and the warm air inside the shaft begins tochimney upward past the bundled reeds. Suddenly the inner face—the face with the masons’ overlapping palm-crescents and the single climbing vine—becomes the thirsty side. The water inside me turns around in the dark, microscopic pores and begins migrating *inward*, toward the geckos.

That turn is dangerous. When water changes direction inside uncured clay, the platelets tug unevenly against one another. A tiny pocket of trapped air—left behind eight weeks ago when a tired builder slapped one ball of alluvial mud slightly crooked onto the last, trapping a bubble the size of a lentil between his palm and his wrist-bone—begins to stretch.

I feel the tension gather around that hollow lentil in the dark. Half an inch away on the surface, the wax-leaved vine’s pale rootlet was pricked out of a cooling-check an hour ago by the woman’s fire-hardened stylus. I felt that, too: not as pain, because I have no nerves, only cohesion, and the root was stealing water faster than the surrounding mud could shrink to match it. When she tucked a fresh dab of floor-clay into the pore and smoothed it with her thumb, the relief was instantaneous—a broken circuit closed, the local pull smoothed back into the general sheet of tension.

Higher up, six feet below me on the east face, I am changing taste. Where she patted the roasted mussel-shell and wood-ash into the fat hollows of the palm-prints, sharp calcium ions are soaking inward through the wet skin, trading places with sodium in the river-silt, flocculating the slickest mud into tiny, firm crumbs that grip one another instead of greasing past. It feels like a muscle tightening in sleep.

Now the boy comes down the northeast corner.

I feel him before the geckos see him. Each thumb-pocket and heel-cup in that eighty-degree chimney was hardened early with lime, so they are stiffer than the rest of me—long, knotted tendons running down my corner. As he steps down from pocket to pocket, forty-five pounds of compact bone and horn-sole load each cup for three seconds and release. A quiet, vertical plum-line of compression steps down through my corner mass: *here, here, here, here,* testing my spine, finding no give, passing cleanly down into the razor basalt below without leaving a tremor behind.

When he reaches the bottom and settles his back into the smooth concavity on the south face—his shoulder blades narrower than hers, leaving a gap at the edges that his body warmth slowly fills—the shaft settles into its night-engine.

Outside on the street face, a hearth is banked against my flank; a patch of me three feet wide warms to a hundred degrees, driving a slow wedge of dryness inward, hardening a shield behind the plaster. Inside on the shaft face, four inches from the lentil-sized air pocket, three geckos park themselves over a patch of warm thermal updraft, their tiny hooked toes catching on the dry ridges of a forearm-groove. Down at my foot, the hairline seam of calcium water lays down one more white crystal of travertine across the joint where my lowest mud-ball bites the black, unweathered glass of the earth’s split.

All night I will draw tighter. By dawn I will have lost four gallons of bound river to the night sky above the reeds, exhaled invisibly up the chimney past the boy’s quiet breathing, without cracking once. By next season, when the plain shudders again along its raw fault, I will no longer be river mud at all. I will be an arch that remembers the shape of hands, holding forty tons of sleeping town clear of the sharp stone below.
