I am down in the warm, dark grease of the under-floor, three yards beneath the main stamping mill of the Fourth Grove, sitting on an upturned rosin-keg with my ear pressed to the flare of a copper listening-cone.

My knees are wide, my lead-free indoor clogs planted firmly in the damp, velveteen mat of black moss that cushions the pit. Above me, the floorboards—two feet thick, adzed directly out of the living substrate and hardened with hot linseed—flex downward a quarter-inch every four seconds as the great drop-forges strike. *Boom-boom-boom.* I don’t hear the treble of the iron anymore; that went in my twenties, traded away for a steady paycheck and a dry bunk. What I get is the marrow-thump through my sit-bones and the rich, cello-deep hum gathering inside the wide bell of my horn.

The narrow end of the horn is pressed not into my ear canal, which is plugged with oiled wool, but directly against the mastoid bone behind my jaw. I listen with my teeth. 

I am a Splice-Tender, second class. Not grand enough to wear the reed-stiffened coats of the Line-Walkers who brave the flat, bright madness outside, and not rich enough to own a family spanner with six generations of thumb-grooves worn into the iron. My coat has bone buttons—carved from the shin of a cousin who went into the grain twelve winters back—and only three small brass rivets holding the left shoulder together, salvaged from a blown gauge. My job is the seam. Right here, under the looms and the hammers, where the dead lowland iron of the main turbine mount is bolted seven feet deep into the living grey muscle-wood of the plateau.

Wood wants to heal. Iron wants to rust. My whole life is keeping them from ruining each other.

In my lap sits a stoneware pot of warm tallow-and-pitch, mixed with bitter root-ash. Beside me, coiled around the warm exhaust pipe that bleeds steam from the upper cylinders, a pale millipede the thickness of my wrist shifts its hundred chalky legs, clicking softly like dry knitting needles. I don't bother it, and it doesn't bother me. Beyond the pipe, clustered thick around the base of the number-three tie-rod, two dozen Valve-Crabs sit hunched in their crusty amber shells, sipping the sweet, hot mist that sweats through the iron collar. As long as they stay hooked to the bark, my heartbeat stays slow.

Through the copper cone, the plateau sounds like a thick rope being twisted underwater. *Groan. Creak. Thrum.* That’s healthy. That’s the deep xylem two miles down pulling cloud-water up through the dark, feeding the pressure-chests. 

Then, through my molars, I catch a dry, rhythmic *tick*. 

It’s faint, buried three layers under the forge-beat, pitched like a beetle tapping inside a seasoned beam. Except there are no beetles up here, and this wood isn't seasoned; it's four hundred thousand years alive. 

I set the cone down, pick up my feeler-iron—a slender rod of spring-steel, flattened at the tip like a butter knife—and wade crouch-legged through the warm, turpentine-thick air toward the number-four bulkhead. My knuckles brush the weeping wall. It feels like the flank of a feverish horse, dense and rubbery, twitching microscopically against the massive square washers biting into its flesh. 

Here, the geometry is already getting greasy. The lantern hanging from my belt throws my shadow onto the grey callus-wood ahead of me, except the shadow isn't hunched and barrel-chested like I am; it’s tall, spindly, wavering at the edges as if blown by a breeze that isn't down here in the pit. An old fern-memory, leaked out of the compressed grain because the bolts are pinched too tight. 

I don't look at the shadow. Looking at it gives you the eye-rot, makes you misjudge the reach of your own arms. Instead, I close my eyes and slide the tip of the feeler-iron into the hairline gap between the iron collar and the swollen grey lip of the bark.

*Tick. Tick.*

There it is. A knot of reaction-wood is forming six inches in, curling around the threads of the giant anchor-bolt. Worse, it’s a *time-burl*. As my steel blade touches the buried knot, my wrist jerks backward from the recoil of a hammer-blow that hasn't happened yet upstairs. One, two, three seconds pass—and then *THUD*, the floor above me drops a quarter-inch as the forge strikes. 

If I let that burl harden, by Tuesday the turbine shaft will be spinning a half-second ahead of the steam-chest feeding it. Iron doesn't forgive that kind of argument. The shaft will shear clean through, twist sideways along the warped grain, and punch straight up into the conservatory glass, letting the thin, freezing outside air shriek into the warm looms.

I pull my iron back, unhook a short-handled brass syringe from my belt, and draw six ounces of dark, pungent fluid from a stoppered flask: distilled sap-gall, tapped from the stagnant, dream-heavy burls out under the dead viaducts by the Line-Walkers and sold to the Grove apothecaries. It smells like crushed pine needles and ozone, cold even in this ninety-degree trench. It’s an anesthetic. It tells the ancient wood to forget it has an iron splinter in its side, just for another month.

Before I slide the needle into the seam, two of the Valve-Crabs unhook their claws, fold into knobby yellow stones, and *clack-clack-clack* down into the dry gutter. Not all of them—just two. Not a surge, then. Just a localized shiver.

Still, my thick plum-colored fingers pause on the plunger. 

Right beside the iron collar, where the wood has been rubbed shiny by generations of tenders’ shoulders, a fist-sized blister of pale, unbarked sapwood has pushed through the grey hide since my last shift. As I watch, a seam splits down its middle without a sound, weeping a single drop of clear, stinging water that falls *upward*, hitting the underside of the floorboards above me with a sharp *pop!* 

Out of the split pokes a tiny, blind fan of woody filaments—a miniature ribcage, or a leaf-vein, or the gill of one of those translucent deep-shrimps, carved entirely in wet ivory cellulose. A Ghost-Graft, no bigger than my thumb, opening and closing rhythmically in the lantern-light, trying to breathe an atmosphere that vanished before mountains knew how to stand this high.

Regulations say you scrape a nascent graft flat with an adze and pack the wound with hot pitch. They drain hydraulic pressure, and they carry spatial drift with them; leave one to grow, and soon your wrench will slip out of your hand and turn up embedded in a wall forty yards down-corridor.

I look at the little wooden gill flexing against the rust-bleed of the iron plate. My shins ache slightly—an old phantom pain from the one month in my youth I spent down the great cable-lift in the lowland quarries, standing on dead, mute granite that gave nothing back when you stepped on it. Down there, when something breaks, it just stays pieces. Nothing remembers. Nothing tries again.

Slowly, keeping my eyes slanted away from my tall, frond-feathered shadow, I slide the brass needle in *below* the blister, angling the tip deep toward the anchor-bolt’s threads. I press the plunger with the heel of my palm, forcing the cold gall straight into the time-burl. 

Through the sole of my clog, I feel the tight, angry knot in the grain sigh and slacken. The *tick* smooths out, melting back into the vast, underwater *groan* of the ten-mile root. The three-second stutter snaps shut; my ears pop softly as cause and effect slide back into the same groove.

I pull the needle out, wipe the brass clean with an oiled rag—brass is dynastic, you don't let the sap pit it—and reach into my stoneware pot for a thumbful of warm tallow-pitch. I smear it carefully around the iron seam, sealing the gap, sealing out the air, hiding the pale little Ghost-Graft behind a smudge of black grease where the shift-foreman’s inspection lamp won't catch it. 

It’ll dry up on its own by tomorrow, starved of the leak, curling into a harmless curl of dead wood-shaving in the moss. Let it have tonight to pump its little blind ribs in the warm dark.

From forty yards down the trench, the iron tie-rod shivers against my elbow: *rap-rap, pause, rap.* 

My apprentice, down by the main sump, asking if number-four is holding square.

I pick up my spanner, check its worn iron face in the dim light, and strike the collar twice, firm and flat. *All straight.* Then I settle back onto my rosin-keg, press the copper cone behind my jaw, close my eyes, and let the slow, tremendous heartbeat of the floor pace mine until the evening relief-horn blows.
