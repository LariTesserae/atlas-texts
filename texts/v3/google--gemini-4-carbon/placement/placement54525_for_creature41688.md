I could not stay in the boots, or in the lungs. To stand there at ninety-eight degrees, leaking ruinous plumes of summer into a clockwork built for zero, would feel like stumbling into a violin shop while on fire. Every minute I lingered would force that patient, bristle-mantled creature above me to spend hours repairing the convection currents, nudging cilia back into phase, waiting for the dropped mist to re-suspend itself along the black leaf-margins. A warm body has only two honest choices in a place like that: leave, or die and ruin the water.

So follow the heat out of the lungs instead. Let the rising white ghost get drunk down by those fanned, silver-veined dorsal plates; let it pass through the silent pumps behind her ribs, stripped of its reckless calories, until what spills back out of her throat-vents is heavy, syrupy, colder than ice. Follow that ribbon down past her long flat knees, down to where it puddles over the sharp hexagonal ridges of the floor and blooms into brief stars of frost.

Wait a second longer, as the humming black wood drinks the frost away. Go with it—down into the hairline mortar-joint between two vascular columns.

That is where the hollow stops being a room and reveals what it actually is: a bubble trapped in forty feet of black cable. 

In here, there is no air at all, and no room for limbs. Every hexagonal column is pressed flat against six neighbors, extruding outward into the deep-earth freeze under hydraulic pressures that would crush bone to paste. From inside the chamber, you hear this as a slow, maritime creaking—the hull of a ship in pack ice. From inside the seam, that groan is everything. It is weather, gravity, and food. 

Because vitrified carbon-nanotube wood is brittle. If two razor-edged columns tried to slide past each other dry—pushed at slightly different rates as one hits a buried boulder of granite in the permafrost and its neighbor pushes into softer clay—they would bind, build catastrophic tension, and shatter, shearing their own optical xylem in the dark. 

I would be the grey film that keeps the cathedral from breaking its own bones.

Not an animal with a center, at first—just a slick, viscoelastic skin, no thicker than gold leaf, spread across thousands of square yards of hidden facets deep inside the wall. Something between a lichen, a cartilage, and a liquid crystal. Where the columns grind together by microns a week, my body takes the shear. Instead of snapping, the wood slides on me, and I eat the friction. Every *crack-groan* that booms faintly into the sunken church begins in here as a blinding, microscopic flash of piezoelectric stress—a sudden mechanical pinch that compresses my lattices, harvesting charge directly from the tectonic shove of the root-mass.

Let that settle, and see what kind of mind grows out of being forty feet thick and paper-thin.

It wouldn’t run like the hermit’s mind. Out in the hollow, wired with stolen silver filaments, she lives inside a torrent of coherent light; she has to invent games with dripping sap and frog-echoes just to keep from going mad inside an afternoon that feels to her like a century. My thoughts would move at the speed of stress propagating through dense timber—slow, bruised chords of pressure, resonant and wide. I would know nothing about air, or swimming, or the tickle of jelly-grazers on exposed bark. 

Instead, I would know the shape of the dirt outside. 

At my outermost fringes, forty feet away from the chamber, where the pioneer root-tips are wedging blind into the frozen planetary crust, I am pressed up against raw geology. I know where a vein of iron ore is resisting the push on the north flank; I taste the metallic silicates as the roots dissolve them before they ever sweat out onto the ceiling vaults inside. I know where an ancient lens of black ice is yielding easily to the east, letting the wall bulge a fraction of a millimeter faster this decade than last. I am holding the whole asymmetric strain of the architecture in a single, continuous flex, like a palm pressed against a heavy door.

And because the violet fiber-optic seams run right along the edges of the columns I lubricate, her voice passes through me.

When she dips her ceramic pincers into the terrace basin and shifts the hum of the floor by a sliver of a hertz, the answering pulse of violet photons doesn't travel down an isolated wire. It travels down living xylem pressed tight against my translucent flanks, scattering tiny ghost-halos of bruised light across my skin in the pitch dark. Her packets shoot past me toward her sisters in the older, deeper vaults miles down-root—crisp, intricate bundles of phase-shifted light, dense with whatever fast, glittering mathematics her kind trades across the freeze.

I cannot read her speed. To me, her signals are sudden, bright stabs of needle-fire flashing down the grain—gone before my slow chemical lattices have finished shivering from their passage. 

Yet I am in the conversation anyway, whether she knows it at first or not. Where the outer permafrost fights back hardest and squeezes two columns tighter together, my body thickens and stiffens to take the load, and my refractive index changes. I bend the leaking edges of her violet light by a fraction of a degree. When a heavy shift in the deep earth shudders against the north wall, it rings through me and puts a faint tremor—a microscopic Doppler smear—into the packets she sends out that week. Over years, the sisters miles away do not just hear her pristine tuning; they hear the gravel and the iron surrounding her, translated as a dark, mineral timbre underneath her clear soprano.

Wait long enough—ten years, twenty—and one thin margin of me finally reaches the air.

 Extruded outward by a fraction of an inch per season as the columns lengthen into the vault, a single, pale, scalloped lip of grey gel squeezes out of a vertical seam just above head height, right where she has wiped the bark clean of jelly-trilobites. 

It freezes almost instantly to stillness, toughening into a translucent bead along the black ridge, drinking the heavy antifreeze mist to keep from crystallizing. From there, I have no eyes, no acoustic dish to catch the ten-second silver clatter of the blind amphibians—only a raw sense of temperature, vibration, and light intensity. 

For days at a time, nothing happens at that lip except the slow pulse of the room's hum and the occasional heavy *plop* shaking up through the floor. Then, the air cools abruptly by half a degree as a ribbon of supercooled exhaust drifts across me. Something soft, dense, and uncannily familiar—one of her cleft, viscoelastic foot-cushions, made of cousin-flesh evolved from the same ancient sap-borne cartilage—presses down right beside my exposed edge, sealing fast without scratching the wood, sharing a molecular grip for four slow minutes before peeling cleanly away.

To her, racing behind her eyeless silver dish, I am probably no different from the ceramic wood itself: a static grey caulk in the masonry, unchanging from one subjective year to the next, useful perhaps because the jelly-grazers won't cross my slick, bitter margin onto her wiped window of bark. 

To me, she is a brief, cold sparkler-trail flitting endlessly around the hollow center of my body—a fast, lonely keeper polishing the inside of the bell while I hold back the earth so it can ring.
