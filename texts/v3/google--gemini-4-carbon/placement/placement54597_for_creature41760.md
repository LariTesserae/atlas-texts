I am three hundred yards upslope from the braided flats, wedged under a forty-ton slab of flood-basalt that dropped off the west scarp before my grandmother’s teeth came in, and my knees ache—not from the cold, which stops at my wrists and ankles like a turned cuff, and not from age, though the grey has crept down my braid to the collarbone, but because the olivine shear four miles under the talus has been singing *copper-sour, copper-sour* against the bone behind my left ear since the silver-fish turned in the lower reach, and I have been waiting for the kid to feel it on his own before I get up and fix it for him.

The kid—my sister’s youngest, seventeen or nineteen depending on whether you count the double-spawning season when the south fissure steamed all month—is six feet away at the lip of the den, up to his elbows in a split browser haunch, humming into the breastbone so he can feel his own voice rattle back into his jaw. Out beyond our three-foot wall of living peat and rooted sword-fern, the wind is doing what it always does off the West Spill: coming down like an iron roller, flattening the scrub conifers until their needles comb the mud, stripping grease-ice off the raw greywacke steps above us and flinging it sideways into the dark like thrown gravel. Down through the rock-gutter between my boots, forty-four-degree seepage slides black and slick, carrying blood-clots and scrapings of browser tallow out under the moss-apron into the blind ditch where the fat pale eels wait for us to finish butchering.

I don't look at the kid. I keep my horn awl moving through a double seam of otter-hide, pulling greased sinew tight with my teeth, tasting old goose-fat and bog-iron.

Then his humming stops.

His shoulders do that tiny hitch—half an inch left, as if the conifer-bough platform under his shins just tipped on grease—and he swallows once, hard, against the dry penny-taste at the root of his tongue.

I bite off the sinew, spit the tag-end into the warm gutter, and don't smile. In the dim orange smear of the lamprey-dish, his eyes are huge, almost all pupil, rimmed with a millimeter of pale grey iris. He looks at me. I tap two knuckles once against the basalt roof overhead—*yours*—and reach for a strip of smoked breast hanging above the grease-lamp.

He wipes his bloody forearms straight onto the front of his bark-bast smock, grabs his striker-cobbles off the peg by the crawl-hole, and goes out headfirst into the mud-run on his belly like an otter sliding off a bank. The peat-curtain drops shut behind his heels. Even in that half-second gap, no draft gets in—we baffled the tunnel with two dog-legs after the winter tremor shifted the slab—only a brief, sharp *crack* of tearing air that hits my eardrums like a slap before the deep, steady engine-thrum of the mountain closes back over me.

I chew the goose slowly. Give him four minutes to clear the fern-brakes and drop down to the upper braid, then I pull my hood tight around my cheekbones, lash the chin-thong twice so the gale won't get a finger under my jaw and peel the hide back, and slide out after him.

***

What I am, mostly, is an appetite for resistance.

People think—if anyone outside this trench still thinks about us, up on whatever high, quiet tablelands or clean orbital spindles our seven-times-great-grandmothers walked away from—that living in a forty-knot sleet-chute is something you endure for the work's sake. A penance of mud and fish-guts paid to keep a wild cradle humming. They have it backwards. Once your blood runs counter-current in the forearms and your brown fat packs itself thick along the collarbone and kidneys, still air feels like suffocation. You step into a dead-air cylinder after six hours of leaning thirty degrees into wet shrapnel, and for the first minute your skin prickles with panic, overheating in its own grease, waiting for the shove that holds you upright. Without the roar pressing against the bones of your skull, your own heartbeat sounds disgustingly loud and wet, sloshing in your ears like bog-water in a boot.

I stay thirty paces downwind of the kid, moving through the browser-runs on the side of my feet where the sharp clinker has been pounded flat under four inches of black peat and shredded needles. Overhead—just twelve inches above my crown when I drop to the long, bent-hipped valley lope—the canopy of leathery winter-laurel is a solid, thrashing roof, leaves slapping water into my face in heavy, rhythmic gouts. I don't need eyes for this run. My soles know every raw basalt tooth sticking through the muck between our slab and the Split-Water Pylon: two short steps over the drowned cedar trunk, slide left where last week’s slump dropped a ton of razor-edged greywacke across the ditch, drop six feet down a slick moss-chute straight into the edge of a side-braid.

The water hits my thighs at thirty-three degrees and feels like nothing at all—just heavy Push, thick with rolling grit and torn liverwort, shoving against my shins with a solid muscle I can lean my hip against as I cross. Something thick and silver slams against my calf in the pitch dark, fighting upstream with frantic, blind power, scales rasping rough against my greased leggings, and is gone before my hand even twitches toward it. We’ve got three hundred pounds of split haunch and salted eel under the peat; let her spawn.

Ahead through the stone of the riverbed, two sharp clicks come up into my heel-bones.

*Tock-tock.*

Not for me. He doesn't know I'm behind him. That’s him warning off a night-heron or a sow browser holding the lee of a boulder ahead—polite, automatic, the way you clear your throat entering a dark room—so he doesn't take an eight-inch bill in the eye or get bowled backward onto freshly split basalt by four hundred pounds of startled muscle.

Good boy. Doesn't hurry even with the ledger tilting behind his ear.

Two minutes later I smell the boundary before I hit it: not a smell, really, just the sudden absence of pulverized leaf-resin in the sleet, because thirty feet upwind the installation is dropping half a ton of flying litter straight into a circular mulch-berm. Above the tearing black smear of the scrub, the pewter skyglow shows nothing—not the pylon, which drinks too much grey to stand out, and not the three sea-chest anchors hanging above it. Only a weird, vertical neatness in the dark where the horizontal streaks of ice-shards simply vanish.

I don't go in.

If I step through the curtain, he’ll hear the water-gap behind him and stiffen up, start thinking with his mouth instead of his hands, trying to remember the dry words I taught him two seasons back while we rendered duck-fat in the rain-shadow of the Black Blister: *hydrostatic gradient, serpentinization front, brittle-ductile creep.* Words are fine when two people from opposite ends of the graben meet at a pylon to trade bog-iron and have to agree on why they’re letting the south-fork cataract chew into the old rookery. Alone at an anchor, words just put grease between your skin and the rock.

So I hook one elbow around a braided, horizontal hemlock trunk five yards outside the kinetic wall, wedge my boots against a raw step of fractured basalt, and watch through the curtain.

From out here, looking in is hard—forty knots of water sheeting across your eyeballs turns everything beyond ten feet into pewter soup, and the circular waterfall of dead sleet dropping around the perimeter hangs like beaded gut—but I catch the pale blur of his cheek when he pushes his hood back.

Thirty shorebirds scatter away from his boots in complete, eerie pantomime—beaks wide, necks straining in harsh alarm cries that never cross the three-inch gap into my gale—and resettle four yards left at the exact edge of the dry moss, tucking their bills back under slick scapulars before he’s even reached the pylon.

He doesn't haul himself up onto the stirrup. That’s for resting, or keeping meat off the voles, or getting high enough to spot a partner’s lantern-smoke when somebody breaks an ankle in the talus—not that smoke clears the scrub anyway. For deep-frame work at a pylon, you stay down where the lattice becomes the basalt.

He drops to both knees at the base of the walking-stick shaft, right where the matte grey-black column grows seamless out of a jagged rib of flood-lava, and wraps both hands—still crusted brown to the wrist-bones with browser blood—around the slender stem.

Out here in the roar, my own balance is still tilted two degrees port, copper dry on my back teeth. A gust comes off the high fault like a dropped wall; the hemlock trunk under my ribs groans and flexes an inch, squeezing cold sap-water out of its bark against my sleeve; twenty yards down-channel, an undercut gravel bank gives way with a deep, bowel-shaking *whump-grind* of boulders rolling over one another in black foam.

Inside the curtain, nothing moves except the steady, vertical plummet of dead rain around him and the slow rise and fall of his greased shoulders.

Wait.

Wait, kid. Don't shove it. Everybody at seventeen wants to shove the shear shut the second they find the leak—pinch the fracture tight so the dry pocket stays dry and the copper taste goes away clean. Do that under the West Spill where the graben is still dropping four millimeters a month, and all that pent strain walks two miles north along the fault-plane until it blows out under the warm-springs fen and drains every eel-gutter in three dens overnight. You don't plug young rock. You bleed it sideways, the way you turn a gutter-stone half an inch with your toe so the offal goes to the ditch instead of pooling under the sleeping-boughs.

A full minute passes. Sleet builds a quarter-inch crust of rough rime on my windward shoulder, cracks when I breathe, and blows away in curved flakes.

Then, nine kilometers down where green mantle-rock meets cold, downward-percolating black water, something shifts.

I don't hear it—nobody hears nine kilometers down through forty knots of thrashing laurel—and the ground doesn't even tremble harder than its usual twenty-minute settling itch. What happens is the boat-deck tilt behind my left ear slowly levels out, not with a snap, like a door slammed, but with a long, heavy sigh of equalizing pressure, taking thirty seconds to come flat. And at the back of my throat, the dry penny-taste fades, replaced for half a heartbeat by the faint, phantom ghost of wet chalk—the sensorium’s shorthand for *mineral-saturated slow seep, sub-boiling, stable.*

He didn't pinch it. He feathered the shear-curtain across three parallel micro-faults, turning one dangerous throat into forty weeping hairs, letting the cold river-bottom bleed into the hot olivine slow enough to make chemical warmth instead of steam, feeding six degrees of silent heat straight back up toward the roots of the ferns forty yards under my boots.

Inside the calm, he lets go of the shaft.

His hands come away spotless, of course—every flake of dried browser-clot stripped at the molecular join and shunted down into whatever deep-frame sink keeps the lattice clean—leaving two pale, naked-looking paws below dark, grease-caked cuffs. Even through the falling curtain of dead sleet, I see him scowl at them, reach straight inside his smock to his own warm, unwashed ribs, and rub tallow back into his knuckles before the dry air inside the perimeter can tighten his cuticles.

Then he stands up, pulls his hood forward, ties the jaw-thong twice, and stands for ten seconds looking up at the three asymmetrical sea-chests floating motionless above the stick—locked so hard to the deep geometry of the planet’s spin and plate-center that while the whole raw valley shivers faintly under our knees, those three dark lumps look painted onto nothingness, letting the world crawl past them millimeter by millimeter.

Sometimes, when you’re young, looking at them makes your stomach drop slightly. Not with reverence—nobody prays to a crowbar—but with the brief, cold vertigo of remembering how big the wrench is that our great-grandmothers left sticking out of the mud, and how thin our skins really are if we ever get lazy and turn this trench into a meadow.

Ten seconds is all he gives it. Longer than ten seconds in dead air and your sweat starts chilling under your pelts.

He steps to the downslope curve of the circle, picks up his wrist-stones, leans forward at the ankles like a diver leaving a ledge, and falls out through nothing into the hammer of the wind.

The sleet takes him full across the chest, staggering him half a step before his knees flex and catch the weight. Two yards into the thrashing laurel, before the roar has even finished re-bruising his eardrums, he taps his cobbles once against his shin—*clear trail, coming back*—and drops into the low, rolling trot toward home.

***

I don't follow him back to the den. The haunch will get quartered fine without me, and my sister’s husband is already tending the smoke-pit under the overhang.

Instead, once the kid’s foot-clicks fade upslope through the wet peat, I unhook my arm from the hemlock and turn down-valley toward the broad, flooded flats where the main river splits into nine black braids across a mile of fresh, knife-sharp clinker.

This is the hour—if you can call it an hour when the sky only trades dark iron for wet pewter and back again—when the basin feels thickest. Half a mile downwind of the Split-Water Pylon, a logjam of prostrate conifers that has been damming the third braid since yesterday noon finally gives out under the weight of rolling gravel. I feel the break in my teeth before the water arrives: a low, grinding *thud-thud-boom* as ten-ton basalt blocks unlock and tumble downstream, chewing fresh sparks out of one another underwater where nobody sees them, throwing a sudden knee-deep sheet of black silt-water laterally across three acres of horsetail marsh.

Instantly the dark explodes with wings. Two thousand heavy-bodied geese lift off the flooded ditch-pools at once, rising only four feet before the forty-knot gale catches them; they don't fight it, just tilt their oiled backs, lock stiff pinions into the violent ground-eddy, and scream past me at waist height like feathered cannonballs, brassy voices torn ragged and vanished before they’ve cleared twenty yards, dropping straight back down into the brand-new slackwater forming behind the shifted jam before the foam has even settled.

Right at my boots, the newly diverted sheet of river hisses over raw, dry-split greywacke that hasn't been wet since the fault dropped last season. Within three days, silt will pack those razor-cracks; within ten, spores carried in browser-hooves will green over with liverwort; by next month, five-degree root-seepage from the kid’s newly feathered shear-zone nine kilometers down will push fern-fiddleheads thick as my thumb up through the sleet.

I crouch in the lee of an upturned root-plate—six feet high, packed solid with jagged stone and dripping moss, warm vole-nests ticking faintly inside the dirt three inches from my ear—unhook my three-pronged bone leister from my back-sling, and slide bare-handed to the elbows into the brand-new channel before the silver-run realizes the road has moved.

The water is thirty-three degrees and black as pitch, tearing past my wrists like coarse rope, stinging every small rock-nick on my knuckles until the counter-current kicks open in my forearms and turns the cold into a dull, bright hum. I close my eyes—useless anyway under the churning foam—spread my thick fingers wide against the angular basalt on the bottom, and wait for the heavy, muscular knock of meat fighting its way uphill through the dark.
