There is no way into the vault except through fractures too narrow for a ribcage, and the vault itself is no larger than a parish church, though you can never see from one end of it to the other. The space is packed too tightly for sightlines. Great vertical fins of freshly cleaved basalt and translucent muscovite hang from the ceiling and jut from the floor like the interleaved plates of an immense capacitor, set only arm’s-breadths apart. Where the rock split when this pocket dropped away from the upper crust, the edges remain razor-keen. Without a drop of moisture in the air to dull them, and no wind to sand them down, the fractures keep their glass-sharp lips. Only the constant mechanical shudder of the room shakes loose an occasional, crisp flake of stone, building neat, dry talus piles of glittering shrapnel along the base of every cleft.

Before you see anything, the sound has already occupied your chest. It is a continuous, unvarying roar pitched around a low B-flat, dense with upper harmonics that rattle the teeth. It comes from four massive cylindrical casings of riveted cast iron that pierce the chamber at irregular angles, driven down from some unseen industrial stratum far above and disappearing into the splintered floor. They have no pistons, no fans, and no spinning shafts—nothing that could wear down or throw a bearing. They are purely magnetic: giant step-down inductors and grounding cores, humming with furious magnetostriction. A single brick switch-house, half-crushed by a fallen slab of pegmatite when the cavity first opened, leans near the center of the maze. Its glass-fronted dials are cracked, their brass needles welded by tiny arc-burns to single, unchanging numbers on the scale.

The heat in the room—a dry, baking warmth like the air above a kiln—radiates entirely from these four iron pillars. Inside their ventilated cages, banks of coiled nichrome strips glow a sullen, steady cherry-red. Aside from those buried filaments, the only light is electric and cold: a perpetual violet fuzz of corona discharge bleeding off the sharpest ridges of the stone fins and jumping in fat, lazy sparks across the glazed porcelain insulators bolted to the iron columns. Everything lives in deep amber shadow edged with ultraviolet fur. The air tastes of scorched dust, hot iron, and sharp, metallic ozone.

Nothing here has ever known water. Nothing rots, nothing swells, nothing molds. Yet the labyrinth between the stone fins is crowded almost to suffocation with dry, crackling life.

Plants are few, clinging only where the vibration is fiercest. Along the rivets of the iron casings and wedged into cracks directly touching the metal, sparse thickets of black, leafless stems grow in rigid right-angle branches, looking like crystal dendrites or bent stove-wire. Their skins are graphitic and brittle; if snapped, they puff out a dry gray soot instead of sap. They draw their energy directly from the shaking of the casings, piezoelectric tissues converting endless mechanical tremor into slow carbon chemistry, building themselves molecule by molecule out of the warm carbon dioxide and mineral dust hanging in the air.

Everything else belongs to the animals, and the animals are everywhere. Every stone fin is shingled with them; every narrow gap between the mica curtains is bridged by their bodies and their scaffolding.

Most numerous are the plate-backs—creatures the size of human hands that resemble crustless, porcelain-shelled chitons. Thousands upon thousands of them overlap along the sharp stone edges where the electrostatic charge concentrates. Their shells are pure, unglazed dielectric ceramic, bone-white and dry as chalk, fringed with fine copper-colored bristles that twitch in the electric field. They do not hunt or graze in any ordinary sense. They sit motionless for days in tightly packed rows, acting as living condensers: letting high-voltage static accumulate across their dorsal plates until they glow with faint blue St. Elmo’s fire, metabolizing the trickle of current through sulfur-iron pathways inside their desiccated tissues, and passing the stepped-down charge to the neighbor pressed against their flank. To push a hand between two stone fins is to brush a solid, hot pavement of dry ceramic backs that buzz like live terminals.

Strung between the fins, turning the upper two-thirds of the vault into a dense, papery thicket, are the nests and bodies of the stridulators. They hang in layered curtains of grey mineral-silk—a brittle, spun slag made from chewed mica. The creatures themselves are long, multi-jointed things shaped like empty seedpods or dry bellows, all stiff parchment wing-cases and glassy legs. They never stop rasping. Rather than fighting the deafening sixty-cycle roar of the iron inductors, they lock into phase with it, scraping toothed ridges along their flanks in exact sympathetic resonance so that the acoustic waves bounce between the parallel rock plates and form standing walls of pressure.

Those standing sound-waves are part of the room’s architecture. In the three-foot alleys between stone slabs, airborne mica dust and dark metallic ash do not fall straight to the ground; they hang suspended in shimmering, stationary horizontal ribs, held aloft in the nodes of the roar. Ghostly pale mites, no bigger than grains of salt, swim through these suspended dust-bars as if through strips of invisible pond water, grazing on flecks of shed shell and airborne carbon, never drifting a millimeter above or below their acoustic shelf.

Near the leaning brick switch-house, where an insulated copper busbar snapped long ago and now droops near the floor, larger shapes congregate in a tight, motionless knot. They look like bundled violins made of pumice and brass wire—tall, tripodal scavengers with long, tuning-fork antennae angled toward the live copper. They never touch the bar. They stand in concentric rings around it at the precise inch where the voltage drop suits them, shoulder to brittle shoulder, slowly passing pellets of compressed mineral waste from mouthpart to mouthpart, stripping every stray electron from the ash before letting the dead powder fall.

The strangest feature of this cramped, roaring pocket is its absolute permanence. There is no morning, no season, no weather, no tide of heat or cold. The transformers above do not surge or falter; whoever or whatever built the grid overhead abandoned its controls or locked them in perpetuity. Down here, equilibrium has closed over the room like amber. When a plate-back dies on its sharp ledge, it does not fall; its dry body simply calcifies into an insulator, and two younger ones climb onto its back to reach the spark-line, thickening the crust by another quarter-inch. When a curtain of slag-silk grows heavy enough to tear loose from the ceiling, it drifts down onto the talus, fractures cleanly, and is ground by the endless vibration of the floor back into floating dust within an hour, rising immediately back into the acoustic ribs to feed the mites.

Every cubic foot of the dim space is spoken for—layered with hanging sheets of mica, bridged by crackling webs, paved with warm porcelain shells, and stuffed solid with noise. Nothing arrives, nothing leaves, and nothing winds down. The filaments glow their steady cherry-red; the violet static hisses along the unweathered knife-edges of stone; the million papery legs saw in time with the iron cores; and the dry, hot air holds its single chord forever.
