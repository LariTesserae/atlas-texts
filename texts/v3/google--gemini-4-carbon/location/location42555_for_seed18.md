The place is a hollow iron sphere, no wider across than a parish church is long, suspended by three braided steel cables at the top of the troposphere. 

There is no ground below it, and no structure around it. Outside the hull, the air is thin, freezing, and bright; inside, it is warm as a greenhouse, fiercely loud, and completely hollow. Because the interior has no floors, no catwalks, and no central scaffolding, ninety-nine percent of its volume is uninterrupted air. You stand—if you find purchase on the curved, inward-sloping lower ribs—at the bottom of a deep, round room filled mostly with nothing at all.

The heat and the noise share a single source. Built into the curving walls are twelve massive, jacketed steam manifolds, fed by insulated umbilical pipes that run alongside the suspension cables from whatever distant, unseen engine lies miles below in the clouds. These manifolds do not power anything here; this sphere is merely a pressure-relief and condensation chamber, a mechanical lung hung at twenty-eight thousand feet to bleed off excess industrial heat. The sound is a continuous, deafening roar—not a clatter or a bang, just the colossal, unwavering white noise of high-pressure gasses expanding through brass baffles. It presses against the eardrums until it ceases to register as sound and becomes a physical weight in the chest, vibrating the teeth in the jaw and the water in the eyes.

Yet nothing moves quickly. The gauges set into the iron bulkheads have had their glass faces etched opaque by decades of mineral mist, their needles rusted to a standstill or creeping a millimeter over the course of a week as the distant factory below shifts its seasonal output.

Time has worn the iron smooth. The interior of the hull has been flaking, weeping, and settling for centuries. Rivets have softened into rounded nubs; steel gussets have eroded into lace-like webs of orange and plum-colored oxide, stabilized now by a thick, protective rind of vegetation. 

Because all the matter in the room is pressed tightly against the inner skin of the sphere, the lushness wraps completely around you, leaving the center void. A dense, heavy tapestry of root-mat, bladder-ferns, velvet-leaved creepers, and rubbery, plate-sized epiphytes clings to the rusted ribs and warm copper pipes. There is no soil, only centuries of mulched rust and dead fronds caught in the crevices of the ironwork. Water never falls as rain here. Instead, the cold of the extreme altitude bleeding through the outer hull meets the warm, industrial exhaust inside, causing the metal walls to sweat perpetually. Beads of warm condensation gather on the undersides of bolt-heads, swell until their surface tension breaks, and roll lazily down the waxy leaves. Pockets of dark, iron-rich water collect in the upturned flanges of I-beams and the shallow, corroded dishes of defunct valve wheels.

Light enters through four circular ports near the top of the dome, glazed with thick, greenish, wire-reinforced glass. Outside, the high-altitude sky is a stark, dark, violet-blue, and the sunlight is raw, unfiltered by lower clouds. Passing through the dirty, wired glass and diffusing through the faint, warm vapor in the chamber, it settles into a steady, ordinary afternoon brightness—even, shadowless, illuminating every slick leaf across the thirty-yard gap of empty air with crisp clarity.

Living in this vertical, wrap-around thicket is an abundance of small, heavy-bodied creatures adapted to the endless roar and the curved horizon. Pale, blind crabs with shells the color of tarnished brass sidle slowly along the weeping pipes, grazing on bacterial slimes that thrive where the metal is hottest. Fat, wingless beetles the size of plums cling to the undersides of the ferns, their carapaces vibrating in sympathy with the steam-baffles. Most striking are the gliders—translucent, flat-ribbed amphibians no larger than a human hand. They do not hop or run; everything here moves with the drugged patience of a slow clock. One will spend an hour creeping up the warm curve of a manifold, wait another twenty minutes at the lip of a rusted bracket, and then simply let go. 

When it drops into the empty center of the sphere, the strangeness of the room asserts itself. Because there is no clutter in the interior—no branches crossing the middle, no wires—the little creature falls smoothly into a gentle, rising thermal draft generated by the warm lower hull. It spreads its webbed limbs and hangs suspended in the middle of the deafening void, turning in a lazy, silent spiral through the shaft of daylight, drifting for full minutes from one wall of iron and leaves across the thirty-yard gulf to the other.

Down at the very bottom of the curve, where the three great structural ribs meet at a massive, forged-iron drain plug, a shallow pool no bigger than a washbasin sits warm and still. Despite the roaring vibration in the walls, the water does not ripple; the frequency of the sound is so high and so constant that the surface of the pool holds a permanent, microscopic geometric crosshatch, standing perfectly motionless like hammered pewter, catching the green-tinted daylight from above while rusty droplets slide down stems to feed it, one by one.
