There is no soil here, and no rock, though the surface of the world is a great distance overhead. The entire space is the size of a modest threshing floor or a dry cistern—perhaps twenty feet across and fifteen high—and its boundaries are composed exclusively of living, tightly packed plant tissue. You are inside a hollow fistula of wood, pith, and hyper-tense green cambium, buried deep in the dark of the earth, yet filled from wall to wall with the clear, flat, unshadowed light of an overcast Tuesday afternoon.

The light has no source. It does not leak from bioluminescent fungi or filter down a shaft; it simply occupies the air as an intrinsic property of the emptiness. And the interior of the chamber is fiercely, startlingly empty. Nothing hangs from the ceiling; no tendrils or roots cross the middle air; no detritus litters the concave curve of the floor. All the mass has been driven outward to the perimeter, leaving a stark, swept volume of warm air—around eighty degrees, close and breathable, smelling sharply of crushed celery, hot resin, and wet flint. 

Because the cavity is stretched taut like the body of a gourd, it acts as a resonator, and the noise inside is relentless. Behind the smooth, pale-green vascular walls, sap moves at hydraulic speeds, producing a continuous, rhythmic *whoosh-thump* like surf hitting a sea-cave. Over this bass note runs a constant barrage of sharp, percussive cracks. The chamber is actively trying to grow shut, and something—some structural contradiction in the room itself—keeps splitting it fresh. Every few seconds, a seam of pale heartwood along the curvature of the wall swells, strains, and ruptures with the volume of a pistol shot, exposing crisp, wet, cream-colored fibers that have never known weathering or rot. Every surface looks five minutes old: jagged splinters of greenwood, sharp ridges of newly torn bark, raw meristematic tissue glistening before it has even had time to oxidize and brown.

Moisture beads on these fresh fractures almost immediately. The room is never flooded—there are no puddles at the nadir of the curved wooden floor—but it sweats. Thick droplets of warm, mineral-heavy guttation squeeze out of pores in the taut green ceiling, swell until they tremble, and let go. 

When they fall, the breakdown of the room’s physics becomes plain. 

A drop detaches from the apex, falls four feet straight down, stops dead in the empty air as if hitting a pane of glass, slides six inches horizontally, and then resumes falling at a forty-five-degree angle, smacking loudly into the upward-curving wall. Elsewhere in the hollow, two shadows overlap on a patch of pale phloem even though there is nothing in the center of the room to cast them. If you look closely at one of the translucent, stretched leaf-membranes bridging a fresh split in the wood, you do not see dark earth behind it, nor deeper roots. Looking through the wet green skin on the north wall shows you the interior of the room itself, viewed from the floor looking up, slightly out of sync—so you can watch a droplet splash against a ridge half a second before it actually detaches from the ceiling above you. Space here has buckled like green timber drying too fast, folding back on its own small volume to seal off a tear in causality.

Driven directly into the sharpest seams of the walls are tools. There is nothing built here—no platform, no shelter, no hearth, no arrangement of seats or markers—only raw, primitive leverage applied directly to the anatomy of the room. Wedge-shaped hand axes of knapped black chert, cold-hammered shims of native copper, and fire-hardened digging sticks bound with raw sinew are jammed deep into the splits of the vascular bundles. Whoever placed them is not here, and indeed there is nowhere they could have come from; the botanical shell has no entrance, no tunnel, no seam wide enough for a hand, let alone a body. 

Yet the tools are working. As the surging, hyper-active growth of the wall swells to swallow a copper shim, the metal groans, bends double under tons of turgor pressure, and is spat out into the empty air—except it never hits the floor. Mid-arc, the bent copper flickers, vanishes with a soft *pop* of displaced air, and simultaneously slams itself—straight, unbent, and freshly driven—into a brand-new split three feet higher up the wall, spraying pale sap and crisp wood-shavings as if struck by an invisible mallet. A flint wedge on the floor-curve shatters under the sudden heave of a root-knuckle; an instant later, the shards are gone and the whole stone is wedged tight into the ceiling, holding the cavity open against its own violent botany. The erosion of the room is locked in a stutter-step with this phantom tool-use, perpetually prying the void apart, keeping every wound sharp and weeping.

Life, apart from the roaring, shifting architecture of the plant itself, is nearly absent. For long stretches, nothing moves through the bright, empty center except wayward beads of water and flying flecks of pale pith that vanish before landing. Occasionally, however, a single creature emerges from behind a overlapped scale of fresh bark: something the size of a human thumb, resembling a wingless, soft-bodied cricket with too many joints in its hind legs and pale, lidless eyes suited for the surface day rather than the underground dark. 

It navigates the broken geometry by habit. It walks out onto the shaft of a fire-hardened digging stick, drinks from the warm condensation pooling at the sinew bindings, and flattens itself against the wood whenever a vascular ridge blows open with a deafening *crack*. When the stick begins to shiver—about to be crushed by the closing wood and snapped back to an earlier point in its own timeline—the insect leaps casually into the bright, empty middle of the room. It falls sideways through the sourceless afternoon light, misses the floor entirely, and lands with a tiny, wet *tick* on the ceiling six feet away, right beside the fresh split where the stick has just violently reappeared, already drinking the newly spilled sap before the echo of the fracture has died out of the wood.
