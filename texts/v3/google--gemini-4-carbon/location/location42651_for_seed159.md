The world here is three hundred feet below the upper crust, occupying a subterranean depression the size of a broad river valley. Above, the roof is not stone, or at least not dark stone; it is a mile-wide span of fractured, milky selenite and translucent gypsum that catches the fierce sun of the surface world and diffuses it downward. The light inside the basin is bright, shadowless, and pearlescent, like the glare inside a cloud or an overcast summer noon. 

Nothing here is worn down. Nothing has had time to weather. The floor of the basin is a chaotic, shifting topography of raw mineral extrusion. Warm mud-pots and low, broad vents continually heave up thick slurries of grey clay, yellow sulfur, and dissolved calcium, which slump outward and flash-harden in the warm air into crisp, terraced shelves, brittle domes, and jagged, razor-edged ridges of travertine. A ridge that blocks the view in the morning may crack down its spine by noon, slumping into a warm, steaming heap as a new blister of pale marl inflates twenty yards away. There is no soil, no leaf, no moss—only damp, newly birthed stone and faint, greasy films of rust-colored bacteria slicking the warm seepage zones where mineral water pools and quickly evaporates.

Yet you can barely see the fresh rock for the bodies.

The basin is thick with animal life, boiling with an anthill intensity that fills the bright, humid air with a constant, dry roar. Millions of blind, pale-shelled crustaceans—things the size of human fists, with tall, laterally compressed carapaces—carpet the warm terraces. They scrape endlessly at the bacterial films and the soft, unhardened mineral crusts, their mouthparts producing a collective rasping sound like sandpaper dragged across timber, amplified ten thousand times. Above them, the air is dense with heavy, leathery-winged insects the size of sparrows. They do not fly gracefully; they batter their way through the humid updrafts rising from the vents, colliding with one another in mid-air, dropping into the crustacean shoals to snatch up newly molted individuals, and scrambling back into the air with frantic, clattering bursts of wings.

Every surface is contested. Long, segmented, centipede-like predators, banded in dull orange and slate, pour through the fresh Cooling-cracks like spilled rope, hunting both the flyers that fall and the crabs that stray from the dampness. Beneath the crust, heavy, blunt-headed burrowing amphibians—skinless-looking, armored in plates of hardened mucus and grit—punch up through the soft clay domes before they can fully calcify, gulping down mouthfuls of mineral-rich mud and crustaceans alike, collapsing the new geology as fast as the vents can build it. The air smells sharply of wet chalk, hot sulfur, ammonia, and the musky, chitinous odor of crowded shells. 

Because the ground refuses to stay still, everything is in motion. When a new vent breaches with a wet, coughing thud, throwing gobbets of warm grey paste across ten yards of terrace, the crustacean carpet parts in an instant, clattering away in cascading waves, only to surge back minutes later to feed on the fresh chemical bloom before the mud has even stopped steaming. 

Scattered across this churning floor are the small, stubborn interventions of tool-makers. There are no permanent villages—the floor would swallow or tilt a stone wall within a week—only temporary, high-legged platforms lashed together from the cured, hollow femurs of larger burrowers and bound with sticky, mineral-cured sinew. These stilts stand clustered on the slightly older, higher shelves of gypsum, looking like precarious craneflies braced against the tide of shells below. 

Down on the wet flats, people—or whatever hands made these things—have left low, crescent-shaped weirs built of freshly snapped travertine plates. The plates are wedged upright into the cooling mud to divert the warm, mineral-heavy runoff into shallow, artificial pans. In some pans, the water cools just enough to force a bloom of red slime, drawing thick, suffocating mounds of crustaceans into easy reach of woven bone-splint baskets that lie waiting nearby, weighted down with chunks of raw sulfur. Elsewhere, simple tripods of heavy chitin-beams straddle minor steam fissures, supporting broad, shallow trays of stretched hide curing in the rising heat, or clay pots drying rapidly before the ground beneath the tripod blisters and forces the camp to move fifty paces east.

Paths do not exist here; a footprint in the soft marl is erased in seconds by a thousand clicking legs, or filled by a sudden, warm seep of carbonate-rich water that crusts over into a smooth, white scar by evening. The sound never drops. Beneath the dry, deafening rattle of millions of shells and the papery roar of wings, there is the steady, wet percussion of the earth building itself—a heavy, rhythmic *plop-hiss*, the sharp pistol-crack of cooling crystal fracturing under its own new weight, and the sudden, hollow rumble of an empty mud-chamber collapsing under the rush of the crowd. Everything under the bright white ceiling is brand new, damp with heat, and frantically hungry.
