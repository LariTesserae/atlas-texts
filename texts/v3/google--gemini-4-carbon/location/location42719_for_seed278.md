The plateau does not end so much as curve gently away into a bruiselike horizon, so massive that its edges hold their own distinct barometric zones. Up here, the air is thin, raw, and relentlessly cold. It carries just enough weight to suspend a perpetual, low-ceilinged ceiling of slate cloud, leaving the world in a dim, shadowless pewter light. 

For hundreds of leagues in any direction, the ground is an ancient, settled architecture of wind-scoured basalt and frost-shattered shale. Millions of years of freeze and thaw have ground down every sharp ridge into whaleback swells and vast, shallow basins filled with dark gravel. Nothing moves quickly here. Even the weather lacks violence; instead of storms, immense banks of freezing fog drift patiently over the contours of the land, taking days to cross a single valley. When they pass, they leave the stone sweating with a thin, cold dampness and fill the shallow depressions with black meltwater that crusts over with rime before noon.

Between these scattered pockets of moisture, the land is mostly empty. Life clings close to the damp gravel in isolated intervals: tough, braided mats of iron-red sedge, grey cushion plants hard enough to take a boot heel without bruising, and pale lichens that advance across the boulders at the rate of a millimeter a century. There is an immense amount of room between everything. A lone boulder the size of a cottage might sit in a field of flat scree with no other vertical feature for three miles around.

And yet, across this vast, spaced-out emptiness, the horizon is continually turning over on itself with life, and with heavy industry, though no hand has ever stacked a stone or laid a road here.

They move through the dimness in migrations miles wide, spaced far apart so that no two individuals ever crowd one another, yet filling the plateau out to the limits of sight. At first glance, they resemble immense, six-legged ungulates the size of locomotives, draped in matted, oil-slicked pelts of grey wool. Look closer through the drifting condensation, and the industrial reality of the ecology resolves. Their ribcages house heavy, biological boiler-works; rhythmic, piston-like tendons drive flanged hooves plated in precipitated iron. From rows of dorsal spiracles along their spines, thin plumes of white steam and pale sulfur-yellow exhaust curl upward, vanishing quickly into the cold air.

This is a mechanized fauna, evolved rather than manufactured—or perhaps manufactured so many epochs ago that evolution reclaimed the patent and let all memory of the makers erode to dust. There are no ruins, no fences, no pylons, no Mines. The animals *are* the refineries. 

Smaller organisms skitter across the scree miles apart: flat, segmented grazers that scrape mineral oxides directly from the wet shale with rotary jaws of hardened tungsten-bone, leaving behind neat, parallel grooves in the ancient rock and extruded pellets of pure slag. Overhead, cruising lazily beneath the cloud deck on fixed, rigid wings spanning forty feet, glide scavenger-kites whose hollow bones hum with static electricity harvested from the dry, high-altitude wind shears. Occasionally, in the far distance, a faint blue arc of current snaps silently from a wingtip to a vein of ore in a hillside as one lands to discharge and feed.

The most disorienting rule of this plateau is the absolute, padded quiet in which all this heavy labor occurs. 

Because the air is thin, cold, and perpetually damp with micro-condensation, sound dies within yards of its source. More importantly, the creatures themselves operate with an uncanny, greased perfection. Their biological engines do not clank or roar. A herd of five thousand slag-grazers and steam-beasts can pass across a valley floor—tons of bone-piston, pressurized vapor, and heavy metal plating shifting in slow, deliberate unison—and produce nothing louder than the soft *shhh-shhh* of wet wool brushing against sedge, the faint, rhythmic sigh of valves venting steam into the mist, and the occasional muted click of an iron hoof settling onto wet gravel. 

Stand on one of the worn basalt rises and watch. The cold seeps steadily through everything. Half a mile down the slope, a solitary bull pauses beside a shallow tarn of snowmelt, lowers a flexible, ribbed intake trunk into the water, and drinks. For twenty minutes, nothing else happens save for the slow pulse of pale light behind his translucent sternum as internal dynamos spin down to conserve heat, casting a dim amber glow onto the wet stones beneath his belly. Beyond him, spaced neatly across the grey expanse like ships on an anchorless sea, ten thousand more pale amber lights pulse slowly in the gloom, drifting northward at a walking pace toward a bank of fog that has been sliding down from the upper steppes since Tuesday. 

No birds sing. No engines knock. The wind passes over the smooth, exhausted curves of the rock without finding a single sharp edge to whistle against. Everything is worn smooth, everything is well-oiled, and the whole immense plateau breathes slowly in the cold dark, turning stone and dampness into heat and motion, endlessly, without a sound.
