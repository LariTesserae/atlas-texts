The basin lies several hundred feet beneath the outer crust, a sunken cavity four miles across where a deep stratum tore cleanly away from the stone above it. Overhead, instead of sky, hangs a continuous, fractured ceiling of translucent selenite and milky quartz. Light pours through this mineral roof in a blinding, diffused glare—white, shadowless, and entirely constant. There is no arc of a sun, no dimming toward evening, no drift of cloud shadows across the expanse. The radiance presses straight down through the crystalline plates, turning the entire subterranean valley into a sealed, blazing hothouse.

Because neither wind nor rain has ever entered this chamber, the exposed rock has never weathered. Where the perimeter walls drop sheer to the valley floor, and where black basaltic dikes thrust upward through the green mass, the stone looks as if it split an hour ago. Every ledge is a razor-edged cleavage plane; every talus pile at the base of the walls consists of pristine, unrounded shards with glassy, glittering faces. There is no sand here, no silt, no clay, and no soil. Dust does not exist. 

In place of earth, there is only plant.

Vegetation fills the basin not as a covering over ground, but as the ground itself. A continuous, self-supporting mattress of interlocking roots, lignified tubers, and swollen, woody stolons spans the miles from wall to wall, bridging deep tectonic fissures and burying the raw bedrock twenty or thirty feet deep in living timber. You do not walk on dirt; you walk on the backs of trunks that grow horizontally, fused at their points of contact into a gnarled, springy pavement the color of pale bone and wet ash.

Above this living floor rises a dense, crowded architecture of stems, aerial buttresses, and overlapping tiers of foliage, packed so closely that branches graft into their neighbors’ bark, forming rigid wooden lattices. Ordinarily, so thick a tangle would plunge its lower reaches into gloom. Here, the vegetation has adapted to the relentless brilliance rather than fighting it. Leaves are pale—chartreuse, bleached amber, milky jade, and chalk-white—and most are translucent, built with glassy, water-filled cells and wide fenestrations. Light passes right through a dozen layers of canopy, refracting off waxy cuticles and pale limbs until the deepest hollows of the thicket are just as bright as the upper crowns. Stepping deep into the tangle feels like stepping inside a sunlit block of pale yellow ice.

The heat is immense, thick and motionless, hovering perpetually just above body temperature. It does not bake the valley dry, nor does it brew storms. Moisture exists in a closed, frugal loop. There are no streams, no waterfalls, no dripping bog-lands. Instead, the air hangs heavy with a warm, sweetish dampness that condenses wherever stone meets bark. Along the razor-sharp basalt spires that pierce the canopy, beads of sweat gather on the dark mineral faces and slide silently down into waiting collars of sponge-tissue wrapped around the stone’s base. Huge, rigid rosette plants—some twelve feet wide, wedged into the forks of fused boughs—hold shallow, lukewarm pools of clear water in their central cups, coated with a thin, iridescent skin of natural wax that prevents evaporation. Brush against a stem, and your skin comes away slick with warm condensation; press a fingernail into the pale bark underfoot, and a drop of clear sap beads immediately in the cut.

Sound travels poorly through the dense, leafy baffles. Without wind, the foliage never rustles. You can look out from one of the higher root-ridges across two miles of crowded, luminous canopy—a motionless sea of pale fans, coiled tendrils, and jagged black rock fins shimmering slightly in the heat—and hear almost nothing at all. Close by, there is the occasional sharp *tick* of woody tissue expanding, or the soft, heavy *plop* of a condensation drop falling three feet into a rosette basin. Every few minutes, a faint, glassy ping echoes from the high perimeter cliffs where a sliver of unweathered stone settles by a fraction of a millimeter.

Animal life is startlingly scarce in all this abundance. The plants need no pollinators; they propagate by runner, graft, and heavy, blind nuts that drop straight down into the root-mat and fuse with their parents. Now and then, a flat, translucent beetle the size of a child’s palm sidles slowly across the underside of a leaf, nearly invisible against the glowing veins. Pale, whip-tailed skinks rest motionless for days on warm ledges of unweathered obsidian, waiting for a flightless midge to wander near. Nothing calls out. Nothing hunts at speed.

Scattered at wide intervals through the tangle are small, quiet works left by hands that used no metal and no fire. 

Where a spine of raw chert thrusts out of the root-floor, someone has pried loose flat, sharp-edged slabs—requiring no quarrying, since the stone parts cleanly along natural fractures—and stacked them without mortar into low, circular dry-stone cairns and waist-high cistern walls. Inside these stone rings, broad, waxy leaves have been bent over from surrounding shrubs and pinned down with wooden pegs so their tips all point toward a central bowl of hollowed burl-wood, funneling their slow, steady sweat into a single drinking pool. 

Farther out toward the center of the basin, high in the middle tiers where the branches fuse into broad, crooked avenues, stand occasional single-room shelters. They are half-built, half-grown: living shoots braided together generations ago into domed ribs, thickened over time into solid wooden cages, with raw flakes of translucent mica wedged into the gaps like shingles to diffuse the overhead glare into a slightly softer, pearly glow. Inside one such pavilion, the floor of living wood has been scraped smooth with sharp stone edges, and a row of five melon-sized quartz crystals sits arranged by size along a thickened root-knee. There are no ashes, no potsherds, no discarded bindings or rotting scraps. Even abandoned woven mats have simply been overgrown by a millimeter of clean, pale cambium, sealed seamlessly into the floor.

Nothing in the basin decays in the usual way, and nothing blooms in a sudden rush. When a leaf finally ages, its moisture is drawn back into the stem before it dries into a crisp, parchment-white curl that remains attached for years, too dry to rot and sheltered from anything that might tear it loose. There is no morning chorus, no seasonal flush of green, no gathering dusk. The white ceiling blazes; the sharp black rocks sweat into the pale roots; the warm, bright silence holds everything exactly where it is.
