The entire settlement occupies a shallow, steep-sided basin notched into the shoulder of an upland ridge, a natural stone bowl no wider across than two or three village greens laid end to end. Walk to any edge of it and the ground either rises abruptly into grey, jointed bedrock or drops away into the pale, empty air of the lower valleys, though from inside the basin only the sky and the immediate rim are visible. It is a contained, high-altitude room open to the weather.

Within this tight boundary, almost no raw earth remains exposed. The floor of the hollow has been completely translated into civic space: leveled into broad, stepped terraces, paved with heavy flags of blue-grey limestone, curbed, guttered, and divided by low retaining walls and wide, shallow stairways. It has the grammar of a city—thoroughfares, intersections, public courts, stormwater channels, and distinct quarters—reduced to the footprint of a monastery and emptied of crowd. 

The buildings themselves stand remarkably far apart. Where a normal town would pack walls shoulder to shoulder, here a single square storehouse or narrow, two-story dwelling sits isolated in the middle of an expansive paved court, thirty or forty paces of clean stone separating it from its nearest neighbor. There are perhaps twenty structures in all, arranged with deliberate, airy geometry across the tiers. Between them stretch generous plazas, wide avenues that carry no traffic, and stepped amphitheaters of dry-laid stone facing nothing in particular. The emptiness does not read as ruin; there are no rubble piles, no robbed foundations or missing walls waiting to be rebuilt. The spaces between buildings were meant to be open, paved and graded with the same care as the thresholds.

Everything is made by hand from local rock, split slate, and minimal timber, worked with chisels, wedges, and mauls. The masonry is dry-laid—no lime mortar, no cement—just dense blocks dressed patiently until their faces met with hairline precision. Centuries of frost, wind, and settling weight have locked them together. Whatever could shift or fall did so ages ago; the walls have settled into slight, enduring batters, swelling almost imperceptibly toward their bases like the trunks of old trees. Sharp tool-marks have softened into smooth dimples. Stair treads dip gently at their centers, polished to a dull pewter sheen by leather and horn, though nothing seems to wear them down any further. Even the rare pieces of metal—a heavy bronze hinge-pin, an iron ring leaded into a tie-stone—have developed a dark, dense patina that seals the core against deeper rust. Timber lintels, hewn from slow-grown mountain larch, have cured in the cool dry air to the hardness and silver-grey color of bone, pegged with wooden dowels that neither shrink nor rot.

Light falls into the basin clear and even, the straightforward daylight of high ground under a pale, slightly washed sky. Shadows cast by the isolated buildings are crisp-edged and dark grey, sliding across the wide flagstones at an unhurried pace, measuring the day across empty courts without obstruction. The air stays cool even when the sun stands directly overhead; the sheer mass of shaded stone absorbs little heat, breathing a steady, cellar-like chill back up from the deep joints of the paving.

There is no stream here, and rain comes only in passing, thin squalls, yet the place is persistently damp. Moisture arrives mostly through low cloud and night condensation. Along the northern wall of the basin, where the natural cliff leans slightly inward and never catches direct sun, the rock sweats continuously. Long ago, builders cut neat diagonal grooves across that cliff face to gather the film of water, guiding it down into narrow, open runnels chiseled directly into the flagstones of the streets. All day, dark ribbons of wetness trace their way across the pale plazas—not flowing fast enough to ripple or make a sound, just a slow creep of water pulled by a fraction of a degree of slope. These channels feed half a dozen square, sunken cisterns set at street corners and covered with slabs of slate propped on short stone piers. Peer beneath the slabs and the water sits black, cold, and dead-level with the overflow lip, holding a flawless reflection of the underside of the roof stone.

Life takes root only in the narrow margins permitted by stone and cold water. Along the damp edges of the runnels, velvet cushions of dark green moss grow to the exact width of the seepage and stop cleanly where the limestone dries out. Elsewhere, crustose lichens—ochre, chalk-white, and dull sage—map the older windward faces of the buildings in static, overlapping rings that expand by a millimeter a decade. Out of deep vertical crevices in the retaining walls push isolated tufts of stiff, wiry fescue and leather-leaved saxifrage. In the lowest court, a single square opening was left unpaved around a rowan tree; its trunk is twisted as rope and thick as a man’s thigh, yet its crown rises barely higher than a doorway, pruned to a permanent, unchanging sphere by the winter wind that skims the rim of the hollow.

Movement is rare enough that the eye catches every instance of it from across the entire settlement. A pair of red-billed choughs occasionally drops over the eastern cliff, folding their wings to land on the slate ridge of a tall granary; their claws click sharply against the stone before they stand motionless in the sun. Down in the shaded angles of the curbstones, small, pale-shelled snails remain sealed to the rock by dried mucus for days at a time, waiting for fog. Sometimes a mountain hare sits upright in the middle of a broad thoroughfare, ears black-tipped against the grey paving, looking like another carved marker until it lopes three paces and stops again.

Because the basin walls block the wider winds of the ridge, the interior holds a pocket of profound quiet. High overhead, air can be heard brushing past the upper crags—a faint, dry rushing like distant surf—while down on the terraces a stray feather lies unmoving on a step. When a single drop detaches from a cistern lip and strikes the standing water below, the *plock* carries cleanly across two plazas, briefly defining the hard, resonant volume of the bowl before the silence closes back over it. Nothing is decaying, nothing is growing larger, and nothing waits to be finished; stone, water, lichen, and cool air have arrived at an exact truce, holding their positions under the clear sky.
