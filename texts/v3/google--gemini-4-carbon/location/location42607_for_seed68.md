The whole settlement fits inside a single split in the lowland rock, a flat-bottomed cleft no longer than four city blocks and narrow enough that a good throw from the eastern rim would carry a stone halfway across the floor. Yet standing at the bottom, the place feels startlingly open. Nothing crowds anything else. Between one structure and the next stretches fifty or sixty yards of bare, pale greystone, fractured cleanly along flat bedding planes like slate struck once with a chisel. There is no soil in the flats, no sand, not even pebbles rounded by weather—only sharp-cornered plates and freshly exposed mica facets that catch the clear, direct daylight like scattered salt. The cliffs enclosing the basin look as though they dropped apart within living memory. Every ledge meets its vertical face at a crisp ninety degrees; fallen slabs at the margins still bear matching mineral veins to the scars directly above them, clean of dust and untroubled by frost-shatter.

Into this raw stone room, an entire electrified town-ward has been bolted, spaced out with the deliberate, airy geometry of chess pieces near the end of a game.

Only eleven towers rise from the basin floor, with another half-dozen shops, bathhouses, and transformer sheds cantilevered straight out from the sheer rock walls on riveted steel brackets. Each tower does double duty: the lower three stories are massive brick engine-houses pierced by arched, multipaned windows, while the upper three or four stories are narrow urban tenements faced in glazed green tile, complete with iron fire escapes, potted root-boxes, and grocer’s pulleys. Wide, quiet-looking plazas of raw bedrock separate them, crossed only by narrow-gauge tram rails laid directly onto iron sleepers pinned into the stone, and by long, solitary strings of streetlamps on cast-iron poles. Overhead, high in the bright cool air, heavy braided steel cables and bundles of copper busbars strung from white porcelain insulators span the emptiness from cliff to tower to cliff, casting sharp, thin diagonal lines across the bright floor below.

Despite all that empty visual space, the air is deafening.

The fresh, unweathered rock walls act like an uncovered cistern of polished granite, throwing back every vibration without softening an edge. Underlying everything is the continuous, chest-vibrating roar of high-pressure air bleeders at the tower bases and the steady sixty-cycle thrum of the open-air transformers—a deep mechanical chord that fills the whole stone trough. Overtop of the machinery rides an immense, chaotic din of animals. Thousands of cliff-dwelling birds, heavy-bodied creatures the size of gulls with glossy blue-black backs and hooked yellow bills, crowd the warm brick cornices, the steel trusses, and the porcelain insulator racks, keeping up a relentless, metallic clattering and trumpeting that echoes four times before dying. Whenever a pressure valve vents a sharp two-second shriek of vapor, whole sheets of them lift into the sunlit air between the towers, wheeling lazily through the high wires in noisy, glittering clouds before settling back onto a neighboring ledge.

The machinery runs slowly—almost imperceptibly so—because the cleft itself is the engine. The lowland plateau around the basin is still pulling apart at the rate of a fingernail’s breadth each week, a patient, low-elevation rifting with no heat or tremor beneath it. The heavy cables spanning the basin are anchored deep into opposing cliffs and wound around twenty-foot cast-iron drums inside the lower brick halls. As the rift widens by fractions of a millimeter, the tension draws the drums through immense step-up gear trains. Through the tall, clean glass of the ground-floor windows, brass governors the size of dining tables revolve at a stately, hypnotic stroll; giant counterweights of Pig-iron suspended beside the external walls drop less than an inch an hour along greased vertical rails. Everything mechanical here possesses immense torque and almost no hurry. Even the single-car electric tram that ferries residents and tool-crates across the wide empty bedrock moves at the pace of an unhurried walker, humming softly beneath its overhead trolley pole.

There is no creek on the basin floor, no drainage ditch, and rain passes over the low country only in brief, stray squalls weeks apart. Yet half the vertical surfaces are smothered in riotous, dark-green jungle.

The moisture comes out of the cool air itself, wrung out wherever industrial warmth meets cold young stone, or where deep pneumatic intakes sweat perpetually in the shade. Every brick engine-house wears a swollen, dripping skirt of greenery thirty feet high. Exhaust flues breathe continuous, lazy plumes of warm vapor against the chilly rock walls; five yards downwind of a vent, the sharp-angled cliff disappears completely beneath tiered overhangs of tree-ferns with fronds eight feet long, thick velvet mattresses of liverwort, and tangled curtains of waxy, broad-leaved climbers whose aerial roots dangle twenty feet into empty space, dripping bead by slow bead onto the dry stone below. Moisture collects inside the cold steel ribs of the cantilevered walkways, condensing every morning in the cool air until the ironwork is entirely encased in glossy clubmoss, bloated pale-green pitcher plants, and wild figs whose pale roots strangle the rivets.

This produces a stark, patchy landscape: twenty yards of blindingly clean, bone-dry grey bedrock where boot heels click crisply in the sun, ending abruptly at an opulent wall of black-green foliage ten feet thick, smelling intensely of crushed fern, damp coal-oil, and cold ozone.

Within those lush, sweating pockets, a second tier of wildlife thrives at astonishing density. Thumb-sized copper-colored frogs pack the water-cups of epiphytes by the hundreds, adding a piercing, rhythmic *tink-tink-tink* like small hammers hitting pipe-work to the birds’ clamor above. Long-tailed, gray-furred climbers—something between a marten and a macaque—use the greased tension cables and copper ground-straps as highways between the vertical gardens, pausing on sunny iron turnbuckles to crack hard-shelled snails against the bolts while ignoring the residents hanging quilts from tenement balconies ten feet away. Fat, slow-moving beetles the size of pocket watches graze in clusters along the wet lime scale where brass condenser pipes meet the stone, their lacquered shells shining in the clear daylight.

Life in the apartments and cantilevered workshops accommodates the noise and the slowness as simple physical laws, like gravity. People rarely shout across the wide plazas; instead, small enameled semaphore paddles click quietly outside stairwell windows to signal when bread comes out of the electric ovens in Tower Four, or when a mechanic is needed on the western anchor-wall. Children cross the broad, sparse expanses of raw rock on wooden stilts or low wheeled carts, steering wide around the sharp, step-like fractures in the stone floor and tossing bruised fruit husks into the base of the fern-thickets, which immediately boil with rustling tails and snapping beaks.

In the cool brightness of mid-afternoon, the sun clears the sharp western lip directly enough to illuminate the whole basin at once. Shadows drop straight and black behind every iron pole, every razor-edged boulder, every solitary brick stack. A main tension pawl inside Tower Seven reaches the end of its three-hour travel and drops into the next tooth with a single, percussive *clank* loud enough to shudder through shoe soles fifty yards away. A thousand blue-backed birds burst off the sunlit wires in a deafening wave of wingbeats and brassy calls, circle once across the wide, uncrowded air between the verdant walls, and settle right back down where they were, while the brass governors behind the glass keep turning at the speed of clock hands, condensing bright beads of water out of the crisp air onto their own cold frames.
