You feel the ground through your teeth before your eyes adjust to the dark.

The entire basin—twenty miles from rim to rim, shallow as a saucer—is vibrating at a pitch just above the threshold of hearing and simultaneously roaring at a pitch just below it. There is no silence anywhere in the valley to measure the noise against. It is a continuous, concussive wall of sound: the subterranean thunder of massive turbines turning in flooded housings deep underfoot, overlaid by the tearing-canvas shriek of high-pressure steam and cold mist venting from exhaust stacks spaced miles apart across the dark plain. The air presses against the eardrums with physical weight. Speak, shout, or strike iron with iron, and the sound vanishes instantly, swallowed at your lips.

Nothing underfoot was ever rock, and nothing has a sharp edge left.

If you dug down a hundred feet, you would hit no clay, no limestone, no granite—only older, denser strata of foundry slag, vitrified brick, compressed ash, vulcanized matting, and laminated steel plate. This is a landscape constructed down to its bedrock, and then left out in the cold rain for so long that time has done to the factory floor what it does to mountain ranges. What were once retaining walls, rail beds, gantry footings, and transformer plinths have slumped and weathered into low, rounded swells, gentle knuckles of smoothed concrete from which rusted rebar emerges only as faint, ochre-stained veins flush with the surface. Bolts and rivets have been polished down to barely perceptible blisters. Expansion joints have widened and softened into shallow, meandering creeks. The whole floor of the basin has settled into a final, exhausted topography—a dark, undulating pavement of worn aggregate and dull iron, slick with perpetual condensation.

Water is everywhere, though never deep. It sheets across the shallow pans in dark films an inch thick, seeping endlessly up through hairline fractures in the ancient decking and weeping down the sides of rounded concrete hummocks. Because of the vibration, the water never lies still. Every puddle, every flooded wheel-rut and wide, shallow catchment is locked in a rigid, trembling geometry of standing Faraday waves—millions of tiny, crisp diamonds of black water shivering in place, shifting pattern instantly whenever a distant valve slams shut and changes the dominant chord of the valley’s roar.

The air is cold—the damp, penetrating chill of an unheated cellar—and thick with drifting, metallic-tasting fog. Overhead, low cloud bellies drag across the darkness, lit from within only by a faint, bruised-plum twilight that never brightens into day and never quite drops into pitch black. Across the vast emptiness of the pan, artificial lights burn in sparse isolation. Three miles off, a cluster of sodium-amber beacons glows dully through a translucent crust of mineral scale that has grown over the lenses across centuries, turning each lamp into a dim, weeping stalactite of orange glass. Farther out, almost at the limit of vision, pale blue-white flashes stutter irregularly against the low clouds—exposed busbars or sagging high-tension lines arcing cleanly across the wet air into grounding rods worn thin as needles.

Between these points lies immense, uncluttered space. You can walk for an hour across the wet, shivering contours of worn iron and fused gravel without encountering anything taller than your knee. That emptiness makes the surviving machines look less like buildings and more like geological vents.

They stand miles apart: immense, windowless cowls of cast steel and monel metal, each the size of a city block, their once-angular housings eroded by millennia of acid drizzle and grit into sleek, sloped domes like wet river boulders. Inside their five-foot-thick casings, protected from the weather that has Planed the rest of the world nearly flat, heavy industrial cycles continue without pause or supervision. Every twelve minutes, a dome two miles to the north inhales. You feel the barometric drop across the open flats; the fog rushes sideways toward its sunken intake grilles, skimming over the puddles, and the pitch of the valley’s deafening choir drops half an octave into a chest-rattling grind as millions of gallons of cold surface water are sucked down through subterranean culverts. Four minutes later, an exhaust stack five miles west blows its relief dampers—a towering, invisible column of cold, atomized water and compressed air blasting skyward with a sound like a continuous cannonade, falling back across the leeward miles as a sudden, drenching downpour.

The valley makes its own weather out of pumping schedules.

Nothing with nerves or lungs lives here. There are no birds Quartering the mist, no rats in the conduits, no worms in the black silt of ground-up glass and rust that collects in the hollows, not even midges above the standing water. The acoustic pressure alone would rupture small eardrums and scramble the navigation of insects, and the water carries a bitter load of dissolved copper, zinc, and machine oil leaked from sealed sumps deep below. The stillness of the spaces between the machines has that distinct, unmistakable emptiness of a world without eyes—nothing darts away when a shadow moves; nothing waits under a ledge.

Yet things grow. They are sparse, lonely, and entirely adapted to metal, current, and noise.

Along the warm seams where buried steam-mains still leak faint heat through ten yards of fused overburden, thin ribbons of pale, gristly lichen cling to the smoothed concrete, branching in jagged, dendritic patterns that trace the invisible paths of stray electrical ground-currents. In the shallow drainage channels, sparse clumps of black, wire-stiff reeds stand three feet high, spaced widely apart like hair on old skin. Their stems contain so much drawn-up iron that they clink faintly when the wind pushes them together, though no one could ever hear it. Strangest are the bladder-mats that colonize the vibrating puddles near the turbine vents: flat, rubbery rosettes the color of bruised liver, anchored to rust-nodules. When they are ready to spore, they do not wait for wind or animals; they stiffen their stalks until they catch the exact resonant frequency of the ground’s roar, shattering their own spore-caps into a fine grey smoke that the intake drafts pull miles across the basin.

Now and then, the flat horizon changes shape. Half a mile away, a low ridge that looked like a permanent fold of ancient slag shudders, breaks its seal of black silt, and rises six feet on massive, pitted hydraulic rams. Water cascades off its rounded back in sheets, gleaming faintly in the distant violet flash of an electrical arc. It is a weir-gate, threequarters of a mile long, adjusting the drainage gradient from one sector of the pan to another according to the blind logic of counterweight floats and pressure governors buried in grease-packed vaults beneath the floor. Slowly, water across hundreds of acres reverses direction, its trembling, geometric ripples sliding smoothly backward over worn foundry marks and vanished roadways toward a dark intake mouth three miles away.

Once the gate locks into place, the mud settles back around its edges. The rain from the western exhaust stack drifts past, cold on the face, smelling of wet iron and ozone. Under the low dark, the smooth, ancient floor shines like polished pewter, drinking the water down just to pump it up again, roaring at the empty clouds at the top of its iron voice.
