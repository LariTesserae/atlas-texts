There is no dirt here, and no bedrock beneath the floor. If you were to take an oxy-acetylene torch to the heavy, diamond-plate iron underfoot and cut downward, you would breach a warm-water return main, then a structural I-beam, then a ceramic cable-conduit, and eventually drop a wrench into a dark, humid maintenance plenum sitting over another layer of identical diamond-plate. The floor is a continent. It stretches away in flat, terraced basins, stepping down only a foot or two every few miles, bound together by rivets the size of fists until the sheer curvature of the world pulls the far scaffolding below the white, hazy horizon.

The air is warm—about eighty-five degrees—and smells faintly of machine oil, wet slate, and warm copper. Light falls evenly from an overcast sky that never clears and never darkens into storm. It is a bright, shadowless daylight, produced by the permanent thermal equilibrium between the vast, warm iron floor and the cooler upper atmosphere. Because the machinery below never stops radiating its steady, low-grade heat, the air is perpetually saturated. Every surface sweats.

Look closely at the infrastructure, and its youth is startling. This is an industrial sprawl the size of an ocean, packed tight with valve-trees, cooling towers, catwalks, transformer banks, and miles of bundled brass and cast-iron piping, yet none of it is rusted through. The edges of the steel gusset plates are still crisp from the shear. The stamped serial numbers on the turbine housings haven’t been softened by wear. Faint, pale-orange blooms of surface oxidation dust the weld seams, and shallow, mineral-white water stains streak the sides of the galvanized vats, but the lathe-marks on the brass couplings still catch the flat daylight. It looks like a foundry complex finished three years ago and left running on an infinite, closed-loop throttle.

Nothing clangs. There are no pistons slamming, no steam whistles, no grinding gears. The industry here is entirely fluid and electrical, operating in deep, subterranean equilibrium. What rises to the surface is only a profound, physical hum—a sixty-cycle vibration you feel in the soles of your boots rather than hear—accompanied by the gentle, continuous patter of condensation. Warm water beads on the undersides of ten million overhead pipes, swells, lets go, and *plinks* into the shallow puddles standing in the stamped troughs of the deck plates. In the stepped basins, clear warm water collects to the depth of an ankle, lapping sluggishly against the footings of vertical iron stanchions before slipping silently through bronze floor-grates.

There is not a blade of grass in ten thousand miles. No moss softens the bolts; no weeds split the mastic joints. The only concession to non-animal growth is a flat, velvety black biofilm, slick as axle grease, that coats the submerged grates and the overflow lips of the cooling weirs, feeding directly on dissolved iron and sulfur in the warm runoff.

And yet, you cannot put your foot down without nudging something alive.

The architecture is smothered in quiet, heavy, warm-blooded—or warm-adapted—fauna. Every horizontal pipe, every recessed flange, every warm transformer casing is occupied. Across a row of waist-high steam jackets, thousands of broad, shell-less gastropods the size of loaves of bread sit flank-to-flank, their pale grey mantles rippling in slow, synchronized waves as they graze the microscopic film precipitated by the sweating metal. They are warm to the touch, dense as wet clay, and move only inches an hour. 

Look up into the dense latticework of the overhead gantries and pipe-racks—stacked thirty, forty, fifty feet high in tangled corridors that block out half the bright sky—and the iron seems upholstered. Millions of blind, segmented crawlers with matte, papery carapaces the color of primer-red and zinc-grey cling to the steel webbing. They hang in overlapping clusters like shingle tiles around the heat-exchangers, drinking from the condensation seams, shifting over one another with a dry, barely audible *shhh-shhh* like shifting grain. 

In the ankle-deep basins, flat, translucent ribbon-eels lie packed together like spilled linguine across the diamond-plate, absorbing heat straight through their bellies from the water-mains pulsing beneath the deck. Long-legged waders—creatures built like stripped-down Pelicans made of cartilage, oil-slicked leather, and pale bone, entirely wingless because there are no predators to fly from and no seasons to outrun—stand shoulder to shoulder in the warm shallows. There are tens of thousands of them visible down a single straightaway corridor between two aqueduct walls. They do not squabble. They do not call out. They dip their spatulate bills into the warm water, sift out ribbon-eels and slug-spawn with a soft, wet *clack-clack* of keratin, swallow, and stand motionless again in the white glare.

The strangeness of the place lies in its utter lack of urgency. On a natural world, life surges and starves with the tilt of an axis or the passing of a front. Here, the climate is bolted down. The light remains fixed at that bright, high-noon overcast—perhaps the sky sits beneath a permanent, high-altitude vapor shroud generated by the world-machine itself, diffusing the sun into a timeless luminous ceiling. The water never cools by half a degree. The biofilm grows at an exact millimeter per week; the slugs graze it back at an exact millimeter per week; the cartilage-waders harvest the slugs at the precise rate they spawn. 

Walk for three days down the narrow steel alleys. You have to step carefully, sliding your boots across the wet iron rather than lifting them, gently shoving aside warm, heavy bodies that yield without panic and close back behind your heels like heavy batter. You climb a crisp, freshly galvanized ladder—wiping harmless, warm-bellied scale-mites off the rungs with the side of your glove—to gain the top of a forty-foot condenser drum, hoping for a landmark. 

From the top, the view opens out to the limits of atmospheric haze. Far off, five or ten miles away, the warmth rising from a particularly dense knot of manifolds creates its own slow, vertical mist-column, drifting straight up into the bright grey ceiling without a breath of wind to bend it. Everywhere else, as far as the eye can resolve detail, the low, dense labyrinth of pipes, catwalks, grates, and basins repeats in crisp, young steel. Every railing is lined with silent waders; every warm dome is mounded with breathing flesh. A drop of condensed water swells on the rim of a pressure gauge beside your knee—glass clean, brass bright, needle trembling imperceptibly on the exact center mark—hangs for three seconds, falls into a crowded puddle with a tiny *plink*, and immediately begins to gather again.
