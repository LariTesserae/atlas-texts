The space is no larger than a root cellar, wedged forty feet beneath the drowned peat of a cold coastal fen, where a recent slump cracked a seam of black traprock cleanly in two. The fracture is young enough that its walls have not yet been smoothed by silt or current. Every ledge, step, and jutting prism of stone keeps its razor edge, fresh as cleaved glass, though you cannot see these edges until your knuckles graze them. 

There is almost no room here that is not already occupied. The cavity is flooded to its pinched ceiling with water just above freezing, dense and mineral-heavy, tasting of iron and crushed fern. Only at the highest corner, tucked into an irregular three-inch cleft where two slabs lean together, does a flat blister of stale air survive, flattened against the rock like mercury. Everything else is liquid, and the liquid is packed solid with cold-water growth.

What grows here requires no sun. Thick, rubbery bolsters of black liverwort coat the fresh-split stone, crowding over one another in overlapping lobes the size of open hands. Out from between them push tangles of pale, translucent bladder-fronds and stiff, cartilaginous stalks tufted with dark red filaments. They grow so densely from every vertical and overhanging face that their branches interlock across the center of the chamber, turning the six-foot span of water into a continuous, cold thicket. Moving six inches requires parting cold, slick stems that immediately press back together behind you with slow, muscular patience.

There is no daylight, and no path for daylight to ever reach this low. What keeps the darkness from being absolute is a faint, sourceless bruised-violet stain that clings to the sharp fracture-lines of the rock itself—not bioluminescence from the plants, which are entirely blind and dull, but a dim cold leakage out of the freshly broken stone, as if the split occurred slightly out of alignment with the rest of the earth and something unlit is showing through the seam. Near these violet hairlines, the geometry of the pocket begins to fray.

You feel it before you make sense of it: the water does not all pull the same way. 

In the left half of the chamber, shed spores and minuscule flecks of slate drift upward over the course of hours, gathering like sediment against the ceiling. Two feet to the right, entangled in the exact same braid of pale stems, dead fronds sink normally toward the jagged floor. Between the two zones hangs a vertical sheet of water slightly colder and thicker than the rest, clear as ice, across which stems pass at bent, impossible angles—not refracted, because there is hardly light enough for refraction, but physically kinked in space. If you follow a single tough runner with your fingers from left to right, you feel it turn ninety degrees without bending, continuing straight into a wall that ought to be two feet further back, only to emerge again right beside your wrist. The pocket is smaller on one side of its center than on the other, yet completely enclosed.

Someone knew this before the fissure finished flooding, or else found a way down through the peat pipes afterward and worked entirely by touch. Wedged tightly into the crooks of the sharp basalt ledges, half-swallowed now by encroaching mats of black moss, sit small, deliberate constructions of split bone, marsh-reed twine, and flat gray pebbles unrelated to the surrounding rock. They are no bigger than birdcages—little three-sidedlintels and stacked cairns tied together under tension so they do not drift apart in the confused gravity. Inside one, pinned down by a shard of traprock, three knotted cords of braided hair pull taut in three separate directions at once, each knot straining toward a different "up." Another stack sits upside down on the underside of a jutting shelf, its unmortared river stones resting securely upward against the rock, held fast by whatever localized weight governs that ten-inch corner. There is no metal on any of them, no carved script, no cut marks made by anything sharper than flint. Only knots, balanced stones, and small hollow reeds wedged into the glowing purple cracks like listening tubes.

Sound barely travels through the packed vegetation. The dense mats of frond and liverwort swallow vibration almost instantly. Mostly there is an immense, cold, pressurized hush. Every ten or twelve minutes, a single bubble of marsh gas squeezes out from the lower silt, clicks faintly against a stiff leaf-stalk, and begins a long, erratic crawl—sliding sideways along a stem, pausing at an invisible boundary in the water, flattening itself against nothing, and then slipping slowly downward into a crack in the floor, making a tiny, wet *tock* as it disappears into solid stone. 

Very little lives among the plants. There are no fish; there is nowhere to swim. Once in a great while, a blind white flatworm, broad as a thumbprint and thin as wet paper, glides out from one of the miniature stone lintels. It stretches across the cold gap between one leaf and the next, easing half its body across a seam in the water's logic—so that for several minutes its head feeds slowly on bacterial film near the ceiling while its tail is still creeping across the gravel at the very bottom of the room, unbroken, unhurried, grazing across a chamber that has not yet finished deciding what shape it is.
