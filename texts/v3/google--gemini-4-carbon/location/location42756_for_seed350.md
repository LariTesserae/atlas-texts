There is no true surface here, only a violently disputed boundary that tears and re-knits itself at different heights across the basin. You are almost entirely underwater, except where the water forgets to occupy a cubic meter of space and leaves a jagged pocket of hot, pressurized steam hanging thirty feet down, or where a vertical wall of brine stands upright like a structural column before collapsing sideways into foam. 

The liquid is warm—blood-warm, bordering on feverish—and thick with suspended silt and chopped chlorophyll, reducing the light to a bruised, olive-amber gloom. Far overhead, if there is a sky, it registers only as a shifting ceiling of leaden, over-bright smears, instantly swallowed by the turbidity. Light mostly comes sideways, stuttering out of submerged arc-lamps and the white-hot cavitation halos spinning off orphaned machinery.

The sound does not travel through this medium so much as it occupies it as a physical, crushing weight. It is a continuous, deafening concussion: the low-frequency gut-roar of millions of tons of warm water colliding with itself, perforated by the metallic shriek of unlubricated turbines running past redline and the sharp, percussive *crack* of stone splitting open. In a fluid this dense, the noise enters straight through the ribs and jawbone.

Despite the relentless hydraulic violence, nothing at the bottom of this broad, shallow depression is worn smooth. The basin spans maybe three miles from rim to rim—a low pan of freshly extruded, pitch-black basalt and raw grey clinker—and every edge of it is razor-sharp. Slabs of rock jut out of the churning murk with fractures so fresh their crystal facets haven't even dulled. Water hammers against a spire of brittle volcanic glass at forty knots and fails to round its corners, because time and friction are uncoupling down here. When a current hits a ridge hard enough to snap it, the broken shard doesn't tumble away and turn into gravel; it flickers, duplicates its own edge, and re-welds itself three inches to the left, jutting up sharper than before. There is no sand. There are no pebbles. Only jagged, newly minted geometry and black mud made of pulverized plant matter.

Out of those knife-edged fissures grows an overwhelmingly dense, thrashing jungle of warm-water flora. Thick, rubbery stolons anchor directly into raw stone and rusted iron alike, sending up forests of broad, perforated strap-leaves, bloated gas-bladders the size of oil drums, and muscular vines plated in overlapping green scales. They grow with manic, spasmodic speed, fed by the soup of dissolved minerals and thermal vents. Because the currents never settle into a single direction—ripping north at the floor, corkscrewing upward six feet higher, shearing violently east above that—the vegetation is permanently whipped into frenzied, braided tangles. Fronds ten feet long snap like wet canvas, shred at the tips, and visibly push out fresh, pale-green meristems from the torn veins within minutes. 

Nothing eats them. Part a curtain of slick, boiling bladder-kelp, check the underside of the broadest frond, sift a handful of the hot black muck between the rock teeth: there isn't a single snail, shrimp, worm, minnow, or water-beetle. No schools dart through the gloom; no scavengers cling to the stalks; no insects buzz inside the stray pockets of trapped air. The absence is absolute, almost sterile inside the rot. When plant matter dies, there are no mouths to break it down—only bacterial slimes and the mechanical blender of the turbulence itself, beating dead stalks into green grease that coats everything in a slick film.

Scattered every few hundred yards across the drowned flat sit the machines. There is no town here, no roads, cables, or catwalks connecting them—just solitary, brutalist industrial installations bolted straight into the fresh rock, operating at frantic capacity for no discernible network. 

A three-story cast-iron pump house sits tilted in a cleft of sharp shale, half its brickwork sheared cleanly away, exposing twin flywheel pistons the size of locomotives. They slam back and forth through flooded cylinders, boiling the water immediately around their housings into a permanent shroud of white vapor, pumping massive volumes of river-warm brine out of an intake pipe and blasting it directly out a discharge nozzle ten feet away. Half a mile off, visible only as a rhythmic strobing smear through the thrashing weeds, an automated stamping mill hammers endlessly at an empty anvil, each drop sending a spherical shockwave through the water that temporarily forces the surrounding liquid into geometric lattices—cubes and octahedrons of clear, dry space—before the murk slams back in with a thunderclap. The breakdown of order thickens near these engines, though whether the machines cause the fractures in the world or were built specifically to brace against them is impossible to tell. Approach a submerged boiler tower and gravity develops a grain, pulling diagonally toward the intake grates. Currents loop back into themselves, forming tight, roaring toroids of water that spin faster than the surrounding fluid, trapping uprooted bushes in endless, blurring orbits. 

Sometimes a sheet of flatness slices right through the basin at chest height—an abrupt, localized horizon where the water simply stops above and resumes two feet higher, leaving a narrow horizontal slot of steaming, deafeningly loud air stretching across half a mile of drowned valley.
