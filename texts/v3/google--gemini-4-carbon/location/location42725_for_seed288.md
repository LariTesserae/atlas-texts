The depression has no sharp edges left. Millions of years ago, the limestone roof of a massive subterranean basin collapsed, and in the ages since, wind and gravity have planed the rim down to a long, gentle slope of grey dust. You walk downward for half a day before realizing you are below the level of the surrounding plain. There are no cliffs here, only a vast, sunken bowl miles across, floored in fine, dry silt and ancient, rounded cobbles that have forgotten the river that made them.

The air is cool and profoundly dry, tasting of chalk and old stone. Above, a permanent lid of high, thick cloud—or perhaps the underside of a higher overhang miles up—filters the daylight into a flat, pewter twilight. Shadows do not have edges; they are merely darker smudges beneath things.

Spread across the broad floor of this basin is a city, though no human hand ever laid a course of its stone. 

It is an architecture of cemented silt, dried saliva, and packed guano, built over tens of thousands of years by generations of colonial insects and burrowing mammals, then abandoned, worn smooth by the dry wind, and reoccupied by larger creatures. The structures rise like squat, fluted towers, terraced mounds, and sprawling, multi-tiered pueblos, colored the pale ochre and bone-white of the valley floor. Because there is no rain to melt the mud-brick, nothing ever washes away. The oldest districts at the center of the basin have simply settled under their own weight, their lower chambers compressed into solid rock, their doorways worn into wide, polished ovals by the passage of countless flanks and feet. 

Between these massive, porous complexes lie wide, empty avenues of swept gravel. The city is sparse, laid out with the generous spacing of creatures that require territory, leaving broad plazas of pale dust between one towering warren and the next. 

Yet the masonry is draped in deep, dry green. 

Where water is absent, the flora lives on the cool, stagnant air and the rich nitrogen of the ancient mounds. Thick, velvety mats of grey-green moss and silver lichen upholster the leeward sides of the towers. From the high fissures and empty window-sockets hang forty-foot curtains of rootless air-plants, their tough, strap-like leaves curled to trap the microscopic moisture exhaled by the city's inhabitants. In the wide, dusty spaces between the mounds, groves of giant succulents and pale, bloated-trunked shrubs grow with agonizing slowness, their waxy skins cool to the touch, their forms twisted into smooth, sculptural curves. A leaf dropped here does not rot; it dries to parchment and blows slowly across the avenues until it catches in a drift of grey chaff at the base of a wall.

You feel the sound in your teeth before you understand what is making it. 

It is a deafening, continuous roar—not chaotic, like a storm, but layered and relentless, filling the entire sunken bowl from rim to rim. The architecture itself acts as an amplifier. Every hollow spire, every honeycomb wall and subterranean gallery is packed with life. Millions of large, armored cicada-kin cling to the pale masonry and the hanging gardens of bromeliads, vibrating their tymbals in a massive, rolling drone that rises and falls in slow, oceanic waves. 

Underneath that high, metallic wall of insect-sound is a deeper, resonant rumble: the cooing,clicking, and wing-beating of hundreds of thousands of pale-feathered rock doves and blind, heavy-bodied swifts that roost in the upper tiers. Their movements stir a constant, dry draft. On the avenues below, herds of long-eared, goat-sized browsers pad slowly over the gravel, cropping the tough, fibrous skirts of the air-plants, their hooves clicking in a steady, clattering percussion, their throats producing low, honking contact-calls to pierce the din. Rock hyraxes and pale, thick-furred rodents pack the lower doorways, adding sharp, barking choruses that echo down the plastered tunnels.

Despite the overwhelming volume of noise and the sheer number of breathing bodies, very little seems to happen. The light does not shift noticeably from hour to hour. The cool temperature holds steady, buffered by the deep earth and the thick, dry walls. A flock of birds lifts from a eroded dome not in a sudden burst, but in a heavy, wheeling column that spirals slowly over the grey gardens before settling onto an adjacent mound. The browsers move a few paces an hour, chewing methodically, their pale coats blending into the dust. 

Even the builders—blind, thumb-sized termites that still maintain the deep foundations—work out of sight, adding a fraction of an inch of cemented dust to a buttress over the course of a year, patching what the dry wind gently sands away. The whole sunken metropolis sits in the grey gloom, roaring at the top of its lungs, utterly unhurried, wearing down grain by grain while building itself back up at the exact same pace.
