At three thousand fathoms above the nearest timberline, the sky loses its softness and turns a hard, mineral blue, dark toward the zenith even at noon. There should be nothing up here but rime ice and shattered stone. Instead, wedged into the split apex of a newly uplifted spire, there is a room-sized cleft of wet, deafening warmth.

The rock is geologically fresh—dark, glassy basalt and sharp-angled grey porphyry, split open recently enough that not a single edge has been rounded by weather. Every ledge is a blade; the talus underfoot consists of flat, clinking splinters and fresh prismatic columns that look pried apart yesterday. The enclosure is scarcely forty paces from one jagged sheer wall to the other, shaped like the hull of a narrow ship open to the sun. Step onto the notched rim, and the world drops vertically away into a blinding sea of stratocumulus clouds far below, where ordinary mountain ranges barely poke through like dark shoals. Step back down inside, out of the knife-thin wind, and the air immediately thickens into a warm, wet bath.

Heat wells up steadily through hairline fractures in the floor—not boiling, not violent, just a massive, patient exhalation from deep in the mountain’s plumbing. Where this warm, damp breath meets the freezing, low-pressure air at the rim of the cleft, moisture drops out continuously. It does not fall as rain; it simply materializes on every surface. The sharp basalt walls weep in unbroken sheets. Every fracture line channels a warm rivulet, collecting in angular, flat-bottomed basins before spilling silently over the lip into the abyss, where it freezes into mist fifty feet down.

Because the light at this altitude passes through so little atmosphere, it strikes the basin with raw, unbuffered clarity. Shadows under the rock overhangs are pitch-black, while every wet leaf and stone facet in the sun glares like polished metal. Beneath this fierce UV and inside the perpetual greenhouse warmth, vegetation crowds every square inch of the cleft that isn't sheer glass-rock. It is an improbable, marooned jungle, packed dense and overlapping, though subtly altered by the thinness of the air. Because carbon dioxide and oxygen are scarce up here, the plants grow thick, waxy, and heavy-bodied to hoard their gases, pushing right up against one another in layered tiers. Broad-leafed aroids with dark violet undersides—pigmented against the merciless sun—lean over rubbery, waist-high ferns whose coiled fiddleheads drip clear mucilage. Stiff cushions of lime-green moss bridge the razor-sharp crevices like upholstery, fed by warm condensate, yet when pressed they give way instantly to the raw stone blades underneath. Thick, woody creepers trace the thermal cracks closely, mapping the underground heat in twisty ribbons of foliage.

The whole stone chamber roars. 

Anchored directly into the widest fractured shelf at the center of the cleft sits an unmanned pressure-head and dynamo station—no walls, no roof, just naked heavy industry bolted down with thick steel pins into the fresh rock. Three cast-iron turbine housings, painted a faded machinery-green now streaked with orange mineral scale, straddle the main thermal fissure. Thick copper-jacketed pipes emerge from drilled boreholes, feeding subterranean gas and steam through a manifold of brass baffles and heavy flywheel governors. The noise is immense and completely unvarying: a deep, chest-vibrating mechanical drone layered beneath the high, tearing hiss of pressurized vapor bleeding through slotted vents. Contained by the steep basalt walls, the sound fills the small hollow entirely, pressing against the eardrums until it feels less like noise and more like a physical property of the air, as solid as the humidity.

Despite the violence of the sound, nothing moves quickly. The heavy iron flywheel turns at a steady, hypnotic crawl, throwing tiny beads of condensed water and amber grease from its rim onto the glossy leaves pressing against its base. Behind a thick disk of fogged glass, an oiled brass pressure needle sits motionless at a black hash mark, trembling slightly but never advancing or retreating. From the back of the dynamo housing, two braided steel power cables as thick as a human wrist run across the wet gravel, held aloft on short porcelain insulators spaced every ten feet, before disappearing straight over the eastern precipice into the clouds below. A rusted metal tool chest, chained to an eyebolt and half-swallowed by creeping liverworts, sits beside a short segment of narrow-gauge rail that goes nowhere—leftover staging from whatever high-altitude rig lowered this machinery onto the peak years ago.

Life without roots rarely makes it across the vertical miles of ice and thin air below, leaving the lush pocket strangely still beneath the roar. There is no buzz of flying insects; wings struggle to find purchase in air this thin. Instead, along the warm iron flanges of the pipes and across the wet basalt slabs, a few pale, fist-sized gastropods inch through the mineral slime, their coiled shells thickened and heavily ridged with white calcium leached from the vent water. High on a ceramic insulator, just out of reach of the rising steam plumes, a single dark-plumaged mountain swift sits fluffed in the bright sun, soaking up the radiant heat of the machinery, entirely indifferent to the hundred-decibel drone vibrating through its feet.

Hours pass here with almost no perceptible change beyond the slow tilt of the blinding white sun across the vertical stone walls. The warm vapor rises a few yards toward the hard blue sky, hits the invisible ceiling of freezing upper-troposphere wind at the cleft's rim, turns instantaneously into flat ribbons of white mist, and rolls back down the glass-sharp rocks as warm water, feeding the ferns pressed tight against the turning iron wheel.
