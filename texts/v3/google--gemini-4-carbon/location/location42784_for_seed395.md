Beneath everything lies a continental sheet of young flood basalt, flat and low, so recently cooled in geological time that no river has ever carved a channel across it and no rain has softened its edges. Where the dark rock breaks the surface, its fractures are still glassy and sharp, twisted into ropy coils and jagged plates that clink likefired ceramic if stepped on. There is no standing water anywhere—no creeks, no marshes, no dew-slicked hollows. The air smells of warm clay, dry resin, sun-baked chitin, and crushed succulent sap.

Yet there is scarcely a square foot of bare stone left exposed. Over this raw volcanic floor rises an unbroken, horizon-spanning metropolis of packed earth and living timber, crowded wall-to-wall across thousands of square miles.

It is a city built entirely without tools, raised grain by grain in the mandibles and beaks of its inhabitants. Towering spires of cemented red silt and pale subsoil—mined by deep-digging termites from damp strata far beneath the fractured basalt caprock—stand shoulder to shoulder like buttressed tenements, fifteen and twenty feet high. Each spire is fluted with cooling vanes, riddled with dark ventilation shafts, and joined to its neighbors by thick, vaulted arcades of dried mud that cross over the razor-edged lava like covered bridges. Between and directly out of the nutrient-rich skirts of these mounds grows a dense, interlocking forest of succulent and drought-hardy flora: swollen-trunked baobab-like trees with smooth coppery bark, thickets of towering green euphorbias ribbed like organ pipes, sprawling jade bushes with leaves as plump as thumbs, and woody vines that bind mound to tree and tree to mound in a single continuous fabric.

In the upper branches, communal birds have packed tons of dry grass stems into apartment-sized thatch structures with hundreds of downward-facing entrance tubes, roofing over the gaps between the earthen chimneys until the space below becomes a labyrinth of shaded plazas, narrow alleys, and low-ceilinged passages. There is no open ground. Where a mound ends, a buttress root begins; where a root dips into a fissure of black rock, a plastered mud highway runs along its spine, flanked by tight rosettes of spiky agave and velvety, heat-reflecting shrubs.

Daylight falls clear and bright across the roof of this world, baking the tips of the clay chimneys to brick-pale ochre and warming the waxy upper canopy. Down in the packed lanes between the mounds, the light filters into an amber half-shade, striped by shafts of dust-motes. The warmth is steady, dry, and enveloping, held overnight by the thermal mass of millions of tons of earthen masonry and radiated gently back through the morning.

For all its crowding, the place is astonishingly quiet.

There are no running streams to murmur, and the packed labyrinth of thick clay walls and fleshy, water-hoarding leaves acts like an endless series of baffles, swallowing the wind before it can drop below the canopy. More than that, the abundant animal life moves in an almost total hush. Inside the mud arcades and beneath the crusted tunnels, millions of pale, blind workers haul damp pellets of deep clay upward from the subterranean water table, repairing cracks and extending galleries in absolute darkness, communicating entirely through chemical trails and faint, rapid knockings of their heads against the mud walls—a sound no louder from the outside than dry sand trickling through fingers.

Outside, in the narrow, sun-dappled streets between the towers, life is in constant, silent motion. Broad-headed lizards with shovel snouts patrol the vertical flutes of the mounds, snapping up stray workers who breach a damaged plaster seam; their claws make only the faintest dry *tick-tick* on the sun-hardened silt. Small, large-eared mammals with felted soles slip along the shaded causeways between root-buttresses, browsing on fallen seedpods and fleshy petals, freezing instantly when the shadow of a gliding hawk passes overhead. Snakes as patterned as dried lichen coil inside the abandoned lower chambers of older mounds, waiting for warmth-seeking rodents. Even the birds that maintain the massive woven-grass roofs above do not sing to claim territory; living centimeter from their neighbors in permanent visual contact, they court and argue through silently flared throat-feathers, rhythmic head-bobs, and the soft, dry rustle of stiffened tail-quills.

Every process feeds another without pause. When an old mud spire fractures under the pry of a thickening tree trunk, fresh squads of builders surge up from the basalt cracks within minutes, darkening the pale breach with wet, saliva-darkened clay hauled from fifty yards underground—the only moisture that ever reaches the surface world. Broad-leaved shrubs immediately send fine surface roots into the damp patch before it dries, drinking the builders' mortar, while beetles and tortoises scrape at the mineral-rich crust before it hardens to stone. Overhead, the birds weave fresh dry shoots—pruned from the upper twigs—into their sprawling roofs, while beneath those roofs, generations of dropped husks, feathers, and castings pile into deep, dry middens that feed dense carpets of ground-hugging succulents and burrowing skinks.

Only by climbing the highest, newest volcanic ridge—a raw black pressure-ridge of buckled basalt not yet fully swallowed by clay and greenery—can the true scale of the basin be seen. Low, flat, and unbroken, the packed fabric of pale ochre spires, thatched brown canopies, and deep jade foliage runs outward in every direction until the atmosphere turns it blue-gray at the curve of the earth.

At this scale, the living city makes its own weather. Each afternoon, the collective heat rising from millions of sun-baked earthen chimneys and respiring vents gathers into vast, invisible columns of warm dry air. Dust-devils of fine red silt lift silently off the mound-tops, braiding upward into towering convective plumes. High above the dry basin, miles up where the warm updrafts finally hit cold air, long parallel streets of flat-bottomed white clouds condense and drift across the bright sky. Their shadows—miles wide—sweep slowly across the packed, silent metropolis below, cooling five thousand earthen districts at a time, prompting a million reptiles to shift from the shaded west flanks of the towers to the warm east walls in a single, soundless wave, before the sun emerges again and the dry heat resumes.
