Far below the surface of the crust lies the White Hollow—a subterranean volume so immense its far walls are lost in the pale haze of internal horizon, and its ceiling is hidden miles overhead behind shifting banks of freezing fog. There is no stone underfoot, nor is there soil. Every boundary of this abyss is composed of a single, hyper-active botanical architecture.

To keep its gargantuan interior empty while remaining entirely plant, the flora grows exclusively as tensioned, vaulted shells and sweeping buttresses—thin, violently growing membranes of pale fibrous wood that enclose cubic miles of freezing, brightly lit air. 

The brightness is startling, clinical, and sourceless at a glance. Billions of bundled, translucent root-hairs hang from the unseen ceiling and spiral along the sweeping structural ribs, emitting a fierce, cold, actinic glare—like high-altitude noon trapped inside a glacial crevasse. Shadows are razor-sharp where a beam of living wood crosses another, then wash out entirely in the blinding white scatter of the distance. It is cold enough that breath turns instantly to ice dust, yet nothing sleeps. The vegetation runs on a frantic, exothermic chemistry at its growing tips, fueling the relentless light while leaving the vast ocean of air between the spans well below freezing.

Because this hollow is geologically young—a subterranean pocket only recently colonized and forced open—none of the wooden terrain has softened or rotted into humus. Everything is sharp, raw, and angled. Great planar floors of pale timber meet at jagged, splintered seams where two competing growth-fronts recently collided. There is no detritus; old wood does not decay here, it simply dries under the cold glare and fractures cleanly like flint.

The hollow is overwhelmingly empty, yet furiously unstable. To look across a ten-mile gulf of dazzling white air is to watch architecture tearing itself apart and rebuilding in real time. Great tensioned cables—vines thicker than aqueducts, stretched taut across three miles of void—constantly adjust their load as new vaults swell outward from the distant walls. Under the blinding light, whole translucent sheets of young cambium unroll across empty chasms at the speed of a brisk walk, stiffening into rigid bridges within minutes, while older, over-stressed ribs suddenly buckle. 

Despite this ceaseless violent rearrangement, the space is mostly quiet. The sheer volume of cold, thin air swallows noise before it can echo. You see the catastrophe long before you hear it: five miles out in the glare, an entire suspended terrace of jagged white bark snaps loose from its buttress, tumbling lazily downward into the lower depths like tossed kindling. Thirty seconds later, the sound arrives—not a roar, just a dry, crisp *crack*, like a knuckle popping in an empty room. Closer at hand, there is only the faint, continuous creak of swelling timber and the dry hiss of frost sliding off Tilting leaves.

Water exists only in pockets and sweating margins. Where the furious growth generates localized heat, plumes of steam bleed out of fresh bark-fissures, rise fifty feet into the freezing glare, and condense back down as brief, localized squalls of sleet or cold drizzle. Moisture collects in the freshly cracked V-shaped troughs where huge limbs meet, forming narrow, fast-running rivulets of slushy meltwater before freezing solid into clear lenses, or getting drunk instantly by thirsty nets of grey capillary roots. 

Animal life is sparse, small against the staggering distances, and adapted to perpetual motion. Across the miles of empty brightness, solitary gliders occasionally trace silent arcs—flat, broad-winged things colored like bleached parchment, riding the erratic thermal updrafts rising from newly split growth-zones. Closer to the shifting bark, long-limbed, spider-like browsers with hooked tarsals pick their way across the sharp ridges, scraping frozen sap-pearls from the fissures. They freeze constantly, feeling the vibrations through their feet whenever the wooden continent beneath them prepares to lurch.

Scattered thinly across the more stable, older nodes—places where six or seven colossal trusses lock together in temporary equilibrium—are the traces of people. They are tiny, stubborn interruptions in the pale expanse. Using heavy stone wedges, bone-handled adzes, and crude copper saws, travelers have learned to hijack the turbulent flora. You find deserted camps where someone drove flint shims deep into a growing seam three days ago, forcing the surging wood to curl upward and roof itself over into a windbreak before freezing hard. 

There are dark smudges of charcoal tucked into dry wooden alcoves—startling spots of black in a world of blinding cream, ice-grey, and translucent green. Braided ropes of harvested root-fiber hang from jagged spurs, marking routes across expanses that have already shifted twenty yards to the east since the ropes were tied. Here and there sits a wooden sled with runners shod in hammered native copper, abandoned when a structural plain tilted too steeply into the white abyss. Beside a slush-filled basin carved intentionally into the groin of a sweating bough, a row of simple ironwood pegs holds split-twig snares, waiting in the cold, glaring quiet for whatever pale creature comes to drink before the branch beneath it tears itself in two and starts becoming a wall.
