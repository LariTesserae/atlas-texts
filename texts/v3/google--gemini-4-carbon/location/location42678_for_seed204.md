The entire space is scarcely wider than a courtyard, wedged twenty-five feet deep into a freshly split joint of high-altitude granite. Overhead, a clean rectangle of pale, thin sky lets in direct daylight—bright, sharp-edged, carrying almost no warmth. The air smells of wet flint and ozone.

Because the rock cleaved recently—whether by tectonic heave or deliberate shear—its faces have not had time to round off. The granite walls step downward in crisp, angular planes, sparkling with raw mica and unweathered feldspar. Yet the pocket is unmistakably urban, a tiny, recessed node in a vast, sparse mountainside settlement whose neighboring structures remain entirely out of sight beyond the rim. 

Built directly into the jagged stone are terraced conduits and slim, cantilevered walkways made of a matte, ceramic-like metamaterial the color of slate. There are no rivets, welds, or visible seams where the synthetic architecture meets the raw cliff; the pale grey composite simply grows out of the mineral grain, bridging gaps with wafer-thin spans that do not flex under weight. Embedded flush into the walls are tall, vertical louvers of smoked glass. Behind them, faint interference patterns—pale violets and greens, like oil on water—ripple continuously upward, silent and rapid.

Though the ambient air hovers just above freezing, the space is constantly wet, caught in an active, localized weather cycle of its own making. At this elevation, clouds do not drift past so much as pass directly through the upper opening. Every few minutes, a ribbon of bright alpine fog drops into the cleft. As the mist touches the smoked-glass louvers and thedark metamaterial, some invisible, localized phase-shift pulls the moisture instantly out of suspension. The vapor snaps into liquid on contact, sheeting down the synthetic panels and bleeding onto the raw granite. 

Water is everywhere in motion, though never in deep accumulation. It weeps down the sharp rock facets in silver films, drips briskly from the undersides of the razor-thin walkways, and races along hair-fine micro-channels scored into the floor. Where the water strays out of reach of the infrastructure's subtle thermal fields, it freezes almost immediately into clear, needle-sharp rime along the shadowed stone joints, only to thaw and slough off minutes later when the angle of the sun shifts or a louver pulses warmly beside it. The floor is a shallow, stepped basin of wet black stone and seamless grates, perpetually slick, drinking the runoff down into hidden municipal cisterns below the frost line.

Nothing green grows here. There is no moss in the damp corners, no lichen softening the raw granite, not a single blade of grass wedged into the fractures. The cold, the thinness of the air, and the sheer youth of the cleaved stone leave the mineral surfaces entirely naked, save for a faint, soot-colored smear of non-photosynthetic microbes clinging to the warmest seam below a louver—a slick, oily tarnish no wider than a human hand.

Despite the constant, rapid industry of the water and the shifting light patterns inside the walls, the courtyard is remarkably quiet. The machinery uses no fans, pumps, or moving gears; whatever physics drives the condensation and draws heat through the stone operates below the threshold of sound. All that can be heard is the crisp, erratic percussion of water: a fast patter of drops hitting a wet walkway, the faint *clink* of a sliver of ice detaching from a granite overhang and striking the grates, the thin hiss of wind shearing across the open roof twenty-five feet above without ever quite reaching the floor. 

There is plenty of room between the built elements. A single, unrailed stair hugs the eastern face; a lone cylindrical pylon rises near the north corner, beaded with cold sweat; the rest of the pocket is left open to the wet rock and the bright sky. 

Once in a great while, a small, heavy-chested mountain finch drops over the rim to escape the ridge wind. It lands on a dry patch of warm metamaterial above a louver, fluffs its grey plumage against the chill, drinks two or three drops of freshly pulled sky-water from a chamfered edge, and watches the shimmering violet bands slide upward behind the glass. Beneath a submerged grate in the basin floor, a couple of pale, blind amphipods—translucent things adapted to high-altitude meltwater—dart out to test a newly fallen speck of windblown dust, then vanish back into the dark plumbing. 

Then another bright shred of cloud spills over the sharp granite lip. The air dims for three seconds, the louvers flare faintly with pale green light, a fresh sheet of water flashes across the walls, and the little room clears again, gleaming and cold under the hard mountain sun.
