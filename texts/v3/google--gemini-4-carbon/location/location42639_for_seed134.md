High in a jagged mountain pass, where a recent tectonic wrench split the dark basalt into a steep-sided cistern no wider than a cathedral nave, the water sits clear, cold, and impossibly bright.

Because the altitude puts the surface close to a thin, cloudless sky, daylight plunges straight to the bottom without losing its edge. There is no silt to soften it. The rock walls are young—newly broken, unweathered, full of sharp vertical steps, fresh conchoidal fractures, and brittle obsidian seams that haven't yet been rounded by time. Nothing grows on them. Aside from a faint, rusty mineral stain weeping from an iron-rich fault line near the floor, the stone is completely bare of moss, kelp, or algae. Yet you can barely see the stone at all, because the basin is packed wall-to-wall with slow, heavy, clamoring machinery that grew here on its own.

The fauna of this flooded shaft are litho-mechanical organisms, evolved from extremophile tube worms and high-altitude crustaceans that began smelting dissolved heavy metals through bacterial symbiotes millions of years ago. Without predators or room to roam, they have packed the vertical cleft into a dense, interlocking foundry of living brass, iron sulfide, and greased chitin. 

It is deafening underwater. Water conducts sound four times faster than air, and in this tight, hard-walled box of fresh rock, every noise ricochets endlessly until the whole liquid volume vibrates like the inside of a struck engine block. 

Nothing moves fast, because the water is just above freezing and metabolic budgets are tight, yet the sheer leverage of the creatures makes the slowness monumental. Anchored to the sharp basalt ledges are massive, barrel-shaped siphon-beasts wrapped in oxidized copper plating. Once every forty seconds, one of their intake valves snaps shut with the dull, percussive *thud* of a drop-forge, trapping a measure of mineral-heavy water. Beside them, stacked in suffocating proximity, colonies of blind, six-limbed crawlers cling to one another in greasy, metallic tiers. Their shells are matte black iron, jointed with pale, cartilaginous gaskets that hiss softly as they bleed excess pressure in thin, silver ribbons of hydrogen bubbles. 

There is no human hand anywhere in this place—no rivets, no straight welds, no symmetry of design, no ruined hull at the bottom to explain how it started. The industrialism is entirely anatomical. Spiral-shelled gastropods the size of millstones inch across the vertical rock faces at a rate of feet per week, their muscular feet secreting a harsh acidic slime that scores raw grooves into the fresh basalt. As they harvest the dissolved silicates and heavy ores, internalratchets within their shells wind tighter and tighter to store mechanical energy, clicking like massive, submerged clockwork—*clack... clack... clack*—echoing sharply off the narrow walls. 

Near the center of the flooded room, suspended in the open water column between the crowded walls, hangs a dense, tangled shoal of piston-jellies. They do not pulse with soft muscle; they haul themselves upward inches at a time using rigid, calcified crank-arms driven by internal gas bladders, venting sharp, rhythmic bursts of cloudy yellow steam from copper-rimmed spiracles before sinking slowly back down through the shaft of crisp mountain sunlight. Their bodies clank together when weak thermal currents nudge them, sounding like heavy iron chains dragging across a stone wharf.

Because the basin is so crowded and its inhabitants so slow, everything lives on top of everything else. Small, scavenger-crabs with pincers shaped like wire-cutters pick methodically at the flaking slag on the backs of the larger pump-worms, snipping away dead, oxidized hull-plates that drift down into the narrow trench at the floor. The bottom of the cistern isn't mud; it is a jagged talus slope of shed gears, brittle iron husks, snapped torsion-rods of bone, and fresh black rock chips, all brightly lit by the cold sun overhead.

At the very top of the water—just feet above the highest encrusted ledge—the boundary of the world is a flat ceiling of blinding white light. From beneath, you can see the wind whipping across the surface in sharp, silent ripples, though down in the cold mass of the basin the water barely stirs. A fine, oily sheen of biological lubricant, coughed up from a thousand slow-chugging vents below, gathers at the waterline in rainbow-tinted eddies, trapped between the sheer, dry, untouched cliffs of the outer mountain air. Beneath that thin, shimmering skin, the slow, cold factory grinds on, turning raw mountain rock into living iron at a pace too patient to watch and a volume too loud to bear.
