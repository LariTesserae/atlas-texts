You are standing at the top of a world that has finished being solid, though it refuses to lie flat. 

The elevation is dizzying, continental in its height, yet there are no jagged peaks left anywhere in sight. Everything has been ground down past rock, past gravel, past sand, into an ocean of hyper-fine, dry mineral talc—the ultimate end-state of a mountain range ten billion years old. If the air were still, this entire hemisphere would be a flat, silver-grey plain. Instead, the landscape is locked in violent, perpetual upheaval. 

Because the atmosphere is extraordinarily thin—a crisp, cool, dry envelope hovering just above freezing—there is almost nothing to carry sound. You watch a ridge the size of the Himalayas collapse three miles away. Millions of tons of pale, ancient dust slough off an invisible incline, boiling upward into towering, frantic plumes that twist and cannibalize themselves at highway speeds, and it happens in dead, eerie silence. All you hear is the faint, dry hiss of microscopic grains skating across your boots, like silk dragged over glass. 

Above, a normal, clear-white sun hangs in a sky that shades from pale slate at the horizon to a deep, airless indigo at the zenith. The light is sharp and shadow-heavy, catching the turbulent dust-fronts and turning them into blinding walls of silver.

What keeps this pulverized world at extreme altitude, and what drives its chaotic, silent storms, are the anchors. 

Scattered leagues apart across the churning vastness are structures left by an engineering discipline that treated gravity and inertia as malleable materials. They are small—no larger than a watchtower or a shipping container—and made of a seamless, matte-black substance that drinks the clear sunlight. Most are tilted, half-drowned in the swirling talc, or suspended effortlessly fifty feet in the air with no visible propulsion. They are field-pins, ancient tectonic stabilizers whose parameters frayed eons ago. Instead of holding the crust still, they have gone feral, cycling through erratic, conflicting gravity gradients and localized kinetic bursts. 

The terrain responds to them like iron filings to a panicked magnet. A valley will suddenly invert, the talc rushing skyward in a silent, furious funnel, holding the shape of a needle-thin spire for twenty minutes before the invisible field stutters, shifts three miles east, and lets the whole mass drop in a slow-motion, soundless avalanche. There is no water here—not a drop of moisture to bind the dust into mud, no ice to cement the slopes. Only dry friction, electrostatic charge, and the erratic, invisible geometry of broken physics. Where the dust rubs against itself hardest, brief, silent threads of violet static stitch through the grey plumes.

Life exists here, spaced out across massive, lonely intervals, surviving entirely on the kinetic energy of the chaos and the trace minerals in the air. 

You see the flora first as dark smudges on the blinding pale slopes. Up close, they resemble sprawling, brittle mats of copper and blackened wire. These plants have no roots; roots are useless when the ground relocates every hour. Instead, they grow in tangled, tumbleweed-like lattices that roll and surf atop the churning talc. Their stiff, metallic fronds vibrate constantly in the silent wind, harvesting piezoelectric charge from the friction of the dust storms to split trace atmospheric carbon. Occasionally, one is caught in a sudden updraft from a malfunctioning anchor below and launched half a mile into the cold sky, spinning lazily in the bright sun before drifting back down to another freshly formed ridge.

Fauna is rarer still. You might walk for four days along a temporarily stable crest—feeling the unsettling, spongy give of aerated dust beneath you, maintained only by a hum of localized field-tension you feel in your teeth rather than hear—before seeing anything move under its own will. 

When something does appear, it belongs to the emptiness. Vast, translucent things, like manta rays made of dry parchment and static electricity, ride the violent, silent updrafts between the canyons. They have no eyes and almost no mass; their bodies are ninety-percent hollow lattice, vast sails stretched across hair-thin silicate bones. They graze the upper reaches of the dust plumes, filtering airborne spores and shedding excess electrical charge from their wingtips in tiny, soundless sparks. Lower down, spider-like scavengers with legs three yards long skate across the shifting talc like water striders on a rapid river, clustering briefly around the matte-black tech artifacts to Bask in the waste-heat of decaying quantum cores, vanishing the instant the ground beneath them decides to become a cliff.

As the white sun drops toward the distant, heaving horizon, the shadows stretch for dozens of miles, razor-sharp and pitch-black. The temperature plummets instantly in the thin air. The turbulence doesn't slow down in the cold; if anything, the thermal drop triggers a fresh cascade among the scattered black monoliths. Across a hundred-mile vista, entire plateaus unmake themselves in the amber twilight. Great, sweeping curtains of silver-grey powder rise into the darkening sky, catching the last rays of daylight high above, twisting into monumental, intricate architectures that hold for a breath, silent as a photograph, before scattering back into the void.
