From any high spur, the distinction between geology and habitation fails miles before the horizon. The plateau is an upthrust continent of dark schist and pale, fractured granite, too young and too dry to have been softened by rivers. Its ridges are serrated, broken along sharp cleavage planes by the slow levering of ice. Out of this endless rubble of frost-shattered stone, the city has been gathered and stacked—not in isolated citadels, but continuously, across basin after basin, ridge after saddle, until the built terrain has the scale of a mountain system of its own. 

The air is thin, brilliantly clear, and bitter. Under a deep, dark-blue zenith, the sunlight falls with unfiltered sharpness, blinding where it strikes white quartz veins or patches of hoarfrost, dropping into knife-edged shadow behind every wall. Yet in the shadows, nothing ever fully thaws. Even at noon, the air temperature hangs near or below freezing; warmth exists only as a radiant skin on dark, south-facing rock, vanishing the instant a cloud passes.

There is no mortar here, and almost no timber. Every structure is dry-laid from the native stone that splits naturally off the crags in flat, angular slabs. Walls are six, eight, ten feet thick at the base, battered inward for stability, rising into corbelled vaults and beehive domes, layered three and four tiers deep up the talus slopes. Nothing is plumb in the machined sense, yet everything locks together under immense, patient weight. Where wider spans are needed, rafters are framed from the bleached, curving ribs and jawbones of great highland grazers, lashed with rawhide thongs that dried decades ago to the hardness of horn, then shingled over with overlapping slate and weighted down against the wind with rounded river cobbles hauled up from gorges days’ journey below. 

Because heat is life, the urban fabric is packed to near-solid density. Thoroughfares are sunken, meandering trenches scarcely wide enough for two laden beasts to pass, kinked every twenty paces to break the sweep of the wind. Roofs serve as streets for the tier above; courtyards are deep stone wells stepped down into the permafrost, lined with beaten felt and stuffed hides. Across fifty miles of continuous settlement, millions of slate roofs tilt toward the winter sun like the scales of a dormant reptile.

So many breathing lungs and smoldering hearths concentrated at this altitude make their own weather. In the mornings, a vast, flat canopy of freezing fog hangs exclusively over the denser quarters—the condensed exhalations of people and livestock mixing with the pale smoke of dung fires. As it drifts against the jagged windward walls and tall stone cairns erected specifically along the ridge crests, the vapor freezes out as thick, feathery rime. This is how the city drinks. There are no open rivers on the plateau, and snow falls only in sparse, dry flurries like salt that the wind scours instantly into crevices. Instead, moisture is harvested. Long, serrated walls of black basalt trap the morning condensation and afternoon frost; as the midday sun warms the dark rock, a brief, silent trickle runs down grooved channels into covered cisterns lined with pounded clay and tallow, where a skin of ice forms again before dusk. Down in the deep gullies between districts, intermittent streams flow beneath thick roofs of dirty glaze ice, audible only as a faint, hollow ticking under the stone.

What little grows here clings low and tough to the margins. Cushion plants, dense and hard as green boulders, press into the lee of retaining walls. Stiff, wire-stemmed sedges and grey-green tufts of fescue sprout along the seepage lines below cisterns and dung heaps, while crusts of orange, sulfur-yellow, and bone-white lichen coat every undisturbed slab of granite on the windward faces. On broad, walled roof-terraces sheltered from the gale, shallow beds of black night-soil nurture rows of tiny, frost-sweetened tubers no bigger than thumbnails, covered each afternoon with woven grass mats before the shadow of the peaks reaches them.

Despite the barrenness of the soil, the stone labyrinth teems with animal life—so thick in places that the city feels less like a human enclave than a vast, shared winter rookery. Herds of heavy-shouldered, wool-skirted ungulates—shaggy beasts with curved horns and split hooves broad as saucers—move continually through the sunken alleys, driven inward from the distant steppe basins in slow, multi-month migrations. They crowd into sunken stone pens at the heart of residential blocks, their packed bodies radiating the damp, lanolin-rich heat that warms the sleeping chambers built directly above their stalls. Every niche and chink of the dry-stone masonry is colonized. Plump, round-eared pikas and rust-colored rock voles dart continuously in and out of the unmortared gaps, stuffing crevices with dried sedge hay. Overhead, the artificial cliffs support staggering populations of birds: flocks of gloss-black choughs with red bills wheeling in the updrafts, thousands of pale cliff-pigeons cooing in rows along warm smoke-vents, and immense, bearded vultures with eight-foot wingspans perched motionless on butcher-towers and sky-burial plinths, waiting with prehistoric patience.

For all this crowding, the place is extraordinarily quiet. The thin, cold air carries sound poorly, and there are no wheels—every ramp is stepped, every load borne on wool-padded backs or dragged on bone-runner sledges that whisper over frost-glazed flagstones. People move bundled in thick layers of greased sheepskin and felted hair, shod in soft-soled boots that make no strike on the rock. There is no ring of iron; tools are knapped flint, ground slate, horn wedges, and small, precious blades of cold-hammered native copper. The prevailing sounds are close, dry, and small: the soft clack of horn against stone, the rustle of stiff hides, the breathy grunt of an animal shifting weight in a narrow passage, the occasional crisp *tink* of a mason splitting a frost-fractured slab with a diorite maul, and, underlying everything, the low, dry hiss of wind combing through millions of stone interstices.

Nothing happens quickly. Oxygen is scarce enough to punish haste, and the cold punishes sweat. A wall slumped by frost-heave is dismantled and relaid stone by stone over an entire season; hides cure in the dry wind for winters on end; drovers ease their packed herds down a two-mile stepped ramp across a whole afternoon, pausing at every terrace landing. Even decay is arrested—bone, wool, dung, and horn persist for centuries without rotting, worn down only by the patient, mechanical polish of passing shoulders and hooves, leaving the lower courses of the ancient alleys rubbed as smooth and dark as greasewood while the newly quarried ridges miles away remain raw, splintered, and bright.
