The light hits the basin like a scalpel. At this altitude, the sky is not blue so much as a thin, violet-black membrane stretched over a blinding sun, and every drop of moisture in the cramped stone amphitheater catches the glare and throws it back doubled. You cannot look down without squinting; you cannot look up at all. 

The place is no bigger than an old town square, walled in on three sides by weathered, smoothly scalloped basalt cliffs and opening on the fourth to a sheer drop into blinding cloud-glare. Yet within this pocket, everything is stacked, wedged, overlapping, and furiously in motion, operating under a profound and eerie hush.

Water shouldn’t be liquid in air this cold—air that burns the throat and stiffens the eyelashes within seconds—and for long moments, it isn’t. Heavy, brine-thick water weeps constantly from horizontal seams in the ancient cliff faces, too salty and pressurized by subterranean currents to freeze inside the rock. As it spills out into the subzero brightness, a turbulent phase-war begins. Streams flash into slush, freeze into bulbous glass-clear pillars, shatter from the pressure of the liquid surging behind them, and collapse into cascading heaps of grease-ice and freezing foam, only to be melted again where the merciless high-altitude sun strikes dark stone. 

Built directly into and out of this churning slush-matrix is a vertical settlement of astonishing density. There is no wood here, no forged metal, only dark slate, whale-colored bone, cured hides stiff as iron, and woven bladder-kelp hauled up from some unimaginable coast far below. Narrow towers of dry-stacked, water-smoothed flat stones rise five and six stories high, leaning against one another for warmth and structural survival, lashed together with thick braided sinew that groans—just barely audibly—as the ice shifts beneath their foundations. Catwalks of polished rib-bones spiderweb between balconies barely wide enough for two shoulders. Gutters carved from split horn redirect the violent bursts of weeping brine away from doorways, shooting glittering arcs of liquid across the narrow alleys where they freeze mid-air into jagged, falling chandeliers before hitting the ground.

And yet, for all this structural precariousness and crashing ice, there is almost no sound. 

The silence is physical, an acoustic deadening born of two things: a thick, spongy carpet of porous rime-slush that coats every horizontal surface and drinks vibration like wet wool, and the air itself, which hangs at a weird, localized pressure inversion—thin, razor-cold, and uncannily reluctant to carry high frequencies. When a three-hundred-pound shelf of glaze-ice shears off an upper aqueduct and smashes into the alley below, it lands with the muted *thump* of a dropped sack of flour. A frantic burst of spray hits a hide awning with the faintest whisper of blown sand.

Through this blinding, muffled chaos moves a staggering abundance of life. 

There is almost no green—only occasional tough, bruised-purple lichens crusting the sunward faces of the slate towers, and sparse tufts of wire-stiff silver moss growing directly out of rotting hide bindings—but the animal density is suffocating. Every warm crevice, stone ledge, and bone-rafter is packed. Thousands of pale, bowling-ball-sized avian creatures—wingless, covered in dense, greasy white filaments like spun glass—huddle and scramble in ceaseless, rolling waves across the rooftops. They move like liquid themselves, pouring down stone staircases in frantic, silent stampedes whenever an ice-dam breaks, scooping up the tiny, translucent brine-shrimp that gush out of the mountain's weeping seams before the water freezes solid. 

Larger beasts crowd the slush-choked lower thoroughfares: six-legged, barrel-chested ungulates with coats of matted grey felt and flat, shovel-like horn plates on their snouts. They violently bulldoze the constantly forming ice-sludge to keep the narrow lanes passable, driven by human figures bundled so heavily in layered blubber-skins and stitched gut-parkas they look like walking Cairns. Everyone wears snow-goggles carved from bone with hairline slits burned into them. Nobody speaks. In cold this severe, opening the mouth wide enough to shout steals body heat and frosts the lungs; instead, communication happens in a furious, hyper-kinetic sign language using short batons of polished black slate flashed in the brilliant sun.

Looking across the settlement from a high ledge is dizzying. Nothing stays still for three seconds. The dynamic of the hamlet is that of an anthill kicked open in slow-motion mime. Over on the eastern wall, a primary water-seam surges; instantly, twenty bundled inhabitants and a dozen horned beasts converge, prying up stone foundation blocks with bone levers, shifting an entire three-room dwelling sideways onto rollers before the expanding ice-blister can shatter its walls, re-wedging the slate roof-tiles in frantic synchronization while hundreds of glass-plumed birds swarm over their boots and shoulders, pecking frantically at the fresh brine-spray. Ten yards away, another crew is harvesting freshly flash-frozen sheets of clear ice, hauling them up on sinew pulleys to serve as windbreaks and magnifying lenses on the upper terraces, Focusing the intense solar glare onto dark stone basins to create brief, steaming pools of drinkable meltwater.

Down in the tightest spaces between the towers—gaps barely two feet wide where the sun still plunges straight down at noon like a white-hot blade—translucent, segmented worms as thick as a human forearm undulatingly scale the wet stone walls. They secrete a thick, oily mucus that keeps the rock from frosting over, and behind them climb agile, spider-like crustaceans with shells the color of tarnished pewter, grazing the mucus away. Inhabitants reach out of narrow window-slits with notched bone hooks to pluck the crustaceans off the walls without breaking stride in their indoor work.

The architecture has existed here for centuries, deeply eroded and smoothed by generations of frost-heave, salt-eat, and millions of rubbing hides and chitinous legs. Every stone threshold is dished ten inches deep in the middle. The basalt walls of the enclosing cliffs are grooved with ancient, abandoned channel-cuts from eras when the subterranean water wept from different fissures. Everything is worn round, greased with lanolin and brine-slime, jammed tight against its neighbor, coated in dazzling diamond-dust frost that evaporates into sudden, swirling ground-fogs wherever a sunbeam hits a dark slate wall, then instantly precipitates back out as falling ice-needles.

Stand in the center of a bone-bridge at high noon: the brightness presses against your slit-goggles like a physical weight. All around you, the walls are bleeding silver; ice is exploding silently into rainbows of dry powder; warm, musky, grease-coated bodies—human, feathered, felt-furred, shelled—brush past you in relentless, urgent currents from front and back; towers creak and settle; black slate batons clack once, softly, in the still air to signal a shift in the water’s path; and the entire tiny, vertical city pivots, rebuilds, feeds, and scrambles inside its blinding box of rock, making scarcely more noise than a watch ticking under a pillow.
