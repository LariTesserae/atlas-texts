The basin lies three hundred feet below the surrounding crust, not as a gorge or a sharp crater, but as a vast, shallow dish miles across. Time has had its way with the rock here until no edge remains. The floor is a sweeping expanse of ancient grey marl, soapstone, and pale,chalky slate, rubbed down to gentle, overlapping swells like the backs of sleeping whales. Above, there is no cavern roof—only a collapsed ceiling long since ground away, leaving the depression open to a sky of blinding, shadowless white. 

The light is fierce, pouring down through a thin, cool overcast that acts like a diffuser over a midday sun. It reflects off the pale stone and the dampness that coats everything, turning the basin into a bowl of cold glare. You have to squint to look across the town.

And it is a town—an urban sprawl of bone-colored stone, stretched thin across the mile-wide floor. Because the basin is so open and the rock so abundant, nothing is crowded. Low, circular dwellings made of unmortared, flat-rubbed slate sit fifty or a hundred paces apart from one another, linked by wide, sweeping causeways of packed grey clay and raised stone paths. There are plazas wide enough to hold thousands that sit entirely empty of furniture, ringed by low, tiered amphitheaters and squat, beehive-shaped kilns that haven't seen fire in generations. Every wall, lintel, and paving stone is smooth, rounded by millennia of wind and water before human hands ever stacked them. There is no timber here, no thatch, no iron nails. Roofs are corbeled stone or stretched hides bleached white by the glare, weighted down against the wind with smooth river-cobbles tied in fiber nets.

Despite the solidity of the ancient ground, the air above it is in violent, ceaseless motion. 

Cold, dense air spills constantly over the lip of the upper plateau, dropping into the basin and colliding with pockets of slightly warmer air rising from the damp stone. The result is a relentless, chaotic turbulence—silent, invisible whirlwinds that tear across the plazas, whip through the wide gaps between houses, and send sheets of fine mist skittering sideways across the slate. Yet, astonishingly, the basin is quiet. There are no trees to rustle, no dry leaves to scrape, no loose gravel left unpolished, no metal hinges to shriek. The wind moves over the aerodynamic, time-flattened rock and the rounded domes of the houses with scarcely a breath—just a soft, sub-audible pressure shifting against the eardrums, and the occasional *thrum* of a taut hide roof flexing in a sudden downdraft.

Moisture rides this turbulent air. There are no rivers here, no standing lakes, only a perpetual, shifting dampness. A dry plaza will suddenly darken in patches as a cold eddy drops its load of condensation directly onto the stone, slicking the soapstone causeways to a mirror shine in seconds. Minutes later, a dry gust tears through, drinking the moisture back up, leaving behind only faint rings of white mineral salt and thin, crusty smudges of grey-yellow lichen—the closest thing to plant life the basin supports. Water collects briefly in the wide, shallow dimples of the bedrock and in carved stone cisterns placed at the intersections of streets, shivering constantly with tight, chaotic ripples driven by the erratic drafts.

Through this bright, windy hush moves an abundance of life, hyperactive and soundless.

Flocks of pale, broad-winged birds—hundreds of them, built like heavy swifts with oily, water-repellent plumage—ride the erratic thermals and downdrafts just ten feet off the ground. They do not cry out. They bank, dive, and scatter in explosive, silent bursts between the stone houses, snapping up the clouds of blind, translucent midges that hatch from the damp seams in the marl. On the ground, colonies of flat, pale-shelled land crabs skitter sideways across the slick causeways, moving in synchronized, erratic waves as they chase the shifting patches of condensation, scraping microscopic biofilms from the wet rock with soft, rapid *ticks* of their claws that vanish five feet away. Long-legged, hairless hounds—or creatures very like them, bred by the basin’s builders—trot along the raised pathways, noses to the wet stone, their padded feet making no sound at all.

The people of the town match the rhythm of their animals and their air. Dressed in slick, greased leather wraps and heavy woven-hair cloaks weighted at the hems with smooth stones, they move through the wide spaces with brisk, continuous urgency. Because the moisture is fleeting, work happens at a sprint. When a heavy mist-squall slams down into the eastern quarter, dozens of figures emerge from the low doorways at once, dragging wide frames of stretched gut and absorbent hide across the plazas to trap the water, wringing it into clay jars before the dry counter-gust arrives to steal it back. Elsewhere, teams hurry to reset stone wind-baffles—long, curving walls of stacked slate that they constantly dismantle and rebuild a few yards to the left or right, trying to steer the chaotic currents toward drying racks hung with salted crab meat and bird carcasses, or away from open courtyards where potters are shaping grey clay by hand. 

Everything is done through gesture, posture, and eye contact across the bright, empty distances. Shouting into the erratic wind is useless—a voice is torn apart and thrown backward before it crosses half a street—so the settlement operates in near-total pantomime. You can stand on one of the gentle, ancient swells near the center of the basin and watch the whole silent storm of it at once: the blinding white sky above, the slick, gleaming miles of grey stone below, thousands of birds twisting like blown ash through the sparse grid of pale domes, and hundreds of figures hurrying along the wide, wet causeways, shifting stones to catch a wind you can feel shoving against your chest, though the world makes hardly a sound.
