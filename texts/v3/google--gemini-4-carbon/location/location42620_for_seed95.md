At this altitude, the mountains have forgotten how to be jagged. What remains is a regional expanse of worn, whale-backed swells stretching miles from rim to rim—an ancient plateau where the granite and gneiss have been ground down over hundreds of millions of years into smooth, domed pavements and shallow, dish-like basins. There are no loose scree slopes left to slide, no sharp ridges left to fracture. Every stone surface has settled into its final angle of repose, polished to a matte, soapy finish by ages of wind and chemical patience. 

Despite the height, the air is remarkably warm. The plateau traps solar heat in its dark, iron-rich stone, holding a steady, dry warmth under a pale, high-altitude sky. The light is ordinary, clear and unhurried, casting soft-edged shadows across miles of open, rolling rock. Yet if you look closely at those stone swells, you realize you are almost never looking at the rock itself.

The density here is entirely alive. The plateau is thick, crowded, and layered with animals—not swift, noisy herds, but a staggering, motionless saturation of slow-bodied fauna. 

Across acres of smooth granite, flat-shelled creatures the size of dinner plates overlap like roof tiles, their edges fitted precisely against one another to conserve moisture. In the wide, shallow troughs between the domes, thousands of pallid, velvety slugs as thick as a human forearm lie packed flank-to-flank in sluggish, braided rivers that move only a few inches an hour. Tall, spindle-legged browsers—something between a crane and a giant, soft-bodied harvestman—stand in clusters of hundreds, their narrow bodies spaced mere inches apart, swaying almost imperceptibly in the warm updrafts. Everywhere, things touch, lean, stack, and nestle. Carapaces serve as perches for smaller, segmented grazers; thick, wrinkled hides host colonies of tiny, pearl-colored mites that groom the skin in slow, synchronized waves. 

With such an overwhelming crush of bodies, the silence is deeply strange. There are no territories left to shout over, or perhaps vocalization simply costs too much water in the thin air. No birds shriek; no insects stridulate. The landscape holds a massive, pressurized hush. When sound occurs, it is purely mechanical and dry: the faint, papery rustle of ten thousand chitinous plates shifting simultaneously as the sun’s angle changes; the soft, wet *shuck* of a muscular foot detaching from warm stone; a collective, low sigh of air pushed through spiracles that sounds only like a brief, localized breeze.

Water never rushes here. There are no streams, because there are no steep gradients left to carry them. Instead, moisture arrives as low-bellied clouds that drift horizontally across the plateau every few days, dragging through the crowded basins. When the fog comes, the entire landscape engages in silent, meticulous harvesting. The tall spindle-creatures raise fanned, feathery appendages to comb the dampness from the air, letting droplets run down grooves in their legs directly into their mouths. Mist condenses on the cool morning carapaces of the plated colonies and trickles underneath them into dark, spongy mats of rootless moss and grey-green lichen. 

Vegetation is scarce, outcompeted for space by flesh and shell, surviving only in narrow, damp seams in the bedrock or in the shaded, humid microclimates directly beneath the bellies of the largest, slowest beasts. Low, rubbery cushions of pale yellow flora hug the margins of shallow rain-pans—dish-like depressions in the ancient rock that hold half an inch of warm, mineral-heavy tea for weeks at a time. Around these pans, the crowding becomes absolute: concentric rings of soft snouts, siphons, and absorbent fringes pressed gently into the damp silt, drinking without a ripple.

Scattered every half-mile or so across the open swells stand small, dome-like cairns and curved windbreaks, chest-high and mortarless. There is no metal here, no glass, no carved timber, no hinge or wheel. The structures are assembled from heavy, lens-shaped slabs of weathered rock and the bleached, interlocking rib-plates of immense, long-dead animals, fitted together by hand or claw so long ago that their contours have worn down to match the bedrock. 

Whatever built them—whether a vanished people who never needed fire or tool-making, or an older, larger caste of the plateau’s own quiet inhabitants—built them to catch condensation and hold shade. Their interiors are cool and damp, smelling faintly of wet slate and musk. Now, they are simply denser knots in the living tapestry. Inside each stone crescent, coiled bodies with translucent, veined skin sleep piled three-deep out of the afternoon sun, breathing once every four or five minutes. 

Along the curve of a mile-wide stone basin, faint, smooth grooves run across the granite—ancient pathways worn six inches deep into solid rock not by wheels or iron shoes, but by the patient, unhurried friction of soft bellies and leathery pads following the exact same contour from shade to dampness for ten million years. Nothing hurries, nothing flees, and when one of the crowded multitudes dies, it does not even fall; held upright or pinned in place by the press of its neighbors, it is quietly, cleanly dismantled and absorbed before the next fog rolls across the ridge.
