From edge to edge it is no wider than a village green, perhaps seventy paces across and forty high, open at the top to a plain, warm afternoon sky. Yet almost the entire cubic volume of the place is vacant. Nothing crosses the middle air. Nothing grows up through the broad, flat floor. All mass has been pushed to the perimeter, leaving a steep-sided rectangular hollow full of clear daylight and a sound so immense it behaves like structural masonry.

There is no soil here, and beneath the floor there is no stone. Everything—paving, gutter, facade, cornice, stair, and alley—is a single, ancient, continuously living plant body. Centuries of growth have lignified its lower plates into a pale, silvered hardwood as dense and weathered as old limestone, polished glassy along the margins by generations of passing feet and claws. Looking closely at the sunlit floor of the central court, you can see the faint, tight braid of flattened buttress roots fused seam-to-seam without a crack for dirt to gather in. Where the floor meets the walls, the organism rises straight up for four and five stories in uncanny civic geometry.

Nobody built this town, and there isn't a nail, hinge, kiln-brick, or cut timber anywhere in it, yet it is unmistakably municipal. The plant grows in colonnades, stepped tenements, blind dormer windows, recessed entryways, rainwater downspouts, and cantilevered balconies connected by narrow overhead walkways. Calloused bark swells into lintels and curb-stones. Vascular bundles run along the undersides of arches precisely like conduit pipes, pulsing visibly twice a minute as warm sap pumps from the sunny east face toward the shaded west. The architecture is old and settled—balustrades worn rounded, lower corners smoothed back, split joints long ago healed over with bulbous amber knots of scar-resin—an established town square grown entirely out of wood-flesh, mimicking the habits of an urban civilization that has never existed here.

The noise starts inside your teeth before your ears fail to parse it. It is not a single roar so much as ten thousand overlapping percussion tracks, hydraulic whistles, and stridulations packed into a space built like the soundbox of a cello. Hollow bladder-pods along the upper cornices vent pressurized gas in sustained, brassy chords; millions of stiff, dry, papery leaves lining the narrow side-streets clap together in frantic polyrhythms despite the absence of wind; and packed into every window-slit, portico, and drain-groove are the animals, all of them loud.

Because of the sound, the center of the plaza cannot be entered.

Acoustic standing waves criss-cross the sunlit void in an invisible grid of high-pressure walls and dead pockets. The air in the middle of the square shivers faintly, bending the clear afternoon light just enough to throw thin, oily rainbows along the edges of shadows. When a dry husk or a dead beetle falls from a fourth-story balcony toward the open court, it does not drop to the ground; three feet out from the facade, the sheer physical punch of the sound batters it sideways, pinning it back against the bark-masonry or suspending it mid-air at a nodal intersection, where it spins furiously in empty space until friction shreds it to powder. The floor of the square is swept immaculate by pure decibels. Only light and noise occupy the center.

All life—and there is an extraordinary amount of it—moves flat against the skin of the walls and through the shallow, hollow galleries behind them. Even the tenements themselves have almost no depth; behind the grand, weathered facades, the plant’s walls are papery, hollow shells braced by thin struts of pith, mostly dark air inside.

Along the damp, shaded base of the walls and up the grooved vertical avenues, fauna moves at a relentless, jittery clip. Long columns of flat, six-legged crustaceans the color of terracotta scuttle two-abreast along sunken curb-channels, strictly observing lane directions, turning sharp right angles at street corners as if obeying signage. Broad-headed, heavy-bodied geckos the size of forearms cling upside down under the lintels, dropping onto passing columns to snatch stragglers before scrambling up drainpipes into third-floor windows. Every window-hole boils with activity: colonial wasps with wings like hammered mica cycling in and out in synchronized bursts; small, featherless, frog-throated climbers leaping from balcony to balcony, inflating bright yellow vocal sacs that add wet, hammering cracks like pistol shots to the general deafening din. Predation happens at street-level speed—a sudden ambush out of a doorway sphincter, a brief thrashing on a worn step, and whatever fluid spills onto the porous wood is drunk dry inside of thirty seconds, the pale bark flushing briefly dark green where it feeds.

Water comes and goes without rain. In this low, warm pocket, the giant plant transpires heavily whenever the sun clears the upper rim. Moisture sweats directly out of smooth pith-plaster walls inside the shaded arcades, beading into heavy drops that gather along leaf-vein moldings and run down grown-in gutters. Narrow alleys smell sharply of wet cedar, crushed chitin, and warm pond-water. Small, brief indoor downpours patter down stairwell shafts for ten minutes at a time when a vascular main overhead dumps excess pressure through rows of weeping pores, leaving glossy damp streaks down the pale facades and shallow, lukewarm puddles in the worn hollows of steps, where crowds of iridescent flies and tiny, frantic amphibians gather to drink before the afternoon warmth dries the wood chalk-pale again.

Look too long across the empty sunlit court, or follow one of the darting, busy streets into a corner, and the logic of the space comes apart.

A stepped alley on the north side climbs briskly past three tiers of shuttered bark-windows, turns once beneath a dripping root-viaduct, and lets out onto the ground-level Arcade directly beneath its own bottom step, without ever descending. Sunlight enters from one plain sun directly above, yet the crisp shadows of opposite balconies slant toward the same corner. Some of the arched openings in the facade do not lead into the hollow darkness of the plant at all; peering through an empty second-story doorway from across the square, you look straight out through an archway on the wall behind your own back, watching the rear plates of the beetles crawling past your ankles, delayed by the blink of an eye—until a hunting lizard darts across the sill and steps cleanly from one side of the town to the other without crossing the uninhabitable void between.

Where reality slips hardest is in the ongoing renovation. The plant does not grow leaves on its inner faces; it grows *fixtures*. In response to the traffic of its inhabitants and the bruising vibration of the air, fresh green shoots extrude from split moldings and stiffen over the course of hours into blind staircases ending flat against ceilings, ornamental railings around nothing, tiny empty courtyards no larger than a cupped hand complete with miniature gutters, and rows of hinged-looking doors grown shut from birth. Halfway up the eastern wall, an entire three-story gable leans out over the acoustic abyss at an impossible angle, held up by no visible brace, resting squarely against a dense knot of solid noise as though the uproar itself were a timber prop.

Nothing slows down. Clouds of shredded husks spin and disintegrate at invisible points twenty feet above the spotless wooden plaza. Gutters gush suddenly with warm, sweet-smelling sap-water and run dry three yards downslope. Swarms shift neighborhoods in sudden cascading panics, leaving whole tenement blocks momentarily bare before a rival species pours out of the vents to claim the ledges. Above it all, the open rectangle of sky sits bright, ordinary, and mild, pouring clear afternoon light down into a small, hollow, screaming wooden town that has been worn smooth by centuries of rush-hour traffic and has nothing at its center at all.
