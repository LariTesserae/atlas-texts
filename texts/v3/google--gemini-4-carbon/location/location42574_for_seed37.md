Forty feet beneath a perfectly ordinary stretch of topsoil, sealed off without tunnel, shaft, or root-crack to explain how anything arrived here, there is a pocket no larger than a modest courtyard. It should be pitch black. Instead, it is flooded with a ruthless, shadowless glare—the high, clinical brightness of an overcast noon on snow, pouring directly out of the parts of the walls that have not yet decided what kind of rock to be.

The room is being born, piece by piece, and the birth is going wrong.

Where the stone *has* arrived, it has zero history. Nothing here has ever been rubbed by wind, tumbled by water, or softened by acid. Slices of pale orthoclase, raw grey chert, and wet, soapy anhydrite jut into the space with edges so fresh they split single hairs on contact. There is no dust on the floor because nothing has existed long enough to crumble. Between these crisp, jutting mineral plates hang irregular patches of pure, radiant vacancy—flat panes of blinding white luminescence that feel like cold glass if touched, waiting to curdle into limestone or slate. Every few minutes, a section of the glare shudders, dims, and snaps into solid stone with a sound no louder than a fingernail flicking paper. Sometimes the new rock arrives inside rock that was already there, and the two occupy the same space without breaking each other, flickering back and forth depending on where an observer stands.

The air sits at a crisp, cellar-cool fifty degrees, clean and mineral-sharp, carrying no scent of rot. There is no stream, no puddle large enough to cup in two hands, yet everything is damp. Moisture skips the stage of falling rain; it simply declares itself. Broad beads of cold sweat materialize directly on the pristine flint planes, swell until heavy, detach, and drift sideways rather than down, gathering into suspended ribbons of mist before vanishing again into dry air. For ten seconds a corner of the floor will be slick dark slate; on the eleventh second the wetness has relocated three feet upward onto the underside of a protruding ledge, leaving the floor bone-dry without evaporation.

Despite the tight boundaries—one could throw a pebble from wall to wall in a single lazy arc—the chamber feels sparse, wide-open, almost vacant. Distance misbehaves in the middle of the floor. Two boulders sitting side by side require twenty paces to walk between, though a long stick laid across them bridges the gap with inches to spare. Because of these empty, stretched intervals, the sparse vegetation looks marooned. A single pale liverwort clings to a raw seam of quartz, its green lobes growing, dying, turning transparent, and regrowing green in a brisk three-minute rhythm. Ten paces away—or two inches, depending on the angle—three tall, leafless reeds stand rooted in dry solid chert, with no soil at all, swaying in a breeze that does not touch the damp air around them. When their stems cross one another, they pass cleanly through without collision.

And yet the room is teeming with animal life. 

It does not crowd the space because very little of it possesses full thickness. Across the blinding white gaps in the east wall, hundreds of translucent, multi-legged grazers—the size of human hands, shaped vaguely like flattened sea-slaters—scuttle in frantic overlapping shoals. Turned broadside, they are intricate lattices of pale veins, pulsing stomachs, and combing mouthparts; when they turn ninety degrees to climb a ridge, they have no width whatsoever and vanish entirely until they turn again. In the cool middle air, ribbon-thin things with the damp sheen of peeled inner bark undulate in tight, silent figure-eights, hunting bursts of pinhead midges that blink in and out of visibility like blown sparks. 

Predation here is relentless, fast, and uncanny. Beneath an overhang of raw, sharp-edged gypsum, clusters of boneless, radial creatures resembling pale terrestrial brittle-stars ambush anything that crosses the wet patches. They fling long, sticky feeding threads across the broken geometry—a thread launched three inches to the left emerges six feet behind its prey from bare air, wraps a flattened grazer, and reels it in. When caught, the prey does not bleed; it unspools into dry, chalky outlines that the predator absorbs through its skin in seconds. Over in a low niche, pale amphibians with too many joints in their limbs leap after the bark-ribbons, miss the landing because gravity in that corner tilts thirty degrees toward the wall, and continue running seamlessly along the vertical stone without breaking stride. Hundreds of lives are hatched from bead-like cysts in the condensation, hunted, eaten, and recycled every hour, keeping the chamber in a state of ceaseless, darting agitation.

Almost none of it makes a noise. 

It is as though the pocket’s capacity to carry sound was left out when the rock began forming. A frantic chase involving forty skittering creatures produces less noise than dust settling on wool. Jaws snap shut in total muteness; sleek wet bodies slap against glassy stone and yield only the faintest, muffled *thup*, as if heard through three closed doors and a pillow. The dominant sound in the bright, churning room is a profound, pressurized quiet, broken only by the occasional soft *tick* of a fresh crystal facet locking into reality, or a faint wet sigh when a sheet of suspended dew collapses all at once.

Scattered across the floor, separated by wide stretches of untouched stone, sit five or six tiny monuments left by hands that are no longer here—or perhaps never managed to stay fully solid long enough to remain. There is no metal among them, no pottery, no carved wood or woven cloth. Whoever worked here had only raw biology and raw stone, and used both to interrogate the broken rules of the room.

Near the center stands a low, tripod-like lintel no taller than a shin, made from three unweathered blades of black flint balanced without mortar, bound at the apex with dried, translucent gut. Hanging from the gut are three plumb lines spun from heavy animal hair, each weighted with a small mammal’s jawbone. Though suspended from the exact same knot a thumb’s width apart, the three lines hang taut at wildly divergent angles, pointing toward three separate centers of the earth.

Further back, tucked near one of the glaring, unfinished wall-panes, is a half-circle of tiny mud-and-spittle cairns, each the size of a clenched fist, arranged to mark the exact border where distance stretches. A stick has been pushed into the damp paste of the nearest cairn; its shadow falls *toward* the bright white light source rather than away from it, dark and razor-sharp against the pale floor, though nothing else in the chamber casts a shadow at all. Beside the cairns lies a neat tally: seventeen shed carapaces of the flat grazers arranged from smallest to largest on a slab of anhydrite. Every time one of the live ribbons drifts over the slab, the seventeenth carapace briefly becomes live again, twitches its antennae in silent panic, and goes back to being an empty shell the moment the ribbon moves on.

Nothing rots. Nothing erodes. Nothing echoes. Above the ceiling—which is simply a flat plane where raw stone abruptly stops and a hard, bright glare presses down—tons of blind dark earth, worms, tree roots, and ordinary rain continue their slow, heavy business, wholly severed from this cellar. Down here, in the cool hospital-bright hush, another jagged shelf of pristine feldspar clicks into existence out of the white void, slicing neatly through the middle of a mud cairn without knocking either half over, and immediately a dozen paper-thin hunters dart onto the brand-new stone while it is still sweating its first cold drops of water.
