The air at this altitude is thin, crisp, and perpetually cool, holding the steady chill of an early autumn morning that never warms into noon and never slips toward dusk. Far overhead, the sky is a clear, pale, cloudless vault, and the sun hangs pinned at roughly forty degrees above the jagged western rim—bright, distinct, and entirely stationary.

Because the sun does not move, the shadows do not travel. Across the miles-wide highland basin, every shadow cast by crag, tower, and lintel is a permanent geographical feature.

The rock of the basin is geologically fresh, lifted up cleanly without the softening of millennia. Cliffs and outcrops break in sharp, angular planes of dark slate and splintered basalt. Scree slopes below the ridges consist of flat, knife-edged shards that have never been tumbled round by floods or ground down into sand. There is no dust here, no silt, and no wind to carry it. Yet the rock is not barren. Stretching from one wall of the basin to the other is a vast, low-density city constructed entirely from this freshly cleaved stone, woven plant fiber, and dark mountain timber.

It is unmistakably urban in organization—a unified grid of raised causeways, stepped districts, sunken plazas, granary towers, and miles of stone-lined channels—yet it is laid out with immense, deliberate openness. Hundreds of paces separate one compound from the next. Broad avenues of fitted grey flagstones run straight across angular ravines on dry-laid corbeled arches, leading to open courts that contain nothing at all save a single cistern or a low plinth of beaten, green-tarnished copper. Between the districts lie wide expanses of unworked terrain, leaving the city feeling less like a crowded hive and more like an intricate stone framework dropped lightly over an open valley.

The technology of the builders is simple and manual, visible in every joint. There is no mortar, no iron, no glass, and no arch built with a keystone. Stones are split along natural cleavage lines and stacked with knapped precision, their edges still razor-sharp to the touch. Roofs are framed with peeled, resinous logs lashed together with braided fibrous cordage that neither frays nor rots. Where metal appears, it is soft native copper, cold-hammered with riverless stone mauls into thin sheets that line water troughs and cap the tops of wooden posts. Nothing is crumbling. There are no half-collapsed walls, no toppled columns, no weeds prizing apart the paving stones. Every structure stands in crisp equilibrium with the ground beneath it.

Moisture governs the city’s strangest division, working in tandem with the fixed light. There is no rain here, and no rivers cascade from the surrounding peaks. Instead, the cool air remains permanently near its dew point. Wherever the unchanging sunlight strikes a paved street or the eastern face of a house, the dark stone is dry and faintly warm. Inches away, inside the crisp geometric boundary of a wall’s permanent shadow, the temperature drops just enough for the air to sweat.

Every shadow is damp. Stone surfaces facing away from the sun glisten with a continuous, microscopic film of condensation that beads, trickles down vertical grooves carved into the masonry, and collects in shallow basins that never overflow and never dry out, losing moisture at the exact edge of the light at the precise rate they gather it in the shade. 

Consequently, the vegetation grows in strict, architectural geometry. In the sunlit expanses between buildings, only low, tough cushions of silver-green highland moss and stiff rosettes of waxy leaves take hold in the sharp crevices of the rock. Step across the razor-line of a tower’s shadow, however, and the world turns suddenly, extravagantly lush. Long, ruler-straight corridors of shade stretching behind the city’s walls are choked with waist-high ferns, glossy broad-leaved shrubs, thick velvet liverworts, and tangled mats of pale, translucent creepers. Gnarled cloud-trees with weeping dark foliage pack the permanently shaded courtyards and narrow alleys, their branches stopping abruptly where the canopy meets the glaring plane of the sunlight, sheared flat as if by a gardener’s blade. From the high ridges surrounding the valley, the city looks striped: clean grey stone and pale open courts alternating with deep, wet ribbons of emerald black-green running off to the east of every upright structure.

This quiet stone-and-greenery expanse is thick with animal life. Despite the absence of human footsteps or voices, the city is heavily occupied. Along the sunny copings of the causeways, flat-bodied lizards the color of oxidized copper lie lined up by the hundreds, half their bodies positioned in the warmth, their tails resting in the damp cool of the shadow-line, barely needing to shift an inch over the course of an age. Plump, slate-feathered birds the size of geese roost in dense rows along every timber eave and terrace parapet, peering down with dark, unblinking eyes.

Down in the wide greenswards between the sparse housing blocks, herds of stocky, hornless grazers with thick, silver-brown fleeces move with an almost imperceptible slowness through the shadow-gardens, cropping leaves that regenerate without seasonal haste. Web-footed amphibians, pale and sleek, float motionless at the brims of the brimming stone cisterns. High-legged, long-necked waders pick delicately through the wet ferns beneath the viaducts. Small furry climbers with wide, splayed paws cling to the rough dry-stone facades of the towers, resting on projecting bonding-stones built into the walls.

For all this abundance of breath and heartbeat, the valley is hushed. There is no wind to hiss through the mountain passes or rustle the dense wet foliage in the shade corridors. There are no territorial shrieks, no mating calls, no frantic scatterings of prey—there is no season to race against and no disruption to announce. The grazers step across the sharp basalt gravel on thick, spongy footpads that absorb the crunch of stone. When one of the heavy slate-colored birds drops from a roof beam to glide across an open plaza toward another tower, its broad, fringed wings beat the thin air with a muffled, velvety *whuff* that dies away within ten yards.

Only the smallest sounds carry in the thin air: the occasional soft click of a split hoof against a flagstone; the slow, rhythmic *drip* of condensation falling from a copper spout into a brimming basin, one bead every few seconds in an unvarying tempo; the faint, dry rustle of lizard scales shifting on warm slate. Across the wide, bright valley, the sharp mountains hold their edges, the shadows hold their marshes, and the sprawling stone city sits open and intact in the cool, unmoving afternoon.
