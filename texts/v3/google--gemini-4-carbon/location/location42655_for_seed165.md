The altitude makes itself known first in the throat, and second in the ears. The air is so thin it carries almost no warmth, holding steady just above the freezing point—not cold enough to lock the world into white ice, just cold enough to keep everything perpetually numb and sweating. Above this high, sunken basin, the sky does not pale into morning or deepen into night. It sits at the color of tarnished pewter, lit from behind by something diffused and starless, casting an even, shadowless gloom across five miles of smooth grey slate. 

And it roars. 

There is no wind. A feather dropped from the hand falls straight down, slow only because the air scarcely resists it, landing on damp stone without twitching. Yet the valley is filled with a deafening, continuous, sub-audible thunder—a dry, sustained *shhhhhhhhhhh-krrrrrr* that vibrates in the sternum and behind the eyes. It has no source and no echo. It is the sound of ten thousand tons of scree sliding down a mountain, except nothing is sliding. Every ridge, every broad saddle of dark rock, every wide, shallow bowl in the stone was worn smooth ages ago and has long since settled into absolute rest. It is as if the violence that carved this place finished its work millennia back, packed up its physical weight, and left its noise behind, trapped in the thin atmosphere like a ringing bell that cannot bleed off its energy into the near-vacuum.

Because there are no shadows in the dim, sourceless light, distance misbehaves. A kilometer away looks like fifty paces until you walk toward a boulder and spend forty minutes reaching it, your boots clicking inaudibly beneath the roar. Worse, the horizon does not stay level. Look at the western rim of the saddle—a long, gentle curve of weathered basalt—and it lies below eye level. Walk toward the center of the basin, where the ground visibly dips, and the rim somehow sinks lower, while the flat floor behind you tilts up like the interior of a shallow dish. Gravity never changes; your feet press straight down into the damp gravel, and water never flows uphill. Yet every sightline insists you are standing at the lowest point of the valley, no matter where you stand, and that the edges of the world are peeling slightly away from the sky.

Water exists here, though rain never falls and streams never gather. Instead, moisture simply *arrives* on surfaces. Smooth depressions in the rock hold shallow, mirror-still pools an inch deep, dark as oil. Where the stone rises into low, rounded ribs, beads of cold sweat cling to the leeward sides—though there is no windward—gathering until they grow heavy, detach, and slide two inches down the rock before halting abruptly, held by surface tension in exact, unchanging rows. 

In the spatial tears—places where two overlapping planes of rock fail to meet properly, leaving a dark, two-dimensional seam hanging an inch above the ground—moisture gathers thickest. Around these hairline faults in the air, the sparse vegetation crowds. There are no grasses or shrubs, only flat, rubbery rosettes the color of bruised plums, splayed tight against the wet slate like suction cups, and pale, hair-thin filaments that grow straight upward for six inches and terminate in tiny, water-filled bladders. Nothing sways. The bladders act as lenses; if you crouch and look through one, the rock behind it is not magnified, but shown from an angle slightly to the left of where you are kneeling.

Scattered far apart across the basin are the cairns, though calling them cairns implies someone stacked them from loose rubble. They are low, windowless shelters the size of a kneeling horse, built of dark, flat stones fitted without mortar. Their edges are as rounded and ancient as the bedrock itself, worn to the exact same matte sheen, so they look half-melted into the floor of the pass. Inside them, the roar is no quieter—the stone walls do not muffle the sound at all, proving the noise isn't traveling through the air in any ordinary way. On the floors of these shelters lie small arrangements: spirals of pale knucklebones, smooth slate tablets scored with parallel grooves, a wooden bowl turned nearly to stone by age and cold damp, holding three centimeters of black water that never evaporates. Whoever built them had no iron, no glass, no wire. Near the eastern shelf stands a row of seven stone slabs planted upright; if you align them by eye from the south, there are seven. If you walk around and align them from the north, there are five, and there are no gaps where the other two ought to be.

For hours of looking, the valley appears entirely dead—vast stretches of empty air separating one damp boulder from the next, one stone hut from its neighbor half a mile off. Nothing decays here, and nothing seems to grow. 

Then something moves, and you realize it has been standing twenty yards away the entire time. 

The creatures that inhabit the pass resemble long-legged, rib-thin ungulates the height of a tall dog, covered in coarse, wet-looking pelts the exact grey of the slate, with wide, cartilaginous Dishes where ears should be, angled downward toward the stone. They do not graze by walking. Walking seems to violate some truce they have made with the broken geometry of the basin. Instead, one stands motionless for what might be hours, neck craned down toward a patch of plum-colored rosettes, completely indistinguishable from an eroded outcrop. Then, between one heartbeat and the next, without lifting a hoof, it is standing six feet to the east, head slightly raised, water beading along its snout. You never see the stride. There is a faint, greasy smear left in the air for half a second—like looking through heat haze, though the air is bitter cold—and the animal is simply occupying a different patch of the valley's sparse emptiness, chewing slowly, unbothered by the roar.

High overhead, near the pewter ceiling where the extreme altitude thins toward nothing at all, pale, ribbon-like shapes occasionally cross the basin. They do not flap. They hang flat against the sky like shed skins caught on glass, drifting from one rim to the other at a steady, mechanical crawl, never descending, never banking, casting no shadow on the damp stone below.

Nothing warms. Nothing cools. The pools do not freeze and do not dry. A bootprint pressed into a patch of wet grit beside a stone hut holds its crisp rim indefinitely, filling slowly from the bottom up with clear, seeping cold water until it matches the level of every other puddle in the pass, while the endless, dry avalanche thunders on inside your teeth, loud enough to drown out a scream, steady enough that after three days you forget what silence was supposed to do.
