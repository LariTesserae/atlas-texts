The depression is no larger than a village square, sunk forty feet into a peneplain so ancient that the surrounding horizon is merely a ruler-flat smudge of gray talc. Down inside this shallow pan, however, there is no stillness. The basin is an open-air sump of dry, polished stone, smoothed to the texture of soap by eons of wind, and it is brimming from rim to rim with living, dry-shelled things.

Light falls into the bowl clear and shadowless, filtered through a high, dry overcast the color of zinc. The air is brisk—cool enough to sting exposed skin, completely devoid of moisture, smelling sharply of ozone, pulverized flint, and warm machine grease. There is not a blade of grass, not a lichen spot, not a drop of dew. Every crevice where soil might have gathered is packed instead with drifts of shed carapaces ground down over millennia into a pale, pearlescent flour.

What looks at first like the floor of the basin heaving in an earthquake is actually the fauna. Millions of segmented, heavy-bodied creatures—some the size of thumbnails, others as broad as dinner plates—pack the smooth granite hollows three and four layers deep. Their shells are matte gray, slate-blue, and tarnished copper, evolved to match the ancient minerals they graze upon. Because there is no water to drink and no plants to eat, the ecosystem runs entirely on friction, static, and lithotrophic bacteria. And on the machines.

Scattered across the basin are seven or eight squat, cast-iron housings, half-buried in the chitin-dust. They are remnants of an old, automated extraction line—industrial donkey-pumps, flywheel housings, and thick-walled junction boxes held together by square-headed bolts. Their original purpose is long gone; the pipes they connect to were sheared flat by erosion ages ago. Yet three of the engines are still running, powered by some deep, stubborn geothermal tap that hasn't failed yet. A heavy iron piston kicks out of a rusted cylinder, pauses, and slams back in. Thirty feet away, an unshielded flywheel spins with a steady, rhythmic wobble, throwing off tiny, regular snaps of blue static electricity where its copper brushes drag.

The sound in the pocket is immense, trapped and amplified by the smooth, concave rock walls. Underlying everything is the low, percussive *thud-clank, thud-clank* of the surviving engines, vibrating right up through the soles of the feet. Layered over that is a relentless, deafening roar of dry friction—like ten thousand burlap sacks filled with gravel being dragged across concrete. It is the sound of millions of mandibles scraping stone, of heavy ceramic-hard plates grinding over one another in a constant, churning tide.

The machinery has become the sun and rain of this miniature world. Where the spinning flywheel throws off heat and static, the creatures pile into dense, writhing mounds two feet high, competing fiercely for the warmth against the cool air. Broad, flattened grazers press their undersides directly against the vibrating iron housings, absorbing the kinetic energy to jump-start their sluggish, cold-blooded metabolisms. Smaller, needle-legged scavengers dart between the moving linkages, timing their sprints to the half-second intervals of the plunging pistons to scrape away the thick black mineral-grease that weeps from the seals—the richest source of hydrocarbons for miles. 

Predation happens right in the open, under the even, clinical daylight, and at a frantic pace. Heavy,钳-jawed hunters the size of terriers plow trenches through the packed carpets of smaller bugs, tossing broken shells into the air like woodchips from a lathe. When a hunter makes a kill, there is no blood—only a puff of dry, sulfur-yellow powder and a swarm of tiny, mite-like opportunists that instantly blanket the site, reducing meat and shell to clean dust in seconds. 

Around the edges of the working machines sit the dead ones: an overturned hopper worn thin as paper by millions of passing feet; a length of heavy chain whose links have been polished to a mirror shine by creatures constantly crawling through them; a brass pressure gauge, its glass long gone, its needle bent, packed entirely full of tiny, pearl-colored eggs laid there to shelter from the wind. 

Everything in the pocket moves in tight, ceaseless currents. Heated creatures slump down from the engine blocks, heavy with energy, radiating outward toward the cooler stone edges to breed and shed, while cold, sluggish waves push inward from the perimeter to take their place on the iron. Dust devils of ground shell spin briefly where a breeze dips below the rim of the pan, skimming across the backs of the swarm, settling over the tireless, chugging pistons, and sliding right back down into the fray.
