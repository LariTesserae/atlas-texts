At first glance, the region shouldn’t be able to stand. It is a vast, hollowed-out basin of extreme altitude, miles wide, where the bedrock was eaten away eons ago until only the hardest, most stubborn cores of pale quartzite remained. Everything softer vanished down into a drop so deep its floor is lost in haze. What is left is a skeletal architecture of stone: needles a thousand feet tall and no thicker than an oak tree, sweeping natural arches worn thin as collarbones, and isolated, flat-topped pedestals separated by half-mile gulfs of empty air. 

There is almost no mass here. The landscape is ninety-five percent sky. Yet across these precarious, widely spaced ribs of ancient rock, the world is violently, overwhelmingly green. 

Because there is nowhere to root in soil, the flora has spent millions of years mastering suspension. Thick, braided cables of woody vine leap across the chasms, anchored to the quartzite needles like rigging on a fleet of colossal, invisible ships. From these primary cables hang acres of epiphytic mats, swaying curtains of broad-leaved creepers, clusters of bulbous air-plants the size of wine barrels, and trailing moss-beards fifty feet long. It is a lush, horizontal canopy stretched over an abyss, catching the warm, bright afternoon sun while casting shifting, lacy shadows onto nothingness below.

And it never stops thrashing. 

The air at this altitude is thin, warm, and ferociously unstable. Thermal updrafts boil out of the unseen depths, colliding with high-altitude crosswinds that shear over the rim of the basin. The result is constant, chaotic turbulence. There is no prevailing wind; there are only colliding invisible rivers of air that twist, drop, and rocket upward. The entire suspended forest is in a state of perpetual, frenzied motion. Great nets of foliage billow upward like parachutes catching a gust, hang inverted for three seconds, and then snap downward with a crack that echoes off the distant stone spires. 

The sound is physical, pressing against the eardrums and vibrating in the sternum. Without soil or dense forest floors to absorb the acoustics, every noise multiplies across the open miles of the basin. The wind doesn’t whistle; torn through thousands of taut vine-cables and hollow, fluted stone arches, it produces a deafening, discordant organ-roar that shifts pitch wildly every few seconds. Layered over this bass drone is the relentless, percussive thrashing of millions of stiff, waxy leaves slapping against each other—a sound like a torrential downpour hitting tin roofs, though the sky above is a clear, ordinary blue. When a particularly violent vortex tears a centuries-old mat of orchids loose from its moorings, the snapping of the main tendril booms across the gulf like cannon fire before the whole green island cartwheels silently down into the bright void.

Water arrives not from rain, which is rare, but from fast-moving pockets of cloud that get sucked upward from the lowlands, shredded against the stone needles, and flung through the whipping canopy. For ten minutes at a time, a section of the basin is engulfed in blinding, sideways-shooting white mist. Everything becomes slick and gleaming. The plants are built for this intermittent drenching: bulbous pseudobulbs swell rapidly, cupped leaves snap shut like mouths around trapped mouthfuls of condensation, and velvety aerial roots turn from silver to bright emerald in seconds as they drink from the passing vapor. Five minutes later, the cloud is torn apart by an updraft, the warm sun hits the wet leaves, and plumes of steam whip away into the blue, leaving the stone dry and hot to the touch while droplets still cling to the undersides of the swaying mats.

Life of the moving, breathing kind keeps a low profile in the gale. You might stare across three miles of heaving green rigging and pale stone ribs and see nothing at all, until a sudden slackening of the wind reveals a solitary glider—something leathery, flat as a stingray, with hooked claws on all four corners—releasing its grip on a quartzite pinnacle to ride an updraft, vanishing half a mile higher in three heartbeats. Closer in, tucked deep inside the violently shaking baskets of woven fern-roots, live heavy, armored crabs with pale shells, moving slowly through the damp, shadowy interiors of the plant-clusters, feeding on wind-blown pollen and trapped insects, entirely indifferent to the fact that their home is swinging through forty-foot arcs over a mile-deep drop.

Scattered at vast intervals across this roaring, whipping openness are signs that people have figured out how to use the place, or at least survive passing through it. 

High on the lee side of the widest stone needles, wedged into smoothed-out hollows where the ancient erosion left shallow caves, sit small, bunker-like shelters. They are built of woven vine-wood and flexible bone, lashed directly to the rock with braided plant-fiber ropes thick as a man's thigh—structures designed to flex rather than resist. None of them look permanently inhabited; they are caches, storm-cellars, or waystations. 

More striking are the modifications to the stone itself. Whoever moves through this country carries simple, heavy chisels of dark bronze or meteoric iron. On the windward edges of certain stone arches, notches and grooves have been laboriously pecked into the quartzite, altering the way the gale hits the rock—perhaps to bleed off the worst of the destructive vortices before they hit a favored bridge of vines, or perhaps to change the deafening roar in a specific sector into a distinct, recognizable chord so travelers can navigate by ear when the flash-mists blind them. 

Here and there, spanning a gap where the living vines failed to cross, hangs a hand-plaited rope bridge, three hundred yards long, empty andbucking wildly in the crosswinds like a furious whip. Wooden toggles and smoothed river-stones—carried up from some unimaginably distant lowland—are tied at intervals along its bottom edge as counterweights, clacking rhythmically in the chaos, waiting for someone brave or desperate enough to clip onto the guide-line and pull themselves hand-over-hand across the bright, roaring sky.
