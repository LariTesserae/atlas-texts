The entire place can be taken in from one side to the other without squinting—a shallow, saucer-like depression perhaps two hundred paces across, cupped within low swells of stone so old they have lost all memory of fracture or edge. Everything geological here has slumped into its final, most stable geometry. The perimeter rises no higher than the roof of a cottage, composed of pale, pinkish-gray bedrock worn smooth as river soap, its ancient strata blurred into faint, milky swirls. There is no gravel, no scree, no sharp sand; millions of years of warm rain and seeping groundwater have dissolved the grit away, leaving only undulating, whale-backed expanses of slick stone that dip gently toward a flat, drowned center.

Sunlight pours into this shallow pan with an almost physical weight. The sky above is washed pale by bright, humid haze, and the wet stone bounces the glare back up, filling the basin with a shadowless, golden-white brilliance. Warm water is everywhere, though nowhere deeper than a knee. It weeps continuously from horizontal seams in the low perimeter rock, sliding down the polished slopes in glassy, lukewarm sheets to collect in wide, still mirrors across the floor. 

At first glance, the basin looks starkly minimalist—an open court of sunlit water and pale stone, punctuated by only a dozen or so widely spaced islands of vegetation and a handful of small, incomprehensible fixtures. There is plenty of empty room between things. Twenty or thirty yards of open, shimmering water separate one feature from the next.

Where the plants do take hold, anchored in deep, silt-filled solution pits in the ancient rock, they grow with extravagant, crowded lushness. Each island is a towering, dripping fountain of broad, waxy leaves, swollen emerald stems, and heavy, chartreuse mosses that trail into the warm water like curtains. Platter-sized pads overlap at their bases, and thick, fleshy orchids hang from the outer stalks, sweating nectar in the heat. Yet these verdant mounds never spread across the pan; they remain discrete, lush oasis-pillars spaced far apart across the bright floor.

Then the eye adjusts to the glare, and the emptiness vanishes—not because the space fills with objects, but because every surface is moving.

What looked from twenty paces away like rippled golden silt on the pool bottoms is a continuous, living carpet of flat, translucent organisms the size of fingernails, overlapping like scales and gliding ceaselessly over one another in slow, circulating currents. What looked like dark mineral staining along the wet rock slopes is a dense aggregation of tiny, pearl-backed crustaceans, hundreds of thousands of them, grazing on invisible films of algae. The air between the sparse plant islands is threaded with drifting, gossamer-winged creatures no heavier than dandelion seed, rising on thermal updrafts in silent, shimmering columns. Every lush hummock of foliage is bowed under the weight of basking life: sleek, limbless amphibians coiled around stems like lacquered vines; plump, velvet-skinned crawlers clustered six-deep on the broad leaves; long-legged, spindly waders stepping delicately over the backs of flat-shelled bottom-dwellers because there is nowhere to put a foot down without touching flesh.

By any ordinary rule, a place packed this tightly with active animal life should roar with buzzing, croaking, splashing, and clicking. Instead, an eerie, padded hush hangs over the basin. 

The quiet radiates from the scattered remnants of machinery—if machinery is the word for things that have no moving parts and ignore the erosion that melted the surrounding granite down to nubs. There are only six or seven of them across the whole depression, small and seemingly incidental, arranged in no obvious symmetry. Near the western rim, a slender rod of absolute, non-reflective black rises four feet out of the weeping rock, dry to the touch despite the sheet of water parting around its base; the stone for a yard around it has sunk away over geologic time, yet the rod stands at a precise, unwavering forty-five-degree tilt, anchored to the space itself rather than the bedrock. Farther out in the shallow pan, two waist-high rings of clear, refractive material hover a hand’s breadth above the water without supports. When the warm surface current drifts beneath them, the water lifts into beads, rises lazily *through* the rings, and vaporizes instantly at their upper crests into plumes of warm, bright mist that drift outward and condense again on the lush plant islands.

These scattered fixtures do something subtle to pressure and vibration within the bowl. Sound does not travel well here; sharp noises are swallowed within a few feet, damped down as if the bright, humid air has the acoustic properties of heavy felt. All that survives is the soft, intimate *shhh-ullick* of water sheeting close to one’s ankles, a faint, intermittent *plip* of condensation falling from a broad leaf, and the whisper of thousands of tiny legs shifting at once over wet stone—a sound no louder than turning a page in an empty room.

Deprived of noise, or adapted over eons to its absence, the overwhelming animal life conducts its business through motion and light. The basin is in constant, silent agitation. A ribbon-bodied predator—pale blue, three feet long, undulating like a whip—slips out from beneath a fern-mound into the bright shallows. Instantly, a shockwave of color races across the submerged floor: millions of the translucent bottom-dwellers snap their chromatophores from silt-gold to mirror-silver in a synchronized ripple that travels fifty yards in a second, blinding the hunter with reflected sky. On the wet rock domes, swarms of tiny crabs part cleanly ahead of lumbering, soft-shelled grazers, flowing around them like liquid mercury and closing ranks behind without a single audible click of shell on shell. 

Above the water, mating displays and territorial warnings happen in rapid, silent strobes. The velvet-skinned amphibians clinging to the lush stalks inflate throat-sacs that do not croak, but glow—pulsing fierce, saturated magenta and ultraviolet even against the noon glare. Drifting clouds of aerial filter-feeders tilt their iridescent membranes in unison, throwing brief, sharp rainbows across the pale rock before banking away from lunging, clear-winged hunters that snatch them mid-air without a rustle.

Every few minutes, one of the hovering refractive rings reaches some unseen threshold in its cycle. The air inside the ring briefly warps, bending the image of the low rock rim behind it into a tight spiral; a faint smell of ozone and hot rain drifts across the water, and the warmth in the shallow pools ticks upward by half a degree before settling back. The animals pay it no mind. Three inches from a rod of physics-defying shadow that has outlasted mountains, a cluster of iridescent, six-eyed mudskippers blinks slowly in the wet heat, flashing yellow stripes at one another in the bright, breathless quiet, while warm mist drifts down from the clear sky to feed the puddles again.
