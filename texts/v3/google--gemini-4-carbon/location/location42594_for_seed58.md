The chamber is no larger than a tall cathedral nave, cut straight down into the basalt, and there is barely an inch of empty air that hasn't been claimed by iron. 

The rock walls, where they are visible behind the scaffolding, are fresh and sharp-edged. You can still see the half-round flutes of the pneumatic drill-bits and the jagged, glassy fractures of a recent blast. Nothing down here has had time to wear smooth. Even the dust on the catwalks is coarse, gritty, unweathered. Yet into this raw vertical slot, an entire multi-tiered district of heavy industry has been wedged, stacked floor upon grated floor, pipe over girder, boiler slung beneath walkway, until looking up from the sump-level is like looking through the woven reed-work of a very dark, metallic basket.

It is fiercely, breathtakingly hot. The heat does not come from the air, which is stagnant, but radiates directly out of the stone at this depth and pours off the flanks of the machines. It presses against the eyeballs and dries the nostrils on the inhale, only to coat the skin in a slick, immediate glaze of sweat. Because there is water here—not flowing, never pooled for long, but weeping steadily from a cracked stratum near the roof and bleeding out of a hundred pressure-relief valves. The moisture hits the hot iron jackets of the condensers and flashes instantly into vapor, rises ten feet, cools just enough against an intake pipe to bead into heavy droplets, and falls again as a warm, greasy indoor rain. Everything is dry to the bone in one corner and slick with condensation three paces away. Rust blooms on the fresh steel in bright, velvety oranges before the metal has even lost its mill-scale.

For a place of heavy machinery, it is uncannily hushed. There are no hammers, no sirens, no shouting crews, no clattering assembly lines. The work here is thermal and barometric. Huge, brick-jacketed retorts and copper-wound pressure vessels sit inches apart along narrow alleys of checkered plate, and they are all working furiously, silently, at capacity. Needles tremble against the glass of brass gauges. Counterweights on greased cables slide slowly down the walls, hit a trip-lever with a soft *clack*, and begin to rise again. Sight-glasses bubble with thick, clear fluids rising from below. Every thirty seconds or so, a pneumatic governor sighs—a long, wet exhalation into a canvas muffler—and a bank of louvers high in the gloom pivots open in unison, shifting the drafts. The dominant sound is a deep, sub-audible thrumming through the soles of the boots, overlaid with the quiet ticking of expanding metal and the irregular *pat, pat, pat* of condensed water hitting hot tin.

The light is thick and low, stained amber by vapor and suspended oil-mist. Carbon-filament bulbs in heavy wire cages hang at intersections where two catwalks cross, glowing like dull coals. Higher up, a few long, narrow shafts angled toward some distant surface let down blades of pale, dusty daylight that lose their strength halfway down, dissolving into the mid-level steam before they ever reach the sump. You navigate by the glow of firebox slits, illuminated pressure dials, and the faint, greenish reflection off polished brass lubricators.

There are no rats in the bilges. No beetles, no spiders stringing webs between the turnbuckles, no bats in the upper vaulting. The heat and the faint, sharp tang of sulfur and machine-tallow in the air keep all breathing, crawling things out. If a moth falls down one of the light-shafts, it dries to ash on a manifold before it reaches the bottom.

Instead, where the leaky steam-joints keep a patch of brick perpetually damp and a caged bulb hangs within a foot or two, things grow. Stiff, waxy ferns with fronds like stamped tin uncurl out of the mortar seams, thriving in the baking, pressurized greenhouse of a valve-niche. Thick pads of pale, heat-loving moss—almost white in the dimness, hot to the touch—cushion the underside of iron stairs where exhaust plumes bathe them twenty-four hours a day. Long, ropy, leafless vines, tough as vulcanized rubber, have rooted in the mineral sludge at the base of the weeping rock wall and climbed the warm return-pipes, wrapping tightly around the insulation to drink the warmth and the sweat of the joints. Where a pipe runs too hot, the vine chars black and stops; two inches away, where a flange drips, it sends out pale, blind shoots at visible speed, colonizing a newly installed bracket in a matter of days.

The whole packed pit is in constant, driverless adjustment. A seam in the young basalt shifts with a sharp *crack* as the rock settles around the excavation; dust sifts down onto a hot cylinder, smells briefly of scorched flint, and is washed away by the next cycle of condensate. A counterweight reaches the top of its track; a main valve turns itself down; the amber bulbs dim for four seconds as a dynamo shifts load, then brighten again; a cloud of white vapor blooms on Tier Four, obscuring the ironwork, rises, condenses on the cold intake main on Tier Six, and rains gently down through the grating onto the waxy ferns below, which tremble under the drops in the breathless heat.
