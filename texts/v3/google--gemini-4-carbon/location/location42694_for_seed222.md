The light hits first—unfiltered, high-altitude white that bleaches the pale stone of the basin until the horizon wavers only slightly less than the sky. Up here, above the timberlines of whatever lower country falls away beyond the rim, the air has the thin, crisp bite of great height in the lungs, yet it sits against the skin like bathwater. It should be cold at this elevation. Instead, a dry, steady warmth radiates upward from the ground and hangs motionless in thebowl of the plateau, sustained by no visible volcanic vent or heavy greenhouse haze.

The basin is ten or twelve miles across, ringed by worn, molar-smooth ridges whose peaks lost their jaggedness millions of years ago. Everything stone has settled into gentle, exhausted curves. Broad pavements of buff and pinkish-gray limestone dip toward shallow hollows, grooved by centuries of runnels that carry water only in brief, glistening intervals. Between features, there is mostly space: long, clean sightlines across swept rock and gravel flats where single boulders sit half-buried in their own pale grit, hundreds of yards apart.

Wind crosses the tableland—you can see it flatten the sparse tufts of wire-thin, bronze-colored sedge wedged into the bedrock joints, and you can see small funnels of mica dust spin up, travel fifty yards, and collapse—but it makes almost no sound. The air seems unwilling to carry vibration. Drop a stone onto the limestone pavement and it produces a single, dull *tick*, clipped off without echo, as if the sky were lined with heavy felt.

Scattered at irregular intervals across the valley—one on a low rise half a mile off, another three miles distant near a saddle in the rim, perhaps a dozen visible across the whole sweep of the plateau—stand the structures responsible for both the warmth and the hush. None are larger than a shepherd’s hut, and none look built so much as inserted into the local geometry. Nearest at hand, a slender torus of matte, non-reflective black hangs four feet above a weathered rock pan, anchored by nothing at all. Its shadow falls directly beneath it regardless of the sun’s angle. Half a mile further down-slope, three seamless splines of something resembling tarnished mercury curve out of the solid stone, braid together without touching, and pass back into the bedrock without a seam, crack, or drill-mark around their entry points—the limestone has eroded inches downward over millennia around them, leaving tiny pedestals where the rock was shielded, proving the artifacts were already there when the plateau was taller. Around these installations, the air is warmest, bleeding off a clean, waste heat from processes occurring somewhere orthogonal to ordinary matter, while simultaneously flattening longitudinal pressure waves. Any sound sharper than a breath is simply absorbed into local stillness within a few paces of its making.

Because the air cannot hold noise, the valley’s abundant animal life conducts its business in violent, flashing color.

Everywhere across the open stone, things are moving. In the middle distance, a herd of sixty or seventy long-legged, slab-sided grazers—built like sighthounds on cushioned, two-toed pads—trots in loose formation from one damp hollow to the next. Their flanks are broad and flat, covered in skin as smooth as wet chamois. As a pair of low-slung, six-limbed ambush predators shifts position behind a rounded tor half a mile upwind, the lead grazer’s flanks erupt in concentric ripples of electric magenta and stark black. Instantly, the whole herd answers, sixty bodies strobing in blinding, silent chorus against the pale rock before wheeling east at a dead run. Their padded feet strike the stone in a frantic drumroll that arrives across the gap as nothing more than a faint, dry rustle, like tissue paper turning in an empty room.

Water governs where they stop, though there isn't a stream or pond in the entire basin. Instead, moisture arrives through localized, rhythmic condensation. Wherever the terrain dips into deep, smooth-walled solution pans—some the size of dinner plates, others wide enough to bathe a horse—and wherever deep fissures hold pockets of blue shadow away from the glare, the warm air meets cooler subterranean stone—or passes through subtle thermal inversions cast by the scattered artifacts—and sweats profusely. Every morning and again in the late afternoon, sleek dark films spread outward from cracks in the pavement. Beads gather on the polished rock lips, coalesce, and trickle down established, velvet-smooth grooves into shallow pools an inch or two deep.

Around these damp seams, the sparse plant life gathers without ever crowding. Solitary stalks the height of a man rise at wide intervals along fracture lines, jointed like bamboo and coated in pale, chalky wax to bounce back the fierce sun; when the afternoon condensation begins, hair-fine copper filaments unfurl from their nodes to comb moisture directly from the passing currents. Down in the rock pans themselves, dense, rubbery mats of ochre and chartreuse growth hug the stone margin, swelling visibly when water trickles in and tightening back to leathery scabs by midday when the glare drinks the center of the puddle dry.

The damp margins are crowded with smaller, tireless traffic. Broad, translucent skimmers with legs spanned wide like calipers skate across the half-inch films of water, harvesting microscopic blooms that wake and divide within minutes of wetting. Plump, segmented browsers the size ofhares—armored in overlapping plates that mirror the sky so thoroughly they look like holes punched in the ground—creep along the wet rock seams, scraping at the chartreuse mats while flicking long, feathered antennae that taste chemical plumes on the breeze. Above them all, riding the invisible, silent columns of heat rising off the scattered artifacts, dozens of ribbon-thin gliders turn in slow spirals. They have no voices and no feathers; their membranes are stretched crystal-clear skin veined with dark pigment that flushes gold or violet to claim airspace from rivals, casting faint, tinted shadows that slide rapidly across the sun-baked limestone below.

Near one of the larger anomalies—a waist-high hemisphere of substance so optically strange that looking directly at it forces the eye to focus three miles behind it, as though gazing through a hole into a cellar of pure light—the ecosystem has worn a faint, circular track into the stone. Predators and prey alike pass within yards of it. A solitary stalker, long-bodied and pale as chalk dust, lies stretched beside the hemisphere, soaking in the steady radiant warmth while its skin slowly cycles through slow, drowsy bands of grey and sand-yellow. Ten yards away, small jumping creatures with oversized, turret-like eyes flick from pebble to pebble, snatching airborne mites that drift up from a nearby damp cleft. None of them touch the hemisphere; when windblown grit strikes its surface, the grains do not bounce off so much as vanish for half a second and reappear an inch away with zero momentum, dropping straight down into a neat, millennia-old ring of fine flour-dust around its base.

By mid-afternoon, clouds sometimes build at the far edges of the plateau—blindingly white towers of vapor pushed up from unseen lowlands beyond the rim. They crest the worn stone ridges, spill partway into the warm, high bowl, and promptly dissolve into the dry air before dropping more than a brief, warm mist that darkens a mile-wide swath of stone for twenty minutes. Wherever that passing dampness touches, dormant crusts bloom instant rust-red, the tall waxy stalks throw open their copper fans, and scores of silent animals converge from across the bright emptiness, streaming down the smooth, ancient slopes in flashing ribbons of warning and invitation, under a sun that illuminates everything and hears nothing.
