There is no ground here, only floor. 

For several miles in every direction, a shallow basin lies open to a blinding, featureless white sky. Every inch of this basin—the broad, flat plains, the stepped Causeways, the wide, low-rimmed saucers that hold the water—is composed of unglazed red and ochre terracotta, shaped by hand, dried, and fired. You can see the thumb-smears at the joins where one slab was pressed into the next. You can see the faint, sweeping striations of stiff grass brushes used to smooth the wet clay before baking, and the crisp, unweathered ridges of excess slip squeezed up between the tiles. Nothing has worn these edges down. Though water sits upon the clay and giant roots pry into the mortarless seams, the corners of the steps are as sharp as the day they were pulled from a kiln, entirely free of silt, rot, or abrasion. 

The light is immense. There is no visible sun, only an uniform, shadowless glare pouring down from the entire dome of the sky, turning the wet surfaces of the brickwork into sheets of white fire. The heat is equally total—a heavy, kiln-warmth saturated with moisture, pressing into the lungs like steam from a boiling pot. Because the light comes from everywhere at once, nothing casts a true shadow. Beneath the massive, overarching leaves of the flora, the terracotta is just as brightly lit as it is out in the open flats.

Despite the total absence of soil, the basin is lush, though its abundance is arranged with a strange, breathable sparsity. Great distances separate one botanical cluster from the next, leaving wide, glistening courts of bare brick between them. Where the plants do grow, they are staggering in their vitality, erupting directly from narrow, pre-cut slots in the clay floor. Thick, waxy-leaved vines with stems as broad as a man’s thigh uncoil across the tiles, drinking from the shallow puddles that collect in the saucer-depressions. Towering,broad-leafed stalks—resembling crossbreeds of banana palms and giant philodendrons—stand twenty feet high in isolated groves, their emerald foliage so engorged with water that the edges of their leaves continually bead and drip. There are no dead leaves. Nothing browns, wilts, or falls to decompose into mulch. The greenery exists in a state of permanent, turgid climax, locked at the exact peak of its growth.

Water is everywhere, yet it goes nowhere. It sweats directly through the porous underside of the terracotta, weeping up from whatever impossible reservoir lies beneath the world's constructed crust, gathering in ankle-deep, lukewarm sheets across the lower terraces. It condenses on the undersides of the woven-reed awnings that span between rough-hewn timber posts—structures lashed together with thick, wet plant-fiber cordage, spaced half a mile apart across the flats for no clear purpose. A drop of water swells on the tip of a leaf, falls, and strikes a puddle; a single ring expands across the bright water, hits the sharp clay lip of the basin, and vanishes, leaving the surface instantly glassy again. There is no current, no drainage, and no evaporation. The wetness simply persists.

And in this bright, hot stillness, reality loses its grip on geometry and sequence.

Look across a quarter-mile expanse of wet, empty brick toward a cluster of hand-coiled clay cisterns. As your eye travels the distance, you realize the perspective refuses to converge. The cisterns a quarter-mile away occupy the exact same visual scale, with the exact same crispness of thumb-printed texture, as the tiles at your feet. Distance here is a matter of walking, not of seeing; you have to cross the space to prove it exists, while your eyes insist everything is pressed flat against a single, blindingly bright pane of glass. 

Elsewhere, the construction loops into quiet impossibilities. A low retaining wall, built of mud-mortared river cobbles that must have been carried from somewhere outside the world, runs straight across a flooded court and smoothly dovetails into the fleshy green stalk of a giant arum lily—stone becoming plant cell without a seam, the grey cobbles gradually taking on a waxy cuticle and chlorophyll sheen while retaining the marks of the stone-hammer. Three steps lead up onto a broad platform, and three steps on the far side lead up again, and yet the platform remains level with the plain around it. Water sits neatly in a square, woven-wicker basket, filling it to the brim without leaking through the wide gaps between the reeds, held in place by an invisible skin of pure, stubborn equilibrium.

The animals are everywhere, thriving in the open spaces, and they share in the landscape's eerie, breathless poise. On the warm, wet flats between the groves, hundreds of pale, soft-shelled turtles the size of dinner plates sit arranged in loose, scattered constellations. Large, heavy-bodied wading birds with plumage the color of wet ash stand single-legged in the saucers of water, spaced twenty paces apart across the miles of red clay. Fat, iridescent amphibians—frogs with skin like hammered copper and sluggish, yard-long salamanders—cling to the vertical faces of the stepped terraces or rest in the crooks of the giant leaves. 

There are thousands of creatures within sight, yet the basin is wrapped in a profound, heavy quiet. 

The frogs do not chorus; their throats pulse slowly in the heat, Silent. When a wading bird shifts its weight or dips its long, bone-colored bill into the water, the liquid parts without a splash. A heavy-winged beetle the size of a fist drones past, and its flight produces only a faint, dry papery rustle that dies an inch from its wings, swallowed instantly by the thick, humid air. There is no wind to rattle the palm-fronds, no buzz of midges, no territorial cries. Nor is there any predation. The birds do not strike at the frogs; the salamanders do not snap at the slow, creeping snails that trace glistening, disappearing trails over the woven reed mats. Hunger seems as absent here as erosion. 

Occasionally, a creature will glitch against the stillness of the world. A copper-skinned lizard darts across a warm clay slab, stops abruptly, and for ten seconds its body exists in three places along its path at once—three identical, breathing lizards spaced a foot apart on the bright red brick, until the trailing two simply cease to be there, leaving only the frontmost one blinking its golden eyes in the glare. A wading bird steps forward, its foot passing cleanly through a solid terracotta bollard as if the fired clay were smoke, before settling onto the wet tile beyond.

Nothing builds this place anymore; there are no makers in sight, no smoke from distant kilns, no footprints drying on the paths. Yet the entire valley feels finished only moments ago. A crude wooden mallet and a wooden-bladed trowel lie beside a half-laid course of wet, unfired grey clay bricks near the basin's center. The clay of the unfinished bricks is still slick and malleable under the fierce white sky, refusing to bake dry, holding the exact, glistening curl where the trowel last sliced through it—waiting in the hot, bright quiet for a next movement that does not need to come.
