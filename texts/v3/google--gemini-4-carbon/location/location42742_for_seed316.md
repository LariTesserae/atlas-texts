The mountains are still here, all of them, down to their basalt roots, though not one of them rises higher than an ankle. Over aeons, the continental shields were ground down not into sand, which still possesses the sharp memory of rock, but into an infinite, warm, mineral grease. There is no horizon, only the eventual failure of the dim, amber light to push any further through the broth of the air. You are standing at the bottom of an atmospheric ocean that has forgotten how to rain and instead simply sweats, condensing onto every surface until there is no boundary between what is sky and what is mud.

To call this low pan empty because nothing grows tall here is to misunderstand how matter behaves at the end of time. The space is packed to suffocation. Every cubic inch is occupied by the dense, churning residue of everything the world ever was, pressed flat and set to boil without heat. 

Look down: the ground is a hyper-saturated slurry of smooth, flattened ovals—billions upon billions of pebbles polished to lenticular perfection, packed edge-to-edge like scales on a feverish flank, suspended in warm, brackish tallow. And they do not stay still. Driven by barometric tides that sweep across thousands of miles of unobstructed flatland, the ground itself undergoes violent, rhythmic liquefaction. A wave three hundred miles wide passes beneath your boots, and for four minutes the ancient stones lose their friction, sliding over one another in a frantic, sideways avalanche that goes nowhere. Millions of tons of primordial chert, jasper, and ironstone rub shoulders in the wet dark, settling, unsettling, trading places, surging up into momentary blisters the size of houses that immediately slump back into the pan with a wet, continental sigh.

The noise of it is physical. It enters through the soles of the feet and rattles the teeth in the jaw before the ears even register it as sound. It is a deafening, inescapable, wet roar—the planetary grind of infinite rounded stones clicking and sluicing together, overlaid by the tearing-canvas shriek of warm gales dragging water across the flats. There is no silence to measure it against. Wind-walls of flying warm mud collide with sheets of horizontal mist, creating sonic booms of pure slap and suction. You cannot hear yourself scream here; the air is already too full of friction to carry a voice.

Because the world has been rubbed so thin, its rules have lost their purchase. Friction and duration are wearing out alongside the granite. In the dim, bruised twilight—a sourceless ochre glow trapped beneath miles of dense vapor—distance refuses to stay calibrated. A low, weeping blister of black clay might sit twenty paces away, slick with running water, yet require three hours of slogging through ankle-deep, churning shingle to reach, while a squall line bruising the sky ten leagues to the west abruptly washes over your shoulders between one breath and the next. 

Water forgets gravity in patches. Where the barometric pressure knots itself into violent, localized spasms, sheets of warm marsh-water peel upward off the ground, hanging at chest height as quivering, horizontal lenses three acres wide. They stay suspended for minutes at a time, trembling with the sub-audible bass of the grinding earth, trapping swirling sediment and frantic, blind eels within their floating panes before the knot unties and the whole tonnage drops back onto the stones with a concussive crack that sends mud shooting sideways for a mile. Sometimes the stone-slurry churns up memories instead of minerals: for ten seconds, a patch of mud will violently organize itself into the exact, sharp-edged geometry of a mountain peak that eroded four hundred million years ago—made entirely of quivering brown water and spinning pebbles—before losing coherence and collapsing back into the flat.

Life persists only by consenting to the turbulence, sparse and tough as gristle. Every few hundred yards, where the slurry briefly eddies into heavier clay, pale, bloated tubers the size of hogs breach the surface, anchored by taproots that corkscrew thirty feet down into the shuddering silt. They have no leaves—leaves would be shredded in seconds by the flying grit and horizontal water—only rubbery, translucent vents that open with a wet pop to gulp the thick air, filtering organic broth out of the flying spray before snapping shut against an oncoming wave of stones. Long, black, whip-like reeds grow completely flat against the mud, radiating outward in hundred-foot starbursts that thrash and writhe in the currents like severed nerves, scraping clearings around themselves in the packed shingle.

In the wake of the shifting stone-banks move the few creatures built for this terminal churn. Broad, flat-bodied things, boneless as tongues and heavy as lead aprons, ride the liquefaction waves just beneath the surface of the pebbles. You rarely see them whole—only a glistening, mottled ridge of grey hide arching briefly out of the froth to vent steam, or a row of heavily lidded, milky eyes rising on stalks just high enough to read the pressure-faults in the dim air before sinking back into the grinding dark. Overhead, riding the violent updrafts where warm water-spouts collide, drift vast, ragged membranes—colonial organisms like wet, tattered sails, miles from tip to tip, straining plankton and airborne silt out of the screaming sky, tearing apart in the crosswinds and seamlessly re-fusing when the gusts slam their pieces back together.

And scattered through the infinite, packed frenzy are the cairns. 

They should not still be standing. Nothing here has the right to remain vertical. They are small, dome-shaped huts and low, spiraling windbreaks, spaced leagues apart, built from the very lenticular stones that churn around their bases, mortared with nothing at all. There is no metal in them, no glass, no wire, no hinges—only wet stone stacked by hands long gone, or hands that belong to something you haven't seen. A wave of liquefied earth will come roaring across the pan at forty miles an hour, tearing up tuber-beds and tossing tons of water into the ochre gloom, and split cleanly around a waist-high ring of unmortared river-rock as if the structure existed in a slightly adjacent afternoon. 

If you crawl inside one of these low, dripping shelters, the packed stones overhead sweating warm drops down your neck, the deafening roar of the continent drops away—not fading, just abruptly severed at the threshold—replaced by the frantic, wet ticking of your own pulse. Scratched into the soft, ancient soapstone of the interior walls are shallow, spiraling grooves, worn nearly smooth by condensation, mapping currents and stone-tides across a geography too vast to hold in the mind. When you crawl back out into the dim, roaring soup, the cairn you just left is already half a mile behind you, sliding steadily eastward on a river of boiling shingle, intact and dry at its core, while the wet sky presses down to fill the space where you were just kneeling.
