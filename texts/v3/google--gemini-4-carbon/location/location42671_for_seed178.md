You hear the valley miles before you can see into it, and when you are inside it you cannot hear yourself shout. The wind comes off higher ground somewhere out of sight and drops into this broad, low basin like water over a spillway, funneled between fresh fault walls until the air itself feels packed, dense with cold spray and flying leaf-litter. Everything is near freezing—one or two degrees above zero in the mud, half a degree below on the exposed windward ridges—so the moisture never quite settles on a state. Horizontal rain turns to grease-ice on stone, gets stripped off by the next gust, and goes airborne again as stinging shards.

It is nearly pitch dark. Above the tearing cloud-wrack there may be a winter moon or high-latitude twilight, because a faint, pewter dimness leaks downward—just enough to separate the thrashing black mass of the canopy from the slightly paler grey of churning water and sky. 

Underfoot, the earth has not had time to soften or round off. The lowland is a wide tectonic graben, recently dropped and still settling, floored with shattered flood-basalt and raw greywacke. Every rock edge is razor-sharp, freshly split, stacked in jagged talus aprons and stepped scarps that haven't seen enough centuries of frost to crumble into gentle slopes. Yet despite the raw youth of the rock and the biting cold, the basin is choked with vegetation. Warmth bleeds up from the fractured crust into the groundwater—not enough to steam, just enough to keep the root-zone five or six degrees warmer than the gale above. Into every knife-edged fissure, massive cushions of moss, liverwort, and coarse black peat have wedged themselves, drinking the incessant rain. Out of those soggy mats grows a dense, muscular jungle of the cold: thickets of leathery, broad-leafed evergreens flexed horizontal by the gale; waist-high giant ferns with stalks as thick as wrists; tangled, prostrate conifers whose trunks braid over the sharp talus like ship's hawser. Everything grows crowded, layered five deep, leaves slapping and sawing against one another with a continuous, frantic roar like surf on gravel.

Water is everywhere, never deep enough to drown the forest, never still enough to freeze solid. It sheets off the slick foliage, pools in angular rock basins, and spills over fresh fault-lips in thousands of temporary cataracts that vanish and reform fifty yards away when a logjam breaks or a bank collapses. Braided streams slice across the valley floor, black and swift, constantly chewing through beds of angular clinker, jumping their channels in the dark with the grinding boom of boulders rolling underwater.

Nothing here sleeps through the storm, because the storm isn't an event; it is the permanent condition of the basin. The darkness is crowded with warm blood and motion. Flocks of heavy-bodied, oil-feathered waterfowl ride the violent ground-eddies just feet above the whipping scrub, dropping like stones into leeward ditch-pools by the hundreds, their harsh, brassy honking swallowed ten paces downwind. Beneath the fern-canopy, where the wind drops from a shriek to a heavy, wet draft, the mud is churned into a labyrinth of runs. Compact,barrel-chested browsers with double coats of matted guard hair push single-file through the thorny underbrush, stripping bark and tough winter fronds, entirely indifferent to the branches thrashing inches above their spines. In the flooded gravel braids, heavy silver fish fight upstream through water thick with silt and torn moss, their backs breaking the surface in the dark, drawing sleek, thick-furred mustelids and low-flying night herons that hunt by touch and vibration along the roaring banks. Every hollow beneath an upturned root-plate or basalt overhang is packed with small, hot-blooded things—voles, amphibians tough enough for ice-water, heavy flightless beetles clinging to wet stone.

Scattered at wide intervals across the miles of churning lowland are the installations, though "installation" is almost too heavy a word for how little ground they occupy. You find them first by the sound disappearing.

Walking through the roaring dark, head down against the sleet, you step across an invisible threshold and stumble because the forty-knot gale against your chest has simply stopped. In a cylinder perhaps twelve meters across, the air is dead calm and ten degrees warmer. Around the perimeter of that invisible boundary, blown rain and torn fern-fronds strike nothing at all, lose every fraction of their kinetic energy instantly, and drop straight down in a neat, continuous curtain of falling water and debris that rings the dry clearing like a circular waterfall. 

At the center of such a clearing there is never a fence, a wire, a concrete pad, or a bolted seam. There might be a slender column of something matte and grey-black, no thicker than a walking stick, rising twenty feet out of the jagged basalt without a drill-scar around its base—stone and post-metallurgical lattice interpenetrating at the atomic scale so the rock simply becomes the pylon. Six feet above its tip, three smooth, asymmetrical solids the size of sea-chests hang unmoving in the dark air, separated from the shaft and from one another by empty space. If you push against one with all your weight, it does not budge, sway, or hum; it is locked not to the ground beneath it, which trembles constantly with micro-tremors as the young valley settles, but to some deeper geometric frame that lets the planet's crust shiver fractionally around it.

Half a mile away across a foaming braid of river, another structure spans a newly opened fault fissure: a faint, hair-thin line of cold white refraction stretched taut from scarp to scarp. Landslides of wet talus and uprooted trees periodically slump toward the ravine; where the tumbling rock meets that hair-thin line, the stone parts cleanly, silently, without sparks or dust, falling into the torrent in neat, glass-smooth halves while the filament never vibrates. Farther down-valley, half-buried in a boggy hollow of giant horsetails, sits a low, featureless blister of material that drinks every photon of the faint skyglow, darker than a hole in the earth. Droplets of condensing marsh-mist touch its surface and vanish before they can bead, split or shunted elsewhere to feed whatever silent accounting the object runs. Animals use the dead-air pockets around these artifacts casually—thirty soaked shorebirds tucked nose-to-wind just inside a kinetic boundary, sleeping in crisp silence three inches from a howling wall of sleet; moss growing right up to the seamless join of a spatial anchor and stopping cleanly, neither burned back nor encouraged.

Step two paces out of that calm cylinder and the valley takes you right back. The roar slams against your eardrums at full volume—wind tearing across miles of sharp rock ridges, millions of tough wet leaves thrashing in unison, fresh landslides clattering down into black water, birds screaming out of the dark overhead. A gust rips the cold rain off the canopy in a solid sheet, a fault-step shifts underfoot with a dull, sub-audible thud through your boots, a stream spills over a freshly cracked lip of basalt and begins cutting a brand-new trench through the moss, and for miles in every direction the dark, freezing green basin heaves and remakes itself without pause.
