High near the crest of a jagged, arid massif, just below the elevation where the thin air freezes solid at night, the stone opens inward into a sunken vault no larger than a village church. Outside is bare talus and starless scree; inside, the mountain exhales heat. 

There is almost no light here. Far up where the vault narrows into crooked chimneys, the rock twists enough to baffle the sky completely, letting down neither moon nor starlight, only thin downdrafts of freezing mountain air that never reach the floor before they are swallowed by the furnace warmth rising from below. What illumination exists is scarce and treacherous: a dull, iron-red incandescence deep inside the narrow floor-fissures—so faint the eye loses it when looking directly at it—and the occasional blue-white prick of static electricity snapping across the dark when dry bodies brush too quickly past one another.

Because there is no bare rock left to touch. Every square inch of the chamber is occupied.

At first encounter, in the pitch dark and the forty-five-degree dry heat, the walls feel upholstered. Press a hand out and it meets something yielding, suede-dry, and hotter than human blood. It shifts slightly to accommodate the pressure, and three or four neighboring forms shift with it in a slow, muscular ripple, like heavy sacks of grain settling in a hold. From floor to vaulted ceiling, packed into alcoves, layered three deep across ledges, wedged into cooling cracks and clustered in hanging festoons from the overhangs, the cavern is solid with living flesh.

Water is nearly absent from the mountain, and entirely absent as a free liquid in the vault. Nothing drips. Nothing condenses on the upper stone. The air smells of hot basalt, static ozone, dry musk, and pulverized chalk. What scant moisture exists in this place has been locked inside a closed biological treasury for thousands of years, passed from prey to predator, mother to offspring, parasite to host, without ever touching the air. When something dies or is punctured, nothing spills; tissues are dense, waxy, and viscous as warm tallow, drunk dry by neighbors before a drop could evaporate. Waste falls only as fine, bone-white pellets of pure crystalline uric salt, drifting down through the dark like dry sleet to dissolve and vaporize against the scorching lips of the floor vents.

The geology beneath the press of bodies has been shaped less by weather than by touch. Water never carved this hall; originally a gas-blister in an old volcanic neck, it has been widened, smoothed, and rounded over millennia by biological friction. Where a narrow ridge of black trachyte juts out between fissures, millions of generations of leathery flanks and blunt, horn-plated feet have rubbed it to a mirror polish, slick with baked skin-oils. Deep troughs wind across the floor from vent to wall—highways worn knee-deep into solid igneous rock by soft, relentless traffic. The chamber has settled into an ancient, worn equilibrium: stone giving way micrometers a century to hide, horn, and bristle.

Nothing green grows here, and no root has ever split the ceiling. At the very edges of the hot fissures, where dry sulfurous gases hiss faintly out of the mountain’s throat, a pale, powdery tarnish coats the rock—a lithotrophic crust, half mineral bloom and half bacterial ash, baking in the heat. That dry crust is the entire foundation of the vault’s frantic, silent economy.

Broad, flat-bodied grazers the size of flagstones clamp over the fissure rims like living shingles, scraping the bacterial powder with rasping, toothless mouths while enduring temperatures that would blister bare skin. Stacked directly atop their backs—because there is nowhere else to be—are tiers of narrower, multi-limbed gleaners, blind and pale under coats of dense, felt-like down that insulates them BOTH from the scorching updrafts and from the crushing body-heat of their neighbors. Between and over them move the hunters: long, flattened, ribbon-muscled things with wide sensory fringes along their jaws that read the room entirely in thermal gradients, slipping through crevices between packed bodies the way eels slip through reef-coral. 

Overhead, the air itself is crowded. Strong convective columns rise continuously from the floor vents toward the cold upper chimneys, then curl and sink along the outer walls. Riding that rolling thermal cell is a thick, slow whirlpool of gliders—creatures stretched on membranes of dry parchment-skin, lacking eyes or ears, steering by heat-pits and electrostatic hairs. They never flap; there isn't room. They bank and spiral inches apart in the dark, filtering floating flakes of shed skin, bacterial soot, and tiny aerial mites out of the rising column of hot air. Every few seconds, two dry wing-tips graze in the desiccated air and strike a hairline spark of pale violet static, lighting up for a thousandth of a second a solid mosaic of velvet backs, breathing spiracles, folded limbs, and twitching thermal fronds before pitch blackness snaps shut again.

For all this furious saturation of life, the chamber is uncannily hushed. 

Voices have no utility in a space so packed that every neighbor is already touching you, and hard chitin or clattering hooves would waste precious energy and give away movement to vibration-sensitive mouths. Evolution here has muffled everything in felt, suede, rubbery cartilage, and soft dust. Furthermore, the sheer mass of bodies deadens acoustics the way heavy velvet drapes deaden a theater. There are no echoes. What sound exists is a continuous, low-register murmur just above the threshold of hearing: a dry, papery rustle of millions of overlapping breaths, the soft *shhh-shhh* of leathery bellies sliding over oiled stone, the faint mineral *tick* of salt-pellets hitting hot rock, and a deep, sub-audible tremor of collective digestion and muscle-shift that enters a listener through the soles of the boots rather than the ears. Life and death happen inches away in velvet silence. A hunter slides its heat-fringe under a sleeper’s flank, uncoils a dry proboscis, and empties the body over twenty minutes while the surrounding crowd merely presses inward to fill the slackening volume as the body deflates.

Scattered through the chamber, half-swallowed by the living press, are small, stubborn remnants of human or near-human making. 

Near the wider ledges stand low, circular enclosures of dry-stacked slate, no bigger than wells or hearth-rings, built without mortar from flat stones carried in from the outer scree. Their tops are rounded and greased smooth by the creatures pouring endlessly over them. Carved into a basalt buttress near the northern wall is a series of shallow, hand-chiseled basins and vertical tally-grooves—perhaps left by people who once crawled down the freezing upper chimneys holding breath against the sulfur heat to harvest the pure salt-crusts, or shed velvet pelts, or the waxy fat of the vent-grazers. In one recessed alcove stands a single waist-high cairn of river-cobbles carried up from valleys miles below, completely foreign to the volcanic peak. Right now, a dense mat of segmented, warm-blooded brooders—each the size of a loaf, pulsing slowly in synchronization—has plastered itself so thickly over the cairn that only two pale, water-worn stones still peek through a gap in their breathing flanks, holding the shape of a monument underneath a blanket of flesh.

Nothing about these structures interrupts the room; they have simply become more topography, more surface area in a world desperate for surface area. Around a carved stone lintel, gliders bank in the dark; inside a ritual niche meant for an offering bowl, three coiled, blind vipers—or things converged on the shape of vipers—share a cubic foot of hot stagnant air with a brood of downy mites that clean their scales.

High up near the narrow chimney throats, where the rising fifty-degree draft finally collides with the sub-zero mountain night, a turbulent invisible boundary hangs in the rock shaft. None of the creatures cross it. Rising gliders feel the sudden knife-edge of cold on their dorsal membranes, fold their wings inward for half a heartbeat, drop ten feet through the dark like stones, and spread wide again in the rich, suffocating, velvet warmth below—turning endlessly back into the crowded dark.
