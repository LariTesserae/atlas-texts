The space is a subterranean chamber no larger than a railway terminus, hollowed out of deep earth long ago, though there has been no rock or dirt visible here for centuries. Every wall, every vaulted overhead rib, and every square inch of the floor consists entirely of living, lignified root-matter. It is a single, seamless, hollow architecture of pale, barkless wood, slick with warm condensation and smelling sharply of bruised sap and hot machine oil. 

Despite this total enclosure of living matter, the middle of the room is remarkably empty. There is no undergrowth, no dangling curtain of vines, no clutter of leaves to choke the sightlines. The space preserves the stark, open geometry of an industrial nave. What fills the air is not foliage, but the low, amber gloom of sodium-vapor lamps recessed into the wooden walls, and an almost intolerable volume of sound.

Built directly into the living floor and walls—and by now thoroughly grafted to them—is the infrastructure of a compact, self-contained factory town. Cast-iron catwalks span the open air fifty feet up, their bolted brackets swallowed up to the rivets by smooth, calloused swells of amber wood. Heavy flywheel engines sit in sunken pits along the perimeter, rhythmic and tireless, their brass pistons slick with grease. There are no boilers or coal chutes; instead, thick, muscular taproots plunge directly into the engine housings, feeding volatile, turpentine-rich resins straight into the combustion cylinders. The machines and the mega-flora have settled into an ancient, weathered equilibrium. Iron pipes disappear into smooth woody burls, and miles of insulated copper cabling run neatly alongside pulsing, translucent phloem-tubes, bound together by tarnished brass clamps. Every steel edge is rounded by centuries of damp friction; every iron beam carries a velvety patina of rust that the surrounding bark continually drinks in for trace minerals.

There is no flowing water here, no underground stream or standing pool. Moisture arrives entirely by breath and sweat. Every twelve minutes, the massive, hidden pressure-valves of the deep root system shift, and the central engines Vent. A great, warm sigh of exhaust steam rolls out from brass louvers near the floor, filling the dim chamber with a temporary, pearlescent fog. The mist rises through the open air, hits the cooler vaulted ceiling, and condenses on the smooth wooden ribs, pattering back down in brief, localized showers of warm, slightly oily rain before the thirsty floor drinks it dry again.

If the open air of the chamber feels sparse in its architecture, its surfaces are terrifyingly alive. 

At first glance, the thick, rounded contours of the root-walls, the undersides of the iron catwalks, and the casings of the engines appear to be textured—fluted, perhaps, or shingled in dark slate. Step closer, or wait for a pressure valve to shriek, and the texture detaches itself in a violent, roaring wave. 

The fauna here is absolute, a living crust several inches thick that coats almost every stationary surface in the room. Millions of heavy-bodied, wingless arthropods, pale-shelled isopods the size of clenched fists, and blind, velvet-skinned climbers pack themselves shoulder-to-shoulder along the warm wooden vaults and up the vibrating cast-iron pillars. They leave the central air empty because they cannot fly, clinging instead to the architecture in dense, shifting carpets. 

Everything they do is governed by the industrial rhythm of the room, creating an acoustic environment that presses physically against the eardrums. Underneath it all is the deep, percussive *thump-hiss* of the resin-engines, a bass note felt in the teeth. Layered over this is the dry, deafening *shhhk-shhhk-shhhk* of millions of chitinous legs shifting in unison across the bark, sounding like tons of gravel being dragged endlessly across iron sheets. 

They are farmers, parasites, and maintenance crew all at once. Where the engines vibrate hardest, clusters of pale, soft-bellied browsers gather to lick the warm condensation and oil-sweat from the brass fittings, keeping the moving parts immaculate. Higher up, along the dim upper vaults, heavier,scissor-jawed predators patrol the living carpet, carving neat, geometric channels through the pressing crowds of smaller creatures. When a predator strikes, a localized ripple tears through the wall-fauna—hundreds of creatures letting go of the ceiling at once to escape, dropping fifty feet through the empty, dim air to hit the wooden floor or the iron walkways with a relentless, hailstone clatter of shells, only to immediately right themselves and scramble back up the nearest vibrating pipe.

Along the terraced edges of the chamber, small, tiered housings—more like stacked iron lockers or narrow switchman’s shanties than proper homes—cling to the walls, linked by spiral stairs. Whether anyone human still tends the switches inside them is impossible to tell through the grime-caked, condensation-beaded portholes. If they do, they move by the same strict rules as everything else in this small, thundering hollow. 

Every few minutes, a dynamo whines up to pitch, shifting the electrical load. The recessed amber lamps flicker down to a dull, cherry-red glow. Instantly, the millions of creatures lining the cathedral walls freeze, and a sudden, shocking silence falls over the biological layer, leaving only the heavy chug of the pistons. Then, somewhere in the dark overhead, a brass whistle blows a single, flat note, the steam vents erupt in a white hiss, the lights swell back to dusty gold, and the walls begin to roar and crawl once more.
