The bedrock has been worn down past the point of having features. Once, epochs ago, there was an alpine range here, pushed three miles into the troposphere; the altitude remains, vast and breathless, a plateau the size of a continent, while the peaks themselves have been planed away to a nearly level shield of ancient grey gneiss. The stone is polished smooth not just in texture, where it shows at all, but in macro-topography—hundreds of miles of gentle, exhausted swells that rise six inches over a league and sink back again. The air is thin and bitterly cold, hovering just above and just below freezing under an ordinary, cloudless afternoon sun. The light is crisp, colourless, and completely honest, the kind of plain high-altitude daylight in which you feel you ought to be able to trust your eyes.

You cannot.

Across this flattened roof of the world stretches a city built of hand-split slate, river clay, woven sedge, and cold-hammered native copper. It has all the anatomy of a dense, organized municipality: kerbstone-lined avenues, stepped cisterns, multi-story tenements with corbelled stone roofs, covered marketplaces framed in dark bog-timber, and miles of open-channel drainage gutters. Yet there is almost nothing around you. Between one dry-stone house and its immediate neighbour next door lies four miles of unbroken, freezing greenery. Between the north side of a market square and the south side stretches half a day’s walk of empty plateau, though the braided grass awning meant to shade the stalls spans the entire gulf—twenty feet of woven reed stretched across six miles of sky without sagging, tearing, or thinning, its individual fibres looking just near enough to touch for the whole length of their impossible span.

Space here has come unmoored from measurement. The city was not built scattered; it has been dilated by a slow, active weather of broken geometry that moves continuously across the plateau. Neighbourhoods drift apart like pack ice while remaining topologically joined. If you stand at the threshold of a clay-chinked storehouse and look through its low doorway, you do not see the dark interior; you see the interior standing open to the sky eleven miles to the west, where the back three walls of the building currently reside, surrounded by deep moss. Should you toss a pebble over the stone sill at your feet, it vanishes across the eleven-mile gap in an instant and clatters quietly against the rear slate wall on the horizon.

The silence in which this happens is profound. Nothing flies here. Nothing burrows, grazes, scurries, or hums. Turn over a flat stone in a damp courtyard and the pale soil beneath holds no beetles, no worms, no woodlice—only clean mineral grit and a dense white mat of root-hairs. There is no bird-call to measure the thin air, no insect whine in the scrub. The quiet is broken only by the soft, continuous *tick-drip* of condensation, the occasional wooden groan of a roof-beam adjusting to a local warp in breadth, and the whispered rush of cold water running along stone conduits that do not connect to one another visually, pouring out of a carved slate spout in one district and emerging simultaneously from a dry culvert three horizons away.

In the absence of animals, the vegetation has claimed the plateau with startling, silent vigour. Despite the biting cold, the ground between the scattered buildings is lush and deep. Enormous cushion-plants—dense domes of tiny, tightly packed emerald and oxblood leaves—swell waist-high over the ancient rock, firm enough to walk on, hoarding pockets of solar warmth inside their spongy interiors. Between them sprawl thickets of low, fleshy-leaved frost-shrubs dark with antifreeze saps, silver-green liverworts the size of dinner plates, and sprawling carpets of wet, rusty sphagnum. The dampness comes not from heavy rain, which the high, thin sky rarely manages, but from restless condensation cycles. Banks of heavy mist materialize abruptly out of clear sunlight along invisible seams in the air, drenching a square mile of moss and masonry in minutes before vanishing just as cleanly, leaving every copper pin, woven wall-mat, and dark leaf beaded with freezing water.

Even the water obeys a ruined memory of the world. Though the mountains were ground flat a billion years ago, gravity has not entirely caught up with the erosion. Above the flat plains of lush tundra, invisible ghost-valleys and phantom ridges of the primordial mountain range still exert their pull. Cold mist condenses in mid-air forty feet above the ground, clinging to the slopes of peaks that no longer exist. Runnels of clear snowmelt gather in the empty sky, trickling down steep, unseen inclines of pure gravitational habit, braiding and pooling in mid-air before spilling over invisible ledges to splash quietly into the stone cisterns of the city below. The plants have learned to climb these ghosts. Thick, woody vines root in the ground-level peat and twine upward into nothing, spiralling around the vanished granite spires, spreading wide canopies of waxy, dark-green foliage against invisible cliff-faces high in the bright cold air, drinking from the sky-streams.

Everything is in quiet, tireless motion. The ecosystem and the broken physics feed one another at a steady, deliberate pace. Across a wide boulevard—whose twin rows of kerb-stones are currently separated by a league of crimson bog-moss—a front of spatial compression passes like a silent gust of wind. For twenty minutes, eight scattered tenements, a copper-sheathed watchtower, and a sunken bathhouse slide smoothly inward across the landscape without disturbing a single stem of sedge. Their foundations do not scrape the earth; the earth between them simply ceases to have width. As their stone walls draw within yards of one another, the air trapped in the narrowing alleys grows momentarily warm from concentrated sunlight, and the mosses crowning their slate roofs flush a sudden, vivid chartreuse, releasing clouds of pale yellow spores that drift horizontally into open windows and doorways. Then the front passes. Distance floods back into the street. The buildings recede toward the pale rims of the world, carrying dustings of each other’s spores into isolation.

Walk along one of the paved roads—great slabs of hand-dressed gneiss laid without mortar, fitted with patient, primitive precision—and the ground feels entirely solid beneath your boots, yet your stride cannot be trusted. Five steps might carry you past a hundred yards of cushion-willow; the sixth might take ten minutes of steady walking to complete while your boot hangs an inch above a puddle of meltwater that refuses to pass behind you. Looking down into that puddle, the reflection does not show the pale afternoon sky overhead, but the sky from six hours ago, dark blue with morning twilight and dusted with fading stars, though the sun is warm on the back of your neck.

At the edges of vision, perspective itself loses discipline. Because light travels across patches of variable metric density, a massive stepped granary built of stacked turf and timber twenty miles to the north can appear larger in the eye than a boundary stone ten paces ahead. It looms on the horizon in crisp, sunlit clarity—every frost-split peg in its rafters visible, beads of cold dew trembling on the thatch—occupying a third of the northern sky while remaining twenty miles out of reach. Next to it, a stone aqueduct only half a mile away is shrunk to a fine grey thread.

Still, the city functions as a landscape. Water enters the primitive intakes high on the invisible mountain slopes, runs through miles of split-log pipes and stone troughs across severed geometries, waters the courtyard gardens of giant frost-ferns and bulbous, heat-generating cabbages that steam faintly in the near-zero air, and drains away into hand-dug sumps where the rock has grown slightly porous with age. Hearth-pits lined with river cobbles sit in the centres of roofless rooms, empty of ash, their stacked bundles of dried peat pristine and unburned, yet radiating a faint, perpetual, flameless chill—an inversion of fire left running like an untended millwheel. Over all of it, the plain, bright daylight moves steadily westward, casting sharp, dark shadows of low shrubs, scattered huts, and vanished, jagged mountains across an endless, breathing carpet of cold green.
