It is not an ocean, because an ocean has a bottom that stays put and a surface that opens to the sky. Instead, imagine a volume of warm, bruising water the size of an abbey nave, suspended high in the ribs of a mountain range that hasn’t finished breaking.

There is no clear boundary where the stone ends and the liquid begins, only a threshold of violent pressure. The chamber—if it can be called a chamber when its walls refuse to remain architectural—hangs six thousand feet above the lowlands, pinned between jagged, unweathered slabs of black basalt and raw greenstone. These rocks are brand new in geological time. Their edges haven't been sanded down by centuries of silt; they are fresh fractures, razor-sharp, flaking off in glassy shards the size of dinner plates whenever the current shifts. And the current shifts every few seconds.

The water itself is bath-warm, hovering just above skin temperature, thick with microscopic bubbles that never quite rise to a surface because gravity here has lost its singular direction. Up is negotiable. The water churns in three-dimensional gyres, boiling without heat, slamming against the raw stone walls and tearing away only to collide with itself in the center of the space. 

Because of this, the sound is absolute. It isn't the rhythmic crash of surf or the steady roar of a waterfall; it is an overlapping, inescapable concussion. Water hammering rock, rock shearing against rock, and beneath it all, the terrifying acoustic phenomenon of cavitation—millions of tiny vacuum pockets snapping shut simultaneously inside the liquid, producing a continuous, deafening crackle like burning timber amplified through a brass horn, layered over a sub-audible bass grinding that vibrates directly inside the sternum and behind the eyes. To exist in this space is to be beaten by sound. It presses against the eardrums until hearing stops being a sense and becomes a purely physical impact.

Light has almost nowhere to enter, and what little exists doesn't behave correctly. There is no sun filtering down from above. Instead, a dim, bruised luminescence leaks directly from the freshly cracked seams in the basalt—not lava, for it carries no scorching heat, just a dull, exhausted violet-grey glow, as though light had been trapped inside the stone during the mountain’s formation and is now bleeding out into the flood. When the turbulence rips a new fissure in the wall, the room brightens briefly to the shade of wet slate, illuminating the vast, empty stretches of churning water between the floating debris, before the suspended silt drinks the brightness back down to gloom.

The rules of containment are failing here. Near the upper edges of this cathedral-sized pocket, the water simply stops being continuous, fracturing into colossal, wobbling spheres of liquid interspersed with pockets of breathless, mist-choked air that whip through the current like silver tornados before collapsing back into flood. If you look across the sixty-foot expanse—the maximum sightline before the walls pinch inward—the geometry of the far stone face stutters. A jutting shelf of black rock appears twice, three feet apart, flickering in the dim violet murk like a double exposure, until a violent surge of warm water crashes through both of them and resolves them back into a single, newly broken spire. Space is slightly larger on the left side of the vortex than on the right; swimming across it takes longer in one direction than the return trip, even when fighting the exact same current.

Yet, remarkably, someone tried to build here—or else what they built elsewhere fell through the seams when the mountain tore open.

Scattered widely across the jagged, pitching walls are remnants of dry-stone masonry, entirely out of place and utterly devoid of metal, mortar, or machinery. Near a lower cleft, three courses of hand-dressed limestone curve into the start of a low wall, clinging to the black basalt as if fused to it by sheer misunderstanding. Higher up, wedged into an inverted chimney where the water spins horizontally, sits a crude, four-sided shrine no larger than a doghouse, built of pale river cobbles that do not belong to this high, sharp elevation. Occasionally, spinning lazily through the roaring center of the space before being obliterated against a fresh ridge, comes a woven reed basket or a split timber beam grooved with stone-axe marks—artifacts from a quiet, pre-iron world, completely whole for ten seconds, then shredded into pulp by the deafening hydraulic shear. Beneath the roar of the water, the stone arches seem to hum when the turbulence strikes them, holding their shape against physics purely because they haven't yet realized they are underwater.

Life is scarce, separated by huge gulfs of empty, violent water, surviving only by accepting the instability. Anchored into the freshest, sharpest cracks—preferring edges keen enough to slice leather—grow solitary tufts of tough, cartilaginous weeds. They have no leaves to be torn away; they look like bundles of slick, pale-ochre antlers, flexing furiously in the cross-currents, absorbing warmth and whatever strange mineral energy leaks with the dim light from the stone. There might be only a dozen of these growths in the entire cavern, isolated pale markers in the roaring dark.

Even rarer are the animals. Now and then, moving through the deafening murk with utter indifference to the chaos, comes something shaped roughly like a flatworm the length of a human forearm, translucent and ribbed with faint, milky cartilage. It does not swim against the boiling currents. Instead, it slides *along* the breaks in reality—gliding smoothly along the invisible boundary where two opposing walls of water shear past each other, disappearing halfway across an open expanse only to emerge instantly twenty feet lower down, grazing on the pale antler-weeds before peeling off into the dark again. There are perhaps three or four of them in the whole roaring basin. They have no eyes and no ears; in a place this dark and this loud, both organs would be liabilities.

Nothing settles. No silt accumulates into a floor, because there is no consistent floor for it to rest upon. When a massive wedge of greenstone finally gives way under the relentless acoustic hammering, it doesn't fall downward; it gets caught in the warm, deafening centrifuge, tumbling end over end through the dim purple murk, knocking two stone steps off a vanished staircase, grazing a lone tuft of pale weed, and slamming into the opposite wall to strike open another brief, bruised line of light before the water swallows the scar and keeps turning.
