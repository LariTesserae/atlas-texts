At the very top of the uplift, where the planetary crust has been thrust so high into the thinning sky that noon looks like deep twilight, the basalt needle splits open internally without breaking its outer skin. Inside is a single, vertical blister of space—no wider than a city square, perhaps ninety meters from wall to wall—sealed away from the freezing stratosphere outside. 

It should be bitter cold at this altitude, miles above the cloud decks, yet the pocket is stiflingly hot. Heat pours upward from the narrow magmatic throat at the floor, trapped inside this stone lung until the air feels thick enough to chew. The rock is geologically infant, torn and cooled only centuries ago: black volcanic glass, sharp-ribbed tachylite, and jagged columns of porphyry whose edges are still razor-keen. Only the constant, sweating dampness has begun to dull them. Because the outer shell of the peak is pressed against near-space, the upper walls of the chamber are cold enough to force the rising geothermal vapor to condense. The ceiling weeps continuously. Warm, mineral-sour droplets gather on obsidian teeth, let go, and fall through the dark.

They do not fall straight down. 

Suspended in the hollow center of the chamber, occupying very little of its actual volume, is an unmistakable municipality—though nothing with lungs or footsteps ever lived in it. It is an architecture of sparse, floating districts, spaced wide apart in the dim air so that the overwhelming impression of the room remains its emptiness. Slabs of matte, non-reflective gray ceramic, no bigger than tabletops yet detailed with thousands of recessed porticoes, cantilevered balconies, and sunken plazas, hang motionless without cables or pylons. Between them run hair-thin thoroughfares of coherent, dim amber light. The falling water droplets curve smoothly around these structures, deflected by localized metric distortions that give each tiny, floating city block its own private vertical and its own private dryness, before resuming their drop toward the floor.

The light is scarce and indirect. At the very apex of the chamber, a jagged rent in the stone about the width of a doorway opens directly onto the upper atmosphere. Nothing rushes out; an invisible tension—a flat, seamless plane of altered physics—holds the hot, pressurized air inside like the skin of a drum. Through that narrow window, the sky is a bruised, airless violet-black where a few hard, unblinking stars remain visible alongside the distant, shrunken glare of the sun. Very little of that brightness makes it down into the pocket. Instead, the gloom is underlit by the faint, dull-red thermal bleed of the rock floor and the occasional cool cyan flicker running through the suspended infrastructure, like traffic moving at relativistic speeds along wires too thin to see.

There is no soil here, no moss softening the ledges, not a single spore or lichen. The nearest thing to biology is a dry, iridescent tarnish that creeps slowly across the lower basalt walls where the heat is fiercest. Under close inspection, the tarnish resolves into geometric step-pyramids of bismuth-like crystal that assemble and disassemble themselves over the course of hours, feeding on the steep temperature gradient between the scorching stone and the cooler condensate trickling down from above. Whether this crust is a pre-biotic mineral habit or shed maintenance-residue from the suspended city is impossible to tell. Nothing crawls on it. Nothing grazes it.

Despite the absence of life, the room is restless. It operates on a tight, self-contained meteorological and mechanical rhythm. Every few minutes, a pocket of superheated steam belches silently from the narrow cleft at the bottom of the cavern, sending a shimmer of heat-haze rippling upward through the empty intervals between the floating plazas. As the thermal plume rises, the suspended blocks subtly re-index themselves. Whole tiered neighborhoods—complete with miniature viaducts, towers no thicker than needles, and dark, windowless basilicas—slide a few centimeters to the left or drop half a meter in absolute silence, tilting on unseen gimbals to let the updraft pass or to harvest its kinetic energy. 

When the vapor hits the cold stone vault near the sky-window, a brief, localized squall forms. For thirty seconds, hot rain pelts down through the upper third of the chamber. The drops hiss against the warmer conduits, bead up on invisible barriers in mid-air—forming temporary, trembling lenses of water suspended in nothingness—and then spill sideways in miniature cataracts onto the jagged obsidian scree below, where they flash back into steam before they can pool.

Sound barely carries, muffled by the odd behavior of the air around the suspended structures, which seem to drink acoustic energy the way they drink heat. There is no roar of wind from outside the atmospheric membrane; the stratosphere beyond the rent might as well be painted on glass. Inside, there is only the soft, irregular patter of condensation hitting fresh volcanic glass, the sharp *tick* of young rock cooling or expanding by a fraction of a millimeter, and, underneath everything, a dry, sub-audible hum that isn't heard in the ears so much as felt behind the teeth.

Standing on one of the narrow, sharp-edged basalt shelves that ring the chamber, you can take in the whole world in a single turn of the head: the black glass walls sweating in the dark, the patch of star-pricked purple sky overhead, the hot void beneath your boots, and, hanging in the middle of the room like dust motes caught in a dim shaft of light, the quiet, immaculate avenues of an empty metropolis constantly adjusting its altitude in the steam.
