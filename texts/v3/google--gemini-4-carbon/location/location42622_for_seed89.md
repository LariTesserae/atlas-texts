Outside the cleft, at nearly twenty-two thousand feet, the mountain is dead shale, thin wind, and frost that sublimes straight into black sky without ever melting. Step four paces inward through the crooked basalt lip, however, and the air thickens like broth. 

The chamber is no larger than a village church, tilted slightly off-plumb inside the throat of a long-choked volcanic parasitic cone. Nothing here has collapsed or shifted in thousands of years. The dark andesite walls have been licked smooth—first by ancient steam, then by centuries of weak carbonic condensation, and finally by the ceaseless, oily rubbing of millions of bodies. Every corner is rounded to the texture of soapstone. Even the floor is not rubble, but a deep, stratified pavement of ancient guano, shed chitin, and windblown dust that has mineralized over millennia into a brown, undulating phosphorite rock, then been polished all over again into shallow bowls and slick chutes.

Light scarcely exists. Seventy feet up, the chimney pinches down to a jagged slot choked with rime ice on its outer edge; a crooked shaft of bruised, high-altitude twilight reaches partway down the eastern wall for a few hours at midday, illuminating floating motes of feather-sheath dust and rising vapor, before failing altogether. Below that dim grey smudge, the darkness is nearly total, broken only by the occasional wet glint of an eyeball catching stray sky-glow or the faint, sourceless pallor of mineral crusts.

Heat rises from hairline fractures in the floor—not the violent heat of active magma, which retreated miles downward ages ago, but a steady, blood-warm exhalation smelling faintly of wet iron, chalk, and ammonia. Because the outside air is starvingly thin and freezing, moisture cannot escape the throat without condensing. Where the warm updraft brushes the cooler upper stone, the ceiling sweats. Big, heavy drops gather on smooth bosses of rock, hang for minutes, and let go, splashing into palm-sized basins worn into the ledges below. The stone is universally damp to the touch, coated in a microscopic, slick biofilm that makes every surface feel vaguely like warm tallow.

Only along that single diagonal smear of twilight does anything green survive, and even there it holds back from the crowd. A few straggling curtains of pale, rubbery spleenwort cling to overhangs where nothing heavy can perch, their fronds thick as boot-leather to survive the ammonia-heavy air. Beneath them, watered by steady ceiling-drips, grow sparse cushions of whitish-green thermal moss and leafless, waxy tubers wedged tight into vertical cracks, extending long, pale aerial roots out into the dark to drink the mist off passing wings.

Everything else is flesh, shell, fur, and feather, packed together at a density that makes the stone itself seem secondary. 

Because the surrounding peaks are an anoxic desert, every warm-blooded or soft-bodied thing within thirty miles of sky and scree is drawn into this single breathable pocket, and because the air even here holds barely half the oxygen of sea level, nothing moves quickly. Life operates at a drugged, patient crawl. Predation happens in slow motion, almost consensually, inches away from animals that do not bother to flinch.

The upper walls, just below the sweating condensation line, are shingled three-deep in heavy, broad-winged birds the size of ravens, with naked, whiskered faces and oiled chestnut plumage. They do not roost side by side so much as stack like roof tiles, breast over back, shifting their hooked feet once an hour with a dry, scaly *scritch*. Beneath and between them, every flutes-width of crevice is stuffed with blunt-snouted, wool-bellied rock rodents wedged hip to jaw in slow-heaving piles, their respiration synchronized into long, collective sighs. Lower down, where the stone warms further, the walls are pebbled with fist-sized, soft-shelled land snails and flat, blind harvestmen whose six-inch legs overlap in tangled, gently pulsing mats like dark thatch. The floor teems with wingless, grease-black beetles and pale pill-bugs as big as plums, plowing through the warm, damp drift of shed down and droppings at the pace of clock hands.

Despite this lethargy, the noise inside the chamber is immense. The domed stone acts as a resonant bell, trapping and multiplying every small friction until the air vibrates against the eardrums. The whiskered birds navigate the dark by tongue-clicking—individually a dry, wooden *tock*, but emitted by ten thousand throats at once, it merges into a continuous, deafening roar like heavy hail on slate. Under that clatter runs a thick bass rumble: the guttural, open-mouthed purring of the packed rodent colonies, the wet, rhythmic *slap* of ceiling drips hitting stone basins, the rasping hiss of thousands of snail radulae scraping biofilm from the rock, and the dry, unending rustle of millions of chitinous legs turning over husks in the dark. There are no sharp shrieks, no sudden flurries of panic—just an unbroken, industrial volume of breathing, clicking, chewing, and digesting.

About twelve feet above the chamber floor, a broad lip of phosphorite juts out into the black air, worn concave like an old threshold. Here, tucked slightly out of the main rain of droppings, sits a small troop of six or seven barrel-chested, nocturnal primates—or something very close to them—adapted to the thin air with immense ribcages, short necks, and sparse, coarse pelage slick with cave moisture. They build nothing; they have no hearth, no shelter walls, no woven bedding. Yet the ledge around their calloused haunches is littered with tools that have been used in this exact dark spot for generations. 

There are heavy cobbles of dense green river-stone—carried up miles of freezing talus from valleys far below—now worn smooth and dimpled at their centers into portable anvils. Beside them lie flaked cores of black vitreous basalt with edges chipped and re-chipped into blunt choppers, alongside splintered vulture-ulnas ground against rough sandstone blocks into narrow marrow-picks and snail-hooks. In the pitch dark, working entirely by touch and memory, one of the broad-chested figures lifts a hammerstone with a slow, deliberate heave, rests a calcified snail shell in the dimple of an anvil between its heels, and brings the stone down. 

*Clack.* 

Even through the continuous roar of ten thousand echolocating birds, the sound of worked stone striking stone cuts cleanly across the room. A twist of a polished bone pick pries the meat loose; the empty shell is pushed slowly off the lip, tumbling into the slow, waiting carpet of beetles below. Nobody hurries. Outside the narrow crack overhead, a glacier inches down the flank of the peak and stars wheel across thin black air; inside, warmth seeps up through ancient cracks, water beads on the ceiling, and the crowded dark turns itself over by millimeters.
