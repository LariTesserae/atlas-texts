It is a room-sized hollow twenty meters beneath the surface crust, sealed off long ago when an upper chimney choked itself with fallen scree and mineral cement. From wall to wall it measures scarcely six paces across, maybe four from the peaty floor to the low, vaulted ceiling, yet the space feels vast to the touch because it is packed nearly solid with cold, blind life.

There is almost no light at all. After an hour inside, dark-adapted eyes begin to register a ghostly, colorless grain in the air—not quite illumination, more the faint visual static emitted where moisture touches the scattered relics embedded in the stone, giving off stray, low-energy photons one by one. Everything else is known through proximity, resistance, and the sharp, clean smell of wet stone, crushed evergreen resin, and freezing humus.

The air sits precisely at the freezing point. Near the upper ceiling, where draughts thinner than a knife blade seep down through packed rubble from the world above, moisture crystallizes into dull, gray hoarfrost that coats the limestone flutes. A meter lower, the frost gives way to liquid damp. Water never flows here; it sweats. It gathers over days on smooth, long-weathered rock ribs—stone rounded and scalloped by some vanished subterranean stream thousands of years dead—until a heavy bead detaches and falls into the thicket below.

That thicket fills the pocket from hip-height down, climbing the walls in thick, shingled tiers. Without sun and without warmth, nothing should grow here, yet the vegetation is extravagantly dense. Stiff, broad fronds overlap like slate tiles, their blades thick as shoe leather and swollen with glycol-rich sap that keeps their cells pliable at zero degrees. Beneath them, cushions of coarse bryophytes swell half a meter deep over coiled, woody stems no thicker than fingers but centuries old. In full light their pigments would look almost black—deep, light-hungry purples and slate greens—though down here color is moot. Because there are no insects, no slugs, no rodents, not even soil mites, every leaf is whole. Nothing has ever been chewed, bored through, or torn. Fronds press against neighboring fronds in immaculate, crowded layers, growing a millimeter a year, dying just as slowly, and compacting underneath into a firm, frigid sponge of undecayed litter held together by white fungal threads.

What feeds the garden is scattered through the chamber with no obvious design: four or five small structures half-swallowed by rock sinter and creeping roots. None is larger than a boundary stone. Near the center of the floor stands a knee-high plinth of dark, non-reflective material that feels neither warm nor cold, because it refuses to exchange heat with anything that touches it. Not a single bead of condensation clings to its surface; crowding leaves bend fractionally away from its faces without making contact, stopped by an invisible, hair-thin boundary of sheer repulsion. Higher up on the east wall, three slender, seamless polyhedra sit fused directly into the limestone—not mortared into carved sockets, but interpenetrating the bedrock at the molecular level, the stone’s ancient fossil grains passing uninterrupted into the smooth, matte facets. Hair-fine filaments, fainter than spider silk and impossibly rigid, span short gaps in the stone crevices before vanishing back into solid rock.

These remnants do not hum with power or glow like machines. Whatever work they perform operates below the threshold of ordinary mechanics, quietly skewing the local rules of energy within this buried pocket. They bleed a steady, non-thermal gradient into the damp substrate—nudging electrons across cell membranes, breaking mineral bonds in the wet stone, feeding the root-mats directly so the flora can build heavy, waxy tissue in pitch-black cold without ever needing a sun.

The room is quiet, though never entirely mute. Without wind or breath or scuttling feet, tiny noises claim the whole space. Every ten or twenty seconds, a drop of supercooled condensation breaks free from the ceiling and strikes a stiff lower leaf with a crisp, hollow *tock*, followed sometimes by a fainter *pat* as it slides off the waxy margin into the moss below. Deeper in the background lies a dry, intermittent ticking—half sound, half pressure against the eardrum—where the embedded filaments adjust some internal phase state, shedding tiny increments of entropy into the surrounding bedrock. Occasionally, near the frosted ceiling, a microscopic film of ice expands inside a weathered seam and lets out a faint, crystalline sigh.

Nothing hurries. A fallen frond takes thirty years for the cold-adapted molds to dismantle into black soil. A calcite collar crept halfway up the central plinth before the water channel shifted dry centuries ago, and there the mineral lip remains, stopped cleanly a tenth of a millimeter shy of the artifact's frictionless skin. The air stays still, heavy with cold damp and the faint, metallic ozone trace of physics held gently out of joint, while the dark leaves press slowly, endlessly against one another in the blind quiet.
