The place is a notched throat of fractured andesite, sixty feet across and perhaps twice that in length, wedged nearly fourteen thousand feet up the spine of an equatorial massif. At either end of this narrow rock corridor, the ground simply ends, dropping away into black, vertical air. There is no sky overhead and no horizon below—only the interior of a fast-moving cloud at midnight, pushed through the gap at gale force by the pressure differential between two unseen valleys.

Because the cleft acts as a funnel, nothing inside it is ever still. The thin, cold air slams through the passage in erratic, booming gusts, tearing laterally, corkscrewing off the walls, dropping into sudden vacuums, and reversing within seconds. 

Despite the altitude and the chill, the notch is choked to the point of claustrophobia with vegetation. Equatorial cloud-forest has climbed into the fault line and packed every vertical fissure and jagged shelf. Gnarled, papery-barked trees grow sideways out of raw stone, their trunks braided together for survival, their crowns flattened and dense. Beneath and through them surges a waist-high, impenetrable tangle of mountain bamboo, stiff leather-leaved shrubs, and giant cushion plants, every branch furred thick with dark bryophytes and trailing lichens. In the pitch dark, this mass of foliage behaves like surf in a narrow sea-cave. Canes whip and clack together; wet, heavy leaves thrash wildly, slapping stone, shredding their own edges against rock teeth that have never been softened by time.

The rock itself is geologically brand-new. Seismic uplift and frost-wedging have cleaved the walls along razor-sharp planes. Where roots have not yet bound the floor, the underfooting is a treacherous pack of fresh angular scree—flakes and blocks of dark grey stone with edges sharp enough to slice leather, shifting and grinding slightly whenever a particularly violent gust loads the trunks of the trees embedded among them. There is no soil in the conventional sense, only black, fibrous root-mat wedged directly into raw quarry-like fractures.

There is no rain, and there are no streams—any liquid water that pooled would either drain instantly through the shattered rock floor or be blown into the abyss. Instead, the moisture here is horizontal. Cloud-vapor strikes the cold leaves and raw stone at forty miles an hour, condensing instantly into slick skins of water. Every surface is clammy, beaded, and weeping, only for the next blast of wind to shear the droplets free and fling them sideways as cold spindrift. 

Directly in the center of this thrashing dark sits a piece of heavy, un-housed industrial machinery. 

Nothing was built here to receive it. There is no concrete pad, no corrugated shed, no leveled roadbed, not even a fence. A massive steel skid—dropped straight down out of the sky by heavy-lift rotorcraft during some rare fifteen-minute window of calm—rests askew on the raw boulders, held from sliding into the void by thick chains and turnbuckles bolted directly into the cliff walls with iron expansion pins. On the skid sits an automated, turbocharged diesel hydraulic unit and a tandem battery of heavy cast-iron roller sheaves. 

Stretched across the length of the notch, passing barely four feet off the jagged ground through a violently self-pruned tunnel in the brush, is a two-inch braided steel haul cable. It belongs to an ore-conveying ropeway spanning miles of impassable peaks, using this natural cleft instead of a constructed tower to crest the ridge. 

The machine is running hard, starved for oxygen in the thin air. Its six cylinders roar at a strained, uneven high idle, the turbocharger whining beneath the deeper, chest-rattling thrum of the hydraulic pumps adjusting tension on the sheaves. The moving steel cable groans, pops, and sings as it rides over the iron rollers, oscillating up and down by several inches as unseen buckets miles down the line catch the wind. Thick black grease flings off the spinning bearings and coats the nearest ferns, which beat tirelessly against the vibrating steel frame—*thwack-thwack-thwack*—stripping themselves down to wet green fibers against the expanded-metal radiator grille.

The darkness is nearly absolute. If there was ever a work lamp mounted to the rig, the wind or a whipping branch shattered it long ago. The only light in the entire gorge comes from two places: a recessed, grease-smeared amber LED inside a waterproof control box, illuminating three inches of wet steel housing, and the faint, dull-cherry heat signature of the exhaust manifold deep inside its cage. Everything else must be understood by touch, smell, and volume. The air reeks of raw chlorophyll, crushed moss, wet pulverized stone, and hot diesel exhaust that gets ripped away before it can linger for half a second.

No animals live in this pocket. No insects can hold station in the draft; no birds roost in branches that whip like landing-gear cables; no small mammals tolerate the unceasing ninety-decibel collision of combustion engine, screaming steel wire, and barreling wind. Life here is strictly botanical, resilient and deaf, clinging to fresh-broken stone in the freezing dark, growing directly over the anchor chains, wrapping tendrils around trembling hydraulic hoses, drinking the cloud as it tears past into the black drop beyond.
