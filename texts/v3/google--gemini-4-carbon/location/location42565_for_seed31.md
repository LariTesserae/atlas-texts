The city occupies a high volcanic shelf, several thousand feet up, where the mountain drops its shoulder into a broad, tilted basin before plunging again into the clouds below. The rock here has not yet learned to soften. Every ledge, cleft, and outcrop is sharp-edged dark basalt and pale, porous tuff, fractured along clean geometric planes as if split yesterday by frost or subterranean heave. There is very little soil in the conventional sense—no deep, ancient loam—only coarse black grit caught in the hollows of the stone.

Yet the shelf is drenched. At this altitude, warm, buoyant air rises continuously from the unseen lowlands and presses against the raw stone face, condensing without ever forming a true storm. Water does not fall in curtains here; it simply arrives, beading on every surface, pooling in the angular depressions of the rock, and sliding in thin, glassy sheets across the tilted flats. Every vertical fracture weeps. The whole broad expanse of the shelf glints under a clear, steady daylight—an open, high-sky brightness that turns the wet black stone into mirrors of pale blue and silver.

Across this wide, terraced basin sits an urban sprawl built entirely without mortar or metal, yet organized on the scale of a true town. Because the ground offers so much room and rises in natural, stepped shelves, the builders had no reason to crowd their work. Plazas fifty paces wide—paved simply by levering flat slabs of native stone into level beds—sit completely empty between clusters of low, square dwellings. Long, raised causeways of fitted basalt run straight for hundreds of yards across shallow, sky-reflecting pools, connecting neighborhoods that sit far enough apart to look like distinct villages, though they clearly belong to a single planned grid.

The architecture relies on weight, friction, and the clean fracture lines of the young rock. Walls are dry-stacked from angular blocks pried directly from the cliff bases with wooden wedges and heavy stone mauls; you can still see the pale, bruised strike-marks along the lintels where hammers of harder greenstone dressed the edges. Roofs are pitched steep and framed with peeled, straight timber dragged up from lower elevations, thatched thickly with bundled marsh-reeds harvested from the standing basins, already turning dark green with slick algae at their eaves.

Water management is the true infrastructure of the place, binding the sparse districts together. Hand-chiseled channels, scarcely four inches deep and sharp-rimmed, cross the wide plazas in strict parallel lines, catching the constant seep from the upper cliffs and guiding it past doorsteps, through sunken courtyards, and down stone spillways to the next terrace below. Where the shelf flattens into broad marshy pans, low retaining walls hold the water in shallow geometric basins, keeping the causeways dry by half a foot. 

Between the buildings and out across the unbuilt expanses of the shelf, plant life grows with vigorous, well-fed lushness wherever roots can find a pocket of wet grit, leaving vast stretches of slick, bare rock entirely clean beside it. Thick cushions of bright emerald moss swell over the edges of the retaining walls. Broad-leafed, rubbery marsh plants—waist-high and glossy—crowd the shallow geometric basins and cluster thickly along the base of every weeping cliff, drinking the warm mist. Clumps of pale-stemmed cane and heavy ferns burst from the cracks in the natural rock pavements, unbothered by frost or drought. The warmth is steady, humid, and mild, the kind of equable mountain-tropics air that feels neither hot in the sun nor chill in the shade.

What defines the basin most completely is its stillness. There are no birds wheeling between the cliffs, no frogs calling from the reed-beds, no goats clattering on the dry-stone stairs, not even water-striders dimpling the sky-colored pools. Nothing grazes the ferns; not a single leaf shows the ragged crescent of an insect bite. And whether the builders have long vacated the settlement or simply rest indoors out of sight, no smoke rises from the thatch, no footsteps scuff the grit, and no voices carry across the water-flats. 

The air sits in warm equilibrium, devoid of wind. The reed plumes stand upright and motionless against the distant drop-off of the plateau. Sound is pared down to its smallest possible mechanics: the faint, glassy *plink* of condensation falling from the tip of a broad leaf into a puddle; the whisper of a millimeter-deep film of water slipping over a sharp basalt lip into a carved stone gutter. Under an even, unchanging afternoon light, the water runs through the channels at the exact rate it gathers on the cliffs—never overflowing the low stone rims, never drying back from the moss—holding the dark, angular town in a bright, warm hush.
