The whole of the place could fit inside a railway roundhouse, or the sunken nave of an unroofed abbey. You can feel the walls not by seeing them, as the dark is nearly absolute, but by the way the cool air turns back on itself after sixty or seventy paces—a bounded, low-lying pocket where cold damp settles and stays. 

Yet within those modest walls, there is almost nothing there. 

It is an urban district—tenements, viaducts, switching yards, overhead cranes, power looms, and row-house chimneys—entirely composed of living plant tissue, and entirely missing its own interiors. There is no soil and no bedrock beneath it, nor any solid mass to the buildings themselves. Everything exists only at its edges, grown in thin, dark-green vascular outlines suspended in a vast, hollow volume of black air. Where a four-story factory wall ought to stand, there is only a crisp, rectangular rigging of wet woody stems no thicker than knitting needles, tracing the mortar-lines of non-existent bricks and the geometry of window frames around panes of pure emptiness. You can thrust an arm right through a load-bearing pier, your sleeve brushing nothing more than two parallel threads of taut, chilled bark, and feel eighty feet of unobstructed void yawning on the other side.

Nothing here is worn down. Nothing has had time to weather, rot, or fray. Every surface possesses the razor-clean, rubbery sharpness of a shoot that split its seed coat ten minutes ago. Cornices unfurl from swollen apical buds at exact ninety-degree angles; stairwell banisters extrude themselves upward through the dark like pale asparagus spears, crisp and turgid, free of dust or scar tissue. It is a town caught in the first embryonic stage of coalescing into matter—an industrial settlement being drafted into empty space by meristem and root-hair before the physics of solidity have arrived to fill in the walls.

Light occurs only as an occasional physiological twitch. Deep in the abyssal hollows where basement dynamos are sketching themselves out of coiled black rootlets, a hairline spark of cold violet electricity will jump across an empty yard from one wet node to another. For a tenth of a second, the flash illuminates a bewildering scaffolding: skeletal streetcars made of ribbed seed-husks sliding along taut phloem cables; hollow outlines of smokestacks releasing coiled, translucent tendrils that mimic the shape of exhaust plumes without dispersing; row upon row of tenement rooms stacked in the dark with no floors, no ceilings, only faint green borders floating in strict perspective. Then the spark dies, and only a dim, sickly phosphor lingers for a moment in the sap-veins, showing beads of cold condensation clinging to the underside of every horizontal stem.

Water never falls as rain here, and never gathers into streams. There is nowhere for a stream to bed. Instead, moisture weeps continuously from stomata along the undersides of living girders and condenses out of the chill air onto cooler, metallic-skinned leaves shaped like flywheels and rivet-heads. When a droplet grows too heavy, it lets go, falling cleanly through four stories of phantom apartments—passing unimpeded through what should be floorboards, kitchen tables, and iron stoves—until it strikes a taut, drum-tight membrane leaf fifty feet below with a single, muted *plip*.

That sparse dripping, along with a pervasive, breathy friction, is nearly all the sound the place makes. For all its industrial machinery, nothing clangs. Across the dark vault, dozens of engines are running at full speed, but because their parts have no bulk, they labor in near-silence. Pistons made of pale, ring-shaped sphincter muscles contract in rhythmic sequence along invisible shafts, pushing pockets of cool mist through cages of green ribs. Great spoked wheels—mere hoops of tough vine tethered to central hubs by spider-fine filaments—spin rapidly above the switching yards, humming no louder than a moth’s wing. Looms shuttle damp pollen-threads back and forth at dizzying speeds across wide gaps of empty air, weaving thin architectural planes that vanish again as soon as they are eaten.

Because they are eaten constantly. Despite the profound emptiness of the volume, the wireframe town is teeming with animals, though they take up scarcely more mass than the buildings they inhabit. 

Some are flat—literally without thickness—sliding along the rectangular geometry of the unformed brick walls like slips of wet black ribbon, grazing on the mucilage that coats newly sprouted architectural angles. When a cold spark jumps in the dynamo-roots below, you see thousands of pale, long-legged climbers no heavier than crane flies racing in dense, silent traffic along the vascular power lines, their bodies translucent sacs pulsing with stolen current. They pause at swollen junction-boxes made of gall-tissue, drink a drop of ionized sap, and dart away before the ribbon-hunters can fold around the corner of an empty beam to peel them off.

Through the vast hollow spaces *inside* the buildings—through the missing bedrooms, the absent boiler-cores, the hollow viaduct piers—flocks of blind, gill-winged swimmers cycle endlessly in tight, silent vortices. They are the size of swifts, boneless and cold-skinned, propelling themselves through the damp air with rapid, undulating ripples that leave faint trails of disturbed mist. Predators hang suspended in the non-existent floors between stories: radial lattices of pale cartilage and sticky nerve-thread stretched across the wireframe doorways like orb-webs, quivering slightly whenever they intercept a swimmer and draw it inward to be dissolved in quiet, rapid spasms.

Where reality breaks down entirely is in how the town’s geometry handles motion and distance. Because the interiors of objects have not yet grown in, space occasionally forgets whether a boundary is meant to keep things out or shorten the way across. A line of soft-shelled, many-legged haulers—creatures resembling segmented velvet thumbs—will march steadily up a vertical stem framing the edge of a lamppost, turn ninety degrees onto a horizontal wire that marks the roofline of a factory forty yards away, and arrive there instantly, having crossed an overlap in perspective rather than the forty yards of cold air between. Inside the skeletal outline of a rotary turbine, water droplets fall *sideways*, drawn toward the centrifugal pull of an iron mass that isn't there yet, beading along vertical stems as though the stems were flat ground. 

Everywhere, processes run hot in pacing and cold to the touch. A tenement gable splits open from a glossy black bud, shoots crisp green rafters into the dark at visible speed—an inch a minute, unfolding tiny, damp dormer-windows with razor-sharp sills—and immediately a swarm of glass-bellied grazers descends out of the hollow air to prune the shoots back, pursued seconds later by silent, whip-tailed things that drop from the overhead crane-vines, snap up the grazers with the soft *clack* of wet beak-plates, and vanish again into the black gulf of an unfinished street. 

Stand still on one of the thicker root-catwalks—three cold, woody cables braided wide enough to hold a boot—and your breath fogs faintly around your face before vanishing into the dark. Below your soles, there is no earth, only forty feet of black space laced with dimly twitching violet veins and silent, cycling flocks. Around you, invisible in the gloom until the next hairline arc of current fires, a hundred hollow rooms and silent, breathless engines continue furiously building and consuming the outlines of a city that has not yet acquired weight.
