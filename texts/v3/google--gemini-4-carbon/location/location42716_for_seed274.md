The place is a sunken pocket in the limestone, perhaps sixty paces across from one weeping wall to the other, low enough in the coastal shelf that the air sits heavy and undisturbed at the bottom of it. Above, the rim of the sinkhole is choked by overhanging ledges of slumped shale and collapsed boulders that leave only narrow, crooked slots to the sky. No direct sun ever reaches the floor. What falls through is a diffuse, pewter-colored twilight, filtered further by a permanent, warm vapor that hangs motionless from wall to wall. 

There are no open spaces here. Every square foot of the basin is crowded with stone that water has spent ten thousand years softening and rearranging. Thick, fluted columns of travertine rise until they crowd against the underside of the ceiling shelves. Between them, tier upon tier of rimstone dams step down toward the center of the hollow, packed tight as roof tiles, each curving lip holding a shallow, warm basin of mineral-heavy water. Where the flowstone hasn't built up, fallen slabs of older rock lean against one another in tight wedges, their edges completely rounded off, smoothed like river cobbles or old soap. To move from one side of the hollow to the other requires turning sideways to slip between slick mineral pillars, stepping from the submerged rim of one stone pan to the next, hands constantly against wet rock.

The warmth comes from below—not a violent volcanic heat, just the steady, bath-warm seepage of a deep aquifer pushing up through fissures in the floor. Because the air is just as warm and trapped beneath the overhanging rock, nothing ever dries. Every surface sweats. Water doesn't rush or cascade here; it creeps in continuous, glassy skins over the swollen curves of the flowstone, pooling in the packed terraces, spilling silently over smooth calcified lips by the millimeter. 

Nothing green grows in the dimness. There are no mosses, no ferns tucked into the crevices, no trailing roots from the world above. Instead, the rock wears thin, slick films of ochre and rust-colored mineral bacteria, and here and there in the tightest, darkest pockets between boulders, clusters of pale, rubbery cup-fungi no bigger than thumbnails cling to wet clay. They look almost indistinguishable from the waxy nodules of cave-pearl and calcite that form right beside them. 

The quiet is thick and close, muffled by the sheer density of the damp stone surfaces. There is no wind, no rustle, no rushing current. Sound consists entirely of small, separated plinks: a heavy drop detaching from a blunt stalactite into a three-inch pool; another answering three seconds later from behind a curtain of brown stone; a faint, hollow *blup* where a bead of warm gas rises through saturated silt in a corner basin. 

Someone has used this place, or uses it still, though rarely enough that the stone has half-claimed everything left behind. Along the lower terraces, shallow channels have been pecked into the soft travertine lips with heavy stone mauls or cold-hammered copper chisels, guiding the warm seepage from three natural basins into a single deeper trough wedged between two leaning pillars. The tool marks are old, softened and glazed over by fresh layers of translucent mineral crust, turning rough grooves into smooth, creamy gutters. 

Tucked onto dryish ledges just above the waterline—places you only notice when your shoulder nearly brushes them—sit heavy, unglazed clay jars with thick rims and rounded bottoms, wedged into natural sockets in the rock so they won't tip. Those sitting slightly too close to a drip have grown pale collars of calcium around their necks, cementing them gently to the shelf. In a narrow, submerged cleft near the center, flat slabs of dark slate have been stacked without mortar to make a knee-high platform, level and tight, just wide enough for one person to crouch on out of the water. Lying beside it in two inches of clear, warm brine is a wooden scoop carved from a single heavy burl, waterlogged black and dense as ironwood, completely unrotted in the mineral-saturated water, resting where a hand set it down. Nearby, wedged into a vertical seam, are three copper pins green with smooth patina, holding nothing now, their protruding ends beaded with condensation.

Life moves so seldom here that the water surfaces remain unbroken glass for hours at a stretch. If you stand entirely still on the slate platform, letting the damp warmth soak through your clothes, you might eventually see a pale, blind amphipod, translucent as a paring of fingernail, walk slowly across the ochre silt at the bottom of a basin. Higher up on the crowding pillars, where the stone bulges outward in damp folds like heavy drapery, a flat, mottled cave-salamander clings upside down—motionless, ribs barely shifting, waiting in the warm, wet dark for days at a time because nothing in this closed room ever hurries, cools, or dries out.

The light does not seem to travel across the walls as the day passes outside; it merely brightens slightly to the color of wet ash, holds steady for hours, and then dims back toward slate gray. The warm water slips over the chiseled lips at the exact pace it held a century ago, laying down another hair's breadth of stone over the clay rims and the sunken slate, keeping everything full, close, and utterly still.
