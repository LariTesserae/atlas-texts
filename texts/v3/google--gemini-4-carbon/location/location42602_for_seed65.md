The air is so thin here that the sky does not pale into blue; it sits directly overhead in a bruised, violet-indigo canopy, and the sun hangs inside it like a white-hot magnesium flare. Light does not diffuse. It strikes the rock with physical weight, casting shadows as crisp and black as spilled ink, leaving nothing in soft focus for twenty miles across the basin.

This is a broad, high-altitude amphitheater—an ancient caldera or uplifted plateau sitting so far above the planetary lowlands that the clouds live entirely beneath its rim. From the perimeter, a pale, motionless sea of stratocumulus stretches away to the horizon, flat as milk, never rising high enough to spill over the lip. Inside the basin, the geometry is immense, scooped-out, and entirely bare. There are no jagged spires or fresh talus cones left here. Eons of wind have planed the pale granite and greyorthoquartzite into long, whale-backed ridges, smooth troughs, and vast, sweeping stone aprons. Every edge has been rubbed away. The rock looks polished, almost greasy in the glare, worn down into a final, settled equilibrium where nothing falls anymore because everything loose was blown off the rim ten thousand years ago.

And then there is the sound.

Because the terrain is shaped like a series of massive, parallel flutes miles wide, and because the high-altitude jet stream pours across this saddle without ever stopping, the entire basin is an acoustic organ locked on a single, sustained chord. It is not the gusting, whistling noise of normal mountain wind. It is a continuous, steady-state roar—a vast, chest-rattling *thrummm* layered beneath a tearing, high-register white noise, like standing fifty feet behind an engine that never throttles down. The air moves fast and laminar across the polished stone, cold and dry until it hits the precise center of the basin, where pressure drops just enough to squeeze moisture directly out of the gale. 

There are no streams, no rainstorms, and no snowpacks—snow would sublimate in the brutal UV before it could drift. Instead, moisture arrives exclusively as horizontal, high-velocity rime and cold condensation. Along the leeward curves of the smooth granite swells, a thin, perpetual film of dampness clings to the rock, dark as oil in the blinding sun. When a shadow crosses one of these wet bands, it flashes instantly into a crust of clear glaze ice; when the shadow moves off, it sweats directly back into cold water, lingering for an hour in shallow, scalloped rock-pans before the wind drinks it again.

Life clings exclusively to these damp lee-stripes, spaced hundreds of yards apart across miles of blinding, empty stone. 

The vegetation grows entirely in two dimensions. There is nothing with a stem, nothing that rises half an inch into the boundary layer of the gale. Tough, rubbery crusts of ochre and charcoal-black lichen map the condensation zones in sweeping contour bands. Tucked into shallow fissures are dense, iron-hard cushions of dark green moss, packed so tightly you could strike them with a stone hammer and bounce off. Every few hundred paces, a single woody runner—a gnarled, flat-pressed shrub no thicker than a thumb—snakes ten feet along a seam in the granite, anchored by root-tendons wedged deep into microscopic cracks, putting out tiny, leathery grey leaves oiled against the glare.

Yet despite the emptiness—despite the vast expanses of naked, roaring stone where nothing stands between one ridge and another three miles off—the rock is crawling with animals.

They do not populate the air; anything that opened a wing above the boundary layer would be torn away over the rim in seconds. Everything lives flat against the lithic skin, thriving in the four-inch pocket of calmer air directly against the sun-warmed stone. In the dazzling light, the grey ridges shimmer with movement. Thousands of broad, plate-backed reptiles—flattened like river cobbles, dark-skinned to drink the solar radiation amidst the forty-degree chill—cling to the granite with suction-padded toes, grazing slowly along the lichen bands. Beneath them, huge flightless beetles with fused, streamlined carapaces scuttle backward and sideways through the wind-shadow of low stone ripples, feeding on wind-borne lowland pollen and dead insects continuously dropped out of the upper atmosphere like manna from the lower world.

Predators move with the same flattened, creeping geometry: pale, long-bodied marsupials or mustelids with ears folded completely shut into furred slits against the deafening roar, flowing over the whale-backed swells like spilled mercury. Even the birds have given up the sky. Squat, heavy-legged grouse-like creatures with stiff, hair-like plumage crouch in flocks of fifty inside the shallow rock-pans, heads pointed uniformly northwest into the gale like weathervanes. None of these creatures make a sound. Calls are useless here; lungs cannot compete with the valley's own voice. They communicate entirely through flash and posture—lizards flipping bright cobalt throats that catch the fierce sunlight like mirrors, birds raising stiff white tail-fans, predators signaling territory by slapping heavy, calloused tails directly against the resonant granite.

At the wide, swept saddles between ridges, there are small, deliberate disturbances in the stone that belong to no weather pattern. 

There are no huts, no stacked windbreaks, no cairns—anything piled loose would eventually chatter apart in the vibration or catch the wind and skate away. Instead, shallow crescent-shaped scoops have been pecked directly into the living granite on the lee sides of the larger swells, deepened just enough for two or three bodies to lie flat below the slipstream. Around these depressions, the stone is scarred with crescent bruising where heavy quartz cobbles have been used to pound dried lizard meat and oily beetle paste directly on the bedrock. 

Left wedged securely into deep thermal cracks—slotted in tightly so the roaring air cannot pluck them free—are heavy, unhafted hand-axes of black basalt brought up from far below the cloud rim, alongside thick bone scrapers ground smooth on the quartzite pavement, and wide, shallow slate platters used to catch and pool the morning condensation off the rime-crusts. Whoever passes through here brings no fire—there is nothing to burn, and no air to feed a flame evenly—and builds nothing vertical. They move through the basin on the terms the basin demands: low, deafened, squinting through slotted bone goggles left tucked beside a grinding slick, harvesting the abundant, sun-baked fat of the boundary-layer herds under a white sun that never seems to warm the air, inside a gale that never rises, never falls, and never stops.
