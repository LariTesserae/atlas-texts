The floor is nine miles below the crust, and the ceiling is four miles above the floor, though neither can be seen together. Between them lies a continent-sized lung of unweathered stone, sealed off from the upper world when the planet’s mantle cooled and shrank away from its shell. There is no sky here, only an overarching vault so high that its stone exerts its own slight, localized pull on the stagnant air beneath it.

The air is dry as kiln-ash and perpetually thirty-four degrees Celsius. Because the heat radiates uniformly from the deep basalt beneath and the massive, insulating stone above, there are no thermal gradients. Without gradients, there is no wind. Without water, there is no rain, no drip, no chemical rounding of the rock. Every fracture looks as though it split ten minutes ago. Stepping across the lowlands means navigating fields of obsidian scree, pristine hexagonal basalt columns sheared at violent angles, and waist-high blades of mica and quartz that retain edges sharp enough to part leather. Dust does not blow here; where rock flour settled during the great cooling millions of years ago, it lies in level, undisturbed drifts like dry grey snow, holding the crisp imprint of anything that crosses it until something else walks over the exact same spot.

Yet the emptiness is crowded. Because the vault is vast and unbroken by valleys or rivers, the sense of space is governed not by distance, but by obstacles. Forests of mineral needles—selenite and dry sulfur crystals—grow not from dripping water, but from ancient, slow outgassing that ceased eons ago, leaving dense thickets of translucent, razor-edged pillars leaning against one another at forty-degree angles. Narrow slots wind between house-sized blocks of fallen ceiling. The space is packed with surfaces, ledges, overhangs, and blind chimneys, layered so tightly that moving fifty yards forward often requires climbing three hundred yards up and down through pitched stone labyrinths. 

Light exists only as an exception, never enough to reveal the horizon. Where tectonic weight squeezes the bases of the giant quartz pillars, a faint, steady piezoelectric glow bleeds into the stone—a dull, static amber that illuminates nothing beyond a few inches of crystal face. The rest of the light walks, crawls, or hangs. 

The darkness is thick with dry life. Without plants or moisture, the ecology feeds at the bottom on lithotrophic crusts—thin, brittle films of grey and rust-colored organisms that digest iron and sulfur directly from the fresh rock faces, growing a millimeter a century. Over these crusts graze thousands of species of desiccated,静-bodied fauna. They are creatures built of chitin, spun silica, and dry electrostatic membranes. Things shaped like flattened, multi-legged looms creep across the basalt, scraping the mineral film with stone-hard radulae. Above them, suspended between the crowded crystal spires, hang vast, motionless curtains of webbing spun by colonial arachnids the size of hounds; the silk is dry and metallic, tuned to vibrate at the slightest touch.

Because water is nonexistent, the animals do not bleed or sweat; they are sealed systems, recycling a few internal drops of heavy, oily solvent from birth to death, passing it directly into their offspring or losing it immediately to scavengers when their shells crack. When they move, they move with agonizing economy to avoid generating waste heat in the already-warm, stagnant air. A ledge forty feet wide might hold two hundred creatures—long-antenned, papery millipedes, blind, velvet-skinned gliders resting flat against the vertical sheer, clusters of brittle, crab-like scavengers—all within inches of one another, nearly touching, entirely still for days at a time. 

Sometimes, a creature flashes a brief, cold-blue bioluminescent lure from the tip of a spine, just bright enough to glint off the sharp edges of three dozen neighboring carapaces and the obsidian blades around them before winking out.

Sound behaves strangely in the dense, windless dark. With so many sharp rock faces and dense crystal thickets, echoes die fast, shattered and absorbed by a million angles, yet small, high-frequency noises carry clearly over fifty paces. The ambient sound of the world is a faint, dry *ticking*. A claw shifting on volcanic glass. A silica shell brushing a mica sheet. The soft, papery rasp of mandibles grinding iron-crust. There is no hum, no rustle of leaves, no rushing air—just a vast, muffled quiet punctuated by small, crisp clicks coming from above, below, and a yard from one’s ear.

Scattered through the mineral thickets are the cairns and shelters. Whoever built them—or still builds them, out in the unmapped dark thousands of miles away—had no wood, no clay that could be wetted, and no fire, for there is nothing to burn in an atmosphere low on free oxygen and devoid of fuel. Instead, they worked by fracture and tension. 

You find their lean-tos wedged into the junctions of giant selenite crystals: flat slabs of slate levered from the floor and stacked with dry-stone precision so exact that a knife blade cannot slip between the courses. Around these low, doorless domes lie flaking floors—shallow depressions carpeted six inches deep in struck obsidian chips, left behind from knapping blades and scrapers in the dark. 

Occasionally, stretching across a crevasse, there is a bridge or a fence woven from the harvested metallic silk of the ceiling-spiders, braided into thick, stiff cables and anchored around basalt horns with crude copper rings beaten cold from native nuggets. On prominent, sharp-edged boulders sit small markers: three shards of milky quartz balanced tip-to-tip, held together only by friction and weight. Because there is no wind, no rain, and no tremor in this dead-calm geology, a balanced stone placed ten thousand years ago stands with the exact same precarious poise as one set down an hour ago. Near the shelters hang chimes made of thin, knapped flint blades suspended on hair-fine wire—silent alarms waiting for a careless shoulder in the dark.

Nothing rots here. Near a slate wall might lie a discarded tool—a heavy basalt maul bound to a femur-like strut of giant chitin with shrunken, glass-hard tendon—resting beside the empty, feather-light husk of a creature killed ages back, every joint and sensory bristle intact. Time has no weather to work with. The warmth holds steady, pressing gently against the skin like black wool; the sharp stones keep their edges; the blind, crowded millions wait inches apart on the rock faces, conserving their oil and listening for the next dry click in the stillness.
