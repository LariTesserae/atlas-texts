The plateau does not hold the ocean in a basin; the ocean merely sits upon the plateau, held together by a weary, ancient gravity and a surface tension that forgot how to break eons ago. 

You are high enough here that the sky above the water—hundreds of feet overhead—is thin, bruised violet, and nearly airless. Yet down here, pressed against the continent-sized tableland of worn grey-rose granite, the water is bathwater-warm, syrupy with dissolved silica, and thick as broth. There are no jagged peaks left on this roof of the world. Billions of years of warm, heavy currents have polished the mountain range down to a rolling expanse of immense, whale-backed swells of stone. Every corner has been rubbed out of existence. Even the sand has been ground past being sand; the floor is coated in a velvet, colloidal silt that behaves like smoke, rising in waist-high, slow-motion storm fronts whenever a thermal current drags across the flats.

Light enters from above not in shafts, but as a dim, sourceless amber bruise. The sheer density of the suspended minerals and the staggering thickness of the water column scatter any harsh glare long before it reaches the bottom. Everything exists in a perpetual, murky twilight, glowing faintly as if lit through oiled parchment. Because of the extreme altitude, the water boils at a much lower temperature; where geothermal vents sigh warm breath through cracks in the ancient bedrock, pockets of water flash instantly into silent, silver-skinned vapor bubbles the size of houses. Rather than rushing to the surface, these immense lenses of warm gas drift sideways through the heavy brine, stretching into flat, mirrored ribbons that catch the dim light and reflect the seafloor back onto itself, so that looking up often shows you the ground ten miles away, inverted and undulating.

Into this warm, crowded twilight grows a tangled, rootless jungle. Because the stone is too slick and ancient to offer purchase, the flora anchors itself to *pressure*. Thick, vascular kelp-vines with translucent, maroon leaves grow downward from the dense thermoclines overhead, their buoyant bladders gripping the invisible boundary where warm brine meets slightly cooler upper water. Below them, sprawling forests of sponge-like bryozoans and fleshy, fern-leafed hydrocorals crowd every square yard of stone and drift-silt, weaving together into massive, suspended mats. You cannot move ten feet without pushing through warm, slick curtains of amber bladder-wrack, mucus-coated fronds, and pale, pulsing tubers that drink dissolved stone directly from the current. It is intensely, claustrophobically lush, layered ten stories deep in the water column, every organism brushing against the next in a constant, slow-motion sway.

Yet for all this frantic movement—the churning thermal rivers, the drifting gas-mirrors, the restless undulation of a billion heavy fronds—the place is terrifyingly quiet. 

Water this dense, and this crowded with soft, mucilaginous plant matter, swallows acoustic energy whole. A massive boulder of salt-crust detaching from an overhang makes no crash, only a soft, felt *thump* against the ribs, followed by the faint, wet whisper of settling silt. The warm, roaring geysers that drive the plateau’s submarine weather systems sound, from twenty paces away, like someone exhaling slowly into a wool sleeve. Sound dies within yards, leaving only the intimate, tiny clicks of mineral accretion and the soft, rhythmic *shhh-shhh* of leathery leaves sliding over smooth granite.

Here and there, wedged into the shallow troughs between the smoothed mountain-roots, are the cairns. 

Nobody knows what built them, or if "built" is even the right word for things so heavily digested by time. There are low, circular walls of stacked basalt spheres—stones worked completely round before they were ever placed—and occasional lonely, five-sided arches of pale bone-lime that lead nowhere. There is no metal, no glass, no wiring, not a single straight edge or hinge. Just hand-sized stones fitted without mortar, now fused into single lumps by millennia of mineral glaze, and massive, shallow bowls carved directly into the granite swells. 

Around these scattered ruins, the rules of the water fray entirely. 

Perhaps the plateau is simply too old, or perhaps it sits too close to the thinness of the upper sky, where the planet’s habits weaken. Within the stone rings, the ocean occasionally forgets to be wet. You can watch a ribbon of dry, dusty highland wind blow straight along the seafloor, parting the heavy kelp without mixing into the surrounding brine, rolling dry pebbles across dry stone for half a mile before the water abruptly remembers its weight and closes over the gap without a splash. Elsewhere, columns of heavy, warm rain fall *through* the ocean—distinct drops of denser, silver-clear water plummeting downward through the amber murk, pattering softly onto the broad leaves of the underwater ferns and pooling in the carved stone bowls like mercury. 

And in the deepest hollows, time pools like cold brine. Silt storms drift into certain depressions and stall, hanging frozen in mid-swirl for weeks while currents rush smoothly around them. Plants growing across the lip of an ancient stone arch will thrash rapidly in a fast, warm eddy on their left side, shedding spores and decaying and regrowing in hours, while their right-side branches hang locked in amber stillness, unmoved for centuries.

Life with eyes or limbs barely dares to exist here. You might travel through thirty miles of dense, swaying kelp-meadows and drifting mirror-bubbles without seeing a single swimming thing. When fauna does appear, it is vast, solitary, and ghost-quiet. A creature like an immense, unspooledbolt of pale silk—fifty feet long, paper-thin, edged with hundreds of tiny, lidless dark eyes—will glide out of a wall of brown frond-forest, riding the boundary between a wet current and a dry wind-ribbon. It does not hunt so much as absorb whatever drifts across its path, rippling through the ancient stone arches without disturbing a grain of silt. Or you might look closely at what appears to be a rounded granite boulder covered in red moss, only to realize it is an impossibly old, shell-less gastropod, moving an inch a year, grazing on the calcified remains of a half-dissolved wall.

Overhead, the distant surface of this high-altitude sea rolls in slow, fifty-foot swells under a dark sky, completely unseen from the floor. Down here, the warm currents keep turning. A silent storm of grey silt sweeps over a half-buried ring of round stones; a forest of maroon fronds leans forty degrees to the east as a thermal river shifts its bed; a bubble of warm, dry air the size of a cathedral detaches from a granite dome and drifts sideways through the trees, carrying the faint, impossible smell of sun-baked dust through the heart of the deep.
