The entire municipality sits inside a shallow, saucer-shaped depression no more than five hundred yards from rim to rim, bounded by low embankments of smooth grey chert that cut off any view of a farther horizon. Overhead, the sky is a bright, shadowless sheet of pearl—ordinary daylight, clear and flat, hanging without glare or movement, as if noon had stalled permanently behind high, thin stratus. The air is cool, steady at the temperature of a cellar, carrying a sharp tang of ozone, wet iron, and warm tallow.

It is unmistakably a town, laid out with civic rigor, yet pared down to an austere minimum of structures separated by vast, open aprons of blue-grey vitrified brick. Wide avenues with granite curbs intersect at right angles, leading from nowhere to nowhere, flanked by widely spaced cast-iron standards whose carbon-arc globes burn even in the pale daylight. Across the whole breadth of the basin stand perhaps nine structures in total: a tall brick powerhouse with unglazed, round-arched windows; three squat cylindrical condenser towers spaced a hundred paces apart; an elevated iron viaduct that describes a neat oval before re-entering its own switching shed; a windowless municipal hall bristling with ceramic insulators; and two open-sided stamping mills. Between them stretch generous, empty plazas, slick with condensation. Nothing green grows anywhere. There is no moss in the mortar joints, no lichen on the chert embankments, no weed prying at the curbstones—not even the memory of soil. Everything mineral has been worn into a state of polished, settled permanence: brick corners rounded like river cobbles, stone steps dished at their centers, iron bollards rubbed to a dark, greasy mirror.

Despite the wide, bare distances between buildings, the basin is deafening and crowded to the point of suffocation with animal life.

Every structure is thickened to twice its architectural bulk by living bodies, while the broad plazas between them remain starkly open save for narrow, unswerving highways of creatures marching from machine to machine. There is no chaos in this multitude; it behaves like plumbing. Across sixty yards of bare, glistening pavement between the powerhouse and the eastern condenser tower runs a yard-wide ribbon of flat,segmented, porcelain-colored arthropods, each the size of a dinner plate, moving three deep and shoulder to shoulder. Two feet to the left of their column, the wet bricks are completely vacant, clean and shining in the cool light. Their thousands of calcified legs strike the pavers in a continuous, rattling roar like gravel poured down a tin chute.

Higher up, the walls of the powerhouse and the girders of the viaduct are upholstered in heavy, pale-skinned vertebrates roughly the shape of pelicans crossed with leather bellows. They have no feathers—only smooth, greased blubber pleated at the throat—and broad, spatulate bills of dark horn. Tens of thousands of them cling tier upon tier along every cornice, window ledge, and truss, packed so tightly their flanks heave as a single undulating wall of putty-colored flesh. Each time the great flywheel inside the powerhouse completes its stroke and vents a rhythmic gout of warm, mineral-rich vapor through the roof louvers, an entire facade of these creatures inflates its throat-sacs and answers with a brassy, percussive *honk* that locks precisely into the mechanical beat.

The noise in the basin never dips and never peaks. Underneath everything sits the heavy, sixty-cycle hum of the transformer bank outside the municipal hall—a vibration felt in the teeth and sternum—layered with the heavy *thud-shhh, thud-shhh* of the two stamping mills. In those open sheds, automated drop-hammers rise on oiled cams and fall onto thick billets of soft grey alloy, flattening them onto a conveyor that feeds them straight back into an induction trough, where they melt, recast into billets, cool under misting nozzles, and return beneath the hammers. Around the warm splash-guards of these troughs cluster mounds of blind, fist-sized gastropods with spiral shells of tarnished iron sulfide, grazing on the metallic scale and the thin, dark paraffin grease that sweats from the bearings.

Moisture comes not from rain—the bright overcast never darkens or breaks—but from the constant collision of machine heat with the chill basin air. Every cast-iron column weeps. Cold droplets gather on the undersides of the viaduct girders, run down into shallow, century-worn channels in the granite plinths, and spill across the plazas in paper-thin films before vanishing into slotted storm grates. In those grates live dense tangles of pale, bristle-gilled eels that strain pulverized shell and shed skin from the runoff, pumping the filtered water back down toward the deep intake sumps.

Overhead, strung between the municipal hall and the mills, heavy copper power lines sag under the weight of still another population: thumb-sized, six-winged flyers with glassy, mica-like carapaces. Whenever the line voltage arcs across the damp ceramic insulators with a sharp *crack*, clouds of these creatures lift in unison, shimmering in the flat light like thrown handfuls of filings, drink the ionized air along the corona, and settle back onto the humming cables in the exact distribution they held three seconds prior.

Walk across one of the wide, damp squares—stepping over a rigid, ankle-high procession of plated scavengers that seamlessly splits around a boot without slowing—and the strangest quality of the town becomes clear. Nothing is accumulating. There are no drifts of guano, no rotting carcasses, no rust flakes gathering at the base of a pillar, no worn gears waiting to fail. When one of the heavy bellows-throated creatures loses its grip on a cornice and drops dead onto the bricks below, a side-eddy immediately detaches from the nearest ground-column; within four minutes, horn, bone, and blubber are pared away to nothing, leaving the damp pavers spotless. The machines pump up deep subterranean brine and mineral tallow, convert it into heat, noise, electricity, and warm mist, and draw the cooled remainder down again; the animals intercept the heat, lick the tallow from the pistons, harvest the sparks, and return their own dissolved minerals to the storm drains. 

From the rim of the grey chert embankment, the whole town can be taken in at a single glance: nine dark, isolated structures standing in wide pools of wet, empty brick, wrapped in seething pale coats of flesh and shell, roaring at the top of its iron and cartilaginous lungs under a mild, unchanging sky.
