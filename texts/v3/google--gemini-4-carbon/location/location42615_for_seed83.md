The air hits the back of the throat first, carrying the flat, greasy taste of tallow, wet copper, and baked brick. There is no sky here, and no earth either. If you reach out in any direction, your knuckles graze something forged, cast, or mortared, though the surfaces have been stewing in their own heat for so long that they have lost the crispness of human manufacture. 

The space is roughly the volume of an upended grain silo or a sunken nave, sealed hundreds of feet beneath an unknown and irrelevant surface. You cannot see the boundaries. Darkness is nearly absolute, broken only by the dull,arterial glow of three resistance coils buried inside a ceramic housing near the floor, and the occasional pinprick of amber from an indicator bulb so crusted in mineral salt it barely bleeds light two inches into the murk. You navigate this place by thermal radiation and the close, pressing geometry of iron.

It is a thicket of dormant and semi-dormant machinery, packed so tightly that moving five feet requires turning sideways, ducking beneath sagging lead-sheathed conduits, and stepping over bundles of cast-iron piping as thick as tree trunks. Everything is close. Huge cylindrical boilers crouch shoulder-to-shoulder with stepped turbine housings and square, riveted ballast tanks. Catwalks of diamond-plate steel weave between them, though many have buckled slowly over the decades, leaning into the flanks of condensers like tired horses. There is no raw rock anywhere. The floor is vitrified tile, heaved and cracked by thermal expansion, overlaid with cast-iron grating. The walls—when you manage to squeeze past the manifold banks to touch them—are curved courses of glazed brick, weeping a thin, warm slime.

The heat is staggering, static, and breathless. It does not blow from a vent; it simply resides in the mass of the metal, radiating outward at well over a hundred degrees. It is an old, settled heat, coming up through the soles of your boots from whatever immense geothermal tap or forgotten district-heating main passes beneath the sub-floor. 

Because of the heat and the sealed masonry, the room runs its own slow, internal weather. There is no standing water, no roaring drain, just a perpetual, heavy sweat. Moisture beads on the cooler return-pipes near the vaulted ceiling, gathering weight over hours until a single fat, tepid drop lets go. It hits a hot manifold thirty feet down with a brief, soft *tiss*, flashing instantly back into vapor, or lands on a cast-iron flange to trace an ancient, ochre groove of rust down to the tiles. In the shallow depressions where the tiled floor has subsided, little scums of warm, mineral-heavy water collect, never more than half an inch deep, filmed over with rainbow oil.

Centuries of this slow-motion distillation have softened the industrial edges into something resembling karst cave formations, though entirely metallic and chemical. Bolts and rivets have swollen into smooth, reddish-black nodules of iron oxide. Brass pressure gauges have wept long, pale-green beards of verdigris down the fronts of steel cabinets, their glass faces crazed and opaque, needles rusted permanently to the pins. Asbestos lagging on the overhead steam lines has sagged and calcified, hardening into pale, bulbous stalactites where the condensation tracks along their undersides. Nothing is collapsing anymore; whatever was going to snap or fall did so generations ago. What remains has slumped into a dense, interlocking equilibrium, fused at the joints by scale and corrosion.

Sound is muffled by the sheer density of metal and the thick, damp air. There is no roar of engines. Instead, there is a deep, sub-audible fifty-cycle hum felt mostly in the teeth and sternum—electricity still trickling through heavy, oil-cooled transformers somewhere behind the brick bulkheads. Over this sits the patient, sparse percussion of the room: the *tick... tick... tick* of cooling or warming flanges shifting by fractions of a millimeter; a slow, wet *glug* inside a vertical sight-glass once every twenty minutes; the sudden, papery whisper of dry rust flaking off a flywheel and sifting down through three tiers of grating.

Yet things live here, clinging to the weak gradients between scorching metal and merely hot brick. 

Where the amber indicator bulbs or the cherry-red heating coils throw a tiny radius of dim, relentless wavelengths, sparse patches of vegetation hold on. They are pale, rubbery things—liverworts the color of dirty wax, and thin, wiry ferns with translucent fronds that look spun from glass wool. They root in the greasy lint and powdered rust packed into the corners of angle-irons, drinking the warm condensation that runs down the conduits. Higher up, entirely in the blind dark, long, sparse filaments of black fungus—tough as bootlaces—drape from pipe to pipe, feeding strictly on the hydrocarbon sweat of ancient lubricants and the decaying gutta-percha insulation of dead wiring bundles.

And if you stand entirely still on a narrow, tilted walkway, letting the sweat run down your ribs until your own heat matches the ambient press of the metal, you begin to hear the scratch of rare, small lives. 

A blind, bleached crustacean—something descended from a terrestrial woodlouse, now the size of a human thumb—picks its way delicately along a copper grounding strap, antennae sweeping the dark in slow, rhythmic arcs. It pauses at the lip of a weeping valve to drink the copper-tainted water, its shell clicking faintly against the brass. Lower down, in the warm, oily puddles between the floor tiles, pale, flattened worms inch from one patch of iron-bacteria slime to the next, taking an entire afternoon to cross the width of a wrench dropped and forgotten on the floor a hundred years ago. Higher in the gloom, almost never encountered except by the shed husks left caught on rough rivets, lives a species of heavy-bodied, flightless cricket with legs span-wide like calipers. It never chirps. In a room this small and packed with hard surfaces, sound travels crookedly and betrays too much; the insects find each other solely by tracking subtle plumes of warmth and ammonia in the dead-still air.

Every few hours, the only large dynamic event occurs. Deep inside a colossal, brick-jacketed cylinder at the center of the chamber, a counterweight reaches the bottom of an unseen, viscous dashpot. A pawl slips. With a heavy, greased *clunk* that shudders briefly through the grating underfoot, a brass lever four feet long rotates three degrees downward. Somewhere behind the walls, a damper shifts. For the next six hours, the air in the western half of the tangle grows four degrees hotter, and the condensation on the eastern return-lines beads slightly faster. The waxy ferns curl their translucent tips inward by a fraction; the pale woodlice migrate six inches down the pipe toward a cooler flange. Then the room settles back into its hot, dark hush, holding its breath while the counterweight begins its invisible, creeping climb back to the top.
