The whole place is no larger than a cistern, suspended inside the uninsulated iron ribs of an atmospheric condenser tower two miles above sea level. There is no rock here, no soil, not even the memory of ground—only stamped steel plating, riveted flange-beams, and braided copper grounding straps, all of it bolted together so recently that the sheared edges of the sheet metal are still razor-sharp and bright, completely untouched by rust. Even the grease packed into the collars of the overhead drive-shafts is fresh and unoxidized, smelling sharply of whale oil and tallow in the freezing air.

It is brutally cold, hovering just half a degree above the freezing point of the heavily mineralized water that saturates the chamber. Because the giant intake turbines directly behind the bulkhead draw thin, high-altitude cloud straight through the cooling matrices, this pocket is perpetually drenched in frigid, condensing runoff. Water beads instantaneously on every pristine bevel and bolt-head, sheeting down the grey-blue mill-scale of the walls, pooling an inch deep in thestamped diamond-plate troughs of the floor before spilling through narrow drainage grates into the abyss below. The air itself is thick enough to choke on—a frigid, aerosolized mist kept suspended by the violent vibration of the walls.

The noise does not rise and fall; it is a solid, architectural thing. Four feet behind the eastern iron plate, a main reduction gear the size of a locomotive wheel turns at a locked, governor-controlled RPM. Its roar enters the chamber not merely through the ears, but up through the soles of the boots and into the teeth—a colossal, unchanging mechanical drone pitched somewhere between a rock-crusher and a gale-force wind screaming through rigging. You cannot hear water dripping here. A dropped wrench hits the flooded steel floor in absolute pantomime silence, swallowed whole by the steady, deafening thrumming of the tower.

Light enters from only two places: a thick, wire-reinforced bullseye lens set into the ceiling, filmed over with grey grease and slush, and the dull, submerged-looking amber glow of three carbon-filament gauge lamps mounted along a manifold pipe. Everything is dim, reduced to cold pewter highlights on wet metal and deep, drenched shadows wedged between closely packed vertical pipes. The space is cramped to the point of claustrophobia—a dense, three-dimensional thicket of pressure-relief valves, parallel brass conduits, structural tie-rods, and overhanging iron brackets that force anyone inside to stoop and turn sideways to pass.

Yet you cannot pass, because every square inch of this freezing, thunderous metal pocket is packed with living flesh.

Clinging to the weeping steel walls, wedged three-deep between the copper conduits, and heaped in slick, shivering mounds around the warmish collars of the gauge-mounts are thousands upon thousands of creatures that look like shell-less, translucent horseshoe crabs crossed with heavy-bodied alpine moths. Some are the size of thumbnails; others are broad as dinner plates. Their pale, cartilaginous undersides work like suction cups against the unrusted iron, drinking the mineral-rich condensate straight off the metal, while stubby, feather-like dorsal gills beat frantically in the icy spray. They are packed so tightly that their overlapping flanks look like pale roof-shingles or layers of wet, breathing mica. 

Mixed among them, threading through the narrow gaps between their bodies, are dense tangles of dark,segmented brittle-worms with hooked copper-colored jaws, grazing on the clear mucus the larger creatures secrete to keep from freezing. Hovering in the few cubic feet of open air between the pipes—somehow holding station against the violent acoustic buffeting of the walls—are tight, spherical swarms of blind, heavy-winged midges, so dense they look like clots of suspended soot in the dim amber instrument light. 

There is almost nothing green. Only along the warm seam directly beneath the three gauge lamps do a few straggling, wire-thin filaments of black-green iron-moss manage to hook themselves into the hairline gaps between newly mated steel plates, growing half an inch long before the grazing worms shear them back to the metal.

What makes the chamber profoundly strange is its utter refusal to shift or progress. Outside, storms may strike the tower or winter may deepen, and below, great engines may labor for some vast unseen industry, yet inside this rib-pocket an exact, eternal deadlock holds. The governor on the unseen turbine never lets the pitch of the deafening roar waver by a single hertz. The thermal bleed from the shaft bearings balances the high-altitude freeze down to the decimal, holding the water forever liquid at thirty-two point five degrees—never icing over, never warming. The iron does not corrode because the mucous secretions of the pale, gill-backed creatures coat the fresh metal in a slick, protective biofilm that preserves the raw factory mill-marks beneath them as if under glass.

Even the overwhelming crush of animals seems locked in a static equilibrium. None appear to hunt, flee, or migrate. When one loses its suction grip and drops into the inch-deep flood on the floor, its spot on the steel is filled instantly by the lateral slide of six neighbors, and the fallen creature simply washes against a drain grate where a knot of brittle-worms disassembles it at the exact rate new larvae emerge from jelly-sacs glued behind the pipe flanges. The soot-dense balls of midges hang in the exact same standing-wave nodes created by the roaring bulkhead, trapped in invisible pockets of acoustic pressure, vibrating furiously just to stay completely still.

Stand wedged in the doorway hatch for an hour, or ten hours, and nothing alters. The three amber filaments burn at unchanging voltage. Cold water sheets down unweathered steel bevels over a living wallpaper of pulsing, pale gills. The thunder shakes the water droplets into standing geometric ripples across the flooded floor-troughs—sharp little diamond grids of frigid water that tremble violently in the dimness, never breaking, never flowing differently, held in place forever by the pitch of the machine.
