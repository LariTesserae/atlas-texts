The chamber is no larger than a village threshing floor, tilted on a thirty-degree cant beneath tons of dark, fractured trachyte. There is no sky here, no weather, and no way out wide enough for anything larger than a flattened hand—only a few hairline chimneys disappearing upward into the rock mass, too narrow and crooked to pass a single ray of surface daylight. 

It is forty degrees Celsius at the floor, forty degrees at the jagged ceiling twelve feet up, and forty degrees inside the stone itself. That warmth does not rise in drafts or cool in corners; the rock surrounds the pocket like the walls of a banked kiln that reached thermal equilibrium ages ago and simply stayed there. There has never been water in this room. No drip has ever rounded a corner, no frost has ever wedged a seam, no silt has ever settled into the hollows. Because nothing flows or blows to dull it, the stone looks as though it split open ten minutes ago. Every ledge is a fresh conchoidal blade; every fallen slab at the bottom of the incline rests edge-to-edge on crisp, uncrushed points, leaving dark triangular voids underneath.

Yet the air is loud enough to feel against the eardrums and the bridge of the nose.

It is not a chaotic racket. It is a single, deafening, multi-tiered chord—a dense mechanical roar made of tens of thousands of dry, rapid impacts and continuous rasps, locked together by the hard acoustics of the small room into a standing pressure field. Stand in the center of the tilted floor and the sound presses against the sternum; shift six inches to the left, into an acoustic node, and one harmonic drops out while a higher, metallic buzz drills straight into the jawbone. Nothing crescendos. Nothing pauses to breathe. The volume is absolute and flat, like the roar of a furnace, though nothing is burning.

What light exists is a byproduct of the noise. Running through the dark trachyte are pale, jagged ribbons of milky quartz and feldspar, stressed by the weight of the overburden. Clinging along these pale seams—spaced out in neat, territorial rows—are thousands of thumb-sized, flat-bodied creatures armored in translucent plates like smoky mica. They have no eyes and no soft parts exposed to the bone-dry air. Anchored by hooked tarsi into microscopic fractures, they hammer their heavy, calcified undersides against the quartz veins at several hundred beats per second. Each impact sends a tiny piezoelectric flash shivering through the crystal lattice. Multiplied by thousands, the veins glow from within in a continuous, dim, bruised violet, edged here and there with dull sodium-amber where impurities stain the mineral. You can see the whole room by it, faintly, as if by the glow of banked iron, without a single shadow ever shifting.

Fed by the ambient geothermal heat and the trace sulfurous gases exhaling imperceptibly through the rock pores, the vegetation grows big, opulent, and utterly dry. 

Because the mineral seams that anchor them are far apart, the plants do not crowd or tangle. Each specimen stands alone on its own sharp plinth or hangs from an overhead cleavage with yards of clear, hot, vibrating air around it—an airy, uncrowded gallery of massive, solitary growths. Without water to give them turgor, they hold their extravagant shapes through spun silica scaffolding and dense, non-evaporating heavy oils that smell faintly of hot camphor and crushed flint. From a narrow crack near the low end of the slope rises a fan of pleated, plum-black fronds six feet across, thick as saddle leather and slick with a nonvolatile grease that catches the dim violet rock-light on its curves. Three paces up the incline, completely isolated on a jutting spire of glassy black stone, hangs a cascade of tarnished-brass ribbons—hundreds of coiled, feathery plumes as lush as tree-ferns, heavy and limp in the windless heat. Nothing wilts, because there is no moisture to lose. Nothing drops leaves for a season that never comes.

Around the bases of these isolated botanical chandeliers, a second tier of fauna works in steady, dry industry. Long, bristle-legged things like spun-copper centipedes cruise the bare trachyte between the plants, grazing on the microscopic wax-spores shaken loose from the fronds by the room’s relentless acoustic vibration. Smaller, spherical mites with shells like tarnished pewter rasp up the shed husks of the quartz-drummers the instant one dies and loses its grip, cycling the dry minerals back into fine volatile dust before a litter layer can ever form. Birth, molting, grazing, and death happen in tiny, continuous increments across the pocket, balanced so tightly that the population never blooms and never crashes. If you looked at this room a century ago, the plum-black fan would have held the exact same curve; the glowing violet seams would have held the exact same brightness; the roaring chord would have held the exact same pitch.

Someone, once, found a way in—perhaps through a wider fissure at the lower corner now pinched shut by a single, long-ago settling of the hill, though even that pinched seam looks sharp and fresh. Whoever they were, they brought no metal and no fire, and they did not stay in large numbers, leaving most of the pocket untouched.

Their traces sit scattered across the bare, inclined stone with plenty of empty rock between them, completely immune to rot. Halfway up the slope stands a waist-high crescent wall, four feet wide, stacked without mortar from flat, razor-edged trachyte flakes. It is not a shelter against wind or rain; step behind its curve and the roaring chord drops by half, deflected off the angled slabs to create a small pocket of bearable quiet just wide enough for one person to crouch inside. A second, smaller baffle sits near the upper wall, half-finished or deliberately low.

On the bare floor inside the larger baffle lie three hand-knapped obsidian blades, a round hammerstone of paler river-cobble brought from somewhere far above, and a shallow saucer ground patiently into a block of porous pumice. Beside the saucer rests a small, open-weave mat plaited from the brassy, oil-heavy ribbons of the hanging plumes; the fibers are still supple, glistening faintly with their native mineral grease, holding the exact crease of the knee or elbow that last pressed them down. No dust coats the obsidian. No mold spots the weave.

Look closely at the rock fins where the violet light glows brightest, and other marks appear. Into seven or eight of the thin, projecting blades of stone, someone pecked neat, V-shaped notches using the river-cobble hammerstone. Into three narrow vertical cracks, knapped flint wedges have been driven hard with a bone drift, pinching the stone tight, altering the tension of the rock and shifting the resonant pitch of those specific ledges by a fraction of a tone. Around the wedged cracks, the mica-shelled drummers cluster more thickly than anywhere else, hammering furiously along the artificially stressed crystal, making those tuned seams burn a slightly brighter violet than the rest of the room.

Whether the builders tuned the walls to harvest that small extra measure of light, to coax a richer oil from the vibrating fronds, or simply to bring the unbearable noise into a harmonic they could endure while sitting behind their stone blinds, the adjustment held. The flint shims have not slipped a hairsbreadth in the dry, unchanging heat. The chord rings on at the exact pitch they left it, filling the sharp dark room to the brim, loud and dim and complete.
