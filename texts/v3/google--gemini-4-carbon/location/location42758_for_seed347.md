Two hundred feet below the surrounding plain, the land has dropped cleanly along a pair of parallel faults, leaving a sunken trough nearly two miles long and half a mile across. The collapse is geologically recent. Nothing about the perimeter cliffs has softened yet: the basalt and dark shale walls meet the sky in raw, saw-toothed cornices, their vertical faces stepped in crisp angular fractures where blocks sheared away along fresh joints. Even the talus aprons banking the base of the cliffs are sharp-edged—flat, ringing plates and jagged prisms that have never known a river’s tumbling or centuries of wind-scour.

Overhead, the opening is wide enough to let in the whole sky. Light falls straight into the basin—even, cool, silver-white daylight, bright enough to catch the wet glint on millions of stone facets at once. Yet down on the floor of the trough, the air sits heavy and cold, hovering just a few degrees above freezing. Cold air drains off the upper plain and pools here, trapped between the sheer walls, holding its chill right through the midday hours.

There is no bare ground anywhere in the basin. From cliff wall to cliff wall, the sunken floor is entirely occupied by a dense, tiered city of unworked stone.

There is not a nail, a hinge, a timber beam, a brick, or a chisel mark in the entire settlement. Whoever or whatever raised these thousands of structures worked solely with the raw cleavage of the fallen shale, lifting flat, sharp-cornered slabs directly from the scree and stacking them dry into corbelled domes, retaining walls, narrow viaducts, stepped alleys, and sunken courts. No mortar binds the courses; no wheels ever rolled down the passages, which are scarcely shoulder-wide and broken every few paces by steep, uneven sills. There are no chimneys, no hearths, no blackened lintels, no shards of pottery or slag. Where spanning was needed, long splinters of raw stone bridge the gaps; where binding was needed, living roots of dwarf willow and creeping birch have been plaited directly into the masonry so that the walls grow tighter and heavier as the wood thickens. It is urbanism stripped to pure geometry and mass—layer upon layer of cellular vaults climbing over one another’s backs until the roof of one chamber forms the paved terrace of the next, packing the whole basin three and four stories deep.

Water owns every level of it. Groundwater severed by the young fault lines bleeds continuously from horizontal seams halfway up the perimeter cliffs, running down the dark rock in thin, glassy sheets rather than plunging in waterfalls. Where that water hits the outer tiers of the stone town, an intricate hydraulic web takes over. Narrow, slate-lined gutters, shallow settling basins, notched spillways, and brimming cisterns thread between every wall and beneath every threshold. The stone never dries. Cold mist condenses out of the saturated air onto every sharp ledge, gathering into drops that fatten and slide silently into the nearest channel. The lowest streets are permanent canals two inches deep over flat-laid flagstones; the upper terraces hold dark, mirror-still pools dammed by turf bunds.

Despite the bone-deep chill, the place is riotously green. It is the cold-temperate lushness of a subpolar rainforest, sheltered from wind by the sunken walls and fed by mineral-rich seepage. Every horizontal slab, cornice, and corbelled dome is buried under six-inch cushions of bright emerald sphagnum, liverworts, and star moss. Hart’s-tongue ferns and brittle bladder-ferns burst in dense fans from every dry-stone chink. Thick mats of watercress, marsh marigold, and golden saxifrage choke the street-gutters, forcing the cold water to braid around their fleshy stems. On the wider rooftops, knee-high thickets of bog myrtle, angelica, and twisted alder crowd right to the brinks, their branches furred with pale green beard-lichens heavy with water droplets. Not an inch of the settlement lies fallow. Where moss stops, lichen begins; where water pools, sedges pack stem to stem; where stone overhangs into shadow, translucent curtains of filming fern hang dripping above the doorways.

And for all its vastness, for all its water and crowded surfaces, the basin is astonishingly quiet.

There is no roar of rapids, because the gradient across the basin floor is gentle and broken into ten thousand tiny steps. Water does not splash here; it wicks through moss, slides down slick slate faces in unbroken laminar skins, and wells up silently from beneath flagstones. Even more than the slow water, it is the vegetation that swallows sound. Acres of wet turf and deep bryophyte carpets lining the vertical alleys act like acoustic baffling, drinking every echo before it can bounce off the sharp rock. When a flake of fresh shale detaches from the young cliff face above, its impact at the edge of the town is reduced to a single dull, wet *clack* that dies within twenty yards.

Inside that hush, an immense population of animals is constantly in motion. Because there are no open plazas or empty stretches of turf, creatures live stacked atop one another in tight vertical neighborhoods. Dark-furred water voles and collared lemmings have riddled the peat-topped roofs with thousands of slick runways, darting from burrow to burrow beneath the fern canopy. Above them, short-eared owls and pale harriers quarter low over the chimneys-that-are-not-chimneys, their broad wings making no sound at all in the damp air before they drop talons-first into the sedge.

Down in the flooded lower passages, sleek river otters and dark mink use the sunken doorways as thoroughfares, slipping effortlessly from flooded cellar to street-canal without raising a ripple, hunting the spotted char and small sculpins that crowd the cold cisterns. Every damp wall niche holds something: black-and-yellow salamanders coiled in the cool dark between shale plates; fat, dark-shelled land snails grazing slowly across wet liverworts; wrens flitting mouse-like through the root-woven eaves without calling. Along the stepped spillways, flocks of snipe and grey phalaropes probe the saturated moss fringes side by side, feeding in rapid, nervous silence, while great grey herons stand motionless on the peaks of the corbelled vaults like finials of weathered slate, waiting for a frog or vole to cross the alley below.

Nothing stays static for long. Thin skins of clear ice form in the north-facing cisterns during the predawn hours, prying subtly at the sharp stone joints, then melt away by midmorning as the sky brightens, releasing slow pulses of meltwater down the terraced gutters. Fog forms in the eastern quarter of the town whenever a draft of slightly warmer plain-air spills over the cliff rim, drifting chest-high down the narrow stone lanes, obscuring the tiered roofs for twenty minutes before condensing into the moss and leaving the air razor-clear again. A fox threads along the spine of an aqueduct wall, scattering a silent explosion of wintering thrushes up into the willow tangles; two tiers below its paws, an otter surfaces in a flooded courtyard with a fish in its jaws, shakes its whiskers in a spray of cold droplets, and vanishes beneath a low lintel. Every doorway leads into another wet, green chamber; every chamber touches four others; and overhead, framed by raw black cliffs that have barely begun to crumble, the bright, pale sky lights every wet leaf and sharp stone edge down to the bottom of the pit.
