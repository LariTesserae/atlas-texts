There is no dirt under your boots, nor has there ever been bedrock within reach of a shovel. What takes the place of a valley is a basin five miles across, packed floor-to-horizon with hand-laid slate, unmortared fieldstone, split timber, and woven ash-wattle. Nothing here grew where it stands, and nothing fell here by chance. Every surface bears the fresh, chalky white scar of an iron wedge or the clean, fibrous tear of a froe. The edges of the stones are dangerously sharp. Wind and water have not had time to round a single corner; the entire basin is in the raw, abrasive state of having just been split, stacked, and wedged into place, and it is still being raised in the dark.

Light does not come from the sky. Above is only a low, suffocating ceiling of dark vapor, the color of wet soot, hanging just high enough to swallow the tops of the tallest timber scaffolds. Illumination is strictly local and weak: shallow clay pans set into wall-niches, burning knots of resinous pine and rendered tallow. They throw quivering, orange pools across twenty feet of wet slate before the blackness swallows the geometry again. Looking out from any rise in the masonry, the middle distance is an incomprehensible depth of black shapes betrayed only by thousands of tiny, trembling grease-flames, clustered thick in some quarters and strung out like frayed rope in others, marking the drop-offs of dry-stone terraces and sunken causeways.

The air is cellar-cool, sitting steadily just above the point where breath turns to white smoke, and saturated with a chill, clinging damp. It rarely rains, yet everything gleams dimly in the tallow-light. Vapor condenses continually on the millions of square yards of freshly cleaved slate, beading on the sharp ridges, gathering into slow trickles that find the gaps between stones, and pooling in shallow, hand-chiseled basins cut into the terrace floors. Walk ten paces through any of the narrow, shoulder-width alleys and your sleeves are heavy with cold moisture wiped from the bulging wattle fences and sweating stone buttresses. 

Because there is no open ground, the density of the place is overwhelming without ever closing over your head. It is an open-air labyrinth built to chest height, head height, two stories, three—an endless, packed crowding of low dry-stone retaining walls, timber A-frames lashed with rawhide, cantilevered slate stairways that end abruptly in mid-air waiting for the next course of stone, great cribs of notched logs filled with loose rubble, and acre upon acre of tightly woven hurdles pinned together with wooden pegs. There is nowhere to stand flat-footed without touching three separate structures. Yet if you climb a rubble-crib, the view falls unobstructed across miles of this bristling, jagged topography, dipping down toward the central trough of the basin and rising up the opposite slopes in tiered, chaotic ranks.

Not a single blade of grass splits the flagstones. There is no moss softening the north faces of the blocks—the stone was broken open too recently for spores to catch, or perhaps spores do not exist in this air. The only non-mineral growth is a pale, blistered crust, somewhere between tallow-drip and cave-lichen, that forms around the rims of the water-catchments, slick as soap under a heel and completely odorless. The timber used in the scaffolds and cribs has been barked and squared with adzes, smelling faintly of turpentine and cold river-mud, entirely dead, brought from somewhere outside the reach of the basin’s dim lamps and jammed into the stone sockets by sheer leverage.

What keeps the darkness from feeling entombed is the noise. The valley is deafening, resonant like the inside of a wooden drum. Stone and hard timber bounce every sound across the dark miles without soil or foliage to drink it up. From every quarter comes the sharp, ringing *clink-clink-clack* of iron chisels scoring slate, followed by the heavy, wooden thud of mauls driving wedges home, and then the gunshot crack of a slab parting along its grain. Beneath that sharp percussion runs a constant, grinding roar: sledges of raw stone being dragged over wooden rollers greased with fat; ropes groaning bitterly through dry-ash pulley blocks; cascades of fresh scree being tipped from wicker baskets down wooden chutes to fill the foundation cribs. And threaded through the labor-din is the sound of the damp itself—thousands of unseen trickles dropping down vertical stone shafts into cisterns far below, echoing up through gaps in the dry-stone floor as a hollow, metallic drumming.

The place shifts while you watch it, though you rarely see the hands responsible. The builders—whatever or whoever they are—do not cluster in crowds, or else the maze hides them well. Instead, the landscape rearranges itself through sheer mechanical momentum in the gloom. Two hundred yards away across a sunken court of stacked flagstones, a forty-foot shear-leg derrick creaks forward against the dark sky, ropes taut, lifting a two-ton lintel six inches at a time until it settles with a bone-shaking *boom* onto twin pillars of stacked slate. Closer by, a woven chute suddenly vomits a torrent of fist-sized chert into a retaining crib, raising a cloud of grey, dry rock-dust that instantly turns to paste in the wet air and settles onto the surrounding walls like wet flour. Sections of older construction—if anything here is older than a season—occasionally settle under the weight of new tiers added above them; a wall bulges, dry stones spit outward like melon seeds, and fifty tons of unmortared masonry slumps into a tighter, denser wedge with a sound like thunder rolling down a gorge, immediately stabilized by its own rough friction.

Life, aside from the unseen labor heaving at the ropes, is sparse and adapted entirely to fresh crevices and grease-smoke. Long-legged, pale-shelled scuttle-things, the size of a splayed hand and flat enough to slip between two courses of unmortared slate, dart out near the tallow pans to eat the black crusts of burned wick and fallen moths. Now and then, perched high on the raw pine crossbeams of an unfinished tower, sits a heavy, slate-colored bird with wide, wet eyes like greased obsidian and hooked claws cut for gripping vertical rock edges rather than branches. When a stone splits nearby, the bird doesn't startle; it merely shifts its weight on the adze-rough wood, drops down into a three-foot gap between two rubble walls after something unseen that clicked in the wet dark, and beats its stiff wings furiously against both walls at once to climb back out into the cold, smoky air. 

You can walk for hours along the narrow tops of retaining walls, stepping over taut hemp guy-lines that hum with tension in the dark, descending rough-cut steps slick with rock-paste into courts packed tight with stacked timber awaiting assembly, climbing out again onto fresh platforms that smell fiercely of struck flint and iron filings. Behind you, someone extinguishes a clay lamp or moves it around a corner; ahead, a fresh spark catches on frayed bark, blowing up into another wavering orange pinprick, lighting six more feet of raw, sharp-edged stone waiting to be laid atop the rest.
