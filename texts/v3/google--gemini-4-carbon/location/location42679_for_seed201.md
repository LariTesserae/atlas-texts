At this height the sky does not look blue so much as overexposed—a taut, radiant white-violet stretched directly across the jagged lip of the basin. There is no middle distance here, and no view outward unless one climbs the sheer walls. The entire landscape is a single sunken cleft split into the crown of a newly risen peak, no wider across than a cathedral nave and perhaps twice as long, walled in by vertical slabs of black andesite that fractured cleanly not many centuries ago. Nothing has been rounded off yet. Every ledge is a fresh chisel-mark; the talus at the base of the walls lies in flat, sharp-edged plates like broken slate roofs, completely clear of soil or dust. Between the walls, the central volume of the basin is startlingly empty—just cold, diamond-clear air so thin it barely diffuses the glare, turning the hollow interior into a dazzling well of raw sunlight.

The air itself stays well below freezing even at noon, biting at exposed skin, yet the stone at the bottom of the cleft is perpetually damp. Along the fault lines that cross the floor, silent ribbons of subterranean warmth seep upward—not hot enough to hiss or boil, merely mild breaths of vapor rising from deep within the young mountain. The moment this damp breath meets the frigid air, it condenses. Every shaded overhang drips slowly; every sunlit boulder wears a slick film of meltwater bordered by a crisp collar of rime ice. 

Out of this combination of bitter cold, constant condensation, and ferocious solar radiation grows a thick, improbable garden. None of it grows tall—the hollow center of the basin remains unobstructed—but across the broken stone floor and up the dampened fissures of the lower walls, the vegetation crowds in lush, tight-packed tiers. There are bolsters of emerald cushion-moss three feet thick that yield like wet sponges before freezing solid at their edges. Rising out of the moss are waist-high thickets of velvet-leaved rosettes, their foliage coated in dense white hairs that trap dead air against the cold and bounce back the excess ultraviolet glare so brightly they look frosted in silver. Strangest are the bladder-sedges clustering along the thermal cracks: swollen, translucent stalks filled with clear, mucilaginous sap that act like living greenhouses, catching the blinding light and glowing from within like blown glass while sheltering tiny, dark green leaves coiled safely inside their own fluid. Parabolic flowers, wide as soup bowls and waxy yellow, pivot noticeably over the course of an hour to keep their centers aimed squarely at the sun, focusing heat onto their dark pistils.

Because the basin is small and its walls are high and jagged, shadows move across the floor like the sweeping hand of a clock. This moving boundary between blinding glare and deep, blue-black shade drives everything inside the cleft at a feverish pace. 

The place is crowded with animals, all of them moving urgently, and almost all of them operating in near-total silence. In air this thin and cold, making noise wastes both oxygen and body heat, and sound does not carry cleanly anyway; a stone dislodged from a ledge hits the scree below with a dry, flat *clack* that dies instantly without an echo. Instead of calling out, the plump, short-eared rock hares that swarm the moss banks communicate by flicking the undersides of their tails, flashing patches of starkly reflective white fur like heliographs across the basin. Dozens of them feed at once along the sunlit edge of the damp cracks, cropping the silver rosettes rapidly before the shadow line catches them. 

Above and among them, motion is constant. Clouds of soot-black alpine moths—dark to drink in every scrap of solar warmth—lift from the parabolic flowers whenever a shadow touches them, drifting in dense, noiseless eddies toward the next lit terrace. Long-bodied, furred lizards with broad, flattened ribs scuttle out from the warm fissures to snap moths from the air, flattening themselves onto sun-struck andesite plates for thirty seconds to charge their blood before darting back into the damp moss. High in the empty air of the cleft, pale grey falcons with soft, owl-fringed primary feathers drop out of the blinding glare on folded wings, making no whistle as they cleave the thin atmosphere, snatching hares or basking lizards from the sharp rock and riding the subtle thermal updrafts back toward notches near the rim. Even a kill produces only the dry rustle of feathers against velvet leaves and the faint click of claws on fresh stone.

Tucked into the margins of this frantic, hushed bowl are the scattered works of people who visit the cleft but clearly cannot live in it for long. None of the structures stand taller than a crouched person, and all of them make use of the sharp, unweathered rock at hand. Along the widest thermal cracks, flat blades of black stone have been stacked without mortar into low, curved windbreaks and small beehive cairns, their interiors lined with dried moss mats to trap the rising damp warmth. Stretched across several weeping overhangs are triangular sheets of scraped, translucent gut-skin anchored into hairline crevices with wedges of carved horn and bone pins; they catch the condensing vapor and funnel the drip down twisted sinew cords into chipped stone bowls set safely in the sun so they will not freeze solid. 

Here and there on high, precarious ledges sit shallow dishes of polished quartz and flakes of volcanic glass propped carefully on three-stone tripods, angled by hand to throw reflected beams of sunlight into dark fissures where dense clusters of medicinal glass-stalks grow. Near the lowest notch in the eastern wall—the only plausible scramble out of the basin—dozens of bleached rib bones from larger lowland beasts have been wedged upright between the shattered rock plates, hung with small, flat discs of hammered native copper and split mica. There is scarcely enough wind inside the deep cleft to knock them together; they hang almost still, turning half an inch left or right in the rising breath of the vents, throwing sudden, silent needles of white light across the silver plants and the darting hares below.
