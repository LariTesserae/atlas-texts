The water here is so cold it refuses to freeze only because of its salt content and the immense, unrelenting velocity of its current. It is an inland sea perched two miles above the planetary baseline, trapped inside a continental caldera whose walls are too far away to ever be seen. 

To look outward in any direction is to look through water so devoid of silt, plankton, or suspended matter that sight fails not from murk, but from sheer geometric distance. Light falls into it cleanly from a pale, cloudless sky above the surface, penetrating hundreds of feet down without scattering. There is no green here, no murk, no drifting snow of organic detritus. The light stays crisp and colorless—a stark, clinical daylight that illuminates an emptiness of staggering proportions.

Beneath thousands of feet of this frictionless, crystal-clear brine lies the floor: fresh basalt and obsidian, spilled out in the geologically recent past and flash-cooled before water ever filled the basin. Because the current moves cleanly across the mid-water and rarely drags rubble along the bottom, the stone looks as if it cracked yesterday. Ridges rise in razor-edged steps; hexagonal columns stand snapped off with zero rounding at their corners; glassy scree slopes down into trenches without a grain of sand or mud to soften the angles. Nothing grows on the rock. There is no kelp, no algae, no cushion of moss—only the occasional papery smudge of a sulfur-eating bacterial film clinging to a fresh fracture, pale grey against the pitch-black glass.

Yet the place is deafening. 

There are no waves on the surface—the water moves as a single, locked mass, a sheet-flow forty miles wide and half a mile deep pouring endlessly from east to west at a steady twelve knots. When this massive, unvarying volume pushes across the raw, sharp-edged mountain ranges beneath it, the topography acts like the reed of an instrument. The jagged basalt ridges sing. It is a continuous, bone-shaking roar pitched somewhere between a blast furnace and a bowed saw, amplified and carried instantaneously across hundreds of miles by the dense, frigid water. The sound does not rise or fall. It has no rhythm, no gusts, no lulls. It is a solid wall of acoustic pressure that vibrates in the teeth and hollows out thought, entirely constant, century after century.

Spaced twenty or thirty miles apart across the black ridges stand the listening posts and turbine-anchors—small, brutalist structures of bolted iron and cast concrete. They are industrial relics built for heavy work, entirely unmanned, bolted directly into the volcanic glass with explosive-driven pitons. Each station is no larger than a silo, fitted with thick flywheel housings and grease-packed gears that harvest torque from the endless current. High-tension cables, thick as a man's thigh and braided from galvanized steel, stretch from one station to the next across the empty miles. Pulled taut by the current, these cables hum at a slightly higher pitch than the roaring rock, shedding minuscule trails of cavitation bubbles that instantly collapse in the frigid high pressure. Near the anchor bases, small yellow inspection lamps still burn behind thick, wire-caged glass—powered by the very turbines they illuminate, casting unnecessary circles of amber onto rock already lit by the cold sun far above. Occasionally, a stamped steel wrench or a section of flanged pipe lies caught in a basalt cleft beneath a tower, completely unrusted in the oxygen-poor, hypersaline chill, looking as clean as the day it was dropped.

Because the environment is so vast and the objects within it so sparse, you can drift for hours in that blindingly clear, roaring void seeing nothing at all—only the black glass miles below, the flat silver ceiling of the surface far above, and the faint, hair-thin line of an industrial cable vanishing into the blue-white distance. 

And then the fauna pass through, moving with the current.

With no plants to graze on, the ecology feeds directly off the physics of the basin. Millions of translucent, hard-shelled creatures—shaped like corrugated iron spades the size of dinner plates—anchor themselves temporarily to the vibrating cables and the sharpest rock ridges, their internal organs engineered to convert the relentless acoustic vibration and kinetic shear directly into metabolic energy through piezoelectric cartilage. When they detach to spawn, they drift in massive, sparse clouds miles long, glittering like broken windshields in the clear daylight, clicking against one another in a chatter wholly swallowed by the deeper roar of the sea. 

Hunting them are larger animals: sleek, pale-flanked swimmers built like wingless fuselages of white muscle and heavy bone, twenty feet long, completely eyeless despite the bright light because the acoustic landscape renders vision redundant. They navigate by reading the shadows cast in the sound-field by the basalt spires and the iron stations. Hundreds of them hang motionless in the twelve-knot current, facing east, barely flicking their stiff, crescent tails to hold position beside a vibrating steel cable, waiting for the drifting clouds of shell-plates to wash into their open mouths. 

Nothing rots here. When one of the great white swimmers dies, the cold brine pickles the flesh instantly; the carcass sinks onto the jagged obsidian floor and remains there, stark white against the black stone, slowly shaved away over decades by the clean, razor-sharp edges of the rock it rests upon while the water thunders overhead without pause, without weather, without season.
