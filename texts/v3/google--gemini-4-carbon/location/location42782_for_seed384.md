The entire ward fits inside a drowned stone quarry no wider than a cathedral’s nave and twice as deep, sealed at the top by four feet of black,ubble-stratified ice. Only where the warm exhaust risers poke upward do narrow wells of open water reach the freezing air above, exhaling thin plumes of vapor that immediately fall back as rime. Everywhere else, the ceiling is a rough, inverted terrain of dark ice-keels hanging down toward the rooftops.

Below that lid, the water is hyper-saline and kept liquid only by its salt—hovering a degree or two below the freezing point of fresh water, heavy as thin oil, and pitch-dark except where the machinery makes its own stingy light.

It was built as a town, or a factory disguised as a town, and there is barely room to pass an open hand between one structure and the next. Four-story tenements of vitrified brick lean inward over alleyways three feet wide, their window frames unglazed, their parlors threaded through with cast-iron penstocks, busbars as thick as thighs, and bundled lead-sheathed cables that drape from eave to eave like wet drapery. Iron catwalks, switch-towers, and overhead gantry rails cross-stitch the narrow shafts between buildings in six or seven overlapping tiers. Everything has been submerged so long that the sharp geometry of construction has softened into a stable, pickled maturity. Brick corners are worn round as river cobbles by slow convection currents. Cast iron has surrendered its outer millimeters to a dense, protective rind of black sulfide and pale calcareous scale that stops further rust. Nothing crumbles anymore; the architecture and the brine have finished negotiating.

Despite the crowding of iron and masonry, the place is dead quiet. There are no turbines, no hammering pistons, no cavitating propellers. Yet everything is working. 

The industry here is galvanic and peristaltic, driven by temperature differentials between deep, lukewarm vents in the quarry floor and the freezing ice-pan overhead. Massive banks of bimetal thermocouples rise through the gutted stairwells of the tenements, generating a steady, low-voltage direct current. Along the flooded streets, endless loops of gutta-percha hose as thick as firemains flex and swallow in slow, rhythmic waves, nudged forward by silent magnetic collars that slide along copper guide-rods. Suspended between the buildings are hundreds of vertical wire harps—cathode frames—onto which the mineral-saturated brine continuously plates pale, feathery dendrites of tin, silver-lead, and crystalline salts. Every twelve minutes, a counterweighted bronze wiper descends along a greased track, shaves the accumulated metallic frost from a harp, and lets it snow silently down intoglazed stoneware hoppers waiting in the courtyard below. 

When the bronze blade meets the frame, it makes no scrape; every moving joint in the basin is cushioned by a millimeter of slick,bacterial mucilage and fine graphite silt. Even a dropped spanner falling through four stories of iron grating would land with no more than a dull, felt-like *thud* muffled within three yards by the dense water.

Light exists only as a byproduct of upkeep. Deep inside glass-brick housings and bell-jars filled with transformer oil, resistive carbon filaments burn at a sullen, cherry-red simmer to keep critical valves from icing over. Elsewhere, submerged vacuum tubes and mercury-arc rectifiers tucked under brick archways cast a bruised, ultraviolet-blue pallor across a few square feet of pebbled masonry before the darkness swallows it. Where a current leaks from a frayed cloth-insulated line, tiny beads of hydrogen fizz upward in a single-file thread, catching the dim red glow like rising sparks until they flatten against the underside of a stone lintel.

All through the drowned rooms and under the arched ceilings of the cellars, those escaped gases have collected over decades into upside-down puddles of stale, metallic air. Looking up while passing beneath a porch roof or an inverted iron vat, one sees a flat, shivering mirror of trapped gas pinned against the ceiling—a mercury-bright ceiling-pond that ripples silently whenever a nearby hose-pump pulses.

Because the water stratifies by salinity and heat, false horizons cut right across the architecture. Halfway down the second-story windows hangs a sharp halocline: below it, the brine is denser, colder, and faintly milky with suspended mineral flour, refracting the dim red heater-lamps into smeared vertical pillars. Looking down an alley is like looking into a flooded cellar inside an already flooded house.

Life holds on at the margins of the circuitry, sparse and opportunistic. On the warm copper collars of the induction coils grow sparse tufts of stiff, rust-colored wire-weed—neither quite kelp nor fungus, stiffened with absorbed iron, reaching three or four inches into the currentless gloom. Along the shadowed mortar joints below the halocline, pale, calcified tube-growths extrude hair-thin feeding nets to catch the drifting metallic snow missed by the hoppers, sorting out the trace sulfur bacteria and dropping the pure tin into neat little conical mounds on the flagstones. 

Larger presences are rare enough that the water holds their wake for minutes after they pass. Once an hour or so, a flat, scaleless fish the color of wet parchment detaches itself from the side of a warm transformer housing—where it has been pressed flat like a wet rag to steal the fractional heat—and undulations carry it seamlessly through an empty tenement bedroom, out a sash frame, and into a pipe gallery. Down in the milky sump at the quarry bottom, blind white isopods the size of shoe-lasts walk deliberately along the cable bundles, grazing on the velvety bacterial film without ever biting through the lead sheathing; the voltage inside keeps them polite.

High up at the top of the basin, where a single copper riser melts a throat twenty inches wide through the ice ceiling, a ribbon of slush shifts gently up and down—an inch up, an inch down—breathing in time with some distant pressure against the ice sheet miles away, the sole reminder that a sky or a wind exists outside the masonry walls. Below it, a counterweight reaches the bottom of its shaft, a magnetic latch releases without a click, and across the dark courtyard, six rows of wipers begin their slow, greased climb back toward the roofs.
