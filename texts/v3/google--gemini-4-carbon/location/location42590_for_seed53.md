The cleft sits just below the false summit of an unnamed grey massif, so high that the sky above it is no longer pale blue, but a taut, bruised violet-indigo. There is no horizon here—only the enclosing walls of the amphitheater and the sheer drop at its single open lip, beyond which lies a solid, blinding floor of cloud tops three thousand feet below. 

The space is roughly the size of an abbey nave, tilted upward toward the sky, and packed tightly with vertical stone. This is a forest of weathered dolomitic limestone, eroded over millennia into thousands of slender hoodoos, fins, and perforated needles. They stand shoulder to shoulder, separated by narrow, winding corridors barely wide enough to turn in. Because of the extreme altitude, the sunlight is unfiltered and ferocious. It pours down into the stone labyrinth with physical weight, bouncing off pale, frost-shattered rock and patches of old snow until the glare inside the maze is nearly painful. Every shadow cast by the stone needles isn't black, but a sharp, luminous cobalt blue, lit by bounce-light from neighboring pillars.

Nothing green has ever grown here. The cold is deep, dry, and metallic, thinning the air until every breath tastes of iron and flint. Yet the place is intensely, restlessly active, driven by a relentless diurnal engine of light and phase-change.

Every afternoon, thick plumes of cloud tear loose from the sea of fog below and race up the mountain’s Invisible thermal chimneys, spilling over the lip of the cleft. For twenty minutes at a time, the blinding light turns into a luminous whiteout—a glowing, freezing mist so bright you cannot look directly into it. As the mist pushes through the dense stone forest, the sub-zero rock snatches the moisture directly out of the air. Rime ice—feathery, delicate, crystalline—grows visibly on the windward edges of the stone fins, extending outward by fractions of an inch per minute against the breeze. 

Then the updraft stalls. The violet sky snaps back into view, and the unfiltered sun hits the newly formed rime. 

Instantly, the labyrinth begins to sweat and tick. In the direct light, the surface temperature of the pale stone spikes just above freezing, even while the air inches away remains bitterly cold. Half the rime sublimates directly back into ghostly wisps of steam that curl off the stone tips; the other half melts into beads of water that run down deep, centuries-old solution flutes carved into the pillars. A drop slides six inches in the blazing sun, passes behind the shadow of an adjacent spire, and freezes solid mid-run, building clear, knobby curtains of ice in the blue shade. 

The sound of this cycle is very quiet, muffled by the thinness of the air, which fails to carry noise properly. There is no roar of wind inside the cleft—the outer ramparts deflect the high-altitude gales upward, leaving the interior weirdly calm, save for slow, breathing drafts. Instead, the space is filled with small, crisp percussions: the faint *tink* of ice lenses popping loose from warming rock, the glassy chime of thin icicles snapping and falling onto scree, the soft hiss of snow-cornices settling.

There are no birds, no insects, no marmots, not even the crusty orange smears of high-alpine lichen. What comes closest to ecology here is purely mineral, though it behaves with an unsettling, slow-motion mimicry of growth. At the base of the stone needles, in shaded hollows where meltwater pools briefly over rich pockets of mineral silt before refreezing each dusk, grow dense colonies of salt-and-ice flowers. Dissolved sulfates and carbonates leached from the limestone are pushed upward through microscopic pores in the ground as the water freezes from the bottom up. They extrude into the freezing air as curling, fibrous ribbons—translucent white petals twisted like sheep’s horns or shaved bone, three or four inches tall. By midday, where stray shafts of sun strike the floor, these mineral blooms collapse with a faint whisper of escaping air, dissolving into damp grey slush, only to re-extrude in entirely new geometries four hours later when the shadows lengthen.

Someone—or some transient succession of people—has worked this cycle for a long time. 

Woven through the dense cluster of rock fins are subtle, scattered constructions built with simple hand tools and immense patience. No mortar has been used, and no timber exists within miles to be carried up. Instead, whoever came here pried flat slabs of frost-split slate and limestone from the talus and wedged them horizontally into the narrow gaps between vertical spires. 

There are perhaps two dozen of these interventions scattered across the small basin. Some are low windbreaks, waist-high dry-stone crescents tucked deep in the maze, their floors paved neatly with flat gravel to insulate against the permafrost. Others are stranger, functional apparatuses built into the stone architecture itself. 

High up between closely spaced needles, cords twisted from pale, coarse animal hair—stiff with ice and frayed by intense ultraviolet light—are stretched taut, rigged with smooth river pebbles brought up from unimaginable depths below to act as counterweights. Slung from these cords are shallow troughs chipped out of softer soapstone, angled precisely to catch the afternoon rime-melt dripping from specific limestone overhangs. 

Beneath these troughs sit rows of small, heavy bowls hammered out of impure, greenish native copper, left behind in the shade. The meltwater drips from the soapstone channels into the copper bowls, carrying the heavy mineral load dissolved from the rock. In the dry, low-pressure air and fierce reflected glare, the water slowly evaporates or sublimates out of the ice, leaving behind layered crusts of pale blue and chalky yellow salts coating the hammered metal. Next to one cluster of bowls lies a smooth horn scraper and three leather pouches pinned down under heavy flat stones so the erratic thermal drafts won't take them; the leather is bleached nearly white by the sun, freeze-dried to the stiffness of wood, waiting for a hand that might return next month, or next summer, or never.

At the very center of the cleft, where five tall limestone fins lean inward until their tips nearly touch like stacked spears, a single sheet of beaten bronze—thin, uneven, green with verdigris—has been wedged high into the gap. When the afternoon mist surges over the lip and the thermal draft threads the center of the stone forest, the bronze sheet shifts slightly in its stone socket. It doesn't ring; the thin air steals the resonance. It makes only a dull, rhythmic *clack* against the limestone, keeping irregular time with the ticking of the falling ice and the silent, blinding sweep of shadows moving across the stone floor.
