The sound hits before the ground is fully under foot—a thick, grinding, multi-tiered roar that vibrates in the sternum and behind the teeth. It has no single source. It is the combined acoustic mass of millions of bodies chewing, scraping, clicking, falling, and beating wings inside a wide, dark basin.

The basin is miles across, a collapsed volcanic caldera or a fresh graben dropped recently enough that its walls have not yet slumped into gentle slopes. You cannot see the far rim, only feel the openness of the air above the canopy, an expanse of warm, restless night that holds no stars—perhaps obscured by a permanent low ceiling of geothermal haze, perhaps simply an overcast sky that never clears. Beneath that open dark, the valley floor is a jagged labyrinth of raw basalt. The rock has not had time to round off or turn to deep soil. Instead, it stands in shattered columns, knife-edged pressure ridges, and sudden, narrow fissures with edges sharp enough to slice leather. 

There is almost no standing water here. No rivers cut the stone; no lakes pool in the depressions. Rain never seems to fall. Instead, moisture comes from beneath. Warm, mineral-heavy steam sighs intermittently from thousands of hairline cracks in the basalt, condensing instantly on every cooler surface. Everything is slick to the touch, coated in a thin, warm sweat of condensation, yet if you dig down beneath the root mats, the shattered rock below is dry and powdery with fresh volcanic ash. 

Despite the lack of soil, the flora has choked the basin in a blind, sprawling frenzy. Because there is almost no light—only the faintest bruised-purple glow from the clouds above, and occasional transient sparks of pale yellow bioluminescence down in the rot—nothing grows green. The lushness is entirely pale, waxy, and structural. Thick cables of ivory andbruised-violet vine bridge the razor-sharp chasms, woven so tightly together you can walk across them ten feet above the stone without knowing a gap is there, until a foot slips through into empty warm air. Enormous, fleshy stalks shaped like fluted organ pipes thrust upward out of dry rock crevices, drinking the steam directly through velvety skins. Broad, rubbery fronds the color of tallow and rusted iron overlap in dense, suffocating tiers, trapping the rising heat and moisture beneath them so the lower three feet of the landscape feel like the inside of a mouth.

Nothing stays still. These plants grow on an accelerated, metabolic clock, fueled by geothermal heat and chemical vents rather than the sun. A vine uncoils visibly over the course of an hour, blindly whipping in slow circles until it strikes a basalt spire and cinches tight. Bloated seed-bladders swell on the sides of pale trunks until their skins stretch translucent, then burst with wet, percussive *thwacks* that sound like pistol shots, flinging sticky tendrils yards away into the gloom. Whole mats of pale canopy suddenly tear loose under their own rapidly accumulated weight, crashing down onto the jagged lava beds below, instantly burying whatever was beneath them and opening a gaping hole into the open, dark air of the valley—a hole that surrounding stalks begin leaning in to close within minutes.

And over, under, and inside every square inch of this pale tangle is the fauna. 

The valley is an engine of flesh. Without light, sight is useless; life navigates here by touch, heat, vibration, and sound. You cannot set a hand against a stalk to steady yourself without brushing three different pulses. Heavy, segmented millipede-things as thick as a forearm unspool across the wet basalt, their hundreds of hooked feet making a dry, continuous *shhhk-shhhk-shhhk* against the stone like coarse sandpaper. Above them, the air is thick with heavy, blind flyers—creatures halfway between moth and bat, with leathery, fringed wings that beat frantically against the warm drafts, colliding constantly with fronds, with rock faces, and with each other, dropping into the brush, scrambling upward, and launching blindly again.

The deafening noise is an evolutionary arms race of echolocation and territorial screaming. Millions of cricket-like stridulators saw their legs together inside hollow tuber-caves, using the plant chambers as amplifiers until the bass drone shakes loose droplets of condensation from the leaves. Arboreal amphibians—pale, eyeless, muscular bags of throat and claw—cling to the swaying vine-bridges and shriek in sharp, metallic bursts to map the shifting geometry of the rapidly growing plants around them. When a bladder-fruit explodes or a basalt ledge cracks from thermal stress, a wave of panic ripples outward for half a mile: a sudden, thrashing tempest of snapping stems, hissing spiracles, and cascading bodies plunging through the layered canopy like hailstones.

Predation is constant, frantic, and clumsy in the dark. Thick, velvet-skinned worms drop on mucus threads from high fronds to snare passing flyers; crab-like scavengers with oversized, asymmetrical crushing claws swarm over fallen canopy mats within seconds, shearing through tough white wood and trapped animals alike with a sound like splintering bone. The smell of the place is overwhelming—a hot, churning soup of wet sulfur, crushed celery, musk, formic acid, and the sweet, rapid fermentation of things rotting almost before they have finished dying.

Amid this overwhelming, blind riot, there are small, deliberate anomalies. 

Here and there, tucked beneath overhanging shelves of fresh, sharp obsidian where the steam-drafts swirl sideways, the chaos has been shoved back by hand. Someone—or something—works this valley with tools. 

You do not see houses, walls, roofs, or cleared paths; the plants grow too fast and the ground heaves too often for any permanent architecture to survive a week. There is nothing built here. Instead, there are signs of opportunistic, transient craft left behind in the dark. Heavy nodules of black volcanic glass have been struck against basalt anvils, leaving crescents of razor-sharp flakes scattered over the damp moss. Tough, fibrous tendrils of the strangler-vines have been stripped of their slime, braided into three-ply cordage, and lashed tightly around swelling bladder-fruits—not to harvest them, perhaps, but to aim their eventual explosion, or to choke them into bursting early to stun the swarm of flyers feeding nearby.

Further along a sharp ridge, six hollow, fluted plant-stalks have been severed cleanly at angles with stone blades and jammed wedged into a steam-fissure. As the geothermal gas blasts up through the cut tubes, they blow like monstrous, discordant whistles—a sustained, screaming chord that cuts directly across the frequency of the clicking amphibians. Within a twenty-foot radius of these whistling pipes, the sonar-guided predators are completely deafened and disoriented; they stay away, leaving a temporary, quivering pocket of relative safety in the dark. Inside that invisible acoustic shelter, piled on bare, sharp stone, lie split carapaces neatly scraped clean of meat with obsidian thumbnails, and bundles of fire-hardened wooden spears tipped with snapped crab-pincers bound in wet gut. 

Whoever made them is already gone, moving continuously through the pitch-black basin ahead of the collapsing vine-mats and shifting vents. Already, pale creepers are sliding over the abandoned obsidian flakes. A thick, eyeless grub is chewing through the bindings of the jammed steam-whistles. One of the pipes slips loose, its note cutting off instantly; the deafening wall of the valley's noise rushes inward a few feet closer, and out in the warm, turbulent dark, claws begin scraping back up the sharp rock.
