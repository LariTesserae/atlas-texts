The whole place fits inside a freshly cracked chime of black basalt, perched high on a ridge where the clouds snag and tear. It is no larger than a great cathedral’s nave, open to a pale, bright sky, and almost entirely hollow. Most of the volume here is just crisp, thin mountain air, smelling sharply of ozone, crushed stems, and wet flint. 

Nothing here has ever been smoothed by time. The walls of the chime rose from the crust recently enough—in the slow calendar of stone—that every ledge and fracture retains a glassy, razor-edged precision. Step carelessly against a wall and the rock will slice leather cleanly. There is no sand, no silt, no softened gravel at the base of the cliffs; there are only angular, freshly calved blocks locking together over an abyssal draft. Yet, despite the total absence of soil, the stone is buried in green. 

Vegetation does not wait for dirt here; it drinks directly from the weeping rock and the relentless highland condensation. Lush, cold-weather flora—thick-leaved bergenia, rubbery silver-green succulents, cascading curtains of clubmoss, and bloated bladderworts—grips the microscopic pores of the raw stone. Everything shines with moisture. Water doesn't pool or run in established creek beds because the rock hasn't worn grooves for it yet. Instead, it sheets down the geometric faces of the basalt in erratic, shivering films, gathering at the sharp tips of leaves before letting go. 

Around the perimeter of this hollow void clings a city, though there is hardly anyone in it, and it encloses mostly empty air. 

It is an urbanism of the sheer wall, built entirely without iron, mortar, or heavy timber. Thousands of narrow terraces, galleries, aqueducts, and tiered habitations have been constructed by wedging split, unweathered slate into the hairline fractures of the cliff, bound together under immense tension with green-hide thongs and braided roots that have kept growing after being tied. Because the builders used primitive hand-tools—antler picks, stone wedges, wooden mallets—they did not carve the mountain; they simply stacked and cantilevered its own sharp debris against itself, then trained the aggressive, fast-maturing vines to calcify around the joints. The result is an intricate, dense ring of streets, switchback stairs, and woven-reed plazas clinging to the perimeter walls like barnacles inside a barrel, leaving the vast central cylinder completely vacant. 

From one side of the town, you look straight across two hundred paces of bright, chilly nothingness to the opposite neighborhood, watching water drip from your neighbor’s eaves down into the misty plummet below.

The light is remarkably clear—the high-altitude sun pours straight into the open top of the chime, illuminating the glistening wet leaves and the wet obsidian glint of the fractured rock. Shadows are hard-edged and cold blue. When a cloud drifts directly through the settlement, the bright day turns briefly into a blinding, luminous pearl, soaking every woven screen and slate roof anew, before passing on and leaving the air sparkling again.

For a place so vacant and slow, the noise is staggering. 

The rock chamber acts as an acoustic horn. Every drop of condensing water falling fifty or a hundred feet onto broad, drum-tight leaves or thin, tensioned slate roofs strikes like a hammer blow. Multiply that by ten thousand constant drips, and the baseline sound of the town is a roaring, percussive clatter—like a crowd applauding inside a stone cistern. Beneath the roar of the drops, the mountain itself is still settling. Every hour or two, the slow tectonic relaxation of the young ridge sends a gunshot *crack* echoing horizontally across the void as a fresh seam splits somewhere in the wall, followed by the ringing, musical cascade of glassy shards bouncing down through the tiered roofs. And when the high wind crosses the jagged lip of the opening overhead, the entire hollow space hums a single, chest-vibrating bass note, like someone blowing across the top of a jug the size of a town.

Movement, by contrast, barely registers. The moisture cycles, the shifting of stone, and the creep of binding-vines happen at a glacial pace. Even the rare wildlife moves as if thickened by the cold. Once in a great while, a pale, plate-backed salamander no bigger than a thumb pulls itself over the razor edge of a stone lintel, taking ten minutes to cross the width of a hand. High up near the rim, a pair of heavy, flightless cliff-cranes with oiled grey feathers pick their way along a woven wooden gutter, stepping slowly, lifting each Scaled foot and holding it poised in the bright air before setting it down. Their calls are lost entirely in the deafening drumroll of the dripping water. 

Down on the cantilevered balconies, rows of unglazed clay pots sit beneath linen drip-catchers, filling drop by drop over days. Stone weights hang on braided vine-ropes from the undersides of walkways, slowly stretching the living fibers into straight structural cables over the course of seasons. Whoever built these dense, layered galleries built them to work alongside the slow rise of the rock and the steady swell of the greenery—wedging a stone in the autumn so the winter ice and spring moss will lock it into a threshold by summer. For now, the plazas stand clean, wet, and empty in the crisp sun, booming loudly to themselves in the middle of the sky.
