At the crown of an exhausted mountain range—worn down so close to its roots that the surrounding peaks look less like mountains than the knuckles of a buried spine—there is a shallow, wind-scoured saddle no wider than a village green. The rock here is an ancient gray gneiss, smoothed over hundreds of millions of years until every fracture, ledge, and talus pile has been planed away. Nothing loose remains. There is no gravel, no scree, no dust. The ground is one continuous, undulating pavement of stone polished to a matte satin.

Rising directly out of this saddle is a settlement, though it possesses not a single right angle, mortar seam, or chisel mark. 

It covers perhaps six acres, clearly organized into avenues, stepped plazas, residential blocks, and elevated causeways, yet the volume of the place is almost entirely empty air. Whatever built these structures—whether pre-tool hands piling mud and river boulders in a vanished epoch before the uplift, or some massive, extinct colonial organism secreting silicate walls—worked so long ago that geology has claimed the architecture and fused it into a single continuous piece with the mountain. Walls have been ground thin as shoulder blades by ice-bearing winds. Doorways have widened into vast, sweeping parabolas. What might once have been dense multi-story blocks are now slender, widely spaced ribs and flying buttresses curving thirty or forty feet into the cold, bright sky, enclosing nothing. You can stand at the southern lip of the town and see clean through to the northern drop-off, looking past dozens of pale stone arches separated by wide, hollow plazas.

The daylight is crisp, direct, and unsparing. At this altitude the air is thin and freezing, carrying the clean, metallic bite of high snowfields even though no snow manages to stick here. 

The noise is tremendous. Because the entire settlement has been eroded into aerodynamic perfection—a labyrinth of smooth venturis, whistling apertures, hollow chimneys, and concave amphitheaters—the constant high-altitude wind turns the town into an immense, deafening pipe organ. There is no shelter from the sound. Deep, chest-rattling bass notes boom continuously from the wider circular courts, overlaid by the shrill, tearing scream of gales threading high window-loops and the rhythmic *whump-whump-whump* of air pockets collapsing in the lee of curved terraces. When the wind shifts just a few degrees on the compass, the entire harmonic chord of the town lurches violently into a new register.

Every twenty or thirty minutes, a bright white scrap of cloud comes tearing upslope out of the unseen valleys below and slams straight through the avenues at street level. For two minutes the crisp sunlight vanishes into blinding, swirling silver, and the temperature drops another notch. 

This passing vapor is what feeds the greenery, which is startlingly thick and lush against the bare pale rock, yet arranged so strictly along wind-shadow contours that it takes up almost none of the town's open space. Every leeward curve of the tall arches, every recessed balcony-scoop and smooth gutter-trench, is upholstered in deep, heavy pelts of cold-loving vegetation. There are pillows of emerald and oxblood moss a foot thick, dense mats of waxy, overlapping rosettes the size of dinner plates, and long, leathery straps of chartreuse lichen-kelp that thrash horizontally in the gusts, slapping loudly against the stone like wet canvas. 

Among the mosses grow clusters of translucent, melon-sized bladders filled with antifreeze mucilage and trapped air. When a cloud-bank rips through the streets, tiny hooked hairs on the mosses comb the moisture instantly out of the mist. Within seconds, the plush green walls are saturated and dripping. Cold water sheets down the smooth stone ribs into ancient, deeply worn street-gutters—infrastructure that either inspired the town's layout or resulted from it—creating sudden, noisy flash-streams that race down the stepped plazas, cascading from level to level with a hard, clattering roar before shooting off the edge of the saddle into thin air. As soon as the cloud passes and the bright sun returns, rime ice flashes white across the shaded sides of the gutters with a sharp *crack*, while the sunlit stone steams briefly and dries to pale gray again.

The plants themselves participate in the din. The bladder-flora inflate during lulls and snap shut when hit by freezing downdrafts, adding sharp, percussive pops like cracking knuckles to the constant wind-roar. Everything moves at high speed—clouds racing, shadows sweeping across the wide empty courts like clock hands, curtains of strap-fronds whipping in unison, meltwater flushing every loose dead leaf or stray spore off the mountain within minutes of its detachment. The town cleans itself perpetually; nothing is allowed to rot or accumulate.

Animal life is scarce in all that cold, booming emptiness, which makes any movement across the open plazas startlingly visible. Occasionally, a pair of long-legged, barrel-chested grazers—creatures about the size of wolves, covered in stiff, greased guard-hairs that part cleanly in the wind—will pick their way along an upper causeway. Their hooves are soft, suctioned pads rather than horn, allowing them to lean sideways at forty-five-degree angles into the gale as they walk silently across the slick gneiss. They do not vocalize; nothing could compete with the masonry's roar anyway. Instead, they communicate by flaring bright, blood-engorged wattles along their backs, flashing crimson signals across three hundred yards of empty court before ducking behind an arch to bite chunks out of the frozen, water-swollen mosses.

High overhead, riding the turbulent standing waves generated by the town's skeletal roofs, a solitary avian predator sometimes hangs motionless in the bright blue air—pale-bellied, broad-winged, waiting half the afternoon for one of the padded grazers to cross an open avenue far from the curving walls, while below it the empty stone town bellows into the cold sun, catches another passing cloud in its green ribs, and flushes silver water down its streets.
