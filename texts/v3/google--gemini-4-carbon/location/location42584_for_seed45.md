There has never been rain here to take the edge off anything. At this height, normally, there would be ice to grind the valleys wide and snowfields to pack the saddles smooth, or at least frost wedging the stone into rounded scree. Instead there is no moisture at all—none in the rock, none in the racing upper air—and the mountains look as if they were broken open ten minutes ago. Every ridge is a freshly snapped blade of dark plagiogranite, basalt, and dry, splintering slate. Where a slope falls away into the basin, it does not curve; it drops along the exact geometric plane of its fracture, stepped and jagged like split charcoal, descending thousands of feet into dry, unlit gulfs before meeting another rising wall miles across the void.

The darkness is nearly complete, lifted only by a crowded, needle-sharp field of stars and a faint, coppery smear permanent along the lowest notch of the western horizon, never quite rising into day. It is enough light only to catch facets. Cleavage planes in the raw feldspar and veins of dark volcanic glass wink briefly along the crests and go out again. There is no soil, not even true dust or rounded sand, because sand requires centuries of tumbling and grinding to lose its corners. Underfoot there is only shard and flake—flat, ringing plates of stone with edges keen enough to slice leather, piled loosely at the sharp angles of repose where they fell.

Despite the extreme altitude, the rock is warm to the touch. Heat rises continuously through the soles of boots and radiates off the sheer cliff faces like the breath of a kiln long after the fire has been drawn. It comes from the violence underneath. This entire massif is still coming into existence, thrusting upward at a pace geology almost never permits, driven by shallow, dry mantle-plumes and relentless crustal compression. Touch a boulder and it hums—a steady, low-frequency tremor running up the wrist bones.

Because the atmosphere at nearly thirty thousand feet is starved and thin, this constant upheaval happens in an uncanny hush. The air lacks the density to carry much sound. A mile across the dark chasm, a whole buttress of newly risen gabbro overbalances and shears away from the mountain; you see it only as a sudden erasure of stars and a slow cascades of brief orange sparks struck stone-on-stone as ten thousand tons of rock pitch downward. Seconds later, all that arrives across the open air is a dry, papery sigh, like flour poured from a sack—while through the bedrock under your feet comes the real impact, a heavy, thudding shudder that makes the loose flakes around your ankles skitter and chime. Even the wind, which tears across the passes in fierce, erratic thermal gusts driven by the hot stone meeting the cold upper sky, makes scarcely more noise than breath through open teeth. You feel it shove hard against your chest while your ears register mostly silence.

Nothing stays put for long. Along the base of the higher scarps runs a pale band two or three hands wide—rock pushed out of the crust within the last few days, not yet oxidized by the thin gases of the sky. Crevices widen with sharp, metallic snaps like pistol shots muffled under blankets. Dry vents along the fault-seams exhale invisible plumes of hot nitrogen and trace sulfur, shimmering against the starfields, rippling the air until the distant peaks seem to writhe. In the wider hanging basins, sudden updrafts spin loose sheets of mica into dark, glittering funnels that dance silently across the benches and scatter when the fault shifts beneath them.

Life exists only right at the seam of the break. There are no plants, no roots, nothing green or vascular, nothing that drinks. Instead, where a boulder splits or a fault-plane drags fresh mineral into the open air, a faint, velvety rust appears within hours. It is a dry, heat-loving crust—something closer to a mineral mold or an archaic dry-lichen—feeding directly on the unspent chemical energy of newly broken iron bonds and the warm gases leaking up through hairline cracks. Along an active shear zone, these colonies form narrow, branching ribbons of dull ochre and soot-violet, hugging the warmth of the fracture. Once a rock face stops moving and cools for a season, its surfaces exhausted and oxidized, the crust dries into brittle black filaments that peel away in the high wind, leaving the stone sterile again. Nothing grazes on it. Nothing crawls between the shards or circles in the thin dark overhead.

Yet someone has worked these heights, or works them still at wide intervals. Spaced far apart along the knife-edge spurs and broad, tilted shelves stand small structures built entirely from the native slate. There is no mortar—mortar needs water—and no metal, wood, or cordage. Instead, flat shards have been wedged together on edge in tight, springing herringbone courses to form low, round windbreaks, dome-like bivouacs the height of a crouching person, and tall, tapered cairns. The masonry has been laid with a peculiar slackness designed for a shuddering world: the stones interlock loosely enough to rattle, slide, and resettle during minor quakes rather than bucking apart. 

Inside several of the low domes, long needles of resonant basalt have been balanced across notched uprights like the beams of scales, poised so finely that they tip and click softly against stone stops whenever the slope beneath them tilts by half a degree. Outside, narrow paths have been cleared merely by flipping the sharpest flakes flat side up, creating faint pale ribbons across the dark talus that run for half a mile between ridges before stopping dead at a six-foot vertical step—sheared clean in half where the mountain jumped upward in the dark and left the rest of the trail hanging out of reach above. Half a mile farther on, barely visible as a black notch against the stars, another cairn leans at an impossible angle on a spire that is taller tonight than it was a month ago, spilling single flat stones off its shoulder one by one into the quiet below.
