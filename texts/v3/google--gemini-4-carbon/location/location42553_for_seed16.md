The entire city fits inside a single blister of the low salt-crust—a collapsed subterranean bubble no wider than four hundred paces across and eighty high, sealed off from whatever lies above by a sagging ceiling of dark, vitrified slag. There has never been a drop of water here. The air is baked to the temperature of a kiln, bone-dry and tasting faintly of copper and roasted dust, yet the heat is so constant and reliable that it acts as a kind of medium all its own.

What fills the pocket is architecture, though it takes up remarkably little of the space. Generations of builders working with nothing more than stone adzes, bone chisels, and dry-pressed ash mortar carved the interior of the chamber into an intricate,three-dimensional lattice. Rather than solid houses or thick walls, the settlement consists of thousands of slender pillars, narrow catwalks, and skeletal, open-ribbed domes stacked from the sunken floor nearly to the roof. Every surface has been sanded down over centuries by dry convection currents. Corners are soft as soap; stair treads are dished into smooth, shallow crescents; mortar lines are recessed, leaving the darker volcanic blocks protruding like knuckles. Because the structures are all frame and no mass, the pocket feels hollow. You can look straight through six or seven terraced neighborhoods at once, seeing right across the dim, shimmering gulf of the chamber to the opposite wall. 

Light enters only through a scattering of pinhole fissures in the low ceiling where the crust above has worn thin, letting down needle-thin shafts of blinding white sun that die halfway to the floor, absorbed by floating motes of red clay. The rest of the illumination comes from the floor itself: wide, shallow trenches cut by hand into the bedrock where dry, superheated mineral gases seep upward, making the stone glow a dull, bruised cherry-red. 

Out of these glowing trenches, and clinging to the spindly stone ribs of the lower city, grows a thick, restless flora that has no use for moisture. These are thermophytes—plants built of flexible silica, fibrous pumice-bark, and thin, metallic-sheened skins that metabolize raw heat and airborne sulfur. Broad, rust-colored fronds the size of woven mats unfurl across the ancient plazas, curving toward the hot floor vents. Vines like braided copper wire spiral up the slender columns, draping from catwalk to catwalk in lush, heavy curtains that sway in the constant updrafts. Without water to make them turgid, their stems are structured like dry reeds and hollow quills, pressurized by warm gas.

Nothing walks these streets. There is no dung, no web, no burrow, not the smallest beetle or mite clicking against the stone. If a dry leaf detaches, nothing comes to eat it; it simply tumbles through the open grid of the empty houses until the rising heat catches it and grinds it to powder against the ceiling.

Yet the cavern is deafening, and nothing stays still. 

The heat rising from the floor trenches hits the cooler, sealed roof and plunges down the outer walls, driving a relentless, rolling wind-engine through the perforated city. The builders designed the streets for it, cutting thousands of angled flues, slots, and resonance chambers into the stone lintels—perhaps once to cool their kilns or power simple wooden looms, now long turned to dry charcoal. Today, the entire settlement plays itself like a vast, low-pitched organ. Deep, throbbing hums roll out of the lower cisterns, overlaid by the breathy hooting of high window-slits. 

Against that constant drone, the dry jungle clatters and snaps. The plants grow and shed at a furious pace in the furnace air. Hollow gourd-like bladders swell with trapped thermal gas among the rafters until their brittle skins split with reports like cracking whips, flinging dry, winged spores into the updrafts. Great silica-fronds expand as the vents surge, creaking like dry leather, scraping rapidly back and forth across the worn stone balustrades with a sound like a thousand whetstones sharpening iron. Every few minutes, a shift in barometric pressure down in the deep crust sends a fresh pulse of hot, invisible gas roaring up through the floor channels. The red glow brightens for ten seconds; every vine in the lower wards stiffens simultaneously with a loud, metallic *shhhk*; a fresh snow of ash-gray pollen boils upward through the empty rooms; and then the sigh subsides, the fronds droop back over the smooth-worn stairs, and the city goes on roaring softly to itself in the dark.
