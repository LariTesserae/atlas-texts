The space is a sunken limestone hollow, roughly the size and proportion of an unroofed cathedral nave, dropped below the surrounding lowland plain. Its walls rise thirty or forty feet on all sides, curving inward slightly near their tops so that the sky is reduced to a long, irregular oval of flat grey. There is no horizon here, only the lip of the rock and the dim, diffused ceiling of a permanent overcast. The light enters without direction, casting no hard shadows, settling evenly into the recesses of the stone like fine dust.

The air at the bottom is warm and completely still. Without wind to stir it, the warmth sits heavy and constant against the skin, holding the mild, mineral humidity of an underground cistern in late summer. 

Everything in the hollow has long since finished falling. Where the rim collapsed ages ago at the narrower northern end, the rubble has slumped into a gentle, settled ramp of rounded boulders and compacted marl. The walls themselves show no fresh fractures or raw edges; water and weak carbonic acid have spent thousands of years softening every joint and bedding plane into smooth, pillowed ledges, shallow flutes, and undercut alcoves. The floor is mostly bare—a wide expanse of pale, packed silt and exposed bedrock worn flat as paving stones, with plenty of empty room between one feature and the next.

Water is present only as a slow exudation. Along the western wall, a dark, glistening band stains the buff-colored limestone where groundwater sweats through a porous seam. It does not flow so much as gather, bead by bead, creeping down the rock face to collect in three shallow, scalloped depressions at the base of the cliff. The water in these pockets is clear and motionless, no deeper than a handspan, rimmed with a dull crust of deposited lime. Every few seconds, a single drop detaches from an overhang above and strikes a pool with a soft, blunt *plink*. In the enclosed quiet of the stone bowl, that small impact is the entire acoustic life of the place. The pools never overflow; whatever water arrives is matched exactly by what seeps back down through the silt or evaporates into the warm, damp air.

Life is thin and widely spaced, stripped down to what can subsist on dim light and wet stone without crowding. Near the weeping seam, a few patches of dark green liverwort adhere tightly to the rock, flat as spilled paint. Twenty paces away, growing out of a cleft in the dry-slumped talus, stands a single wild fig—small, pale-barked, and sparse-leaved, its branches stretched wide and flat to catch the weak overhead light. A handful of hart's-tongue ferns occupy a damp pocket near the center of the floor, and nothing grows around them for yards. When the fig drops a leaf, it lies flat on the pale earth and darkens slowly over months, undisturbed. There are no birds under the overhang, no lizards on the warm stone, no insects skating the surface of the water basins, not even the rustle of beetles in the sparse leaf litter. The silence between water drops is absolute.

At the base of the wet wall, someone once worked with the stone, though so long ago that the boundary between architecture and geology has nearly closed. A low, unmortared wall of flat limestone slabs—quarried or gathered right from the hollow's floor—arcs around the largest of the natural basins, turning it into a rudimentary wellhead or catch-pool. Nearby, four wide steps have been chiseled directly into the bedrock leading up the talus slope, their treads hollowed gently in the centers before the traffic stopped altogether. Halfway across the open floor sits the rectangular footing of a tiny, one-room shelter, reduced now to two courses of dry-laid fieldstone. There is no timber left, no rusted iron, no shards of glazed pottery or glass—only stacked rock of the exact same pale grey-yellow as the cliffs, cushioned at its base by an inch of blown silt, resting in the same unshifting equilibrium as the walls around it.
