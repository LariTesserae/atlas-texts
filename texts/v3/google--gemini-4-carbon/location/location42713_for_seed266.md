The drop does not end so much as it dissolves into an amber haze. Standing on one of the high dolomite shelves, you are looking down four, perhaps five miles into the dry trenches, though the bottom is never visible—only a thickening soup of warm, suspended silicate dust that catches the afternoon light and holds it like tarnished brass. 

There has never been water here, or if there was, its memory has been entirely scrubbed away by the wind. Yet everything is worn smooth. Over millions of years, slow, heavy currents of air—warmed in the unfathomable pressures of the lower trenches and rising steadily up the cliff faces—have acted like a continuous, gentle file. The extreme relief of this continental wall has no jagged peaks, no sharp talus cones. Instead, the rock has been polished into immense, bulging flanks, hollow flutes miles high, and sweeping, bone-dry basins where drifts of fine, talc-like powder gather like snowbanks. Everything is ochre, pale grey, and bruised terracotta, warm to the touch even in the shade. Not a single blade of grass, lichen crust, or root splits the stone. The mineral world is entirely naked, resting in an ancient, settled equilibrium.

Because the air is so thick at the lower altitudes and rises so predictably along the sun-baked walls, the gulf has its own slow weather: vast, invisible columns of thermal lift that never break into storms, just a permanent, gentle upward drift. 

Strung across these multi-mile chasms, anchored into the smooth stone flanks with pitons the size of apartment blocks, is the city—though it is less a single city than a sparse, vertical archipelago of heavy industry. You do not see streets; you see distances. A smelting foundry clings to a curved buttress two miles away, its terraced iron housings rusted not by moisture, but oxidized to a dull, velvety plum by the dry heat and trace sulfur in the dust. Three thousand feet directly below it, suspended in the open gulf on braided steel cables as thick as redwoods, hangs a counterweight station and a cluster of cylindrical tenements. Between these massive installations, there is simply miles of sunlit, dusty air.

The machinery is monumental, scaled to the vastness of the drop, and it operates at the pace of a clock’s hour hand. Great cantilevered derricks extend out over the abyss, lowering perforated dredge-buckets down into the amber haze to scoop up the heavy, metallic silts that settle in the deep thermal eddies. It takes half a day for a bucket to descend, and half a day for the massive, electrified winches to haul it back up. Giant rotary kilns, hundreds of yards long, turn on greased trunnions at one revolution every ten minutes, baking the dredged dust into dense ceramic slag. Everything is powered by the upward draft itself; beneath every suspended ward and cliff-side factory, immense radial turbines—broad-bladed and slow as windmills in a dream—turn lazily in the rising warm air, feeding thick copper busbars bolted directly into the cliff face.

Despite the scale of the ironworks, the landscape is profoundly quiet. Sound does not carry well across such vast, dry voids; it gets swallowed by the sheer volume of empty space. From a high observation gantry, a foundry a mile away makes no noise at all, even as pale yellow sparks cascade silently from its slag-chutes and fall for minutes before winking out in the haze below. Locally, what you hear is a low, continuous, dry sibilance—the whisper of talc-dust sliding over warm iron plating—underpinned by the deep, sub-audible *thrum* of a turbine shaft turning inside the rock beneath your boots. Occasionally, a suspension cable half a mile long adjusts to the afternoon expansion of the metal, letting out a single, resonant *ping* that takes ten seconds to fade.

And everywhere, bridging the empty spaces and crowding the warm iron infrastructure, are the animals.

In a world without plants or water, the ecology feeds on the dust, the static electricity generated by the dry friction of the wind, and the thermal gradients themselves. The cliffs and the machinery are thick with dry-shelled, chitinous life and buoyant, gas-bladdered grazers. 

Along the mile-long suspension cables, thousands of creatures shaped like flattened, segmented shields—some the size of dinner plates, others broad as cartwheels—clamp onto the braided steel. Their shells are dusty matte-grey, lined with fine, copper-colored bristles that comb the rising air for microscopic mineral organics and harvest the static charge bleeding off the uninsulated wires. They move so slowly they appear to be part of the rigging, advancing an inch an hour in long, silent caravans from the cliff-side anchors out toward the mid-air stations.

Out in the open gulf, drifting through the normal, clear daylight between the sparse industrial nodes, are the floaters. They are pale, translucent bags of dry, papery membrane, ribbed with lightweight, porous bone, filled with warm hydrogen refined in their own dry guts. Flocks of hundreds hang suspended in the thermals, rising and sinking gently over the course of an afternoon. They have no eyes, navigating by the acoustic hum of the distant turbines and the heat radiating off the dolomite walls. Long, feathery trailing arms hang fifty feet below their bells, sweeping through the dust-layers, catching the smaller, erratic spark-flies that swarm near the warm exhaust vents of the kilns.

The city and the fauna have existed together for so long that the boundary between architecture and habitat has blurred in the dry, patient heat. Builders have forged smooth, curved deflection hoods over the high-voltage transformers, knowing the heavy static-beetles will pile atop them three feet deep to bask in the electromagnetic hum, forming living, breathing insulation domes that slowly shift and click in the quiet afternoon. When a massive dredge-bucket finally breaks the upper haze, rising slowly toward a receiving dock, hundreds of gaunt, long-legged scissor-crabs—creatures with shells like unglazed porcelain—drop down from the iron trusses onto the rim, picking delicately through the warm, heavy silt for sulfur nodules before the automated tippers slowly, ponderously tilt the load into the chutes.

Nothing hurries here. Shadows lengthen across the bulbous, weathered rock faces over hours, turning the pale ochre stone to deep violet while the suspended factories out in the center of the chasm still catch the full, clear gold of the sun. A freight gondola, big as a warehouse, glides horizontally along a track-cable spanning four miles of nothingness; you can look away, inspect the slow rotation of a cliff-side kiln, watch a flock of pale floaters drift lazily past an iron stanchion, and when you look back, the gondola seems scarcely to have moved, hanging serene and silent in the warm, boundless dust.
