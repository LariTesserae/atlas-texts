The basin is roughly the length of a freight barge and half as wide, hemmed in on all four sides by four-story retaining walls and flush-built utility tenements of dark red brick. There is no street level visible from the floor of it—only the rim of corrugated eaves fifty feet up, crisscrossed by steel pipe-bridges, tension wires, and glass-insulator racks against a flat, bright afternoon sky. Everything within this brick well sits low in the water table, sunken below the surrounding river plain so that water does not merely visit the yard; it presses continuously through the mortar from behind, beads on the undersides of cast-iron lintels, and sheets across the flagged walkways in warm, clear films a quarter-inch deep.

You cannot see the flagstones anymore, nor much of the brick. Warmth and constant mist have turned the entire court into a solid, tiered mass of living tissue grafted directly onto industrial hardware. Thick mats of star moss, liverwort, and creeping fig velvet every vertical surface up to the rooflines, rounding off the ninety-degree corners of pilasters and swallowing window sills until the casements look like deep, glazed burrows set into cliffs of green pelage. Where bolted steel brackets carry hot return pipes along the walls, giant aroids and woody climbers have wedged their root-stocks behind the iron collars. Their aerial roots drop straight down for twenty or thirty feet in dense, reddish curtains, braiding themselves around insulated high-voltage conduits and dipping their pale tips into the drainage channels below. Overhead, broad-leaved canopy creepers span the steel gantries, turning utility catwalks into suspended tunnels of overlapping foliage that drip steadily in the windless air.

The sound fills the enclosure up to the brim, physical and unvarying. At the northern end of the court, river water drops through twin iron penstocks into an enclosed turbine house, and the combined roar of the spillway bypass and the deep, sixty-cycle hum of the generators vibrates through the soles of a boot. It is loud enough to erase footsteps and swallow speech at six paces. Beneath the hydraulic white noise runs a slower mechanical cadence: the heavy, greased rotation of an exposed flywheel beside the grease-trap sump, completing one revolution every four seconds, and the periodic hiss of a pneumatic relief valve venting warm, oil-tinged vapor into a thicket of wild ginger and broad-leaved elephant ears.

Nothing here is abandoned, and nothing is new. The brickwork has settled millimeters into the alluvial clay over decades until its arches hold a slight, permanent sag, locked in place by both iron tie-rods and the wrist-thick stems of strangler figs that have fused across the masonry joints like living buttresses. Cast-iron housings wear ten coats of blistered municipal green paint under patches of velvet algae. Brass pressure gauges, mounted at chest height beside wheel valves, remain legible behind convex glass domes ringed with verdigris, their needles quivering fractionally on steady pressure marks. Someone—never present for long, moving on a schedule measured in months rather than days—still greases the zerk fittings on the sluice gears and clips back the rubbery vine shoots within six inches of the transformer bushings. Beyond those narrow, pruned halos around live porcelain and moving teeth, the vegetation has total license. Ferns sprout from the fibrous packing of leaky flange joints, drinking warm condensate drop by drop. Mats of duckweed and water lettuce pack the slow-moving settling tanks so tightly they look like mown lawns framed in riveted steel, parting only for a hand's breadth where a submerged discharge pipe stirs a lazy, continuous boil.

Daylight enters evenly from above, normal clear light softened only slightly by the rising mist from the cooling louvers before striking wet metal and waxy cuticles. Where it hits unobstructed, leaves shine like lacquered tin and droplets flash along the bellies of horizontal pipes; ten feet lower, under the layered shelves of palm-grass, tree ferns, and cable trays, the light turns cool and olive-tinted, bouncing from wet leaf to wet brick until there are very few sharp shadows anywhere. The air smells heavily of river silt, crushed chlorophyll, hot copper windings, and wet rust—a thick, soup-warm atmosphere around eighty-five degrees that sits motionless in the pocket of the walls.

Despite the overwhelming volume of leaf and root, almost nothing moves on legs or wings. The unceasing low-frequency vibration in the masonry and the faint metallic sheen where bearing oil meets the runoff channels keep amphibians and schooling fish out of the sumps. No birds nest in the shaking steel trusses above the spillway. Life here is overwhelmingly botanical and sessile. If you stand long enough on the grated iron landing—itself cushioned under an inch of tough, fibrous root-felt that yields slightly underfoot like wet cork—you might spot a single banded snail inching across the warm porcelain skirt of a dead insulator, or a small cluster of pale midges hovering in the dead-air lee of a brick buttress. Otherwise, motion belongs entirely to the water sliding over mossy concrete lips, the unhurried turn of the governor shaft, and the imperceptible outward push of cambium tightening its grip around warm iron.
