There is no sky here, only an upper limit where the warm, saturated air grows slightly cooler and turns its moisture back into droplets. In the near-total dark, distance cannot be seen, only inferred from the way the air behaves—a pressure so even, so unbroken by wind or thermal draft, that it could only belong to an interior plain of continental breadth. The basin sits low and flat for thousands of miles, an immense, shallow depression in a young crust that has never known a river or a storm.

Where the bedrock breaks through the biomass, it is razor-sharp. Slabs of freshly faulted basalt and glassy black silicate jut upward at steep angles, their fractured edges crisp enough to slice skin, unblunted by sand, ice, or running water. Nothing here erodes. There is no weather violent enough to grind stone into soil, and so there is no soil at all. Instead, the rock is swallowed directly by the vegetation, which serves as its own ground, its own architecture, and its own bedrock-substitute—a continuous, three-dimensional mat of living and vulcanized plant matter fifty feet deep, stretching from horizon to unseen horizon.

To move through it is to move through the interior of an endless, unbuilt refinery that grew from seed. 

There are no cleared paths, no bolted seams, no symmetry, no sign of a maker’s hand or an intention behind the layout. Yet every cubic meter of the gloom is crowded with heavy, solid-state industry. Thick, columnar stalks rise out of the matted floor, sheathed in naturally secreted, cross-linked rubber as tough and matte-black as conveyor belting. Their bark does not peel or rot; it cures in place into fluted, sharp-ribbed casings that radiate a steady, feverish warmth—about thirty degrees Celsius, never rising, never falling. Inside those casings, pressurized columns of sap and volatile hydrocarbons separate by density, rising and condensing in silent, continuous fractional distillation. 

Higher up, invisible in the dark except where a rare, pale-violet corona discharge clings to a serrated tip, broad canopy-plates spread out like cooling fins. They are metallic to the touch. The roots far below leach iron, copper, zinc, and nickel directly from the sharp, unweathered fractures of the young rock, drawing the dissolved ions upward and plating them out along leaf margins and hanging tendrils. Millions of thin, bare copper-organic filaments dangle from the mid-canopy into shallow, stagnant basins held in the cupped cradles of lower leaves. 

The water in these basins is warm, slick, and mildly acidic. It never flows; it simply brims. Each pool is half-marsh, half-galvanic cell. Anode-roots of porous zinc-wood sit opposite cathode-mats of braided carbonized fiber, steeping in a broth of rainwater and organic electrolytes. Across the vast plain, billions of these wet cells sit wired together through the continuous subterranean root-graft, forming a planetary capacitor bank at permanent full charge. 

The darkness is profound, thick as oil, relieved only at the absolute limit of human vision. Every few hundred yards, where two discordant root-systems nearly touch across a wet rock cleft, a hair-thin arc of blue static bridges the gap and holds there, motionless and silent, casting just enough cold light to reveal a hand’s-breadth of glistening, wet latex bark, a cluster of crystalline salt-deposits with needle-sharp facets, and the mirror-flat skin of a condensation pool. There is no bioluminescence, because nothing here has eyes to be signaled or lured.

There are no insects circling the faint electrical coronas. Nothing swims in the warm basins, nothing burrows in the vulcanized root-mat, nothing calls from the upper tiers. Without animals, there is no predation, no pollination, no grazing, and no decay as another world would know it. Dead fronds do not moulder into humus; they polymerize and mineralize where they hang, hardening into rigid, sharp-edged baffles and structural trusses upon which newer growths layer themselves. The air smells cleanly and heavily of ozone, wet iron, warm pine-tar, acetic acid, and steamed rubber.

The silence is nearly absolute. Because the entire mechanized ecology operates without pistons, gears, or combustion—relying entirely on osmosis, turgor pressure, capillary draw, electrolysis, and thermoelectric gradients—it makes almost no noise at all. If you stand completely still on a wet, rubbery shelf of root, you hear only the slow, unhurried *blip* of condensed distillate dropping from a cooling-frond into a pool below. Beneath that, right at the floor of perception, is a hum—not an acoustic sound in the air, but a faint, continuous vibration in the soles of the feet, the magnetostrictive thrum of massive direct currents equalizing across a thousand miles of metallic root-cable.

Nothing is growing anymore, and nothing is dying. The plain reached its climax state ages ago and locked into a flawless, closed-loop equilibrium. Geothermal heat from the young, fractured crust rises into the roots; the plants convert the heat and mineral salts into voltage and chemical bonds; waste warmth rises as vapor from the open vats, meets the stagnant upper air, condenses onto the sharp metallic fins of the canopy, and drips back down to replenish the cells. There are no seasons, no dawns, no successions. Every drop of water, every ampere of current, and every gram of metal is already where it needs to be, circulating at a crawl through the warm, wet dark forever.
