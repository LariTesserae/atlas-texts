There is nowhere to step back. Your shoulders brush wet wattle on the left and fresh-split cedar on the right, and if you tilt your chin up, your forehead meets a low vault of bundled reeds lashed tight with raw, unbleached gut. The entire world here is the size of a potter’s kiln—perhaps nine paces around if paces meant anything—and every cubic inch of it is crammed to bursting while hanging fifty miles above the curve of an unseen earth.

Through a thumb-sized gap where two green timbers cross without quite touching, the outside shows itself: not mountains, not even cloud-tops, which lie far below like a thin smear of salt at the bottom of an abyss, but the black-violet rind of the upper sky, empty of air and shivering with silent, geometric fractures. Out there, nothing has finished becoming sky yet; colors separate at the edges like oil on water. Yet none of that freezing vacuum enters the gap. Instead, the hole weeps hot water inward, droplets trembling against nothing before breaking over the sill.

Inside, the heat is the heat of a closed mouth, hovering just below scalding. Because the altitude makes a wreck of atmospheric pressure—and because the rules holding matter together have come unpinned—water boils and condenses at the exact same moment. Every surface sweats thick, warm beads that hiss into cold-looking steam, hit the low ceiling of woven sedge, and rain immediately back down as heavy grease-warm drops. The air is dim, lit only by a dull, sourceless amber glow trapped inside the resinous sap bleeding from the wall-beams, leaving the cramped corners pooled in thick brown shadow—except the shadows point toward the glowing sap rather than away from it.

Nothing here is natural, and nothing here has ever known a single minute of age. There is no bedrock, no soil, no underlying cliff holding this pocket aloft. The floor beneath your ankles is six layers of hand-beaten red terracotta tiles wedged over cross-hatched bamboo poles, and the clay is still soft enough to take the whorl of a thumbprint—in fact, it is covered in thousands of sharp, Crisp thumbprints whose tiny ridges have not lost a single grain to wear. No dust exists. No moss blurs a seam. Where flint adzes hacked the cedar ribs into shape, the pale wood fibers stand out clean and razor-sharp, smelling fiercely of turpentine, wet as the second the blade lifted. Strips of hammered native copper—beaten uneven with river stones—bind the corner posts, bright salmon-pink without a speck of green verdigris. Erosion has not started here; time hasn't gripped the surfaces yet. If a heavy drop of water strikes soft mud daubed into the wall, it doesn't pit the mud; the mud swells upward to meet the drop, forming a crisp, raw lip around the water.

The noise inside so small a hollow is percussive and relentless. Outside the thin mud-and-reed shell, stratospheric gales shear past with a hollow, tearing roar that vibrates right through the soles of your feet, though the basketwork never sways. Inside, everything clicks, groans, slaps, and boils. Green bamboo joints pop like pistol shots as they swell in the steam. Tightly knotted sinew cords sing like overtuned bowstrings, tightening around timbers that are still actively thickening.

Crammed into every gap between the scaffolding, vegetation crowds the humid dark. There is no dirt for it; broad, meaty, heart-shaped leaves erupt directly from the knots in the hemp rope, and fat, translucent roots braid themselves seamlessly into the woven floor mats until it is impossible to tell whether the basketry sprouted the vines or the vines grew already woven into herringbone twill. Pale ferns unfurl at a visible crawl, their fiddleheads snapping open with wet, leathery *thwacks*, crowding against your ribs, slicking your skin with warm condensation. When a frond grows too wide for the cramped room, it doesn't bend against the clay wall—it passes halfway through its own stem, occupying the same space twice, darkening to a double-exposed bruise of green where the two halves overlap.

The geometry of the pocket refuses to settle. Reach out to brace yourself against a wall three feet away, and your elbow locks straight before your fingertips touch the wet daub; pull your hand back and lean sideways, and that very same smear of red clay presses hot against your ear. Four walls meet at five corners. A gourd lashed to a rafter spills warm water upward along the grain of a wooden peg, where the trickle pools on the ceiling and drops down again into the very gourd it just left, slapping rhythmically against itself. Reality here is wet mortar laid too fast at too dizzying a height, slumping before it sets.

In the dense tangle of sweating tubers and copper-bound joints, movement flickers—rare, sudden, and wrong. Something the length of a human finger, armored in pale, soft plates like a boiled woodlouse, clings upside down to an obsidian wedge driven into a beam. There are perhaps three of them in the whole roaring chamber. When one moves to drink a boiling bead of sap-lit water, it leaves a smeared trail of yesterday's posture hanging behind it in the steam for three heartbeats—sixteen legs tapering back into four—before its front end snaps cleanly to a broad leaf a foot away without crossing the space between. Lower down, wedged between hot mud bricks that are actively extruding fresh clay from their own seams to crowd the floor tighter, a single blind, throat-pulsing thing like a skinless frog swallows steam, its wet croak emerging not from its mouth, but from inside the hollow bamboo pole two feet above its head.

Still the room builds itself tighter. Fresh splinters of bone-white wood push up between the woven reeds to wedge gaps that weren't there a breath ago; new coils of wet clay roll autonomously along the rims of half-finished jars crammed into the wall-niches, pinched flat by invisible thumbs; vines thicken, ropes tighten, steam hisses louder in the dim orange murk, everything packing denser and hotter into its tiny cradle at the torn roof of the sky.
