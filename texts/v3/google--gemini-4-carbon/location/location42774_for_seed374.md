The sound arrives before anything can be seen clearly through the vapor—a flat, unvarying, physical pressure that sits against the eardrums and vibrates in the teeth. It has two parts that have fused into a single wall of noise. Underneath is the low, heavy roar of water dropping over a sheer fault-line along the northern rim of the basin, millions of gallons an hour striking raw stone without pause. Packed on top of that bass note is the piercing, metallic din of living throats and carapaces: an unbroken sheet of croaking, clicking, whistling, and stridulation rising from every wet ledge and cistern across three miles of terraced rock. Nobody here speaks at an ordinary pitch. Nobody hears silence, ever, from birth to death.

The air is warm—skin-temperature, thick with condensation—and hangs under a low, seamless ceiling of slate-colored cloud that never breaks and never darkens into storm. What light filters through is bruised and shadowless, the dim pewter glow of a late dusk held permanently in place. Moisture beads on every surface. It runs in thin, tepid films down black stone walls, pools in shallow geometric basins, and rises again as faint breath-like vapor where warm subterranean seepages meet the air.

Nothing green grows anywhere in the depression. There is not a blade of grass, not a shrub, not a scrap of moss softening a crevice, not a splinter of wood in the entire valley. The ground is too young, too mineral-heavy, and entirely untouched by soil. Where the crust split and settled into this low basin, the black basalt broke into crisp, angular shelves and hex-columns whose edges are still razor-true, unrounded by wind or rain. Instead of wearing the rock down, the water here builds it up. Warm springs welling through fractures carry dissolved lime and silica in heavy suspension; wherever the water sheets across the dark basalt, it leaves behind growing rims of pale, crystalline travertine—sharp-lipped terraces, blistered crusts, and bone-white mineral curtains that thicken by the breadth of a fingernail every year. Footpaths do not wear into ruts; they calcify upward under the steady trickle, forcing inhabitants to chip the pathways flat again with heavy cobble mauls.

Across this wide, low floor—and climbing every stepped shelf of the basin’s bowl—stretches a city built entirely without timber, mortar, or forged metal.

From any slightly raised terrace, the settlement extends into the dim haze as an immense, honeycombed crust of stacked stone. Tens of thousands of enclosures press wall-to-wall, layered three and four tiers deep, separated by slotted alleys barely wide enough for two sets of shoulders to pass, connected by slab bridges, narrow viaducts, and hundreds of miles of open stone water-channels. The builders work with the rock’s own habits. They lever fresh, sharp-edged basalt prisms from the cooling seams with bone wedges and rawhide slings, dry-stack them into tight courses, and then divert a warm mineral runnel over the new wall. Within a few moons, the seeping water cements the joints in hard white stone, fusing whole neighborhoods into continuous, monolithic reefs of black rock and pale sinter. Roofs are pitched slabs of split slate or broad vaults of interlocking shells and heavy rib-bones smeared with gray thermal clay.

Because there are no plants, there is no smoke—only the tallow-smell of rendered fat lamps flickering red inside low doorways, and the faint, clean mineral tang of wet chalk and warm iron. Cooking is done in unglazed clay pots wedged directly into steaming floor-vents, or submerged in communal boiling-troughs along the hotter fissures.

Yet for all the barrenness of its stone, the city is choked with animal life. The food chain begins in the water itself: thick, ochre-colored and rust-red bacterial slicks coat the warm mineral pans and aqueducts, thriving on sulfur and dissolved salts. Feeding on that slick are billions of organisms. Blind white midges rise in dense, shimmering columns from the shallow flats; flat-shelled snails cluster so thickly along the stone channels that they look like cobbles; pale, heavy-clawed crabs the size of two fists clatter through every gutter and cistern.

Everything else eats them, packed into the urban stonework at staggering density:

*   Along every warm wall and lintel sit fat, glistening, bronze-skinned toads, pulsing their throat-sacs in endless, deafening chorus, snapping midges out of the wet air by the mouthful.
*   Broad-headed lizards with rough, beaded scales pile three-deep over the hotter geothermal slabs, utterly indifferent to human bare feet stepping among them.
*   Every upper cornice, parapet, and purpose-built stone pigeonry is jammed with colonial cliff-swifts and heavy, flightless basin-rails whose guano is scraped daily from the ledges into clay baskets.
*   In the flooded lower courts—shallow, warm stepped pools hemmed in by dwellings—schools of whisker-mouthed, scale-less fish churn the clouded water so densely their backs break the surface in a continuous oily ripple.

Human infrastructure and animal habitat are indistinguishable. A wall is simultaneously the side of a house, an aqueduct carrying warm mineral water to a lower terrace, a snail-grazing trough, and a honeycomb of recessed niches built specifically so nesting birds and egg-laying lizards stay within arm’s reach. People crouch along the wet causeways cracking snail shells with flint strikers, stringing dried fish on twisted sinew lines stretched between basalt pillars, or scraping hardened calcium scale out of the sluice-gates with obsidian-edged bone chisels. Tools are blunt, heavy, and immediate: hafted stone hammers bound with wet rawhide that shrinks iron-tight as it dries, baskets woven not from reed—which does not exist—but from split bird-quills and coarse hair, knives flaked from black volcanic glass that gleams wetly in the flat gray light.

To communicate through the roar of the northern cataract and the ear-splitting wall of amphibian noise, people rely on sight and sharp percussion. Across the open breadth of the city, figures stand on slick flat roofs striking resonant slabs of dense phonolite rock with horn mallets—sharp, ringing *clacks* that cut cleanly over the biological din—or signaling down the stepped avenues with broad paddles of bleached bone that catch the dim sky-glow. Up close, conversation happens nose-to-ear or entirely with hands, fingers flicking briskly in the warm, damp air between chest and chin.

Walk half a mile in any direction through the narrow, glistening corridors and the conditions do not shift by a degree. There is no morning coolness, no afternoon glare, no dry season to crack the mud, no winter to quiet the frogs or thin the midge clouds. Water spills over the black fault-scarp at the exact volume it spilled ten generations ago; warm seepage coats the fresh basalt in gleaming, unweathered skins of white stone; millions of throats hammer the heavy air with the same relentless chord; and under the low, motionless gray belly of the sky, a city of chipped obsidian, bone, and self-cementing rock continues to accrete upward, terrace upon wet terrace, in permanent, deafening equilibrium.
