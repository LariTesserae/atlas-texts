The caldera is no wider than a village square, punched cleanly into the shoulder of a high, dry plateau. Because the rock broke recently—geologically yesterday—nothing here has been rounded off. The rim is a jagged collar of black trachyte and pale, unweathered pumice, its fractured facets still sharp enough to catch the skin of a knuckle. Above it, the sky is the deep, dry blue of high altitude, pouring clear afternoon light straight down into the pit. Outside the rim, the plateau is barren mineral dust, swept clean by thin winds. Inside, sheltered from the wind and holding the sun's heat like an earthenware kiln, the space is packed nearly solid.

There is no water here, not even a glistening film on stone, yet the pit is lush and urban.

Rising from the talus at the pit’s floor—and cantilevered directly out of the sheer walls—is a dense, terraced settlement built without a single tool, nail, or dressed stone. The architecture is a stacked honeycombing of hexagonal chambers, bridged walkways, ribbed vaults, and stepped plazas, all formed from the chewed, regurgitated mineral paste of its builders and fused together by thick, amber-colored plant resins. The walls look like spun unglazed porcelain, pale gray and ochre, streaked where dark basalt dust was mixed into the mortar. Every edge is fresh and crisp; no rain has ever fallen here to slump the clay or streak the facades. 

Woven completely through this masonry—acting as rebar, roofing, and plumbing simultaneously—is the vegetation. Plants that have abandoned water entirely in favor of viscous, slow-moving oils and liquid waxes grow in heavy, braided profusion. Thick, bloated vines the color of oxidized copper snake out of crevices in the raw trachyte and thread directly through the walls of the settlement, swelling on either side of an aperture to lock the dry mortar in place. Broad, rubbery canopies of slate-green and dusty violet fan out over the narrow alleys, creating three and four distinct tiers of dappled shade. Their leaves do not flutter; they are too heavy with dense lipids, stiff as saddle leather, holding their angles motionless in the warm, still air. Where a frond cracks under its own weight, it doesn't bleed sap that evaporates, but extrudes a slow bead of clear, turpentine-scented balsam that hardens over weeks into a structural glassyamber node.

The air inside the pit is markedly warmer than the plateau above, baked by the black rock and heavy with the dry, resinous smell of warm wax, crushed sage, and static electricity. 

Move down one of the narrow, stepped thoroughfares—barely wide enough for a human’s shoulders, though nothing human made them—and the sheer crowding of the place becomes clear. Every vertical surface is claimed. Beneath the overarching wax-canopies, smaller lithophytes crust the porcelain walls in overlapping, shingled rosettes of silver and rust-red, drinking trace sulfur and warm updrafts from hairline vents in the young volcanic rock below. Hanging gardens of dry, fibrous globes dangle on tough filaments across archways, brushing against one another without rustling.

And everywhere, moving through the shafts of clear mountain sunlight and the warm ochre shadows of the arcades, are the inhabitants. 

There are thousands of them within this cathedral-sized bowl, yet the entire settlement exists in a state of breathtaking, pressurized hush. There are no lungs here to shout, no vocal cords to chirp, and the air is too dry and still to carry much vibration anyway. The primary architects are creatures the size of loaves of bread—broad, six-limbed, tortoise-like invertebrates clad in matte, velvety plates that absorb sound rather than clicking against the stone. Their footpads are wide cushions of dry setae that grip the vertical pumice and porcelain walls through sheer molecular adhesion, allowing them to walk upside down beneath the bridges in total silence. 

They move at the pace of the minute hand on a clock. Two of them meet on a narrow resin-bound viaduct: they pause, extend feathery, saffron-colored antennae to taste the static charge and volatile oils on the other's shell, and spend ten minutes slowly rotating around one another’s bulk before continuing on. Along the parapets, dozens more press their mandibles against the edges of unfinished walls, slowly extruding ribbons of dry, frothy stone-paste that cures hard in the sun. 

Around and over these slow masons moves the rest of the pit’s abundant life. Long, flattened lizards with skins like beaded sandstone lie draped across the warm copper vines, so crowded their tails overlap like braided rope, blinking clear membranes over dark eyes once every few minutes. When one shifts to follow a creeping patch of sunlight, its belly-scales slide over the wax-leaves with the faintest dry *shhhk*—like a piece of chalk drawn lightly across slate. Swarms of fingernail-sized, wingless mites, colored dull turquoise, migrate in slow, coherent rivers up the sunny sides of towers to harvest microscopic salt-crystals sweated out by the plants, flowing seamlessly over the backs of the sleeping lizards and working builders alike without disturbing them. Deeper in the shaded undercrofts, pale, multi-jointed things with limbs like dry twigs tend to spherical vats made of leaf-leather, capping and uncapping stores of thick golden plant-grease that serve the town instead of cisterns.

Sound here is an event so tiny you have to hold your breath to catch it. There is no wind inside the rim, no trickle of moisture, no buzz of wings. If you sit motionless on a freshly fractured ledge of black glass halfway down the wall, the quiet settles in until your own heartbeat feels impossibly loud and frantic against the local tempo. Then, gradually, the micro-sounds of the city emerge: the faint, dry pop of a seed-pod splitting open over the course of an afternoon; the whisper of pulverized pumice being chewed into dry paste; the soft, papery tick of thousands of velvet feet shifting weight in unison as the shadow of the western rim begins its slow crawl across the central plaza.

Nothing rots here. Without water, decay as a wet, collapsing thing does not exist. In the lowest, oldest crevices at the bottom of the pit—perhaps fifty or a hundred years old, dating back to the first decade after the rock cooled—dead vines and shed carapaces simply cure in the dry warmth, turning brittle and translucent like parchment. The builders do not clear them away; they plaster directly over them, incorporating hollow shells as insulating voids in the foundations and using petrified stems as trusses for new plazas above. 

As the afternoon advances, the angle of the normal, bright mountain light tilts. Half the pit drops into crisp blue-gray shadow while the eastern terraces flare bright gold and terracotta. The temperature drops a slow fraction of a degree. Across the tiered facades, thousands of slate-green leaves hinge upward by an inch over the span of an hour to hold the warmth inside the arcades, narrowing the streets into covered galleries. The turquoise rivers of mites stall and cluster into rosettes in the corners of windowless niches. The broad, silent masons tuck their limbs beneath their matte shells right where they stand on the walls, looking indistinguishable from decorative bosses in the pale masonry, waiting out the cooling dusk at the bottom of their sharp-edged, waterless world.
