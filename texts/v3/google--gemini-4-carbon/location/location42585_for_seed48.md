The air hangs like a soaked wool blanket, hovering just above a hundred degrees Fahrenheit. It does not shimmer with dry mirages; it sweats. Above, a ceiling of bruised, low-hanging cloud diffuses the sun into a flat, pewter glare that casts no distinct shadows. Visibility drops off a mile or two out, swallowed by a permanent, warm mist that smells faintly of iron, wet concrete, and salt.

This is a broad, sunken basin—an old coastal delta or subsided industrial plain sitting barely above the water table. There is no soil to hold roots, and nothing green grows here. Decades of heavy alkaline runoff and thermal discharge long ago scoured the ground down to a pale, leached hardpan of compacted clay, slag, and crushed shell, smoothed over by years of warm rain and sheet-flooding. Only slick, dark films of thermophilic algae and ochre bacterial mats cling to the depressions where water pools.

Across this wide, flat pan lies an industrial district built for space rather than height. Because the ground is soft and wet, the architecture does not crowd together. Instead, vast, featureless expanses of flooded tarmac and sunken concrete plazas stretch out for hundreds of yards between structures. It feels hollow, a city laid out on an oversized grid where the buildings occupy a fraction of the footprint. Far off across the steaming flats, the silhouettes of refinery cracking towers, immense cylindrical storage tanks, and long, low-slung assembly sheds sit isolated from one another like islands in a shallow bay. Elevated pipe-racks run between them on weathered concrete pilings, bridging the empty quarter-miles in straight, rusting lines. 

Everything built here has settled into a deep, quiet patina of age and moisture. The corrugated iron of the distant warehouses is oxidized to the color of dried blood, streaked vertically by decades of condensation. Concrete retaining walls have been worn smooth at their bases by lapping surface water, their edges rounded and stained dark brown. And yet, the infrastructure is still running. It is an automated, late-stage industrial landscape requiring almost no human foot traffic. Miles of insulated steam pipes hiss softly at their joints, bleeding white vapor into the already saturated air. High-voltage transformers hum behind chain-link enclosures, a low electrical vibration you feel in your teeth before you hear it. At regular intervals, massive submerged pumps kick on beneath the grates of the drainage canals, pushing warm, brackish water slowly toward the unseen coast with a muffled, rhythmic thrum. 

In the absence of human crowds and plant cover, animal life has claimed the warm concrete and warm water in staggering numbers. 

They operate mostly in silence. Across the vast, glistening tarmac aprons, millions of fiddler and marsh crabs move like a shifting, clicking carpet. When a drop of condensate falls from an overhead gantry forty feet up, a fifty-foot circle of carapaces parts instantly and closes back up again. Every concrete piling, rust-pitted steel beam, and warm brick wall is encrusted to the waterline with clusters of mussels, barnacles, and pale, heat-tolerant snails, slowly rasping away at the microscopic biofilms. 

The puddles and broad, ankle-deep retention basins teem. The water is constantly dimpled and swirling—not from rain, which falls only as a warm, silent drizzle, but from the dense, churning bodies of mudskippers, eels, and armored catfish thriving in the thermally heated outflow from the cooling towers. They move through the murky shallows in thick, dark ribbons, occasionally breaking the surface with a soft *plop* to gulp air.

Higher up, the warmth of the active machinery draws reptiles and birds by the thousands. Thick-bodied lizards and dark water snakes drape themselves along the insulated steam mains, absorbing the radiant heat through the metal jacketing, motionless except for the slow, heavy pulse of their throats. Above them, lining every horizontal crane boom, catwalk railing, and concrete parapet across the two-mile basin, sit silent ranks of cormorants, herons, and gulls. They do not wheel and cry as they do on an open coast; the oppressive heat dampens them. They stand shoulder to shoulder in the dim grey light, wings held slightly open to shed warmth, dropping down in sudden, noiseless glides to strike at the crowded shallows below.

At dusk—which arrives merely as a deepening of the grey ceiling into charcoal—sodium-vapor floodlights click on automatically along the distant tank farms and empty roadways, casting wide pools of amber across the wet pavement. Instantly, the air beneath the lamps thickens into swirling, silent tornadoes of midges, brine flies, and heavy-bodied moths, rising from the warm canals in clouds so dense they dim the bulbs. Bats and nightjars slice continuously through the amber cones, mouths wide, making no sound louder than the soft leather snap of a wingtip turning in the thick air. Below them, small packs of feral dogs and lean, wet-furred scavengers trot quietly along the raised concrete medians, weaving carefully through the parting seas of crabs to hunt the shoreline of a warm, humming, concrete marsh.
