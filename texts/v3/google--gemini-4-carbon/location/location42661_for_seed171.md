There is no soil here, and there are no rivers, because nothing has been here long enough to wear anything else down.

Where stone shows through the mass of living matter, its edges are razor-sharp—freshly cleaved, glassy dark silicates and pale, unoxidized mineral faces that have never known wind-scour or frost-shatter. A fractured ledge will slice leather cleanly. There is no sand, no silt, no gravel, no rounded cobble anywhere across the plateau. Geology is still arriving: raw, angular thrusts of crust heaving upward into the cool highland air, still ticking with internal tension, and before a newly cracked seam can stay open to the grey sky for an hour, a pale, pressurized wedge of root-tissue has already forced itself into the cleft and sealed it shut.

Everything else—upward, downward, laterally for thousands of kilometers across the high continental table—is plant.

Not a forest, which implies trunks with air between them and a floor beneath. This is a continuous, three-dimensional thatch so tightly packed that there is no interior space at all. Stems the thickness of anchor chains braid around fluted stalks, which are themselves strangled by tiered shelves of rubbery, overlapping bracts, every gap plugged by dense bolsters of stiff, branching moss-analogues and coiled tendrils under immense hydraulic tension. To move forward a meter would require sawing through a solid wall of wet, fibrous cable, only to watch the cut ends bleed clear, cold fluid and swell shut behind you within minutes.

The noise is physical. It presses against the eardrums and vibrates in the sternum, never dropping below a roar.

Because there are no eroded valleys or carved stream-beds to gather runoff, water cannot collect into rivers; instead, the entire continent drains vertically and chaotically through the body of the vegetation itself. High above, in the dim, slate-colored murk where the upper canopy meets the thin highland air, billions of broad, slate-black fronds shudder at a precise, high-speed frequency. It is an active, mechanical tremor—a biological piezoelectric hum—that strips moisture straight out of the cold cloud-deck, snapping vapor into heavy liquid sheets on contact. That water plunges downward through fifty or a hundred meters of packed foliage, hammering against drum-tight leaves as stiff as sheet metal, sluicing down grooved bark-chutes, pooling in vast cup-bracts until their rims give way and dump tons of cold water onto the tiers below. Add to the endless hydraulic cataract the acoustic violence of the growth itself: the sharp, rifle-shot cracks of freshly extruded rock splitting under root-pressure; the deep, percussive *thump* of internal valves cycling inside massive vascular trunks as they pump water back upward against gravity; the deafening, rhythmic hiss of pneumatic bladders venting surplus oxygen into the mist.

Look closely at a single leaf in the dim, shadowless light, and it becomes clear that this biology was not arrived at by blind evolutionary drift. Nothing here is built—there is no metal bulkhead, no ruin, no pylon, no wire—yet everything is engineered at the scale of the cell and the lattice. The underside of a frond is ribbed with hexagonal trusses of spun silica that give it the tensile strength of aircraft skin. Cut into a woody stem and the grain reveals microscopic, iridescent channels conducting faint thermal and electrical currents across kilometers of linked root-network, balancing pressure and nutrient loads across an entire weather system. When a cloud-bank thickens overhead and drops the light from twilight-dim to near-darkness, a silent phase-change ripples across the upper canopy from horizon to horizon: the leaves alter their surface refractive index, turning from matte black to a slick, oil-film violet to trap the longer, weaker wavelengths filtering through the overcast.

Nothing eats any of it.

Every leaf is pristine. Across three thousand miles of saturated, heaving biomass, there is not a single chewed margin, no borehole in the bark, no web strung between stalks, no bird-call buried in the din, no movement that isn't hydraulic, atmospheric, or botanical. Nothing crawls in the wet dark down near the raw stone. Even decay seems absent, or bypassed; when a lower branch is shaded out by newer growth surging overhead, it does not rot into humus—neighbouring stems simply fuse directly into its rind, drain its vascular fluids clean, and vitrify the hollow husk into rigid structural scaffolding of pure carbon-silicate, building the plateau higher layer by layer.

Only where a fresh tectonic fault has just jacked a ridge twenty meters above the surrounding level can you catch any sense of the scale. Clambering onto one of those cold, knife-edged spines before the encroaching root-wall swallows it, you see no mountains and no valleys—just a single, unbroken, charcoal-and-verdigris ocean of vibrating foliage stretching out beneath a low ceiling of churning slate cloud. Thirty miles off, a massive band of vapour is rising straight out of the canopy like steam from a boiler, exhaled in unison by millions of acres of synchronized stomata, cooling instantly in the brisk high-altitude air, condensing, darkening, and collapsing right back down into the leaves in a fresh, roaring deluge. Even as you stand on the raw black stone, slick tendrils are already sliding over the sharp mineral lip beside your boots, humming faintly against the rock, wet with cold mist, locking themselves into place.
