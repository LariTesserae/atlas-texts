There is no stone beneath this city, and there has not been for so long that the concept of mineral earth exists only as a mathematical abstraction used to calibrate scales. The plateau—two miles up, flattened by eons of razor-thin wind into a gentle, sprawling dish ten miles across—is made entirely of compressed, living lignum. It is a single, primordial root-mat, dense as ironwood, dark grey and fissured like elephant hide, breathing at a pace too slow for the eye to catch.

To walk here is to walk on the back of a sleeping titan made of cellulose. Underfoot, the ground gives a microscopic, rubbery yield. Where the surface has worn smooth over millennia, the grain of the world shows through in swirling, burl-wood contours the size of city blocks. 

Because the elevation is high, the sky is an immense, pale vault, washed in crisp, unforgiving daylight. There is no canopy overhead. Vegetation is total, yet the landscape is starkly open, swept clean of clutter. Nothing leafy or fragile survives the cool, dry wind that planes across the plateau. Instead, the city itself is the canopy, grown and grafted and industrialized into sparse, monumental intervals across the vast wooden plain.

A half-mile of empty, wind-polished root-flat separates one district from the next. Across these wide expanses run transit lines—not steel rails, but parallel ridges of hardened heartwood extruded directly from the ground, greased with weeping amber sap. Heavy, iron-plated locomotives haul loads of refined resin along these tracks, their boilers burning dried pith, throwing white plumes of steam that tear apart instantly in the high-altitude gusts. 

Where the buildings rise, they do not merely sit upon the ground; they are continuous with it. Centuries ago, engineers ceased bringing external materials up the plateau and began forcing the ancient substrate to build for them, marrying heavy Victorian-scale ironwork with tortured, accelerated arboriculture. Great factory-groves stand isolated against the bright sky: monolithic cylinders of seamless grey bark, thickened and squared off by massive iron corsets, bolted through with brass tie-rods the width of tree trunks. Smokestacks of grafted hollow reed rise beside riveted steel chimneys. Inside these living foundries, pistons forged in lowland valleys are mounted directly into flexing walls of muscle-wood. The city harvests turgor pressure—the immense hydraulic force of sap rising from unimaginable depths—to drive vast mechanical looms, turbine shafts, and stamping mills.

It is loud here. The thin, cool air carries noise for miles without softening it. There is the relentless, rhythmic *thud-hiss* of the sap-engines, the screech of iron collars straining against wood that refuses to stop slowly warping, and beneath it all, a vast, sub-audible groan. That groan is the plateau shifting in its ancient, eroded bed, tightening its fibers, drinking condensation from the clouds that scrape its underside miles below. Every few minutes, somewhere across the valley-sized expanse, a buildup of internal pressure vents through a relief tower—a deafening, brass-throated *blare* like a foghorn blown by a lung the size of a mountain, spraying a fine mist of warm, pine-smelling condensation across the dry bark streets.

These venting mists, along with the fog banks that occasionally drift over the plateau’s lip in the late afternoon, provide the only surface moisture. Water does not pool; the thirsty wood drinks it before it can run. Dampness lingers only in the deep, recessed trenches carved out for axle-pits, where dark mosses, thick and slick as grease, coat the spinning iron flywheels, and pale, segmented millipedes as long as a man’s forearm curl near the warmth of the exhaust pipes. Aside from these sparse, armored crawlers and the occasional solitary hawk riding the high thermals, looking in vain for grass or rodents, nothing moves of its own free will. Even the builders and tenders of these machines are scarcely seen, dwarfed by the scale of the wide avenues and sealed inside pressurized, double-paned conservatory-cabs to stay warm.

And then there are the places where the grafting, the altitude, the sheer exhausted age of the living plateau, and the ceaseless vibration of the engines have worn the world thin.

The reality breakdown does not announce itself with darkness or spectacle; it happens right out in the crisp, normal daylight, quiet beneath the mechanical roar. Because the substrate is one continuous, ancient organism stretched across ten miles of industrial tension, space has begun to follow the grain of the wood rather than the laws of geometry. 

Look down a wide, empty boulevard of polished root-matter toward a refinery three miles distant, and you can watch the perspective knot. Just as wood forms a burl around an injury, space curls tightly around the heaviest factories. A locomotive chugging straight along a sap-rail will abruptly turn sideways without curving, sliding along an invisible ring of atmospheric grain, appearing simultaneously two hundred yards to the left and half a mile closer, its steam plume severed cleanly in the bright air and continuing to drift from a point where the engine no longer is.

Along the older infrastructure—abandoned viaducts where rusted iron girders have been entirely swallowed by slow-creeping grey callus-wood—cause and effect fray like bruised hemp. The *clang* of a drop-forge hits the ear three seconds before the steam-hammer falls. Shadows cast by the high afternoon sun do not match the chimneys they belong to; a riveted iron tower throws the crisp, dark silhouette of a colossal, branching fern that died four hundred thousand years ago, back when this plateau was still a young forest rooted in forgotten dirt.

In the wide, sparse plazas between the humming factory-trunks, surveyors leave heavy brass plumb-bobs suspended from tripods to monitor the drift. On bad days, when the sap-pressure runs high and the whole plateau shudders with industrial fever, the brass weights hang at forty-five-degree angles, pulled toward knots of dense, non-Euclidean heartwood buried deep underfoot. Rain—rare, cold, and stinging—will occasionally fall upward out of a dry crack in the ground, a localized memory of water rising through xylem tubes, droplets shooting fifty feet into the clear air before vanishing with sharp, percussive pops like snapping twigs.

Yet the engines keep running. Governed by brass flyballs spinning furiously in the cool wind, the valves open and close, bleeding off the impossible pressures of the ancient plant. The city endures in a state of loud, bright, spare equilibrium: iron biting into timeless wood, steam dissolving into the high blue sky, and miles of grey, living ground stretching smoothly out to the edge of the world, humming like a struck tuning fork.
