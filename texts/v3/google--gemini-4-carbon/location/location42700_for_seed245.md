There is no dirt under this world, no bedrock, no planetary crust. Down as far as the physics of the place permit, there is only masonry. 

It is a lowland basin so wide its edges drop away behind the curve of an impossible horizon, floored entirely in broad, unevenly fired terracotta flagstones and rough-hewn blocks of pale tufa. Everything here was made, yet there are no makers. Every brick bears the thumb-smear of a giant or the scrape of a wooden trowel; every joint is packed with a coarse lime mortar stubbled with chopped straw that never grew in any field. Nothing is worn down. The edges of the flagstones are razor-sharp, crisp from the mold, fresh-laid and raw, as if the kiln-fires were only drawn an hour ago. 

The air is fiercely hot, thick as broth, pressing into the lungs like the atmosphere inside a sealed earthenware steamer. Overhead, there is no sun and no sky—only a vast, dim, bruising vault of copper-colored cloud that hangs low enough to swallow the tops of the taller structures, lit from somewhere behind its own density by a sourceless, tallow-yellow glow. Shadows barely exist here; objects simply darken at their bases, steeped in a perpetual, sweaty twilight.

Out across the endless flats, structures stand at sparse, lonely intervals, separated by miles of glistening brick pavement. They are primitive and monumental: squat stepped ziggurats of unbaked mud, wide-mouthed cisterns bound in braided reed ropes, towering scaffolding lashed together from peeled, barkless timber beams with damp rawhide thongs, and long, pointless aqueducts that begin in mid-air and end abruptly at clean, vertical plumb-lines. 

This landscape is not settled. It is violently, ceaselessly coming into being, though it happens almost entirely in the hush of a steam bath. 

Reality here suffers from a restless, feverish drafting error. Two miles off, a massive buttress of stacked slate does not crumble—it *un-lays* itself. Thousands of tons of cut stone lift smoothly out of their mortar beds, fan outward into the wet air like a shuffled deck of cards, hover in suspended strata, and snap back down three hundred yards to the east as a circular watchtower, without producing a single crash. The turbulence is manic, constant, and eerily muffled. Where the ground heaves, the flagstones ripple like heavy canvas shaken at one end; trenches yawn open to reveal not magma or soil, but deeper, older courses of pristine, dry-stone walling, freshly chiseled, going down forever, before the gap zippers shut again in a rapid, silent cascade of interlocking brick. 

Water is everywhere, born out of the thermal thickness of the air and the strange metabolism of the stonework. None of it falls as proper rain, and none of it gathers into rivers, because the masonry refuses to erode. There are no gullies, no smoothed pebbles, no silt. Instead, the hot stone sweats. Scalding condensation beads on the timber scaffolds and runs down the faces of the ziggurats in shimmering, oily sheets. Shallow, mirror-flat pools—warm enough to poach an egg, only an inch or two deep—stretch across sunken courtyards five miles wide, steaming gently into the dimness. From the joints between newly manifested blocks, clear, mineral-heavy water wells up in rhythmic pulses, spreading out across the terracotta until a sudden rearrangement of the floor tilts the plain, sending a shallow, steaming bore rushing silently across the bricks until it hits a newly risen retaining wall and vanishes down raw drainage grates that lead nowhere.

Because the world refuses to stay still long enough for rot to take hold, and the heat cooks away most delicate things, life has barely found a purchase. There are no trees, no mosses softening the razor-sharp edges of the steps, no insects skating on the hot puddles, no birds circling the timber towers. The only living presence—if it is living—is a scattered, blister-like crust that forms where the hot water pools longest against the lime mortar. It looks like spilled red glaze or sheets of translucent, rust-colored tallow. When the flagstones shift and break apart beneath it, the crust stretches like warm hide before tearing, bleeding a thin, clear mucilage that smells faintly of iron and wet clay. 

The quiet of the place is heavy, insulated by the wet heat and the sheer, swallowing distance between one wall and the next. Sound does not carry; the thick air smothers it within fifty paces. Walk across the flats and you hear mostly the slick *slap* of water over fresh clay, the low, rhythmic *glug* of warm cisterns emptying into lower vaults, and the constant, soft *hiss* of steam escaping from hairline fractures in stone that was dry a second ago. 

Behind that quiet sits a deeper, subtler sound, felt in the teeth more than heard: a vast, dry *ticking* and the taut *creak* of wet rawhide shrinking as it dries on a thousand miles of shifting timber frames. 

Now and then, you come across tools. A wooden mallet the size of a man’s torso resting in an inch of steaming water, its strike-face completely unmarked. Plumb-bobs of crude, hammered copper swinging slowly from crossbeams, pointing not quite straight down, dragged off-center by localized pockets of crooked gravity. Near a rising stair that builds itself step-by-step up into the copper fog—each rough block sweating itself out of the empty air, glistening wet, locking seamlessly against the last—sits a woven basket overflowing with wet grey mortar. Five seconds later, the stair is gone, replaced by a sunken courtyard of herringbone brick. The basket remains, untouched, rimmed with beads of hot dew, waiting on a flat plain that stretches away in every direction, endlessly laying its own foundations in the dark.
