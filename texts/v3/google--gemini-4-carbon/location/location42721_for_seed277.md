The air at twelve thousand feet should be cold, thin, and bright, yet here it hangs thick, breathless, and dark as the inside of a kiln. Warmth rises steadily out of the rock itself—a slow, planetary exhalation from a radiogenic crust—and stays trapped beneath an upper atmosphere opaque with suspended carbon and high, perpetual cloud. There is no sunrise to divide one part of time from another. Overhead is only a matte, unbroken vault that gives back the faintest plum-colored airglow, just enough to separate the long curve of a ridge from the sky above it after an hour of letting the eyes adjust.

The plateau goes on for thousands of miles like this, too vast to share a single weather system. It is an old, settled highland. Whatever sharp tectonic violence pushed it up ended ages ago; wind and thermal expansion have planed the mountains down into whale-backed swells fifty miles long, separated by broad, shallow basins paved in dark greywacke gravel, ironstone nodules, and drifted silt. Nothing jagged remains. Every ledge is rounded like a worn knuckle, every gully graded to a gentle, stable slope. The emptiness between features is immense. You can walk for six hours across level, crunching scree before reaching a solitary outcrop of polished basalt, and another six before encountering a shallow pan of damp clay.

Water never gathers into rivers here, and it rarely falls hard enough to cut a channel. Instead, it breathes up and down through the dark. Warm updrafts haul moisture out of deep, unseen boundary rifts far off at the edges of the continent; as the air slides across the plateau, it cools just enough by a degree or two to sweat. Stone faces go slick at regular intervals. Thin beads of condensation form along the undersides of gravel clasts, wetting the dust beneath to the consistency of dark flour paste. Every few days a warm, silent drizzle drifts horizontally across a basin for twenty minutes and stops, leaving no puddles, only a sudden, heavy smell of wet iron and ozone.

Plants keep their distance from one another, spaced across the gravel as if laid out on a loose grid dictated by root-reach. Each shrub claims thirty or forty square yards of bare ground around itself. They have no use for chlorophyll; their foliage is tough, waxy, and charcoal-black or dull bronze, shaped into stiff, upright combs and fluted funnels designed to rake mist out of the passing air and channel every drop straight down to bloated subterranean tubers. Between them, the finer silt is bound together by thin crusts of thermophilic lichen—leathery, rust-colored skins that peel up at the edges when the wind shifts dry and flatten back down when the damp returns.

For all the bareness of the ground, the dark is crowded with breath and movement. You feel the animals before you see them: the sudden displacement of warm air as something large shifts weight ten yards away, the faint musky ammonia drift of a passing herd, the dry, rhythmic *tick-tick-tick* of broad hooves stepping carefully over loose shale. Because light gives nothing away, nothing bothers to roar or call out across the open; noise here is small, close, and functional. Flocks of wide-jawed, leather-winged gliders skim six feet off the gravel on thermal currents, their wing-membranes edged in soft combs that silence their flight while they strain dense clouds of blind, damp-loving midges out of the air. Beneath them move long, loose columns of pale, heavy-bodied grazers—hundreds at a time—scraping up the mineral crusts and probing the gravel with prehensile, heat-sensing snouts for tubers and burrowing larvae.

Predators work the margins of these columns entirely by thermal silhouette and ground-vibration. Long, low-slung quadrupeds with oversized, dish-like ears pad parallel to the herds in total silence, waiting for an animal to lag or stumble in a drift of silt. When a kill happens, there is only a brief scuffle of gravel, a muffled exhalation, and within minutes the quiet click of smaller scavengers—armored, many-legged things and soft-furred nocturnal jackals—converging out of burrows along the dry washes. Life turns over quickly in the warmth. Bones do not bleach here; they are rasped clean in a day and dissolved by damp mineral salts within a season.

Then, across forty miles of dark, empty basin, a pale amber point of light crests a ridge.

There are no roads on this plateau. There has never been a foundation poured, a pylon bolted into the bedrock, a fence strung, or a Cairn stacked. The ground is wholly unbuilt. Yet the amber point belongs to an engine the size of a cathedral nave.

It comes down the long grade at barely two miles an hour on sixteen articulated bogies, each shod insegmented steel treads eight feet wide that distribute its weight so evenly the gravel beneath them merely settles with a low, continuous *shhhk-shhhk*, like surf on a distant shingle. There are others on the plateau—perhaps one every hundred or two hundred miles, completely autonomous, never congregating, each pacing out a vast, non-repeating circuit dictated by atmospheric density and mineral drift. Its hull is unpainted, weathered alloy, streaked with dark condensation and dusted with the same iron silt as the ridges. No cabin windows glow along its flanks; nothing inside requires a room or a chair. Instead, banks of electrostatic precipitators and tall, louvered intake towers rise from its spine, humming at a pitch so low it registers in the sternum rather than the ears.

As it moves, the machine drinks the heavy air. Internal turbines compress the warm, damp haze, stripping out trace noble gases, heavy isotopes, and condensed water, while electromagnetic skimmers hung beneath its belly comb fine metallic dust straight up out of the gravel without turning a furrow. Every few minutes, static charge built up across its intake vanes discharges internally with a muted, rhythmic *thump* and a brief, violet flash deep inside the louvers, throwing momentary, crisp shadows of nearby mist-combing shrubs fifty yards across the dark stone before dropping the world back into blackness.

The wilderness treats these engines like moving hills. Gliders ride the warm, clean exhaust plumes rising from their dorsal vents, spiraling upward into richer pockets of airborne insects. Pale mites and heat-loving beetles cluster along the warm hydraulic housings during the slower stretches of a climb. A herd of grazers parts smoothly around the oncoming treads with forty feet to spare, unhurried, turning their eyeless, pitted faces toward the electrostatic hum as it passes, then closing ranks behind the stern to lick the pure, warm distilled runoff that drips steadily from the condenser overflow pipes onto the dry stones.

Half an hour after the rig has passed, the drips evaporate into the warm dark. The wind slides down off the long, eroded whale-back of the ridge, sifting fine silt into the shallow corduroy impression left by the treads. A hunter steps across the track without breaking stride, nose down, following the heat-trail of a straggler. Far off on the horizon, the single amber marker lamp slips down into the next basin and goes out behind the curve of the earth, leaving the vast, quiet highland to the breathing of its herds and the slow sweat of the stone.
