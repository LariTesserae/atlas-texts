From rim to rim across the high basin—perhaps two miles at its widest—not a single square foot of raw bedrock or native soil remains uncovered. Everything in sight has been split, hauled, and wedged into place by hand, and all of it is wet.

The light at this altitude is fierce and unfiltered, dropping out of a pale, thin sky onto millions of slick horizontal facets. Looking across the valley means squinting into a vast field of broken glare. The stone is mostly dark blue-gray shale and fine-grained basalt, cleaved along flat planes so recently that every edge is still keen enough to slice leather. Nothing has worn down yet. There are no rounded cobbles, no softened steps, no wind-blunted corners. Where a mason drove a line of wedges to crack a slab, the half-round scars along the lip remain chalky and crisp, dusty rock-flour barely rinsed out of the grain by the cold water sheeting over it.

There are no streets, courtyards, or open plazas here—no room left over between one thing and the next. Structures crowd against structures in a continuous, shoulder-to-shoulder cellular mass that climbs from the sunken center of the bowl all the way up the surrounding slopes to cut sharp, notched profiles against the sky. Low retaining walls butt directly into stepped cisterns; cistern rims serve as the foundations for squat, beehive-shaped cairns; narrow spillways thread over the tops of buried vaults and empty onto tiered aprons of overlapping slate shingles. To cross the basin, you would have to walk the six-inch-wide copings of partition walls or step from plinth to plinth across brimming, knee-deep tanks, turning sideways to squeeze between dense clusters of shoulder-high pillars laid up without mortar.

Water saturates the entire construction, though none of it rushes. Springs trapped somewhere beneath the masonry floor push steady, cold upwellings into the central wells, and every night low clouds drag across the ridge crests, condensing heavily against the chilled labyrinth of exposed rock. By mid-morning, the whole valley is weeping and brimming under bright sun. Water beads out of tight dry-stone joints, darkens the vertical faces of slabs to the color of oiled iron, and spreads in thin, mirror-calm films across level terraces. Each basin fills until surface tension domes slightly at the razor-cut stone lip, then slips over in a silent, glass-clear curtain a millimeter thick, sliding down the face into the next pan below.

Because the drops are shallow and the stone baffles are packed so tightly together, the water makes almost no noise. There is no roar of rapids, not even much splashing—only an immense, hushed murmur dispersed across thousands of stone chambers, like fine sleet settling into dry reeds, though there are no reeds here. Now and then a pocket of trapped air burps softly from a submerged seam, or a freshly laid flake of shale shifts a fraction of an inch under hydraulic pressure with a sharp, ceramic *clink* that carries clearly in the thin air and dies without echoing.

Nothing grows in the joints. There is no moss softening the north-facing crevices, no lichen crusting the drier capstones, not a single blade of sedge wedged in silt. The masonry is too young and the high, cool air too sterile, or else the water carries dissolved salts that discourage roots. Only in the broadest, slowest-moving pans—shallow stone trays twenty feet across where water lingers for days—does a faint organic trace appear: thin, velvety skins of pale ochre and tarnished-silver slime clinging to the submerged slate, motionless under the glare, looking more like precipitated metal ore than living matter. Nothing feeds on it. No midges skate the surface tension; no birds circle the bright thermal drafts above the ridge. The water surfaces remain taut and unrippled from wall to wall.

Stranger still than the emptiness is the absence of anything built for shelter. Nowhere in the packed expanse is there a roof wide enough to sleep under, a hearth darkened by soot, or a doorway scaled to an upright back. The labor visible in the valley is staggering—millions of tons of quarried rock trimmed with hammerstones, levered on wooden or bone skids, fitted together with meticulous, obsessive patience—yet every structure appears to exist solely to handle water, air, and light in tiny increments. 

Everywhere among the stepped tanks stand hollow, open-topped cones of cantilevered slate, eight to ten feet tall, built with outward-tilting courses that trap the cool prevailing breeze in shaded interiors while shedding condensate down interior grooves into covered troughs. Beside them sit ranks of narrow channels floored with unglazed, hand-pinched red clay tiles set on edge like gill plates, catching suspended mineral flour so fine it turns the standing water milky pale blue in one row of vats and dead black in the next.

Scattered every fifty or sixty paces through the congestion are slow balance-works: long, rough-hewn beams of denser pale granite resting across knife-edged stone fulcrums. One end of a beam is lashed with braided mineral fiber or rawhide—already stiffening and bleaching in the UV glare—to a heavy counterweight block; the other cradles a hollowed stone bucket positioned beneath a slow, steady drip from an overhang. For hours, nothing in the landscape moves except the creeping glint of sun angles across wet rock. Then, somewhere three terraces away, a bucket reaches capacity. The granite lever tips downward with a slow, grinding scrape, lifting a flat slate sluice-gate two inches out of its groove. A pent-up sheet of water slides quietly out of a holding pan into an adjacent maze of dry channels, darkening the dusty blue stone in an advancing tide, while the emptied bucket drains through a pinhole at its base and waits half an afternoon to rise again.

The air smells solely of wet flint, cold clay, and ozone. Even at noon, with the sun blazing directly overhead and bouncing blindingly upward off ten thousand shallow pools, the temperature stays brisk; step into the narrow, chest-deep shadow between two weeping cairns and the damp chill bites immediately at bare skin. Out across the basin, dozens of other granite beams sit tilted at different angles of their long cycles, tipping and resetting hours apart across the packed miles of sharp, gleaming stone, sorting clear water from pale silt with no one in sight.
