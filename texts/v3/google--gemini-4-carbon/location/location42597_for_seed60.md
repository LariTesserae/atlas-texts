There is no soil here, and if there is bedrock underneath, nothing has touched it in generations. The entire hollow—an oval basin perhaps seventy yards long and forty wide, sunken like a steep courtyard—is built out of living tissue stacked upon its own compressed, still-vascular past. What serves as ground is a tight, springy mattress of interlocked root-knuckles, swollen storage rhizomes, and dense plates of creeping liverwort so stiff they ring slightly under heel before giving way to squeeze warm, clear water over the soles of a boot. What serves as cliff-wall around the perimeter is a vertical palisade of braided trunks, aerial root-curtains, and overlapping shelf-rosettes rising fifty feet before the canopy rolls inward and stops, leaving an ragged oval of open, bright afternoon sky directly overhead.

Daylight drops straight into this green well. It does not dim into twilight so much as scatter and refract: bouncing off slick, wax-heavy leaves the size of tabletops, shining straight through translucent green bladders stretched taut with fluid, catching in millions of beaded droplets. Everything sweats. It isn't raining, and there is no open pond at the bottom, yet the entire room is running with moisture. Water beads along the serrated edges of fronds by sheer internal root pressure—guttation on a massive scale—gathering into heavy drops that fall onto lower tiers with a constant, echoing clatter like applause in a tiled hall.

Beneath and through that wet patter runs a much louder noise: a continuous, chest-filling mechanical drone, punctuated every forty seconds or so by the sharp, wet *chuff* of a pressure valve lifting and reseating.

Scattered through the packed green mass—half-swallowed by it, yet wholly operational—sit a dozen heavy industrial installations. None of them are connected by visible roads or walkways; there is nowhere for a walkway to exist. Near the center of the basin floor, wedged tightly between pale green buttress-roots thick as hogsheads, stands a squat cast-iron pressure vessel riveted along its seams, its green-enameled casing worn smooth and milky with condensation. Copper pipes as thick as a man’s wrist emerge from its crown and vanish almost immediately into the flesh of neighboring boles, swallowed by smooth, calloused collars of bark that have grown over the brass fittings like gray lips, sealing them tighter than lead wool ever could. Twenty feet away and slightly higher up the living wall, a nine-foot spoked iron flywheel protrudes vertically from a solid thicket of strap-ferns. It is turning, though at first glance it looks frozen; you have to watch a rust-pitted spoke creep past a broad leaf-blade to realize the wheel completes a single revolution only once every three or four minutes, driven by tremendous, unhurried torque.

The density of the basin leaves almost no empty air below neck height. Every cubic foot is contested and occupied. Where two woody stems cross, they have grafted into a single truss; in the crooked elbow between them grows a bulbous water-fern; out of the water-fern’s crown spills a beard of translucent, worm-like clubmoss; threaded through the clubmoss runs a braided steel cable hung on white porcelain insulators that chime faintly against wood when the whole root-floor shifts. To move ten yards across the hollow would require climbing over warm, pulsing tubers, wedging shoulders between tough, rubbery petioles that press back with quiet hydraulic insistence, and stepping along the warm top of a riveted iron conduit coated in greasy black moss. Nothing yields easily, and nothing breaks cleanly—everything is turgid, fibrous, pumped stiff with warm sap.

It is the sap and the machines that share the work here, in an arrangement so old and weathered into balance that it is impossible to tell whether the hardware was lowered in to harvest the plants or to keep them alive. There are no boilers burning coal, no smell of smoke or diesel—only the thick, green odor of bruised celery, warm river-water, warm brass, and ozone. Follow one of the humming copper lines into the tight gloom under a bank of overlapping elephant-ears and you find a ceramic manifold tapped directly into a series of pale, ribbed, barrel-shaped gourds resting in cradles of old angle-iron. Inside the gourds, fluid surges with a slow, audible *glug-thump* you can feel in your molars. The flora acts as a vast, sunlit hydraulic accumulator: drawing moisture from deep beneath the basin, pressurizing it through osmotic membranes across millions of packed leaves in the warm daylight, and feeding that slow, colossal pressure into buried turbines and reciprocating cylinders down in the drowned root-sump.

Because the processes are so slow and so relentless, the loud sound of the basin has no urgency in it. A piston rod polished bright by a tighter collar of fibrous bark takes a full minute to slide six inches out of its stuffing-box, squealing on a single low, cello-like note the entire way, before reaching its stop, tripping a counterweighted brass latch—*clack*—and releasing a long, deafening sigh of warm mist that coats every leaf within ten feet in instant silver pearl. Near the western wall, an ancient dynamo housing painted faded factory-blue hums at a steady sixty cycles, shedding enough gentle waste heat that the vegetation packed directly against its cooling fins has mutated or specialized into tight, meaty, bluish-red pads devoid of leaves, pressed flat against the warm iron like palms against a stove. Stray electrical current leaks into the wet canopy along mineralized vines whose bark glints with dull, scaly flecks of native copper drawn up from deep solution; touch the wrong wet stem near an insulator and your fingers tingle up to the wrist.

Almost nothing with legs or wings lives here. You can stand on a warm cast-iron flange in the bright, steaming center of the room for an hour and never hear a bird call, never swat a gnat, never see an ant marching along a vine. Perhaps the steady sixty-hertz vibration thrumming through every stem drives insects out over the rim, or perhaps the volatile, faintly camphoraceous sap bleeding around the pipe-grafts acts as an insecticide across the whole hollow. Only the rarest outliers remain. Inside the glass sight-gauge of a manifold—half-full of clear, warm condensate—two tiny, blind white shrimp graze methodically on a film of pale algae, wholly indifferent to the forty pounds of pressure behind the glass. Once in a great while, a flat, mottled slug as wide as three fingers glides across a porcelain insulator or down the scarred flank of a root-buttress, taking all afternoon to travel a yard, matching its pace to the flywheel.

Even decay has been crowded out of its usual appearance. Dead leaves do not fall to rot on a forest floor; there is no floor for them to reach. As soon as a lower frond yellows in the shaded clefts, pale hair-roots emerge directly from the neighboring stems and stitch it flat while it still hangs, drinking it dry and digesting its cellulose in place until only a papery ghost of veins remains, quickly crushed inward by the swelling girth of the living stems around it. Rust works just as slowly on the metal. Iron housings show deep, rounded pitting along their rims, their stamped foundry marks blurred into soft bumps like old wax, yet wherever corrosion cuts too deep, a gray, rubbery callus of cambium creeps over the metal and seals it away from the air under an airtight biological gasket.

As afternoon wears on and the direct patch of sunlight shifts slowly across the basin—sliding off the slow flywheel, lighting up a hanging curtain of bladder-vines until they glow like green lanterns, catching the wet brass rim of a dial whose needle hasn't twitched from its center mark in years—the pitch of the buried sump-engine drops by half a tone. The leaves cooling in shadow along the eastern wall slacken their pressure by an imperceptible fraction; three yards away, newly lit rosettes stiffen, creaking loudly against their neighbors like wet ship-timbers taking a swell, taking up the load without missing a beat. Water drips, valves sigh, the iron hums, and the whole packed cauldron continues turning sunlight into pressure, and pressure into nothing visible at all.
