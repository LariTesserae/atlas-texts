The chamber is the size of a drydock, or an overturned hull, hollowed out of pale, chalky marl a hundred feet beneath the plain. Yet it is blindingly bright. There is no ceiling in the conventional sense—only a shallow, unbroken dome of translucent selenite and fibrous gypsum that acts as a massive optical diffuser for the sun overhead. Whatever harsh, directional weather occurs on the surface is translated here into a shadowless, milky glare. The stone walls, worn smooth by millennia of settled air and slow chemical sloughing, glow like tallow. Everything is visible in extreme, flat clarity. There are no dark corners. If you hold your hand an inch above the pale dust of the floor, it casts no shadow, only a faint, warm occlusion, like a hand held over milk.

Because the space is small and entirely uninterrupted—no stalactites, no pillars, no rubble heaps—its emptiness feels pressurized. The volume of the room is simply air: warm, breath-temperature, holding the faint, alkaline tang of damp plaster. You can see from the north curve to the south curve in a single glance, and the space between is utterly vacant.

Water never falls here, and it never flows. Instead, the chamber sweats. Along the lower flanks of the bowl, where the marl transitions into a denser, greenish soapstone, moisture beads up through microscopic pores. It gathers over hours into heavy, tepid droplets that lose their grip and slide silently down the smoothed stone, leaving dark, temporary tracks before evaporating back into the bright air or soaking into the silt at the chamber's nadir. Every few yards, a natural dimple in the floor holds a teacup’s worth of mineral-heavy water, clear as lens glass.

Around these damp seams grows the room’s sparse flora. There is too much light for cave-fungi and not enough soil for true surface weeds, so what survives is adapted to glare and stone-sweat. Tough, olive-drab cushions of cushion-moss cling to the soapstone, swollen with hoarded moisture. Rising out of the dry silt at wide, polite intervals are single, leafless stalks—pale green, waxy, segmented like horsetails, standing three or four feet high. At their tips, instead of flowers, they bear wide, papery, translucent fans held horizontally to catch the diffused radiance of the ceiling. They do not sway; there is no wind to move them. Over weeks, they rotate almost imperceptibly on their bases, tracking seasonal shifts in the brightest patches of the gypsum roof. 

For all the starkness of the room, the walls are restless with small, quiet lives. The fauna have claimed the vertical margins, leaving the vast central air empty. Thousands of flat, coin-sized creatures—something between a tortoiseshell beetle and a limpet—crust the mid-levels of the marl. Their carapaces are mirrored, polished to reflect the relentless overhead brightness, so that a patch of wall looks from a distance like hammered tin. They graze on invisible films of endolithic algae that bloom just beneath the skin of the damp rock. They move at the pace of clock hands. Every few minutes, one detaches with a dry *tick* to drop into the silt, rights itself with pale,hair-thin legs, and begins a slow migration toward a damper seam.

Higher up, near the glowing suture where the marl meets the selenite roof, live the weavers—pale, long-legged harvestmen with bodies no larger than dried peas, and blind, velvet-skinned geckos the color of bleached bone. The geckos have no need for pigment or camouflage in a shadowless world; you can see the faint, pinkish shadow of their lungs expanding and contracting through their ribs, and the dark oval of a swallowed beetle passing through their gut. They hunt by heat and vibration, waiting hours beside a weeping pore in the stone for smaller mites to come and drink.

Down in the flat bowl of the floor, the marks of a different presence are pressed into the dry marl-dust, though there are no walls, hearths, or shelters. Near the widest of the green soapstone seams, someone—or several someones, visiting rarely—has worked the stone with patient, rudimentary intent. Shallow, thumb-wide flutes have been gouged into the soft rock using harder, dark river-chert brought down from elsewhere, channeling the stray beads of condensation so they converge into three carved basins, each the size of a melon. Beside the basins sits a pair of those dark chert hand-axes, flaking and dull, alongside half a dozen marrow-bones scraped clean and split lengthways to make awls. 

Stuck upright into the silt around the basins are four long, peeled willow switches—foreign wood from some surface creek—arranged not as a frame, because nothing covers them, and they bear no weight. Tied to their tips with braided plant-fiber are flat, chipped discs of the ceiling’s fallen selenite. They hang motionless in the dead air. Beside them, a small, neat pile of the mirrored beetle-shells has been sorted by size on a flat slab of gypsum, next to a lump of yellow ochre ground flat on one side. Nothing here is lived in; it is a workshop or a pantry or a quiet station left carefully in order. Footprints—unshod, narrow, toe-deep in the fine talc of the floor—lead away from the basins toward a low, horizontal squeeze at the eastern base of the wall, where the stone pinches down into a bright, dusty throat leading elsewhere. Because there is no wind and no rain, those footprints look made ten minutes ago, though the water in the basins has grown a thin, crystalline skin of floating calcite that takes months of absolute stillness to form.

The acoustics of the smooth dome swallow resonance rather than amplifying it, pressing sound flat. Mostly, there is nothing to hear. If you sit beside the stone basins and hold your breath, the quiet resolves into minute, dry punctuation: the soft *shhh-t* of a pinch of marl-dust detaching from the wall and sliding an inch down the slope; the faint, crisp click of mirrored shells bumping edges as two grazers pass one another; the slow, percussive *plip*—once every four or five minutes—of a single fat drop finally overbalancing the lip of a carved groove and breaking the calcite skin of the basin. Above it all, or perhaps only imagined because the light is so pervasive, there seems to be a vast, silent thermal hum, as the giant lens of stone overhead drinks the afternoon heat of an unseen sky and radiates it steadily downward into the bowl.
