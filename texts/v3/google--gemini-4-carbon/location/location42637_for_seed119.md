There is no earth underfoot here, and there is no sky overhead, though rain still gets in. 

The entire world is a single hollow volume—a broad, bell-shaped vault perhaps seventy paces from wall to wall and nearly as tall at its crown, enclosed on every side by hand-dressed stone. Nothing stands in the middle of it. There are no columns, no partitions, no fallen heaps of rubble to break up the interior. The sheer, uninterrupted emptiness of the air inside the shell presses on the eyes. When you stand near the curved perimeter and look across, the far wall hangs behind a thin suspension of cool mist, dim and pewter-colored, separated from you by nothing at all except sixty yards of open, clammy space and whatever happens to be flying across it at the moment.

Every surface was laid by hand, long ago, with tools no harder than river mauls, antler wedges, and sand-water saws. The lower courses are blocks of dark greywacke the size of crouched oxen, their faces still bearing the faint, overlapping dimples of stone hammers where the masons bruised them into shape. Higher up, as the walls lean inward in a massive corbelled dome, the stone shifts to lighter, flatter slabs of greenstone and slate, stepped inward thumb-breadth by thumb-breadth until they meet at the distant apex. Even the floor is wholly fabricated: millions of oval basalt cobbles wedged upright on their narrow ends, packed tight without metal or modern cement, held fast in a bed of ancient lime-ash and crushed shell that has calcified over centuries into a continuous, undulating crust.

Age has taken the crispness off all of it without threatening to bring it down. The room has settled into its permanent posture. Where water has run for generations down the stepped corbels, the sharp slate ledges have been licked smooth into dark, bulbous tongues, glazed with mineral deposits the color of tallow. Grooves meant to channel runoff laterally around the walls have worn through their lips ages ago, spilling water down unintended faces until those faces, too, hollowed out into shallow flutes. The cobbled floor dips toward the center like a shallow dish, polished slick as wet horn by friction and constant damp.

Light enters only at second and third hand. High up near the curve of the ceiling, six deep rectangular shafts pierce the masonry at steep, crooked angles—baffled chimneys lined with pale, lime-washed slabs designed to catch external daylight and bounce it downward without ever admitting a direct line of sight to the clouds. What arrives in the vault is a sourceless, slate-blue dusk. It gleams coldly on the wet curve of the walls and turns the thin film of water on the floor into a dull pewter mirror, leaving the upper recesses in grainy shadow. The air smells of wet slate, cold ammonia, crushed shell, and cold river-weed. It sits just above a chill—cool enough that a warm animal’s breath ghosts pale grey for a second before vanishing into the draft.

Because the room is an empty stone bell, nothing happens quietly. A single drop detaching from the crown fifty feet up hits a puddle on the cobbles with a sharp, metallic *plink* that ricochets three times around the curve of the walls. And there is never just a single drop. Thousands of beads of condensation and seep-water patter steadily down from the stepped stone courses, merging into a continuous, restless hiss like frying fat. 

Built into the upper half of the dome, mortared horizontally right into the courses of greenstone so their open mouths face the empty center of the room, are hundreds upon hundreds of coarse, low-fired earthenware jars. Whoever laid the vault set them there as acoustic traps or lightening pockets in the masonry, their rims pinched by human thumbs before firing. Time has cracked a third of them and rimmed the rest in crusts of white lime, and they have transformed the chamber into an instrument. Whenever wind moves across the hidden exterior mouths of the light-chimneys outside, air pressure shifts inside the vault, and the hundreds of clay mouths begin to moan—a low, braided chord of hollow fifths and minor thirds that swells and dies away through the damp twilight.

The animals use the jars, and the jars amplify the animals.

Every intact vessel above thirty feet is occupied. Small, heavy-breasted birds with oil-dark plumage, bristle-feathered faces, and oversized black eyes—creatures somewhere between a swift and a nightjar—roost tail-outward in the terracotta throats. When they call to their mates or challenge a neighbor, they do not call into the open room; they duck their heads inward and shriek directly into the belly of the clay pots. The earthenware catches the high, rasping screech, strips off its thinness, and throws it back out into the vault as a startling, chest-rattling *boom-chatter* that overlaps from wall to wall in rolling waves of sound. Dozens are always in the air at once, banking tight circles around the empty interior, never landing on the floor, snapping up the thick clouds of cold-tolerant midges and stoneflies that dance in the downdrafts beneath the light-shafts. 

Out of the sparse green smudges near those upper shafts—where a little pale liverwort, black lichen, and starved tufts of bone-white sedge manage to cling to pockets of old guano—pale roots dangle six or eight feet straight into empty air, drinking mist. Clinging to those roots, and flattened against the weeping vertical greywacke below, are creatures the size of human hands with wide, cartilaginous rib-flaps: mottled grey vault-newts. They are silent where the birds are deafening. Every minute or two, one peels its suctioned toes from a slick stone high on the east curve, spreads itself flat as a dry leaf, and glides silently downward across forty yards of open twilight, banking slightly on the cool drafts to slap wetly onto the opposite wall a few feet above the floor, scattering a burst of tiny, translucent springtails.

Down on the dished cobble pavement, the rhythm of life is governed by the flushes.

Low in the north wall, three shoulder-high conduits of Fitted sandstone blocks penetrate the masonry, capped with hinged wooden clack-valves pinned with greasy, yellowed whalebone—simple counterweighted gates darkened and petrified by centuries of immersion. Behind the walls, unseen catchment basins gather groundwater or rain from whatever lies outside. Roughly three times an hour, the hidden weight of that water overcomes one of the bone-pinned gates. With a heavy, shuddering *clunk* that echoes through the soles of the feet, the timber slab swings upward, and a thick, twisting rope of cold, frothy water vomits out across the cobbles.

Before the water even hits the stone, the floor clears. Hundreds of pale, copper-colored crustaceans—spindly things with bodies no bigger than walnuts on legs as long and thin as knitting needles—stop picking at fallen feathers and algal film, lift their bodies high on their tiptoes, and skate sideways up the gentle slope of the basin walls with a dry, synchronized clatter like handfuls of flung gravel. 

The surge hisses across the dish of the floor, a hand’s-breadth deep, spinning drifted wing-quills and flecks of mortar toward the dead center of the chamber. There, the cobbles give way to a wide circular ring of thick slate slabs, each slab laboriously sawed and drilled with rows of wrist-wide drainage holes. As the flush pours roaring down the perforations into the black under-channels beneath the floor, the hunters hidden below come up to meet it. Long, muscular, eel-bodied things with blunt, shovel-shaped snouts and tiny eyes set far back on armored skulls thrust half their slick, olive-spotted lengths straight up out of the drain holes against the sucking current. They whip their heads blindly across the wet cobbles in a three-foot radius around the grates, jaws snapping shut with wet *clacks* on any long-legged crab slow to skate upslope, or on stunned insects washed down from the walls, or occasionally on a low-skimming bird that misjudged the height of the spray.

For two minutes the center of the vault is a loud, churning thrash of white water, booming clay pots overhead, whistling wings, and slick olive bodies wrestling over the slate grates. 

Then the hidden basin outside empties. The water-head drops; the heavy timber clack-valve slams shut in its stone seat with a dull *thud* that sets the upper jars humming briefly in sympathy. The sheet of water thins to a sheen, gurgles, and sucks away down the drilled holes with a long, throat-clearing rattle. The eel-things slide backward out of sight into the flooded dark beneath the masonry, leaving only wet rings of slime around the slate apertures. 

Within seconds, before the dripping walls have retaken command of the room's acoustics, the spindly crabs come tiptoeing rapidly back down the curved slope of the cobbles from every direction, tapping the slick grooves between the stones with hair-fine antennae to claim whatever fresh detritus the culvert washed in. High overhead, three pale newts launch at once through a slant of dim grey shaft-light, crossing paths silently in the immense, cool hollow of the room, while six tiers up, a bird ducks its head into an ancient clay jar and booms into the damp.
