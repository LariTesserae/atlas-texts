The chamber is an abrupt, vertical tear in young basalt, no wider than a city square and perhaps twice as tall as an old-growth pine. Its walls are unweathered, fractured along crisp geometric planes that look as though the earth split open last Tuesday, leaving sharp flinty overhangs and clean, unrounded corners. There is no sky here. What serves as a ceiling is a solid plug of dark, glassy rock, sealed tight against whatever surface world sits forty yards above, pierced only by bundles of heavy copper conduit and thick iron Sweat-pipes descending from the dark.

Within this tight geological pocket, an entire industrial ward has been packed with suffocating precision. There is no open ground, no plaza, no empty air. Catwalks of stamped steel, brick-lined boiler housings, narrow tenement-boxes made of corrugated tin, and immense cast-iron pressure vessels are wedged directly against the jagged stone and stacked atop one another like luggage in a hold. To move five yards horizontally requires climbing three flights of spiral iron stairs, ducking beneath the belly of a vulcanized rubber cistern, and squeezing sideways between a hot brick chimney and a sheer blade of raw basalt.

The heat is tremendous, thick and motionless, pressing against the skin like hot tallow. It radiates endlessly from the machinery—though nothing here clatters or roars. The industry of the pit operates on slow, thermal pressure rather than violent combustion. Enormous, riveted tanks sit wrapped in yellowing asbestos blankets, quietly stewing chemical baths or drawing up deep geothermal brine through silent, heavy valves. Thick brass gauges tremble infinitesimally over the course of an afternoon. Great flywheels, ten feet across and slick with dark grease, turn at the pace of an hour hand, driven by silent hydraulic rams that take forty minutes to complete a single stroke. 

Because there is no wind and no escape for the heat, the air holds a permanent, heavy dampness—not a wet mist, and never rain, just an oppressive sweat. Water beads endlessly on the cold basalt walls where groundwater seeps through hairline fractures, immediately warming until it trickles down the sharp stone faces in tepid films. It collects on the undersides of iron girders, gathering at the lowest point of bolt-heads before dropping—*plink*—onto broad, waxy leaves below. 

The flora here has seized every square inch that the ironworkers left behind, and much that they didn't, turning the packed machinery into an armature for a dense, suffocating conservatory. Because the light is perpetually dim—provided only by hundreds of low-wattage, amber-filament bulbs caged in thick glass, and the faint, dull-cherry glow of banked heating elements—the plants have grown dark and strange to drink every available photon. Huge, velvety leaves the color of bruised plums and dried blood drape over the handrails. Pale, leafless vines as thick as mooring ropes twine directly around hot copper steam-pipes, feeding directly on the radiant warmth rather than light, their bark-like skins blistered and tough. Deep in the cramped crevices between turbine housings and rock walls, clusters of translucent, bladder-like ferns swell with trapped condensation, glowing faintly murky-orange when an electric bulb shines through them. 

The strangeness of the place lies in the marriage of this flora with the slow machinery, and the utter absence of visible operators. If people live in the stacked tin rooms with their louvered shutters and rusted balconies, they keep behind closed doors, perhaps sleeping away the crushing heat, or moving through the interior crawlspaces rather than the exterior catwalks. Yet the ward is clearly tended. Fresh grease glistens on the slow-turning gears. Copper wire has been carefully used to trellis heavy, night-blooming gourds away from pressure-release vents. Here and there, glass carboys filled with clear, viscous fluid sit strapped to steel grates, connected by rubber surgical tubing directly into the thick stems of bloated, barrel-shaped cycads—whether to feed the plants from the machines, or to tap the plants to lubricate the engines, it is impossible to tell.

Sound dies instantly in the packed mass of foliage and iron. There are no echoes; the dense tapestry of dark leaves and rusted surfaces swallows noise whole. You hear only the microscopic sounds of high heat and slow tension: the soft *tick* of cooling metal contracting a fraction of a millimeter; the wet, rubbery creak of a heavy vine shifting its weight as a flywheel carries it upward an inch an hour; a faint, subterranean hum felt more in the teeth than the ears, vibrating up through the soles of your boots from the deep pipes.

Living creatures are sparse, sluggish, and adapted to the dark warmth. Once in an hour, a pale, blind millipede the length of a man's forearm will uncoil from around a warm porcelain insulator, its shell gleaming like oiled ivory, and flow silently into a seam in the basalt. Near the lowest sumps—narrow, shadowed trenches at the base of the pit where black oil and warm runoff pool beneath blankets of pale green duckweed—a few heavy, shell-less snails the size of fists graze on mineral crusts, their bodies pulsing faintly with the slow rhythm of the hydraulic rams above them. 

Nothing hurries. A drop of brown condensation forms on the lip of a brass pressure-valve, swells slowly in the amber gloom, reflects the upside-down tangle of pipes and black-purple leaves, and finally lets go, striking the casing of a warm dynamo with a tiny, instantaneous hiss that vanishes before the next drop begins to gather.
