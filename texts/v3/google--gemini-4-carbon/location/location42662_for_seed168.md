There are no riverbeds here, not even dry ones. That is the first thing the eye struggles to correct as it scans the basin: the complete absence of drainage geometry. Where sixty square miles of high, tilted tableland ought to gather into rills, gullies, and alluvial fans, the rock simply breaks along pristine crystallographic planes. It is newly risen crust—pale grey andesite, bands of raw anorthosite, and long, glassy seams of black tachylite—pushed nearly nine thousand feet into clear, warm air and left untouched by a single drop of rain. Every edge is fresh enough to cut leather. Step onto a talus slope and the shards beneath your boot have no dust rounding their corners; they lock together like clean-broken porcelain, still sharp from the tectonic heave that split them.

Above this raw floor, the space opens out to an immense, dizzying emptiness. Thin, motionless air fills a wide depression rimmed by jagged, flat-topped scarps twenty miles away, visible in every hairline detail under bright, unremarkable daylight. The sun hangs in a pale blue sky stripped of haze and cloud, laying down crisp shadows that never soften at their edges. It is pleasantly, evenly warm—the steady warmth of sun-baked stone meeting mild geothermal heat bleeding slowly upward from the young rock. Nothing blows across the expanse. No dust devils rise. Because there is no moisture to drive weather and very little thermal differential between one ridge and the next, the atmosphere sits over the plateau like clear glass in a kiln that has been held at a single gentle temperature for centuries.

Out of these bone-dry, unweathered fissures grows an extravagant abundance of plant life—and woven directly through the plants, completely unhoused, sits heavy industrial machinery.

Neither was built into a landscape of structures. There are no concrete pads, no corrugated roofs, no service roads, fences, pylons, or footprints. Instead, fifty-ton induction transformers with bare, ribbed cooling fins rest wedged directly into raw splits of black volcanic glass, miles apart across the open shelf. Between them, suspended over vast gulfs of empty canyon air, thick cables of braided aluminum and copper sag in kilometer-long catenaries. There are no towers holding them up; they anchor straight into raw rock pinnacles via fused mineral plugs, and along their entire suspended length they are smothered in lush, dry foliage.

The vegetation thrives without a trace of water, running on a slow, solid-state metabolism driven by warm crustal gases, bright sunlight, and direct electrical current bled off the cables. Because the basin is so spacious and empty—miles of clear, dry air separating each outcropping and each suspended span—the flora grows into huge, airy, hyper-extended forms that claim tremendous volume with almost no mass. From the lip of a chasm, curtains of pale olive and celadon fronds hang four hundred feet straight down into open space, each leaf broad as a sail yet thin as tracing paper, translucent in the sun, structured from dry, flexible organo-silicates that never wilt because they contain no sap to lose. Around the naked cast-iron casings of the transformers, thicker growth mounds up in opulent, velvet-textured banks: waist-high rosettes of tarnished-gold blades, spiraling lattices of dry, woody pith, and wide canopy fans that overlap like shingled brass. 

Touch a leaf and it feels warm, smooth, and dry as talc, yielding softly before springing back into exact position. Brush through a thicket and nothing rises—no gnats, no beetles, no spiders, no spores riding damp air. There has never been an animal here. Without herbivores, the leaves bear no thorns, no bitter alkaloids, no ragged bite-marks along their margins. Without pollinators, there are no blossoms and no fruit. Every frond is whole, geometrically flawless, held in quiet suspension. When old growth reaches the limit of its span, it does not rot—there is no moisture for fungi or bacteria to work with. It simply cross-links into rigid, airy lace, bleaching to the pale silver of birch bark while new green-gold shoots thread cleanly through its open latticework.

The machinery operates in the same state of untended, silent equilibrium. Walk two miles across clean, sharp stone slabs to one of the valley’s sparse mechanical concentrations, and you find an unshielded flywheel assembly the size of a threshing engine sitting naked among waist-deep plumes of dry fern. There is no housing around it, no nameplate, no walkways, no grease-stained gravel—just dark, machined steel resting on leveled tabs of raw rock, spinning at high speed on frictionless magnetic bearings. Because nothing rubs and no wind buffets the blades, it makes almost no noise at all. Stand three paces away in the bright sunlight and all you hear is a faint, steady sixty-cycle hum in your teeth and the occasional microscopic *tick* of a cooling fin expanding a thousandth of an inch in the noon warmth.

Thin, root-like tendrils from the surrounding plants press directly against the bare copper busbars emerging from the dynamo’s base. At the point of contact, the plant tissue darkens into carbonized, conductive pads, sipping low-amperage current straight out of the grid to split dry carbon dioxide and sulfur compounds vented from hairline cracks in the rock below. In return, the root mats wick excess heat out of the iron casings and disperse it through thousands of square yards of broad, shaded canopy. Whether the engines were dropped into the nascent plateau to feed the engineered flora, or the flora was seeded to cool and insulate an open-air electrical array extracting heat from the cooling crust, the distinction no longer holds. They form a single, sparse, self-regulating circuit stretched across the valley.

From a high, jagged bench near the eastern rim, you can take in thirty miles of the basin at once. Most of what you see is sheer open volume: sharp, pale plains of newly split rock, immense pockets of crystal-clear air between widely spaced monoliths, and the clean, bright sky. Threading that emptiness are the long, graceful swoops of green-draped cable, casting thin, stationary lines of shadow across the raw stone far below, linking distant islands of lush, olive-colored foliage where bare iron rotors glint calmly in the sun. Nothing stirs the leaves. No clouds cross the light. The warmth stays fixed on the skin, dry and clean and faintly scented with warm resin and ozone, while the whole wide plateau holds its quiet, steady charge.
