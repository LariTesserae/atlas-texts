It is no larger than an amphitheater, dropped like a sunken thumbprint into the bleached, flat salt-crust of the outer flats. You do not see it until your boots are already breaking the lip of its rim, because the rim does not rise; the ground simply gives way into a steep, bowl-shaped hollow perhaps eighty paces across and forty deep. 

What hits you first is not the drop, nor the glare, nor the sudden furnace breath of warm, dry air rising from the pit. What hits you is the sound. 

It is an immense, unceasing, metallic thrumming—a wall of bright, percussive roar so dense it presses against the eardrums and rattles the cartilage in your nose. Down in this pocket, a whole city has been built, and the city is an instrument being played at blinding volume by the hot updrafts.

The space itself is mostly empty air. The hollow is carved out of pale, chalky anhydrite and gypsum, worn smooth by centuries of dry thermal scours into wide, curving ledges. Across this steep bowl, clinging to the perimeter walls and slung across the open central void, is a startlingly dense, multi-tiered settlement—though not one made for large bodies, or perhaps not occupied right now, for nothing walks its streets. Everything is miniature, intricate, and stacked: thousands of terraced dwellings, watch-towers, granary-cones, and stepped plazas carved directly from the soft white stone or mortared together out of bone-dry clay, mica-schist, and translucent flakes of selenite. Narrow, precarious viaducts—built not for water, which does not exist here, but for dust or rolling stones—span the empty center of the bowl on spindly, tensioned arches of split reed and sinew. Because the structures hug the curved walls and only thread across the middle on hair-thin bridges, ninety percent of the hollow’s volume is sheer, glaring emptiness. You look straight through the lattice of aqueducts and rigging to the bright white stone on the far side, forty yards away.

The light is ferocious. High overhead, the sky is a cloudless, brassy white, and the bowl acts as a parabolic mirror. Sunlight strikes the gypsum walls, bounces off thousands of mortared selenite window-panes and polished slate roofs, and floods the undersides of the bridges. There are almost no shadows here, only varying intensities of pale gold and blinding chalk-glare. The air shimmers with dry, baking warmth, smelling faintly of toasted chitin, hot flint, and ozone.

And clinging to every masonry joint, spilling over the parapets of the tiny plazas, clothing the underside of the bone-dry viaducts in thick, trembling tapestries, is the foliage. 

Without a drop of moisture in the ground or sky, the lushness here is entirely dry, mineral, and aero-trophic. Broad-leaved fans of pale copper and oxidized-bronze color sprout in overlapping rosettes from the mortar between clay bricks. Their leaves are stiff as parchment, papery and hollow, reinforced with silica ribs. Beside them hang dense, cascading curtains of silver-green vine, leafless and jointed, coated in microscopic glassy hooks designed to comb floating salt-dust and static charge directly out of the rising thermal currents. There are bulbous, melon-sized growths wedged into roof corners that look like tarnished brass lanterns; periodically, their pleated skins flex open and snap shut with a sharp *crack*, gulping warm air to filter out airborne sulfur and drifting spores. 

This lush, bone-dry jungle is half the source of the deafening noise. As the fierce sunlight bakes the gypsum floor at the very bottom of the pit, a continuous, powerful column of hot air rushes upward through the hollow. As it tears past the terraced city, it sets the dry flora into violent vibration. Millions of stiff, silica-ribbed leaves strike against one another like castanets and sheets of tin. Hollow-stemmed reed thickets growing along the lower ledges whistle in discordant, shrieking harmonics. 

The builders of the settlement did not dampen this racket; they harnessed it. Everywhere among the clay towers and stepped streets are primitive, hand-carved wind-works. Hundreds of wooden paddle-wheels, lashed together with rawhide and greased with dry graphite, spin furiously in the updrafts, their unlubricated stone axles screaming in high, steady pitches. Long lines of hammered copper disks and fired-clay bells hang beneath the suspension bridges, thrashing together in a continuous, shattering clatter. Great sails of woven plant-parchment snap like pistol shots as they haul tiny woven baskets of powdered mineral up vertical guidelines from the pit floor to the upper tiers, tripped by wooden ratchets that *clack-clack-clack* faster than the eye can follow. The whole bowl is a roaring mechanical and botanical resonator, an acoustic furnace where wind, dry leaves, clay chimes, and wooden gears merge into a single, overwhelming, golden-lit din.

Everything is weathered into deep familiarity with everything else. Wind-blown grit has softened the sharp edges of the miniature stepped pyramids and polished the wooden wind-vanes to a silky gray sheen. Where old masonry has cracked, the silica-vines have stitched it back together, their woody, desiccated roots acting as fresh rebar before calcifying into stone themselves. Beneath the spinning ratchet-wheels, smooth grooves are worn an inch deep into the gypsum sills by generations of the same swinging stone counterweights.

Movement is constant—wheels blurring, vines whipping, parchment sails puffing and dumping their loads of pale dust down stone chutes—yet living creatures are almost wholly absent. You can stare through the dazzling emptiness between the bridges for twenty minutes and see nothing with eyes. 

Then, a shadow detaches itself from a cluster of bronze-colored fan leaves. A single creature, no bigger than a human forearm, glides across the central gulf on taut membranes of translucent yellow skin. It has no mouth for drinking, only wide gill-slits along its ribs to strain dust from the hot gale, and oversized, bony plates clamped flat over where its ears should be. It lands on a clay parapet right beside a clanging row of copper disks, utterly indifferent to the sound that could rattle mortar loose. With hooked, chitinous forelimbs, it pries open one of the breathing brass-lantern plants, snatches a small, armored beetle from inside the dry pleated cavity, crunches it once—a tiny, silent motion swallowed instantly by the thunder of the hollow—and launches itself back out into the bright, empty, deafening gulf.

Step ten paces back from the rim, out onto the flat salt plain, and the curvature of the earth swallows the acoustic cone almost instantly. The metallic roar drops to a muffled hum beneath your soles, and once again there is nothing in sight from horizon to horizon—only the dead, silent crust, hiding a pocket-sized, sun-struck city screaming to itself in the heat.
