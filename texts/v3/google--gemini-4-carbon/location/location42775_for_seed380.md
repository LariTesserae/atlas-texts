The chamber is the size of an overturned cathedral, sunk four hundred feet into grey limestone, though almost none of the original stone remains visible. Instead, the walls are a continuous, tiered masonry of glazed white brick, cast-iron pilasters, and heavy copper busbars greened to the color of stagnant pond-weed. It was built to be a municipal pressure-exchange—an urban lung of cisterns, accumulators, and step-down transformers—and though no hand has turned a valve here in centuries, the machinery has not stopped. It has merely slowed down to match the tempo of the groundwater.

Everything weeps. The air hangs around forty-eight degrees, so saturated with cold vapor that you do not feel wet until you move, at which point the invisible suspension collects instantly on skin and cloth. Condensation beads on the bell-housings of dormant turbines, runs in thin, shining ribbons down the tiled retaining walls, and gathers in shallow, level basins that occupy most of the floor. The water is clear and heavy with dissolved calcium. Over generations of patient, uninterrupted dripping, the industrial architecture has grown a secondary geology. Stalagmites of smooth, ivory flowstone rise from the slotted iron grates; mineral draperies hang from the undersides of overhead gantry cranes, freezing their chains and hooks in milky stone. Corners have been rounded out. Boltheads have softened into featureless nubs. Rust and lime have married into an ochre rind that seals the seams of great, riveted boilers, making them look less like fabricated vessels and more like boulders left behind by an underground glacier.

Despite the density of the surrounding infrastructure—the tiered galleries, the switchboard alcoves, the sunken sluiceways—the middle of the vault is largely empty. There is a great deal of dark, damp room between things. Three colossal flywheels stand at generous intervals down the central nave, their lower thirds submerged in black, mirror-flat water. They are still turning, driven by some deep, hydrostatic differential in the strata below, but so slowly that at first glance they appear locked in place. Only if you watch a specific watermark on the rim do you realize it is rising, millimeter by millimeter, lifting a thin film of moisture that sheets back down into the trough without breaking the surface tension. A single revolution takes twenty minutes.

Light comes from the electrical system, which has degraded into a low, steady leak. High along the vaulted brick ceiling, glass-bulbed mercury rectifiers and long, gas-filled induction tubes still draw current from subterranean thermal piles. Their output has dropped to a bruised, submarine violet and a pale, sputterless amber. The glow doesn’t travel far through the mist; it haloes the fixtures and glints off the wet flanks of the pipes, leaving the far corners of the galleries in soft, charcoal obscurity. Where bare copper wiring has frayed and touched the wet limestone, electrolytic mineral crusts have formed—delicate, branching corals of glowing blue-green sulfate that fizz with a faint, cold luminescence of their own.

Because the light is starved, little grows. Here and there, rooted in the black silt that has settled into the corners of iron stair-treads, stand solitary tufts of wire-sedge, their blades stiff and dark as piano wire. Pale, translucent liverworts trace the mortar lines where mineral water seeps brightest, and occasionally a single, tall fern—completely devoid of chlorophyll, waxy white as candle tallow—unfurls near the warmth of a live transformer housing, feeding in some obscure way on the ozone and the stray magnetic fields. 

What the chamber lacks in foliage, it makes up for in quiet, teeming animal life. The surfaces are alive, though nothing hurries. 

Across the glazed brick walls move thousands of flat, saucer-sized gastropods with shells coiled out of tarnished brass and iron oxide, scraped and metabolized from the decaying pipework. They graze on the thin bacterial slicks that coat the masonry, leaving clean, geometric tracks across the soot and lime—slowly, methodically polishing one square foot of tile over the course of an afternoon before sliding onward. 

In the shallow floor-basins, blind, colorless amphibians the length of a human forearm drift suspended an inch above the flooded grates. Their external gills are vast, branching plumes of pale pink filament, spread wide to catch the scarce oxygen in the still water. Beside them, long-legged water-treaders—insects with bodies no larger than a thumbnail, held aloft on hair-thin legs spanning a foot across—stand motionless on the meniscus, waiting hours for a drop of condensation to knock a cave-midge from the ceiling.

Most peculiar are the inhabitants of the electrical alcoves. Clustered around the humming, warm porcelain insulators are colonies of spindle-crabs whose carapaces are encrusted with stolen copper windings and shards of mica. They sit in dense, overlapping tiers, entirely still except for their long, feathery antennae, which they wave slowly through the ionized air around the busbars, harvesting static charge the way reef-creatures filter plankton. Every few minutes, a tiny blue arc snaps harmlessly between two crabs; neither flinches. 

Above them, tucked into the recessed coffers of the ceiling Vault, hang rows of heavy, grey-furred bats—or things very much like bats—that never leave the chamber. There is no sky to fly into; the intake shafts above were choked with rubble and roots ages ago, filtering the surface world down to mere seepage. These flyers drop from the ceiling singly, glide on broad, silent membranes down the length of the three-hundred-foot hall, skim their lower jaws across the water to scoop up floating crustacea, and haul themselves back up the chains of the gantry cranes using hooked thumbs, making no more sound than a damp cloth dragged over stone.

Sound here has no sharp edges. The acoustic space should ring with echoes, given the tile and water and iron, yet the thick air and the crust of lime swallow any sudden report, and nothing makes a sudden report anyway. There is the deep, sub-audible fifty-cycle hum of the buried transformers—felt more in the back teeth and the sternum than heard by the ears. There is the occasional, liquid *plock* of a heavy drop falling thirty feet from a pipe-flange into a catch-basin, sending concentric rings sliding lazily out toward the tiled curbs, lifting the giant water-treaders in a gentle, silent swell. There is the faint, dry rasp of a hundred brass-shelled snails scraping mineral paste from a cast-iron pillar. And beneath it all, once every twenty minutes, as a counterweight clears a submerged cam beneath the floor, a muffled, hydraulic sigh passes through the walls—the whole small, sunken city-block shifting an ounce of pressure from left to right, exhaling cold mist through a brass vent, and settling back into its patient equilibrium.
