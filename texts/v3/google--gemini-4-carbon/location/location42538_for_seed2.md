The bottom is poured concrete, miles of it, cured in immense flat slabs whose expansion joints run straight toward every horizon until they vanish into the blue-gray haze. Above, there is no sky—only the underside of the surface, far overhead, or perhaps a ceiling of riveted hull-plate so high that the light diffusing down from it behaves like an overcast noon. Between the floor and that distant upper boundary lies an ocean of cold, clear, moving water, wholly contained, wholly built, and large enough to have its own tides.

Because the basin is young—filled perhaps ten or twenty years ago, a blink in geological or structural time—the edges of everything are still crisp. Where a step-wall drops another hundred feet into a secondary sump channel, the corner of the concrete is a clean ninety-degree angle. You can read the grain of the wooden formwork pressed into the gray cement, and the stamped serial numbers on the cast-iron sluice housings look as though they came out of the foundry last winter. Rust exists only as bright orange blooms around the collars of high-tensile bolts, weeping thin, vertical streaks upward or sideways depending on the pull of the pumps. Nothing is softened yet. Nothing has slumped into silt.

To look outward here is to confront an almost dizzying emptiness. The volume of water is staggering, an interior sea occupying millions of cubic acres, yet almost nothing interrupts it. Three miles off, a vertical intake tower rises from the slab—a cylinder of reinforced masonry two hundred feet wide, flanked by steel guide-rails—and between here and there, there is simply cold water. Visibility stretches remarkably far because there is no mud floor to stir up, only washed aggregate and steel. Distant structures don't blur so much as step backward in pale, monochrome silhouettes: a manifold station five miles north reduced to a faint geometric smudge of slate-blue; a diagonal tension-cable the thickness of a freight train slicing across the mid-water, entering the visual field from nowhere and exiting into pale aqua nothingness. 

Light pours down through this open volume in broad, shifting shafts. Along the distant upper reaches—installed either on massive submerged gantries just beneath the waterline or burning down through acres of thick wired glass from dry galleries above—banks of heavy industrial arc-lamps and high-output sodium floods work alongside channeled daylight. The result is bright, utilitarian, and cool. Shadows on the concrete floor thirty fathoms down are diffuse, pale gray rectangles that sway slightly as surface chops and internal currents bend the rays. You can see clearly for half a mile in any direction, watching dust-motes of lime and micro-bubbles drift horizontally at a steady four knots.

And it is loud. Water carries everything, and a steel-and-concrete basin acts like the soundboard of an instrument the size of a state. There is no quiet anywhere in the column. Beneath your feet, felt through the water against the ribs and jawbone, runs a permanent, fifty-cycle mechanical hum—hundreds of megawatts of turbine and impeller shaft turning inside dry casemates behind the retaining walls. Over that baseline rumble rides the shifting, heavy roar of volume transfer. Miles away, a twenty-foot gate-valve cracks open on a worm gear; the scream of its bronze threads travels instantly through the water as a clear, metallic singing, followed ten seconds later by the freight-train rush of pressurized outflow colliding with still water. Pressure-relief vents burp gouts of compressed air from the floor plates with percussive, booming *thwumps* that send silver mushroom-clouds of bubbles racing toward the distant ceiling, expanding and tearing apart into fizzing curtains as they climb.

This moving, engineered hydrology acts just like weather. When the eastern pump-batteries cycle up in the afternoon, a cold front arrives: water drawn from deeper, unlit subterranean reservoirs pushes across the bright flats in a shimmering, denser wedge, dropping the ambient temperature by eight degrees in three minutes. Where that cold sheet collides with warmer return-water spilling down from condenser outfalls high up on the walls, genuine underwater squalls break out—rolling, visible shear-zones of refractive distortion and violent eddies that spin stray debris into slow, wide cyclones half a mile across before smoothing out again into laminar drift.

Into this spare, roaring, brightly lit geometry, life has poured through the intake screens and made itself native.

Plant life has barely gained a foothold on the vertical smooth concrete—there is no soil, and the currents scour too clean—so vegetation remains sparse and opportunistic. Long, tough ribbons of olive-brown kelp anchor themselves strictly to bolt-heads, ladder rungs, and the expansion seams between slabs, streaming straight out sideways like stiff pennants in a gale. Patches of bright green filamentous algae cling to the leeward sides of steel H-beams where the flow slackens, trembling constantly. Across five hundred yards of bare gray floor, there might be only one ragged line of brown weed tracing a seam, waving in unison.

The animals need no soil at all. They hang in the empty spaces like smoke in an auditorium. 

Halfway up the water column, shimmering silver clouds of herring-like baitfish—tens of thousands to a school—orient themselves to the current lines, holding station just up-flow from the steel cross-bracing of pipe bridges. They turn all at once when the intake valves shift pitch, flashing white-bellied against the pale blue distance, then vanishing edge-on as the school pivots. Beneath them, cruising along the bright concrete flats where dropped rivets and gravel accumulate against three-foot baffle walls, schools of heavy-shouldered bottom feeders—carp-colored, thick-scaled things, alongside fast, streamlined river-cod and striped bass—work the eddies. Predators use the architecture with absolute precision. Barracuda-shaped pike and packs of fast, dark-backed mackerel hang motionless inside the crisp rectangular shadows cast by overhead catwalks, waiting for the pump surges to tumble smaller fish out of the turbulence zones, then darting out into the glare in tight, slashing arcs.

Where the steel structures do concentrate—around a junction manifold where six forty-foot riveted penstocks converge into a concrete blockhouse—life crusts the metal only thinly so far, leaving plenty of black anti-fouling paint still visible. Young blue mussels pack tightly into the angles of the flanges; pale anemones dot the cast-iron valve wheels like wet thumbs. Crabs with long, spindly legs walk briskly across painted warning stencils—*SECTOR 4 LOW SUMP / CLEARANCE 80 FT*—unbothered by the deafening, rhythmic *clack-boom, clack-boom* of a non-return flapper gate swinging inside the pipe just beneath their claws. 

Occasionally, down out of the bright ceiling-haze, diving birds appear from whatever air-space exists at the very top—cormorants or grebes kicking hard down fifty, sixty feet along the vertical anchor chains, trailing strings of silver air from sleek black feathers, snapping fish out of the eddy behind an I-beam before shooting back upward toward the silver-white skin of the roof. 

Then a distant siren—actually a submerged acoustic horn used to clear maintenance diver zones or signal a lock cycle—sounds three flat, brassy notes that ring off every wall for twenty miles. A series of chain-driven louvers high on the western bulkhead slowly slats open, throwing new, sharp ladders of bright light across two square miles of empty water. The current shifts five degrees south. Every strand of kelp along the concrete seams swings quietly to match the new bearing; a quarter-million silver fish tilt their fins in unison against the fresh pull; and the cold, clean water presses steadily onward across the spotless gray floor.
