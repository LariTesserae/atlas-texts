The entire world here is no wider than a courtyard and no taller than a three-story house, sealed beneath the weight of a coastal mire. It is a freshly ruptured pocket inside the subterranean root-mass of a single, colossal bog-tree—a hollow torn open only seasons ago when frost-heave split the living timber underground. Because the tear is young, nothing inside it has been smoothed down. The walls, floor, and vaulted ceiling are composed entirely of jagged, living heartwood and coarse, ropy cambium, splintered into sharp terraces and needle-like overhangs as crisp as broken slate. There is no soil and no stone. Everything underfoot and overhead is dense, frigid wood-flesh, pale under the barkless gloom, its grain still tight and defensive around the wound.

It is nearly pitch black. Only at the highest point of the jagged ceiling, where bundles of tangled surface-roots pull slightly apart, does a faint, bruised-grey seepage of bog-light indicate when it is noon outside; a foot below that pinhole, darkness swallows it completely. What remains to see by is an irregular, chalky dusting of pale-green fungal luminescence clinging to the rawest splinters of wood—just enough to betray wet edges and moving silhouettes after twenty minutes of waiting for the eyes to widen.

The cold is heavy and damp, hovering a few degrees above freezing. Ground-water from the marsh above presses constantly through the living wood, beading along the freshly split fibers like cold sweat before gathering into thin, steady runnels. Vapor hangs in the blackness without rising, condensing onto every sharp wooden spur until it drops.

Across this cramped, pitch-dark chasm stretches an intricate, unmistakably urban architecture—crafted entirely with stone blades, bone awls, plant twine, and thin strips of cold-hammered bog-iron. Whoever built here worked at the scale of a cramped township, organizing the hollow into distinct neighborhoods separated by wide, deliberate expanses of empty black air. Pinned into the sharp wooden ledges are multi-tiered tenements of woven sedge and shaved lath, no larger than dovecotes or crouched huts, connected across the void by taut, narrow suspension bridges of braided hair-moss. There is real municipal infrastructure: tiny, hand-split wooden aqueducts pitched at precise angles to catch the weeping marsh-water from the walls and carry it across the dark open room into tiered, clay-caulked cisterns; miniature retaining walls of notched twigs holding back bulging nodules of fast-growing scar-tissue; ladder-streets descending into the lower clefts; waste-chutes dropping straight into the flooded V-shaped keel at the bottom of the chamber. Between each cluster of ledges and bridges, the central volume of the room is left starkly empty—yards of cold, dark air where nothing hangs, giving the tiny settlement the spaced-out, geometric rigor of an archipelago.

Not a single human or tool-maker is visible, or perhaps the structures were built as livestock-housing from the start, because the entire city and the living wooden cavern around it are utterly, suffocatingly thick with animals.

Every plank, every sharp wooden spire, every underside of every miniature aqueduct is carpeted in cold-adapted, sightless, or night-eyed fauna, layered three and four deep, constantly shifting over one another in the dark. The sheer biomass makes the air immediately over the surfaces feel slightly warmer than the center of the room. Inside the woven sedge tenements and hanging from the rafters of the sluice-gates are thousands upon thousands of large, flightless winter-moths with bodies as fat as thumbs and furred in greasy, waterproof grey pile. They pack themselves into the primitive buildings like shingle-tiles, shivering their stunted wings in slow, synchronized waves that travel down the wicker streets to generate communal heat.

Threading directly through and over the moths are legions of spindly, translucent pale-crabs—no wider than coins, with long, whisker-thin legs adapted for scaling vertical wet wood. They patrol the aqueducts in continuous two-way traffic, grazing on the sweet, freezing sap that bleeds from the root-walls where iron pins were driven in, and picking sluggish parasites off the shivering moths. Below them, in the sharper clefts of the split timber and coiled inside the clay cisterns, lie heaps of eyeless, gelatinous mud-eels and flat-headed black salamanders, tangled together like glistening roots, their throats pulsing slowly in the icy water.

For all this writhing density, the chamber is startlingly quiet. There are no calls, no stridulations, no territorial cries—nothing here wastes calories on noise in the freezing dark. The soundscape is entirely tactile and wet: the steady, hollow *plink* of cold water dropping from wooden stalactites into the cisterns; a dry, continuous rustle like sand pouring through a sieve as ten thousand moth-wings shiver in unison; the faint, brittle ticking of crab-claws against shaved willow; and the occasional wet, soft *slap* of an amphibian dropping from an upper terrace to snatch something off a lower bridge.

The room is never still. It operates like an engine running in complete secrecy. Water seeps through the living roof, hits the primitive gutters, spins tiny, hand-carved wooden paddles set into the sluices—whose only apparent purpose is to agitate the cisterns so a skin of ice cannot form—and drains down into the flooded keel. Along the walls, the giant tree itself is actively trying to heal its split, pushing out slow, pale, bulbous swells of callus-wood that threaten to crush the miniature wicker plazas; and grazing along the edge of that new growth are dense herds of armored, fist-sized woodlice with milky shells, rasping away the fresh scar-tissue as fast as the tree can extrude it, inadvertently keeping the streets clear. Predators move through the crowds without causing panic. Spider-like hunters with spans as wide as dinner plates and bodies no bigger than peas step delicately across the backs of the packed moths, descending down vertical plumb-lines of silk from the sparse suspension bridges, lifting out single crabs or weakened insects with mechanical, unhurried precision.

Stand on one of the broader wooden platforms pinned to the lower wall, and the whole microcosm presses in without ever touching the center of the room: freezing sap dripping onto the shoulders, the faint smell of crushed wintergreen and wet chitin filling the nose, and all around in the blackness, faintly outlined in sour green fungal glow, an intricate city of twigs and sluices breathing and shifting under a living coat of silent mouths and legs.
