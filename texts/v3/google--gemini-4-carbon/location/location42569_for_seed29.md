The whole space is no larger than the interior of a kiln, and you could not stand upright in it even if there were room between the crystal growths to put your feet. 

It is a gas blister, trapped four meters beneath the rubble apron of a high volcanic ridge—five thousand meters above sea level, where the outside air is thin and freezing, though none of that cold reaches here. The rock around it cooled recently enough, in geological time, that nothing has ever softened it. Every surface is embryonic and surgical. There is no soil, no silt, no rounded pebble, not a single grain of sand worn smooth by wind or water. The ceiling is hung with frozen black drips of basaltic glass still sharp enough to slice leather, and the walls are split into pristine contraction cracks lined from edge to edge with dry, fragile needles of orthorhombic sulfur and flat, mirror-bright blades of specular hematite. These crystals have grown inward until they nearly meet at the center, turning the cavity into a dense, interlocking thicket of mineral knives. Nothing fits here without touching three or four things at once.

There is no water. At this altitude and at this temperature—somewhere past fifty-five degrees Celsius, a dry, radiant oven heat rising from a magma dike still cooling far below—liquid moisture cannot persist. What comes up through the hairline fractures in the floor is a dry exhalation of sulfur dioxide, carbon dioxide, and trace hydrogen sulfide, shimmering invisibly in the heat. Instead of condensation, there is sublimation: gas turning directly into solid yellow frost on the slightly cooler upper needles, adding microscopic layers over weeks and months, slowly choking the remaining airspace even further. Down in the narrowest cleft at the base of the pocket, where the temperature climbs higher still, tiny beads of amber-dark molten sulfur sweat directly out of the stone, coalesce with agonizing slowness, and freeze into waxy stalagmites a few centimeters higher up.

It should be silent in a sealed bubble of summit rock, and it should be entirely wild. Instead, the chamber roars like the inside of a running engine block.

Six meters away, straight through solid andesite, an industrial geothermal extraction well plunges two kilometers into the mountain’s flank, part of a sprawling high-altitude power array serving a copper smelter down on the altiplano. Nothing has been built inside this blister; no human eye has seen it, and no tool has shaped its walls. Only once, years ago, a stray directional pilot bit grazed the upper left quadrant of the pocket before being retracted and grouted off elsewhere. Left wedged in that sheared notch is an eight-inch stub of sheared high-carbon alloy drill collar, snapped off in the raw stone and smeared with baked molybdenum grease. 

That metal stub acts as an acoustic bridge and an electrical ground. Through it, and through the dense volcanic rock itself, the relentless industrial thunder of the extraction field pours directly into the cavity. The whole blister acts as a Helmholtz resonator for the machinery next door. High-pressure steam turbines, injection pumps, and deep-rock hydraulic rams fill the tiny space with a continuous, chest-flattening sixty-hertz hum braided with the metallic shriek of stressed steel piping. The sound has nowhere to dissipate; it bounces off the crowded hematite plates until the brittle sulfur needles quiver at their tips, occasionally snapping off with a faint crystalline *tink* that is instantly swallowed by the drone. Stray induced currents bleed off the lodged steel stub into the iron-rich hematite veins, building up slow electrostatic charges in the bone-dry air until, once every few minutes, a hair-thin violet spark snaps across a two-millimeter gap between mineral blades.

Apart from those brief electrostatic ticks and a dull, barely perceptible cherry-brown glow radiating up from the deepest, hottest throat of the floor crack, the chamber is pitch-dark.

And yet it is packed with animals.

There is not a scrap of green life anywhere—no moss, no lichen, no fungus, no stray blown seed from the distant valleys miles below. The base of life here is an invisible, dry crust of chemolithotrophic bacteria coating the warm basalt, drawing meager energy from oxidizing the rising sulfur gases and iron minerals. Feeding on that bacterial crust, and on each other, is a suffocatingly dense congregation of arthropods that found their way in over decades through pinhole thermal vents in the overlying scree. 

Every niche between the sulfur spears and glass stalactites is crammed with bodies. Flat, rust-colored darkling beetles with fused wing-cases wedge themselves three and four deep into the cooler upper crevices, their thick waxy cuticles sealed tight against desiccation, extracting every drop of metabolic moisture they will ever know solely from the dry food they chew. Blind, pale-amber pseudoscorpions anchor themselves to the undersides of hematite blades, pincers held open in the dark, crowded shoulder-to-shoulder like commuters. Tiny armored oribatid mites—thousands upon thousands of them, looking like clustered grains of dark pepper—blanket the bacterial films near the warm vents, piling over one another in slow, seething mats. Thread-legged harvestmen, adapted to the tight geometry, fold their limbs into dense triangles among the glass needles so their joints do not snag on the razor edges.

Because there is no rain, no wind, and no liquid decay, nothing that dies here ever washes away or rots cleanly. The floor of every ledge is packed solid with desiccated husks, shed exoskeletons, hollow beetle thoraxes, and brittle spider legs, all slowly being encased in fresh yellow sulfur frost. Living beetles walk directly over a compact mattress of their ancestors, which crackles beneath their tarsi—a dry, rasping static completely drowned out by the turbine roar coming through the walls. Crystal needles grow straight through empty eye-sockets and pierced carapaces, pinning the dead into the architecture of the room until biology and fresh geology are packed into a single tight conglomerate.

Despite the deafening industrial violence of the sound and the baking heat, almost nothing moves quickly. Speed wastes moisture, and in air this thin—carrying scarcely half the oxygen of sea level, further displaced by heavy volcanic gases pooling in the bottom half-meter—bursts of energy are fatal. Life here operates on the same patient clock as the sublimating sulfur. 

A pseudoscorpion takes four minutes to rotate forty degrees on its hematite perch. The beetles graze across the mineral crust at a pace barely faster than the minute hand of a watch, antennae tapping methodically through the hot dark, navigating entirely by chemical gradients and the steady mechanical vibration vibrating up through their six legs. When the geothermal plant shifts load miles down the power line, changing the pitch of the subterranean pumps by half a tone, the entire massed population pauses at once—tens of thousands of tiny armored bodies locking still in their tight mineral slots until the new resonance settles into the stone. Then, slowly, in the pitch dark and the dry furnace heat, the mandibles begin working against the rock again, scraping bacteria off brand-new glass while yellow crystals lengthen imperceptibly overhead, closing the pocket millimeter by millimeter from the inside out.
