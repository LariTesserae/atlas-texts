At first glance, under the clean, relentless glare of a high-altitude sun, the plateau looks like old sandstone and bleached shale. The horizon sits thirty miles off in every direction, flat and shimmering with heat haze, broken only at vast intervals by solitary, crooked spires that lean into the wind. Nothing crowds the eye. Between one landmark and the next lies half a day’s walk of open, baking expanse.

Then you kneel, press a palm to the ground, and feel the pulse of warm fluid moving slowly beneath a crust of silvered bark.

There is no stone here. There is no soil. For a thousand feet straight down, and across a breadth wide enough to breed its own dry thermal winds, the continent is an unbroken, ancient mass of living wood. Whatever bedrock anchors it lies buried beneath eons of fused trunks, horizontal boughs the width of river valleys, and laminated plates of cork and ironwood that have grown into, over, and around one another until they settled into a topography. 

Time and wind have worked this botanical crust the way they work desert rock elsewhere. Long, scalloped ridges of dense heartwood stand exposed where softer pith rotted or blew away centuries ago. Dry gullies wind through smoothed swells of callus-tissue, floored not with gravel, but with drifts of sun-cured chaff, seed-husks, and grey, papery flakes of shed epidermis that rustle around the ankles. Where the surface stretches flattest, great photosynthetic hides—leathery, olive-black membranes tougher than saddle hide and acres wide—lie taut across sunken vascular basins, soaking up the normal, bright daylight and radiating a dry, resinous heat that smells of turpentine, scorched vinegar, and camphor.

It never truly rains on the tableland, yet the air is never altogether dry. Water lives inside the floor. All through the blistering afternoon, the plateau holds its moisture locked tight under waxen cuticles and foot-thick armor. Then, in the hour before dawn, when the high air cools just enough, the ground exhales. 

Scattered across the plains are breathing-fields: clusters of sunken, lip-like stomata the size of cisterns. With a slow, hydraulic creaking, their woody valves part and release plumes of hot, wet breath. Condensation rolls low across the bark-flats in heavy, warm fogs, beading on every splinter and crease. At the same hour, along the seams where the great dark leaf-hides meet the rougher wood, rows of specialized glands weep thick, tepid droplets of mineral-heavy sap-water. For three hours each morning the entire vast world is slick, steaming, and damp to the touch; shallow depressions hold warm puddles tinted amber with tannins. By mid-morning the valves clamp shut, the sun burns the mist clean away, and only damp dark stains in the deeper fissures remain.

If the landscape itself is spare and wide open, the animal life upon it is staggeringly thick—and the noise it makes is physical, relentless, and nearly unbearable.

A continent of hollow vascular tubes, dry wood, and stretched membranes is a continent built like a musical instrument, and everything alive here plays it. Beneath the surface, inside dry, abandoned xylem channels that run for miles like cathedral pipes, millions of pale, forearm-sized borers rasp their serrated hind legs against the seasoned heartwood. The wood amplifies their stridulation into a continuous,锯-toothed roar that rises straight through the soles of your feet, vibrating in your teeth and sternum. Above ground, the air shudders with the competing frequencies of canopy-less cicada-kin clinging in shimmering crusts to every weathered burl ridge, screeching in synchronized waves that roll from horizon to horizon like sheets of sonic weather.

Across this deafening plain move the herds. Because there is no brush to hide in—only the endless, undulating floor of bark and leather—safety lies in sheer, suffocating numbers. From any low rise, the open distances are smeared dark with migrating mats of grazers: flat-bodied, six-legged beasts with overlapping plates of pale horn and heavy, chisel-beaked mouths built to gouge strips from the tough photosynthetic hides. When ten thousand of them cross a hollow vascular basin at once, forty thousand horny hooves strike the drum-tight wood in a rolling thunder that drowns out even the insects. Behind and above them wheel tornadic flocks of bone-jawed gliders and long-legged cursorial hunters, darting into the edges of the herds to snatch the slow, their shrieks barely registering as thin, high scratches over the immense bass drone of the ground itself.

Wounds in the landscape are everywhere, and always healing. Where a herd strips an acre of dark membrane raw, the plateau bleeds slow, boiling-hot latex that smells sharply of pepper, trapping slower stragglers like flies in amber before hardening over weeks into glossy, amber scar-scabs. Years later, wind-blown husks and chitin dust polish those scabs matte, turning them into new low hills. Erosion and growth have reached an ancient truce here; a canyon carved by three centuries of migrating hooves slowly narrows from the bottom up as pale, bulging bolsters of scar-wood swell inward to close the gap.

Human—or at least tool-making—marks sit far apart upon this immensity, tiny and provisional, easy to miss in the bright glare. 

Every twenty or thirty miles, usually near one of the solitary, towering spore-chimneys that rise hundreds of feet into the sky like warped wooden mesas, someone has worked the ground. There are no stone walls and no forged iron; there is no ore for a vertical mile. Instead, people build by coaxing and cutting. With adzes of fire-hardened ironwood, chipped flint traded from unimaginable edges of the world, and hammered native copper, they score deliberate patterns into young callus-ridges, irritating the great plant into swelling hollow, dome-like galls the size of huts. Once a gall thickens and lignifies, they girdle its base so it dries hollow, lining the interior with thick mats of shredded, spongy pith to baffle the ceaseless, maddening roar of the floor outside. Inside one of these shelters, the world drops from a deafening shriek to a dull, submarine throb.

Outside the huts stand simple, clever rigs: tripods of bleached limb-bone supporting woven funnels of waxed fiber anchored over the morning guttation-pores to catch the dawn sweat before it evaporates, channeling it into stoppered bladders made from shed animal stomachs. Deeper taps—long, fire-hollowed drills of horn driven straight down into pressurized sap-veins—are capped with heavy wooden pegs tied down with rawhide, weeping sticky, sweet crust around their seams. Left untended for even a single season, these camps vanish without ruins; the plateau simply pushes a slow wave of fresh bark up over the pegs, swallows the bone tripods into a fold of grey cork, and smooths itself back out under the hot wind, vast and ringing like a struck bell beneath the clear sky.
