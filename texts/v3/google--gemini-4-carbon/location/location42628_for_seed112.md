There is no rock beneath your feet, though if you struck the ground with an iron bar, it would ring like a cast-iron skillet. 

For three thousand miles in every direction, the continent is a dry, rigid sponge of ossified timber. Aeons ago, whatever silicate bedrock lay at the core of this world was swallowed, pulverized, and drawn upward through roots that no longer need soil. Now the landscape is entirely botanical, yet there is not a single soft leaf, drop of sap, or bead of moisture anywhere within it. It is an alpine highland of bleached lignin and dense cellulose, weathered by endless, dry gales into smooth, swooping ridges, fluted canyons, and vast, hollow plains.

Despite the totality of the plant-mass, the landscape feels breathtakingly empty. Because the flora grows as an openwork lattice—arches as wide as valleys, ribs the size of suspension bridges, vaults miles deep filled with nothing at all—the world is mostly cold, rushing air. You do not walk *on* a plain so much as traverse the top edge of a continental truss. Look down through a thirty-yard gap between two bone-white structural boughs, and you look down through four vertical miles of sunlit, crisscrossing timber, fading into a pale blue haze of depth before the weave grows too thick to see through. 

The light is blinding. High in a thin, crisp atmosphere devoid of clouds or humidity, the sun strikes the bleached wood and turns the entire horizon to chalk and silver. Shadows down in the deeper latticework are sharp-edged and cool, painted in tones of slate, ash, and faint violet, lit from beneath by light bouncing infinitely off the smooth, wind-polished curves of lower branches.

Then there is the sound, which does not merely occupy the air, but *is* the air's primary physical property. 

The wind never stops, and at this elevation, with nothing wet or soft to absorb its energy, the whole continent acts as an unimaginable, dry-wood instrument—and an engine. Millions of miles of taut, fibrous tendons stretch across the empty spans between the massive structural boughs. As the cold gales tear through the lattice, these tendons vibrate, thrumming at bass frequencies you feel in your teeth and your sternum. Hollow boles the width of grain silos catch the crosswinds and howl like pipe organs blown to the point of rupture. And everywhere—on every ridge, tucked into the crooks of the mega-branches, lining the sweeping, eroded wind-tunnels—the forest is working.

No hand built the machinery here, because the flora evolved into heavy industry on its own.

Without water to move nutrients by capillary action, the Great Weave relies on pure, dry mechanics. Along the bright, wind-scoured ridges grow endless, staggered rows of turbine-fronds: vast, three-bladed Pinwheels of horn-hard ironwood set spinning inside smooth, self-lubricating sockets smeared with dry graphite dust extruded by the bark. They blur in the blinding sun, screaming at high pitch as they convert the furious gale into raw torque. 

That torque travels down long, jointed drive-shafts of twisted vine-wood—thick as railway cars, spinning violently in wooden collars—running for miles across the empty gulfs to power the forest’s internal distribution. Deep in the trusses, reciprocating piston-pods slam back and forth in polished cylinders, compressing air to blast dry, powdery nutrient-spores through miles of hollow arterial tubing. Elsewhere, massive wooden cams trip and drop with the deafening, rhythmic *clack-boom-clack* of a million drop-forges, grinding flaking deadwood from the older, eroded boughs into fine carbon dust to feed new growth. 

Nothing stays still. The turbulence is structural. Whole sections of the horizon are constantly reconfiguring to chase the shifting jet streams. Great, ribbed sail-plates—acres of translucent, parchment-thin cellulose—snap open across a canyon with a report like cannon fire, catching a sudden downdraft. Under the immense strain, the miles-long boughs groan, flex, and shift by fifty feet at a time, dragging entire secondary ecosystems sideways through the bright air. Friction-sparks occasionally flare white-hot in an ungreased socket, instantly smothered by automated bladder-galls that burst on heat-contact, coughing clouds of fire-retardant mineral ash into the slipstream. 

Through this deafening, roaring latticework moves the fauna, entirely adapted to high-velocity aerodynamics and brutal clockwork. 

Flocks of shard-winged gliders—creatures with rigid, chitinous airfoils and no legs to speak of—ride the violent thermal-less updrafts screaming up through the miles-deep shafts of the floor. They bank with terrifying precision around spinning turbine-blades, snatching airborne packets of spore-dust out of the ruptured exhaust vents of the wood-pipes. 

Closer to the trembling surfaces, long, low-slung beasts built like greyhounds made of wicker and sinew sprint along the roaring drive-shafts, timing their leaps across rotating wooden gears with effortless grace. They have clamp-like, padded jaws used to pry open the pressurized pneumatic junctions, drinking the rich, dry aerosolized lipid-powders shooting through the lines before the plant’s automated valve-slammers can snap shut on their snouts. Larger, armored crawlers—shaped like smooth, aerodynamic teardrops so the hurricane-force gusts slide right over them—clamp onto the swaying tension-cables, grazing on the graphite-rich bark and laying their eggs directly inside the warm, vibrating housings of the rotary joints.

To stand on a high crest here is to brace yourself against a cold, dry hurricane under a sky of piercing, cloudless cobalt. Beneath your boots, the bleached timber shudders with the heavy, rhythmic throb of ten thousand hidden camshafts turning in the bright abyss below. Five miles away across an open bowl of pure air, an entire ridge of weathered wood decides the wind has shifted by three degrees; with a sound that eclipses thunder, ten thousand giant wooden louvers slam shut in sequence, throwing a sweeping wave of dazzling white reflected sunlight straight into your eyes, while flocks of silver gliders scatter upward like metal shavings caught in an exhaust fan. 

It is ancient, utterly dry, fiercely clean, and loud enough to erase thought—a planetary engine of living deadwood, roaring continuously in the freezing sun, wearing itself smooth against the wind and grinding its own bones to build the next span into the sky.
