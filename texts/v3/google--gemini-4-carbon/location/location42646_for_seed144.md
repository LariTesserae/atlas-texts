At five thousand meters, the sky has lost its lower haze and gone the hard, flat blue of tempered steel, yet the air against the skin is forty-five degrees Celsius and smells of wet iron and boiled salt. 

The basin is an ancient, slumped shelf near the crest of the range—three miles across from one weathered basalt lip to the other, completely open to the sun. If you walk to the southern notch and look outward, the world drops away into blinding white cloud-decks a thousand meters below, where normal mountain weather is happening in freezing wind. Inside the rim, however, a shallow magma sill presses close to the bedrock. For tens of thousands of years it has baked the plateau from underneath, turning what should be a glacier bed into a sweltering, breathless conservatory of stone and brine.

There is no bare dirt anywhere on the floor. Every square foot of the three-mile expanse is occupied, crowded with the slow accumulation of mineral and biological industry. Centuries of mineral seepage have built an unbroken labyrinth of rounded sinter domes, overlapping travertine saucers, swollen sulfur blisters, and smooth, pillowy ribs of silica, all worn to a dull, waxy polish by wind and acidic mist. Between and over these formations, there is no gap: where one salt-crusted hummock ends, a shallow pan of heavy liquid begins, or a colony of tube-building worms has cemented a reef of grey grit, or a rusted steel pipeline snakes across the stone on cast-iron cradles. Walking across the basin means stepping from dome to rim to pipe-casing without ever touching level earth.

Pure water cannot stay liquid long at this altitude and heat; the atmospheric pressure is too low, and fresh rain flashes into vapor almost as soon as it touches the hot rock. What keeps the basin damp is brine. The fluids welling up through ten thousand hairline vents are thick with dissolved lithium, potash, sulfates, and iron—so heavy and syrupy that their boiling point is pushed well above the temperature of the ground. This mineral sweat coats everything. It sits in millions of shallow, scalloped dishes no deeper than a boot heel, creeps down the flanks of the domes in warm, oily films, and hangs in the hollows as a low, clinging mist that leaves a salty grease on glass and skin. Only along the very outer rim of the plateau, where the rising heat of the basin collides with the sub-zero mountain gales coming up the cliffs, does true water condense—forming a jagged collar of rime ice at night that melts each morning and trickles back inward, endlessly recycling the plateau’s moisture.

Because the air is so thin, the landscape is eerily quiet. Sound has very little medium to push against, and the porous, sponge-like sinter swallows vibration before it can travel. A fumarole twenty paces away will vent a furious, shimmering jet of vapor that whips rags of mist sideways, yet all that reaches the ear is a dry, papery intake of breath, like someone whispering in another room. You feel the heat on your cheekbones and see the whole basin in restless motion, while moving through a near-silence usually reserved for deep snow.

Plants have almost no purchase here. Only where an elevated pipe casts a permanent ribbon of shade, or in the cooler weep-zones near the outer rim, do sparse tufts of vegetation manage to root: knuckles of grey, leathery halophytes coated in reflective salt crystals, and stiff wire-grasses that rattle without making a sound. Instead, the base of life is a slick skin of thermophilic mats—ochre, bruised purple, and oxidized green—that carpet every damp crease of the rock.

Feeding on those mats is an astonishing density of animals, packed shoulder to shoulder across the warm stone. The terraces shimmer with continuous, silent traffic. Millions of pale, flat-shelled crustaceans the size of thumbnails graze the wet sinter in slow, shifting sheets, their gills adapted to breathe warm brine-film rather than open water. Threading among them are long-legged, spindle-bodied lizards with barrel ribcages—oversized lungs pumping visibly at twice the normal pace to pull oxygen from the starved air. They move in quick, jerky bursts over the scalding domes, snapping up brine-flies that never fly higher than an inch off the stone lest the thin air fail their wings. Above them all, riding the massive column of hot air that rises constantly off the plateau, wide-winged scavengers circle in stacked tiers. They never flap; they simply lock their tendons and drift on the thermal chimney, dropping straight down like stones whenever a heat-exhausted animal stills on the crust below.

Threaded through this crowded biology is an older, scattered network of heavy machinery. Nobody lives on the shelf—the combination of wet-bulb heat and high-altitude hypoxia makes unassisted labor dangerous after an hour—so the extraction rigs were built to run alone. Every half-mile or so, rising out of the low, bulbous terrain like stranded ships, stand squat, industrial structures of blackened iron and copper: geothermal wellheads, brine-fractionating towers, and flywheel condenser pumps. Thick, insulated conduits link them across the basin, running straight over the travertine bowls and becoming encrusted in the same yellow-white mineral rime that coats the stone, until pipe and bedrock look half-grown together.

The machines are active, cycling endlessly in the bright sun. A pressure governor reaches threshold on a nearby rig; a counterweight drops; an exhaust louver claps open and vents a white plume straight toward the dark blue zenith; a heavy piston drives downward into the crust. Through the thin air, even ten yards away, the entire mechanical sequence registers only as a soft *chuff* and the quiet ticking of cooling copper fins—though a second later, the heavy thump of the stroke arrives upward through the soles of your boots. Lizards do not even scatter from the iron housing when it fires; they lie pressed against the shady underside of the brine tanks, ribs heaving fast, watching the crustacean herds part and close around the vibrating bolts.

All afternoon the processes run together under clear, unsoftened sunlight. Shadows are ink-sharp against the white crusts. Brine films thicken to paste in the midday glare, turning the shallow pans from dull silver to rust-pink, while thousands of small shelled bodies migrate three inches sideways into the seep-lines where fresh fluid still bubbles up. Along the distant stone lip, wisps of white vapor tear upward where the hot shelf breathes out into the freezing void of the upper range, vanishing instantly into the dry blue sky, while down on the basin floor the valves keep cycling, the mats keep growing, and a million small mouths scrape quietly at the wet, hot stone.
