The mountains that once stood here did not fall; they simply ran out of height a few hundred million years ago. What remains is their basement—a vast, nearly level expanse of banded gneiss and pale quartzite planed down to within a few yards of base level, stretching away for miles toward a low, wavering horizon. There are no hills left, only broad, swelling whalebacks of bare stone that rise three or four feet above the water line and take half a mile to crest and subside again. Every edge on every pebble has been rubbed round. Even the sand in the shallow depressions consists entirely of pure, insoluble quartz grains rolled so smooth they feel like grease underfoot.

Water owns almost half the surface, though almost none of it is deep. It lies across the stone in immense, sky-colored sheets an inch to a foot thick, held in broad saucer-basins worn infinitely slowly into the rock, overflowing from one pan into the next without enough slope to raise a ripple. You cannot easily tell where a pool ends and wet rock begins except by the glare. Overhead, the sky is bright and pale blue, slightly hazed at the edges with moisture, and because the ground mirrors so much of it back, light comes from below as fiercely as from above. The water itself is the temperature of bathwater by mid-morning and warmer than blood by afternoon. Where it slips over darker bands of iron-stained rock, thin wisps of vapor curl off it even in full sun.

Despite the warmth and the constant moisture, the land is strikingly bare of tall greenery. Millions of years of leaching have stripped the flatlands of nearly all soil and soluble nutrients, leaving behind sealed kaolin clays and naked shield rock where roots cannot find purchase. Plants exist as isolated specialists spaced widely across the open glare. Every few hundred paces, where two ancient fault fractures cross and hold a pocket of grey silt, a single low, flat-topped tree—knotted, grey-barked, and thick-leaved—stands alone with its reflection intact beneath it. Between them, the expanses are punctuated only by sparse, rust-colored clumps of wiry sedge, scattered rosettes of carnivorous sundews glistening red on the damp clay margins, and dark, leathery crusts of lichen above the high-water stain.

The true meadow here is submerged. Beneath the warm sheets of water, the stone is coated in thick, velvety mats of olive, ochre, and dull purple algae—leathery biofilms bubbling slowly with oxygen in the afternoon brightness. In the quietest basins, those mats have trapped fine sediment layer by microscopic layer over centuries, growing into low, pillowy domes like ancient stromatolites just breaking the surface.

Because the primary pasture is flat and underwater, the landscape looks vacant from a distance, yet it generates a continuous, immense wall of sound. With no forest canopy to muffle it and miles of hard stone and flat water to carry every vibration, noise travels unbroken across the basin. Millions of small, speckled frogs sit wedged in damp cracks and along the edges of the algal mats, calling in overlapping, metallic choruses that rattle like shaken pebbles and drone like taut wires. Cicadas buzz from the solitary trees with a steady, sawing shrillness. Over this heavy baseline rises the sharper commotion of birds: wide-spaced flocks of long-legged waders—ibises, stilts, heavy-billed storks—striding methodically through the shin-deep sheets, clattering their mandibles, honking, and erupting into hoarse, echoing calls that carry two miles across the flats.

Look down into any shallow pan and the abundance becomes obvious. The warm water crawls with small, armored life: pale freshwater crabs scuttling sideways across the submerged gneiss, dense clouds of tadpoles grazing the slime-domes, water beetles rowing through the glare, and lunged, eel-like fishes thick as a wrist sliding lazily over damp stone thresholds from one pool to the next. Flat, broad-snouted reptiles the color of wet ironstone lie motionless on the low whalebacks, mouths slightly open to vent the heat, spaced ten or twenty yards apart like stranded logs.

Nothing human has ever had reason to stay here, and there is nothing built, though a few biological habits mimic architecture from afar. Widely scattered across the higher stone pavements stand hard, kiln-baked pinnacles of pale clay—termite mounds six feet tall, cemented with saliva into something almost as durable as the bedrock, serving as lookout posts for hawks and lizards. Connecting the slightly deeper, permanent wallows are faint, winding grooves polished a quarter-inch deep into solid stone by thousands of generations of heavy hooves, claws, and bellies following the exact same gentle contours between dry perches.

Change moves across this place at a speed barely perceptible to the eye. There is no river channel to flood violently, no steep bank to collapse. When a heavy rainstorm dumps water twenty miles up-basin, no torrent arrives; instead, two days later, the quiet sheets simply creep three inches further up the gently sloped whalebacks, drowning a ring of grey lichen, floating the dry husks of shed crab shells, and linking ten thousand separate puddles into a single breathless mirror. Weeks later, evaporation and an imperceptible southward creep of the water draw the margins back again, leaving faint white rings of dried mineral salt on the ancient rock, while the frogs keep singing without pause in the warm, bright air.
