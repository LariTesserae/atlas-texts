There is no stone here, and there is no sky, though there is a ceiling forty paces overhead that curves like the inside of a skull. 

Everything is rind. Beneath the worn, flat pan of an exhausted continent—sunk just shallowly enough into the lowland crust to escape the surface’s final erasure—lies a single hollow bladder of living wood. It has no color, because there has never been light inside it. Not a stray photon from above, not the cold smear of bioluminescence; pigment has no work to do here and was abandoned ages ago. The space exists entirely as geometry, temperature, smell, and a continuous, deafening resonance.

Press a palm to the floor and it feels like cool horn or polished tallow—dry, dense, faintly vibrating, hovering just above a chill. There is practically no water in this root-gall, nor in the exhausted regolith outside it. Whatever sap moves through the vast, enclosing cortex is a heavy, non-aqueous lipid or a slow creep of dry mineral gas drawn up from deep crustal faults. The organism feeds on barometric pressure and tectonic friction, converting the microscopic groan of ancient, settling plates into tough cellulose and lignin, adding a breadth of a hair to its thickness every few centuries.

Inside the shell, the space is nearly void. Across the fifty-yard span from wall to curving wall, the air is empty—cool, dry as chalk, smelling sharply of camphor, old paper, and crushed coriander seed. Nothing flies through this central hollow. Nothing crawls on its floor. No beetle bores the timber; no mite nests in the crevices; there isn’t enough moisture in a cubic mile of this world to wet the joint of an ant’s leg. 

Yet the inner face of the shell is an unbroken, densely tiered city.

Every inch of the curving perimeter—from the dish of the floor up the steepening flanks to the overhang of the crown—has been worked into habitable infrastructure, half-carved with stone adzes and bone chisels, half-trained while the wood was growing. Whoever built it worked in the pitch dark with hand tools and immense patience, grafting live shoots together to form load-bearing arches, pegging split-root planking with shrunken hardwood dowels, weaving fibrous tendrils into suspended galleries that hug the concave wall. There are ring-streets no wider than a pair of shoulders, stacked twenty levels high. There are switchback staircases whose treads have been hollowed like spoons; rows of blind, capsule-like dwellings with woven-bark shutters; tiny plazas cantilevered over the central emptiness; dry viaducts and branching chutes that must once have distributed grain, dust, or warm rising air from lower vents.

Because the tree—if it is a tree—never died, and because dry air has circulated through this pocket for geological ages, the city has been eroded from within and overgrown without ever losing its form. Sharp tool-marks from flint scrapers have been buffed to a silky, waxen smoothness by millions of years of drifting, flour-fine spore-dander. At the same time, the slow cambium has crept over its own incisions like cold amber, rounding every doorway into a sphincter of smooth wood, fusing mortise and tenon into single uninterrupted bones, thickening the balustrades until they resemble rows of worn molars. It is ancient the way a riverbed pebble is ancient, though everything here is alive and urban.

And the sound Inside the dark is immense.

The hollow acts as the soundbox of an instrument the size of a town square, and the city lining its ribs is the mechanism that plays it. As atmospheric tides push and pull through invisible, tortuous pores from the dead plains above, dry air rushes through thousands of carved vents, thoroughfares, and doorway apertures. The builders appear to have tuned the ventilation deliberately, or else the slow swelling of the wood has stumbled into terrible acoustics on its own. Low, continuous organ-drones roll round and round the curved walls, piling over their own echoes until the empty center throbs with standing waves you feel in your sternum and teeth. 

Over that constant, roaring bass comes the percussion of the plant’s own slow life. Long, braided vascular cables—some thick as thighs, others taut as harp strings, originally strung across balconies to brace the upper tiers—tighten imperceptibly over decades as the surrounding shell warps. Every few minutes, somewhere in the black verticality, a dry fiber reaches its limit and slips across a wooden bridge or snaps a resinous seal. *Crack.* The report slams from wall to wall like a timber splitting in hard frost, echoing through ten thousand empty rooms before dissolving back into the drone. Elsewhere, entire districts creak in slow, rhythmic unison—a vast, dry, wooden grinding, like the hull of a ship locked in polar ice, taking six hours to complete a single inhale of structural flex and another six to let it go.

Nothing hurries. Down on the shallow concave basin at the bottom of the void, a thin drift of dry, powdery chaff—shed skin from the living walls—migrates in slow, sweeping crescents, pushed fractionally each year by the standing acoustic waves, arranging itself into neat, rippled dunes that nobody sees. High up in the dark tenements, a grafted doorframe takes a thousand years to pinch shut, slowly sealing off an empty room whose woven floor-mats have long since fused back into the living substrate. 

The city maintains its streets, repairs its trusses with blind, creeping callus, breathes through its flutes, and bellows into its own hollow belly, waiting for nothing, completely intact in the cool dark.
