There is no sharp line between the sky and the stone here, only a continuous, violent transition of densities. At fifteen thousand feet, the range has been stripped of all youthful geometry. There are no jagged peaks left, no brittle scree slopes or sharp ridges; millennia of relentless hydraulic battering have ground the dark basalt and granite down into immense, smooth, whale-backed domes and plunging, polished troughs. It is an ancient, exhausted topography, smoothed to its hardest, deepest cores, yet it exists in a state of perpetual, roaring agitation. 

The air is essentially a breathable river. Driven upward from warmer oceans far below, colossal banks of cloud slam into the massif and condense instantly into horizontal walls of cold rain and thick, driving mist. Visibility opens and slams shut by the minute. One moment, the pale, diffused daylight illuminates five miles of gleaming, water-sheathed valley—a staggering vertical drop of braided cataracts and wind-whipped green—and the next, a grey squall swallows everything past twenty paces in a deafening hiss of droplets. 

Water dominates every surface because there is nowhere for it to rest and nothing porous left to absorb it. It sheets over the broad, domed summits in glassy, ankle-deep films, gathering speed until it hits the ancient glacial grooves, where it turns white and furious. Millions of gallons per minute pour down smoothed chutes, colliding in mid-air where valleys intersect, kicked backward into forty-foot plumes of freezing spray by the erratic, booming updrafts that tear up the gorges. The sound is physical, an inescapable, layered roar: the low, chest-rattling bass of boulders rolling blindly at the bottom of submerged potholes, the mid-range freight-train howl of wind funneled through wet stone saddles, and the high, tearing static of rain hitting rushing water.

Yet the rock is rarely bare. What looks from a distance like green velvet stretched over the domes is, up close, a waist-deep, hyper-saturated jungle of cold-water flora, engineered for grip and flexibility. Nothing grows tall and rigid here; anything with a woody trunk would be snapped in an hour. Instead, the vegetation forms dense, rubbery mats. In the calmer, fully submerged basins—broad, stepped tarns connected by raging spillways—forests of freshwater kelp and bloated, red-stemmed water-cabbage heave and roll like ocean surf, churning endlessly in the current.

Life thrives in this cold, oxygenated chaos by matching its pace or locking onto its bones. In the white-water chutes, schools of heavy-bodied, slate-grey trout with oversized pectoral fins dart up forty-degree inclines of solid foam, clinging to the stone with modified, suction-cup bellies when the surge grows too fierce. Heavy, oil-slicked waterfowl—built dense as bowling balls, with stubby wings meant more for swimming through rapids than flying—shoot down the cataracts like torpedoes, vanish into deep plunge pools to tear snails and stonefly larvae off the bedrock, and haul themselves out onto shivering moss-mats in raucous, honking hundreds. Along the slick margins, herds of thick-pelted, amphibious ungulates—barrel-chested creatures with wide, splayed hooves lined in spongy keratin—wade belly-deep through the rushing sheet-flow. Their wool hangs in drenched, waterproof cords that swing heavily as they brace against the wind, tearing up mouthfuls of submerged sedge before plunging unhesitatingly into swirling channels to reach the next dome over.

Scattered sparsely across the miles of shining, green-and-black ramparts are the heavy iron footprints of an industrial system built to harvest the deluge. They look less like buildings and more like nautical bunkers bolted directly into the bedrock. At the narrow throat of a high basin stands a low-slung diversion weir of cyclopean concrete, stained neon-green with algae and streaming with runoff. Beside it squats a windowless turbine house made of dark, riveted steel plate and reinforced stone, anchored against the gale by thick braided-wire guy lines drilled deep into the granite. 

Even over the roar of the falls, as you draw close to one of these stations, a secondary sound cuts through: the steady, pressurized, fifty-hertz hum of giant Pelton wheels spinning blindly beneath the floorboards, chewing the kinetic fury of the mountain into current. Thick, rubber-sheathed power cables emerge from the bunker’s leeward wall. Too vulnerable to the ferocious winds to be strung on tall pylons, the lines run across the landscape on stubby, three-foot-tall cast-iron stanchions—triple-guyed and slick with rust and grease—snaking right through the rushing sheet-water and plunging directly beneath the rolling tarns, stitched across the ancient stone like heavy black sutures. 

Every half-mile or so, an automated sluice gate clanks and groans on massive, grease-slathered worm gears. Triggered by pressure sensors upstream, rusted iron baffles rise an inch or drop two, abruptly redirecting a ten-foot wall of white water from one polished stone spillway into another. The landscape adjusts instantly: a foaming cataract chokes down to a glistening wet black chute of thrashing fish and flattened weeds, while fifty yards away, a quiet moss-slope disappears entirely beneath a fresh, thundering avalanche of cold silver. Above it all, a single amber strobe light on the roof of the turbine house flashes steadily through the blowing curtains of cloud—a small, rhythmic pulse of electricity swallowed twenty yards out by the grey, roaring wet.
