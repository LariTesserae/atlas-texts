The water is warm, the temperature of blood or steeped broth, held high in the clouds inside a bowl of engineered stone. From the outside, if anyone ever climbed the sheer, wind-scoured granite peaks to see it, the structure would be invisible, buried entirely inside the summit of a dormant volcanic spine. Inside, there is no raw mountain left. Everything is laid masonry, stacked and fitted by hand so many thousands of years ago that the boundary between one block and the next has largely dissolved into a continuous, velvety rind of mineral sinter.

The space is no larger than an abbey church, though its geometry is crowded and vertical, flooded almost to the vaulting. Only a hand’s breadth of stagnant, humid air remains trapped beneath the ceiling slabs. Everything else is submerged.

There is no sun here, and no memory of it. Light exists only at the very threshold of perception: a faint, bruised-violet luminescence shed by a greasy bacterial tallow that coats the upper lintels just below the waterline, supplemented by the occasional, erratic spark of pale ochre from deep within the stonework. It is enough light only to turn the black water into a thick, charcoal soup, revealing silhouettes within arm's reach and turning everything beyond six feet into solid gloom. 

To move through this cistern is to navigate a dense, drowning architecture of pillars, stepped platforms, lintels, and sluice-channels whose original civic or ritual purpose has been completely erased by water and time. The builders worked without iron or mortar. They dressed blocks of dark basalt and porous tuff with stone mauls, fitting them together with interlocking mortises, and pinned the sluice-gates with heavy bronze dowels. Now, the bronze has long since leached away into soft, turquoise stains embedded in the rock, and the basalt blocks have been rubbed down by the slow convective turn of the thermal water until they resemble knuckles, knees, and polished loaves. Staircases climb nowhere, their risers melted into gentle ramps; rows of closely spaced columns stand shoulder-to-shoulder like crowded sleepers, leaving narrow, vertical alleys barely wide enough for a ribcage.

Not a single leaf, thread of moss, or filament of algae grows in the warm dark. Without light, photosynthesis never took hold. Instead, the ecosystem runs entirely on heat, sulfur, dissolved limestone, and flesh. 

Every submerged wall, archway, and carved niche is crusted thick with animal life. Because the architecture is so tightly packed—balconies overhanging narrow courts, stone screens pierced with round portholes, low-ceilinged vaults—the biomass feels overwhelming, layered three and four deep on every surface. Blind, shell-less gastropods the size of plums graze slowly across the bacterial slicks, their pale mantles undulating gently. Clusters of parchment-shelled tube worms sprout from the dissolved mortar joints like bundles of grey quills, swaying in a current too weak to feel against human skin. 

In the open water between the pillars, thousands of translucent, flattened crustaceans hang suspended like drifting ash. They barely swim; they simply twitch once every few seconds to keep from settling into the warm silt that fills the lower basins. Thread-thin eels, entirely devoid of pigment and no longer than a finger, weave continuously in and out of the carved drainage flutes, hunting the crustaceans by vibration alone. Larger shapes rest wedged into the corners of drowned doorways: flat, leathery creatures somewhere between a ray and a leech, draped over the worn stone sills like discarded wet coats, breathing in cycles that take a full minute to rise and fall.

The water comes from deep within the mountain’s cooling magma plumbing, rising through baffled stone shafts laid at the very bottom of the chamber. Whoever built this place high in the cold peaks went to immense labor to trap and tame a thermal vent, channeling its heat through a labyrinth of soaking pools, settling tanks, and warming benches. They capped it with corbeled stone roofs to hold the steam in. Then they vanished, and an ancient landslide or slow tectonic shift choked the main outflow drains just enough to raise the water level thirty feet, drowning the whole complex up to its eaves.

Because the inflow is now just a seep and the outflow merely weeps through microscopic fractures in the outer retaining walls, the entire flooded hall operates in extreme slow motion. There are no ripples, no splashes, no bubbling vents. The heat rises from the floor in lazy, invisible plumes that take ten minutes to curl through the colonnades and reach the ceiling. 

Sound is muffled to the point of nonexistence. Water absorbs everything, and the stone surfaces are too rounded and cushioned with soft-bodied filter-feeders to hold an echo. If you hold your breath and float among the upper capitals, all you hear is the microscopic, dry crackle of thousands of tiny chitinous jaws scraping mineral film off ancient masonry—a sound like fine sand slipping down the inside of an hourglass. Once an hour, perhaps, a heavy blind crab dislodges a pebble of calcified scale from an upper ledge; it ticks softly twice as it bounces down a submerged stair, then disappears into the velvet silt below.

The rules of this small world are absolute conservation and patient decay. Nothing enters from the freezing, windswept heights outside; nothing leaves except warm moisture sweating slowly into the mountain rock. When one of the pale, leathery drifters dies, it sinks onto an eroded sacrificial table or wedged lintel and is dismantled within days by crowds of milky isopods, its calcium folded back into shells, its carbon feeding the glowing bacterial grease along the ceiling. Even the masonry participates in this slow digestion. Where the rising water is slightly acidic near the floor vents, the old hand-carved reliefs—processions of geometric shapes, perhaps, or tall figures, now smoothed beyond all certainty—are still dissolving, molecule by molecule. Higher up, near the stagnant air gap, the minerals precipitate out again, building blunt, creamy stalactites downward from the roof slabs to meet the upward-reaching pillars, slowly knitting the ancient room shut.
