The chamber is an upright cylinder of riveted cast iron and triple-laminated plate glass, sixty feet across and eighty feet tall, perched on a steel trestle high above a jagged, cloud-washed pass. Outside the glass, the thin air of ten thousand feet blows past in silent gray shreds. Inside, there is no air at all—only eighty thousand gallons of warm, crystal-clear water, held at a steady eighty-two degrees by the massive manifold heaters bolted into the floor plates.

Everything within the cylinder is submerged, save for a narrow iron catwalk hugging the uppermost rim just beneath the domed roof, where the water meets a thin pocket of humid, pressurized gas. Looking down from that rim, the space does not read as a tank or a pool, but as a drowned engine room choked to bursting with botanical life. There is no rock, no sand, no silt, no soil. Every inch of substrate is fabricated: black-enameled I-beams, brass-bolted gusset plates, bundled copper conduits, perforated iron walkways, and heavy vertical drive-shafts descending into the congested depths. 

Because the facility was flooded shortly after the riveting hammers stopped—perhaps only ten or fifteen years ago—the metalwork is remarkably intact. Under the magnifying clarity of the water, the edges of the punched steel grates remain crisp and square. Hexagonal bolt-heads show sharp, unrounded corners, the stamped serial numbers on the turbine housings are legible to the last fraction of an inch, and the machine-lathe grooves along the brass pistons catch the afternoon sun in bright, hair-fine rings. There is almost no rust; the water is chemically buffered, faintly alkaline, carrying the slick, heavy feel of mineral oil against the glass.

Yet you can barely see five feet in any direction because the room is packed completely solid with machinery and leaves. 

Pipes and trusses cross the space in a dense three-dimensional grid, leaving scarcely enough room for a body to pass between a flywheel and a boiler drum. And out of every flange, grease-cup, threaded collar, and ventilation louver, thick-stemmed aquatic vegetation erupts in overwhelming profusion. There appears to have been a deliberate grafting, or else the plants were bred specifically to root in machined tolerances. Broad, ruffled blades of translucent olive-green emerge directly from the cooling jackets of motionless dynamos. Heavy curtains of maroon ribbon-weed drape over corrugated iron baffles, their holdfasts gripped tight around porcelain electrical insulators. Thick, waxy rosettes the size of cartwheels—resembling submerged cabbages made of blown emerald glass—wedge themselves into the V-spaces where diagonal cross-braces meet vertical columns.

Daylight enters easily through the tall vertical panes of the outer wall, high-altitude mountain light that arrives crisp and bright even when diffused through clouds. As it strikes the curved glass cylinder, the water acts as a colossal cylindrical lens, bending the illumination inward in pale, crossing shafts that shift imperceptibly over the course of an afternoon. Because the plant growth and ironwork are so densely layered, the interior becomes a tightly packed mosaic of glowing chartreuse translucence and crisp, blue-black industrial shadow. Deep in the center of the tank, where sunlight must pass through four layers of broad leaves and three sets of perforated iron stairs, brass pressure-gauges glow faintly behind thick glass bezels, their needles frozen at high numbers, lit from within by low-wattage tungsten filaments wired into waterproofed junction boxes.

Nothing swims here. There are no fish darting between the spokes of the wheels, no snails scraping the glass, not even the minute twitch of water-fleas in the sunbeams. Without grazers or current-borne grit, the leaves are immaculate—unbitten, unblemished, each edge as clean and sharply stamped as the steel girders beside them. 

Despite the industrial bulk of the room, the silence is profound, heavy with hydraulic mass. There is no roar of furnaces, no clatter of valves. The great machinery operates on a timescale almost geologic in its patience. A six-foot bronze worm-gear near the center of the chamber is turning, driven by a sealed electric motor buried in the floor, but it rotates at perhaps one revolution every four hours. If you watch a single tooth of the gear against the edge of an iron stanchion, you cannot see it move; only by looking away and returning ten minutes later can you verify that a thick, fibrous vine wrapped around the axle has been drawn half an inch tighter, slowly stretching its rubbery stem until a secondary runner snaps free and floats upward with dreamlike, syrupy laziness.

Elsewhere, thin copper tubes mounted along the ceiling girders release single, perfectly spherical bubbles of carbon dioxide at long intervals. One detaches with a soft, muffled *blup* that travels instantly through the dense water and dies against the foliage; the bubble wobbles upward for three seconds and breaks silently at the narrow gas-space under the dome. Every quarter-hour or so, a thermal expansion joint deep in the submerged boiler-stack adjusts to the afternoon heat with a dull, low *tink*—a metallic vibration you feel in the soles of your boots on the upper ring-walk rather than hear with your ears.

Otherwise, the only motion is the slow, thermal drift of the warm water rising from the base heaters. It lifts the yards-long fronds of pale green thread-fern and holds them upright in vertical columns alongside the elevator cables, swaying them an inch to the left, then an inch to the right over the span of minutes. Tiny pearls of oxygen, respired by millions of packed leaves in the high mountain sun, cling in geometric rows along the sharp, machined edges of iron brackets and brass sight-glasses, refusing to detach until they swell to the size of peppercorns, break loose one by one, and trace silent silver plumb-lines straight up through the tangled green ironwork toward the roof.
