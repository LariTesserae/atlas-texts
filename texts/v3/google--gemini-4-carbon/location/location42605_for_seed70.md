High in the mountains, the basin has drowned, and the water has forgotten how to move.

It sits at seven thousand feet, a valley several miles across, sealed at its lower end not by a gentle sill of glacial till, but by a violent, recent heave of dark porphyry that choked the gorge before the rock even had time to weather. Because the catastrophe is geologically fresh, the drowned mountainsides beneath the surface retain all their terrestrial sharpness. Looking down through the water, there is no soft apron of silt, no rounded cobble. Instead, submerged talus slopes pitch downward at brutal angles, every slab and shard of fractured stone retaining raw, knife-sharp edges. Drowned cliffs drop straight into the warm depths with their vertical strike-marks still crisp, untouched by currents, unsmoothed by sand. 

Yet the water is bath-warm, heated from below by the same shallow magma and restless fault lines that threw up the dam. There are no boiling geysers or violent vents—only a vast, distributed seepage of thermal mineral water rising slowly through a million hairline fractures in the basin floor. The temperature sits at a constant, languid eighty-five degrees from bank to bank, winter and summer. Above the surface, where the thin alpine air cools rapidly in the afternoons, a perpetual, low-lying fleece of steam drifts across the mirror-flat expanse, smelling faintly of wet slate, dissolved iron, and crushed greenery. 

Sunlight at this altitude is fierce and direct, piercing the surface with sharp clarity before diffusing into a dense, golden-green glow. Because the water has virtually no current and rich dissolved minerals, the drowned valley has become an aquatic jungle of staggering, claustrophytic density, suspended in three dimensions. 

Plants here do not fight gravity; they use the water’s buoyancy to build towering, delicate architectures that would collapse instantly in open air. Anchored to the jagged ledges sixty feet down, pale-stemmed macrophytes rise in thick, swaying colonnades toward the light. Their leaves are thin as membrane, translucent olive and rust-red, ruffled at the margins and overlapping by the millions until they form suspended walls and vaulted galleries. Between these vertical forests stretch vast, floating canopies of bladder-fern and water-moss, held aloft at specific thermal strata by tiny, gas-filled pearls grown into their fronds. Some species don't root at all. They hang neutral in the mid-water—great, tangled spheres of emerald root-hair and chartreuse ribbon, drifting an inch an hour, drinking minerals directly from the warm suspension.

The sheer volume of life per cubic yard is overwhelming, yet nothing hurries. The greenery is so packed that one submerged grove presses softly against the next, leaving narrow, winding shafts of clear, sunlit water that drop down toward the dark, angular stone below. Silvery beads of oxygen, exhaled by the sun-drenched leaves, cling to every serrated edge and waxy surface like frost. Only when a bubble swells too large does it detach, wobbling upward through the crowded fronds—*blup, tic, blup*—knocking smaller bubbles loose in a slow, shimmering chain reaction that breaks the surface in tiny, concentric rings.

Aside from this faint, irregular ticking of rising gas, the basin is profoundly quiet. There is no surf, no inlet waterfall—the basin feeds entirely from below and drains by slow percolation through the rubble of the natural dam. Wind crossing the high ridges above rarely touches the water, cushioned by the steep rock walls and the heavy blanket of warm vapor clinging to the surface. Beneath the waterline, the silence is thick and physical, pressing against the eardrums, broken only by the occasional, resonant *clack* of a fresh stone splitting off an upper crag and tumbling down into the underwater thickets, where its fall is cushioned by yards ofyielding foliage before it settles onto the sharp scree below.

For all the riot of botanical structure, the water seems nearly devoid of animal life. You can stare down a sunlit corridor between forty-foot walls of swaying,amber-tinted kelp-grass for an hour and see nothing with eyes. The chemistry is slightly too strange—too rich in carbonates and lithium, too low in dissolved nitrogen—for standard mountain trout or amphibians. 

When an animal does appear, it feels like an apparition. A solitary snail the size of a closed fist, its shell thin and translucent as horn because predators are nonexistent, glides along the underside of the surface tension, grazing on microscopic film. Deeper down, wedged into the clean, ninety-degree crevice of a split boulder, a pale, flat-bodied crustacean sits motionless for days, its long, hair-fine antennae extended six times the length of its body to feel the infinitesimal drift of sinking detritus. Once in a great while, a ribbon-like swimmer—something halfway between a leech and an eel, colored the exact coppery green of the surrounding leaves—uncoils from a dense mat of bladder-wort, undulations rippling down its length in hypnotic slow motion as it crosses a shaft of alpine sunlight, and vanishes into the next wall of green without disturbing a single frond.

Along the shoreline, there is no beach, no marshy transition zone. The raw mountain rock plunges straight into the warm bath at a steep incline. Right at the waterline, where hot water meets cool mountain air, a narrow band of velvety, lime-encrusted moss grows on the stone, drinking the rising steam. Above that line, the high altitude reasserts itself instantly: bare, fractured granite, fierce wind, thin soil, and the stark, dry glare of the peaks. Below that line, just inches beneath the skin of the water, lies the warm, crowded, weightless labyrinth—suspended in bright silence, growing slowly inward until the valley is entirely full.
