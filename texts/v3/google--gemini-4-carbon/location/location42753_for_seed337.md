From the surrounding plain, the land simply drops away along a clean, jagged tear. There are no foothills leading down into it, no rounded lip softened by rain or root-work. It is a sunken graben—a wide tectonic trough nearly two miles across and several hundred feet deep, where a massive block of the crust has recently slipped downward between parallel faults. Because the subsidence is geologically brand-new, nothing down here has been worn smooth. Every exposed cliff face is a fresh break. Strata of dark greywacke, slate, and banded limestone meet the open sky in razor-sharp angles, unstained by lichen, unrounded by wind. Unoxidized flecks of mica and iron pyrite still glitter cleanly in the sheer walls, and where rock has shattered at the base of the scarps, the talus consists not of pebbles or sand, but of crisp, flat-sided shards and heavy stone prisms wedged tightly together.

Daylight pours unimpeded into the wide, open bowl—clear, even, upland light—revealing a basin packed from wall to wall with human construction. There is almost no bare ground left visible on the trough floor. Structure presses against structure in a dense, cellular honeycomb of dry-laid stone, split timber, and packed fault-clay, climbing out of the central depths and stepping aggressively up the raw ledges of the boundary cliffs. Buildings do not stand alone; they lean into and brace one another, sharing walls, buttresses, and roofs, filling every geometric pocket of the fractured bedrock until it is difficult to tell where the natural cleavage of the greywacke ends and the hand-stacked masonry begins.

There are no streets wide enough for carts, and no wheeled vehicles at all. Instead, the city is woven together by thousands of narrow, stepped alleys, catwalks of lashed pine poles, stone-corbeled arches, and flat slate rooftops that serve as public thoroughfares, drying yards, and work platforms all at once. Where one house stops, a kiln-shed wedges into the gap beneath its eaves; above the kiln-shed, a timber cistern-frame straddles the alley; beside the cistern, a stairwell spirals up the spine of a retaining wall. Every square yard of horizontal and vertical space is claimed, built over, shored up, and occupied.

Despite this extreme compression and the constant, visible motion of thousands of inhabitants, the basin is astonishingly quiet. 

Cool air pools permanently in the sunken trough, heavy and damp, and it seems to drink sound before it can travel. More than the air, though, the silence is a product of how the city works and what it is made of. Because the surrounding rock faces are freshly fractured and still settling, sharp concussions are dangerous; nobody blasts, and nobody strikes heavy iron sledges against stone. The technology here is manual, patient, and low-voiced. Down in the packed stone-yards, blocks are parted using wooden wedges soaked in water until they swell and split the slate with a single, muted *thuck*. Elsewhere, workers draw toothless copper blades back and forth through limestone slabs, feeding wet quartz grit into the cut—a steady, rhythmic *shhh-shhh* like waves on fine shingle. Overhead, hundreds of timber A-frames and cantilevered cranes swing baskets of cut stone, clay, and charcoal from tier to tier, their wooden axles heavily smeared with tallow so they turn without a squeak, their braided hemp and bast-fiber ropes whispering through polished hardwood blocks. Footsteps are muffled by shoes of felted wool or woven rush. Even voices stay low, carrying easily across adjacent rooftops without need for shouting.

Water enters the depression not as a river—no surface stream has yet cut a channel across the upper rim—but through severed aquifers in the cliff walls. Halfway up the raw fault scarps, dark horizontal bands of wet rock weep continuously. Cold groundwater beads along the newly broken seams, filming the stone in slick, glistening sheets before dripping into thousands of hand-fitted slate gutters mortared directly onto the cliff face with lime putty. From those high collector-ledges, the moisture is routed downward through a dense vascular network of hollowed-out alder trunks, clay pipes, and open stone runnels threaded right through the walls and kitchens of the city. In the early mornings, when cool night air meets the first direct sun slanting over the eastern rim, thick mist condenses in the center of the basin. Across the highest rooftops, rows of vertical wooden frames strung with coarse, unspun wool stand into the drift, combing moisture out of the passing vapor until droplets gather and trickle into covered stone vats below. Everything smells faintly of wet slate, woodsmoke, raw lime, and cold damp wool.

Soil does not exist naturally down here—only pale, gritty rock-flour ground fine along the fault lines—so plant life is scarce and fiercely localized. Along the weeping cliff seams, thin smears of bright green liverwort and pioneer moss have just begun to colonize the damp stone. In the dry, mortarless chinks between house walls, pale-stemmed stonecrop and small, tough-leaved saxifrages catch hold where windblown dust has settled. The only substantial greenery sits in shallow, rectangular troughs of plankwood arranged along the sunniest roof-terraces, filled with dark topsoil carried down in baskets from the plain above and planted densely with hardy leeks, turnip greens, and short-stalked barley. 

Animal life is equally spare. There is no room and no pasture in the packed masonry for cattle, horses, or sheep. A few small, silent-footed dogs trot along the roof-walks, hunting the rock mice that have found their way down from the rim. High up near the lip of the chasm, small colonies of crag martins dart through the open air, snapping up midges that breed around the water cisterns, though even the birds are relatively few, still discovering a canyon that was not there generations ago. Spiders string flat sheet-webs across the dark, cool angles between unweathered boulders under the house foundations.

Yet the entire sunken expanse hums with continuous, silent industry. Plumes of pale steam and thin blue charcoal smoke rise from hundreds of covered lime-kilns, pottery clamps, and copper-smelting hearths tucked into terraced recesses, drifting straight up in the windless air of the trough before catching the prairie breeze at rim-level and shearing sideways. Lines of porters move steadily up and down the switchback stairs cut into the boundary cliffs, carrying raw timber and prairie sod down, hauling cut slate and burned lime up. Masons constantly adjust dry-stone shoring walls with long wooden pry-bars as the young basin floor shifts by fractions of an inch, setting smallwooden telltales across hairline cracks in the bedrock to read the slow creep of the earth. Shadows of crane-booms and fog-frames wheel cleanly across the crowded roofs as the sun crosses the wide sky overhead, lighting a city still actively wedging itself deeper into a wound in the ground that hasn't yet learned to erode.
