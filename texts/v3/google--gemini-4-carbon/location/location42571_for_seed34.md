From a high ridge, the plateau looks at first like textured bedrock—grey-blue, finely grained, bruised purple under the bellies of moving clouds and struck pale silver where the sun breaks through. Only as the eye tries to find a natural talus slope or an unbroken sweep of scree does the geometry resolve. Every contour from the foreground down into the broad valleys, and up again across thirty, fifty, eighty miles of crisp highland air, is composed of stacked stone.

It is an unbroken settlement the size of an inland sea, built entirely without mortar, timber, metal, or wheel-ruts. There are no towers and no monuments—nothing rises more than two or three stories above its immediate footing—yet the density is relentless. Walls lean inward in slight, stable batters; corbelled domes bulge side by side like dark cobbles in a riverbed; narrow, shoulder-width passages thread between retaining walls, drop down slick slab staircases, open onto sunken courtyards no wider than a room, and immediately split into three smaller culverts or alleys. Where one course of dwellings slumped ages ago, later courses were keyed directly into the settled rubble, turning older roofs into paved terraces and older rooms into subterranean cisterns. There is not a straight line anywhere longer than twenty paces, nor a square yard of ground that hasn't been lifted, split along its natural cleavage, and laid back down by weight and friction alone.

The air sits around forty-five degrees in the sun and drops ten degrees the instant a cloud shadow crosses. At this altitude the light is clean, sharp, and abundant. Because the plateau generates its own weather, the sky is never still: broad fleets of flat-bottomed cumulus form straight out of the damp stone basins, race eastward across the bright blue dome, drag sudden grey curtains of mist and drizzle across five miles of rooftops at a time, and dissolve again only for new vapor to lift off the sun-struck slate behind them. You can stand on a dry parapet in crisp, shadow-etching sunlight and watch four distinct rain squalls marching silently across different sectors of the city at once, lighting and darkening the stone work like breath moving across hammered pewter.

Water is everywhere, constantly in motion, and almost completely silent. 

There are no roaring gutters or plunging falls. Over centuries of weathering, the entire urban crust has settled into a laminar watershed. Every roof dome is shingled in overlapping greywacke flags tilted just enough that collected rain and condensed mist slide downward in an unbroken, glassy film rather than dripping. At the eaves, projecting lip-stones touch the angled aprons of lower walls, passing the water down step by step into shallow courtyard pans an inch deep, over notched stone sills worn smooth as soap, and along miles of ankle-wide channels cut into the centers of the walkways. Water weeps steadily from the dark seams of north-facing retaining walls, wicks across velvety pads of olive-black moss, and sheets across wide tilted plazas that mirror the racing clouds. Even the wind makes almost no sound here. Instead of whistling over sharp edges or rustling foliage, moving air is swallowed and baffled by millions of porous dry-stone joints, recessed doorways, and dead-end alcoves. The whole vast metropolis absorbs acoustics like deep snow. Stand still in a junction of six alleys and all you hear is the faint, glassy *shhh* of surface tension pulling water over wet slate, and the pressure of cool air shifting softly past your ears.

Nothing woody grows at this height. Vegetation appears only where dust and silt have lodged in damp stone seams over generations: crisp rosettes of pale green saxifrage, scattered clumps of stiff, rusty sedge standing six inches tall along the water rills, and flat maps of crustose lichen—ochre, chalk-white, tarnished copper—that bind the exposed stones together visually as if mortar had grown over the outside. In the wide, shallow catch-basins where water warms slightly during clear afternoons, thin mats of dark green algae wave gently over drowned flagstones, releasing pinprick bubbles of oxygen that pop without sound at the surface.

Whatever built these millions of interlocking chambers did so without hinges, lintel-pins, glass, plaster, hearths, or carved marks. Doorways are low trapezoidal openings framed by long split slabs; inside, the rooms are dim, cool, faintly damp vaults smelling purely of wet slate and clean highland air, floored with fitted river-flat stones through which tiny capillary channels sometimes pass from one house to the next. Erosion has taken the raw quarry bite off every corner without dismantling the work. Frost heave and gravity have pressed the courses tighter into the underlying schist until it is often impossible to tell where natural outcropping rock ends and laid foundation begins. A boulder the size of a cart sits untouched inside a courtyard wall that simply flows over and around its curves; a natural fissure in the plateau floor is seamlessly stepped and lined into a deep, black-watered well.

Living creatures exist here at immense intervals, swallowed by the scale of the masonry. In an entire morning of walking across glistening terraces and along silent aqueduct-lanes, you might see only a pair of charcoal-backed diving beetles sculling across a flooded threshold, and then, half a mile away against the bright sky, a single tall, slate-grey wading bird stepping slowly across a field of beehive roofs, dipping its bill into the wet joints between slabs. Small, dense-furred rock mammals the color of wet granite occasionally slip from one wall-chink to another—fast, silent blurs that vanish into the hollow thickness of the stonework before the eye fully registers them. Far down in a broader basin where fifty shallow channels braid across an acre of sunken flagstones, three heavy-coated, short-horned grazers might move at an unhurried walk, cropping the sparse wire-grass from the cracks, their split hooves clicking once on loose shale before the stone muffles them again. 

Behind them, a low bank of cool white cloud drops down to touch the rooftops, turning the distant tiers of alleys pale and indistinct, coating fifty thousand cold stones in fresh beadlets of moisture that immediately gather, join, and begin gliding down the tilted channels toward the unseen rim of the plain, before the cloud lifts and the bright, silent afternoon rushes back across the wet grey horizon.
