There is no ground here, and there has never been stone. Every solid thing in sight—and sight reaches until the dimness swallows it, across gulfs wide enough to brew their own squalls—is living plant matter, and every inch of that plant matter has been bent, split, spliced, and knotted by hand.

It is a continent of hollow wicker suspended in thin, warm air. 

Great ribs of dark, fibrous wood, thick as river barges at their oldest joints and tapering to supple whips, arch across expanses of nothingness ten or twenty miles wide. Where two ribs meet, they have not merely grown together; they were lashed centuries ago with living lianas that bit into the bark, scarred over, and fused into bulbous, load-bearing knuckles smoothed by ages of warm rain. From these primary arches hang catenaries of braided cane, tension-webs of tough, rubbery creeper, and wide, sagging Causeways woven in plain tabby or twill out of flattened reed-trunks that still put out pale, waxed leaves along their undersides. There is no soil. Roots do not seek dirt; they are plaited back into the structure, tucked into the damp clefts of older braids, drinking from the weeping bark of their neighbors and from the wet air itself.

The air feels high—thin enough on the lungs that a brisk climb leaves the blood ringing—yet it holds a constant, breathy warmth, like the interior of a greenhouse at dusk. Light never rises past a bruised, pewter twilight. Where it comes from is unclear; there is no sun disk, only a diffuse, tarnished luminescence leaking evenly from the upper haze and gleaming faintly off millions of wet surfaces.

Because everything is wet. Not submerged, never flooded, just perpetually slick. Warm fog condenses out of the empty chasms, beads on the hairy undersides of leaves, and gathers into rills that follow the grain of the woven walkways. The wood is dark with moisture, polished to a dull, leathery sheen along the center of the paths where generations of bare feet or soft paws have worn down the ridges of the weave. Old tool-marks remain visible everywhere if you look closely: the scalloped bite of a flint adze healed over into stretched, silvery cambium; bone-awl punctures used to thread a graft five hundred years ago, now stretched into vertical eyes oozing slow, amber gum; split-willow lashings that took root in the wound they bound. Nothing was forged here. Nothing was sawed with steel. The entire horizon-spanning lattice is the work of fingernails, chipped stone, fire-hardened digging sticks, and an understanding of tension and sap that no longer requires makers to maintain itself.

Yet for all the totality of the growth, the overwhelming sensation of this place is emptiness. 

Matter makes up almost none of the volume. You stand on a woven bridge six paces wide—solid, springy, slick with warm moss—and to your left, right, above, and below, there is nothing for leagues but dim, damp air and the faint, charcoal-stroke outlines of other strands suspended at impossible distances. The world is a basket with meshes a day’s journey across. In the middle of those vast polyhedral voids, clouds curdle out of nothing, darken, dump sheets of warm, silent rain through five miles of open space, and dissolve into Rising Mist before ever touching a leaf.

Across these distances, space loses its grip on itself. 

The ancient weavers did not merely bend wood; whether by accident of scale or some forgotten knack of the craft, their knots cinch the geometry of the air. A traveler walking along a gently rising rib of braided fig-wood will watch a distant hub of thatched canopies drift *behind* them while remaining dead ahead. Some of the woven hoops—great circular frames thirty yards across, reinforced with spiral bindings of thorny rattan and hung vertically in the mist—do not open onto the air directly behind them. Looking through the wet, dripping frame of one, you see a section of the lattice where the light is slightly greener and the rain is falling sideways; step through the hoop, and the strand you just walked along vanishes, replaced by a weathered lanyard trailing off at an angle your inner ear insists is straight down, though your feet press firmly against the damp bark. 

Gravity itself follows the weave rather than any planetary core. Down is generally toward the nearest thick bundle of heartwood, until it isn't. Droplets of condensation let go from a leaf-tip, fall twenty feet into the void, slow to a halt, hang trembling in the warm gloom, and then drift laterally in lazy spirals to spatter against the underside of the same branch. Long curtains of aerial roots hang at forty-five-degree angles into nothing, pointing toward massive, unseen weights that aren't there.

Despite the thin air and the dizzying gulfs, the lattice crawls and drifts with animals—and almost none of them make a sound.

What little noise exists is immediately eaten by the miles of empty fog or muffled by wet bark and spongy lichen. There is no wind-roar, because the air moves in slow, thermal billows rather than gales; there are no hard surfaces to ring or clatter. A heavy fruit detaches, strikes a woven mat with a dull, wet *thump*, rolls through a gap, and is gone forever without an echo. 

In that hush, fauna moves in huge numbers. Broad, flat-bodied gliders—pale as peeled almonds, with membranes stretched between six elongated ribs—launch themselves in flocks of hundreds from high vascular towers. They cross the three-mile voids in slow, undulating sheets, riding warm updrafts out of the clouds, banking around warped pockets of gravity without a single cry or wing-snap. When they land on a hanging trellis, the only sound is the soft *shhh-pat* of damp skin suctioning onto wet wood. 

Along the thicker, eroded causeways, processions of long-legged, knuckle-walking herbivores crop the fast-growing shoots that sprout from the structural weave. Their hooves are split into soft, splayed pads like tree-frog toes, silent on the slick cambium. Unwitting gardeners, they chew back the wild suckers that might otherwise choke a tension-joint or unbalance a span, their saliva triggering fresh, scar-tissue thickening in the load-bearing vines. Beneath the bridges, clinging upside-down to the shadowed weave, clustered colonies of translucent, shell-less snails the size of loaves pulse with faint internal digestion, scraping excess mildew from the knots, tracked by velvet-furred, serpentine hunters that uncoil from hollow burls and strike without a hiss.

Out in the open gulfs, life needs no branches at all. Translucent bladders, veined with symbiotic algae and trailing forty-foot nets of sticky cilia, hang at neutral buoyancy in the warm, high mist, drifting through the slow rain-cells to strain airborne spores and tiny, swimming midges that live their entire lifecycles inside falling water droplets.

Nothing is static, though everything looks ancient. A steady, hydraulic pulse moves through the world. Twice in every dim cycle of brightening and darkening, a sap-tide surges through the primary ribs. You can feel it through the soles of your feet: a slow, warm swelling in the braided floor, accompanied by a faint, woody creak—the loudest sound the landscape ever makes, traveling miles along the tension-lines like the groan of a moored ship. As the turgor rises, miles-long suspension spans tighten and lift by fifty yards; woven apertures iris shut; certain spatial knots pull taut, snapping distant sectors of the lattice abruptly closer together through the fog, allowing migrating herds to step across an inch-wide seam that, six hours later when the sap ebbs and the fibers slacken, will yawn back into ten miles of empty, weeping air.

Here and there, tucked into the armpits of colossal, three-way grafts, sit hollow pavilions—domes of living basketry, their floors worn concave and satin-smooth, their walls weeping clear, sweet water into cupped burl-basins carved with stone scrapers ages ago. No builders remain in them, or perhaps the builders simply walked through one of the tighter knots and are still a few miles away and several centuries up-strand, splitting green shoots with their thumbnails, tucking the raw ends into the bark, keeping the emptiness held open.
