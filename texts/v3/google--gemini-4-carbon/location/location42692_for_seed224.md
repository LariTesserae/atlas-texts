It is no larger than a sunken courtyard—perhaps eighteen paces from the lip of the fissure to the back wall, though the count changes depending on whether you measure along the northern curve of the stone or cut straight across the basin. Wedged near the crest of an immense, arid massif, the hollow is sheltered from the open sky by an overhang of dark hornfels that leans inward like a cupped hand. Through the narrow gap above, the sky is a strip of dark, starved indigo, thin-aired and cloudless, holding neither sun nor stars in direct view, only the bruised, indirect twilight of extreme altitude.

The cold here is absolute and bone-dry. There is no ice, no rime, no snow tucked into the shaded joints of the rock; whatever trace of moisture once existed at this elevation sublimated ages ago into the near-vacuum breeze. Instead, the cold expresses itself as a tightness in the mineral surfaces, a dense, ringing stillness. Only in the deepest crease at the rear wall, where the ambient temperature dips a fraction lower, does a faint, colorless glaze appear around the slow hours of deepest dusk—a film of condensed atmospheric gas, not water, that vanishes again without ever turning wet.

Everything in the pocket is old and smoothed, though nothing wet has ever flowed here to carve it. The bedrock has been scalloped into shallow, dish-like concavities and rounded ribs, polished to a matte, slate-gray satin. Some of this is the patient work of fine silicate dust turning in eddies over millennia, but most of the wear is stranger: the stone looks rubbed down by friction against its own shifting geometry. Where two planes of rock meet, the seam is often blurred, not broken, as if the solid mineral had spent centuries slowly resolving a disagreement about where its edge ought to be. Fine gray talc lies settled in the depressions, sorted into faint concentric ripples that do not match the direction of the rare wind.

Scattered across the floor and set into the walls are the remnants of whatever enterprise once used this alcove. They are sparse, modest in size, and entirely fused with the stillness of the place. Near the center stands a tripod no taller than a waist, its three legs thin as knitting needles, composed of a dead-black material that refuses to reflect the twilight. One leg rests on the stone; the second ends two inches above the ground, standing solidly on nothing; the third passes cleanly into the hornfels without a drill-hole or fracture, though its continuation can be glimpsed faintly a yard to the left, emerging briefly from a rounded rib of rock like a dark thread through cloth before vanishing again.

Along the western wall runs a row of calibration marks—hair-thin incisions inlaid with a tarnished, silvery substance. Viewed head-on, they form a neat horizontal ruler four feet long. Viewed from three steps back, the intervals between the hashes stretch unevenly, the middle five inches widening until they occupy more visual space than the entire surrounding wall, without the rock itself appearing distorted. A small polyhedron, dull gray and smooth as a river pebble, sits in a stone basin nearby. Above it, three angular chips of dark scree hang motionless four inches off the floor. They are not levitating by any active force; rather, they are mid-fall, caught in a pocket where duration has thickened to tar. A tiny cap of windblown dust rests undisturbed on the upper facet of each falling pebble, accumulated over decades while the stones have dropped perhaps the thickness of a fingernail.

Light behaves like a heavy, sluggish sediment. What filters down through the high slot above is dim and pewter-colored, casting soft, uncertain illumination across the dust ripples. Yet in the lowest bowl of the stone floor, a pool of entirely different light has collected—a pale, exhaustedochre, dim as dying phosphor. It sits in the rock depression with a distinct meniscus, illuminating nothing six inches above its surface. If a stray grain of dust drifts down into it, the grain slows, reddens, and takes minutes to sink to the dry stone beneath, lit from below by a noon that ended somewhere else a very long time ago.

Shadows have come loose from their objects in similar ways. Behind a low, weathered boulder near the entrance lies no darkness at all; its shadow has slid four feet upslope, draped thinly across a bare, smooth bulge of rock where nothing blocks the sky. Over weeks, this stray patch of dark migrates a few millimeters, creeping along invisible contours of tension in the alcove's broken metric.

Nothing breathes here. No insect husk dries in the crevices; no bird has ever flown high enough to find the slit above. The nearest thing to growth occupies a palm-sized patch beside the sunken tripod leg: a flat, lead-colored crust of dendritic crystals. It has no cells and uses no water, feeding instead on the weak shear between two adjacent zones of mismatched time, building microscopic, fern-like fronds out of heavy isotopes sorted atom by atom from the rock. In a century, it may advance the width of a thumb.

The silence is nearly complete, padded by the thinness of the air. The wind passing thirty feet overhead across the mountain's spine does not whistle; it makes only a remote, dry hiss, like flour poured over slate, and even that sound arrives late and slightly flattened. Closer in, within the pocket itself, the only sound comes once every few hours: a tiny, glass-dry *tick* from the walls, not quite stone cooling, more like a ruler snapping back into alignment somewhere deep inside the rock, followed by a long, undisturbed quiet in which the dust, the suspended pebbles, and the pooled, orphan light resume their imperceptible settling.
