The land has given up its angles. What was once a range of coastal mountains is now a basin twelve miles across, planed down to its foundational granite and slate, smoothed like the inside of an old stone spoon. There is no soil—anything soft or loose was washed away epochs ago. Instead, there is only continuous, undulating rock, polished to a dull, oily sheen by uncounted millennia of wet wind and runoff. 

Because the rock is seamless and non-porous, water cannot sink. It collects everywhere in shallow, grey sheets an inch deep, or slides across the slight gradients in wide, trembling films. There are no rivers because there are no banks left to hold them; the entire valley floor is a slow, disorganized spillway tilting barely eastward toward an unseen sea. Occasionally, the bedrock holds the stump of an ancient intrusion—a dike of harder basalt or quartz—worn down now to a long, smooth whaleback rising no more than four feet above the wet pan.

Above it all hangs a low ceiling of bruised pewter cloud. The light is the dim, shadowless grey of late dusk, permanent and unshifting, turning the sheets of water into dull tin. The air sits just above freezing. It doesn't rain in drops; instead, the atmosphere condenses directly onto the cold stone, a relentless, drifting mist that leaves everything slick and dripping.

Nothing green grows here. There is not a blade of sedge, not a tuft of moss. Where life clings directly to the stone, it takes the form of thin, rubbery crusts of black and ochre bacterial mat, slick as grease underfoot, slowly digesting iron and sulfur from the rock and the mist. 

Yet the basin is choked with noise. It is a staggering, physical wall of sound that hits the chest and vibrates in the teeth. 

The noise does not come from the weather, though the wind pushes steadily across the open expanse. It comes from the stone itself, or rather, from what covers it. Across the miles of wet granite, spaced out in vast, shifting shoals with wide, empty stretches of shining slate between them, are millions of creatures. The bedrock is littered with heavy, flattened animals the size of dinner plates, shaped like limpets or trilobites, with shells of dark, layered horn. They graze the bacterial mats, dragging themselves forward on muscular, suctioned feet. As they move, they scrape the mineral slime with rasping plates of silica, producing a continuous, grinding *shhhk-shhhk* that, multiplied by ten million across the stone acoustic dish of the basin, sounds like a glacier tearing gravel.

Above that bass-note of grinding stone rises the shriek of the callers. Dotted sparsely across the low whaleback ridges are colonies of tall, tubular amphibians—blind, pale grey things that anchor themselves in shallow erosion pits. They inflate heavy throat-sacs and expel air through double-valved bony flutes in their skulls. The sound is a piercing, metallic hammering, like bronze anvils struck in rapid, overlapping rhythms. Because the valley is completely bare, smooth, and hard, nothing absorbs the sound. Every click, screech, and scrape bounces off the water films and skips across the low cloud ceiling, amplifying into a deafening, unbroken roar. To walk here is to be entirely deafened to one's own footsteps.

Predators work the margins of the shoals. Long, low-slung things with six splayed, padded legs—somewhere between giant salamanders and starved hounds—trot silently across the inch-deep water, kicking up brief, silvery fans of spray. They carry heavy, blunt heads low to the rock, hunting by vibration rather than sight or sound, waiting to flip a grazing shell-back before its suction locks onto the granite. Overhead, wide-winged scavengers the color of wet ash glide slowly through the mist, dropping down to tear at whatever the six-legged hunters leave behind. 

Human presence—or the presence of whatever hands work tools here—is sparse, swallowed by the open scale of the grey pan, yet unmistakable where it appears. There is no wood to burn or frame with, so everything built is made from what the basin yields: split slate, cold-hammered bog iron, shell, and grease.

Half a mile apart, perched on the slightly drier crowns of the quartz whalebacks, stand solitary cairn-huts. They are low, beehive-shaped domes built of flat, water-smoothed stones stacked without mortar, tightly wedged and chinked with gray clay dredged from deep sump-pools, then smeared thick with rendered animal fat to shed the endless wet. None rises higher than a tall man’s shoulder; anything taller catches too much of the cold wind. Outside these huts stand racks made from the long, curved rib-bones of some larger coastal beast, lashed together with raw leather thongs. Hanging from the racks are rows of dark, split meat drying slowly in the cold draft, alongside heavy chisels of dark stone bound to bone hafts—tools made purely for prying locked shells off the bedrock.

Between these scattered shelters, stretching across the empty quarter-miles of wet stone, run lines of low, V-shaped weirs. They are knee-high walls of stacked cobbles, meticulously arranged against the barely perceptible tilt of the valley floor. They do not hold back deep water—they merely guide the sliding, inch-deep runoff, funneling the drifting silt, shed shells, and sluggish water-worms into basket-traps woven from tough, splitting cartilage. 

Farther out, where the mist thickens and swallows the middle distance, tall, solitary slabs of slate have been levered upright and wedged into fissures. Each slab has three or four fist-sized holes ground completely through it. When the wind shifts slightly to the north, it catches thesebored stones and pulls a low, hollow, howling drone out of them—a deliberate, steady note cut straight through the frantic, clattering din of the grazing herds, marking a path home through the dim grey when the fog drops down to touch the water.
