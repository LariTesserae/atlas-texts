The plateau has no horizon that isn’t architecture. At six thousand feet, the basalt tableland stretches out in shallow, stepped basins across half a continent, and every square mile of it has been quarried, cast, and fitted into a low, heavy sprawl of a city that has forgotten its own edges. The air is thin enough to carry a crisp, high-altitude clarity, yet warm—held at the temperature of bathwater by a million radiant boilers, brick kilns, and subterranean steam-mains that sigh steadily through iron grates in the avenues.

Nothing green grows here. There is no soil, no turf, no weed forcing its way through the flagstones. Yet the masonry has softened over centuries into geological contours. Rainwater and constant condensation have planed the sharp corners off the monumental granite plinths; stairways dip smoothly in the center like riverbeds; massive cast-iron lintels have oxidized into thick, velvety crusts of rust-ochre that merge seamlessly with the underlying red stone. Water is never violently in motion, never roaring, just continuously present. It beads on the underside of elevated tram-trestles, runs in slick, warm films down glazed-brick retaining walls, and stands half an inch deep in the broad stone concourses, reflecting the bright, pale sky like sheets of hammered pewter. The only non-animal life is a faint, mineral-hard patina of sulfur-yellow bacterial mat and slick black biofilm clinging to the spillways where warm factory effluent meets the cool high-air mist.

And yet the stone is scarcely visible beneath the animals.

They are the reason the city feels less like a machine and more like an inhabited reef. Every cornice, gantry, cooling tower, and bridge-arch is cushioned and weighted with living mass. Across the warm slate roofs lie dense, overlapping carpets of heavy-bodied, shell-backed creatures the size of cobblestones, their calcareous plates clicking faintly as they shift by millimeters over the course of an afternoon to track the sun. They filter the soot and mineral dust from the runoff, excreting clean, chalky paste that washes down the gutters in milky ribbons. Above them, every telephone wire, crane-cable, and truss-work frame is thickened to three times its width by roosting flocks—tens of millions of broad-winged, bare-throated scavengers with oiled leathery plumage the color of wet coal and oxidized copper. 

The light is clear and unhurried, the high-altitude sun casting sharp shadows through the rising plumes of white steam from the district condensers. In those shafts of light, the air is thick with slow-moving midges and drifting clouds of fat, translucent-winged locust-things that rise from the warm fermentation vats in the lower districts. The birds do not dart after them; they simply sit along the warm brick parapets with their wide mouths agape, letting the slow convective currents funnel the insects down their throats. Everything moves with a drugged, patient economy. Even the great draft-beasts—blind, six-legged things bred or adapted over ages to haul slag-carts along the sunken rail-cuts—step with the deliberation of clock-weights, water parting smoothly around their pillar-like knees, steam ghosting off their slick gray hides.

Because nothing hurries, the sound of the metropolis has no sharp edges, though its volume is immense. It is a thick, saturated roar composed of millions of tiny wet frictions and heavy mechanical pulses. There is the bass thrum of deep-set turbine halls vibrating up through the wet flagstones; the slow, rhythmic *chuff-hiss* of atmospheric engines venting at ten-second intervals across twenty miles of terraced foundry-wards; and laid over all of it, the inescapable, deafening chorus of the fauna. Millions of throats rattling, croaking, and whistling in low, resonant registers that bounce off the slick stone facades, blending with the steady patter of condensation falling from millions of eaves and tens of millions of folded wings. 

To walk a thoroughfare here—along a broad viaduct spanning a shallow basin of tiered slate tenements and brick chimneys—is to wade through an ecology that has entirely digested industry. A colossal, riveted iron pipeline, ten feet in diameter, runs along the viaduct’s shoulder, carrying warm brine from one salt-works to another three leagues off. Its iron skin is warm to the touch, sweating continuously in the cool mountain light. Along its underside hang curtains of pale, muscular siphonophores or land-tunicates, pulsing once a minute as they drink the trickling condensation. Along its top, herds of slender, long-toed grazers pick slowly through the mineral-crust and shed feathers, stepping carefully around the brass pressure-gauges. 

Every few hours, a municipal maintenance tram rolls down the flooded flange-ways at the pace of a walking man. A heavy brass bell tolls at its prow—*clang... clang... clang*—slow and resonant. Ahead of the iron wheels, the thick mat of mud-colored amphibians and armored detritivores parts without panic, sliding smoothly aside like heavy grease on warm water, and closes over the warm rails again ten yards behind the caboose. 

Far out across the basin, ten or twenty miles distant where the city steps up toward another shallow basalt rim, a bank of low cloud sits directly among the smokestacks. It neither rolls in nor burns off; it simply hangs there, fed by the cooling towers and drunk by the roosting millions, white and bright in the afternoon sun. Beneath it, cranes the size of cathedral spires pivot a few degrees an hour, hoisted by chain-drives thick with grease and nesting swallows, lifting blocks of weathered stone from an ancient district that has settled too deep into the marshy pan, to lay them down again on a higher terrace—continuing a slow, continental turnover of brick, iron, water, and bone that has no reason ever to finish.
