You do not see the hollow until you are nearly standing over its edge. Across the surrounding lowland flats, there is no warning rise, no plume tall enough to survive the crosswinds—only an abrupt, jagged drop where a slab of the plain gave way along fresh fault lines and settled twenty feet down. 

The space is roughly the size of an old stone barn with its roof torn off, open to ordinary, flat daylight. Because the collapse is geologically recent, nothing down here has had time to soften. The walls are stepped faces of dark, fine-grained traprock that cleaved along clean planes when the ground dropped. Every ledge, fallen block, and underfoot flake retains its quarry-fresh edge; drop a canvas bag on the talus at the northern corner and the stone will slice the weave. There is no soil in the bottom, no silt, no rounded gravel. There is only fractured dark rock, plenty of empty room between the fallen slabs, and water everywhere.

It is fiercely, oppressively hot. Not the dry, baking heat of sun on stone, because the sun here is unremarkable—a plain, even sky lighting everything clearly without glare—but an upwelling, saturated heat rising straight through the floor. Water seeps continuously from hairline horizontal joints halfway up the walls, sheeting down the black rock so the entire perimeter gleams as if coated in clear oil. On the stepped floor, it gathers into ankle-deep, linked basins. The water is clear to the point of invisibility; you only know where a dry shelf ends and a scalding pool begins by the slight refraction at the margin, and by the thin, brittle skins of precipitated mineral that form across the calmer surfaces. Because the flow is sluggish—merely a millimeter-thick creeping spill from one flat stone pan to the next—these mineral rafts have time to grow. They look like sheets of frosted mica or tarnished tin floating on the water, slowly drifting a few inches an hour until they touch a sharp rock edge and stack up in fragile, geometric shingles.

The air inside the pit is dense, wet, and loud. 

The sound hits you the moment your head drops below the rim, where the vertical walls catch and hold the acoustics. It is a continuous, unvarying roar, roughly the pitch and volume of a river weir heard from ten feet away, yet nothing visible is moving fast enough to make it. The noise comes from deep inside a dry-looking diagonal cleft on the western wall, mixed with the steady mechanical drone of the equipment anchored near the center of the floor. Down beneath the rock, high-pressure vapor is forcing its way through some narrow subterranean throat before diffusing into the groundwater, turning the entire bedrock basin into a sounding board. You feel it in your teeth and sternum. Speaking requires shouting directly into someone’s ear, yet looking around the space, everything appears nearly static. Steam does not jet or billow; the air is already so hot that vapor only condenses lazily a few feet below the upper rim, forming a thin, translucent ceiling that shreds away as soon as it rises into the cooler plain breeze.

Set out across the wet stone floor, with wide expanses of bare rock left empty around them, are three heavy pieces of industrial hardware. They are not ruined, nor are they new. At the deepest puddle stands a squat, cast-iron manifold bolted directly into drilled rock with thick, galvanized expansion pins. Heavy-gauge steel pipes—insulated in wrapped gray lagging darkened with damp—run from the manifold across the low shelves on little A-frame trestles, converging on an unenclosed heat-exchanger and a heavy rotary pump housed under a corrugated tin hood no bigger than a doghouse. A thick armored power cable snakes straight up the southern rock face, pinned into cracks with iron staples, and disappears over the lip into the distance. 

The machinery runs without pause and without urgency. No pistons slam; no flywheels spin in the open. Inside the casing, an electric motor turns against high pressure at a constant, heavy load, adding a hard, tonal hum—a steady B-flat—to the broadband rush of the rock cleft. On the side of the manifold, behind a glass face beaded on the outside with sweat, a brass pressure needle sits pinned at two o'clock. It does not flutter. Once every several minutes, a spring-loaded brass relief cock on the lowest pipe lets go of a cupful of scalding condensate, dropping it onto the basalt with a brief hiss that is entirely swallowed by the surrounding roar. Aside from the cable, an iron wrench left rusting into a bright orange crust on a dry block, and faint chalk numbers half-washed from the rock wall beside the cleft, there is no sign of whoever installed the rig.

Nothing with lungs or legs lives down here. No water striders dimple the warm basins; no spiders string webs between the pipe trestles and the stone; no birds drop down to drink. Whether it is the water temperature—hot enough to redden skin in three seconds—the punishing acoustic vibration, or a heavy, invisible pooling of carbonic gas hugging the ankles in the lowest corners, animal life treats the rim as a wall. If a beetle tumbles over the sharp lip from the plain above, its shell stays where it lands in the shallow water, immaculate and un-scavenged, slowly acquiring a pale casing of mineral rind over the weeks.

Plants manage only a sparse, calculated foothold, spaced far apart where micro-gradients of temperature allow. None grow on the floor. Halfway up the weeping rock walls, where the descending water is cooler than the rising vapor, patches of rubbery, olive-black thermophilic algae cling to the stone in slick streaks. Higher still, tucked into dry vertical fractures three or four feet below the rim, solitary tufts of stiff, blue-gray wiregrass jut horizontally into the space, trembling slightly in the rising convective heat. Around the bolted feet of the upper cable staples, a pale, leafless lichen—crusty and segmented like dried putty—spreads at a pace measured in millimeters per decade, feeding on the warm damp and trace metals leaching from the iron.

Nothing in this pocket hurries. The water creeps invisibly over its dark stone steps; the floating mineral skins thicken by a hair's breadth across an afternoon; the iron bolts bleed slow, rust-colored fans down the gray rock; the heat-exchanger draws off calories from the earth at the exact same rate at noon as at midnight. Above the sharp black horizon of the rim, pale clouds cross an ordinary sky in complete silence, belonging to another country entirely, while five feet below the grass roots, the wet stone box holds its heat and roars to itself.
