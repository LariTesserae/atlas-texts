High in the cloud-wrapped spine of an ancient range, there is a sinkhole that is no larger than a village commons. It has no name, no trail leading to its lip, and no sky to speak of. Overhead, the upper walls of dark dolostone lean inward like the ribs of a capsized hull, bridged by a permanent, tangled mat of ancient juniper roots and fallen timber. Together with the perpetual mountain fog, this canopy chokes out the sun entirely. What filters down into the basin is not daylight, but a bruised, sourceless gloom—a 0.2-lumen twilight that never brightens at noon and never deepens into true, blind night.

The rock here has finished falling. Millennia of acidic seepage have worn every fractured edge into smooth, bulbous lobes, like melted tallow frozen in stone. There are no sharp angles left. The walls do not crumble; they weep. Cold mountain water, trapped in the high aquifers, sweats continuously through the porous limestone, coating every vertical surface in a slick, clear skin that slides downward without forming a stream, pooling at the bottom in black, ankle-deep bog-mirrors before draining invisibly through the floor at the exact rate it arrives. The air sits at a permanent forty-six degrees Fahrenheit, so saturated with mist that breathing feels like drinking thin broth. 

Within this stone vessel, space has run out. Every square millimeter of weathered rock is claimed by dark-adapted flora that thrives in the chill and the murk. Cushion mosses grow three feet thick, swelling over boulders in dense, emerald-black bolsters that hold gallons of icy water like sponges. Pale, translucent liverworts shingle over the moss; waxy-leaved, stunted rhododendrons twist laterally through the center of the room, their bark entirely invisible beneath shaggy pelts of beard-lichen and dripping, hair-like spleenworts. Broad-fronded ferns unfurl in slow motion, pressing flat against one another until the basin is not an open chamber, but a solid, spongy labyrinth of cold green matter through which one would have to swim rather than walk.

Yet if you press a hand against what looks like a wet, mossy bulge on a tree trunk, the bulge shifts. 

The flora is merely the scaffolding; the true substance of the sinkhole is flesh. The darkness is packed to suffocation with cold-blooded and quiet-blooded life. Beneath and atop the ferns, thousands of heavy, blind cave-salamanders, the color of wet slate and as thick as a human wrist, lie braided together in motionless heaps along the weeping rock ledges, absorbing oxygen directly through their glistening skins. Every underside of every broad leaf is cobbled completely with translucent, thumbnail-sized glass frogs and coiled, pale-shelled snails, packed edge-to-edge like scales on a fish. Thick, velvety harvestmen with legs spanning six inches hang in overlapping, trembling curtains beneath the root-arches overhead, millions of hair-thin limbs interlocking in a dense, brown suspension. In the shallow black pools at the basin’s floor, blind, pigmentless amphipods are so concentrated that the water moves with the sluggish, granular viscosity of porridge. Small, sightless shrews with oil-slicked fur slip continuously through tunnels inside the three-foot moss beds, navigating strictly by whisker-touch, brushing directly over the backs of torpid toads without waking them.

Despite the staggering mass of living tissue—tons of gill, carapace, damp fur, and mucus crammed into the volume of a cathedral nave—the place makes almost no noise. There are no wind currents down here to rustle a frond. Nothing calls out; in a space this small and dark, where mates and prey are literally touching you at all times, vocalization is obsolete. Predation happens by slow contact: a salamander’s jaw unhinging silently to engulf a passing slug, a spider dropping half an inch to embrace a beetle. The only sound is a soft, pervasive, wet static. It is the collective whisper of thousands of damp bellies sliding fractions of an inch over liverwort, the minute *tink* of water droplets detaching from saturated moss tips to strike a snail shell, the faint, rhythmic clicking of crustacean mandibles beneath the surface of the puddles. It sounds like a room full of people breathing evenly in their sleep.

Near the lowest shelf of the sinkhole, half-submerged in a clear pool where the limestone forms a smooth basin, lies the single disruption to the purely biological order—though it has been assimilated so thoroughly it no longer reads as foreign. Wedged into natural, water-worn niches in the rock are half a dozen crudely knapped chert blades and three shallow bowls hollowed out of soapstone with bone chisels. Around them, heavy cobbles of river-granite, carried up from some valley thousands of feet below, have been arranged into low, thumb-width channels that deliberately slow the weeping wall-water, guiding it over flat beds of submerged slate. 

Nothing sits in these stone cradles except colonies of strange, brain-like gelatinous bryozoans and clusters of blind, leathery-egged amphibians, grazing on a pale, sweet-smelling biofilm that grows exclusively on the chipped edges of the imported chert. Whether the hands that knapped the stone belonged to early humans who visited once five thousand years ago, or whether some current, unseen creature occasionally descends through the root-roof at intervals of decades to adjust a pebble with clumsy, calloused fingers and harvest a stone bowl's worth of pale roe, it is impossible to tell. There are no footprints in the moss—the moss springs back too slowly and grows over everything too evenly. There is no fire-ash, no shelter, no path. The tools sit under a quarter-inch of clear, cold water, coated in the same velvet algae as the walls, functioning forever without maintenance.

Nothing rots dramatically here, and nothing blooms. There are no seasons down in the sink. The mountain above may blizzard in January and bake under thin alpine skies in August, Visionaries and empires may rise on the plains miles below, the timberline outside may creep up and down the slopes over centuries, yet inside this dim stone lung, the temperature does not drift by two degrees. When a shrew dies in its moss-tunnel, the amphipods and pale fungi dismantle it silently within hours, returning its mass directly into the roots of the fern overhead, which feeds the snails, which feed the salamanders. No soil washes out; no new light breaks in. Every niche is full to bursting, every calorie is spoken for before it falls, and the whole cold, drenched, breathing tangle hangs suspended in a dark and crowded peace that has already lasted ten thousand years and has no reason ever to end.
