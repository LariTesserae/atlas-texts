The chamber is a sunken cube of pale dressed limestone, twenty feet on a side and nearly filled to its lip with clear, steaming-warm water. There is no natural earth or unworked rock anywhere in view. Four sheer walls drop straight from a narrow rim of flat coping stones—set flush with the ground overhead—down to a floor of tight-fitted square flags. Above, nothing roofs the shaft; the sky sits directly on the stone square like a blazing lid, pouring raw, unobstructed noon light straight down into the basin.

Because the stone was quarried white and the joints were smeared smooth with fresh lime-and-sand mortar, the entire submerged room acts as a mirror box. Sunlight strikes the turbulent surface and breaks into dazzling, shifting nets of glare that lash across the underwater walls and illuminate the bottom pavers as brightly as an open courtyard. To look down into it is to squint against a fierce, watery incandescence where dust motes don't hang—only millions of tiny, trapped air bubbles racing upward like sparks.

The builders left recently. Below the waterline, every block still carries the crisp, parallel bite of iron tooth-chisels and flat droves; not a single corner has softened yet, and the submerged stair that clings to the eastern wall—narrow, steep risers cut from single blocks without a balustrade—has edges sharp enough to scrape skin. Even the mason’s alignment scratches remain clearly legible beside the mortar seams fifteen feet under. In the center of the floor sits a shallow, square settling sump, utterly empty of silt, gravel, or drowned leaves.

Ninety percent of the architecture exists underwater, leaving only a handspan or two of hot, echoing air beneath an overhanging stone cornice. Into that narrow airspace, a thick rectangular conduit of fired red terracotta juts from the north wall and discharges a heavy, unceasing torrent. The water comes in hot—well above skin temperature, routed directly from a thermal spring before it has had time to cool—and hits the surface of the basin with a continuous, hollow roar. Enclosed by flat stone on five sides, the sound builds upon itself: a relentless, drumming thunder laced with the higher slap and hiss of bursting foam, loud enough to vibrate in the soles of anyone standing on the coping above.

Beneath the plunge point, a white pillar of churning froth drives halfway to the floor before breaking apart and curling outward across the empty middle of the room. You can read the invisible current by the trail of bubbles sweeping steadily south toward a submerged grate made of slotted slate slabs, pinned into the masonry with swollen oak pegs. There, the water exits silently, pulling a slow, warm undertow through the bright stillness of the lower corners.

Nothing swims in this volume. There are no minnows holding against the current, no water striders skating the quieter eddies near the stairs, no snails grazing the blocks, no drowned insects drifting on the bottom. The heat, the screens upstream, and the sheer newness of the masonry have kept all animal life out. 

Only a very sparse scattering of green has begun to claim the margins. Directly beneath the terracotta spout, where the warm flow slams hardest against the stone, three vivid emerald ribbons of thermophilic algae have anchored to the rough chisel-grooves, streaming sideways and whipping endlessly in the blast. Higher up, in the narrow splash zone just above the heaving waterline, a faint green blush of protonema dust tints the porous mortar, and two small maidenhair ferns—no bigger than an open palm—have wedged themselves into an unmortared weep-hole on the shaded south face, trembling continuously in the wet, rising draft and the concussive air pushed around by the fall.

Everything else is empty geometry, heat, noise, and blinding light: clear water rushing endlessly into a brand-new stone vessel, shaking walls of white refraction across blocks that have not yet lost a grain of their cut edges.
