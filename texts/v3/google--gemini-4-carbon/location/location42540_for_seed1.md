There is nowhere to set a boot down without stepping on a housing, a valve-lip, a shivering casing of wet cartilage, or the cold iron rim of something breathing. 

From rim to distant, invisible rim, the basin has been planed nearly flat by an incomprehensible span of time. Whatever mountains once stood here were ground down epochs ago into a wide, saucer-like shield of dark grey gneiss and heavy metallic silt, polished smooth as the inside of an old bearing. No wall has ever been raised here. There are no foundations, no roads, no cables strung on poles, no symmetry of design or remnant of an architect. Everything is wild, grown in the blind dark, and packed together as tightly as mussels on a breakwater for twenty miles in every direction.

The cold is fierce, well below the freezing point of ordinary water, burning the nostrils with every breath—yet almost nothing is solid ice. Over the flat expanse hangs a low, dim ceiling of bruised-pewter vapour that continually condenses and dumps cold brine, mineral oil, and heavy, phase-sick water back onto the plain. Water sheets over the packed backs of the fauna, pools in the hollows between bodies, and runs in braided, slushy gutters across the ancient bedrock. Where frost does try to form, the local physics misfire: rime crystallizes in jagged vertical panes a millimeter thick that stand six feet into the wind without snapping, or locks into flickering, half-real lattices that wet the hand right through their apparent solidity.

The noise is tremendous and unbroken—a wet, low-altitude roar composed of ten million small industrial percussion events happening at once. Pistons of dense, greased bone slam home inside marrow-lined cylinders; fleshy bellows suck freezing slush and spit pressurized steam; chitinous flywheels spin at blurred velocities, flinging droplets of grey water into the fog. Because nothing here was built, nothing shares a standard gauge. A creature the size of an oil drum, entirely sessile and anchored to the gneiss by mats of black iron-sulfide hair, does nothing all day and night except draw heavy water through a series of internal turbines, drop the water’s temperature by another four degrees—somehow extracting work from what is already freezing—and vent a rhythmic, deafening *chuff* of bluish exhaust through a horn of flanged keratin. Pressed directly against its flanks are fifty smaller organisms shaped like flattened gear-trains, lacking heads or guts, stealing rotational energy directly off the trembling hide of the larger beast by pressing their toothed margins into its flesh.

No green thing grows. Aside from thin, rubbery films of black sulfur-slime slicking the high-friction surfaces where shells rub together, botany does not exist. Energy moves through pressure, heat-theft, electrochemistry, and predation.

Look fifty yards out into the dim, sourceless gloom—though distance here does not behave cleanly—and the sheer saturation of the plain becomes dizzying. The ground is a continuous, heaving cobbled pavement of wet backs, spinning rotors, shivering manifolds, and glistening bladders. Long, segmented crawler-engines—thirty feet of articulated cast-iron scutes and pale, cold muscle—haul themselves directly *over* the tight carpet of stationary fauna, crushing vents and shearing off chimneys, leaving grooves of leaking black coolant and mangled gearing that are filled within seconds by rolling, bowling-ball-sized scavengers. These scavengers do not eat with mouths; they weld themselves temporarily onto torn metallic flesh, strike a blinding blue electrical arc across the wet gap—briefly throwing stark, jagged shadows for half a mile under the low clouds—and smelt the wreckage directly into their own hulls.

Around those arc-flashes, reality comes apart at the seams. 

The breakdown is neither dramatic nor explosive; it is chronic, worn into the landscape like the erosion of the rock itself. Above the denser pockets of electromagnetic predation, cause and effect lose their grip on sequence. The crack of an electrical discharge slams through the wet air three seconds *before* the blue flash illuminates the fog, and the ozone smell arrives yesterday or not at all. In wide patches of the shallow marsh-slush, mechanical properties detach from bodies altogether. There are pools of dark, freezing water that possess pure *torque*: step or drift into them, and there is no whirlpool, the water lies dead flat, yet anything touching the surface is wrenched violently clockwise as if gripped in the jaws of an invisible lathe. Elsewhere, exhaust plumes vent out of empty air four feet above the mud, severed from the boiler-worms producing them half a mile to the west.

Sometimes a herd of tall, spindly pump-striders—creatures consisting almost entirely of three-jointed dielectric stilts and a high, swinging pendulum of hot lead wrapped in grey blubber—moves across the plain, and as they pass through a bruised fold in the air, one individual multiplies without dividing. For four hundred yards, the exact same strider occupies twelve adjacent positions simultaneously, twelve identical left forelegs striking twelve different shells in the undergrowth with a single shared *clang*, before the smear resolves and eleven of them cease to have ever been there, leaving behind eleven very real dents in the carapace-mat below.

Light never brightens past the level of a winter dusk. There is no discernible sun behind the grease-gray overcast, only a broad, sourceless pewter glare diffused through miles of condensing mist, punctuated below by the dull cherry glow of submerged biological fireboxes and the erratic stutter of short-circuits running across wet backs. When a salt-squall rolls across the low country, the rain comes down thick and syrupy, hissing furiously wherever it strikes an exposed heat-sink, sending up walls of white vapor that hug the ground instead of rising.

Beneath the clamor of valves, stamps, ratchets, and wet exhausts, there is a deeper vibration conducted entirely through the ancient stone underfoot—a slow, heavy, sub-audible thrumming. The bedrock itself has been vibrated at industrial frequencies for so many millions of years that its crystalline structure has given up its solidity in places. In sunken hollows where the water runs deepest, granite behaves like slow tar, rippling in frozen standing waves around the vibrating anchors of the oldest turbine-reefs. Over these reefs, flocks of small, loud, blade-winged things hover without moving their wings, held aloft by pockets of broken inertia, clicking like telegraph keys as they dip long copper proboscises into the boiling cold vents below to drink the stray voltage.
