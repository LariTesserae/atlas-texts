You find it only by stepping off a razor-edge of freshly sheared obsidian, high above the dry cloud-deck, and dropping into a blister in the mountain’s flank. 

It is a pocket no wider than a courtyard, roofed by a half-dome of cooling basalt and open on one side to a white, blinding sky. The altitude makes the air thin and crisp, yet the heat inside the hollow is staggering—a dry,kiln-like radiance baking upward from the floor. There is no dust here, no sand, no pebble rounded by wind or rain. Every mineral surface looks fractured ten minutes ago. Needle-sharp ridges of raw slag and unweathered silicates catch the ordinary, clear afternoon sunlight, throwing crisp black shadows across the stone. Nothing has had time to wear down. In fact, along the back wall, the rock is still arriving: stone doesn't extrude from magma here so much as assemble itself out of the dry air, thin geometric plates of feldspar and iron clicking silently into existence along jagged seams, building the cliff face outward millimeter by millimeter over weeks.

Nothing has been built here by any hand. There are no walls, paths, wires, or cut stones. Yet the hollow is unmistakably industrial, because the lushness growing out of the pristine fissures is a mechanized flora, and the physics of the pocket has slipped sideways into heavy manufacturing.

Because the space is sparse, each plant stands well apart from the others, given gallery-like room on the raw mineral floor. They are broad-leaved, extravagant things, waist-high and thriving without a drop of moisture. Instead of sap, they run on thermal differentials and molten salts drawn straight from the unformed crust. Their stems are woody cables of braided copper and carbon; their wide, drooping fronds are dark, matte-green photovoltaic hides, heavy and greased with dry graphite. Where a fern would uncurl a fiddlehead, these bushes extrude glowing orange coils of hot tungsten that slowly flatten and cool into serrated leaves. At the center of three separate thickets, large floral structures—the size of cabbages and shaped like turbine housings—rotate at an almost imperceptible crawl, one revolution every four hours, refining airborne nitrogen with a faint, hot smell of ozone and scorched iron.

There are no insects scaling these stalks, no lizards baking on the glassy shelves, no birds riding the thermals outside the lip. The ecology has no animals at all, only this sparse, lush botanical foundry quietly running in the clear mountain sun.

And if you watch the empty space between the plants, you realize the engine-work isn't confined to the stems. Reality in this high, hot blister is poorly bolted together, behaving like a factory floor whose machinery has forgotten to take physical form. 

Three feet above a clean slab of obsidian, a patch of empty air shimmers with the exact refractive index of heavy machine oil. Nothing is there, yet the clear afternoon light bends sharply through it, casting a slow-turning shadow of spoked gears onto the stone below—gears that have no metal body above to own them. Near the lip of the drop-off, gravity operates on a cam-shaft cycle: a loose flake of newly materialized mica lifts an inch off the ground, holds steady for six minutes while vibrating at sixty hertz, and sets itself back down three inches to the left, sorting itself by weight along an invisible conveyor.

The heat itself comes in squared-off blocks. Step two paces toward the center of the hollow and the temperature rises fifteen degrees across a boundary as flat and distinct as a sheet of glass; step back, and you are merely warm again. Along these thermal walls, the air smells faintly of hot grease, static electricity, and crushed dry sage.

For all its industrial nature and ontological fraying, the pocket is profoundly quiet. There is no roar of furnaces, no clatter. The pace of the place is too patient for noise. You hear the vast, high-altitude silence of the dry world beyond the ledge, and beneath it, a sparse acoustic texture: the occasional crisp *tick* of a cooling copper leaf expanding in the sun; a low, sub-audible electrical hum vibrating through the soles of your boots from deep inside the raw geology; and once every ten or fifteen minutes, a soft, pneumatic sigh from nowhere at all, as if an unseen valve in the fabric of the air just equalized its pressure with the sky. 

Along the inner seam of the dome, another wafer of raw black rock snaps cleanly out of nothingness, taking its place on the wall. Six feet away, a broad metallic frond shifts a fraction of a degree toward the sun, drinking the clear light, turning heat and broken physics directly into leaf.
