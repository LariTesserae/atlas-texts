You stand inside a single, vast room whose walls, floor, and ceiling are made entirely of old wood and compressed moss. 

There is no dirt here, and if there is stone beneath this place, it has not seen the sky in epochs. Long ago, the land wore down to a flat, coastal-adjacent pan, an exhausted basin with no slope left to give. Into that stillness grew a grove of colossal, buttressed hardwood trees—something akin to ancient cedars or temperate strangler figs—that eventually touched, fused, and grafted together into a single continuous organism. Over millennia, the inner trunks rotted out while the outer cambium thickened, leaving a hollow, cathedral-sized atrium entirely enclosed by living timber.

The scale is intimate enough that you can see the far wall clearly, perhaps two hundred paces away, yet the interior volume feels startlingly empty. There is no understory. No shrubs, no dangling vines, no clutter of branches interrupting the sightlines. The trees long ago pruned their interior limbs, dedicating all leaf-matter to a dense, interlocking canopy high overhead. What remains inside is sheer, sweeping architecture: soaring fluted walls of silver-gray bark, braided root-buttresses arched like flying ribs, and a floor composed entirely of millennia of fallen needles, bark scales, and duff compressed into a springy, peat-like mat covered in pale green leucobryum moss. Every square inch of boundary is alive, botanical, and immense, wrapping around a cool, uncluttered pocket of clear air.

Light enters from above in crisp, normal daylight, diffused through gaps where the upper boughs sway slightly apart, casting pale, dustless shafts across the wide floor. The air is sharply cool—the temperature of a cellar or an autumn morning—and carries a persistent, pleasant dampness. There is no standing water, no stream or puddle, because the spongy root-floor drinks moisture instantly. Instead, moisture arrives as fine mist condensing off the cool bark, leaving dark, slick streaks down the wooden walls and beading in jewel-like droplets on the tips of the moss cushions. 

Despite the lack of buildings, walls, or cleared paths, you know immediately where you are in relation to the rest of the world because of the sound.

A heavy, rhythmic, industrial roar saturates the hollow. Somewhere just beyond the massive outer rind of the living walls—perhaps half a mile over the flat, eroded plain—sits an enormous, unseen facility. Likely a hydroelectric spillway, a geothermal turbine station, or a massive timber-processing plant running at continuous capacity. Inside the hollow grove, the dense, curved wooden walls act like the body of a cello. You feel the low-end thrum of heavy turbines through the soles of your shoes via the root-mat, while the air vibrates with the steady, loud, mechanical *chug-hum-chug* of scaled machinery and the high hiss of escaping steam. Only a single trace of that world crosses the threshold: one thick, rusted iron ground-cable, laid down eighty years ago and long forgotten, running straight across the moss floor before disappearing cleanly into the grafted wooden wall, the bark having swallowed it whole like water closing over a stone.

Because the noise is entirely constant—never rising, never shutting off, day or night—it has ceased to be an intrusion and has become a permanent physical law of the room. And the animals have claimed it.

High up in the vaulted wooden ceiling and packed along the dry, scalloped ledges of the inner bark live thousands of swifts, swallows, and small brown bats, alongside colonies of plump wood-pigeons and squirrels. Outside, predators hunt by ear; inside this resonant wooden drum, the industrial roar masks every rustle of a nest, every scratch of a claw, every begging chirp of a hatchling. Yet to hear one another over the distant turbines, the birds have adapted by singing at the absolute top of their lungs. 

The result is deafening and glorious. Layered directly over the deep, unchanging mechanical bass of the unseen engines is a piercing, bright, chaotic chorus of shrill birdcalls, clicking wings, and chattering squirrels. 

Nothing much moves in the vast middle air of the chamber, keeping the central volume open and clean, save for the occasional swift slicing cleanly through a shaft of daylight from one bark-wall to the other. Nothing grows taller on the floor; nothing erodes any further; the engines outside never throttle down. The dampness beads on the moss, the cool air smells cleanly of wet cedar and faint ozone, and the hollow wooden room holds its equilibrium—ancient, thunderous, and completely alive.
