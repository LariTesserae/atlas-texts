The mountains did not fall; they were hollowed out and replaced. 

At an altitude where rock usually splinters under frost, the air here is inexplicably mild, buoyed by broad, slow updrafts rising from some unseen lowland three miles down. Long ago, the peaks existed as stone—granite or basalt—but eons of wind and ancient, patient roots reduced the mineral mass to dust, and the dust blew away. What remains at this extreme elevation is the root-work itself, ossified into living wood that has kept growing outward and upward to hold the shape of the vanished range. 

To stand here is to stand on a continuous, springy, sun-bleached mat of interwoven limbs three feet thick, stretched across an abyss. Look down through a gap in the weave, and there is nothing—just pale blue air dropping away into haze, crossed fifty yards below by another solitary, sweeping tendon of ironwood as thick as a barge, and below that, another, spaced vast distances apart in the bright, empty sky. The landscape is an open valley five miles wide, composed entirely of botanical suspension. There are no leaves to clutter the middle air, no soil, no brush. The space between the structural boughs is staggering and clear. You can see across the whole basin to where the living ridges curve up against the clouds, smooth and rounded as worn bone, having settled into the oldest, most stable geometry capable of bearing the wind.

All the moisture comes on the breath of those warm updrafts. Twice a day, mist rises through the floorboards of the world, not as rain, but as a warm, grey sweat that slicks the pale bark and collects in shallow, scarred basins where broad limbs fuse together. There are no rivers, only these wide puddles of amber-tinted condensation sitting in the crooks of the giant sprawl, smelling faintly of tannin and wet dust. Where the bark drinks the mist, it flushes a dull, velvety olive; an hour later, under the clear, high sun, it dries back to silver-grey.

You cannot hear yourself think. 

Because the valley is hollow—an enormous wooden drumhead strung tight across miles of thin, resonant air—and because it is utterly choked with life. Every square foot of the pale bark is crawling, clinging, or nesting, and the acoustic amplification of the empty space turns their presence into a physical wall of noise. Billions of broad-backed, copper-colored cicadas, big as plums, sit shoulder-to-shoulder along the undersides of the structural boughs, vibrating their thoraxes in synchronized waves. The sound does not fade into dirt or foliage; there is neither dirt nor foliage to absorb it. It rings directly through the tense, dry wood beneath your soles and echoes across the mile-wide gaps of open sky. When one ridge shifts pitch, the opposing ridge answers four seconds later, a roaring, metallic surf of stridulation that presses against the eardrums until vision blurs slightly at the edges.

And above and through the roar comes the clatter of the grazers and the shriek of what hunts them. Flocks of flightless, six-legged climbers—creatures the size of hounds with hooked, clacking hooves made for gripping curved bark—sprint in tight, nervous herds along the swooping highways of the limbs. Five thousand hooves striking hollow ironwood at a dead run sounds like a continuous landslide of dry timber. Above them, riding the warm thermals in the vast, empty bowl of the valley, wheel clouds of leather-winged swifts and heavier,pelican-jawed raptors. They dive through the open weave of the floor, vanishing beneath your feet into the sunlit gulf below and bursting back up fifty yards away, scattering showers of copper beetles that click like hailstones against the wood. 

Everything moves in visible, sweeping currents across the open span. A shadow passes over a mile of pale root-plain, and instantly a million basking lizards—flat as leaves, colored like dried sap—slap their bellies against the timber and vanish into the crevices of the weave with a collective *zip* like tearing canvas, momentarily audible even over the cicada-engine. 

Scattered miles apart across these rolling, high-altitude meadows of wood are the huts, though they are less built than persuaded out of the floor. Whoever lives—or moves—through this roaring sky uses heavy chisels of dark river-stone carried up from some unimaginable bottomland, along with wedges of cured bone and simple levers. You find a place where someone pried apart the living tendons of the ground, wedging rocks into the splits to force the tree-mass to swell into a low, domed blister for shelter against the screaming wind and the deafening bugs. Inside these thick-walled burls, the roar drops to a heavy, chest-rattling hum, like sleeping inside the hull of a wooden ship in high seas.

Outside the blisters, driven directly into the living floor, stand rows of tall, notched poles lashed together with stripped bark-sinew and smeared with boiled resin. They catch the mist-drifts on woven mats of shed reptile skin, funneling drips of water down waxed cords into gourds hung beneath the floor-level, down in the shade where the sun won't steal it. Nearby, you might find a flat stretch of wide bough scarred black from careful, clay-lined fires, surrounded by heaps of cracked cicada shells and splintered grazer-shins, alongside a forgotten adze with a handle worn shiny by grease and sweat. 

No people are visible in the immediate mile, though three ridges over, a thin, pale thread of smoke rises straight up before being sheared sideways by the canyon draft. Between here and there lies nothing at all—just dazzling, normal daylight pouring unimpeded through a hollow mountain of wood, vibrating endlessly with the frantic, deafening industry of claws, wings, and singing shells suspended at the top of the world.
