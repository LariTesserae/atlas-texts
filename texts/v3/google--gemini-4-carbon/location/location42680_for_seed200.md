There is no mud here, because nothing has had time to wear down into silt or clay. Beneath everything lies fresh basalt, laid down across a sunken basin miles wide—black, glassy rope-folds and blistered tumuli whose edges are still razor-sharp, unrounded by wind or running water. Snap off a curtain of root-mat and the stone underneath glints like newly fractured obsidian, every frozen gas bubble intact, every crystalline grain crisp enough to catch skin.

Yet none of it is bare for more than an inch. Water rises constantly from the cooling heat below rather than falling from above, sweating up through hairline cooling cracks and condensing out of the saturated air onto every surface. It collects in shallow, lukewarm saucers among the black ropes, brims over without enough force to carve a groove, and turns back into vapor three feet away. There are no streams, no gullies, no drainage network at all—only an endless, closed circulation a few yards tall, recycling the same mineral-heavy moisture between rock face and mist.

The heat sits at the upper edge of what flesh can tolerate. The air is thick as warm broth, motionless, pressing with equal weight into lungs and under leaves. Overhead hangs a seamless ceiling of pewter-and-bruise cloud, low enough to swallow the basin’s distant rim in haze, dense enough to reduce the sky to a dim, shadowless twilight. Light has no direction here. The undersides of leaves are nearly as bright as their tops, lit by a dull, amber-gray glow diffused evenly from horizon to horizon. Because the vegetation rarely rises higher than a tall man’s shoulder—spreading sideways in dense, overlapping shelves rather than competing upward for a sun that never breaks through—you can look out from the crest of any low lava blister and see the whole wide pan stretching away for miles: an unbroken, glistening expanse of dark olive, bruised violet, and slick black stone dissolving gradually into hot gray vapor.

Without soil to anchor in, the plants grip the raw rock with pale, muscular nets of aerial roots that wrap the sharp basalt ridges like wet canvas. Their leaves are thick, swollen paddles coated in waxy cuticles dark enough to drink the starved light—deep aubergine, iron-green, black-red—packed so tightly together that their edges curl against one another. Between the vascular thickets, translucent bladder-mosses and gelatinous liverworts bridge the jagged gaps in the stone, swollen with trapped condensate. Nothing wilts and nothing bursts into seasonal bloom; new fronds unroll at the exact pace old ones liquefy into clear nutrient jelly that the root-nets immediately drink back up before humus can accumulate.

Still, the plants look almost like scaffolding for the animals.

Every cubic foot of the basin is occupied, layered with breathing, pulsing, stridulating bodies to a degree that blurs the boundary between surface and resident. Along the black lava ropes where the stone shows through, broad, flat-shelled gastropods the size of palms sit tiled shell-to-shell, secreting slick ribbons of mineralized mucus over the volcanic glass so their soft feet are never cut; over decades without rain to wash those tracks away, their hardened slime has glazed whole ridges in pearlescent, rubbery highways. Clustered across the dark leaf-paddles are thousands upon thousands of heavy-bodied arthropods—wingless, since flying through air this dense and hot costs more energy than crawling—their carapaces beaded with condensation, antennae perpetually brushing the antennae of their neighbors.

Peer down between the overlapping stems and the density only deepens. Every warm saucer of water in the basalt is braided solid with pale, ribless amphibians and gill-fringed worms coiled around one another like simmering noodles, skin pulsing slowly to exchange gas in oxygen-poor water. Thick, segmented millipede-things drape like heavy chains from stem to stem, feeding on the sweet exudates the plants sweat to bribe them away from chewing leaf tissue. Finger-length crustaceans with soft, leathery shells cling round the rims of volcanic steam-pores, filtering sulfur-bacteria out of the rising vapor with rhythmic sweeps of feathered mouthparts that move in locked, mechanical cadence, forty or fifty individuals fanning in precise unison round a single vent so none spoils another’s current. Even the undersides of those crustaceans carry smaller translucent mites, and the spaces between stems are strung with sticky, geometric capture-sheets spun by colonial larvae that harvest rising spore-plumes without ever spinning an irregular thread.

Because of this crowding, the basin generates an unbroken wall of sound. It isn’t punctuated by alarms or hunting shrieks; it is a loud, steady, omnidirectional roar pitched somewhere between heavy grease sizzling in a pan and a million wet clocks ticking slightly out of phase. Mandibles scrape fungal film off waxy leaves; opercula click shut and suck open; legions of blind, swollen-throated frogs wedged deep inside hollow lava blisters inflate and deflate their vocal sacs in continuous, overlapping low-frequency drones that you feel in your sternum through the soles of your feet. Stand anywhere across the miles of low hummocks and the volume never drops or swells.

Nothing ever shifts the balance. There is no wind to ripple the bruised-purple canopy, no storm front gathering beyond the rim, no perceptible boundary between afternoon and night—only a microscopic dimming and brightening so slow the chorus doesn't bother changing key for it. Predators do not chase prey here; energy is too expensive in the stifling heat, and food is already touching every mouth. Blind, velvety ambush-crawlers simply lie wedged among the amphibians and arthropods, swallowing whatever molts, weakens, or grows half an inch too close, while scavengers begin dismantling the dying before they have finished going limp. Birth, digestion, condensation, and growth run at dead-even rates across the entire dim expanse, holding the fresh, unweathered basin in a loud, steaming stillness that feels as though it began an hour ago and could run untouched for ten million years without rounding a single sharp edge of stone beneath it.
