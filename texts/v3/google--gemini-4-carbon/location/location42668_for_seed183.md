The air at this altitude is thin, cold, and blindingly bright, holding no mist despite how thoroughly wet the stone is. Light arrives from a sky the color of bleached bone—not from a single sun, but as an even, shadowless glare that presses into every flute and runnel of the rock. You are standing in a basin the size of a flooded county, rimmed by vertical miles of pale, chalky-gray dolomite and dark, glassy slate, all of it smoothed and rounded into long, weeping ribs. 

Nothing grows here. There is no soil, no moss, no lichen, except in the very deepest, most light-starved crevices where thin, grease-like smears of black bacterial film cling to the wet mineral. Nothing moves on legs or wings. Yet the valley is crowded and loud. 

The density of the place comes from the stone itself, which has been eroded over epochs into thousands of closely packed, leaning pillars, fins, and perforated arches, standing shoulder to shoulder across the basin floor like a petrified crowd. Every surface is slick. Water does not fall as rain; it sweats directly out of the upper strata, condensing out of the dry, high-altitude glare through some local failure of thermodynamics, running in braided, silver sheets down the smooth flanks of the pillars.

As the water descends, reality loses its grip on it. 

At the tops of the pillars, the runoff behaves normally, trickling and pooling in worn stone basins. Halfway down, the streams begin to skip. A ribbon of water will detach from the rock face, fall three feet, and strike an invisible horizontal plane in empty air—splashing outward in a flat, shivering disk before reforming into droplets that fall upward for a yard, then sideways, then vanish entirely, only to reappear twenty feet away, pouring out of a dry overhang. Puddles on the valley floor do not reflect the white sky; they reflect the underside of the rock they sit on, or show the dark, starless interior of stone miles beneath your feet. In places, the water runs in tidy, braided currents three inches above the gravel, casting sharp, dark shadows onto the dry pebbles beneath it—the only shadows in the entire blinding basin.

Because of this, the sound is continuous and strange. It is a loud, echoing clatter of hydraulic percussion, like thousands of faucets left running in a tiled cathedral, mixed with a low, grinding hum where the rock faces are slowly, actively Planing against invisible seams in the air. When a sheet of water hits one of the spatial faults, it makes a sharp, metallic *crack*, like a wet whip or a dropped sheet of tin. These snaps echo off the crowded pillars, overlapping until the whole valley roars with a crisp, cold, mechanical applause.

Scattered through this stone forest are the structures. Whoever built them worked with nothing more complex than chipped flint, split slate, bone-like mineral splinters, and braided cords made from some tough, fibrous crystal harvested from the deep vents—fibers like flexible asbestos. There is no iron, no mortar, no wheel-marks. Yet the builders understood the broken rules of the basin intimately, and built directly onto the faults.

On a ledge fifty feet up a slick gray spire sits a dry-stone cairn, four feet high, built of carefully wedged flat rocks. Its top half is shifted six inches to the left of its bottom half, resting cleanly on a shear-line in the air, completely stable. Further down the valley, spanning the gaps between the crowded rock fins, are dozens of aqueducts and catch-frames lashed together from pale, crooked mineral-rods and Slate shingles. They look like fragile traps or broken looms. They are positioned to catch the water where it falls upward, guiding the erratic streams into wide, hand-pecked stone bowls. 

Inside these bowls, the water spins furiously without a drain, grinding loose chunks of quartz into smooth, milky spheres. Dozens of finished spheres lie spilled around the bases of the pedestals, rolled into neat, inexplicable rows by the vibration of the ground. 

Elsewhere, small, windowless huts of stacked stone cling to the undersides of arches—not hanging, but built as though the arch's ceiling were level ground, their rough plank-slate doors swinging gently open and shut in a draft that blows vertically down from the rock. Faint marks cover the wetter walls near eye level: thousands of tallies chipped with a stone chisel, recording the rise and fall of invisible tides. 

As you look out across the mile-wide sweep of the basin, the processes continue at a brisk, indifferent pace. A high fin of dark slate shudders as the spatial seam cutting through its base shifts an inch; with a sound like a breaking bell, a ton of wet rock shears cleanly off, falls twenty feet, slows to a crawl as if sinking through heavy oil, and settles without a sound onto a dry ledge. Immediately, a fresh sheet of bright, cold water finds the new angle, leaps across the gap, and begins to polish the break smooth. The light never dims. The stone weeps, snaps, and grinds its own bones into spheres, filling the empty, breathless height with cold spray and clattering echoes.
