There is no bedrock visible anywhere in the valley, though the valley itself is real enough—a high, cold trough notched into the spine of a mountain range at nearly six thousand feet. If you dig through the matted roots of the wild ginger and the rotting, black leaf-mold, your spade does not strike granite or shale. It strikes the curved, riveted flank of a boiler, or a course of vitrified brick slick with subterranean grease, or a deck of checkered iron plate three inches thick. 

For four miles from rim to rim, the mountain gap is entirely filled with an ancient, hyper-dense industrial array that has weathered into its own permanent topography. Retaining walls rise out of the gloom like Palisades, only to be capped by tiers of turbine housings, bridged by brick viaducts, and interlaced with miles of pressurized pipework so tightly bundled that a person cannot slip sideways between them. Every square foot of horizontal, vertical, and diagonal space is occupied. Where the iron leaves off, the mountain flora packs the seam.

The air is perpetually cool, thin enough to tighten the chest on an incline, and saturated with low, hurrying clouds. Light comes through this ceiling as a flat, pewter wash, darkening to slate in the deep trenches between the condenser towers. Because the valley sits squarely in the path of high-altitude crosswinds, nothing is ever still. It is a landscape in constant, violent motion—yet almost entirely silent.

Gales tear down from the unseen passes and strike the forest of iron and foliage, whipping the crowns of stunted birches and dense rhododendron thickets into a frenzy. Broad-leaved butterbur and shoulder-high bracken thrash across the catwalks. Above them, thousands of counterweighted crane jibs, wind-vanes, louver-banks, and articulated exhaust cowls spin and pivot wildly in the gusts. The machinery is alive—not with the roar of combustion or the clatter of drop-forges, which would shatter the mountain quiet, but with deep-set, heavily greased, pneumatic and hydraulic power. Pistons the thickness of tree trunks slide effortlessly into bronze collars, shedding rings of condensed rainwater. Massive flywheels turn inside flooded pits without a squeak, balanced to fractions of an ounce a century ago and kept running by the sheer weight of high-mountain runoff channeled through buried penstocks. 

The turbulence is hydraulic and atmospheric as much as mechanical. Cloud-banks snag on the upper gantry frames, condensing instantly against the cold iron into sudden, localized sheets of water that dump onto the lower tiers, swelling the brick-lined sluices from trickles to foaming torrents in seconds, before the wind shifts and blows the trough dry again into swirling eddies of mist. Siphons trip automatically when reservoirs overfill; counterweights drop softly into dashpots of thick oil; whole sections of terraced grating tilt on rusted gimbals to dump loads of accumulated rainwater and torn leaves into the lower conduits, then swing slowly back into horizontal lock.

Everything is worn smooth, rounded off, settled into deep equilibrium. The sharp edges of I-beams have rusted away and stabilized under heavy coats of gray-green lichen and cushion moss six inches thick. Brick corners are scalloped by generations of wind-driven sleet. Copper busbars as wide as planks run down the walls like frozen green waterfalls, completely encrusted in verdigris, though if you place a bare hand near the insulated brackets, you can feel the faint, sixty-cycle hum of live current migrating down from the hydro-heads up-valley. 

Vegetation does not reclaim this place so much as use it as an ideal, richly terraced cliffside. Ferns erupt from every bolt-hole, flange gap, and cable tray. Tangled mats of blackberry, mountain ash, and creeping willow choke the defunct rail spurs, their roots prying into oil-soaked ballast that has turned over the decades into a rich, black, mildly warm peat—heated just a few degrees above the chilly air by the friction of buried drive-shafts and uninsulated steam-return lines below. Every surface weeps. Water beads on the undersides of cast-iron stair treads, drips down chains of delicate liverwort, and pools on tarred roofs where thick carpets of sphagnum moss hold it like sponges.

What makes the turbulence uncanny is its hush. Wind normally howls, and factories normally deafen, yet here the dense, wet sponge of three-dimensional foliage swallows high-frequency sound before it can travel twenty yards. The whipping branches produce only a soft, rushing *shhh*, like distant surf. The machinery operates at immense torque and low RPM, cushioned by water-jackets, lead shims, and thick casings of cast iron buried in wet moss. Stand on an arched iron footbridge—slick with crushed leaves and black grease—and the chaos around you feels almost underwater. Above your head, a massive, lattice-work conveyor gantry tracks abruptly thirty degrees to the left on greased rollers to bleed wind-load, shaking loose a shower of cold droplets; ten feet below, an automatic floodgate irises open, sending a braided column of brown peat-water surging silently down a stepped brick chute; all around you, ferns thrash, vapor boils out of pressure-relief stacks and vanishes instantly into the gale, and ragged gray clouds drag their bellies directly through the scaffolding, obscuring the far side of the valley and then snapping clear to reveal another two miles of wet, churning, green-and-iron terraces stretching toward the pass.

Very little moves here under its own heartbeat. The density of slick metal, shifting counterweights, and tangled briars leaves no purchase for deer or mountain goats, and the ceaseless whipping of the canopy discourages nesting songbirds. Now and then, a solitary raven uses the violent updrafts rising off a warm transformer bank, shooting vertically past ten stories of mossy brickwork without flapping a wing. Down in the dimmest, constantly dripping undercrofts—where immense brass valves sweat cold beads of moisture and slowly adjust their own tension wheels by fractions of an inch—pale, blind cave-crickets cluster near the warm bearings, and black mountain salamanders slip quietly from one flooded bolt-pocket to the next, undisturbed by the slow, unstoppable shifting of the world's iron bones.
