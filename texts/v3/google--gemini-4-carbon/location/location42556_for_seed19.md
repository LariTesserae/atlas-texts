At the top of the world, the mountains have forgotten how to be jagged. 

It is an altitude where the air should be razor-thin and violent, yet the atmosphere sits in a cool, breathless equilibrium. You are standing on the Spine—a vast, high-elevation plateau stretching forty miles from rim to rim, bordered by drop-offs that vanish into a dry, static haze of pale ochre dust. Millions of years ago, these were tearing, vertical peaks, thrust upward by violent tectonics. Now, eroded only by gravity and the microscopic, dry abrasion of thermal expansion over eons, every stone has settled into its lowest possible energy state. The granite and basalt have been worn down into enormous, sweeping, parabolic whales-backs of grey and slate-blue stone. There are no loose scree slopes left; every pebble rolled to the bottom of its groove ages ago and locked into place. Where stone has shattered into dust, the dust lies in deep, level basins as flat as mirrors, entirely undisturbed by wind. There is no weather here. No moisture rises to these heights, no clouds form, and no rain ever falls to carve gullies into the silt. 

The light is normal, clear, and shadow-sharp, pouring down from a sky the color of washed-out slate. There is no visible sun—only an omnidirectional, midday clarity that never shifts toward dusk, casting shadows that seem painted permanently onto the stone in dark, cool indigo. 

Looking out across the wide, open sweep of the plateau, the eye first registers a vast, mineral emptiness. Then, focus shifts, and you realize the ground is not stone at all. 

The fauna here is overwhelming, packed to a suffocating density, filling every basin, coating every rounded ridge, piled in layers three and four deep across the entire forty-mile expanse. Yet nothing moves, and nothing makes a sound.

They are creatures of dry chemistry and broken geometry, adapted to an environment that ran out of momentum before history began. In the dust basins, millions of things resembling pale, articulated carapaces—each the size of a kneeling human—press shoulder to shoulder like cobblestones. Near them, draped over the smooth granite swells, are herds of tall, multi-jointed forms with skins like dry, grey parchment stretched over wicker frames. Closer still, occupying the very air between the larger beasts, hang dense flocks of paper-thin, origami-like organisms suspended two feet off the ground, motionless, caught in a localized failure of gravity. 

Here, at this extreme altitude where time has ground down to a bare, flat wire, reality itself has begun to fray and repeat. Space does not quite add up. As you look across a densely packed hollow of horned, chalk-white grazers, you begin to notice the duplicates. Three yards apart, two identical creatures stand with the exact same chip in the left horn, the exact same twist of the neck, not as twins, but as the exact same entity occupying two coordinates simultaneously. In the densest clusters, the animals overlap without colliding. The translucent, dry-ribbed flank of one long-legged beast passes effortlessly through the stone-hard thorax of another; where their bodies intersect, the air flickers with a dull, colorless static, like grease on a lens. They do not eat one another. They do not breathe in any way that expands a chest. Their metabolism has slowed to match the geological stillness of the plateau, drawing minuscule trickle-charges of energy from cosmic radiation and the piezoelectric tension of the deep rock beneath them. A single blink of a faceted, obsidian eye takes four years to complete.

Because there are billions of them—packed into the valleys, carpeting the ridges right up to the abyssal rims—the silence is profound and heavy, padded by all that dry, stationary mass. Sound does not echo here; it dies immediately, absorbed by miles of chitin, bone-lattice, and dry leather. If you listen with painful intensity, the only sound in the world is a faint, subterranean *tick* every few minutes—the sound of a claw shifting a fraction of a millimeter on ancient granite, or the dry rustle of two overlapping realities sliding slightly out of phase.

Out of this crowded stillness rise the sparse, weird remnants of vegetation. Every hundred yards or so, pushing up directly through the crush of immobile bodies, grows a Silica-Tree. They are low, twisted things, no taller than a man, composed of brittle, copper-colored cellulose infused with quartz. They require no water, pulling trace dry minerals directly from the granite dust. Their branches end not in leaves, but in flat, wide paddles of dark, matte tissue angled precisely upward to catch the static midday light. Long ago, animals pressed against these trees as they grew, and rather than pushing the beasts aside, the slow-growing wood simply grew *through* their spatial coordinates, leaving silent, segmented creatures permanently impaled by branches that neither bleed nor break their skin.

Scattered sparsely across the high ridges—perhaps one structure every two or three miles—are the built things. Whoever walked here before the world settled into its final, weird stasis used only what was at hand, working with simple, heavy manual craft. 

Near the center of a wide, dust-filled caldera stands a low, circular windbreak—built back when there was wind—constructed from dry-stacked slabs of dark slate and bound together with twine braided from the fibrous bark of the copper trees. Beside it lies a hammer: a heavy lump of meteoric iron lashed with stiffened hide to the femur of one of the tall parchment-beasts. Inside the stone ring stands a rudimentary shelter, an A-frame of heavy bones covered in overlapping plates of shed chitin, pinned together with hand-carved wooden pegs. 

There are other marks, too. Tall cairns of balanced, smooth stones stand on the rounded crests of the whale-back hills, marking paths through the sea of motionless fauna. Some of the cairns have suffered from the same quiet breakdown of reality that plagues the beasts. You might walk past a six-foot pile of stacked grey stones, only to see its top three rocks floating six inches to the left of the base, casting a shadow that falls in the wrong direction, entirely solid and immovable if touched. Along a flat face of ancient rock, someone used a copper chisel to peck petroglyphs into the stone—spirals, tall stick-figures, and neat tally marks. Halfway across the rock face, the tally marks lose their sequence, stuttering into visual echoes that trail off the edge of the stone and hang suspended in the empty, cool air like flakes of black ash.

Nobody tends these shelters now, though nothing decays to ruin them. Without rot, rust, or wind, a leather-lashed bone shovel leans against a cairn exactly as it has for ten thousand cycles, cool to the touch. To walk through this landscape requires stepping carefully onto the backs of the cobblestone-creatures, walking across a living, unyielding pavement of shells, weaving sideways to avoid the shimmering patches of air where three or four large beasts occupy the exact same yard of space. Nothing turns its head to watch you pass. The light stays clear and cool; the distant, hazy horizon drops away into the dry void around the plateau; and the billions of inhabitants remain locked in their silent, crowded, fractional drift toward the end of time.
