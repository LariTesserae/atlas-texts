From any given ledge, the nearest neighbor is visible only because the air is thin enough to let light travel forty miles without softening. Across that gulf sits a powerhouse or a residential block of riveted iron and vitrified brick, perched on its own needle of black stone, small as a tooth against the pale sky. Between here and there is nothing at all—just cubic leagues of freezing, bright air, crossed by six braided steel transmission cables that sag for miles into the void before rising again to meet their ceramic insulators on the far side.

This is a city, organized into wards, power grids, pumping districts, and switched rail yards, yet ninety-nine percent of its volume is empty sky. 

The continent—if a landmass broken into sheer vertical splinters at twenty thousand feet can be called a continent—rose recently in geological time and stopped all at once. Nothing here has been rounded off. The rock is fresh, dark basalt and greenish-gray hornfels, cleaved along planes so sharp you could cut your palm by resting it on a parapet. Ledges meet cliff walls at crisp ninety-degree angles; talus slopes at the bases of the spires, miles down in the white haze, consist of jagged shards that still fit together like dropped porcelain. Water runs over every surface, yet cuts no channels. It does not rain, and no snow falls despite the biting, near-freezing cold; instead, the high, wet atmosphere continuously condenses directly onto the freezing rock and metal. Every spire weeps. Sheets of clear, thirty-four-degree water slide permanently down the unweathered stone faces, slicking the bricks of the factories, dripping in curtains from the undersides of cantilevered iron streets, and detaching into the abyss where the wind atomizes them back into mist long before they reach a bottom.

The noise is immense and completely uniform. 

There is the geological sound: a steady, high-altitude gale shearing across millions of razor-edged rock fins, turning the entire mountain range into a single chordal pipe organ playing a raw, hollow roar. Layered squarely into that wind is the industrial sound. Every spire and narrow mesa holds its piece of the municipal apparatus—turbine halls, rotary converters, atmospheric condensers, stamp mills, boiler houses—and all of them are running at full load. Heavy flywheels spin in open-sided sheds; high-voltage transformers hum at a chest-rattling pitch that carries for miles across the thin air; excess steam vents through brass whistles that have been screaming the exact same note for so long they feel less like warnings and more like an attribute of the atmosphere, like barometric pressure. Because nothing ever starts up, winds down, or shifts gears, the din has no rhythm. It is a solid wall of sound, loud enough to drown out a shout at three paces, locked at an invariant volume.

Out of the cold, wet rock and around the vibrating foundations of the machinery grows a thick, dark abundance of plant life. Where there is horizontal surface, it is choked with foliage. Without soil—the rock is too young to have broken down into dirt—the vegetation anchors directly into hairline fractures and wraps around iron girders in dense, dripping mats three feet thick. There are waist-high forests of rubbery, blue-green fronds stiffened with natural glycol against the frost; swollen, translucent bladder-ferns filled with cold water that tremble at the exact frequency of the nearby engines; sprawling curtains of burgundy moss that hang fifty feet down the cliff edges, streaming with condensation. Around the warm exhaust pipes of the pump houses, giant cabbage-like rosettes crowd over the catwalks and press their glossy, ice-slick leaves tight against the vibrating glass of factory windows.

Nothing eats these plants. Not a single leaf bears a chewed edge, a borehole, or a gall. There are no birds riding the updrafts between the spires, no midges hovering in the lee of the brick walls, no rats in the conduit trenches, no worms beneath the moss, and no operators behind the blurred glass of the control rooms. When a frond reaches a certain size, it neither browns nor rots; it simply remains as it is, slick with icy water, quivering in the gale. The biological world and the industrial one appear fixed in the same closed circuit. Warm coolant runoff from the substations feeds the root-mats on the narrow shelves; dense tangles of metallic-leaved creepers filter the mineral-heavy water before it spills back into the intake cisterns cut cleanly into the basalt below.

Above it all hangs normal, clear daylight, slightly bleached by altitude and bounced indefinitely off the deep beds of mist pooled miles down between the pillars. The light comes from a high slant that never seems to lengthen its shadows or deepen toward evening. In its flat clarity, you can trace the infrastructure of the empty metropolis out to the planetary curve: thin iron viaducts stepping from spire to spire over dizzying voids; solitary streetlamps burning steadily in the bright cold day along wet, moss-carpeted promenades; gaunt gantry cranes frozen under load above open-air yards; and fifty miles off, half-veiled in bright vapor, the next district over—another cluster of smoking stacks and sharp, gleaming black crags, roaring into the wind across an ocean of empty air.
