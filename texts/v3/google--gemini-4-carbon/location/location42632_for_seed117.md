The place is no wider than a courtyard, though it is impossible to see from one side to the other because of how closely the pillars stand. It is a sunken basin, a low stone pan dropped thirty feet below the surrounding shale flats, and it holds the cold, heavy air like a cellar holds damp. 

Overhead, there is no sky—only a low, unmoving ceiling of pearl-gray fog that presses right down onto the tops of the rocks. The light that filters through it is shadowless, pewter, and dim, as if dusk decided to stop halfway through and simply stay. 

Water does not fall here as rain; it arrives sideways and emerges from within. Every surface weeps. The basin is packed tight with formations of dark, slaty limestone worn down over millennia into smooth, rounded shoulders, flutes, and deep vertical runnels. There isn't a sharp edge left in the entire depression. Touch any stone and your fingers come away slick with a cold, mineral sweat. Water beads out of the porous rock, gathers into braids, slides down the crowded flanks of the pillars, and pools at the bottom in shallow, interlocking channels of black-tinted water. Stepping anywhere means stepping from one slick, submerged stone shelf to another, weaving sideways between pillars set so close together your coat brushes both walls.

Nothing green grows here. There is not a blade of grass, not a scrap of moss, no algae in the pools. Instead, the rock is coated in thick, cartilaginous skins of pale ochre and slate-blue fungus, rubbery and cold to the touch, overlapping like wet roof tiles. Along the waterlines, colonies of chemo-bacterial slime hang in translucent, motionless curtains, feeding off the iron and sulfur bleeding from the stone—and from the iron that was brought here on purpose.

Because there are machines wedged into the stone, or what remains of them, though they haven't entirely stopped working.

Scattered through the maze of weeping pillars are heavy, squat housings of cast iron, riveted boiler-plate tanks the size of hogsheads, and thick copper pipes green with verdigris that snake along the stone grooves like veins. Whoever put them here built for the wet and the dark. There are no walkways or buildings, just isolated piecework machinery bolted directly into the bedrock so long ago that the limestone flowstone has crept up over their mounting brackets, sealing iron and calcium together into smooth, rust-streaked mounds. 

A few yards into the labyrinth, half-submerged in a knee-deep sump, a brass flywheel the size of a cartwheel still turns. It is driven by nothing more than the steady, heavy sluice of run-off channeled down a stone trough onto its paddles. It rotates once every six seconds, heavily, dipping into the black water and rising again with a slick, rhythmic sigh. Attached to it, an eccentric rod disappears into a sealed iron box buried in silt. Every six seconds, deep inside the box, a valve clicks shut with a muffled *thock* you feel more in the soles of your boots than hear in your ears. Further along, high up where two leaning pillars touch, an ancient ceramic insulator holds a sagging wire that still hums softly, shedding occasional tiny blue sparks when the condensing mist bridges the gap to the wet rock—*zzt*, a faint smell of ozone, and then just the cold smell of wet slate and old grease again.

This cramped, dripping engine-room of stone is crowded with animals. 

With no leaves to rustle and no wind reaching down into the basin, you hear them before you see them, though the sound is remarkably soft—a constant, busy whisper of soft bodies shifting over wet mineral. 

Every niche, pipe-joint, and fluted hollow in the pillars is occupied. Crustaceans the size of human hands, their shells the exact matte charcoal of the wet limestone, cling upside down under the ledges and along the bellies of the copper pipes. They are flat, multi-legged things that move sideways in sudden, silent glides, grazing on the ochre fungal skins with delicate, feathery mouthparts that comb the rock at frantic speed. 

In the dark pools below, pale, blind amphibians—heavy-bodied and gill-fringed, looking as smooth and worn as the eroded pebbles they rest upon—lie stacked three deep in the warmer pockets near the humming electrical conduits. When a crustacean loses its grip or is nudged loose by a rival, it drops with a dull *plop*. Instantly, the water boils without making a splash—just a heavy, muscular rolling beneath the surface—and then settles flat again, silt drifting up like smoke.

Stranger things have adapted to the old industry. Along the rust-bleeding seams of the boiler tanks live colonies of shelled worms that build jagged, interlocking tubes out of oxidized iron flakes and lime. They extend fans of bright, venous red filaments into the trickling water to catch metallic silt, retracting all at once with a soft, wet *snick* if a shadow passes too close. And drifting slowly between the tightly packed pillars, riding the sluggish, freezing air currents that curl off the water, are things like airborne jellyfish—translucent bladders no bigger than teacups, trailing sticky, thread-like tentacles. They bump gently against the wet stones, bounce off the turning brass flywheel, and drift through the gaps, snagging the tiny black midges that rise in dense, low clouds from the bacterial mats.

The rules of this small hollow are density, patience, and cold friction. Nothing moves very far; there is nowhere far to go. Twenty paces in any direction brings you up against the steep, slick perimeter wall where the shale rises toward the empty flats above, cutting the basin off like a plugged well. Everything inside is layered on top of something else. The fungus grows on the iron; the iron is swallowed by the stone; the worms build on the rust; the crustaceans graze the worms' casings; the amphibians wait beneath the crustaceans; the water wears them all smooth.

You stand wedged between two sweating flanks of stone, breathing air so saturated it feels like cold broth in the lungs. Four feet to your left, a copper pressure gauge with cracked glass trembles slightly, its needle pinned against a rusted stop, housing a nest of tiny, translucent spiders behind its dial. Beneath your ankles, the black water slides steadily toward some unseen drain deep in the rock. The flywheel turns, drips, sighs; a valve clicks in the mud; a hundred pale gills flex in unison in the dark pool at your toes; a tiny blue spark snaps high overhead in the gloom, illuminating for a fraction of a second a solid wall of wet stone, glistening shells, and drifting threads.
