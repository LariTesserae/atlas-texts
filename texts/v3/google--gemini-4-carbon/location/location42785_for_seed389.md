At this altitude the sky should be freezing, and the sky itself—a flat, unbleached cobalt overhead, darkening slightly toward the zenith—looks as though it belongs above an icefield. Far below the rim of the peaks, miles out where the mountain drops sheer away, a level sea of white cloud stretches to the curvature of the world, motionless and blindingly bright in the midday sun. Yet here on the saddle, twenty-four thousand feet up, the rock radiates heat like the floor of a kiln.

The mountain has not been in the air long enough to learn how to crumble. There is no soil, no scree rounded by frost, not even dust in the ordinary sense. Everything native to the ridge is raw extrusion: great upturned slabs of gabbro and glassy black trachyte that split along razor-edged planes, stepped columns of fresh basalt still bleeding deep crustal heat, and long fissures from which a dry, sulfurous warmth rises continuously into the thin atmosphere. If you drop a wrench onto the stone, it does not bounce on gravel; it strikes a pristine crystalline facet and chips off flakes as sharp as surgical glass.

Packed into the three-mile trough between the highest horn-peaks—and climbing halfway up their jagged inner walls—sits a city of staggering, claustrophobic density, built entirely of iron, ceramic, and recast slag, and almost completely silent.

Because the air is so thin, sound dies within twenty paces, and because nothing here runs on combustion, little sound is made to begin with. Standing on one of the high catwalks above the central terraced wards, you look out over a labyrinth of foundry-blocks, pressure-vessels, viaducts, tenement towers, and fractionating columns crammed so tightly together that their iron eaves overlap like roof tiles. Thousands of lead-sheathed pipes, thick as tree trunks, weave through the alleys in bundled trusses, stepping over narrow stairwells and ducking through the walls of residential blocks without breaking stride. Yet the roar that ought to accompany such a concentration of heavy industry is absent. What reaches the ear is only an intimate, close-range tapestry of small noises: the occasional crisp *tick* of a cooling girder, the soft wheeze of a leather bellows equalizing pressure, the slow, viscous *glug* of heavy brine shifting inside a ceramic conduit beside your elbow.

The machinery of the city runs on patience and differential heat. Across the wider chasms where the young mountain is still splitting apart—widening by three or four centimeters a year—immense wrought-iron tension bridges have been anchored directly into the opposing walls of black stone. They are not meant for foot traffic, though narrow railed paths cling to their undersides; they are tectonic ratchets. As the rock creeps outward and upward over the months, it pulls giant toothed drawbars through baths of heavy grease, turning reduction gears the size of millstones at a rate invisible to the naked eye. That stored torque is bled off down shafts and torsion-cables into the dense wards below, driving presses, extruders, and cable-cars that glide along overhead wires at the speed of a strolling pedestrian.

Elsewhere, the engines are thermal. Long, ribbed spines of brass and iron alloy stretch out over the hottest fumarolic trenches, bowing upward almost imperceptibly in the rising heat until they trip a silent mercury switch; then louvers slide shut beneath them, a mist of reclaimed water sprays across their flanks, and as they contract they haul thirty-ton counterweights a few inches higher up their guide-towers. Everywhere you look, things are moving, and everywhere they move so slowly you have to align a piston-rod or a crane-boom against a sharp edge of background rock and count twenty breaths before you are certain it has shifted.

Nothing green has ever grown here. There are no lichens on the shaded side of the vats, no moss by the leaky joints, no stray windblown seeds—nothing from the lowlands below the cloud-deck could survive the climb, the thinness of the air, or the scalding mineral chemistry of the moisture. Yet the city is perpetually damp. Warm vapor sighing up from the raw fissures meets the shaded cast-iron undersides of upper-tier buildings and beads into heavy, warm sweat. Pipes drip continuously onto the stepped alleys, forming shallow, lukewarm puddles in the depressions of cast-slag paving stones. Where the dampness lingers, minerals bloom instead of plants: crusts of pale yellow alum, delicate hair-like needles of Epsom salt that grow an inch overnight and collapse at a footstep, velvety green-blue patinas of copper sulfate creeping up the flanks of transformer housings. In the narrowest courts, where four or five tiers of iron balconies block the direct sun, the air hangs thick and muggy as a laundry room, smelling of wet rust, hot oil, and wet slate.

Open pan-works step down the eastern flank of the saddle like metallic rice paddies—hundreds of shallow, brimming trays of riveted boiler-plate resting on forests of adjustable jack-screws. Each pan holds two inches of dense, oily mother-liquor pumped up from deep boreholes. Under the clear, unfiltered high-altitude sunlight, the liquid does not boil; it simply skins over with iridescent metallic films—bismuth, antimony, strange heavy salts—which are skimmed once a day by automated wiper-bars inching from rim to rim on greased rails. Between the pans run narrow walkways of perforated steel grating, hot enough through boot-soles to keep a walker moving, flanked by low housing blocks whose windowless walls are built of hollow ceramic bricks filled with salt-wax that melts during the day to absorb the heat and solidifies slowly at night.

Signs of whoever built and tends this place are ubiquitous, though figures in the alleys are scarce. Brass valve-wheels are worn bright along their rims; chalk tally-marks—neat rows of unfamiliar, fraction-heavy numerals—are updated on slate boards beside hatchways; enclosed, double-doored gondolas with small round portholes creep along the suspension cables between the smelter-towers and the residential stacks, pressurized against the thin air. Now and then a heavy bulkhead wheezes open on a shaded landing, letting out a brief puff of cool, conditioned air before latching shut again.

Out in the open heat, the city belongs mostly to its few, improbable animals.

Perched along the warm copper bus-bars of the electrolytic yards, or flattened against the black trachyte crags just beyond the perimeter walls, are creatures the size of large hounds that resemble nothing so much as radially symmetrical, six-limbed tortoises cast out of dull pewter. Their shells are heavily corrugated, finned like air-cooled engine blocks to dump excess heat or gather it as needed, encrusted with the same mineral salts that coat the pipes. They move at the same pace as the machinery. One will spend an entire afternoon lifting a blunt, scale-plated foot, setting it down on a warm rivet-head, and shifting its weight forward with a faint, dry click of horn on iron. Along the damp undersides of the aqueducts, pale, ribbon-flat worms as wide as a human hand glide over the sweating lead, grazing on sulfur-reducing films and leaving faint, clean tracks through the copper-frost before retreating into bolt-holes when the sun swings round to strike their pipe.

And high overhead, riding the tall, invisible columns of superheated air that rise straight up from the foundry district into the thin blue sky, two or three immense, translucent gliders turn in wide, lazy circles. They have no feathers and do not flap; they are little more than taut, veined membranes stretched across hollow bone spars ten feet wide, drifting like charred sheets of parchment that refuse to fall, dipping down once every few hours to drink the warm mineral distillate pooling on the roofs of the highest condensers.

Because the mountain underneath is still emerging—still shoving fresh, unweathered stone into the sky—nothing in the city is built on a fixed foundation. Look closely at the base of any tower, tenement, or vat-house, and you see that it does not sit mortared to the rock. Every structure rests on massive iron shoes fitted with lead crush-pads and threaded leveling-collars, straddling jagged crevices like a crab bracing itself on dock-pilings. Where a fresh crack has split a plaza since last season, exposing an inch-wide ribbon of gleaming, ink-black stone hotter than the slabs around it, a flexible copper bellows-bridge has already been bolted across the gap, its pleats slowly stretching taut.

The light throughout the afternoon remains remarkably ordinary—clear, shadow-sharp, shadow-black in the deep slots between buildings, neither hazy nor glaring except where it strikes the distant white floor of clouds far below the peaks. There is no wind to speak of; the air is too thin to push hard, moving instead in slow, breathing updrafts that carry waves of shimmering refraction up the dark walls of the caldera. A gondola reaches a pylon, clicks softly over the bracket, and begins the twenty-minute glide across the next hundred-yard span of workshops. Down in a courtyard five levels below, a counterweight reaches the bottom of its forty-foot drop, settles onto a cushion of coiled springs without a sound, and a single brass valve flips over, reversing the flow of warm oil for the rest of the week.
