The light hits first, before the cold registers, and long before the eye adjusts enough to make out depth. At this altitude the sky has little scattering left in it—a bleached, hard-edged cobalt overhead that whitens toward the rims of the basin—and the sun comes down with the flat, surgical intensity of an arc lamp. Everything facing upward shines. Everything facing downward drops immediately into ink-black shadow.

The valley is perhaps six miles across from scarp to scarp, a freshly dropped tectonic trough whose walls have never known a glacier or a proper river. Where the stone shows through at the perimeter cliffs and along the stepped fault-terraces crossing the basin floor, it looks broken yesterday. Slabs of pale granodiorite and banded grey chert jut upward at forty-degree angles, their edges crisp enough to slice canvas. Talus aprons spill down from the cliffs in cascades of angular, planar shards that lock together without mortar or mud. There is no silt here, no rounded gravel, no sandbars—none of the soft debris of worn-down country.

Yet the basin is choked to the brim with growth. 

From any slightly elevated shelf, the valley floor reads as an immense, tightly packed mat of silver-green, slate-blue, and dark waxy viridian, billowing over the jagged stone like something poured thick and left to set. Up close, the density is staggering. There is no bare ground to walk on, nor even true mineral soil—only a ten-foot-deep mattress of cold, damp, slow-rotting peat and woody lattice wedged directly into the jaws of raw fractured rock. Plants pile atop one another in fierce, immobile competition for the glare. Stiff rosettes the size of millstones interlock their serrated leaves; out of their centers rise thick, pithy stalks crowned with rubbery umbrellas; around their bases weave dense thickets of pale, segmented stems coated in white felt. Every leaf is built like armor—fleshy, rigid, dusted with chalky farina or surfaced in a mirror-bright cuticle to survive the ultraviolet bombardment.

Nothing rustles. Even if a draft slid down from the scarps—and the air in this bowl sits remarkably still, heavy and cool despite the blazing radiation—none of these leaves are thin enough to flutter. The whole botanical mass acts as an enormous acoustic baffle. Drop a wrench onto the foliage and it lands with a dull, brief *thud* before vanishing into the dark understory; shout, and the sound dies twenty yards out, swallowed by millions of fleshy, angled surfaces that turn every vibration into tiny increments of heat.

Down underneath that glaring canopy, the world changes rules. Three feet below the sunlit crowns, the light vanishes and the air drops toward freezing. Here lives all the valley’s water. No streams run through the rock clefts below; rain almost never falls from that dry, over-bright sky. Instead, moisture hangs in perpetual, localized suspension. Every night, cold air pools in the basin and wrings vapor out of the thin atmosphere; every day, the sealed darkness beneath the leaves holds onto it. Water beads like mercury on the underside of hairy stems, collects in half-liter cisterns at the bases of cupped bracts, and slicks the raw granite faces hidden down among the roots with a permanent film of icy sweat. Pale, translucent root-velamen drinks directly from the damp air. Spongy cushions of livid emerald hepaticas swell around rock teeth, frigid and clammy to the touch, never dripping, hoarding their dampness in dead silence.

Scattered across the six-mile expanse, spaced half a mile or a mile apart with no roads between them, stand the machines.

From a distance they look almost small against the tilt of the valley walls—dark, geometric interruptions poking above the luminous silver-green scrub. A cluster of bolted steel lattice-masts here; a broad, low dome of riveted iron plates and glazed porcelain half a mile west; further out, a long gantry bridge resting on concrete piers poured straight onto the jagged chert. Linking them across the open air are heavy catenary cables of braided copper as thick as a human wrist, strung on tall, triple-skirted white glass insulators that blaze like stars in the noon glare.

Reach one of these installations by pushing slowly through the packed, unyielding rosettes, and the scale shifts. They are massive, heavy-industry works—built of cast iron, dull weathered bronze, high-test steel, and thick borosilicate glass—and they are running at full load without making a single sound.

There are no exhaust stacks, no cooling fans, no clattering tappets, no vents hissing steam into the chill air. Everything is closed-loop and built to move at the pace of growing crystal. At one station, four hydraulic rams the diameter of oil drums angle downward into a fresh tectonic fissure, braced against a collar of cast concrete. You cannot see them move. Only if you lay a fingernail across the polished chrome shaft where it enters the brass packing gland, and hold it there for five minutes until your hand aches from the cold, will you feel the imperceptible, relentless creep of the piston descending under thousands of tons of pressure. Clear, viscous oil beads slightly at the seal, never quite forming a drop.

Nearby, an array of wide parabolic troughs—surfaced not in silvered glass, which would pit, but in polished white metal—tracks the sun across the sky on massive worm-gears smeared with stiff black grease. The electric motors driving those gears are brushless, potted in solid resin casings ribbed with cooling fins, turning down through such extreme reduction ratios that their movement is entirely noiseless. At the focal line of the mirrors, thick quartz pipes carry a sluggish, amber-colored fluid that boils silently in a partial vacuum, tiny bubbles rising as slowly as pebbles through honey before disappearing into insulated return manifolds buried under the brush.

What the network is harvesting, or refining, or bracing against is never wholly obvious from any single rig. Some installations appear purely extractive: long, low-slung filter presses sitting in clearings where the metallic scrub has been cleanly sheared away at waist height, leaving woody stumps bleeding a white, latex-like resin that crusts immediately in the bright air. Inside heavy flanged vats with round sight-glasses, pale flocculate settles millimeter by millimeter out of dark green liquor. Other structures seem preoccupied with the young rock itself—enormous tensioning frames anchored across fault lines with steel tendons pulled taut as harp strings, wired into transformer banks the size of small houses. You expect a sixty-cycle hum from transformers that large. Standing six inches from their grey radiator fins, warming your chilled knuckles in the thin ribbon of radiant heat rising straight off the painted steel, there is nothing at all. Either it is pure, unrippled direct current moving through those wrist-thick overhead lines, or the frequency sits far outside anything a ribcage or an eardrum can register.

Occasionally, miles off toward the northern scarp, a wheeled harvester-frame spans a swath of foliage on dual rails spiked directly to a tilted limestone bedding plane. Over the course of a week it advances perhaps forty feet. Looking back behind it, you can trace last year’s cut, and the decade’s before that: neat, rectangular steps in the dense canopy where the plants are slowly rebuilding their armored roof, shades of paler chartreuse grading back into silver-blue over seasons.

Because nothing moves quickly, the weirdness of the basin settles in late, after an hour of staring through squinted eyes. The foliage grows right up against the warm cast-iron housings and wraps around the concrete footings without scorching or staining; in places, waxy cyan vines use inactive grounding rods as trellises, their tendrils flattening into wide, sucker-like pads against the metal as though drawing some trace mineral or faint electrostatic charge out of the casing. Where a sealed return pipe runs through the dark understory, cold condensation forms so thickly on its chilled lagging that rare clusters of ghost-white, translucent fungi—stiff as porcelain—flare out from the metal joints in horizontal shelves.

Animal life is nearly nonexistent, or so scarce and adapted to the stillness that it registers mostly as small, puzzling leftovers. Sometimes, cupped inside the dry upper depression of a giant sun-leaf, lies the molted carapace of something flat, segmented, and broad as two open hands—a shell completely colorless and clear, like blown glass, built to let the fierce sunlight pass straight through to the leaf below without cooking the creature inside. Once in a great while, fifty yards away across the packed crowns of the scrub, two round, dark-lensed eyes set high on a narrow, grey-wooled head will break the plane of the leaves, utterly motionless, watching from the shadow of a porcelain insulator tower. Wait ten minutes and the head does not turn or blink; look down to check your footing on a knife-edged boulder hidden under the moss, look back up, and the space beside the tower is empty, the stiff rosettes around it not even trembling.

All afternoon the light stays vast and unsoftened, pouring into the six-mile trough without haze. Shadows creep across the unweathered facets of the talus slopes at the same patient, fractional rate as the greased gears on the mirror mounts. Down in the black pockets beneath the stems, cold dew clings to stone that has never been tumbled; overhead, megawatts slide without a whisper along copper lines slung across pristine air, while a billion rubbery leaves drink the glare and give nothing back to the ear.
