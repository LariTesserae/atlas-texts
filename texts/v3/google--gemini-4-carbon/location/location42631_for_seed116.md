The chamber is forty feet high and twelve feet across, sealed behind four-inch panes of greenish, leaded glass that bow outward under the weight of the water. Outside those panes, there is nothing at all—only the pale, blinding glare of upper-troposphere clouds, miles of empty air dropping away to an unseen ground, and the black, riveted flank of the spire rising further still into the thin blue. 

Inside, the room is entirely drowned. It is a vertical column of warm, clear water, suspended at twenty-eight thousand feet—an ancient industrial sight-gauge or pressure-equalizer built into the exterior of a miles-high atmospheric condenser, forgotten long enough to become its own world.

Because the chamber is so narrow and tall, its geography is entirely vertical, and there is a great deal of open, sunlit water between one solid surface and the next. Light enters cleanly through the exterior glass, refracting into shifting, pale-gold bands that wobble across the back wall. There is no stone here, no mud that did not begin as metal. Every millimeter of substrate was once forged, cast, or stamped, though time and continuous warm flow have ground the machinery down into its final, most stable shapes. Cast-iron valve housings have eroded into smooth, kidney-shaped boulders of dark, graphitic sponge. Brass intake manifolds have worn away until they are thin as scalloped shells, polished bright yellow where the current scours them and crusted with pale green verdigris in the leeward pockets. Even the rivets along the back bulkhead have lost their heads, slumped and smoothed into rows of gentle, rounded nubs like buried vertebrae.

The water is warm—heated from behind the inner bulkhead by the tireless, blind labor of whatever colossal engines still run inside the spire's core. You hear them before you see anything move in the tank: a loud, rhythmic, sub-aquatic *thrum-clack, thrum-clack* that travels directly through the water and vibrates in the teeth, overlaid by the continuous, roaring hiss of high-pressure steam venting somewhere just beneath the floor grates. Every forty seconds, a deep solenoid *boom* shudders up the iron walls, and from the smoothed-out throat of a bronze pipe at the bottom of the tank, a dense stream of warm, silvery gas bubbles erupts.

That regular plume of gas drives the room’s entire weather system. The bubbles expand rapidly as they rise through the forty-foot column, roaring upward like an inverted waterfall, hauling warm water from the lower sump toward the domed iron ceiling before the cooled current peels outward and slides down the cold exterior glass. 

Anchored directly into the path of this rising current, the vegetation grows lush and bright, streaming upward like hair in a wind tunnel. There is no clutter to it; the chamber is mostly open, glowing liquid, which makes the great suspended plumes of green stand out with stark clarity. Thick, translucent ribbons of ribbon-kelp—ten feet long and emerald in the abundant window-light—root themselves into the pitted collars of dead pressure gauges, undulating wildly whenever the forty-second bubble plume tears past them. Lower down, where the eroded iron bulkhead curves inward, mats of broadleaf aquatic fern have colonized a series of rusted ladder rungs, transforming the vertical climb into stacked, hanging tiers of bright chartreuse foliage. Fine, coppery-red moss coats the smoothed undersides of old piston rods, trapping tiny pearls of oxygen until its entire surface shimmers like mercury, releasing the bubbles all at once whenever the deep engine-boom shakes the wall.

Between these hanging gardens, the open water is busy with small, strange traffic adapted to high altitude, warm iron, and perpetual noise. 

Schools of pale, heavy-bellied fish—no larger than thumbs, with oversized, coppery eyes and stiff pectoral fins shaped like hydrofoils—hang motionless in the downward cold current along the glass, facing into the flow. When the forty-second plume fires, the sound makes them dart simultaneously inward as a single flashing sheet, snapping up minute shreds of biofilm shaken loose from the brasswork. 

Along the smoothed metal walls crawl flat, translucent gastropods with shells coiled in tight, hexagonal spirals—a biological mimicry of the worn-down bolt heads they sleep beside. They graze slowly across the glass panes, keeping the view into the terrifying void outside clean of algae, leaving faint, curving tracks that vanish within minutes. Deeper in the fern tiers live the chamber’s hunters: slender, finger-length crustaceans plated in enameled white and rust-orange carapace, equipped with one oversized, piston-hinged claw. They cling to the vibrating kelp stems, swaying violently with the current, waiting for the water-foiling fish to drift too close to the leaves. When one strikes, the snap of its claw adds a sharp, porcelain *crack* right over the bass rumble of the unseen engines.

The rules of this pocket are absolute and dictated by the cycle of the pipe. Everything rises for thirty seconds, held aloft in the warm, roaring updraft of silver air; then, for ten quiet seconds between the booms—quiet only in that the bubbling stops, leaving the constant, loud hum of the hidden machinery behind the wall—everything settles. Loose green fronds drift slowly downward through the clear, empty middle of the tank. Tiny, rust-flaked particles snow gently toward the smoothed iron floor. In those ten seconds, looking straight across through the lush, waving green and out the bowed, leaded glass, you see a flat horizon of brilliant white cloud-tops miles below, motionless in the thin, freezing air, completely severed from the warm, roaring, green room hanging above them. Then the deep valve slams open in the dark beneath the grates, the bronze throat coughs silver, and the whole narrow world surges upward again.
