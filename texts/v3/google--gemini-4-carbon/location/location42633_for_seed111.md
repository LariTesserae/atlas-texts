Near the apex of the massif, where the crust has only recently torn itself upward through the upper reaches of the troposphere, the mountain splits into a single vertical throat. It is not a valley and not quite a cave, though its walls lean inward until they nearly meet two hundred feet overhead, leaving only a crooked, knife-edged seam of sky. Through that narrow slot, the atmosphere is thin enough that even at midday the sky shows as a dark, bruised indigo, pricked with hard, unblinking stars. No direct sun ever clears the lip. Down inside the cleft, the darkness is nearly total—a thick, cool gloom roughly the length and breadth of a sunken cathedral nave, sealed at both ends by sheer curtains of faulted stone.

Nothing here has had time to soften. The rock is newly broken black andesite and glassy hornfels, still raw from the tectonic heave that lifted it above the weather. There is no soil on the floor, no sand, not a single rounded pebble. Everything is blades, splinters, and stepped planes with edges crisp enough to shave horn. Fresh talus lies wedged at steep, unstable-looking angles, locked together by friction alone. If you put a palm against the wall to steady yourself, the stone feels freshly snapped, cold and slick with a thin, persistent sweat.

Water never falls here as rain; the clouds churn thousands of feet lower down the mountain’s outer flanks. Instead, thin ribbons of vapor force their way up through microscopic fault-lines from the mountain's interior, cooling as they rise, and immediately bead upon the freezing rock. Moisture hangs in the cleft as a clammy sheen, gathering along the razor-sharp arrêtes until heavy drops detach and fall long distances through the dark, bursting on the splinters below. In the crooked pockets where three fracture-planes meet, small cups of black water collect, mineral-bitter and dead still.

Out of these damp seams grows what little vegetation can endure both the darkness and the altitude: sparse, tough clusters of pale gray bladder-wort, swollen like blistered thumbs to hold trace gases; wiry tufts of rust-colored filament that digest iron directly from the hornfels; and rubbery, black-skinned tubers wedged so tightly into hairline cracks that their expansion occasionally snaps off fresh flakes of stone.

The stone, however, is barely visible. Almost every square foot of the vertical walls, the overhanging fins, and the jagged floor is covered in living flesh.

In the dark, it takes minutes for the eye to separate geology from anatomy, because nothing moves quickly enough to betray itself. The air at this elevation holds less than a third of sea-level oxygen; haste is metabolically impossible. Yet the chamber is packed to suffocation with bodies—an overwhelming, three-dimensional crowding of cold-adapted creatures stacked flange-to-flange, clinging to knife-edges, draped across gaps, and layered two or three deep in the broader alcoves. Their collective body heat is the only reason the condensation on the walls does not freeze into rime, keeping the pocket suspended in a perpetual, clammy chill just a few degrees above ice.

Before you can see them clearly, you hear them. The cleft is loud—startlingly, relentlessly loud, despite the glacial pace of everything inside it. 

First, there is the breathing. Thousands of oversized, bellows-like thoracic sacs expand and contract in ragged, overlapping cycles, hauling vast volumes of thin air through horny, fluted spiracles and wide, cartilaginous nostrils. Each individual breath takes twenty or thirty seconds to draw and another twenty to expel, producing a deep, wet, whistling roar that rolls back and forth between the stone walls like surf trapped in a cistern. 

Over that continuous bass wheeze rings a sharp, metallic percussion. *Clack. Tink. Chink-clack.*

The dominant inhabitants of the throat are bulky, radially hunched climbers the size of collared bears, covered in overlapping plates of leathery, slate-gray hide thick enough to rest directly on glass-sharp andesite without bleeding. Each possesses four broad, suction-cupped anchoring limbs and two slenderer, multi-jointed forelimbs tipped with boneless, prehensile pads. Nearly every one of these creatures holds a stone.

They do not build. There is not a wall, a nest, a cairn, or a woven mat anywhere in the chasm. Their relationship to craft is entirely immediate, handheld, and ceaseless. Moving at the speed of clock-hands, a climber will lift a heavy cobble of dense dike-rock and bring it down at a precise angle against a protruding fin of glassy hornfels. A sharp, resonant ring echoes across the chamber; a brief, ghostly spark of orange triboluminescence flashes inside the fractured silicate, lighting up a yard of wet hides and clustered eyes for a tenth of a second before the dark swallows it again. A fresh flake drops into the creature's lower gripping-pad. With infinite patience, over the course of an hour, it uses that new razor to saw away a leathery tuber at the root, or to scrape a thin velvet of mineral-eating moss from a crevice too narrow for its mouthparts.

Others use long, three-foot splinters of columnar basalt as pry-bars. Two or three individuals will wedge their stone levers into the same hairline crack and lean their dead weight upon them in absolute stillness, waiting half a day for the mountain's own internal stresses and their combined pressure to pop a slab loose, exposing the damp, pale worms and chemo-synthetic slime hidden in the virgin seam beneath. When a pry-stone snaps, it is dropped; when a hammer-stone wears smooth—the only smooth objects in this entire world—it is still treasured for its heft. If an old climber dies, losing suction and tumbling in slow, heavy bounces to the talus below, neighboring limbs reach out with unhurried precision to pluck the worn striking-stone from its stiffening pads before the body has even finished settling.

Around and upon these tool-holders lives the rest of the thicket. Pale, eyeless creatures shaped like flattened gourds glide across the hides of the larger beasts on muscular feet, grazing on condensed water droplets and shed skin. From the high overhangs dangle dense curtains of colonial, gut-like tubes, swaying imperceptibly in the updrafts, filtering airborne spores and stray flecks of organic dust stirred up by the stone-working. Fist-sized arthropods with spindly, yard-long legs step delicately across the crowded backs and faces of the climbers like water-striders on a dark pond, tapping the rock with hollow antennae that add a dry, woody rattle to the metallic ringing of the hammers. Where a young flake of hornfels has managed to slice through a climber's calloused flank, slow-oozing blue-black blood draws knots of thumb-sized, jawless leeches that drop from the ceiling on threads of clear mucus.

Nothing startles. Predation happens at the speed of growth; a multi-limbed scavenger-hunter will spend six hours easing a sharpened stone spike between the armor plates of a sluggish neighbor, while the neighbor spends the same six hours slowly shifting its weight to crush the hunter's forelimb against the cliff face.

Far overhead, the crooked slit of dark violet sky dims slightly to pure black, then, many hours later, pales back to indigo. Down in the cleft, nothing marks the transition. The moisture beads on the sharp stone; a drop falls, hitting a leathery carapace with a hollow *pat*. Somewhere in the pitch dark ten feet away, a pry-bar groans against a fault, a flake of silicate snaps free with a dull green spark, and the vast, crowded nave rings on—wheezing, chiming, and scraping itself millimeter by millimeter deeper into the raw peak.
