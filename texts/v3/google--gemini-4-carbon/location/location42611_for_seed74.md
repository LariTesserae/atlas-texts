You do not step onto earth here; you step onto the *burl*. 

The place is a shallow, terraced basin no wider than a rural parish, bounded on all sides by a seamless, rising wall of fused timber that curls slightly inward at its rim, framing a pale rectangle of overcast sky. Beneath your boots, there is no soil and no stone. The ground is an undulating, continuous plate of dense, silvery-gray rootwood, polished to a dull satin by centuries of exposure and the slow friction of weather. It feels cool and slightly yielding, like thick leather stretched over iron, crossed here and there by shallow grooves that carry no running streams, only dark, damp streaks of morning condensation.

This is an industrial town, though it contains almost no brick and very little quarried rock. Instead, the settlement has been coaxed, grafted, and pruned directly out of the basin’s living mass, then bolted together with heavy black iron. 

Because the space is sparse and uncluttered, you can read the entire geometry of the district from a single vantage point. Wide, windy plazas of bare heartwood separate the structures. The buildings themselves—four-story workshops, switch-houses, and vaulted silos—are grown from massive, hollow-cored trunks whose bark has grown thick, calloused, and corrugated like slate. Centuries ago, arborist-engineers set heavy cast-iron collars, turnbuckles, and steel trusses into the young wood to direct its growth. Now, the metal and the timber have weathered into a state of deep, settled equilibrium. Rust has bled down into the grain, staining the pale wood in long,ochre-colored teardrops, while the slow expansion of the bark has swallowed the edges of bolts and rivets until the iron looks less like an attachment and more like a native mineral extruded by the trees themselves.

Overhead, the canopy has been suppressed and trained into utility. Instead of wild, leafy crowns, thick braided boughs span the open air between towers like suspension cables, carrying insulated copper power lines, lead-sheathed conduits, and iron pulley tracks. Where foliage is allowed to grow, it forms flat, dense, horizontal mats high above the plazas—living roofs of dark, waxy needles that drink the cool daylight and catch the mist that drifts into the basin every afternoon.

Despite the sparse layout and the heavy, weathered stillness of the architecture, the entire basin is visibly at work. 

It is a silent sort of industry, driven not by combustion, but by turgor—the immense, slow hydraulic pressure of the living substrate. Look closely at the broad root-floor and you can see long, muscular ridges flexing almost imperceptibly beneath the surface. Every few minutes, a deep, muffled *thrum* vibrates through the soles of your shoes as a subterranean valve-root shifts its load. Along the flanks of the buildings, heavy brass pressure-gauges stand mounted on iron stems driven straight into the sapwood, their needles trembling steadily in the green. 

Beside a wide, empty courtyard, an unstaffed flywheel twelve feet across turns at a steady, hypnotic walking pace. Its axle disappears directly into a scarred bole of wood. As a swelling wave of internal sap-pressure pushes through the vascular chambers inside the tree, a series of bark-sheathed pistons rise in sequence, driving the iron crank with a soft, rhythmic *shhh-clack*, *shhh-clack*, venting tiny, ghosts of cool, pine-scented vapor into the brisk air. Across the square, tensioned overhead vines slowly haul a suspended iron hopper from one silo to another—not pulled by a winch, but moved by the deliberate, muscular coiling and uncoiling of a tendril as thick as a ship’s hawser, stimulated by rhythmic clicks of low-voltage current from a rusted switchbox bolted to its flank.

The light is clear, cool, and shadowless, typical of low coastal valleys under high, thin cloud. It illuminates the pale gray of the wood, the dark, oiled sheen of the machinery, and the cushions of emerald-green moss that pack the damp seams where iron meets bark. Moisture here is stingy and localized. It collects on the cold cast-iron counterweights and drips steadily onto the root-plates below, raising small, velvet gardens of lichen and miniature, cup-shaped fungi that thrive solely in the drip-lines of machines.

Very little moves independently of the town's slow clockwork. There are no flocks of birds in the cable-boughs, no weeds breaking through the floorboards—because there are no cracks for weeds to find; the floor *is* the plant, absolute and territorial, healing over any scratch with a slow pearl of amber resin. Occasionally, a solitary, long-legged beetle with a carapace the exact matte-black of painted iron steps carefully across a brass pipe, drinking the condensation before slipping beneath a shelf of bracket fungus. Once in an hour, a small, pale-feathered wren might flit through the wide gap between two mill-towers, its sharp *chip* echoing briefly off the curved wooden walls before being swallowed by the open space.

Otherwise, the quiet holds. The air smells sharply of cold iron, damp cellar-bark, machine grease, and crushed wintergreen. You stand in an open court of polished wood, watching a row of copper louvers high on a living tower slowly open in unison to vent the faint warmth of an unseen dynamo, while fifty yards away, a great braided bridge-bough creaks softly, adjusting its posture by half an inch against the afternoon chill. Everything here is worn smooth. Everything is bolted tight. Everything is breathing.
