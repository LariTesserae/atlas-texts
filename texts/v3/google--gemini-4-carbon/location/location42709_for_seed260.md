The atmosphere here does not hold moisture; it holds uncollapsed dimension. You breathe in a dry, cool static that tastes faintly of copper and crushed quartz, cold enough to ache in the sinuses without ever frosting over. There is no frost because there is no water—not in the sky, not locked in the stone, not anywhere across the miles of jagged expanse. 

Light enters from nowhere in particular, a bruised, submarine violet that clings to the edges of things rather than illuminating the spaces between them. Through this gloom, the valley—if a gap between things that have not finished deciding on their own geometry can be called a valley—stretches out for leagues. It is a regional void punctuated by extreme, vertigo-inducing altitudes. Great stepped monoliths of raw, unweathered bismuth and slate-gray corundum hang miles above a non-existent floor, separated by vast, echoing gulfs of pale, dusty emptiness. 

Nothing here is worn down. There is no wind-scour, no rain-rut, no talus slope of broken scree. Every cliff edge is wickedly sharp, freshly extruded from the fabric of nowhere. If you stare closely at the sheer face of a distant spire, you can see its lower boundary isn't broken off; it is simply *unfinished*, the dark mineral fading into a pixelated smear of grey nothingness, taking centuries to fully render into solid mass. Stone here does not erode; it precipitates out of the spatial fault lines, millimeter by agonizing millimeter.

And the sound of this precipitation is catastrophic.

Because the landscape is entirely still—nothing moves quickly, no wind blows, no pebbles fall—the sheer volume of the sound feels severed from cause and effect. It is a deafening, continuous, physical roar, like standing inside the throat of a bronze bell being struck by a glacier. It is the acoustic friction of reality grinding against itself as raw matter forces its way into three-dimensional space. The air vibrates so violently that dry dust hovers a fraction of an inch off the flat stone ledges, dancing in neat, geometric Chladni patterns. You do not hear it with your ears after the first minute; you hear it with your ribcage, your teeth, the fluid in your eyes.

Feeding directly on this annihilating vibration is the flora. 

Because the pinnacles are spaced far apart across the dim gulf, the overall emptiness of the region remains vast, yet every available shelf of newly-minted stone is choked with lush, extravagant growth. There is no soil and no sap. These plants are dry, dense architectures of flexible carbon, fibrous silicate, and spun metallic glass. Thick, bloated ferns the color of dried plums uncoil from hairline fractures in the raw rock, their fronds pleated like accordions to catch the maximum surface area of the soundwaves. Towering, velvety stalks of charcoal-black kelp lean out over the mile-deep drops, completely rigid in the dead air, yet vibrating at a blur at their tips, drinking the kinetic fury of the roar and converting it into slow, cold growth. Broad-leafed creepers weave thick, dry mats across the razor-sharp terraces, their foliage overlapping like dark scales, shedding flakes of dead mica instead of dry leaves. 

Nothing crawls among these velvet thickets. No insects buzz, no birds ride the dense air, no lizards dart across the pristine facets of the stone. An eardrum or a nervous system cannot survive long in the bass-heavy pressure wave that sustains the silence-starved greenery. Life here stopped at the botanical, perfecting the art of stillness and acoustic photosynthesis.

Yet someone was here, or still is, operating on a timescale too slow to catch in the act. 

Scattered miles apart, clinging to the dizzying edges of the emerging crags, are small, stark interventions of hand-worked craft. There are no roads connecting them—often there isn't even continuous ground between them, as a ledge will simply cease to exist mid-span, its physical laws dropping away into a smooth vertical plane of violet static. 

On one high, isolated shelf of black feldspar sits a low windbreak—though there is no wind—constructed of unweathered stone slabs fitted together without mortar. Inside the shelter lies a scatter of knapped flint blades and heavy, hand-forged copper chisels, dull green without moisture's patina, resting on a bed of harvested, dried acoustic-moss. Half a mile across an impossible chasm, anchored to the vertical wall of a hexagonal basalt column that ends abruptly in empty air, hangs a bridge woven from the tough, metallic vines of the cliff-kelp. It leads from a shallow cave directly into a blank, unformed patch of reality, the woven ropes terminating neatly inside a shimmering flaw in space, as if the builders were patiently waiting for the rest of the mountain to finish existing so they could walk across.

Further down in the violet murk, on a broad, tilting plateau of raw anhydrite, stands a circle of upright stone needles, chipped into shape with heavy hammer-stones. Bound to the top of each needle with dry plant-fiber twine are large, hollow gourds grown by the silicate creepers. They act as Helmholtz resonators. If you step directly inside the stone circle, the deafening, bone-splintering roar of the valley suddenly cancels itself out. Phase-inverted by the primitive gourds, the noise drops to a heavy, suffocating hum, creating a ten-foot pocket of survivable quiet.

Outside the circle, the rules break down further. Perspective refuses to behave across the sparse distances. A towering cascade of dark, lush canopy hanging from a cliff three miles away will appear sharply detailed, every dry, serrated leaf distinct in the dimness, while the raw, razor-edged stone beneath your own boots blurs out of focus, losing its texture for hours at a time before snapping back into cold, hard mineral. Shadows fall upward toward the violet sky, cast by light that isn't coming from below. Gravity holds true on the stone, pulling strictly downward toward the unseen nadir, except where the vegetation grows thickest; there, shed leaves of metallic glass drift sideways when they detach, caught in slow eddies of bent space, taking weeks to slide fifty feet out into the gulf before vanishing entirely.

Nothing hurries. A vine takes forty years to creep across a primitive copper adze left lying on a terrace. A mountain takes ten thousand years to push another three inches of unblemished, razor-sharp granite out of the roaring dark. And the sound goes on without pitch change or pause—vast, cold, dry, and loud enough to hold the sky apart from the stone.
