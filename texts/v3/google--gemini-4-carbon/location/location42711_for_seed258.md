There is no stone under this valley, and no soil. If you kneel on one of the vast, open esplanades and press a palm flat against the ground, what meets your skin is warm, slightly yielding, and slick with a clear, continuous sweat—the taut, waxy epidermis of something living, hardened beneath the surface into dense, pale-green wood. 

The entire basin, miles across and cradled high in bright, thin upland air, is a single continuous architecture of living tissue. None of it was carved, quarried, or assembled with tools; there are no joints, no mortar lines, no scars of blades. Instead, the landscape has grown directly into the geometry of a monumental, empty city. Broad, level terraces step upward toward the rim of the sky in crisp ninety-degree risers, their edges as sharp and unblemished as the rim of a newly uncurled succulent leaf. Between the terraces stretch immense, uncrowded plazas—half a mile of smooth, lime-pale cambium without a single bush, weed, or fallen twig to break the sightline. 

Everything here is sparse, separated by generous gulfs of warm mountain light and open air. Far across a flooded court, four solitary columns rise three hundred feet out of the floor, spaced half a mile apart. They have no branches and no leaves; they are smooth, fluted cylinders of living timber, glistening wet, flaring near their tops into flat, circular parasols that do not touch one another. Further out, near the high lip of the valley where clouds pass at eye level, a line of towering parabolic arches strides across nothing at all, framing empty blue sky. Every surface has the glossy, turgid perfection of embryonic growth—pale jade, ivory-green, and translucent yellow-wood—as though the entire valley finished unfolding twenty minutes ago and has not yet suffered its first scratch, its first speck of lichen, or its first dead cell.

Nothing decays here because nothing advances. The place exists in a locked metabolic stalemate. Under the clear, ordinary daylight, the miles of smooth green pavement drink the sun not to grow taller or put out seed, but simply to maintain their exact crisp margins and to run the pumps.

You hear the pumps before you understand them. The valley is startlingly loud. Despite the emptiness of the wide courts and the stillness of the air, a continuous, full-bodied roar fills the basin from rim to rim, loud enough that two people standing paces apart would have to shout. Beneath your feet, deep inside the lignified bulk of the plazas, colossal vascular bundles pull water upward under tremendous turgor pressure, producing a steady, thrumming rush like blood heard inside an ear, magnified to the scale of geology. At the crisp seam of every stepped terrace, millions of aligned pores—living hydathodes the size of pinheads—weep warm, pure water in an unbroken rhythm. 

There is no rain, yet everything is drenched. Thin, glassy sheets of water slide perpetually across the flat green expanses, catching the bright daylight like wet porcelain, never cutting channels into the tough living skin, never carrying silt because there is no silt to carry. Where the sheets reach the spaced, circular shafts sunk into the plazas—wide, lipped mouths lined with pale pink, ribbed tissue—the water plunges downward into deep, hollow resonance chambers grown into the under-structure of the valley. Each shaft booms on a fixed, unchanging pitch, half waterfall, half organ pipe. Heard together across three miles of open space, the thousands of draining shafts lock into a dense, roaring chord that never swells, never fades, and never stops.

Above the wet sheen of the ground, the air smells clean, warm, and faintly of crushed celery and wet cut grass, without the sourness of rot or humus. Vapor rises off the sun-warmed terraces in thin wisps that vanish quickly into the high sky.

Life apart from the structure itself is rare, visible mostly because there is nowhere for anything to hide. Halfway across an acre of sunlit, running film, a single animal might take twenty minutes to cross from the shadow of one great column to the next: something long-legged and deliberate, built like a crane made of pale chitin or fine bone, stepping carefully so its splayed feet do not slip on the wet epidermis. It lowers a narrow, flexible muzzle to sip from the weeping pores along a step-riser, drinking water faintly sweetened with plant sugars, then moves on, leaving no footprints on the springy, self-sealing rind. High up under the curve of a distant arch, a pair of broad, flat-bodied gliders cling like wet leaves against the green wood, motionless for hours in the roar, soaking up the ambient warmth of the pulsing column beneath their bellies. When they launch, they cross a mile of bright empty air without flapping, dwarfed by the immense, clean intervals between one grown monument and the next.

Nothing falls. No bark peels. No moss softens an angle. At the edge of the valley, where the outermost terrace simply stops above a drop into lower, cloud-filled air, the living wall plunges straight down in a smooth, plumb cliff of green tissue, weeping a continuous bright curtain of water off its crisp lip into the void—brand new, deafening, and entirely finished.
