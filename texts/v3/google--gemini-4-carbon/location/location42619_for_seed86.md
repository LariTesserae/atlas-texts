The floor of the basin lies nine hundred feet below the surrounding plateau, dropped cleanly along a pair of parallel fault lines so recent in geological time that the walls have not yet learned to crumble smoothly. Everything is edge and plane. The encircling cliffs rise in sheer, stepped faces of dark grey limestone and banded chert, their corners crisp enough to slice leather, overhanging slightly near the rim so that the sky above is reduced to a long, pale, mist-choked slot. 

Down here, the light never brightens past the color of wet pewter. High above the rim, a permanent deck of coastal stratus sits pinned against the plateau, diffusing the sun into a shadowless, sourceless gloom that scarcely distinguishes noon from morning, or winter from summer. Because the walls overhang and the basin is miles across—wide enough that the far cliffs dissolve into a flat, slate-colored haze—the air at the bottom is trapped in a cold, heavy inversion. Nothing stirs it. There is no wind, no draft, no thermal updraft climbing the stone. The temperature hangs permanently just above the point where breath turns visible.

Across the miles of the sunken floor, the terrain is open and stark. Giant rectangular slabs, detached during the original subsidence, sit miles apart on vast, flat aprons of shattered scree and dry rock flour. Between these isolated monoliths there is mostly empty space—broad, level corridors of grey gravel where a line of sight runs uninterrupted for half a league. No rivers cut this gravel. Rain almost never falls directly through the narrow throat of the chasm; instead, moisture arrives sideways as heavy air sinking down the walls, condensing on the cold stone in a perpetual, microscopic sweat. Every vertical face gleams faintly. Beneath protruding ledges, the sweat gathers into slow, rhythmic drips that have pooled in shallow bedrock saucers, forming dark, still mirrors yards wide and an inch deep, bordered by thin crusts of white lime.

Around these damp saucers grows the little green the basin can manage: flat rosettes of dark liverwort, wiry clumps of spleenwort wedged into razor-thin fissures, and wide, widely spaced patches of pale, brittle grass that never flowers. Most of the gravel floor is completely bare of roots.

Yet the basin roars with animals. 

What the stone lacks in soil, it makes up for in refuge, and the sheer, unscalable miles of fractured cliff serve as an immense biological trap and sanctuary. Millions of birds—sooty swiftlets, cliff swallows, and heavy-bodied, night-eyed fruit birds the size of crows—pack every horizontal seam and overhang from twenty feet off the floor all the way into the upper gloom. The air across the open valley is never clear of them; it looks perpetually like smoke filled with black chaff, swirling in slow, dense, counter-rotating columns that stretch for miles from wall to wall. Their sharp, metallic echolocation clicks, dry wing-claps, and harsh, grating territorial cries merge inside the stone amphitheater into a continuous, mid-frequency din, like heavy surf dragging endlessly across shingle. You have to shout to be heard ten paces away, and the sound never rises or falls. There is no dawn chorus and no evening hush—as half the flocks spiral upward through the distant slit in the clouds to feed on the unseen surface, an equal mass drops out of the grey ceiling to take their place on the ledges.

Below the roosting cliffs, the floor writhes. Long, low talus cones are slicked white and brown with guano, damp from the rock-sweat, smelling sharply of ammonia and wet chalk. Over these slopes, and spreading far out across the otherwise barren gravel flats, moves an unbroken carpet of scavengers. Flightless black beetles click over the stones in the hundreds of thousands; pale, long-legged cave crickets vault in silent waves ahead of any footstep; blind, translucent isopods crowd the margins of the condensation puddles. Feeding on the insects are thousands of small, dull-scaled lizards and spotted salamanders, sluggish in the fifty-degree air, lying three and four to a stone, barely bothering to dart away. Small, wide-eared foxes and lean rock-martens patrol the wide spaces between the boulders in plain sight, stepping daintily through the drifts of dropped feathers and cracked eggshells, so glutted on fallen fledglings that they ignore one another as they pass. Dead birds are stripped to clean, dry keels of bone within an hour, leaving pale scatterings of hollow ribs across the dark chert gravel that look like frost.

Nothing has been built down here. There are no walls, no cleared paths, no cairns, no shelters roofed against a rain that never falls. The basin refuses habitation by sheer chill, dampness, and the relentless pressure of the noise. 

Still, along the bases of the lower monoliths, there are sparse, quiet marks of hands. Tied around a sharp horn of limestone forty feet up a sheer face hangs a knotted length of three-ply bast rope, stiff with grease and mineral damp, left behind where egg-gatherers descend from some narrow chimney in the cliff. Under a dry overhang half a mile out on the open flats sits a scatter of struck chert flakes—sharp-edged, unweathered, identical in color to the living rock—surrounding two heavy cobblestone hammerstones and a bundle of long, fire-hardened ash poles bound together with rawhide thongs, their tips fitted with simple bone hooks for raking nests from deep crevices. Nearby, a shallow ring of charcoal lies cold on the gravel, fueled once with dried woody stems carried down from the upper world; in the dead, windless air, the grey ash sits undisturbed in the exact shape of the twigs that burned, unblown for months or years. Even the footprints pressed into the damp dust around a nearby seep remain sharp-rimmed until the slow shuffle of beetles and foxes gradually smooths them back down.

Everything in the depression exists in a locked, noisy balance. No streams carve the young, jagged gullies any deeper; no frost splits the fresh boulders; no seasons flush the valley with meltwater or bake it dry. The dim grey light holds steady in the mist slot overhead, the cold condensation gathers and drops at three-second intervals into the shallow stone pans, and the dark, deafening tide of wings turns endlessly between the gravel and the rim.
