There is no sky here, and hundreds of meters of solid overburden press down from above, yet the chamber is flooded with the fierce, shadowless glare of high noon. 

It is a single, enclosed vault—roughly the length and height of a cathedral nave—carved out of pale, dense limestone and dark bands of chert. Nothing in the room is sharp anymore. Every wall, rib, and ceiling vault has been scoured over thousands of years into sweeping, parabolic flutes and deep, scalloped hollows, polished to the slickness of wet bone. The stone looks less like a cave and more like the interior of a gigantic, hollowed-out river cobble.

The light comes from three unanchored nodes hanging twenty meters above the floor, spaced widely apart in the open air. They are not lamps; there is no housing, no filament, no visible hardware holding them aloft. They are simply fists of blinding, blue-white radiance that hum and skitter slightly around fixed points in space, shedding an intense, summer-warm heat.

Because of them, the air inside the chamber is locked in a state of permanent, roaring violence.

Groundwater seeps constantly through fractures in the ceiling and weeps from porous strata high on the walls. In an ordinary cavern, it would drip and pool in the dark. Here, as the cold seepage falls toward the center of the room, it hits the rising thermal plumes and localized pressure-shears generated by the suspended lights. Water shatters in mid-air into stinging, warm spray. Gale-force drafts—trapped with nowhere to go—race up the curved walls, curl across the vaulted ceiling, and slam back down toward the floor in erratic, shifting cyclones. Rain falls sideways, spirals upward in glowing curtains, and sheets across the stone in sudden, ankle-deep surges that vanish down floor-siphons seconds later.

The sound is immense and inescapable. Without open sky to swallow the acoustics, the chamber acts as a resonant drum. The tear of wind, the heavy slap of water sheeting against smooth rock, and a deep, percussive *thrum* from the air-shears overlap into a continuous roar that vibrates in the teeth and sternum. Every ten or fifteen seconds, two opposing gusts collide near the center of the vault with a sharp, barometric *crack*, briefly flattening the airborne mist and snapping the entire drenched chamber into crisp, blinding focus before the white spray boils back up to blur the far walls.

Between these warring currents, the space is remarkably bare. There is no rubble, no talus slope, no clutter—anything loose was pulverized and washed down the drainage fissures ages ago. What built structures remain are sparse and stubbornly intact, standing out against the eroded rock because they have refused to wear away at all. 

Near the northern curve of the wall, a thin, seamless ribbon of matte-black material emerges from the stone, arcs gracefully through empty air for twelve meters—where the limestone that once held it has long since dissolved—and plunges back into the floor. Water will not touch it; even in the heaviest sideways downpour, a millimeter-thin envelope of dry air clings to its surface, splitting the torrent cleanly around it. Farther out on the wide, sloped basin of the floor stand four low, cylindrical plinths, spaced widely apart like the surviving pilings of a vanished pier. Their tops are sheared completely flat, looking less broken than cleanly edited out of existence. Faint, hair-thin geometric inlays still cross the stone near their bases, sitting proud of the surrounding floor by several inches, preserving the exact level the cavern floor held millennia ago before the endless warm storms wore the rock down around them.

Life exists here, but only in the narrow margins where the turbulence becomes predictable. 

In the aerodynamic lee of the deepest rock-flutes, and tucked behind the shelter of the four plinths, sparse colonies of vegetation cling to the slick limestone. They have no stems to snap; they grow as tough, rubbery, emerald-green straps and translucent mats, plastered flat against the rock like wet kelp, drinking the warm spray and gorging on the relentless, twenty-four-hour glare. Where a persistent eddy throws water upward in a steady, unchanging arc, a single cluster of pale, cartilaginous stalks leans directly into the blast, flexing like tuning forks without ever breaking.

Occasionally, when a barometric crack clears the air, movement shows on the bare expanses of the walls. Pale, palm-sized creatures shaped like flattened teardrops—something between a shelled limpet and a mudskipper—cling to the polished stone by suction. They stay motionless for hours in the blast, spaced tens of meters apart across the vast wet stone. When the wind shifts and a dry patch momentarily opens on a rock face, one will release its hold just enough to hydroplane sideways across the water film at startling speed, shooting twenty feet across a vertical wall to snap up wind-stunned cave midges washed in from the upper groundwater, locking back onto the stone an instant before the next heavy curtain of water slams down.

Then the mist closes back in, brilliantly lit from within, turning the empty air of the vault into a churning, radiant whiteout—warm as a greenhouse, loud as a spillway, sealed deep beneath the quiet rock of the world.
