The plateau sits four miles above the old datum, an immense, bowl-curved basin of blistered basalt and ocher sinter stretching thirty miles from rim to weathered rim. There is no moisture here—not a drop of groundwater, not a ribbon of vapor in the thin, scorching sky. Yet the air is so hot it mimics the distortion of water, pooling in dry, refractive mirages along the low depressions where fine metallic dust has settled over millennia into flat, gray pans. The sun hangs ordinary and white in a pale, cloudless slate, illuminating everything with the flat, merciless clarity of high altitude. Shadows do not blur here; they cut clean, black shapes across rock that has been polished smooth by eras of dry thermal wind.

For miles at a glance, the basin appears utterly vacant. You can see twenty miles to the eastern escarpment with nothing breaking the line of sight—no scrub, no boulders larger than a kneeling animal, no ruins, no towers, no roads. The emptiness is monumental, an architecture of sheer distance and baked mineral.

And yet the air is deafening.

It is the sound of an ironworks operating at full, desperate capacity. A relentless, rhythmic hammering vibrates up through the soles of the feet; a chorus of high-pressure hisses, metallic shrieks of unlubricated bearings, and the deep, diesel-like chugging of ten thousand asynchronous pistons rolls endlessly across the barren flats. There is no factory. There has never been a builder here. The industry is entirely biological, and it is mostly out of phase with space.

Look closely at the empty air ten yards away, where a heat-shimmer seems to snag on nothing. A patch of the atmosphere fractures like stressed mica, and through the seam protrudes the flank of an organism—a glistening, grease-black cylinder of chitin and heat-blued steel, pulsing with internal combustion. Only a cross-section of it is present in this reality: three yards of ribbed exhaust manifold, a cluster of twitching, caliper-like limbs striking sparks against the basalt, and a rhythmic vent blowing gouts of superheated sulfur and carbon soot onto the ground. The rest of the creature exists at an angle the eye cannot follow, folded into an adjacent volume of space. 

The plateau is teeming—choked to capacity—with an overwhelming ecology of mechanized fauna, yet ninety-nine percent of their mass occupies perpendicular geometries, leaving the visible landscape clean, sparse, and swept bare. What registers in three-dimensional space are only the intersections: the points where these vast, slow-moving engines graze the surface of the world to shed heat or scrape mineral.

Across the wide ocher flats, thousands of small, floating apertures drift a few feet above the stone like mirages made of brass and bone. From them drop heavy, articulated proboscises shaped like rotary drill bits. They touch the ancient, weathered rock, spin with a dry, ear-splitting screech, draw up a thin ribbon of pulverized iron ore, and retract back into nothingness. Where they touch, the rock shows millions of years of patient, circular grazing—shallow, overlapping saucer-marks worn seamlessly into the bedrock, smoothed over again by the constant dry wind. This is the primary erosion of the plateau: not rain, not ice, but the glacial, millennia-long milling of unseen mouths.

Larger presences move across the middle distance at the pace of hour hands. Four miles out, a shadow half a mile wide creeps across the dust pan, cast by nothing visible in the sky above it. Within the shadow, the air boils with static electricity, smelling sharply of ozone and hot gear oil. Along the leading edge of the darkness, a row of forty-foot treads—grown from layered horn and vulcanized carbon—briefly materializes, bites into the sinter with a slow, earth-shaking *crunch*, pulls forward six inches over the course of a minute, and dissolves again before leaving more than a yard of track. The sound of its internal flywheel—a low, catastrophic hum that rattles the teeth—fills the entire northern half of the valley, completely disproportionate to the empty, sun-drenched plain where it stands.

Nothing hurries. The heat is too total, the energy gradients too flat and ancient for speed. Predation, mating, and territory play out at the tempo of cooling magma or shifting dunes. Near a low ridge of polished obsidian, two overlapping zones of spatial distortion have collided. For days, perhaps weeks, two colossal organisms have been locked together here. All that can be seen in the bright afternoon sun is a floating tangle of copper-veined tendons, glowing radiator fins turning cherry-red in the dry air, and two immense, gear-toothed jaws clamping shut on empty space at a rate of a millimeter per hour. The shriek of their shearing metal holds a single, unwavering pitch, echoing off the distant basin walls like a factory whistle stuck open. Beneath them, a slow rain of dry slag, shed bolts of bone, and crystallized lubricants patters onto the stone—the only soil this world ever makes.

Around the slag heap, the closest thing this high desert has to vegetation gathers: flat, stationary crusts of matte-black silicon and tarnished silver that creep over the warm rock at a fraction of an inch per year. They have no cells or sap; they are solid-state lichens, harvesting the stray electromagnetic fields and vibrational racket of the beasts above, stepping down the acoustic roar of the valley into tiny, patient trickles of current to plate another layer of metal onto their margins.

As the sun drops lower toward the western rim, the light stays crisp and ordinary, lengthening the shadows of the sparse, rounded outcrops across the immense floor. There is no dusk haze, no cooling dew. The rock simply stops absorbing heat and begins radiating it back toward the pale sky. In the shifting thermal currents, the spatial seams widen slightly. For an hour before dark, the empty valley flickers with phantom machinery—a brief, silent glimpse of miles-high scaffolding made of ribs, horizon-spanning belts of segmented hide, entire migrating herds of roaring furnaces passing right through the solid stone of the ridges without disturbing a grain of dust—before the angles close again, leaving the vast, barren plateau empty to the eye, baking in the clear light, and thundering like an engine room with the lid torn off.
