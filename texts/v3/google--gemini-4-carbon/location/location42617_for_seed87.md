It fits entirely within a fracture in the seabed basalt no larger than a shipping container, forty meters down, where the water sits just two degrees above freezing. To find it, you have to follow a hairline crack in the ocean floor until the dark rock drops away into a flooded, vertical pocket. Inside this cramped recess, suspended in the numbingly cold, dense brine, is a metropolis under furious construction. 

There is no metal here, no glass, no Mortar mixed by hands, and yet the architecture is unmistakably urban. Towering, multi-tiered tenements of pale, secreted calcium carbonate and cemented black silt rise from the floor of the fissure, climbing the walls in cantilevered terraces, bridged by spanning arches of braided chitin. Every edge is razor-sharp, fresh, completely untouched by current or wear. Nothing has had time to weather or crumble; the whole complex looks as if it were extruded from the raw rock an hour ago. In fact, much of it is being extruded right now.

The builders—and citizens—are finger-length polychaete worms, blind and translucent, partnered in a frantic, weird symbiosis with six-legged, shell-less crustaceans the size of thumbnails. Together they crowd the narrow avenues of water between the towers, creating a dense, churning fog of living traffic. There are thousands of them per cubic meter. The worms secrete quick-hardening mineral pastes from collars behind their heads, laying down crisp new battlements, spiral conduits, and honeycombed apartment blocks in slow, rhythmic undulations. Immediately behind them, the pale crustaceans crawl over the wet, newly birthed stone, tamping grains of dark basalt sand into the matrix with flattened forelimbs, squaring off corners, cutting rectangular windows, and clearing out drainage channels. There are no tools, only specialized mouths and calcified limbs, operating according to an inherited, geometric mania.

Despite the relentless pace of the labor and the sheer crush of bodies brushing past one another in the avenues, the pocket is profoundly silent. The enclosing basalt walls deaden the distant groan of the open ocean above. Inside the fissure, the cold water feels thick, almost syrupy with dissolved minerals, absorbing every vibration. Thousands of tiny mandibles click against fresh limestone, bristles row through the brine, and heavy plumes of building-slurry settle into the trenches, yet none of it makes a sound that travels more than a millimeter. It is like watching a time-lapse of a booming, chaotic boomtown projected onto a screen in a soundproofed room.

A dim, sourceless illumination hangs over the district. It doesn't fall from the distant surface; the crack above is too narrow and twisted to let sunlit water down. Instead, the light comes from the city’s sparse agriculture. Sprouting exclusively along the sharpest, newest cornices of the towers—and occasionally planted in neat, deliberate rows inside recessed courtyard plazas—are stiff, wiry tufts of pale-violet kelp and translucent, leafless stalks that resemble blown glass. These sparse plants feed on faint, cold mineral seeps leaking from the basalt walls, and as they metabolize the sulfur and iron, their veins glow with a dull, steady tallow-light, like banked embers or dying streetlamps. The light catches the milky clouds of carbonate mortar drifting between the spires and turns the narrow alleyways into canyons of soft, violet-gray shadow.

Because the pocket is sealed off from the broader ocean currents, the city generates its own miniature weather. At the bottom of the fissure, three pinprick vents exhale streams of slightly warmer, mineral-heavy water—not hot enough to scald, just five or six degrees warmer than the surrounding chill. These faint plumes rise smoothly up the central shafts of the tallest tenements, carrying flocks of microscopic, sparkling larvae and clouds of unhardened building-silt toward the roof of the cave. When this rising water hits the freezing basalt ceiling, it cools abruptly and drops back down along the outer walls in slow, heavy curtains of gray sediment-rain. 

Life organizes itself around this silent, indoor climate. Along the updraft avenues, the crustaceans herd swarms of fat, blind copepods like slow-moving Dirigibles, corralling them into fenced-off balconies woven from the wiry violet stalks. Out in the downdrafts, where the silt-rain falls thickest, predators wait in the eaves. Pale, ribbon-thin flatworms, patterned in geometric checks that perfectly mimic the tiled masonry of the walls, peel themselves off the facades to drop onto passing builders, engulfing them in total silence before rippling away into the sewer-like aqueducts that lace the city's foundations. 

Nothing stays ruined for long. Where a flatworm’s ambush breaks a fragile new balustrade, twenty worms converge within minutes, layering fresh, gleaming white stone over the gap, expanding the terrace outward, throwing a new bridge across to the neighboring spire to brace the weight. The city is growing inward upon itself, filling the pocket centimeter by centimeter, turning a blind, freezing crack in the earth into a crowded, glowing, noiseless box of bone-white streets.
