The basin spans several miles across, low and flat-bottomed, bounded by a rim of dark, fractured shale that recedes into a permanent pewter haze. There is no sky to speak of, only an oppressive ceiling of cool, damp cloud that sits almost on the land, diffusing the light into a dim, shadowless twilight. Everything is slick. Nothing is green.

Though the basin is wide, the ground is choked. It is a dense, labyrinthine thicket of mineral spires, slanted slate fins, and clustered basalt columns that have split cleanly along fresh, sharp-angled joints. These stone formations stand shoulder-height to twice the height of a person, packed so closely together that moving ten yards requires turning sideways, stepping over ankle-deep channels of black water, and Ducking beneath cantilevered shelves of wet rock. There is no soil here, no sand or rounded gravel—the landscape is geologically young, recently cracked open, its edges keen enough to slice leather. The only softness comes from a pale, translucent slime—a pre-vegetative bacterial film—that coats the lower foot of every vertical surface like cold grease.

Despite the stillness of the air and the absence of any rushing river, the basin is astonishingly loud. 

The sound comes from the water and the architecture of the stone. Condensation bleeds continuously from the heavy air, gathering on the millions of sharp stone edges and falling into the flooded crevices below. Because the fractured rock is hollow, layered, and tightly packed, every drop strikes a resonant chamber. A drip hitting a thin, cantilevered fin of slate produces a sharp, metallic *tink*. Water spilling over a lip into a deep hexagonal shaft lets out a low, percussive *bloop* that echoes up like a struck marimba bar. Multiply this by ten million surfaces across three square miles, and the valley roars with a dense, static-like clatter—a relentless, overlapping chorus of clicks, chimes, splashes, and hollow thuds. You have to raise your voice to be heard over the sheer acoustic volume of slow, dripping water.

Occasionally, a deeper sound cuts through the percussive rain-noise: a sharp, gunshot *crack* followed by a slow, grinding *creeeeak*. Somewhere in the labyrinth, water seeping into a micro-fracture has finally wedged a fresh slab of stone apart. The rock shifts a fraction of an inch, settles against its neighbor, and stops. This is how the valley moves—imperceptibly, over decades, wedged open by its own wetness.

Woven through this mineral crowd are structures that are clearly built, though their purpose is opaque. Every hundred yards or so, the natural jumble of fallen shards has been cleared into small, circular courts or narrow corridors, and the loose, razor-edged plates of shale have been dry-stacked without mortar. There are low, corbelled domes the size of kilns, entirely windowless and dark inside. More common are what look like acoustic baffles: curved walls of meticulously angled slate tiles wedged between natural basalt pillars. 

Whoever built them possessed no metal, no wheels, no fasteners—only an obsessive understanding of leverage and stone grain. When water drips onto these constructed walls, the chaotic clatter of the surrounding basin resolves locally into a single, sustained, resonant hum. Stand beside one of these stacked cairns, and the roar of the valley turns into a low, vibrating chord you can feel in your teeth. Some of the cairns are half-collapsed, slowly being reclaimed by the shifting slabs; others look freshly laid, the grey dust of chipped stone still clinging to their undersides, unwashed by the mist. No builders are in sight.

Life, beyond the slick bacterial glaze at the waterline, reveals itself only if you stand completely still for twenty minutes. 

What looks like a wet, black ribbon of obsidian draped over a spire will, over the course of an hour, slide two inches downward. These are flat, featureless grazers, cold to the touch and tough as tire rubber, feeding slowly on the translucent film. Deeper in the flooded slots between the rocks, pale, multi-jointed things—all long, hair-thin legs and tiny, translucent bodies—hang suspended across the acoustic shafts like spiders in a flute. They do not spin webs; they hold onto the opposing stone walls with hooked tarsi, letting the relentless vibration of the dripping rock pass through their bodies, waiting days at a time for some smaller, water-borne mite to wash down the stone face into their reach. 

In the middle distance, rising slightly above the jagged grey-black crowd of the basin floor, stands a taller, solitary ring of upright monoliths—long blades of stone dragged upright and leaned inward until their tips nearly touch. Mist curls slowly around their points. Water runs down their flanks in silver sheets, feeding a deep, still pool at their center. From even half a mile away, through the dim, wet murk and over the clattering static of the stone thicket, that tall ring gives off a distinct, hollow, bell-like tolling—one slow note every six or seven seconds, marking the patient time of a cold and waking world.
