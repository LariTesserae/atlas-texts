The chamber is roughly the size and proportion of a sunken nave—sixty paces from the intake grilles at the north bulkhead to the outflow tunnels at the south, and forty feet from the paved invert floor to the crown of the vaulted ceiling. Almost the entire volume is filled with clear, cold freshwater. Only at the very apex of the brick arches does the water end, leaving a crescent of trapped, misty air less than two feet high, trembling constantly against the masonry. Everything else—the galleries, the catwalks, the switchgear housings, the crane rails, the stairwells that spiral down from sealed hatches overhead—exists underwater.

There is no natural rock anywhere in view, no silt bed of earth, no raw stone wall. Every square inch of the basin was laid, cast, bolted, or riveted. Yet the water has run through this room for so long that the architecture has lost all sharpness and settled into the smoothed geometry of a riverbed. Along the lower walls, courses of vitrified engineering brick have been scoured until bricks and mortar joints form a single, undulating, soapy surface, colored like wet slate and old oxblood. On the floor, heavy cast-iron deck plates have had their diamond tread completely erased; the metal lies dark and satin-smooth, dished slightly into shallow bowls where slow eddies have spun fine sand in circles for generations. Even the rows of structural rivets along the submerged plate-girders have been ground down into low, polished domes, barely distinguishable by touch from the steel beams they hold together.

The room is sparse inside its shell. Most of its volume is simply open, chilly water, slightly green in the middle distances, unimpeded except for three slender cast-iron columns rising along the center line and a single vertical drive-shaft descending on the east side. That open water thrums with noise. Sound has nowhere to escape; it rebounds off curved masonry and submerged steel, turning the water itself into a dense acoustic medium. A heavy, continuous bass roar pours from the three circular intake sluices at the north wall, where high-pressure water from some vast, unseen municipal tunnel or diverted subterranean river forces its way into the chamber. Beneath that hydraulic rush sits a slower, mechanical rhythm: the low, grinding thrum of a Francis turbine still turning inside its bronze scroll-case on the floor, rotating smoothly on ancient water-lubricated wooden bearings that have not worn through. Every four or five minutes, when back-pressure builds in the southern outflow, a counterweighted cast-iron flap valve the size of a carriage door lifts on worn brass hinges, vents a surging column of bubbles, and drops back against its granite seat with a muffled, percussive *boom* that travels straight through bone.

Because the old turbine still turns, a sealed brush-less dynamo mounted high near the ceiling vault still pushes a weak, fluctuating current through lead-sheathed cables pinned to the walls. Most of the fixtures along the submerged catwalks died long ago, their thick glass globes filled with cold water and rust, or sheared away entirely. Four remain lit. Down in the gloom, encased in heavy brass cages and thick marine glass, low-voltage carbon filaments glow a dim, steady amber. They do not illuminate the whole room; instead, they hang in the vast dark water like faint coals, casting wide, dusty cones of yellowish-green light that fade out before reaching the opposite walls, leaving the corners and the floor in deep twilight.

Only inside these four cones of electric light does anything green grow. Tightly ringed around the warm glass of the surviving bulkhead lamps, tufts of dark, wiry water-moss and short ribbons of emerald algae cling to the brass cages and trail out into the current like sparse beards. A few feet away from the bulbs, where the lumens drop off, the brickwork goes bare again, save for thin, pale grey films of iron-bacteria clinging to sheltered seams out of the main flow. 

Without soil or weeds to cloud the view, the animal life moving through the open water is startlingly visible. The room acts as a pressurized thoroughfare and feeding ground. Schools of hundreds of pale, finger-length dace and silvery bleak hang suspended in the vast empty center of the hall, all facing north into the current, their bodies holding steady with rapid, synchronized flicks of their tails. When the heavy outflow valve slams shut and sends a shockwave rolling backward through the room, the entire school pivots as one, flashing dull gold as their flanks catch the distant amber bulbs, before settling back into alignment. 

Along the polished floor plates and inside the flanged troughs of the I-beams, large, mottled crayfish walk deliberately against the tug of the undertow, their claws raised whenever a shadow passes overhead. Clusters of small, dark-shelled freshwater mussels carpet the leeward sides of the three central columns—strictly confined to the narrow hydrodynamic shadow behind the iron, their shells sheared cleanly away wherever they creep an inch too far into the scouring current. Higher up, weaving through the wide gaps where wooden catwalk planks rotted out centuries ago, thick-bodied freshwater eels three feet long patrol the structural steel. They coil inside empty conduit boxes and slide down the hollow housings of dead pressure gauges, waiting in ambush. Periodically, a heavy pike or deep-bellied brown trout—dark-backed, large-eyed, scarred lightly along the snout from brushing brick—glides out from beneath the rusted overhang of the turbine casing, cuts swiftly across the empty middle water to scatter the dace, and disappears back into the dimness near the outflow grates.

Every process in the chamber runs on a tireless loop governed by gravity and pressure. Water rushes in cold from the dark behind the north grilles, drives the bronze runner, feeds the four dim lamps, feeds the tiny haloes of moss, sweeps detached strands of algae out to the waiting schools of small fish, and rolls onward through the southern culverts. Up at the vaulted ceiling, in that thin, booming pocket of air, stray bubbles brought in through the sluices burst continuously against wet brick, shedding a fine, chilly mist that drips right back into the flood below.
