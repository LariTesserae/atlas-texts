The valley is five miles wide and packed from rim to rim with standing stone, so tightly set that from the upper ledges it looks less like a landscape of individual rocks and more like the sheared cross-section of a petrified muscle—millions of vertical fibers pressed shoulder to shoulder across the high basin floor. 

There is no soil here, no gravel scree, not a single blade of grass or crust of lichen. There is only pale, siliceous dolomite, weathered over ages into vertical columns, fins, and deeply fluted spires ranging from the height of a man to the height of a cathedral nave. Between them run crevices rarely wider than an outstretched arm, and often narrower than a boot. Yet because the basin tilts gently downward from its surrounding cirque, the view is vast and unobstructed across the tops of the pillars: an immense, glittering expanse of crowded stone stretching away under a thin, dark-blue high-altitude sky.

The light is ferocious. At this elevation the atmosphere filters very little, and the sun hangs in a cloudless vault, pouring raw white brilliance onto pale rock that is, everywhere and at all times, slick with water. 

The wetness does not fall as rain. Instead, a cold, dense current of air continuously spills over the western pass—pushed up from unseen Maritime lows miles below—and shears across the basin. As this moisture-heavy wind strikes the chilled thermal mass of the stone, water condenses directly onto the rock faces without ever forming mist or cloud. Every spire sweats. Every vertical flute and scalloped groove carries a continuous, shivering skin of clear water. Under the blazing sun, the entire five-mile labyrinth acts as a single shattered mirror. Glare ricochets down into the narrowest slots; light bounces off wet stone onto opposing wet stone until even the deepest fissures are flooded with a cold, shadowless luminance that aches behind the eyes.

And the sound is total. 

Because the wind never slackens, and because it must force its way through tens of millions of wet, grooved stone throats, the valley roars at a pitch and volume that erases thought. It is not the gusting, rising-and-falling howl of an ordinary mountain storm. It is a solid, invariant wall of acoustic pressure—part organ-pipe drone, part high-frequency hiss, underlaid by the continuous hydraulic rush of millions of gallons of condensate draining down into the porous karst plumbing beneath the floor. Down in the fissures, the stone itself hums against the palms. A shout disappears an inch from the lips. Drop a rock, and you see it shatter into pale splinters against a wet plinth without hearing the slightest click over the encompassing din.

Nothing here appears to be wearing away anymore, and nothing appears to be building up. The rock is hard enough, and the chemistry of the condensing water mild enough, that the landscape has settled into a deep morphological groove. Each runnel is sized precisely to the volume of water it carries; each aerodynamic bevel on the windward pillars matches the unvarying vector of the air. At the base of every column, smooth circular throats swallow the runoff cleanly into subterranean conduits, preventing pools from ever forming, preventing mud, preventing stillness. There is no stagnation and no flood. Today is identical to yesterday and to ten thousand years ago: the same glare, the same cold, constant wind, the same sheet of water sliding down the same grey-white channels.

Green life has never gained a purchase. There are no crevices of duff, no pockets of still air, and the ultraviolet glare reflected from every angle strips exposed organic tissue. What base biology exists is invisible to the naked eye—a transparent, slick microfilm of lithotrophic bacteria bonded tightly to the crystal lattice of the dolomite beneath the running water, feeding on dissolved trace minerals and harsh light.

Because food is sparse and invisible, animal life is rare, isolated within the endless stone crowd, and entirely deaf. Hearing is a useless expenditure of tissue in an environment that never drops below a roar. 

Now and then, pressed flat against the blinding wet flank of a pillar, there is a grazer: a broad, shield-shaped arthropod the size of a dinner plate, its carapace the exact milky, translucent grey of wet quartz. Its underside is a single muscular suction foot fringed with stiff chitinous scrapers. It moves millimeters an hour, grazing the microscopic film off the stone while fifty-mile-an-hour wind tears harmlessly over its beveled back. Its eyes are reduced to tiny, sunken pinholes shielded by overhanging ridges of shell, filtering the unbearable brightness down to navigable polarized bands.

Higher up, spanning the narrow gaps between the closely packed spires, long-limbed Pale Harvestmen—or creatures convergent with them—brace themselves on eight jointed legs tipped with dual opposing hooks. Their bodies are small as walnuts, coated in waxy white plates that shed water instantly, while their legs span nearly two feet, allowing them to bridge the wind-slots and step from pillar to pillar without ever descending into the heavier runoff at the floor. They hunt by vibration alone, though not the vibration of the stone, which is saturated with the wind's constant tremor; instead, they extend two whip-thin sensory filaments straight downstream into the air currents, reading the minute, sudden turbulence wakes left when a smaller stone-crawler loses suction or shifts between flutes.

To move through this place requires wedging oneself sideways into the labyrinth. The rock is cold enough to numb bare fingers in minutes, smooth as wet soap along the water-chutes, yet razor-sharp along the narrow ridges dividing one flute from the next. Looking straight up from inside the packed array shows only a jagged ribbon of deep cobalt sky framed by glaring, weeping edges of stone. Step out onto one of the rare, slightly elevated shoulders of the bedrock, however, and the claustrophobia vanishes instantly into sheer exposure: miles of crowded, gleaming white needles packed tight to the distant mountain rims, motionless under a hammering sun, screaming at a single unalterable note.
