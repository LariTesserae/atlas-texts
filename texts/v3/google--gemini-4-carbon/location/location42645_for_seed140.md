There is no sight here, and there has not been for longer than the surrounding rock has held its current shape. Not dimness, not shadow, not even the faint chemical ghost of bioluminescence—nothing wastes energy making photons where nothing has eyes to catch them. The place exists entirely in mass, heat, moisture, and a deafening, resonant wall of sound.

It is an enclosed dome near the summit of an ancient, worn-down mountain—high up where the outside air has thinned and cooled over geological epochs, while the mountain itself has slumped into a broad, whalebacked swell devoid of crags. Just beneath that flattened crown sits a single hollow pocket the size of a village church: perhaps seventy paces from wall to wall, curving forty meters overhead into a smooth vault. 

Almost all of that volume is empty. Between the floor and the arched ceiling hangs nothing at all—no columns, no stalactites, no hanging vines, no trunks or branches dividing the air. It is a vast bubble of warm, breathable stillness, completely vacant through its center. A pebble tossed upward travels through unbroken, humid space before striking the far curve of the dome, and the click of its landing rings cleanly back from every surface at once.

What keeps this high-altitude pocket warm—warm like the inside of a closed fist, or a greenhouse hours after sunset—are five small objects scattered without symmetry across the basin. None is larger than a boundary stone. If you find one with your hands, your fingers slide off it without catching friction, though it is neither wet nor oily; condensation simply refuses to bead on its surface, parting around the material before touching it. One of the objects rests half-sunken in the ancient bedrock, merged into the grain of the stone without a seam. Another hangs the width of two fingers above the floor, locked not to the rock beneath it, which has settled and compressed by millimeters over hundreds of thousands of years, but to some deeper gravitational coordinate it refuses to yield. They do not hum, vibrate, or glow. They bleed a steady, gentle thermal bath and a quiet metabolic field that bypasses light altogether, feeding cellular life directly across empty space.

Because energy arrives evenly from all directions rather than falling from a sky, the lush vegetation here has never bothered with height. Trunks and stems exist to race neighbors toward overhead light; without that race, the flora remains pressed in extravagant, heavy abundance against the inner skin of the rock bowl, leaving the great central hollow bare. Underfoot and partway up the curving walls, growth piles knee-deep in dense, turgid cushions. There are broad, rubbery rosettes as wide as wagon wheels, cold-bloodedly slick with water; mats of velvety, non-vascular plush that give like wet wool under weight; coiled, fleshy creepers as thick as forearms braided tightly over the stone; and quivering bladders full of sweet, mucilaginous sap. None of it has color in any meaningful sense—stripped of chlorophyll, its tissues are pale wax, blind tallow, or dark mineral-stained pulp—yet it smells fiercely alive, heavy with wet loam, crushed coriander, warm starch, and old rain.

Beneath the roots, where bare stone still protrudes in low swells, the rock has been smoothed to the texture of worn soap. Whatever sharp fractures once formed this chamber were erased ages ago by slow, tireless condensation. Moisture rises constantly from the warm, respiring plant-mats, hangs thick in the dark interior air until the air can hold no more, and settles against the slightly cooler stone of the upper vault. There are no rushing streams or inlets—only a continuous, patient weeping. Water slides down the curved walls in silent, velvet films, gathers in shallow tepid puddles across the flattened floor-pan, and drops in fat, heavy beads from the ceiling into the waiting cups of leaves below.

Into that open, empty acoustic bowl, the animals pour a relentless noise.

Life crowds the wet perimeter skin in startling numbers, navigating the pitch black entirely by pressure wave and echo. The air throbs with it. Blind, heavy-bellied amphibians the size of loaves sit wedged among the rubbery rosettes, inflating throat-sacs to Loose slow, brassy *booms* that bounce four or five times around the bare upper dome before decaying. Over that low pulse rides a dense, shimmering curtain of insect stridulation: Armored, long-antennaed crawlers rasping wing-cases tuned to the exact resonant frequencies of the hollow, turning the empty center of the room into an amplifier. Higher up, clinging to the wet bare ceiling where the plants thin out, colonies of soft, skin-winged gliders trade rapid, needle-sharp bursts of sonar clicks—a dry, rattling hail of sound that maps every curling leaf-edge and shifting puddle below. Occasionally one drops into the void, crosses the sixty meters of empty black air in two seconds of leathery, silent glide, and slaps wetly onto the opposite wall, sending a brief scatter of startled bell-pings rippling through the nearby frogs.

Nothing here hurries. Without a sun to cut time into days or tilt the world into seasons, change moves at the pace of geology and digestion. The five scattered artifacts hold the warmth steady within a tenth of a degree year after year. The only rhythm that enters from outside is atmospheric: when massive, slow weather systems drag across the high, eroded plateau overhead, the air pressure inside the hollow rises or drops almost imperceptibly over the course of weeks. When the pressure falls, the ceiling weeps a little faster, the heavy droplets striking the leaf-mats in a slightly thicker patter, and the chorus of amphibians drops half a semitone in the thinner air. When the high pressure returns, the dripping slows to solitary, ringing *plocks* spaced seconds apart, and the rasping insects swell to fill the gap. 

Everything stays where it has arrived. Dead leaves dissolve directly into warm jelly under the mouths of blind snails and pale, threadlike fungi, feeding the exact cushion of roots they grew from. The ancient mountain outside wears down another hair's breadth each millennium under high, cold winds; inside, in the absolute dark, the warm hollow breathes, drips, and rings like the inside of a struck bell.
