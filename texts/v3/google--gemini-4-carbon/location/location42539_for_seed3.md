There is no dust here because nothing has been here long enough to be ground down.

At twenty thousand feet, the stone does not weather; it arrives. Across a gulf five miles wide, the mountain range is still in the middle of happening—great, unrounded polyhedrons of black basalt and raw, vitreous quartz pushing outward from seams in the air that have no depth. Where a cliff face meets the sky, the edge is sharp enough to slice leather. There is no scree at the base of the walls, no talus slopes, no rounded gravel. Water does not carve channels here; instead, condensation gathers on the mirror-flat facets of newly extruded rock and spills down in thin, shivering sheets, dropping from terrace to razor-edged terrace until the mist reclaims it.

The light is the color of bruised pewter, sourceless and perpetually low, as though the upper atmosphere were lined with smoked glass. In this dimness, the city reveals itself mostly by its ironwork and its sodium lamps. 

It is an immense, scattered metropolis, built at the scale of an entire cordillera, yet mostly empty space. Districts do not touch. A tenement spire of dark brick and riveted steel clings to a needle of fresh granite; half a mile of thin, freezing air separates it from the next structure—a tiered foundry complex cantilevered over an abyss that hasn't finished rendering its own bottom. Between these isolated islands of masonry hang catenary sweeps of high-tension cables, cast-iron aqueducts, and braided wire tramways. Looking out from any gantry, the view stretches for leagues across an open, dizzying volume of twilight: fifty or sixty distinct industrial wards hung at wildly different altitudes, pinned to cliffs that weren't quite in those positions a decade ago.

The air is cold and permanently damp. Clouds do not float above the valley; they are manufactured inside it, forming wherever the warm breath of the blast furnaces and steam-condensers meets the chill of the extreme altitude. Everything sweats. Riveted girders wear coats of cold beads; brick alleys gleam like wet slate.

And wherever the brickwork radiates heat into that dampness, the vegetation takes hold with startling, crowded ferocity. Because there is no soil, the flora roots directly into mortar seams, beds of wet coal-clinker, and the microscopic tension-fractures of young stone. Enormous, fleshy ferns—the color of dark bottle-glass, almost black in the low light—unurl fronds six feet long beneath the bellies of steam pipes. Thick mats of velvet moss, ankle-deep and spongy with cold water, upholster the roofs of powerhouse sheds and spill down the sheer basalt in hanging curtains a hundred yards long. Pale, woody vines with leaves like waxed paper braid themselves into the latticework of transmission towers, drinking fog straight from the wind.

The plants are crowded out only by the animals, which are everywhere, treating the factories and the broken physics of the peaks as a single, continuous rookery.

Along the warm exhaust flues of the lower smelters, thousands of heavy, broad-winged crag-fowls roost shoulder to shoulder, their oily grey plumage beaded with mist. They do not fly across the miles of empty air in straight lines; they ride the spatial faults. Halfway across the central chasm, there is a visible crease in the gloom—a vertical strip where the background mountains repeat themselves twice, slightly offset. A flock will drop off a foundry roof, glide into that seam, and emerge instantly three hundred yards higher up, saving themselves the climb through the thin air. 

On the sheer walls below the wards, herds of pale, muscular ungulates with split, suction-cupped hooves pick their way across ninety-degree faces of unweathered obsidian. Where the local gravity frays near the extrusion zones, they graze upside down on the undersides of overhanging stone shelves, chewing mouths full of black fern while their breath rises—relative to the valley, falls—in slow white plumes. Swarms of blind, thumb-sized beetles with copper-colored carapaces cluster three inches thick around the ceramic insulators of the transformer stations, drawn to the sixty-cycle hum, clicking their wing-cases in rhythmic, deafening waves.

The valley is loud. The thin air should deaden sound, yet the sheer, glassy faces of the unweathered amphitheater bounce every noise back and forth until the whole region rings like the inside of an iron cistern. There is the constant, ragged roaring of millions of birds and cliff-beasts calling across the void; the sharp, hydraulic *crack* of new stone splitting into existence as a ridge adds another geometric facet to itself; the sizzle of wet moss touching hot manifold pipes; and underneath it all, the immense, unhurried labor of the engines.

Nothing in the city moves quickly. The machinery is built on a patient, geological tempo. In the open-air switchyards, counterweights the size of townhouses descend on greased chains at a rate of an inch per minute, winching slack out of suspension bridges as the peaks they are anchored to drift imperceptibly apart—or occasionally drift *through* one another without colliding, leaving two ghostly overlapping rock-faces occupying the same volume of cold fog. Down in the turbine halls, flywheels forty feet in diameter turn so slowly you can read the foundry stamps on their rims as they pass, driving massive grounding-rods deep into the raw crust to pin the local geometry in place.

A cable-car leaves a residential tower, dangling from a wrist-thick steel hawser. Its brass running-lights glow small and amber against the slate-colored immensity. Beneath its floorboards, drops of condensed cloud let go and fall into the gulf. Halfway down, the drops hit a horizontal plane of nothing in particular, flatten out as if against glass, pool into a shallow, floating lens of black water suspended two thousand feet above the valley floor, and sit there shivering in the vibration of a distant drop-forge—which strikes once, waits four full minutes while the echo rolls from peak to peak and sets every rookery screaming, and then, with tremendous, unhurried weight, lifts its hammer to strike again.
