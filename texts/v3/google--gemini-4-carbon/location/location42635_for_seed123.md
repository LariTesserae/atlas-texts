The entire territory is no wider than a courtyard and twice as deep as a tall man, dropped cleanly beneath the level of the surrounding scrub-plain like a freshly missing tooth. 

Six months ago—or perhaps six weeks—the ground here gave way over an abandoned thermal conduit, cracking open a dry blister in the low country. Because the collapse is so recent, nothing inside has been softened by weather. The walls are packed with jutting, unweathered plates of black vitrified clay and fractured slag-stone, their edges still razor-keen, glittering with raw, newly exposed mica. There is no floor in the flat sense of the word. Instead, the bottom of the pit is an interlocking jam of fallen stone blocks, split ceramic insulators the size of barrels, and thick, angular prisms of mineral salt that have crystallized rapidly in the rising heat. To move three feet requires stepping from the rim of a rusted flywheel onto the sharp ridge of a cleaved boulder, then wedging a boot between two sweating iron pipes that jut from the raw earth and end in midair, sheared clean through.

Daylight pours straight down through the jagged skylight of the collapse—clear, ordinary, warm afternoon sun that illuminates every hairline fracture in the glassy rock. There is nowhere for shadow to stretch out; the space is too crowded with surfaces. Every cubic inch is claimed by stone, metal, crystal, or meat.

Warmth wells up constantly from the sheared conduits at the bottom of the pocket. They no longer carry live steam, only a slow, rhythmic exhalation of humid air smelling of wet iron, machine oil, and baked clay. When this warm breath hits the cooler, sharp-angled stones higher up the walls, it beads instantly into heavy sweat. Water never pools enough to run, except in sudden, three-second trickles down the face of a cleaved slate block before vanishing into a seam. Everything is slick to the touch in patches and bone-dry an inch away. In the crook of a bent steel girder, a shallow teacup of warm condensation holds its level all afternoon, trembling slightly.

Plant life has barely found a purchase in the raw mineral clutter. Here and there, rooted directly into grease-stained mortar or the hairline cracks of a porcelain junction box, grow stiff, wire-thin stems with translucent bladders instead of leaves. A pale, rubbery lichen—the color of old tallow—has begun to plate the bolts of a half-buried turbine casing, drinking the condensation before it can drip. That is all the green there is.

Yet the pocket is boiling with animal life, wedged shoulder-to-shoulder in the bright, cramped warmth. 

They are creatures built for tight tolerances and industrial leavings, operating in near-total silence. Hundreds of flat, segmented things the size of human hands—plated in dull copper-colored chitin that mimics rusted sheet metal—cling in overlapping shingles across the vertical rock faces. They are constantly shifting, sliding over one another's backs with a faint, dry *shhhk* like shuffled cards, grazing on the film of mineral sweat and tallow-lichen. Wedged into the narrow, jagged V-gaps between fallen stones are pale, muscular tubes no thicker than thumbs, each tipped with a ring of stiff black bristles. Every few seconds, ten or twenty of them snap outward in unison to snatch microscopic midges out of the sunbeams, then retract with a soft, wet *pop*.

Near the bottom, perched atop the pristine, unweathered shards of a shattered flywheel, sit three heavy, knuckle-walking creatures shaped vaguely like skins of wet suede stretched over clockwork. They have no eyes, only broad, parabolic dishes of thin bone across their snouts that twitch toward the slightest vibration in the stone. They hunt the copper-plated crawlers, prying them off the rock using long, spatulate middle fingers that look remarkably like cold chisels. When one pries a crawler loose, there is no screech or scuffle—only the sharp *tick* of chitin popping free of stone, followed by the quiet, methodical crunch of shells being ground down between flat molars.

Built remnants are tangled directly into the geology, not as ruins worn down by centuries, but as clean, violent cross-sections of an active system interrupted yesterday and immediately colonized. A single cast-iron pressure gauge sticks out from a cleft of raw basalt at eye level, its glass face unbroken, its needle still twitching minutely between *40* and *45* in time with the warm drafts rising from below. Halfway up the eastern face, a short, orphaned run of narrow-gauge rail tracks protrudes forty inches out over the crowded pit, bolted to a slab of concrete whose underside is already hung with clustered, pulsing egg-sacs that look like greased ball bearings. 

At the very center of the pocket, half-crushed beneath a fresh wedge of shale, sits a heavy brass housing containing an intact governor mechanism. No wire feeds it, and its drive-shaft ends in a jagged twist of steel, yet the thermal updrafts rising through its lower vents keep its three weighted brass spheres spinning—slowly, unevenly, catching the bright afternoon sun and throwing revolving flecks of gold light across the packed walls, the sweating pipes, and the shifting layers of silent, busy shells.

The air holds a dense, localized quiet. Outside, twenty feet above the rim, wind might be moving across open country, invisible and unheard. Down in the crowded throat of the collapse, the world consists entirely of small, dry mechanical and biological clicks: the faint *ping* of dark stone expanding in the sun, the whisper of overlapping plates shifting for warmth, the irregular, ungreased *whir-tick* of the brass governor turning on rising vapor, and the occasional heavy drop of warm condensation falling six inches onto clean iron.

Every few minutes, the buried conduit system far beneath the rubble equalizes its pressure. A silent, invisible wave of warmer, wetter air swells up through the tight spaces between the boulders. Instantly, the entire packed pit responds at once. The glass face of the pressure gauge fogs opaque white; a thousand bristle-tubes bloom outward into the sudden mist; the copper-colored crawlers freeze in unison to let moisture bead on their backs; the blind, chisel-fingered hunters lift their bone-dished snouts to read the shifting air currents. For ten seconds, the cramped chamber glitters with a million microscopic droplets in the clear sunlight. Then the draft subsides, the gauge glass clears from the edges inward, the governor slows by half a turn, and the quiet, crowded feeding resumes across every sharp inch of stone.
