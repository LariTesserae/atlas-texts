The water is not clear enough to look across, even though the basin stretches unbroken for miles in every direction. Every cubic foot of the liquid is crowded with matter in suspension: glacial rock flour ground fine as talc, torn ribbons of dead peat washed down from drowned coastal bogs, clouds of grey mica flakes that catch the minimal light and kill it, and dark, neutrally buoyant tangles of detached sea-wrack rolling over and over in the dark. To be here is to occupy a cold, liquid landslide that never finishes falling. There is vast room—a drowned valley floor five miles wide between low, worn sills—yet there is no empty space at all. Something touches every surface at every second.

Sixty or eighty feet above, there is presumably air, or slush-ice, or a grey chop, though none of it is visible from the bottom. Light arrives only as a faint, iron-colored bruise that hangs in the middle distance for a few hours and then fades back to pitch. It has no direction. Scattered instantly by the particulate load, the dimness comes faintly from sideways and below as much as from overhead, turning everything beyond arm’s reach into a dense, grainy slate.

Beneath this moving soup lies stone so old it has lost all geometry. What were once ridges, ledges, and fault-scarps have been planed by ice and then scoured for ten thousand years by water-borne grit until the bedrock is a single undulating slab of polished gneiss, smooth as greased bone. There is nowhere to hide on it, no overhang, no crevice deeper than a thumb. Where the rock dips into wide, shallow pans, those pans are packed rim-to-rim with rounded cobbles—millions of grey and black ovoids the size of fists and skulls, rubbed so slick they cannot lock together.

The water above them is in violent, ceaseless conflict. Heavy saltwater surging in over the seaward sill collides with dense, freezing meltwater plunging down from sunken river mouths, creating towering internal eddies, shear walls, and rolling horizontal vortices that sweep back and forth across the flat plain. When a heavy bore passes over one of the cobble pans, the entire floor mobilizes at once. Millions of round stones lift slightly and slide over one another in a wide, migrating sheet. 

Yet for all this force, the place is startlingly quiet. There is no crash of surf, no roar of wind, and the thick curtain of suspended clay acts as an acoustic blanket, swallowing high frequencies within a few yards. The turbulence expresses itself almost entirely as pressure and touch rather than noise. When the cobble beds shift, they do not clatter; buffered by thick, near-freezing water and coats of fine slime, they emit only a low, muffled *shuck-shuck-shuck*, like wet grain pouring inside a sack. The dominant sensation is silent, muscular shoving—the water hauling hard to the east, stalling into a tight, boiling knot of mud that blots out even the faintest grey gloom, and then whipping suddenly north with enough drag to tear anything upright loose from its base.

Very little tries to stand upright. Across acres of bare, polished stone, vegetation appears only at long intervals: solitary holdfasts of tough, rubbery brown kelp gripping microscopic pits in the gneiss. Their stems are thick as wrists and flexed completely sideways, pinned flat against the floor by the current. Their blades are perforated like colanders to let the water pass through without ripping them free, and their ragged tips thrash continuously against the rock, polishing clean, pale fan-shapes into the dark stone downstream of every root. Between them, whole half-miles go by with nothing growing at all, just the sweeping sheets of silt and rolling balls of dead, tangled red weed brought in from elsewhere, tumbling past like wet tumbleweeds on an endless plain.

Life keeps low and far apart. An animal may search a quarter-mile of churning murk before crossing the scent-trail of another. Here and there, heavy-shelled whelks clamp down with muscular feet, oriented narrow-end into the flow like iron rivets. Occasionally a flatfish the exact color and texture of wet slate peels itself off a patch of gravel, rides an eddy forty yards sideways without moving a fin, and slaps back down into the rolling grit, vanishing before the silt clears its back. Eelpouts, pale and thick-skinned with eyes shrunk to pinpricks, wind themselves among the sliding cobbles, trusting flexible ribs to survive when the stones shift. Once in a great while, a dark, barrel-shaped bulk—a deep-diving seal or a sluggish sleeper shark—materializes out of the particulate fog, moving through the chaos with slow, heavy indifference, and is erased again in two heartbeats.

There are no structures on the floor, no pilings, no ruins, no timber wrecks, yet the basin is faintly stitched with evidence of hands working blindly from the world above. Out of the dark ceiling, at wide, irregular intervals, thin lines descend. They are twisted from split spruce root, braided rawhide, or three-ply sea-grass, darkened by cold water and frayed by passing grit. They do not hang straight; the cross-currents bow them out into tight, vibrating crescents. At the bottom of a line hangs a fist-sized piece of soapstone or grey basalt with a shallow groove pecked around its middle by another stone, binding the cord in place. Just above the weight, a single carved gorge of caribou antler or split bird-bone twitches in the murk, baited with a strip of white fat already leached pale by the cold.

Where the turbulence has won, the remnants of these efforts roll across the plain alongside the native cobbles. In the shallow depressions where heavier debris collects, grooved stone net-sinkers lie cheek-by-jowl with glacial pebbles, already half-erased back into plain rock by the relentless wet sanding. Sometimes a cylindrical trap woven from unpeeled willow withes comes tumbling end-over-end through the gloom, crushed slightly oval, weighted with flat stones lashed on with bark strips. It hits a smooth rise of bedrock without a sound, hangs for a moment in a rising boil of black silt, spins twice, and goes skidding away down the long, dark grade, joining everything else that cannot stop moving across the worn-out floor.
