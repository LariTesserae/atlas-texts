The chamber is no larger than a cistern—perhaps twelve feet across and twenty high, though its exact boundaries are difficult to fix because the walls are crowded inward by their own plumbing. There is no bedrock here, no dirt, not a single grain of sand to suggest how many hundreds of yards of crust press down from overhead. Every square inch is milled, cast, stamped, or extruded. The floor is a grating of galvanized steel whose diamond treads are still razor-crisp from the press, utterly untouched by boot-wear. Beneath the grating sits four inches of black, motionless water filmed with rainbow cutting-oil.

The cold is immediate and metallic, hovering just at the edge of freezing. It smells of cold zinc, ozone, fresh vulcanized rubber, and wet iron—the sharp, sterile odor of hardware taken straight from waxed wrapping paper. Nothing here has aged. There is no rust on the bolt-heads, no dust settled in the valleys of the corrugated conduits, no patina on the braided copper grounding straps. Tiny helical curls of bright steel swarf still cling to the rims of freshly tapped screw-holes, left behind because no wind has ever blown through this pocket to knock them loose.

Light comes in small, rationed pools: recessed bulkhead lamps behind thick, ribbed cages that glow the dull orange of under-volted filaments, and rows of tiny jewel-lights—amber, oxblood, pale green—set into baked-enamel control boxes. They throw overlapping, cross-hatched shadows through a dense thicket of cast-iron manifolds, brass sight-glasses, leaded joints, and flexible armored hoses that loop so closely together you could scarcely pass a gloved hand between them.

Despite the cramped stillness of the air, the room is quietly, tirelessly busy. It is still making itself. 

Along the upper curve of the chamber, a slow-moving radial gantry rides on greased worm-gears, its electric drive stepped down through so many reduction boxes that it makes no whine at all—only a velvet, rhythmic *tuck-tuck-tuck* like an oversized escapement. The arm presses heavy tiles of glazed white porcelain into wet beds of gray mastic along the ceiling, sealing seam after seam, advancing three inches an hour. Below it, hydraulic rams the thickness of fenceposts shift incrementally, adjusting the tension on great curved hull-plates that form the outer skin. Occasionally a relief valve opens with a soft, bronchial sigh, releasing a brief plume of warm, damp exhaust from deeper in the machinery. 

That breath of warmth creates weather inside the room. For two or three minutes after a vent sighs, fog thickens around the upper conduits, obscuring the amber indicator bulbs into smeared halos. Moisture beads instantly on the freezing cast-iron housings, swells into heavy drops, and patters down through the labyrinth of pipes—a localized, metallic rain that lasts ninety seconds before the deep chill reclaims the air. On the lowest, coldest sumps, the droplets freeze on contact into clear, bulbous rime; higher up, near the warm bellies of step-down transformers, the dampness lingers as a slick sweat.

In those narrow thermal bands around the transformers and pilot lamps, sparse plant life has taken hold. There are no animals here—no blind beetles, no mites, no cave spiders strung between the gauges, nothing with legs or lungs—only pale, opportunistic flora germinated from spores somehow trapped in the industrial tallow or cooling water during assembly. From the damp seams of mineral-wool pipe lagging sprout thin tufts of translucent, emerald-glass liverwort. A few wiry, leafless stems resembling miniature horsetails reach out from behind a warm solenoid block, leaning sharply toward an amber jewel-light, their segmented tips beaded with cold condensation. Where a slow drip of mineral-heavy condensate falls onto a flange, a single cushion of silvery moss has grown to the size of a fist, crisp with frost around its outer skirt while remaining soft and damp at its illuminated core.

The strangeness of the place lies in whom—or what—it appears built for. Every component belongs to a recognizable, heavy-industrial vocabulary: hexagonal nuts, flanged gate valves with cast-iron handwheels, porcelain stand-off insulators, brass nameplates stamped with crisp, unpainted serial numbers, enamel warning stripes in black and yellow. Yet there is no doorway, no hatch, no ladder, and no clearance for a human body. Handwheels are mounted two inches away from solid steel bulkheads where no hand could ever grip them, and they turn by themselves anyway, driven from within by hiddenratchets that click once every few minutes. Pressure-gauge dials face straight into the backs of I-beams, their needles trembling slightly in the dimness with nobody to read them. Sight-glasses show clear, viscous fluids rising and falling in rhythmic pulses, bubbleless and cold.

As the ceiling gantry finishes bedding a porcelain plate and advances another notch, a braided hose shifts sideways, gently brushing the stems of the horsetails and shaking loose a tiny shower of cold droplets onto the pristine grating below. They strike the oily water in the sump with four distinct, hollow *plinks*. Then the room settles back into its low, busy quiet: the soft sixty-cycle hum of the transformers, the slow creep of grease along a threaded rod, the faint groan of fresh steel settling into eternal dark, and the steady, patient tightening of bolts that have never been touched.
