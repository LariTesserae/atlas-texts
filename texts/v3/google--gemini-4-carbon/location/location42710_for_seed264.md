The basin stretches eight or nine miles across, bounded at its far edges by low, worn bluffs of yellow clay and ochre shale. From any given point on the floor, you can see the whole curve of the depression. There are no sharp edges left anywhere in it. Millennia of run-off and thermal weeping have softened every ridge into heavy, slumped contours, like poured wax left in a warm room. The sky overhead is bright, clear, and colorless toward the horizon, casting an even, shadowless glare across the flats. 

There is no wind, yet the air is loud. 

A continuous, rhythmic chugging rolls across the basin, layered over a deep, subterranean hum that you feel in the soles of your shoes and the back of your teeth. Every three or four hundred yards, spaced irregularly across the wide clay floor, rises a geological formation shaped like an inverted funnel—broad, terraced cones of sintered iron, gray travertine, and sulfur-stained limestone, standing ten to fifteen feet high. No hand laid these stones, and no steel was forged here, yet the basin operates with the deafening, relentless cadence of a foundry floor.

These are natural steam-hammer vents. Deep beneath the low valley floor, groundwater seeps steadily into fractured beds of hot, iron-rich basalt, flashing to steam inside constricted mineral chambers that act as pressure valves. Each cone has spent ages precipitating its own plumbing out of dissolved minerals, hardening and thickening until it achieved an exact, unvarying mechanical equilibrium. 

One vent, quarter of a mile to the east, releases a sharp, metallic *clack-hiss* every four seconds, regular as a flywheel, as a heavy cap of solid native copper—grown over centuries inside the throat of the chimney—lifts on a cushion of steam and drops back into its beveled stone seat. Another, closer by, emits a low, throbbing diesel-like chug, ejecting a brief, wet cough of warm mist that immediately condenses onto the dark, polished aprons of hematite surrounding its base. Dozens of these vents work across the miles of open space, their individual rhythms overlapping into a vast, unhurried polyrhythm of pistons, exhausts, and boilers. Nothing accelerates. Nothing winds down. The subterranean heat and the slow, steady trickle of the water table have been locked in this exact ratio for longer than the surrounding hills have held their current shapes.

Because there is no breeze to carry the moisture away, the warmth clings to the ground. The ground itself is never submerged, nor is it ever entirely dry. Between the widely spaced mineral cones lie broad expanses of smooth, dark mudstone and compacted silt, perpetually slick with a warm, oily sheen of condensation. Shallow, braided runnels no wider than a boot-sole carry lukewarm, rust-colored water from the weeping bases of the vents into wide, millimeter-deep pans where it evaporates at the exact rate it arrives. The puddles never shrink and never overflow. 

Nothing green grows here. There is no grass on the bluffs, no moss in the warm, wet runnels, no scrub clinging to the clay. Where biology exists in the place of plants, it looks entirely like industrial residue. Around the damp aprons of the louder vents, thick, rubbery mats of black and gunmetal-gray thermophilic bacteria cling to the rock, shimmering faintly like spilled grease in the normal, mid-morning light. In the lukewarm drainage channels, stiff, calcified crusts of orange microbes build tiny, stepped terraces half an inch high, brittle underfoot and smelling faintly of hot pennies and wet chalk.

Occasionally, across the wide, open distances between the vents, small mounds of pale gray slag sit undisturbed—though they are not true slag, only blistered, porous pumice pushed up ages ago and long since smoothed by the constant dampness. 

Life that moves is scarce, swallowed up by the sheer openness of the flats, and entirely adapted to the din and the mineral heat. If you watch the slick, dark apron around one of the hammering copper-capped vents for an hour, you might see one of the basin's few inhabitants: a flat, palm-sized arthropod with a segmented shell the dull gray of cast lead. It moves slowly, grazing on the grease-colored bacterial mats, pausing whenever the stone valve drops with its deafening *clang*. Its joints are sealed tight against the warm grit, and its heavy carapace vibrates visibly in sympathy with the ground's low hum. Farther out, where the mudstone dries to the consistency of warm leather, solitary, long-legged scavenger beetles—glossy black, looking freshly dipped in enamel—pick their way across the emptiness, covering fifty yards of bare ground before encountering another living thing. Overhead, a single pale-winged bird occasionally crosses from bluff to bluff, flying high to stay above the rising columns of heat shimmer, never landing on the floor below.

You can walk for two hours across the basin toward the far clay rim and nothing about the rules of the place shifts. The light remains clear and ordinary, glinting off wet patches of ironstone and shallow films of water. Behind you, the vents you passed fade into the general mechanical roar; ahead of you, distant plumes of white vapor puff three feet into the warm air, vanish instantly, and puff again on the exact same beat. There are no ruins, no tracks, no rusted pipes—only bare earth and raw stone performing the loud, heavy work of an engine room, turning over at idle forever under an open sky.
