It is no larger than a cellar, wedged thirty feet beneath a crust of dry, fissured hardpan, and there is scarcely an inch of empty air inside it. 

To exist in this pocket is to be pressed from all sides by raw, unweathered edges. The chamber is a tension gash—a sudden geological rip that tore open in the dark rock and then stopped, arresting itself before wind, water, or friction could soften a single angle. Every surface of the dark, glassy host-stone is a fresh conchoidal fracture, sharp enough to shave skin. Thrusting inward from every wall, floor, and ceiling is a suffocating congestion of mineral blades: thick, interlocking shafts of milky, self-luminous spar that crowd the interior like the teeth of a closed trap. There are no aisles, no clearings, no vistas. One prism interrupts another; a shelf of raw chert juts out an inch above a cluster of stone needles; pale, woody roots—stiff as iron wire—thread tightly through the remaining gaps.

The light is staggering. There is no sun here, no shaft to the surface, yet the chamber blazes with a shadowless, clinical brilliance. The mineral blades emit a relentless, cold actinic white from deep within their lattices. Because the light comes from everywhere—floor, ceiling, and every angled facet in between—darkness cannot pool in the crevices. Even the underside of a stone ledge is lit from below to the point of glare. Every serrated grain of rock, every hair on a stem, every bead of moisture is outlined with painful, hyper-focused clarity.

It is cool—the crisp, subterranean chill of deep stone—and perpetually clammy. There is no running water, no puddle at the bottom, and never the sound of a drip. Instead, the moisture exists in a state of suspended condensation. Millions of hemispherical beads of water cling to the slick faces of the luminous crystals and the dark fractures of the basalt. They never grow heavy enough to slip downward, and they never evaporate into the cool air. They sit at exact equilibrium, turning every flat surface into a sheet of tiny, bright lenses that magnify the rock beneath them.

Wedged into the tight polyhedral nooks between the crystals is a sparse, wiry flora: solitary tufts of translucent, bristle-leaved ferns and bloated, gray-green tubers anchored directly to bare stone by flat pads of dry tissue. They do not sway; there is no draft to move them. Around and upon them, packing the remaining cubic inches of the pocket, is the fauna. Thousands of pale, thumb-sized arthropods—broad-shielded, flattened creatures somewhere between a cicada and a trilobite, with shells as bright and chalky as the spar—cling shoulder-to-shoulder along the undersides of the crystal shafts. Between them sit motionless coils of blind, vitreous millipedes and clusters of conical, chalk-shelled snails cemented by dried mucus to the sharp rock edges.

From these crowded bodies comes a continuous, deafening roar. 

The broad-shielded insects vibrate their fused wing-cases without pause, not in chirps or pulses, not in rising and falling choruses, but in a single, unbroken, phase-locked mechanical drone. Confined within the tiny, hard-walled geometry of the chamber, the sound has nowhere to dissipate. It reflects off a thousand crystal facets and builds into a dense acoustic solid—a loud, metallic chord that fills the chest and buzzes in the teeth. It never wavers in pitch or volume. There is no rhythm here because rhythm requires a before and an after, and this room has settled into a locked, timeless stalemate.

Scattered throughout the crush of crystal and chitin are small, unmistakable arrangements made by hands, though no tool marks scar the stone. Where three crystal blades cross to form a shelf, someone—or something small enough to navigate the lattice—has stacked flat, razor-edged flakes of black shale into miniature dry-stone cairns, each no taller than a handspan. In other crevices, bundles of stiff root-fibers have been tied with reef knots around loose prisms, suspending them an inch above the floor like plumb bobs that never swing. Faint smears of yellow ochre, damp and bright under the mineral glare, mark specific crystal faces in sets of three parallel finger-drags. Near the lowest angle of the floor, a tiny, three-sided niche of propped slate slabs houses a neat pile of snail shells sorted strictly by size. No dust settles on these cairns. No lichen creeps over the ochre. Whatever built them is gone, and the chamber holds the work exactly as it was left, neither decaying it nor swallowing it.

Nothing in this bright, roaring box advances. The fractures do not widen; the sharp edges do not crumble into sand. The water beads neither dry nor fall. The pale ferns do not uncurl new fronds, nor do their outer bristles brown. Even the abundant animals seem to exist in a state of metabolic suspension—drawing trace nourishment directly from the damp, ionized air or the weeping mineral films beneath their feet, converting it straight into continuous sound without ever crawling an inch to hunt, mate, or flee. If you were to stare at a single square foot of this wall—at the three ochre stripes, the seven beaded water droplets beside them, the single stiff fern, the four vibrating pale carapaces jammed into the acute angle of two blinding white crystals—and come back a century later, not a leg would have shifted, not a drop would have rolled, and the loud, bright chord ringing off the raw stone would not have dropped by a fraction of a tone.
