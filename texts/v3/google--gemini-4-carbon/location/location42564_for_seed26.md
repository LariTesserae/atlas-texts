You do not step onto stone here, nor soil. Beneath your boots is cast grey composite, poured into hexagonal plates six meters across, their edges crisp and unchipped, separated by shallow expansion joints filled with dark, stiff sealant. The ground drops away in wide, engineered terraces—a subterranean basin miles across, roofed by an unseen ceiling buried somewhere above a thick ceiling of freezing mist. 

There is no bedrock exposed anywhere. Everything from the floor to the sweeping retaining walls in the distance is fabricated: brushed alloy, raw concrete still bearing the clean, parallel grain of its formwork, dark vulcanized rubber, and galvanized grating. It is a young structure. The bolt heads have not rusted; they hold only a bright, frosty bloom of oxidation around their sharp washers. Where heavy machinery has scraped the floor, the gouges show clean, pale aggregate, utterly devoid of grime or centuries of wear. Yet despite the precision of its making, the basin is vast and curiously empty, as if built to house an industry that only partially arrived, or one that requires immense, uninhabited buffer zones between its moving parts.

Cold rules the air—a sharp, biting chill hovering just above freezing that turns every exhalation into a lingering plume. Because the upper reaches of the vault carry massive, uninsulated thermal conduits, the space generates its own indoor weather. Warmth leaking from high, distant pipe-bridges meets the deep, refrigerated sink of the lower terraces, condensing into restless banks of fog. It does not rain, not quite, and water never gathers into deep rivers, but everything weeps. Moisture beads on the flanks of silent, house-sized transformers. It sheets down sloped concrete baffles in slow, shivering films, pooling in the shallow troughs of the floor plates before spilling through scupper grates. Every surface gleams darkly, slick with a millimeter of ice-cold condensation. 

Light comes reluctantly, filtered through the drifting vapor. High up on skeletal towers spaced half a mile apart, clusters of sodium-amber and cold mercury-vapor lamps burn continuously. Their beams do not illuminate the whole valley so much as carve out tall, hazy cones in the mist, leaving vast stretches of the terraced floor in a grainy, bruised twilight. Reflections stretch across the wet composite underfoot—long, smeared ribbons of orange and pale blue that shiver whenever a vibration rolls through the floor.

And the vibrations never entirely cease. The basin is loud, filled with an industrial acoustics that turns every mechanical act into a rolling weather front of sound. Two miles across the flats, a battery of intake turbines pulls air through baffled shafts, generating a low, chest-rattling thrum that serves as the landscape’s permanent baseline. Over this rises the sharper, rhythmic clatter of automated gantry cranes moving along elevated tracks in the murk—steel wheels shrieking briefly against wet rails, the heavy *clack-thud* of solenoids engaging, the sudden, deafening hiss of pneumatic bleed-valves venting white plumes of steam that vanish instantly into the cold air. Every ten or twelve minutes, subterranean pumps kick on beneath the floor grates to clear the runoff, sending a hollow, gurgling roar echoing off the unseen walls of the cavern.

Life has found its way into this raw, weeping geometry, though it remains scattered and fiercely specialized. There is no dirt for roots, so the vegetation clings to seams, vents, and chemical spills. Along the expansion joints of the hex-plates, where water sits stagnant and faintly rich with leached minerals from the fresh concrete, mats of brittle, black-green moss have taken hold, stiff with frost at their fringes. Closer to the heat-exchangers—massive, finned radiators the size of apartment blocks that hum in lonely isolation out on the flats—the warmth allows stranger growths. Pale, translucent stalks, somewhere between kelp and glasswort, sprout directly from the wet rubber gaskets and mineral-crusted bolts. They have no leaves, only bloated, segmented stems filled with antifreeze-thick sap, leaning uniformly toward the humming warmth of the metal fins and drinking the endless condensation that drips from the manifolds. Here and there, across miles of bare grey concrete, wide rusty stains bloom where iron-fixing bacteria have colonized a slow leak, painting fifty-yard streaks of wet ochre across the immaculate grey terraces.

Moving things of flesh and blood are rarer still, dwarfed by the scale of the masonry and the automated machinery that ignores them. You might walk for an hour along a raised catwalk—listening to the drip of water from your coat onto the grating below—and see nothing alive except the pale stalks shivering in an exhaust draft. Then, near a warm sump pit, something shifts. 

Long-legged, pale crustaceans, the size of dinner plates, haul themselves out of the drainage grates. Their shells are chalky and sharp-edged, coated in the same fine grey silt that washes off the fresh concrete. They move slowly in the cold, scraping mineral films and bacterial mats from the wet metal with specialized, shovel-like claws, freezing instantly whenever a gantry crane rumbles past overhead. Higher up, roosting in the warm, humming crooks of the electrical transformers, live small, heavy-bodied birds—or things very much like birds—with oily, slate-grey plumage that sheds the perpetual drip of the ceiling. They hunt the crustaceans and the pale, blind bristle-worms that wash up through the overflow vents, dropping silently out of the amber light-cones, claws clicking sharply against the wet composite before beating heavily back up into the scaffolding.

There is a strange, self-contained logic to the basin’s activity. No operators walk the catwalks; no voices call out over the din of the vents. Yet the landscape is unmistakably busy. Sluice gates open on automated timers, sending sudden, shallow sheets of grey water washing across three-hundred-yard sections of terrace, flushing away accumulated silt and scattering the pale crabs before draining instantly through grated trenches with a deafening, hollow suck. Great louver walls in the middle distance pivot all at once with a booming metallic clang, redirecting the freezing wind across the basin, tearing the fog banks into ragged ribbons and forcing the pale vent-plants to bend flat against the concrete. 

Standing on one of the middle tiers, looking out across the stepped, glistening expanse, the horizon does not curve upward or downward—it simply dissolves into dark, wet vapor punctuated by distant, glowing halos of amber light and the dim silhouettes of towering manifolds. The air smells sharply of wet cement, ozone, cold iron, and the faint, briny musk of the sump-moss. Everything around you is hard-edged, newly minted, weeping cold water, and grinding steadily onward through cycles of pressure and drainage designed for purposes entirely indifferent to anything watching from the mist.
