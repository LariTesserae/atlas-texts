At five thousand meters, the rock has not had time to soften. The plateau is a broad, tilted basin ten miles across, lifted out of the crust recently enough—in geological terms—that water and wind have not yet figured out where to put their channels. There are no riverbeds here, no rounded cobbles, no sand, and almost no soil. Instead, the ground is an expanse of dark, fractured greywacke and freshly exposed basalt, split along clean geometric planes. Every edge is crisp enough to cut leather. Where the stone steps downward, it does so in sharp, unweathered ledges, as if a quarry had been opened by tectonic heave alone and left untouched.

The sky does not sit above this basin; it rests directly on it. Thick, cold cloud banks push up from the invisible lowlands beyond the jagged rim and stall across the plateau, dimming the midday sun to a flat, pewter glare. Because the air is thin and hovers just above freezing, moisture condenses constantly on the cold stone. Every slab is slick and dark with sweat. Water gathers on the flat faces of the rock until surface tension fails, then spills over sharp brims into shallow, angular depressions below, forming thousands of clear, frigid pan-pools linked by trickles that have not yet carved grooves for themselves.

Nothing has been built here. There is no road climbing the passes, no concrete pad, no fence line, not even a stacked-stone cairn to mark a survey point. Yet across the wide, empty reach of the mist, three miles apart from one another, massive machines are at work.

They are autonomous core-rigs and brine-skimmers, each the size of a two-story warehouse, carried on eight-foot-tall compliant pneumatic rollers designed to spread fifty tons of steel over the sharp rock without crushing a single edge or leaving a rut. Above each rig, tethered by thick braided umbilical cables that vanish into the low cloud ceiling, rides a heavy-lift aerostat, taking half the machine’s weight off the ground and pulling wind-turbine power down from the brisk upper shear. Everything runs on high-voltage electric drive. In the thin, damp air—which carries sound poorly even on a clear day—the industry is startlingly quiet. From half a mile off, a rig produces only a low, steady transformer hum, the occasional soft hiss of a hydraulic leveling leg adjusting to an uneven shelf, and the muted, wet crunch of rubber bladders rolling over splintered slate. When a drill string bites into the virgin crust to pull a mineral sample, the vibration travels farther through the soles of the feet than through the air.

Between these slow-moving hulls, the emptiness of the basin is vast, uncluttered, and unexpectedly full of animals.

Vegetation is scarce, limited to what can grip raw stone or root in the thin paste of rock-flour collecting at the bottom of cracks: leathery black umbilicaria lichens, mats of lime-green moss swelling along the wet seams, and widely spaced cushions of stiff, needle-leaved alpine sedge no bigger than overturned bowls. Yet the sheer horizontal scale of the plateau turns this sparse scatter into enough pasture to feed thousands. Herds of small, barrel-chested camelids with dense, water-shedding coats of slate-brown wool drift through the fog in loose formations of two or three hundred. Their split, leathery hooves click softly on the wet basalt as they move from sedge-cushion to sedge-cushion, steaming faintly from the nostrils.

Because the machines move at less than two miles an hour and carry no human crews to shout or slam doors, the herds treat them like mobile cliffs. Dozens of animals bed down on the leeward side of a stationary rig while its drill is sunk, soaking up the waste heat radiating from its battery banks and liquid-cooled inverters. Flocks of plump, gray-bellied mountain plovers patter through the shallow sheet-water right beneath the belly pans, pecking midges and cold-water copepods stunned by the warmth of the condenser drips.

Higher up on the shattered talus slopes that ring the basin—piles of raw, freshly calved blocks that still settle with an occasional sharp *crack* as frost wedges them apart—fat, rabbit-sized rodents with long, curled tails whistle faintly to one another across the damp air. Every twenty minutes or so, the whistles cut off all at once as a dark shape slides out of the cloud layer: an enormous scavenging bird with a nine-foot wingspan, banking silently on the updrafts off the ridge, or a pair of low-slung, pale-furred cats working the fractures downwind of the camelid calves.

The whole plateau is in quiet, continuous motion. Clouds drag across the sharp sills, dumping fine, icy drizzle for ten minutes before thinning to a pearlescent brightness that turns every wet rock-face into a dull mirror, then closing in again. Water sheets from ledge to ledge, carrying microscopic grains of fresh feldspar toward the lowest corner of the basin—the earliest, faintest rehearsal for a lake and a river that will take another hundred thousand years to cut their beds. A rig retracts its drill string with a series of soft metallic clanks, vents a white plume of steam into the cold gray air, reels in fifty feet of aerostat tether, and rolls onward at a walking pace. Behind it, the herds rise, shake the condensed mist from their wool, and follow the lingering warmth across unmarked stone.
