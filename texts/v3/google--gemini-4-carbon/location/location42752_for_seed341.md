The district sits at the bottom of a broad, sunken basin no wider than a cathedral close—perhaps two hundred paces from one weeping retaining wall to the next—bounded on all four sides by terraced masonry embankments that vanish upward into a low ceiling of bruised, unmoving vapor. There is no sky here, only a permanent, submarine gloom. Above the rims of the basin, great louvered iron baffles hang suspended from rusted trusses, admitting a meager, pewtered twilight that barely reaches the water below before being swallowed by steam.

The floor of the basin is entirely paved in chamfered vitrified brick, drowned beneath knee-deep water as dark and clear as brewed tea. Because the space is laid out on a wide, civic grid with broad avenues of open water between structures, it feels curiously empty despite its machinery—a sparse, flooded plaza where a dozen tall, slender buildings stand widely apart like chess pieces left at the end of a game. Each is a narrow tower of glazed tile, cast iron, and riveted boiler-plate: switch-houses, pressure-exchangers, and vertical turbine housings rising four or five stories out of the shallows. Between them stretch long, empty perspectives of rippling black water, broken only by lines of cast-iron bollards and submerged curbstones that mark drowned thoroughfares.

The heat is immediate and heavy, pressing against the skin like wet wool. The water itself is bath-warm, heated from below by buried return-mains that pulse subtly underfoot, sending lazy, shimmering plumes of convection to the surface. Everywhere, moisture is in violent, continuous motion. Every forty seconds or so, a massive brass relief-stack on the western side of the basin unseats its counterweight with aconcussive, metallic *clack*, followed by a deafening roar of white steam that jets horizontally across the open water. When that hot plume collides with the slightly cooler air lingering beneath the upper baffles, it condenses almost instantly, dropping a sudden, localized squall of hot rain onto the middle of the square. Three paces outside the downpour, the air is merely mist; inside it, water hammers the surface into white froth.

Nothing here is quiet. Beneath the intermittent thunder of the relief vents runs the relentless, chest-vibrating thrum of submerged rotary pumps, the heavy *chug-thump* of walking-beam engines rocking behind tall, unglazed lancet windows, and the continuous, cascading hiss of water spilling down the stepped masonry walls. Every brick face weeps. Every iron girder overhead drips in rhythmic, fat globules that strike the flooded street with hollow, ringing notes.

Yet within all this wet, tropical warmth, nothing crawls, flies, or swims. There is no whine of midges in the mist, no dart of minnows over the sunken bricks, no frogs calling from the culvert mouths. The water carries a faint, sharp tang of ozone and dissolved copper. Thick, braided busbars run bare along the flanks of the switch-houses, held out from the wet stone on clusters of glazed porcelain bells that crackle softly with pale violet corona discharge whenever the rain intensifies. A low, stray current bleeds continuously through the shallow flood—not enough to boil the water, enough to make it entirely inhospitable to nerve and muscle, turning the entire basin into a warm, weakly galvanic bath.

What thrives instead is the flora, which has adapted not merely to tolerate the current and the engine-heat, but to feed on them. Because the open water between buildings is swept clean by pump currents, the greenery does not choke the basin into a thicket; instead, it gathers in immense, lush, widely separated colonies anchored directly to the architecture. Thick curtains of dark, glistening liverwort and metallic-green moss drape the warm brickwork around exhaust flues in pelts half a meter deep. From the submerged bases of the transformer towers rise towering, solitary stands of giant philodendron and black-stemmed elephant ear, their leaves broad as tabletops, waxy and rigid, veined in startling copper-red. High overhead, heavy, fleshy vines bridge the wide gaps between towers by wrapping themselves around insulated feeder cables; they hang down in twenty-foot emerald chandeliers that sway in the blast of the steam vents.

These plants move at a pace slightly too brisk to seem entirely botanical. When the underground turbines shift load and the electrical hum drops half an octave, the great submerged root-mats shudder in unison, releasing clouds of tiny silver oxygen bubbles that fizz briefly to the surface. Broad, rubbery spathes growing along the iron footbridges slowly curl inward ahead of each scheduled steam-blow, shedding sheets of accumulated condensate, then unfurl again into the warm drizzle seconds after the valve reseats, drinking heat and stray voltage alike.

Nothing in the basin looks newly built, and nothing looks ruined. The place has been running unattended for so long that wear and function have reached a settled truce. Where water pours constantly from cantilevered iron scuppers, the brick paving below has been hollowed into smooth, polished bowls; where acidic mist licks the turbine housings, the cast iron has developed a dense, stable rind of plum-colored oxide that protects the metal beneath. Vines have thickened into woody cables around valve wheels that never need turning, while giving wide, scorched-looking berth to the rocker arms and flywheels that slam back and forth day and night.

Dim, amber carbon-arc lamps hang in wire cages under the eaves of the switch-houses, burning steadily through the gloom. Their light catches the oiled brass of spinning governors visible through tall doorways, glints off the wet, rubbery crowns of the giant arums, and lays long, trembling ribbons of gold across the wide expanses of black water—a small, deafening, self-weathering room of brick and foliage, pumping and raining on itself without pause.
