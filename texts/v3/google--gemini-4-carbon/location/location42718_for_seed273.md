There is no bedrock here, though if you dig your fingers under the roots of a strangler fig you will hit something just as hard: six inches of compacted fly ash, bird lime, and black leaf-mold, and beneath that, the pebbled, rust-stabilized skin of a cast-iron deck plate. 

The entire basin—four miles across from the western wind-baffles to the eastern condenser towers—is fabricated. It sits at an altitude where the sky goes a hard, cobalt violet at noon and the air is thin enough to make a match burn short and pale. Below the perimeter railings, there is no mountainside, only the dizzying, plumb-line drop of structural piers, cross-braced in riveted steel and vitrified red brick, descending thousands of feet until they vanish into the blinding white wool of a permanent cloud deck. Nothing natural holds this place up, except the geometry of trusses that continue downward out of sight.

By rights, at this height, everything should be locked in ice. Instead, the air is heavy and bathwater-warm. Deep within the lower tiers, massive, low-pressure boilers and rotary kilns—vast brick-lined bellies running on some patient, self-feeding siphon from far below—turn over at a glacial pace. You can feel their pulse through the soles of your boots: a slow, oceanic *thud-hiss* that takes forty seconds to complete a stroke. Heat rises through miles of copper busbars, brick flues, and gridded catwalks. Every surface is warm to the touch. Iron handrails feel like sun-baked stone even at dawn; great glazed-tile chimneys radiate a steady, bread-oven warmth into the thin air.

Because the air is thin and the sun is ferocious, moisture behaves strangely. Rain almost never falls from above—the sky overhead is starkly, relentlessly clear. Water comes sideways and from underneath. Twice or three times a day, swells in the cloud sea below crest over the western parapet, pushing thick, luminous ribbons of fog through the latticework of the gantry cranes and suspension cables. At the same time, thousands of relief valves, condenser coils, and sweating brass manifolds exhale plumes of steam. Where the hot breath of the machinery meets the cool, high-altitude drafts, droplets bead and run. Water trickles down the channels of I-beams, pools in the dished tops of flywheel housings, and drips steadily from the bellies of overhead pipes. Ten minutes after a cloud bank passes, the upper steelwork is bone-dry and glaring in the sun again, while three tiers down, in the dense shade under the viaducts, everything remains slick with warm condensation.

Over decades—or centuries—life has packed every cubic yard of this scaffolding to capacity. 

With no mineral soil, the forest has made its own dirt out of decomposed foliage, rust flakes, soot, and dead insects, wedging it into coal hoppers, rain gutters, and the webbed pockets between girders. Broad-leaved canopy trees with pale, sun-toughened bark grow straight out of the throats of cold smokestacks, their roots braided around ladder rungs for fifty feet down to anchor themselves in the ash beds at the bottom. Thick curtains of woody lianas hang from traveling cranes, swaying almost imperceptibly as counterweights shift an inch an hour along oiled tracks. Ferns with fronds as wide as sheet metal crowd the drip-lines beneath leaking flange joints. Mosses and tough, waxy bromeliads upholster the shady sides of transformer housings, drinking the mist that rises off cooling troughs. Everything grows close together, layered vertically across six or seven overlapping levels of decking, catwalks, and broken glass conservatory roofs, competing fiercely for the drenched patches around leaky gaskets while enduring the harsh ultraviolet glare at the tips of their branches.

The sound of the place never drops below a roar, though it has no urgency in it. The base note is mechanical: the low, cavernous groan of thermal expansion in millions of tons of metal, the heavy rumble of geared turbines turning at three revolutions per minute, the electrical sixty-cycle hum of porcelain-insulated lines strung from tower to tower, and the long, wet sigh of pressure regulators venting into exhaust stacks. Layered directly on top of that—using the machinery’s frequencies as cover and counterpoint—is a deafening, thriving clamor of animals.

Flocks of heavy-chested, green-and-copper birds with hooked beaks roost along warm steam mains by the thousands, shrieking sharply enough to cut through the hiss of valves. Broad-shouldered, reddish-furred primates move in troops along the sagging power cables, their calloused hands immune to the hot metal, shaking down showers of rust and seedpods as they cross the open gulfs between boiler houses. Lizards the length of a forearm flatten themselves on brass pressure gauges to soak up both the radiant heat and the fierce noon sun, snapping at clouds of fat, metallic-winged beetles that breed in the warm sludge of oil traps. In the damp, echoing vaults beneath the main turbine floors, millions of frogs chime in resonance with the steel bulkheads, turning old riveted water tanks into giant acoustic amplifiers.

Nothing here looks newly broken, and nothing looks newly made. The industrial fabric has weathered into the settled permanence of geology. Sharp steel edges have softened under thick, plum-colored patinas of stable oxide that flake away only a millimeter a decade. Brick retaining walls bulge slightly under the slow hydraulic wedge of roots, yet hold fast because the vines themselves have lignified into living tension cables, binding masonry to iron. Glass skylights, shattered generations ago, have had their jagged panes tumbled smooth by windblown grit and cemented into scalloped mosaics along the drainage channels by lime scale and hardened grease.

Change moves at the speed of a counterweight descending on a worm-gear. Over the course of a month, a giant rotary distributor valve at the center of the valley clicks over by a single tooth. When it does, a mile-long line of overhead pipes cools down on the north terrace and warms up on the south. Slowly, without panic, the landscape leans to follow it: the lizards migrate across the trestle bridges, the mosses on the north side curl into crisp brown dormancy, dormant spores on the south side swell and green in the fresh drip of the awakened gaskets, and the whole high, bright, thunderous bowl settles into the next long breath of its engine.
