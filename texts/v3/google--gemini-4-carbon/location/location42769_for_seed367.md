High on a wind-planed shield plateau where the continent’s oldest granite has been rubbed down nearly to the curvature of the earth, there is a sink no wider than a threshing floor. You could walk twenty paces past its lip on the bleached, featureless flat above and never know the ground had opened, except for the sound rising out of the rock—a continuous, dry, thrumming roar like grain pouring into a bin—and the sudden breath of warm, musky air rising against your shins.

The opening is a kidney-shaped throat worn smooth as tallow. Nothing here has a sharp edge. The stone belongs to an age so remote that its crystals have forgotten how to fracture cleanly; every ledge, overhang, and buttress inside the cavity droops in bulbous, polished curves, hollowed out eons ago by vanished chemistry and finished by ten million years of dry highland dust spinning in the thermals. Below the narrow collar of the rim, the cavity bells outward into a single dim vault perhaps ninety feet across and seventy deep, shielded from the sky’s glare by its own overhanging brow. Direct sunlight touches the upper east wall for forty minutes at mid-morning and then vanishes, leaving the interior in a perpetual, dusty, amber twilight.

Into this single stone vessel an entire town has been wedged, stacked, slung, and mortared, alongside a jungle that has never seen a puddle.

There is no free water here—no spring, no seep, no cistern, not even dampness in the mortar. Moisture exists strictly behind living skins. Deep below the floor of the hollow, blind taproots as thick as a man’s thigh—belonging to ancient, swollen-trunked strangler figs and bottle-trees—thread miles of microscopic fissures in the bedrock to sip fossil dampness locked in the mountain’s core. Everything else drinks those trees, or drinks the thin pre-dawn highland air, or drinks each other. Lushness here is fat, waxy, and crowded. Every smooth granite bulge not claimed by masonry is smothered in curtains of silver-grey air plants, tangled mats of climbing aloes, and broad, leathery vines with leaves thick as saddle leather. Caudiciform shrubs with bellies like boulders wedge themselves into wall-joints, extending dense canopies of jade-green paddles into the dim air to trap the exhalations of the crowd.

And the crowd is everywhere. Architecture and ecology are braided so tightly that it is hard to tell where building stops and nest begins. 

The settlement rises in seven or eight irregular tiers around the curving belly of the rock, built without plumb lines or metal. Because water cannot be spared to mix mudbrick, the walls are dry-laid from flat, wind-polished cobbles gathered from the plateau above, chinked with bone splinters, dry dung, and a hard, pale cement beaten out of roasted limestone dust and prickly-pear mucilage. Spanning the central gulf of the pit—a void barely forty feet wide—is a dense three-dimensional rigging of catwalks, rope ladders, storage hammocks, and suspended landings woven from twisted agave fiber and ribbed with the woody skeletons of dead cacti. Houses are the size of cupboards, stacked cheek-by-jowl like barnacles, their doorways worn oval by generations of shoulders brushing through. Smooth troughs are dished deep into the stone thresholds where bare heels have pivoted for centuries.

Yet humans are only a fraction of the census. The pocket is boiling with animal life.

Every unplastered patch of overhang is crusted with the mud-and-spittle cups of cliff swifts—thousands of them, packed rim to rim like honeycomb. Beneath the wooden eaves of the human dwellings hang dense, shivering clusters of small amber-furred bats, packed fifty to the square foot. The air in the center of the shaft is never empty; it is a permanent rotary engine of wings. Swifts drop in from the bright slot above like flung stones, banking hard around laundry lines and wicker granaries with dry, whistling shrieks, snapping up the clouds of gnats, moths, and dusty-winged lacewings that rise continuously from the warm floor.

Look closer at any wall and the stone itself seems to shift. Fat, velvet-skinned geckos the color of dry lichen cling in dozens around every oil-lamp niche and doorway, bobbing their heads, lunging at bark-scorpions and bloated silverfish. Plump, tailless rock-conies—half-wild, half-livestock—scurry along the woven balustrades and sleep in heaps among the storage gourds, chewing dropped rinds and muttering in low,chuckling whistles. Down at the very bottom of the sink, where the light scarcely reaches at all, the floor is no longer rock but a deep, warm, springy mattress of dry guano, shed feathers, seed husks, and leaf litter, churned incessantly by millions of shining black tenebrionid beetles. The floor literally whispers and shifts underfoot as the beetles reduce every scrap of falling waste to fine, odorless dry humus within hours, feeding the knotted root-mats of the giant figs, while plump ground-lizards and banded centipedes hunt the beetles through the gloom.

The warmth in the pocket is entirely self-kept. Up on the plateau, the high altitude bites cold as soon as the sun drops, and the wind scours the rock raw. Down inside the stone bottle, the combined body heat of two hundred people, ten thousand birds and bats, and a million cold-blooded things—insulated by the stone roof and fed by the slow, dry composting of the beetle-floor—holds the air at a constant, sleepy warmth, smelling sharply of crushed sage, dry feathers, woodsmoke, toasted grain, and warm stone.

Sound has nowhere to go; it bounces off the curved, soapy granite until the whole chamber hums like the inside of a drum. Above the steady white noise of wings and clicking beetle-shells rise the rhythms of manual work: the *clack-clack* of bone shuttles on backstrap looms slung across narrow balconies; the rhythmic grit of stoneullers grinding parched seeds in granite hollows; neighbors speaking in low, rapid voices across a ten-foot drop between their front doors while peeling succulent stalks with chipped flint blades. Children scramble sideways along smooth ledges like crabs, raiding swift-nests with hooked sticks while gray-headed kites—three pairs that own the highest, brightest ledges near the throat—watch the commotion below and drop suddenly through the rigging to snatch a careless coney from a roof-beam.

Twice a day, the entire pocket changes gear at once. At dusk, when the slot of sky above turns deep violet, the swifts pour *in* from the high plains in a screaming funnel while the bats drop *out* from the eaves and spiral upward in a black ribbon—two rivers of bodies passing within inches of each other in the narrow stone throat, buffeting the hanging vine-curtains with wind, raising swirls of fine golden dust through the lantern-lit tiers of the little city, before the night shift settles in and the nocturnal frogs—tiny things that breed inside the damp leaf-bases of the giant bromeliads without ever touching open ground—begin to chirp like struck flints in the dark.
