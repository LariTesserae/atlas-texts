There is no surface worth speaking of—only an unreachable ceiling far overhead where the black water churns against thin, freezing mountain air and low, racing clouds, bruising the upper gloom a faint, slate-colored violet before the light dies entirely on its way down. Down here, across the drowned expanse of the high valley, darkness is the permanent condition. Yet the water is astonishingly clear. Because nothing swims in it, nothing sheds scales or shells, and no microscopic swarm clouds the currents, there is no marine snow—only pure, frigid liquid pressing heavy and clean against ancient stone.

The bones of the place are unmistakably upland. Broad glacial cirques, stepped granite ridges, and long, sweeping talus slopes stretch out for miles, their once-jagged edges worn down by ages of submerged flow into sleek, settled contours. Heavy currents pour down from the drowned passes above, channeled by smooth bedrock troughs, sweeping the open valley floor clean of silt. For long stretches there is nothing at all between the ridges: half-miles of bare, dark gneiss and polished slate, vast bowls of cold emptiness where the water moves in massive, invisible rivers. 

Because the water carries sound without losing it, the valley is loud. You hear a ridge before you ever come near it. Deep, percussive booms roll effortlessly across the open dark—the sound of pressurized gas-bladders rupturing or snapping shut—layered beneath a constant, woody clatter and a low, resonant thrumming that vibrates right through the soles of the feet.

The noise comes from the groves. Though much of the valley lies bare, wherever a fault line seeps mineral-rich artesian water or a sheltered basin breaks the worst of the scour, vegetation explodes upward in towering, crowded islands of growth. There are no fragile weeds here; the cold and the current demand heavy architecture. Great pale trunks of rubbery, lignified kelp—thick as oak trees and ribbed like vaulted pillars—anchor themselves to the stone with gnarled, gripping holdfasts that pry into the granite seams. From their crowns, thirty or forty feet up in the dark, dense canopies of broad, leathery fronds thrash and coil in the rushing water. Beneath them, in the calm lee created by their trunks, a lush undergrowth crowds every inch of rock: waist-high thickets of silver-black ferns, bulbous, melon-sized spore sacs tethered on coiled tendrils, and sprawling mats of coarse crimson moss that drink minerals straight from the stone.

In the complete absence of animal life, the plants have claimed motion and violence for themselves. Nothing eats them, so they wage slow, hydraulic wars of territory and dispersal against one another. Hollow, calcified stalks grow in dense organ-pipe clusters along the edges of the groves, knocking together in the eddies like heavy stone chimes. Every few minutes, somewhere in the dark, a mature seed-pod detaches from a canopy and fires a jet of compressed water to propel itself cross-current, shooting out into the barren expanses with a sharp, muffled *thump* and a fleeting trail of cold blue luminescence. When its pale, glowing sap brushes rock, it clings, burning faintly in the pitch dark for hours while its first root-hairs hammer themselves into microscopic cracks in the stone.

Stranger still is how the water stays here at all. At the outer rims of the basin, where a normal mountain valley would drain away and leave dry peaks behind, the currents do not thin out—they accelerate toward unseen spillways while immense, freezing upwellings push continuously from deep artesian throats in the mountain’s core. The entire highland is drowned from within, crowned in a permanent, rushing dome of black water that pours endlessly off the drop-offs into whatever clouds and chasms lie below. Upwellings erupt from circular vents in the valley floor, cold as ice-melt, sending plumes of smooth pebbles dancing and clinking in perpetual suspension—a harsh, rhythmic rattle that echoes off the submerged cliffs.

Scattered across the sparse distances between the groves sit the works of hands—rare, small, and very old, though the near-freezing, sterile water prevents anything from rotting. Nothing decays by worm or beetle here; things only wear down by friction. On the shoulder of a rounded granite saddle stands a low, windowless shelter built of heavy slate slabs, corbelled inward like a beehive, each stone chipped into fit with a hand-maul. There is no hearth inside—fire was never possible here, or if this place was ever dry, the builders knew nothing of ash—only a bench of fitted stone and a cache of dark flint blades lashed with braided plant-sinew to handles of iron-hard, cured kelp-horn.

Elsewhere, the interventions are built directly into the currents. Across a narrow, roaring chute between two crags, thick cables woven from black fibrous stalks stretch taut from one rock wall to the other, anchored into hand-drilled holes with wedges of cold-hammered native copper. As the deep water surges past them, the taut ropes sing—a low, continuous, bass humming that rises and falls in pitch as the flow shifts, turning the whole gorge into a single, monotonous instrument. Hung along the cables are basket-like weirs woven of tough, woody withes, battered and half-unraveled, still catching the occasional drifting, luminescent spore-bladder torn loose from an upstream forest. 

Down on the wide, empty flats, lines of notched boulders mark paths between one lush grove and the next, spaced ten paces apart across the blind dark so something moving along the bottom could follow them by touch or by the faint, whistling turbulence each upright stone cuts into the current. Near the edge of a towering thicket, old harvest marks still show on the pale, pillar-like trunks: clean, concoidal scars where heavy stone adzes once hacked away outer bark, now healed over into smooth, knotted burls. Nobody tends them now. The groves simply swell, boom, and fire their glowing seeds out into the dark, while the cold water pours endlessly up from the heart of the mountain and rushes away over the unseen edges of the world.
