The air hits the back of the throat like hot iron dust dissolved in oil. It is a sunken chamber, no wider than a municipal waterworks and roughly twice as high as a tall oak, carved ages ago out of dark, crumbly shale that has since slumped into soft, rounded shoulders. There are no sharp edges left anywhere in the rock. Centuries of baked, sweating atmosphere have exfoliated the walls into smooth, powdery mounds, leaving deep talus slopes of warm black grit piled around the perimeter. 

Down here, the heat does not come from a sun; it radiates steadily upward through the floor and leaks from the fat, asbestos-lagged pipes that snake along the slumped ledges. It is the relentless, blistered warmth of an engine room that was sealed off and forgotten while still running at half-throttle. 

Light arrives only marginally. High up, where the shale vault pinches together toward an unseen surface, a narrow, crooked fissure admits a weak, soot-filtered greyness—just enough to outline the upper tangles of pipework before dying out halfway down. Lower in the basin, illumination comes from the remnants of an electrical grid: heavy, cast-iron junction boxes bolted directly into the bedrock, connected by sagging loops of leaded cable. Every twenty or thirty paces, a thick glass marine cage houses a carbon-filament bulb. Most are shattered or dark, packed solid with dry rot, yet three or four still burn at a starved, brownish-orange voltage. They cast deep, overlapping shadows through the thick of the flora, painting the undersides of leaves in dull copper.

There is no running water here, no pools or dripping stalactites—it is far too hot for water to sit openly on the stone without flashing to vapor. Yet the air holds a stubborn, pressurized dampness, exhaled intermittently from the machinery itself. Scattered through the dark tangle are squat, riveted pressure vessels and rhythmic, cast-iron condensers the size of slaughterhouse boilers. They are crusted in thick, velvety rinds of orange rust and white mineral scale, eroded until their manufacturer plates are illegible smears of brass. And they are still working, after their fashion. 

The room is loud. A heavy, sub-audible thrumming lives in the soles of the feet, transmitted through the packed shale floor from deep rotary pumps turning somewhere beneath the rock. Over this bass note sits a constant, deafening mechanical respiration. Every few minutes, a brass relief valve buried in the brush lifts on its worn seat and vents a massive, wet shriek of steam. The sound echoes flatly against the dense foliage and soft rock, filling the vault with a tearing hiss that drowns out thought, before choking off into a slow, metallic *clack-clack-clack* as the pressure drops and the counterweight settles.

It is this exhausted steam that feeds the green—though little of it is actually green. Because there is almost no light from above and no groundwater below, the vegetation has adapted entirely to the hot, vaporous breath of the ironwork. It is a crowded, claustrophobic thicket of thermosynthetic and heat-drinking plants, growing so densely around the machinery that metal and stem have merged into single, hulking silhouettes. 

Great, rubbery-leaved shrubs with skins like cured hide cluster tightly around the steam vents, their broad, burgundy blades angled precisely to catch the scalding mist and channel the condensed droplets down grooved petioles directly into insulated root-balls. There is no soil in the conventional sense, only dry shale dust, so the plants anchor themselves to one another and to the rusted flanks of the boilers. Thick, woody lianas, pale grey and hot to the touch, wrap the high-pressure conduits in tight, strangling coils, siphoning thermal energy straight through their bark. Where a pipe joint weeps oily condensation, pale, translucent bladder-ferns swell in dense, layered mats, jostling for space, pressing against each other until they squeak gently when the floor vibrates. 

Between the boiler-groves stand taller, stranger forms: rigid, fluted columns resembling spineless cacti made of dark wax, topped with wide, pleated collars of copper-colored flesh. When a nearby valve blows its deafening cloud of damp heat, these collars slowly, imperceptibly flare open over the course of ten minutes, glistening with captured moisture, then spend the next hour tightening back into fists to keep the blistering air from stealing the water back. 

Nothing crawls on these leaves. No insects circle the dim orange bulbs; no beetles bore into the grey vines; no rats nest in the warm shredded lagging beneath the pipes. The ecology is entirely silent on its own, completely devoid of tooth, wing, or foot. Leaves hang unchewed, pristine in their tough, leathery geometry until they grow too heavy with mineral deposits, tear slowly from their stems under their own weight, and slide down through the crowded understory. Below the living canopy lies a knee-deep mat of parched, undecayed husks—for even most fungi find the intervals between steam-bursts too searingly dry—that crumbles into black powder when disturbed.

Change happens here at the pace of metal fatigue and root-creep. Over decades, a massive iron flywheel—half-sunk in a pit near the center of the chamber, motionless now, its belt long rotted away—is being lifted millimeter by millimeter out of its bearings by a braided trunk of slick, ochre wood. Across the chamber, a catwalk of perforated steel grating hangs at a drunken thirty-degree slant, one of its stanchions dissolved entirely into rust-flake, held aloft only by the dense hammock of stiff, thorny air-roots that grew through its holes a hundred years ago. 

The rules of this sunken room are dictated entirely by the slow decline of whatever deep engine feeds the pipes. Where a conduit finally splits its seam and dies, the thicket wrapped around it turns brittle, bleaches to bone-white in the gloom, and stands forever like calcified coral in the hot draft. Where a fresh pinhole rusts through an elbow joint fifty feet away, a steady hiss of new vapor begins, and within months, pale, blind tendrils come feeling their way across the warm shale grit, crowding toward the roar and the dampness, layering leaf over thick, sweating leaf in the copper-tinted dark.
