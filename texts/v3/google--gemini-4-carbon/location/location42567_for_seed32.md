There is no bedrock here, and no open sky, though the light is blinding. The entire world is the size of a butcher’s larder, sunken into a dry, low-lying basin of the earth, and every cubic inch of its boundaries has been laid by hand. 

It is a dry-stone cistern that never held water, or perhaps a subterranean granary whose purpose was forgotten ten thousand years ago, built so absurdly thick that the walls have become the geology. There is no mortar. Instead, millions of flat, pale-grey limestone slabs and knuckle-sized cherts are wedged together in ferocious compression. The space left over—the actual habitable pocket—is scarcely twelve feet across and nine feet high, and even that small volume is nearly choked with dry-built piers, buttresses, cantilevers, and low, overlapping corbelled arches that step inward until they nearly kiss at the center. To move from one side to the other requires turning sideways to slip between dry-stacked pillars, one’s chest scraping one wall while shoulder blades catch on the next. Every surface is made. Beneath the soles of the feet is not dirt, nor quarry-floor, but the tight, upright edges of flagstones packed vertically like books on a tight shelf, extending downward to an unknowable depth.

Light pours in from a narrow, continuous collar-gap running just beneath the flat capstone ceiling, where the basin rim meets the surface of the salt-pan outside. Because the stone is bleached nearly to the color of chalk and polished by millennia of dry abrasion, that raw, high-albedo glare bounces endlessly through the tight masonry. There are almost no shadows—only slight shifts from stark bone-white to glaring silver-grey. Squinting does little good; the brightness comes up from the floor-slabs, radiates sideways off the pillars, and reflects from the low, stepped ceiling, filling every crevice with a crisp, cold, sterile clarity.

The air is sharp and distinctly cool, carrying the perpetual chill of deep stone shaded from direct heat yet scoured by relentless ventilation. And that ventilation is why the room roars.

Set into the thick outer walls, hidden behind staggered baffles of stacked slate, are dozens of narrow, slitted flues angled into the prevailing, unchanging wind of the low exterior plain. Outside, a steady, dry gale blows across the salt flats year after year without pause or season; inside this dense stone instrument, that wind is sliced, compressed, and forced through thousands of chinks, rubble-voids, and deliberately gapped courses. The sound is immense, physical, and completely invariant. It does not gust or sigh. It holds a continuous, deafening chord—part blast-furnace bellow, part high, crystalline whistle, like an organ pipe wedged open for eternity. Speak at the top of your lungs, and your voice vanishes two inches from your lips, swallowed by the steady, rushing howl of air threading ten thousand stone needles.

Over untold centuries, this ceaseless internal gale, carrying microscopic grains of blown gypsum and alkali dust, has worn the violent edges off the hand-split masonry. The architecture has settled into its final, immutable posture. Stones that once had sharp quarry-fractures are now rounded like dry soap; niches have been scooped into smooth, parabolic pockets; the vertical piers have subtly bellied and grooved along the invisible tracks of the drafts. The dust never piles up—the air moves too fiercely and consistently through the packed geometry to allow drifts—so every crack and joint remains swept clean down to the pale, abraded stone. Nothing settles. Nothing shifts. The masonry locked itself under its own colossal weight eons ago, ground its own joints smooth, and reached utter mechanical peace.

Nothing crawls here. There isn't a spider thread in the crevices, no beetle husks wedged between the floor-stones, no droppings, no burrows. The roaring dryness would desiccate soft tissue in hours. 

Yet the masonry is sparsely stitched with plant life—a tough, weird, woody flora adapted entirely to cold wind, blinding glare, and zero liquid water. Wedged impossibly tight into the mortarless seams between the limestone courses are flattened rosettes of grey-green, calcified scrub, their leaves hard and rough as sharkskin. Elsewhere, emerging from the packed rubble of a central pier, wire-thin stems the color of tarnished pewter press flat against the stone, hugging the leeward contours carved out by the wind. They do not sway; they are as rigid as iron wire, braced across the gaps from one pillar to the next like structural ties. These plants drink only the faint, invisible traces of vapor stripped from the howling air at night—though the light here never seems to dim, held in a perpetual afternoon—binding atmospheric dust directly into mineral rinds along their bark. A dry, faintly resinous smell, like crushed juniper needles mixed with struck flint, hangs permanently in the cold blast, torn away from their scaly leaves and instantly replaced.

Standing wedged between two stout, smooth-worn stone ribs, pressed on all sides by pale rock that vibrates faintly at the frequency of the wind, there is no sense of ruin. Ruins imply decay, a slide toward soil and collapse. This tight, bright, roaring box has nowhere left to fall and nothing left to lose. Every block presses firmly against five others; every branch of pewter-colored brush occupies the exact millimeter of wind-shadow permitted to it; the bright light strikes the exact same polished bevels of chert without shifting an inch; the loud, hollow chord of the walls holds its note without wavering. It is wholly artificial, completely wild, packed to capacity with its own pale matter, and utterly finished.
