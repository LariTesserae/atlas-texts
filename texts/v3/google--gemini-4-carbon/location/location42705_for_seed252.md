The chamber is no larger than a cistern—twenty paces along its main fracture, perhaps four across at the widest belly, pinching shut at both ends into razor-thin seams of solid dark trachyte. Far overhead, through hundreds of vertical yards of unbroken stone, there is a surface, though nothing of it reaches down here except water. 

Yet the room is blindingly bright.

There are no lamps, no cables, no cut surfaces or anchored housings anywhere in the rock. The stone was split open recently—a minor tectonic adjustment, old enough for the dust to have settled and washed away, young enough that every ledge and fallen flake retains a fresh, flinty sharpness that will slice a palm cleanly if leaned upon. Through these hairline fractures seeps groundwater heavy with engineered photonic colloids: sub-micron diamondoid lattices seeded into the crust at a planetary scale to bleed off deep geothermal stress. Squeezed through the rock matrix at high temperature, the particles undergo continuous phononic-to-optical conversion, shedding excess heat directly as fierce, full-spectrum white light. 

It makes the puddles look like spilled arc-welders. Every bead of condensation clinging to the jagged overhang acts as a tiny, pitiless lens. Light radiates upward from the wet floor, bounces off the slick planes of freshly cleaved feldspar, and fills the pocket from corner to corner with a shadowless, noon-white glare. Squinting does little good; the brilliance comes as much from the film of moisture under your feet as from the weeping ceiling three yards above.

Despite the heat-shedding chemistry of the water, the air remains fiercely hot—Well past blood temperature, thick and wet enough to feel like a warm cloth pressed over the mouth. The space runs its own brisk, silent weather cycle. Along the lowest crease of the floor, where the fracture dips closest to a magmatic dike below, thin ribbons of superheated water hiss out of the seam. They do not boil violently; they simply steam, sending wavering, invisible plumes of vapor rising straight up through the glare. Halfway up the walls, where the surrounding rock mass is a few degrees cooler, the vapor condenses instantly against the angular dark stone. The walls sweat in sheets. Fat, brilliant drops gather along the sharp lower lips of cantilevered rock shelves, tremble for a second, and let go, pattering back down into the shallow, flat-bottomed pools below to begin the loop again.

Because the rock broke open so recently, the chamber is mostly bare. There is an austerity to the surfaces—wide expanses of wet, charcoal-gray stone interrupted only by their own geometric step-fractures and crisp, unweathered rubble. Life has barely begun to find the cracks. Here and there, spaced yards apart on the steepest, wettest faces, are thin, translucent smears of thermophilic biofilm, pale yellow or chalk-white, no wider than a thumbprint. Near the warmest vents, a few sparse clusters of tiny, pinhead-sized fruiting bodies—something between a silica-fixing fungus and an extremophile lichen—cling to the sharp edges of submerged stones, feeding on the dissolved minerals and bathing in the relentless artificial daylight of the water. Between these small footholds stretches nothing but clean, wet rock and glaring emptiness.

Sound stays small here. The heavy, saturated air deadens echoes, and the surrounding mass of unbroken stone swallows low frequencies entirely. Mostly, there is just the quiet, syncopated percussion of water: a crisp *plip* into a calf-deep basin, the rapid, whispery *tck-tck-tck* of runoff spilling down a stair-stepped cleavage plane, and every few minutes, a faint, glassy *ping* deep within the walls as the young fracture settles another fraction of a millimeter against the heat.

Only a few small things move through the brightness. In the widest pool—a triangular basin of water so clear and bright its bottom seems dry—two pale, eyeless amphipods, each the length of a fingernail, work their way methodically across the drowned gravel. Their shells are milky and semi-transparent, evolved for pitch-black aquifers, wholly indifferent to the floodlight blazing directly out of the water they breathe. They scrape microscopic mats of bacteria from the sharp edges of flake-stone, darting backward with a sudden snap of their tails whenever a hot droplet from the ceiling strikes the surface above them and sends sharp rings of refracted light racing across the stone.

High on the dryest facet of the east wall, tucked beneath a narrow, knife-edged overhang where the condensation parts to either side, sits a single arachnid—pale tan, long-legged, its body flat as a coin and no bigger than an almond. Its two tiny median eyes have glazed over with a protective mineral crust against the glare, leaving it to hunt by vibration alone. Its front pair of whip-like sensory legs stretch ten inches out across the wet rock, resting lightly on two separate trickles of water, reading the faint tremors of the chamber's miniature rain and waiting, for hours or days in the bright, quiet steam, for something living to wash down through the seams.
