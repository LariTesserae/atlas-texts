High on the planed-off crown of an old continent, where the mountains were ground down to low swells of pale granite tens of millions of years ago, there is a dimple in the rock no wider than a village square. From fifty yards away it is invisible. The surrounding plateau is wind-scoured, dry, and nearly flat, worn down to a final, exhausted geometry under an ordinary, bright sky. Only when you reach the very lip—which is rounded like an old doorstep, devoid of fractures or loose scree—do you look down fifteen feet into the bowl.

Crossing that smooth stone rim feels like stepping through the wall of a greenhouse that has no glass. Outside, the high-altitude air is thin and cool; inside the hollow, it is instantly, envelopingly warm, holding a steady, tropical heat that refuses to blow away even when wind shears across the rim overhead. And the sound hits at the exact boundary of the warmth: a staggering, relentless roar of clicking, whirring, whistling, and rattling that bounces off the curved rock walls until the air itself feels thickened by vibration.

Despite the noise and the heat, the floor of the hollow is surprisingly open. Most of the basin consists of bare, creamy-gray bedrock, smoothed to a dull satin finish by eons of chemical weathering and slow granular creep. There is no soil blanket, no continuous carpet of grass. Instead, the space is organized around wide, clean intervals of empty stone, punctuated by perhaps a dozen small, hyper-dense islands of dark green foliage and seven or eight scattered objects that do not belong to geology.

These objects are small—none taller than a waist, most the size of a clenched fist or a fencepost—and they have no seams, bolts, or rust. One looks like a slightly slumped frame of black, light-drinking wire jutting at an angle from the solid granite, fused seamlessly into the mineral grain without a crack. Another is a shallow, cantilevered dish hovering three inches above a stonedimple with nothing beneath it, utterly rigid if leaned upon, refusing to yield a millimeter. Whatever civilization left them here did so before the surrounding peaks finished eroding away. The granite around them has lowered by yards over geological time, leaving some of these markers perched on smoothed pedestals of protected rock, while others sit flush in the stone floor, still running on power drawn from something deeper than chemistry—perhaps the spin of the planet, or tiny, stabilized gradients in the vacuum itself.

They are the reason the hollow is warm, and they are the reason it is damp.

There is no pond here, no spring, not even a puddle that lasts past noon. Instead, every twelve or fifteen minutes, the air around one of the dark artifacts blurs subtly, as if its refractive index has shifted, and pulls every trace of vapor out of the dry high-altitude breeze overhead. With a sharp acoustic *thwack*—like a wet canvas snapping—a heavy mist precipitates instantly in a six-foot sphere around the device. For forty seconds, warm fog rolls across the nearby stone, beading heavily on surfaces, running in glossy, hesitant rivulets down shallow grooves worn over millennia into the rock floor, before the cycle cuts off and the bright sunlight begins drinking the moisture back up. Water near these devices does not behave quite right; droplets roll uphill for an inch or two along invisible field lines before gravity reclaims them, or flatten into coin-wide lenses that slide frictionlessly across dry stone until they hit a root mat.

Where those damp tracks run, the vegetation grows with fierce, crowded lushness. Because the rock between these condensation zones is bone-dry, the plants exist as distinct archipelagos—five or six paces of pale, empty stone separating waist-high thickets of glossy, broad-leaved shrubs, swollen tuberous stems, and coiled, velvety vines. The leaves are dark, almost purple-green, packed in tight overlapping tiers to trap every drop of intermittent mist. Mosses thick as upholstery pad the underside of the shrubs, swelling visibly when an artifact snaps its fog into existence, exhaling a rich, sweet smell of wet humus and warm sap into the clear daylight. Flowers open and close on twenty-minute clocks synced to the condensation pulses rather than the sun: fleshy, trumpet-shaped blooms with stiff, translucent petals that quiver in the acoustic din.

And over, through, and between these spaced-out thickets moves an almost incomprehensible volume of animal life.

The emptiness of the bare stone is never static; it serves as a hunting ground and highway for swarms of creatures packed at densities that seem impossible for a forty-yard basin. Every shrub is weighted down with armored, thumb-sized beetles whose iridescent wing-cases buzz at resonant frequencies, producing the low, saw-toothed foundation of the hollow's roar. Above them, the sunny air is crosshatched by hundreds of darting, six-winged flyers—things halfway between dragonflies and hummingbirds, with long, jointed proboscises and metallic-red eyes—that hover in shimmering clouds around the artifacts, waiting for the *thwack* of condensation so they can drink the suspended droplets right out of the air before they fall.

Along the bare granite expanses between the green islands, long-legged, pale lizards with flat, shovel-shaped snouts sprint in bursts so fast they look like flickers of shadow. They hunt the columns of jumping crustaceans that migrate constantly from one damp thicket to the next, timing their dashes across the hot, dry stone between mist cycles. Where a hovering dish casts its permanent circle of shade, hundreds of tiny, translucent snails cluster in slow, spiraling heaps, feeding on microscopic films that grow solely within the artifact's distorted local field. High on the curved lip of the bowl, just inside the invisible ceiling of trapped warmth, squat, throat-pouched amphibians cling to the smooth rock with suction-cupped toes, inflating sacs the color of bruised plums to scream territorial calls across the basin—a piercing, rhythmic *kree-kree-kree* that cuts straight through the insect drone.

Everything is eating, mating, molting, or fleeing in plain sight under the flat, honest noon light. A flyer strays two inches too close to the unseen thermal boundary at the rim of the bowl, hits the cold outside air, stalls for a fraction of a second, and falls back onto the warm granite, where three pale lizards collide in a scrabbling hiss of scales to tear it apart. Within ten seconds, nothing remains on the polished rock except a single iridescent wing, which a tiny, spider-like scavenger immediately hoists overhead like a parasol and carries into the nearest clump of moss.

Near the center of the depression stands the largest built remnant: two slender vertical rods of matte-gray material, spaced four feet apart, rising five feet out of the bare floor. Between them, nothing is visible, yet dust and airborne seeds drift up to the plane connecting the two rods and slide sideways around it, as though encountering a pane of glass. A thick, ancient vine has grown out from a nearby thicket across twelve feet of bare stone specifically to reach the base of the left rod. Its tendrils cannot grip the frictionless gray surface, so it has woven a tight, self-supporting basket around the column an inch away from touching it, bathing its leaves in whatever subtle radiation leaks from the seam. Inside that basket of vines, dozens of tiny, featherless arboreal mammals—no bigger than mice, with oversized ears folded tight against the noise—sleep tangled in a pulsing, warm ball, waking every quarter-hour when the neighboring node snaps a fresh veil of mist over them, licking the warm dew from one another’s skin before tucking their heads back beneath the leaves.

Step back three paces up the rounded slope, lift your head above the worn granite rim, and the roar drops abruptly to a muffled, localized hum beneath your boots. All around, for miles in every direction, stretches the silent, bleached expanse of the high plateau—windswept, ancient, and empty under a cloudless sky, betraying nothing of the steaming, deafening room sunken into its stone.
