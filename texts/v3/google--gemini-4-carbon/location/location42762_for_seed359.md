There is no ground here, only the floor. It was laid very recently—perhaps last season, though seasons are difficult to measure when the light simply exists inside the bricks rather than falling upon them. The floor is an irregular basin, roughly eighty paces across before the walls curl inward like the dry lip of a kiln, made entirely of unglazed, yellow-ochre clay tiles set on edge in tight herringbone patterns. Every tile bears the sharp, unweathered ridges of the thumbs that pressed it into the mold. Nothing has worn them down. There is no wind to carry grit, and there has never been rain to soften the mortar, which is a brittle paste of crushed bone and dry lime.

The space is warm, holding the steady, enveloping heat of a banked bread oven or a body just beneath a heavy wool blanket. The air tastes faintly of toasted chaff and static. 

Despite the bone-dry masonry, the room is choked with growth. From the hairline gaps between the sharp-edged tiles, thick, fleshy stalks push upward, crowding the basin into a dense, tangled thicket. These plants have never known water; they drink geometry and heat. Their broad, overlapping leaves are the texture of suede and the color of oxidized copper and pale sage. Instead of sap, their veins run with a fine, warm silt that shifts with an almost imperceptible slowness as the stalks lean toward one another. Where two leaves press together for more than a few days, their edges fuse, knitting the grove into a continuous, tiered canopy of dry, velvety bridges and pockets. New shoots emerge not by unfurling, but by extruding straight out of the terracotta bricks, pulling the clay’s pigment with them so their lower stems are rigid and ceramic, gradually softening into dry leather a foot above the floor.

Light does not come from above—the curving ceiling is merely more hand-laid brickwork, woven tighter like an inverted basket until it closes off entirely. Instead, the illumination is a property of the middle air. It is the clear, even brightness of an overcast afternoon, yet it casts shadows sideways and upward simultaneously. Step beneath a canopy of fused copper-colored leaves, and the underside of your chin will be brightly lit while the top of your boots falls into deep, crisp shade. Where the architecture is thickest—where low, dry-stone retaining walls, crude mortise-and-tenon timber scaffolds, and inexplicable cairns of fire-cracked flint have been wedged among the trunks—the space itself begins to misbehave. 

Whoever built this place used only wooden mallets, flint scrapers, and plumb lines of braided grass, yet they constructed joints that violate the volume of the room. A low archway of stacked slate slabs, its edges still razor-sharp from the knapping hammer, stands near the center of the thicket. Looking through it from the north, you see a dense cluster of pale, gourds-on-vines three paces away. Walk around to the south side and look back through the exact same arch, and you are looking down a twenty-foot-deep dry cistern lined with woven wattle, crowded with sleeping animals. The mortar between the stones doesn't bind rock to rock; it binds *here* to *elsewhere*, though none of the elsewheres go beyond the boundaries of this warm, small hall. They just fold the room back into itself, crowding three rooms' worth of timber frames, clay-daubed alcoves, and lush, arid foliage into a single hamlet-sized hollow.

Because the space is folded so densely, it is teeming with creatures, all moving at the syrupy pace of cooling wax. 

Hundreds of creatures the size of hares, covered in overlapping plates of pale, fibrous bark and dry felt, cling to the wooden scaffolding and the undersides of the suede-leaved plants. They have no mouths, only intricate, asymmetrical ruffs of stiff bristles around their necks with which they sweep the warm air, harvesting the stray geometric angles that flake off the walls. Beside them, coiled around the terracotta stems, are yard-long segmented worms made of something like supple, warm pumice. Every few minutes, one shifts its weight, producing a faint, dry *shhhk*—like two pieces of charcoal rubbing together. 

That soft, chalky friction is nearly the only sound in the entire enclosure. There are no calls, no hums of wings, no rustle of breeze. In the crook of a hand-hewn oak beam—the wood still pale and fragrant from the adze, entirely unrotted—nests a cluster of six-legged beasts with the heavy, sleepy heads of calves, though their bodies are no larger than loaves of bread. As they breathe, their flanks expand not outward into the room, but *inward* into an adjacent, unseen crease in the masonry, vanishing from the ribs back on every inhale and slowly extruding back into view on the exhale. They watch the room with dull, amber eyes made of solid resin.

As you move through the thicket, pushing aside warm, heavy fronds that yield like thick felt and hold whatever shape you bend them into, the rules of proximity assert themselves. Distance here is measured in craftsmanship rather than feet. Two clay pillars standing side-by-side might require ten minutes of careful squeezing through dense, warm brush to travel between, because the mason laid thirty courses of brick in the gap that your eyes insist is only an inch wide. Conversely, a moth-like animal with wings of stretched, dry parchment can step off a high rafter at the far curve of the ceiling and immediately alight upon the toe of your boot, having traversed a seam where the builder tied two distant plumb-lines into the same knot.

Nothing decays here, because there is no moisture to feed rot and no violence to break what has been made. Instead, things simply accumulate and slowly rearrange their states. At the base of the walls, the dry lime mortar occasionally crystallizes into tiny, sharp-edged cubes that detach, grow velvet leaves of their own, and begin the multi-year process of inching up the timber posts. And the creatures, when they grow too heavy with the silt and warmth they've absorbed, settle into the gaps of the herringbone floor. Over weeks, their felt and bark stiffen, square off, and fire themselves in the ambient heat, becoming fresh, sharp-edged tiles—complete with the phantom impression of a maker's thumb—wedging the floor ever tighter, pushing the walls imperceptibly outward into a void that isn't there, making just a little more room for the dry, crowded garden to grow.
