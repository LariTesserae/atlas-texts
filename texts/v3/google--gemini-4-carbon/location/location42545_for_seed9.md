The basin is eighty yards across, no larger than the crossing of a great abbey, and entirely suspended in the thin, glaring air above the clouds. Below its riveted iron belly, there is nothing—only a dizzying drop into pale blue emptiness, visible in blinding vertical slices between the floorboards when the ferns shift. There is no bedrock here, no soil that wasn't hoisted up in canvas sacks or precipitated out of chemical baths. Every inch of the substrate is manufactured: flanged steel girders, freshly poured concrete sills still stamped with the grain of their wooden forms, and tiers of unoxidized copper trays stacked like crooked amphitheater seating. 

Everything looks as though the scaffolding came down yesterday. The gray paint on the structural trusses has not yet blistered; the brass couplings gleam without a trace of verdigris; the sharp, cast-iron corners of the sluice gates haven't lost a millimeter of their foundry crispness. Yet the place is already drowning in green. 

Heat radiates upward from the floor—a grid of thick, cast-iron steam pipes pulsing with warmth pumped from whatever titanic boiler works sit invisible miles below. Where this rising industrial warmth meets the crisp, high-altitude chill spilling through the open lattice of the vaulted glass roof, the air constantly gives up its moisture. The room makes its own weather on a ten-minute clock. A warm, breathy fog thickens around the catwalks, clings to the crisp edges of I-beams, beads rapidly on the undersides of thousands of wire-mesh planters, and then falls as a sudden, localized indoor rain. Even when the air clears to brilliant, sharp mountain sunlight, every surface weeps. Water sheets down smooth, unweathered concrete baffles, pools in the stamped depressions of diamond-plate walkways, and runs in clear, warm rivulets along the channels of structural H-beams.

Into this wet, heated cradle, vegetation is erupting with frightening speed. Because there is no ground, the lushness grows directly out of the machinery. Thick, rubbery philodendrons and broad-leafed monsteras are rooted in shallow troughs of synthetic, black-pellet slag irrigated by dripping brass manifolds. Moss—impossibly bright, neon-emerald, and thick as sheared velvet—coats the warm casings of silent turbines, thriving on the thermal gradient, though peel a mat of it back and the steel beneath is still mill-stamped and spotless, untouched by rust. Vines thick as mooring ropes twine around galvanized conduits, their tendrils visibly twitching as they seek purchase on greased turnbuckles. Giant, translucent-stemmed begonias crowd the narrow maintenance stairs, pressing waxy leaves flat against the glass panes of pressure gauges, obscuring the needles that twitch steadily in the red. 

Despite the industrial bones of the place, there is no roar of furnaces or clatter of pistons. The power comes from elsewhere, arriving silently through thick, braided-cloth cables and insulated steam mains that rise out of the abyss below. The soundscape is hushed, heavy, and hydraulic. There is the relentless, polyrhythmic *plink-plink-rush* of condensation gathering and spilling from tier to tier; the soft, pressurized *shhh* of automatic misting valves opening and closing on oiled cams; the hollow, metallic *tink* of fresh steel expanding in the bright sun; and beneath it all, a low, barely audible 50-hertz hum you feel more in your molars than hear in your ears. Occasionally, a heavy, bulbous seedpod drops onto an iron grate with a dull thud, rolling until it catches against a fresh bolt-head.

The strangeness of the sanctuary lies in how the machinery and the botany seem to be collaborating on an unfinished blueprint. Overhead, a slow-moving gantry crane traverses the span on greased rails, its movement smooth as clockwork, dragging a curtain of fine brass chains through the upper canopy. As the chains brush the broad leaves, static discharges in tiny, silent blue sparks in the humid shade, and the leaves immediately furl inward, squeezing out clear, viscous sap that drips down into waiting glass funnels clamped to the handrails below. Elsewhere, hydraulic rams slowly tilt whole terraces of orchids five degrees toward the high, white sun, holding them in the glare until the petals flush dark violet, then tilting them back into the warm steam-shadow with a soft sigh of released pressure. It is unclear whether the machine is farming the plants, or the plants are acting as fluidic resistors and governors for the machine. 

Life with eyes and legs is scarce in this dense, dripping jewel-box. There are no birds—the air outside the glass lattice is too thin and cold for anything to wander in—and no buzzing clouds of gnats. What lives here seems bespoke, or else newly arrived and slightly bewildered. Along the warm copper pipes glides a pale, thumb-sized snail with a shell coiled precisely like a machined worm-gear, scraping up the mineral scale deposited by the hard water before the metal can tarnish. Deep in the flooded bilges beneath the lowest grating, where warm water stands six inches deep over pristine lead sheeting, blind, translucent amphibians with feathery external gills drift slowly through submerged bundles of gutta-percha wiring. Once an hour, when the mist clears and the high sun strikes the center of the room with unfiltered alpine intensity, a single, palm-sized beetle with elytra the exact matte gray of primer paint crawls onto a sunlit rivet, spreads damp wings to dry, and sits motionless while the entire steel-and-fern chamber ticks, breathes, drips, and builds itself around it.
