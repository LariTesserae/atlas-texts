There is no stone here, though at first glance the pale, fluted canyons dropping away through the water look like sculpted limestone. Touch the floor and it gives by a fraction of a millimeter—dense as bowling-ball lignum, slick with a microscopic cuticular wax, cold as river ice, and entirely alive. 

Everything solid in this basin is plant. It is a single, continuous botanical mass spanning the breadth of a continent, lifted two miles into the thin upper air on its own rigid, interlocked timber-plateaus, cradling an inland ocean in the hollows of its back. Beneath hundreds of feet of submerged root-bed, there is only older, denser heartwood, compressed into cellular pillars that hold a million cubic miles of cold fresh water high closer to the stratosphere than to any true sea level.

Yet for a world made wholly of vegetation, it is startlingly empty.

There are no tangled kelp beds, no drifting weeds, no murky thickets or leaf-littered floors. Because the water sits at high altitude and holds a constant temperature just four degrees above freezing—the exact point at which fresh water reaches maximum physical density—it is astonishingly, dizzyingly clear. Crisp mountain daylight punches straight through the flat surface above and illuminates vast underwater plains with the sharp, shadow-casting clarity of open air. You can look sideways through the cold liquid for two miles before the pale cyan fade swallows the view, and across all that distance see only three or four structures: colossal, smoothed arches of barkless yellow-white wood, each the thickness of a stadium, bridging the open floor in sweeping catenary curves before plunging back into the living substrate. Between them hangs an immense, crystalline sparsity—cubic kilometers of pure, cold water holding nothing at all.

Long ages of current have worked this living terrain into settled, hydrodynamic perfection. Every root-ridge, basin wall, and towering buttress has been worn into smooth flutes and parabolic troughs, not by crumbling away like rock, but through an ancient truce between water friction and cambial growth. Where the current presses hardest, the wood grows denser and slicker, curving seamlessly around the force until turbulence vanishes into laminar glide.

The water is loud. It roars in the chest and vibrates behind the kneecaps before you ever see why.

Follow one of the smooth, wide troughs downward, where the pale floor funnels into a circular throat fifty yards across, and you find the machinery. There are no buildings around it. There are no concrete footings, no catwalks, no hatches, rivets, scaffolding, or maintenance ladders—not a single brick or plank laid by a hand. Instead, seated directly inside a smooth, calloused socket of living ivory-wood, turns a naked twelve-vane Francis turbine cast of dark, passivated steel.

The wood has grown tight around the stator ring like a knuckle around bone. No housing encloses the engine; its massive main shaft extends bare through fifty feet of open water to couple directly into an unshielded electromagnetic rotor wound with copper bars thick as railway ties. Sap—clear, viscous, heavy with dissolved lipids—weeps continuously from specialized pores in the wooden bearing-blocks, lubricating the spinning steel journals without ever mixing into the surrounding cold water, drawn back into downstream vascular ducts by osmotic suction the instant it warms.

These un-housed engines sit miles apart across the floor of the submerged continent, spaced with geometric regularity at the bottom of every major basin, running at a heavy, invariant twelve revolutions per minute. Together they generate the continuous, deafening acoustic pressure that fills the sea: a layered industrial chord of sixty-hertz electrical hum, rhythmic hydraulic *thud-wash*, and the crisp, sizzling roar of electrolysis.

From thick, uninsulated busbars braided directly into the surface roots along the canyon walls, raw current discharges straight into the cold water along exposed platinum-gray nodes. Sheets of fine hydrogen and oxygen bubbles hiss upward from these lines in shimmering, vertical curtains half a mile long and rising hundreds of feet toward the bright surface. Because the atmospheric pressure above the high-altitude ocean is low, the tiny bubbles expand rapidly as they climb, turning from faint white mist near the floor into roaring, silver-bright pillars of fizzing gas that oxygenate the water and drive the tireless, circular convection currents feeding the turbines below. The greenery—buried microscopically inside the pale wood itself rather than spread out on vulnerable leaves—drinks the dissolved carbon and electrical heat directly through the timber skin, pumping sap, feeding the bearings, tightening its grip on the humming iron.

Abundant life moves through the vast open spaces between the arches and bubble-curtains, organized entirely around the sound and the current.

Great, ribbon-like shoals of streamlined, silver-flanked swimmers—each the length of a forearm, insulated with firm white blubber against the four-degree chill—hang suspended in the huge voids of sunlit water. They navigate by magnetoreception, banking in tight, flash-lit unison along the invisible magnetic flux lines radiating for miles around the submerged dynamos. Closer to the pale wooden floor, barrel-bodied grazers with rubbery, insulated ventral pads clamp onto the vibrating root-ridges in clusters of hundreds, scraping away the thin films of electro-trophic moss that bloom wherever stray voltage leaks from the wood. Long, translucent eels drift straight through the deafening curtains of rising electrolytic bubbles, letting the upward rush tumble them effortlessly toward the surface brightness before gliding miles out into the empty blue on rigid pectoral fins. None of them scatter at the noise; their lateral lines are tuned to the sixty-hertz baseline the way terrestrial lungs are tuned to nitrogen. If the turbines ever stopped, the silence would blind them.

The turbines never stop.

Nothing here speeds up, slows down, grows over, or wears out. There is no silt on the floor because there has never been mineral rock to grind into mud. No leaves fall because there are no leaves; no dead wood drifts because every dying cell in the continental substrate is resorbed internally long before it can slough into the water. Even the deceased fauna sink gently into wide, warm vascular sumps beside the generator bearings, where acidic root-hairs dissolve bone and blubber cleanly into trace electrolytes within hours, replating microscopic wear back onto the spinning steel impellers through galvanic deposition.

Above, the water’s surface meets the thin, cool mountain air without a single wave—only the steady, localized boil where the mile-long bubble curtains break and release crisp oxygen into a cloudless sky. Below, crisp noon-bright light slants forever down through hundreds of feet of freezing glass-clear liquid, illuminating smooth pale wood, distant turning iron, silver ribbons of fish suspended in vast emptiness, and the endless, thundering hum of a world locked in absolute equilibrium.
