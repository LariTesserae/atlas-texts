The tableland sits so high that the air carries the thin, sharp taste of upper altitudes, yet it never dries out. It is a subcontinent of weathered greywacke and dark slate, worn down over millions of years into an endless, rolling corrugation of shallow basins, slumped ridges, and broad, waterlogged saddles. From any rise, the horizon drops away at a dizzying distance, obscured only by the curvature of the earth and the low, heavy bellies of clouds that scrape directly across the ground. There are no jagged peaks left here; ice and rain have long since ground the stone into smooth, whale-backed whalebacks, and every square inch of that stone has been claimed.

There is no bare dirt, and almost no bare rock. The surface is a continuous, suffocatingly dense mattress of cold-weather flora, packed together three to five feet deep. Cushion plants, stiff-leaved sedges, rubbery rosette shrubs, and sprawling mats of sphagnum and silver-green moss lock arms in a tight, bouncy weave. If you drop a stone here, it does not hit the earth; it vanishes into wet green sponge. Beneath the top canopy of glossy, frost-hardened leaves, a secondary forest of liverworts, pale lichens, and tangled root-runners crowds every gap, drinking the constant moisture. Water is everywhere, though rarely in great roaring rivers. Instead, the entire plateau weeps. It sheets silently over the lip of buried granite shelves, beads in millions of clear droplets on the undersides of waxy leaves, and pools in dark, tea-colored tarns that fill every dip in the bedrock. Walk ten paces and water wells up cold and clear around your ankles, instantly swallowed again when you step forward as the peat-mat rebounds.

The light is clear, even, and shadowless—the bright, high-altitude daylight of a cool morning that lasts all afternoon, diffused through thin layers of high stratus and reflected off a billion wet leaves. In this crisp illumination, the sheer scale of the tableland becomes disorienting. Mile after mile of mottled olive, rust-red, and slate-grey vegetation rolls outward, braided with silver threads of slow-moving seepages. Weather happens horizontally here. You can watch a squall twenty miles wide drag its grey curtain across a distant swell of land, leaving the terrain behind it gleaming like oiled metal before the mist swallows it again.

And yet, despite the vastness and the wind that pushes the clouds, the plateau is astonishingly quiet. The three-foot carpet of wet moss and dense foliage acts as an immense acoustic baffle. Wind does not howl here; there are no tall trees to catch it, no sharp crags to whistle against. Air simply slides over the greased, aerodynamic curve of the packed shrubs with a faint, breathy hush. Even the water makes almost no noise, trickling through choked root-channels rather than falling over open ledges. 

It is within this thick, damp quiet that the anomaly of the place makes itself felt—first through the soles of the feet, then in the teeth, and finally to the eye.

Threaded across the wild, unbuilt expanse are the lines. There are no roads alongside them, no maintenance shacks, no fences, no gravel pads, no cleared rights-of-way. The wilderness has not been graded or dug up. Yet marching across the boggy basins from one horizon to the other are vast arrays of heavy, dark-iron machinery, dropped into place long ago or planted by something that never intended to walk beside them. 

Thick, oxidized steel pylons—Squat, tripod-legged, built to withstand highland gales—rise straight out of the uninterrupted cushion-plants, their bolted feet completely engulfed by red peat moss and creeping willow. Strung between them at chest height are heavy, braided copper cables, glazed green with verdigris, sagging under the weight of condensed water that drips steadily from their bellies. Every half-mile or so, nestled deep in the wet hollows where the seepage runs strongest, sit cylindrical cast-iron housings the size of railway carriages. They have no doors or windows—just seamless, ribbed iron shells, greased at the joints with dark tallow, turning heavy, recessed flywheels. 

This is an industrial infrastructure operating entirely without human footprint, muffled to near-silence by the landscape and its own heavy engineering. Inside those buried and half-submerged iron drums, high-head water turbines and thermal-exchange pistons drink the relentless, gravity-fed runoff of the plateau. You do not hear clanking or roaring. Because the casings are thick and seated directly into the saturated peat and bedrock, their work translates only into a deep, sub-audible thrum—a steady, rhythmic *thud-thud-thud* felt in the sternum when you stand within fifty yards of a housing, accompanied by the faint, warm smell of ozone, wet iron, and hot mineral oil mingling with the sharp scent of crushed bog-myrtle.

Occasionally, a brass pressure-valve nested in the moss will open, letting out a long, soft sigh of white steam that immediately condenses into droplets on the surrounding sedges before clicking shut again. The copper cables hum at fifty cycles, a quiet, steady vibration that shakes the dew off them every few seconds in synchronized silver sheets.

The wildlife—immense in number, packed just as densely into the plateau as the plants—treats this tireless grid not as an intrusion, but as a native geology with useful weather of its own. 

Because the air is perpetually cool—hovering just above freezing in the cloud-banks and rising only to a brisk chill in the bright intervals—the gentle waste heat of the iron housings drives the entire local ecology. Around every turbine drum, the vegetation grows waist-high and fiercely green, forming dense halos of giant umbrella-leaved perennials and thickets of black-berried heath. Here, the fauna congregates in staggering numbers, moving in the same hushed register as the land.

Thousands of heavy-bodied, flightless rails with oiled, slate-blue plumage slip silently through the warm tunnels beneath the shrub canopy, snapping up the clouds of midges and flightless beetles that breed in the heated moss around the iron casings. Broad-skulled, wool-coated grazers—built low like badgers, the size of sheep, with wide, splayed hooves meant for walking on floating bog—move in herds of hundreds across the swells. They make no vocal calls, communicating instead by subtle ripples of their pale ear-tufts, cropping the sedges cleanly and pausing to rub their wet flanks against the vibrating legs of the steel pylons. 

Higher up on the copper cables, rows of small, silent raptors with owl-soft feathers sit shoulder-to-shoulder, warming their talons on the current-heated wire while scanning the spongy mat below. When one drops, there is no cry and no beat of stiff feathers—just a sudden shadow, a muffled rustle in the wet leaves as a vole or bog-lemming is taken, and the bird lifting effortlessly back onto the humming line. Amphibians, dark and slick as wet slate, plaster themselves by the dozens directly onto the ribbed iron of the turbine shells, soaking up the steady forty-degree warmth, their throat-sacs pulsing rapidly without letting out a single croak. Even the predators of the large herds—long-legged, grey-furred curs with oversized padded paws—hunt by sight and silent pack-flanking across the open swells, emerging out of a drifting cloud-bank without a bark, scattering a herd with only the wet, rhythmic *shuck-shuck-shuck* of hundreds of hooves sucking out of the saturated moss.

Everything is in motion, and everything is hushed. Over the course of an hour, a bright patch of clear sky sweeps five miles across a shallow valley, lighting up the rust-red seed heads of the rushes, the wet backs of three hundred grazing beasts, and a majestic, curving row of verdigris-stained power lines stretching endlessly toward a grey ridge. Behind the sunlight comes the mist again, rolling flat along the ground, condensing instantly on wool, feather, leaf, and warm iron alike. Beneath it all, the hidden water keeps pressing downward through the ancient rock, turning the buried wheels, sending silent rivers of electricity across an untouched continent to nowhere visible at all.
