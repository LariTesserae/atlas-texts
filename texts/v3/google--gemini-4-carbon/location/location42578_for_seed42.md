High in the cold collar of an old mountain range, where the slate ridges normally hold only snowmelt and scree, there is a sinkhole no wider than a cathedral’s nave and perhaps twice as deep. It is filled to the brim with clear, frigid, slowly turning water. There is no rock visible within it. Every boundary—the sheer walls, the curved floor, the terraced ledges—is made of living root and thickened stem, woven over centuries into a single, submerged town.

From above, the surface of the tarn looks merely like dark glass ruffled by mountain wind, catching the pale afternoon sun. Beneath that thin tension line, the light opens up. Because the altitude thins the air above and the water is fed by slow, pure snow-seep rather than silty rivers, the daylight penetrates all the way to the bottom, turning the interior a crisp, shadowless celadon green. You can see from one end of the flooded settlement to the other in a single glance. Most of the volume is open, cold water—great, clear vaults of liquid emptiness separating the sleek green structures.

Nothing here was built by stacking stone or felling timber; it was trained. Generations ago, before the basin slowly flooded or perhaps as it rose, hands using bone wedges, slate knives, and twisted fiber cords guided the native high-altitude water-willows and giant bladder-ferns into architectural forms. Now, the settlement is entirely self-sustaining and entirely submerged, its original builders either adapted beyond recognition or long departed, leaving behind an urban geometry that continues to grow at a glacial pace. 

The "buildings" are hollow, smooth-walled pavilions of fused, living wood, spaced widely apart along sweeping spiral avenues of braided root-mat. Because the plant mass has had centuries to settle into equilibrium with the water, there is no ragged overgrowth, no choking weeds or tangled thickets. Everything is sleek, thick, and worn smooth by the slow, incessant circular drift of the basin's current. Archways have thickened like old knuckles; steps leading down into submerged plazas are rounded like river cobbles, yet covered in fine, photosynthetic bark that drinks the cool mountain light.

Connecting the widely spaced domes and towers are taut aqueduct-lines of hollow reed—each the thickness of a man’s torso—lashed together with living tendrils that have long since grafted into solid collars. Simple primitive mechanisms still function along these lines: heavy slate counterweights suspended on braided vine ropes, and wide, slow-turning paddles carved from hardened burl-wood that pivot once every few minutes as the deep thermal currents sigh upward from the bottom of the sinkhole. They creak as they turn, a low, resonant groan that travels instantly through the dense medium of the water.

Sound is the strangest truth of the place. Looking into the clear, sparse avenues, the eye expects deep silence, the hushed stillness of a shrine. Instead, the water is booming and crowded with noise. Because the basin is small and walled entirely in rigid, water-logged timber, it acts as an echo chamber. Every sound presses directly against the bone. 

The volume comes chiefly from the fauna, which treats the drowned town as an intricate reef. Thousands of thumb-sized, slate-blue carapace shrimp cling to the undersides of the grown bridges, snapping their heavy, asymmetrical claws to stun drifting spores and communicate across the open water; together, they produce a relentless, deafening crackle like fat hitting a hot iron skillet or a cedar forest burning. Beneath that constant roar, bloated, pale-bellied mountain frogs—the size of loaves of bread, having lost the need for lungs in this oxygen-rich, cold water—sit inside the hollow willow-chambers and use the wooden rooms as resonators. They let out slow, rhythmic *booms* that vibrate through the root-floors and make the suspended silt dance. Schools of heavy, sluggish char, silver-sided and speckled with pale orange, drift like flocks of dirigibles down the wide, empty streets between the towers, scraping their bony mouths against the bark-plazas with a harsh, rhythmic rasping sound.

Despite the racket and the abundance of creatures, nothing moves quickly. The cold keeps every metabolism on a tight leash. A char takes a full minute to glide from a round doorway to an opposing archway; a great frog booms once, waits twenty heartbeats, and booms again. Even the shedding of oxygen from the architecture happens in slow motion: all across the smooth green walls and vaulted ceilings, millions of tiny silver pearls swell slowly out of the bark, tremble in the current, and detach in lazy, shimmering curtains that drift upward toward the distant ceiling of sky.

Near the center of the sinkhole stands a sparse market-ring—seven tall, hollow pillars of grafted root arranged in a wide circle, bridged at their crowns by a single hoop of braided bough. Grooves cut centuries ago by stone adzes are still faintly visible in the bark, stretched and softened like scars on an old tree. Tethered to one pillar by a thick, calcified hemp line is a spherical cage woven from living shoots, its bars grown solidly together so it can never be opened without a blade. Inside it, a smooth white river stone rests on a bed of moss, bobbing almost imperceptibly as the water breathes—an old plumb-bob or parish marker, still keeping time for a slow, loud, cold city where the walls drink the sun and the streets belong to the fish.
