The heat hits before anything is visible—a kiln-dry, motionless weight in thin air, smelling of scorched resin, ozone, and old iron rubbed bare. There is almost no light. What little exists is purely incidental: the dull, cherry-red bleed of overheated casings deep underfoot, or the brief violet snap of static discharging between dry fronds. Yet even in pitch black, you can feel the entire basin seething.

For miles across this high, flat-rimmed tableland, there is no soil, no bedrock, and no open ground. Everything underfoot and out to the unseen edges of the plateau is a single, tightly packed mattress of ancient machinery and waterless vegetation, locked together so densely you could not drop a needle straight down for more than a few inches. Long ago—so long ago that every cast-iron housing and structural beam has been sanded into smooth, rounded contours—an industrial complex covered this highland. Whether the flora was engineered to run the mills or broke loose from the vats to swallow them no longer matters. Metal and wood have ground against each other for ages until they share the same polished, blackened patina, and the boundary between machine and organism has worn away entirely.

Nothing here contains water. A drop of moisture would flash to steam instantly in the blistering dark. Instead, the plants run on heavy, non-volatile oils, molten salts, and raw electrical current bled directly from the buried dynamos beneath them. Their trunks are dry, fibrous cables of carbon and silica, supple as heated rubber, braided tightly through toothless gear-trains and around spinning flywheels. Leaves the texture of blackened mica unfurl in seconds, drink the radiant heat rising from exhaust vents, stiffen into brittle husks, and shatter into fine, dry ash—only to have new shoots punch straight through their remains. 

Everything is in furious, chaotic motion. Great burls swell, split, and extrude fresh bundles of copper-veined roots that immediately begin strangling the nearest stanchion, hauling heavy, worn-down pistons up and down in frantic, irregular rhythms. Whole acres of the surface suddenly buckle and heave as subterranean boilers shift, spilling avalanches of dry seed-pods down into freshly opened crevices, which are knitted shut again within minutes by whipping, blind tendrils. Old conveyor belts, long since stripped of their rubber, are dragged endlessly over smooth steel rollers by the muscular peristalsis of vine-masses, carrying nothing at all, or carrying shattered husks back down into the thermal grates to be burned for heat.

It should be deafening. A valley-wide engine writhing at this speed ought to roar. Instead, the landscape is wrapped in an eerie, suffocating quiet. 

Partly it is the altitude; the dry, scorching air is thin, poor at carrying sound. Mostly, though, the silence belongs to the plants themselves. Every crevice between the moving parts is choked with thick, velvety mats of dead dry fiber, asbestos-like bark, and deep drifts of soot-soft spores that swallow vibration before it can travel. There are no sharp metallic edges left to clang—millennia of tireless friction have polished every cam, latch, and rail into smooth, curving bone-shapes that slide past one another as slickly as oiled skin. Standing on top of this heaving dark expanse, all you hear is a vast, low, dry whisper, like millions of pages turning at once, underscored by soft, hollow clicks and a deep tremor that enters through the soles of the feet rather than the ears.

Nothing with eyes or lungs lives here. No insects tick against the hot husks; no scavengers dart through the shifting gaps. Any soft-bodied creature would mummify in the arid heat within an hour, or be effortlessly milled to powder by the relentless, tight-packed shifting of the root-works. Only the flora-machine persists, serving as its own pollinator, consumer, and wrecker. Pneumatic tubes, grown over with calloused wood, still cough softly in the dark, firing dry bursts of metallic pollen across wide sunken troughs where turbine-blossoms spin lazily to catch the dust.

Out toward the middle of the plateau, where the ancient topography has been planed nearly flat by ages of self-consumption, the turbulence reaches a fever pitch. Waves of growth and collapse travel across the dark surface like silent storms. A hundred yards of tightly woven ironwood latticework will suddenly lose tension, slumping inward toward a cooling dynamo below, and instantly the surrounding mass surges in to claim the space—stems telescoping outward with a soft *hiss* of compressed gas, tendrils latching onto worn steel eyelets, ancient stamping-presses waking for ten frantic seconds to punch illegible, eroded serial numbers into passing leaves before stalling out in a choke of fresh roots. Under a sky empty of stars or clouds, in the breathless oven-dark, the plateau endlessly builds, crushes, and digests itself in near-total silence.
