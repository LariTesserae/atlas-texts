At this altitude, the sky has lost its softness. Overhead it is a hard, dark indigo, paling toward the horizons into a band of blinding silver-white where suspended mineral dust catches the sun. There are no clouds—not a shred of vapor—only the relentless, unfiltered brilliance of high-elevation light pouring down onto a plateau so wide its curvature is visible in the way distant ridges drop cleanly beneath the rim of the world.

This was once a mountain range, ages ago, lifted miles into the thin upper air by some forgotten tectonic collision. Time and wind have long since finished arguing with it. There are no crags left, no talus slopes of broken scree, no sharp edges anywhere. Everything has been planed down into immense, settled swells of pale stone—whale-backed domes of bleached granite and feldspar, shallow basins fifty miles across, and long, curving flutes carved straight into the bedrock. The stone has a satin finish, burnished over millions of years by dry silt blowing from one end of the continent to the other. Where the dust settles in the lee of the swells, it forms drifts as fine and white as talc, arranged in motionless-looking standing waves that are in fact migrating inches every hour.

The air is cold. Even at noon, with the sun blazing fiercely enough to warm exposed rock, the wind itself holds almost no heat; step into the razor-sharp blue shadow of a boulder and the chill is immediate, crisp, and bone-dry. There is no water here—no streams, no dampness under stones, no morning rime. Whatever trace moisture exists in this atmosphere is locked away as scarce free molecules, never condensing.

And yet the plateau is extravagantly alive.

Because nothing can be drawn up from a wet earth, vegetation has turned entirely to the sky and the wind, growing large and billowing to maximize its reach. Across the pale stone plains stand vast, generously spaced botanical islands—groves and solitary giants separated by wide, clean avenues of polished rock so that no organism blocks another’s fetch of air. Each grove is a towering, overflowing fountain of dry foliage. Instead of small, armored desert thorns, the plants here have evolved immense, supple architectures: cascading ribbons of pale sage and silver chamois, broad undulating sails of translucent amber membrane, and tiered canopies of feathery, violet-black plumes ten yards long that drink the fierce ultraviolet light. 

To walk near one of these groves—crossing a quarter-mile of bare, sunlit chert to reach the next—is to see lushness divorced entirely from wetness. The leaves are thick with velvety hairs or coated in flexible, iridescent waxes that never let a single internal molecule escape. Inside their trunks, protected by layers of corky, insulating bark against the cold air, dense lipid-rich saps circulate slowly—fluids the plants manufacture chemically for themselves, binding rare atmospheric hydrogen with carbon under the catalysis of intense sunlight, hoarding that self-made cellar of liquid across centuries. When a frond dies, the plant draws back every drop of fluid before dropping a husk as dry and weightless as tracing paper, which the wind immediately carries away and grinds back into bright dust.

The whole continent is in constant, silent motion. Because the air at this elevation is so thin, sound struggles to travel. A thirty-knot wind sweeping unbroken across three hundred miles of plateau does not roar; it passes as a faint, breathy hiss, like silk drawn across stone. Even standing directly beneath a colossal sail-tree as its canopies tack and billow in the current, all you hear is a soft, dry whispering of velvet against velvet, and an occasional faint *tick-tick-tick* of static electricity.

That static is the engine of the ecology. Dry wind rubbing dry dust against waxy leaves builds tremendous electrical charges. Across a basin, you can watch a weather front of golden mineral dust and buoyant spores rolling in—a dry fog bank a thousand feet high and silent as a shadow. As it nears a grove, the plants respond. Thousands of plumed fronds suddenly stiffen and fan outward, charging their filaments, turning themselves into enormous electrostatic nets. As the dust-cloud passes through, phosphorus, iron, and trace nitrogen snap out of the air onto the sticky-charged hairs of the foliage, stripping the air crystal-clear behind the grove. Minutes later, the plumes relax, curling inward toward hidden digestive creases along the branches to swallow their catch, before unfurling into the wind again.

Between the great spaced groves, the lower ground hosts its own sparse rhythms: rolling cushion-colonies of silver-grey filament ten feet wide, spaced across the white silt like stepping stones, and long, rootless tumble-ferns shaped like geodesic spheres of flexible bone-colored reed. These spheres spend decades rolling gently down the endless, shallow gradients of the basins, pausing wherever the wind eddies to spread dark, sun-absorbing petals on the warm rock, then folding up as the shadow of afternoon overtakes them and letting the night-katabatic breeze roll them onward.

Animal life is rare, stretched thin across the immensity, and built for distance and economy. You might look across forty miles of crystal-clear air—vision here is uncannily sharp, unblurred by humidity, so a ridge fifty miles off looks close enough to touch—and see nothing moving except the swaying groves and the slow curl of a thermal dust-devil. Then a shadow slides across the white stone at your feet. High up, riding the powerful convective columns rising off sun-baked granite domes, a glider circles on wings sixty feet across and narrow as knife-blades. It has no need to flap; its body is little more than a lightweight keel of pneumatic bone, pale insulating down, and keen eyes, spending weeks aloft, dipping down only to rake the uppermost fruiting plumes of a canopy for wax-sealed sugar pearls that supply both food and metabolic fluid.

On the ground, days can pass between sightings of a walker. When one appears, emerging from the dazzle of a salt-pale flat, it moves on stilt-like, tendon-sprung legs that cover ground with a slow, pendulum grace, its wide, cushioned feet leaving faint dimples in the mineral flour that the wind erases within minutes. Covered in dense, reflective silver pelage against both the cold air and the scorching UV, it browses selectively—taking a single resinous bud from one grove, then striding miles across the open stone corridor before taking another, trailed at an immense distance by a solitary, low-slung hunter that relies on patience and the blinding glare of the low sun to mask its approach over days rather than minutes.

As afternoon leans toward evening, the landscape shifts gears all at once. Without moisture in the air to hold the day’s warmth, the temperature drops precipitously the moment the sun nears the flat rim of the world. The light turns a piercing, crystalline rose, throwing shadows miles long across the smooth whalebacks of stone. Everywhere across the vast plain, the lush canopies begin to fold. Dark ultraviolet-drinking sails roll themselves tightly inward; huge feathery crowns draw together into dense, insulated spires to trap the radiant heat gathered during the afternoon. Static charges equalize in the cooling dusk, sometimes visible in the deepening twilight as faint, soundless brushes of violet luminescence flickering along the tips of a thousand closed groves, under a sky already crowded with hard, unblinking stars.
