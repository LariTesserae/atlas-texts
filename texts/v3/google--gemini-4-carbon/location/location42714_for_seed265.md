There is no light here at all—no skyglow, no fungal phosphor, not even the pinprick of an indicator bulb or the brief blue snap of an unshielded relay. Everything that exists in this basin exists only in weight, heat, smell, moisture, and the slow, crowded pressure of things occupying space.

Yet the scale of the plain makes itself known immediately. You can feel it in the way warm air travels across the flats for miles without meeting an obstacle, carrying long, lazy ribbons of scent: wet basalt, heavy machine oil, ozone, and the thick, milky musk of thousands of warm bodies. Sound behaves the way it only behaves in wide, low country—a single drop of condensation falling from an overhead conduit clicks against flooded stone, and nothing throws the sound back. There is no ceiling, no canyon wall, only an immense horizontal openness stretching out at a nearly uniform elevation, shallow and sunken, holding warmth like the bottom of a freshly poured pan.

Underfoot, the stone is young. Nothing has had time to wear smooth. The floor of the basin is a continuous expanse of freshly fractured ledges, stepped flats, and razor-edged scree, every break crisp and ninety-degree sharp, as if the crust split open last week and settled without grinding. Water is everywhere, though there are no rushing rivers to round the pebbles. Instead, warm water stands in geometric, ankle-deep sheets across the flat stone tables, seeps continuously from hairline cracks in the rock, and condenses out of the supersaturated air onto every surface. Everything touched is slick and roughly body-temperature.

Despite the barrenness of the young rock—where vegetation is reduced to isolated, yard-high stalks of stiff, rubbery vascular tissue spaced dozens of paces apart, gripping raw stone fractures with mineral-dissolving roots—the darkness is densely packed. You cannot walk three yards in a straight line without touching something alive, or something that has forgotten how to remain strictly local.

The fauna are abundant to the point of carpeting the flats, buffered against the knife-edges of the stone by thick, gelatinous hides, calloused mantles, or sheer ontological imprecision. Broad, low-slung creatures the size of hogs rest flank-to-flank in the warm puddles, their skin slick and hairless, breathing once every two or three minutes with a barely audible *huff* of damp air. Smaller, segmented things cluster in dense, warm mounds around rock fissures, motionless for hours, shifting one leg at a time like cooling slag. Soft, heavy membranes drape across the sharp basalt ridges, slowly digesting mineral salts or electrical leakage. Nothing scurries; nothing shrieks. Predation here is indistinguishable from resting—one warm body settling gradually over another over the course of an afternoon, boundaries dissolving without struggle or noise.

Because reality in the basin has largely lost its stiffness, the crowding goes beyond mere biology. Space itself is over-occupied. Reach out in the dark to steady yourself against what feels like the wet, breathing ribs of a grazing animal, and your fingers press half an inch inward to encounter cold-rolled structural steel, rivet heads, and grease—without the animal ever moving out of the way. Two or three things routinely share the same volume. A shallow pool of warm marsh water hangs suspended at chest height for forty yards, bound by no container, its underside dry to the touch; blind, ribbon-bodied swimmers drift inside it, brushing softly against the faces of passersby who walk along the dry, sharp rock directly beneath it.

Scattered at wide intervals across the plain—half a mile apart, sometimes two—stand solitary pieces of heavy industrial machinery. There are no factory complexes, no fences, no roads connecting them, and no lanterns hung on their frames. A single cast-iron turbine housing rises fifteen feet out of a flooded depression, sweating warm condensation. Elsewhere, a lone walking-beam pump tilts above a borehole, or an oil-cooled transformer the size of a freight car sits squarely on unweathered stone, tethered to thick, lead-sheathed cables that snake away over the sharp rocks and vanish into the mud—or vanish midway through the air, delivering current across an unbridged gap in location.

These machines are active, fully electrified, and extraordinarily slow. A massive flywheel turns at a rate you can only verify by resting a hand on its rim and waiting to feel your arm carried downward half an inch a minute. Inside drowned sumps, bronze pistons complete one stroke every forty minutes, displacing heavy, warm fluid with a muffled hydraulic sigh that barely rises above the ambient drip of water. Nothing clanks; tolerances are submerged in thick grease, and the engines run muffled, patient, and hot. Around these warm iron casings, the animals pack thickest—layered three and four deep in the dark, pressing soft bellies and spiracles against vibrating bolt-plates, drinking the waste heat.

Distance and sequence fray quietly across the flats. Follow a run of insulated iron pipe eastward by touch, sliding your palm over wet couplings and sleeping, velvet-skinned amphibians that do not bother to wake as your knuckles pass over them—or through them. After twenty minutes of level walking, the pipe bends downward into four inches of warm water and connects directly to the intake manifold you left behind, though the vibration in the metal now runs backward, from cold to hot, and the air smells sharply of bruised stems from the sparse, blind plants.

Now and then, one of those solitary stalks—stiff as vulcanized rubber, branchless, utterly devoid of leaves—releases a single heavy spore-drop into the standing water. The ripple moves outward across the dark pool, passes through the iron leg of a derrick as though the metal were mist, lifts the chin of a sleeping herd-beast three hundred yards away before reaching the beast beside the stem, and spends ten slow minutes dying out against sharp stone edges that were never quite in the path of the wave.

Nothing hurries to correct it. The condensers weep, the pistons inch inward through their oil baths, the herds breathe in slow, overlapping intervals, and the warm dark holds it all without friction.
