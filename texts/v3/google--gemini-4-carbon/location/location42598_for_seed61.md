There is no sky here, and no horizon—only an irregular, flattened vault of dark dolostone no wider than a threshing floor and scarcely twice the height of a tall man, buried far beneath the reach of surface weather. Yet the space holds an entire city.

From the damp floor of the pocket nearly to its sweating ceiling, tiered masonry crowds every cubic foot of air. Structures of split black shale, river-smoothed chert, and unglazed clay rise in four, five, and six overlapping stories, pinned into narrow rock ledges and cantilevered out over one another on corbels of chipped flint and cured rib-bone. The scale of the construction sits uncannily slantwise to a human body: too intricate and load-bearing to be a model, yet far too compressed to walk upright within. Main thoroughfares are sunken trenches scarcely wider than two boots set side by side; arched doorways rise only to the knee or the hip; tiny plazas no larger than tabletops sit surrounded by colonnades of hand-ground stone pillars thick as thumbs. Bridges of pegged slate cross over lower bridges at sharp, congested angles, carrying hollowed-out stone gutters three fingers wide from roof to roof. So dense is the layering that looking into any gap reveals only further recesses of walls, window-slits, drainpipes, staircases with half-inch treads, and buttresses wedged tight against neighboring eaves, all slick with cold moisture.

The noise inside the pocket is immense and unceasing. Water never falls here as rain and never gathers into a true river, yet the surrounding bedrock weeps continuously from thousands of hairline fractures along the ceiling and upper walls. Every drop is caught almost the instant it forms. An exhaustive hydraulic network of pinched clay funnels, copper-leaf lips, grooved slate shingles, and tiered aqueducts intercepts the seepage and routes it downward through the stacked architecture. Because the vault is small, hard-walled, and completely enclosed, its acoustics magnify every impact. Tens of thousands of heavy drops strike taut slate roofs, hollow ceramic cisterns, and shallow stone catch-basins every second, blending with the hiss of full gutters and the hollow gurgle of vertical drain-shafts into a single, deafening roar. It sounds like standing inside the chest of a cataract, though no single stream is thicker than a wrist. Speaking aloud in the chamber would accomplish nothing; the sound sits in the air as a physical pressure, unchanging in pitch or volume from one century to the next.

Light comes directly from the plumbing. Nothing green grows anywhere in the vault—no moss softens the lintels, no pale roots pry through the ceiling cracks, no timber exists to rot in the cold, saturated air. The sole trace of anything resembling flora is a paper-thin, mineral-eating slime that coats the insides of the wet gutters and overflow basins. Agitated by the constant percussion of cold water, this slick film gives off a dim, flat luminescence the color of watered milk. It casts no real beams and throws no sharp shadows; instead, it traces the entire cramped geometry of the settlement in faint, cold outlines—thousands of glowing threads running along eaves, spiraling down stepped culverts, and pooling softly in sunken courtyard wells before vanishing through slotted grates in the floor. Away from the channels, the stacked house-fronts and crooked alleys recede into wet charcoal gloom.

Everything in this pocket shows extreme age without showing ruin. The builders worked entirely with hand-tools—pecked stone, scraped bone, cold-hammered pins of native copper now crusted in dense blue-green carbonate—fitting thousands of irregular pieces together without mortar. Over long spans of time, the ceaseless cold water has worn away every crisp chisel-mark and sharp flake-edge. Stair treads dip in smooth crescents; parapet walls feel grease-slick and rounded to the touch, like soapstone sucked down in a mouth. Yet nothing collapses. The water seeping from the upper rock is heavy with dissolved lime and silica, and at the exact rate its steady dripping erodes the grooves and basins, its thin spray deposits milky skins of flowstone over the joints, sealing loose slates together and thickening the bases of strained arches. Wear and accretion have locked into a dead-level truce. A cracked clay spout heals itself in grey travertine before it can fall; a clogged gutter spills over an adjacent lip that has already been smoothed for centuries to receive the overflow.

The air stays chill, damp against the skin, and completely motionless save for the micro-drafts stirred by falling drops. It smells solely of wet slate, cold copper, and chalk.

Animal life is scarce enough that hours pass without a single movement apart from the trembling skins of water in the catch-basins. Now and then, a blind, pigmentless amphipod the length of a fingernail washes down an upper aqueduct, tumbles safely into a curbside trough, and crawls against the current over the glowing slime. High among the dry undersides of the stone bridges, long-legged cave harvestmen hang motionless from tarnished copper pins, their hair-thin legs spanning the width of entire alleyways to feel for the vibrations of tiny mites. Rarest of all are the pale, flat-skulled salamanders, heavy-bodied and slow as cooling wax, each nearly filling the narrow street it occupies. One will emerge halfway from the dark doorway of a knee-high tower, rest its throat upon a worn stone sill while cold gutter-spray beads along its flank, and remain there without blinking while the stone metropolis roars around it in steady, dimly lit equilibrium.
