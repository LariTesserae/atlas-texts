The hollow is no wider than a town square, sunk ten or twelve feet below the flat, sodden moorland that borders it, yet the space feels crowded to the point of suffocation. What fills it is a dense, waist-high labyrinth of slate fins, iron-rich mud-boils, and the half-submerged remnants of a heavy pumping enterprise. Nothing here has a sharp edge anymore. Centuries of rising and falling groundwater have rubbed the fractured stone into dark, kidney-shaped humps and polished the cast-iron housings until they gleam like wet seal skin.

Light comes down evenly from a low, pewter sky—bright enough to show every pore in the rock and every greasy rainbow on the standing water, yet completely shadowless. In this cool, clear illumination, the ground refuses to stay still. 

Beneath the basin, a shallow, pressurized aquifer is perpetually fighting its way through thick beds of grey clay and ancient industrial slurry. The result is a frantic, silent churn. Everywhere you look, the mud is swelling into tight, glossy domes that stretch, thin out, and split apart, folding back into heavy concentric rings before the next surge pushes up beside them. Runnels of cold, mineral-heavy water braid and unbraid across the slate ledges in seconds, abandoning one worn groove to flood another, draining down sudden vortexes that open with a soft *glug* and seal shut behind a plug of silt. There is no roar, no violent splashing—the clay is too viscous, the water too heavy with suspended grey chalk. The entire basin boils at the volume of a whisper. You hear only the faint suck of mud parting, the soft *tick-tick* of pebbles shifting in an under-cut channel, and the occasional breathy sigh of trapped marsh gas venting through a slick seam.

Wedged into this restless floor are the scattered monuments of an old extraction work, built stoutly enough to survive their own ruin. Four massive flywheels, fifteen feet across and cast in sections, sit tilted at conflicting angles in the muck. Because the ground beneath their timber-and-masonry cribbing is constantly inflating and subsiding, the machinery is not dead. As a mud-boil swells under a buried counterweight, a heavy iron rocker-arm slowly tips skyward, water streaming in silent silver sheets from its bolted flanges, before settling back into the ooze a minute later. Thick copper grounding straps, green with verdigris, snake out of the slurry and twitch as subterranean currents pull at their anchors. Near the center of the hollow stands a squat brick accumulator tower, its mortar long ago leached to sand, held together entirely by exterior iron turnbuckles and its own sodden weight. Inside its dark, open archway, pressure gauges with shattered glass faces still register something—their rusted needles trembling and snapping back and forth as pockets of compressed air burp through the buried manifold pipes below.

The air smells sharply of wet slate, cold tallow, and ozone, though nothing sparks. Condensation beads instantly on every surface, fattening on the undersides of iron I-beams and dropping in rapid, irregular rhythms into the puddles.

Life keeps to the margins and the vertical surfaces, sparse and watchful of the churn. There is no turf, no reed-bed—anything rooted in the floor gets swallowed or torn apart within an hour. Instead, tough, rubbery straps of dark olive liverwort cling to the upper rims of the flywheels, riding their slow, tilting arcs. Pale, wire-stemmed grasses grow in solitary tufts out of bolt-holes four feet above the mud, drinking the condensing mist. 

In the narrow, flooded trenches that form and vanish between the slate fins, blind white flatworms as wide as a thumb surf the shifting currents, grazing on the slick bacterial films that bloom wherever iron rust meets rising sulphur water. Now and then, a long-legged shorebird drops down from the surrounding moor, landing on the crown of a stone pillar or the rim of a cast-iron cylinder. It stands motionless in the cool glare, head cocked, watching the silent, frantic rearrangement of the mud below—waiting for the churning clay to roll up a dormant beetle or a drowned worm—before stepping neatly onto a neighboring pipe just as its perch tilts two inches into the grey water. 

Around the whole perimeter, a waist-high lip of weeping peat and bedded shale holds the hollow in check, marking the exact boundary where the madness stops. Step three paces up that dripping bank, and the ground is solid, the puddles are stagnant, and the world goes utterly still; look back down, and the little amphitheater of iron and clay is already unrecognizable, silently folding itself inside out under the flat afternoon sky.
