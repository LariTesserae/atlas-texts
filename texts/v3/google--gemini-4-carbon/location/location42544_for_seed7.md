The chamber sits sixty meters beneath the crust, roughly the volume of an empty cathedral nave, its boundaries defined by stone that has not yet learned to crumble. Every wall shows the crisp, parallel flutes of borehole drills and the sharp, glassy step-fractures of a recent blast. On the floor, shards of dark traprock lie where they fell, edges still keen enough to slice leather, untouched by wind or running water. Nothing here has been rounded off. Even the rock dust settled into the crevices hasn't turned to soil; moisture has merely stiffened it into a gritty gray paste.

The air is hot enough to feel solid in the throat—a breathless, pressing forty-eight degrees that rises not from magma, but from three squat cylindrical exchangers spaced far apart across the rubble floor. They are industrial things of cast iron and lagged steel, painted a blistered olive drab, connected by thick copper busbars that vanish into raw holes in the rock. They do not roar or hiss. Whatever work they are doing happens inside thick casings, deep in the solid state or through slow fluid convection, bleeding waste heat into the sealed vault until the stone itself radiates like the brick lining of a kiln.

Light barely exists. There is no shaft to the surface. Illumination comes only from the recessed indicator lenses on scattered junction boxes—pinpricks of amber and dull neon orange—and from the faint, cherry-dark radiance of exposed resistance grids high up near the vault’s crown, hovering just at the bottom edge of human vision. Most of the chamber is velvet black, its scale felt rather than seen, judged by the way heat pools in the upper reaches and the long, open distances between one dim orange point and the next.

Water never falls as rain here, and never gathers into a stream. Instead, the chamber sweats. Moisture seeps invisibly through micro-fissures in the cooler upper rock, hits the superheated air, and condenses on the iron flanks of the exchangers, along the undersides of steel I-beams, and across the sharp facets of the fractured basalt. Over hours, droplets swell until their surface tension fails. A single bead breaks loose, slides down a freshly cleaved stone face, and collects in a shallow, angular depression no wider than a dinner plate, where it steams faintly before evaporating back into the dark.

Out of these scattered wet pockets and directly off the hot iron housings grows an extravagant, improbable foliage. It is not a tangled jungle—the chamber remains mostly open, cavernous space, great gulfs of hot black air separating one cluster from the next—but wherever a plant has taken hold, its growth is opulent and massive. Enormous, fleshy leaves the color of bruised plum and wet slate unfurl from crevices in the blast-pile, each frond three meters long, thick as saddle leather, hanging in heavy, pleated curtains. Vines as stout as hydraulic hoses climb the steel stanchions of a lone overhead gantry, avoiding the scorching copper busbars by millimeters while draping the structural iron in dense, waxy cascades.

In the near-total absence of light, these plants have abandoned bright green chlorophyll for stranger chemistries, feeding on the infrared wash of the resistance grids and the steep thermal gradients between sweating stone and scorching metal. Their surfaces are lacquered and immaculate. Because there are no animals here—no beetles, no mites, no moths, not a single fungus gnat circling the amber pilot bulbs—not one leaf bears a chew-mark or a stippled scar. Every edge is whole. Pale, heavy flowers the size of cabbages open occasionally along the gantry beams, extruded on pale stalks into the empty middle air; having nothing to attract, they have no scent, and their pollen does not drift. It hangs in golden, greasy threads that stretch slowly downward under their own weight over days until they touch a lower frond or smear across the sharp stones below.

The silence is nearly absolute. With no wind, those massive, rubbery leaves never rustle against one another. With no insects, there is no hum. The industrial apparatus operates below the threshold of audible vibration, save for the rare, soft *tink* of cooling or expanding metal when a distant load shifts somewhere outside the vault.

Overhead, suspended from a single rusted rail bolted into the jagged ceiling, a motorized inspection trolley inches from the north wall toward the south. Its gearing is stepped down so drastically that its progress cannot be seen by watching it; you would have to note its position relative to a hanging curtain of dark leaves, wait an hour in the stifling dark, and look again to see that the grease-packed wheels had turned a quarter-revolution. Behind it, a braided power cable loops down into the blackness and rises again, carrying warm beads of condensation along its belly. When a drop finally detaches from the lowest curve of the cable, falling twenty meters onto a broad, taut leaf below, the single dull *thock* fills the entire cavern, sharp and solitary, before the heat swallows it back up.

Nothing rots quickly in this sterile warmth. When an old frond finally detaches from a stanchion, it drops onto the jagged talus and lies across the knife-edges of stone like a discarded tarpaulin, slowly curing over months into black, brittle parchment while fresh, turgid shoots push silently past the bolts above it, drinking the sweat off the iron.
