High in the warm reaches of a tropical massif, an entire valley floor has been replaced by stone. There is no exposed bedrock left, no native dirt, no original contour of the mountain; from ridge to ridge, for miles across the elevated basin, the ground consists entirely of dry-laid basalt and granite. It is a continuous, colossal masonry of stepped terraces, sunken plazas, retaining walls, dry cisterns, and broad causeways built without mortar or metal tools, fitted together by sheer weight and patience centuries ago. 

Now, it exists in near-total darkness under a triple-layered canopy of strangler figs, wide-crowned laurels, and mountain Mahogany. The trees do not grow out of soil, because there is none. Instead, they grow directly over and through the masonry, their roots pouring down terraced steps like slow, woody waterfalls, wedging into joints, locking the ancient stones into a new, biological tension. Above, the leaves knit so tightly together that noon arrives at the plaza floors only as a bruised, submarine twilight. Look up, and there is no sky—only overlapping tiers of black-green foliage, hanging roots, and faint, scattered pinpricks of grey where high altitude mist drifts past the upper crown.

The air down among the stones is warm, thick, and breathless, kept like a greenhouse beneath the leaves even at this altitude. There is no running river here, no open lake, no crashing waterfall. Water arrives solely through the mountain clouds that drag slowly through the architecture at night, condensing on millions of broad, waxy leaves and the cool faces of shaded basalt. The stone is permanently damp to the touch, slick with dark moss, liverworts, and velvety films of green algae. Moisture collects in the carved basins of old courtyards, pools in the cupped rosettes of giant tank bromeliads wedged into wall niches, and beads along the undersides of stone lintels, dropping at long, irregular intervals into the leaf litter.

Yet despite the stillness of the air and the absence of wind or rushing water, the darkness is deafening. 

Sound here is a physical pressure, a solid wall of biological noise vibrating off ten million damp stones. Every square foot of the masonry and the canopy above is occupied, layered with animal life in overwhelming, crawling, fluttering density. Millions of tree frogs—none larger than a thumbnail—cling to the damp vertical faces of the terrace walls and nestle inside the flooded hearts of the bromeliads, their metallic, percussive clicks and high-pitched whistles merging into a continuous, ringing static that makes the eardrums ache. Beneath that piercing chorus runs the heavy, grinding saw-buzz of cicadas and giant katydids clinging to the rough bark of the fig roots. 

The stone acoustics amplify everything. The sunken plazas and narrow, walled corridors act as amphitheaters. When a troop of howler monkeys opens their throats high in the invisible upper branches, the low, guttural roar drops down into the stone courts and rolls from wall to wall like trapped thunder. Flocks of night-active parrots and oilbirds, adapted to the perpetual gloom beneath the canopy, echolocate and shriek as they navigate the straight lines of the old causeways. 

Every surface moves when looked at closely. The carved reliefs on the stair risers—worn smooth and round-edged by centuries of root-creep and dripping condensation—are carpeted in slow-moving streams of leafcutter ants, carrying pale crescents of canopy leaves down into the deep, dry voids between the foundation stones. Millipedes as thick as thumbs, armored in mahogany-colored segments, graze slowly across the wet moss. Under every loose flagstone, tarantulas, whip-scorpions, and pale land crabs wait in the humid gaps. In the hollow interiors of stepped pyramids and vaulted stone galleries, bats hang in solid, shivering velvet mats from the corbeled ceilings; the floors beneath them are knee-deep in warm, ammonia-rich guano that boils with shiny-backed beetles and pale larvae, feeding the roots of the very trees that hold the roofs together.

Nothing happens quickly, despite the frantic volume of the noise. The valley is locked into a deep, settled equilibrium. The masonry has already collapsed as much as it is ever going to; where a retaining wall slumped two hundred years ago, it formed a rubble slope now entirely sutured together by philodendron vines and basket-roots, stable as a cliff face. The erosion is patient, accomplished by the weak carbonic acid of decaying leaves and the millimeter-by-millimeter pry of growing wood. 

To walk through this place is to move from one built level to another without ever touching raw earth. Your boots strike dressed stone, muffled by damp leaf-mold and crushed snail shells, climbing broad flights of stairs whose centers have bowed inward from ancient footfall and centuries of settling. Ferns brush your shoulders; warm, humid air smells heavily of wet granite, crushed formic acid, ripe fig fruit, and musk. Small, dark-eyed mammals—coatis, kinkajous, mountain pacas—part slowly before you on the causeways without panic, barely visible in the gloom, so numerous that they brush against the stone balustrades in a constant, rustling traffic. Around you, stretching miles across the dark, warm shelf of the mountain, the invisible city hums and screams with throat and wing and leg, entirely whole, entirely overtaken, holding the jungle up in its stone hands.
