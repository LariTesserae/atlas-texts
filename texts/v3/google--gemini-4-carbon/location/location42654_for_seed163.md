The glare hits first. At this altitude, the sun does not warm the skin so much as scour the eyes, dropping straight out of a pale, thin sky onto miles of saturated green. There are no trees to cast shadows, no tall canopies to break the wind or divide the space. Instead, the land is a single, unbroken mattress of vegetation wrapped tightly over fresh, angular geology. 

Beneath the living mat, the mountains are brand new. Fault scarps, basaltic steps, and shattered plateaus sit at sharp, unweathered angles, untouched by the smoothing work of centuries. There has been no time for soil to form, no time for valleys to carve themselves into gentle curves. Yet you never see the raw stone. A dense, rubbery carpet of highland cushion plants, bloated bryophytes, and interlocking sedges—some three or four feet thick—has swallowed the raw rock whole, preserving every sharp ninety-degree drop and jagged ridge in rounded, bright-green relief. Walking across it is like walking on waterlogged cork; every step depresses the tight, fibrous surface an inch, forcing clear, icy water up around the soles of your boots before the plant mass slowly rebounds.

Water is everywhere, unable to sink into the solid, unfractured bedrock beneath the roots. Because no true river channels have eroded into the young stone yet, the water simply sheets across the entire valley. It spills over the sharp green ledges in wide, thin curtains, turning every terrace face into a blinding mirror that catches the high-altitude sun. Millions of droplets hang on the stiff, waxy leaves of the cushion plants, turning the whole open basin into a field of prisms. The air smells intensely of cold snowmelt, crushed chlorophyll, and ozone.

And then there is the sound. Looking out across the empty, sun-blasted expanse, the sheer volume of it feels impossible, disconnected from the visual stillness of the land. 

It begins with the water—not the gentle babble of a single creek, but the combined, relentless hiss and slap of thousands of shallow cascades dropping simultaneously off crisp rock shelves across a five-mile bowl. Beneath that high, white-noise roar sits a much deeper, physical vibration that rattles the teeth and presses against the eardrums. 

Spaced half a mile or more apart across the vast green benches stand isolated, windowless structures of heavy corrugated iron and riveted steel. They look dropped from the sky, bolted directly through the living plant-mat into the raw basalt below by thick concrete pilings. These are unmanned hydro-pumping stations and high-head penstock intakes. Thick steel pipes, painted a dull industrial grey that gleams blindingly where wet, emerge from the flooded moss-beds and plunge straight down the angular steps of the mountain toward unseen turbines far below the plateau. 

Inside those scattered iron sheds, massive intake valves, rotary screens, and pressure-relief vents operate at full capacity. Every few minutes, a governor adjusts with a slow, hydraulic groan, and a bypass vent blasts a horizontal plume of white mist a hundred yards across the bright moss, adding a jet-engine shriek to the steady, chest-thumping thrum of the buried pipes. High-tension transmission towers—wide, skeletal lattices of galvanized steel—march in a single, sparse line across the basin, their ceramic insulators humming and crackling loudly in the thin, damp mountain air. Between the towers, the heavy cables sag over vast, empty stretches of nothing but glowing green cushion-bog and sheeting silver water.

Nothing hurries here. The blinding clouds of mist thrown up by the relief valves drift lazily across the flat green terraces, barely thinned by the cool, steady breeze. The plants grow millimeters a decade, tightly packed against the cold and the ultraviolet glare, slowly digesting the sharp edges of the young stone beneath them while drinking the endless runoff. Rust blooms at an imperceptible pace around the bolt-heads of the steel pilings, stained orange against the lime-green moss. 

Life beyond the total hegemony of the bog-mat is scarce, pushed out by the thin air, the cold water, and the relentless acoustic pressure. Occasionally, a dark, heavy-bodied beetle crawls deliberately out of a rosette of stiff leaves, its carapace flashing like oil in the fierce sunlight before it slips back into the wet sponge. Once in an afternoon, a solitary mountain harrier might cross the valley, gliding low over the glowing expanse, utterly silent beneath the twin roars of cascading meltwater and vibrating steel, traversing miles of bright, deafening emptiness before vanishing over the sharp green rim of the ridge.
