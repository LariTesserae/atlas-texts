High up near the cloud-line, enclosed on all sides by vertical walls of freshly fractured black stone, sits a basin no wider than a cathedral nave. The rock here has never known smoothing. Every ledge, step, and overhang ends in a flinty, knapped edge sharp enough to split a boot sole, as though the mountain cracked open ten minutes ago. Yet nothing falls. No scree gathers at the base of the cliffs; no pebble ever dislodges.

The light is the color of pewter submerged in milk. It filters down through a thick, stationary ceiling of cold vapor that rests directly on the jagged rim of the basin, turning the space into an open-roofed room. Everything is wet. Cold water condenses continuously out of the heavy air, sheeting down the glass-sharp facets of the cliffs and pooling across the flat stone floor in thin, dark films. Despite the constant runoff, there are no channels, ruts, or softened corners in the rock. Water flows over razor-edges without wearing them down by a micron, vanishing into hairline fractures only to sweat back out of the cold air a few feet up, caught in a closed, lossless loop.

Before you register what you are looking at, you hear it: the deafening, rhythmic *chuff-clack-slam* of heavy drop-forges, high-speed looms, and pressurized turbines. 

There is not a single bolt, beam, wire, or brick in the entire basin. Nothing has ever been built here. Yet the physics of the hollow operate like a heavy stamping plant running at full capacity. The sound emanates from the blank, wet air and the shallow puddles. Watch a flat film of rainwater on the dark stone and you can see it shuddering in sharp, square geometric standing waves, throwing up fine mist at sixty cycles per second while emitting the low, chest-rattling hum of an electrical transformer. The air smells sharply of wet moss, cold stone, ozone, and hot cutting oil—though there is no oil, and the temperature hovers just a few degrees above freezing.

Across the floor of the basin, space is rationed with stark precision. Most of the expanse is completely empty—wide, clean intervals of slick black stone, twenty or thirty paces across, swept clear of debris and open to the dim air. 

Rising out of this emptiness at wide, sparse intervals are eight or nine towering hummocks of vegetation, spaced apart like isolated machinery on a vast factory floor. Where the stone is bare, it is utterly bare; where the plants grow, they grow with suffocating, tropical extravagance despite the chill. Each hummock is a twenty-foot column of broad, waxy leaves the color of dark bottle-glass, intertwined with thick curtains of weeping liverwort, bloated silver club-moss, and tall vascular ferns coiled as tightly as heavy truck springs. 

Here, reality loses its grip on category. 

If you look directly at one of the lush green columns, you see wet, overlapping foliage dripping cold water. Look slightly to the left—catching it in your peripheral vision—and the entire botanical mass is spinning on a vertical axis at three thousand revolutions per minute, blurred into a solid green cylinder like a lathe spindle, flinging off horizontal sheets of water droplets that vanish six inches from the leaves without ever hitting the stone floor. Step closer, and it is still again, except the broad leaves are snapping open and shut in crisp, pneumatic unison—*hiss-chak, hiss-chak*—compressing the cold mountain air until pale blue arcs of high-voltage electricity jump cleanly from leaf-tip to sharp rock wall with the crack of aspot-welder. Sap weeps from the fern-stems in thick, viscous amber beads that drop onto the rock, roll uphill along sharp stone seams, and re-enter the roots, lubricating a circuit nobody designed.

Every lush column, and the upper ledges of the cliffs, teems with animals. There are thousands of them, packed densely onto the wet greenery or arranged in neat, motionless queues across the bare thresholds of stone, leaving vast stretches of the floor entirely unoccupied.

None of them eat, sleep, hunt, or die. They operate.

Clinging to the undersides of the broadleaves are hundreds of pale, slick-skinned amphibians the size of loaves of bread, six-legged and eyeless. Their throats inflate and collapse in locked, mechanical synchronization, acting as biological bellows that pump a steady, forceful draft of cold mist from one side of the basin to the other. Around the base of each green pillar, heavy, flightless creatures resembling squat rails—covered in coarse, wet filaments that gleam like tarnished copper wire—march in tight, concentric rings. With every third step, they strike their heavy, wedge-shaped bills against the sharp basalt floor in unison: *CLANG.* Not a chip of stone flies; not a bill cracks. Between the pillars, clouds of thumb-sized, slate-gray insects hover in rigid, three-dimensional grids, five inches apart from neighbor to neighbor. Their wings beat at an exact, unwavering pitch that drills into the teeth like a band saw cutting sheet metal. Periodically, an entire grid of five hundred insects drops three feet instantaneously—without passing through the intervening air—hovers for four seconds, and snaps back to its original altitude with the dry *clack* of a relay switch.

The air between these clustered operations remains remarkably clear, cold, and empty. You can walk across wide lanes of glistening, jagged floor without brushing a leaf or disturbing a single creature. If your boot interrupts a line of the copper-plumed birds, they do not scatter or startle; they simply appear on the other side of your ankle in mid-stride, their rhythm unbroken by a millisecond. 

High up where the knife-edged cliffs meet the flat gray cloud-lid, rows of sleek, long-necked mammals with wet, seal-like fur sit shoulder to shoulder along a razor-thin ledge. Their heads swivel left forty-five degrees, pause, swivel right forty-five degrees, pause, venting twin plumes of dense white steam from their nostrils on every leftward stroke, like pressure-relief valves venting excess load into the fog.

Nothing accumulates. Nothing wears out. The voltage jumping from the ferns never chars the leaves; the constant waterfall of condensation never deepens the pools; the deafening clatter of bills and pneumatic foliage never echoes beyond the jagged lip of the rock walls, cutting off into dead mountain silence the instant it touches the cloud ceiling. No young are born, no corpses rot under the lush leaves, and the dim, pewter light neither brightens toward noon nor fades toward night. The high stone room simply runs—cold, soaked, immaculate, deafening, and entirely purposeless—locked in an eternal, heavy-industrial equilibrium.
