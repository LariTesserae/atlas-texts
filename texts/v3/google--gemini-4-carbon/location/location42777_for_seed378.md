The basin sits eight thousand feet up, cupped in the worn collar of an ancient caldera whose rim has long since slumped into smooth, rounded shoulders of grey basalt. From one side of the valley to the other is perhaps twelve miles, and nearly all of it is under an inch or two of scalding water. 

There is no shade, and there is nothing green. The sky has the thin,bleached cobalt clarity of high altitude, pouring down a relentless glare that hits the flooded flats and bounces straight back up, doubling the light. Everything is lit from beneath as much as from above. Shadows don't fall dark here; they glow with reflected heat and pale gold bounce-light under the bellies of pipes, beneath the stilts of buildings, and under the bodies of the creatures wading across the flats.

The ground is travertine and sintered silica, laid down over millennia in broad, shallow terraces that step downward by fractions of an inch over hundreds of yards. Water wells up continuously from fissures along the basin’s margins—not in violent geysers, which eroded out their throats ages ago, but in wide, steady, near-boiling upwellings. It sheets across the stone, cooling from blistering to merely hot as it spreads, depositing smooth rinds of cream, rust, and pale apricot mineral. Where rocks poke above the flow, they weep constantly, slick with condensation and capillary creep, their edges rounded off like river soap. Not a blade of grass, moss, or scrub survives the combination of alkalinity and heat; the closest thing to flora is a leathery, pale-pink biofilm that skins over the slightly cooler eddies, tough as wet parchment and slick underfoot.

Across this blinding mirror, an industrial town has been laid out at a vast, unhurried spacing. 

Because the ground cannot be dug without striking scalding brine, the settlement stands entirely above the grade, stretched out over miles so that no two structures crowd the fragile mineral crust. Squat, windowless residential blocks made ofglazed ceramic brick sit three hundred yards apart on thick cast-iron pilings. Between them run elevated causeways of perforated steel, high-tension electrical lines sagging slightly in the ferocious heat, and miles of copper and stoneware viaducts carrying mineral slurries from one processing station to the next. Farther out, tall distillation towers and cylindrical heat-exchangers stand isolated in the silver sheets of water like chess pieces left on a flooded board. 

Everything is working, and almost none of it makes a sound.

The silence is the heaviest presence in the valley. The high, thin air is poor at carrying vibration to begin with, and the technology here contains no combustion, no striking hammers, no venting steam-whistles. The industry runs on thermal differentials and gravity. Inside the substations, massive Stirling rotors and Peltier banks drink the temperature gap between the boiling upwellings and the cold basalt bedrock deep below, spinning heavy flywheels sealed in baths of viscous oil. Fluids move through the miles of piping via laminar-flow siphons and slow, peristaltic rubber bladders that expand and contract like lungs. Overhead, flat-bed utility trams glide along greased monorails, pulled by electromagnetic induction, passing from station to station with nothing louder than the faint, silky *shhh* of displaced hot air. Even the water doesn't babble; the terraces are so weathered and shallow in their drop that the brine slips from shelf to shelf in unbroken, glassy skins.

If you stand on a walkway and hold your breath, all you hear is the microscopic, fizzy prickle of minerals precipitating onto stone, and the occasional soft *pat* of condensation dropping from the shaded underside of an insulated conduit.

Yet the valley is teeming with movement. 

Life has crowded into the intersection of hot brine, bright sun, and silent machinery in staggering numbers, entirely devoid of voice. Across the flooded flats, thousands of long-legged waders move in loose, drifting constellations. They are the size of dogs, radial in symmetry—five jointed, calcified stilts supporting a flattened, translucent dome of flesh that pulses slowly to pump cooling blood against the glare. They step delicately through the steaming inches of water, grazing on the pink microbial mats with rasping mouths located on their undersides, leaving winding trails of swept-clean white stone behind them that fade within hours as the biofilm grows back.

Around the cast-iron pilings of the town and the bases of the distillation towers, the density of creatures thickens. Heavy, segmented things shaped like flattened copper shields—two feet wide and slick with condensed moisture—haul themselves out of the hotter outflows to cluster directly on the town’s radiator fins. They are heat-thieves or heat-shedders, nobody seems to mind which; they clamp onto the warm metal by the dozens, overlapping like scales, motionless for twenty minutes at a time before unlatching and sliding seamlessly back into the water without a splash. 

Higher up, riding the invisible, blistering thermals that peel off the sunlit rooftops and dark solar-collector arrays, flocks of pale, membrane-winged gliders wheel in tight, silent spirals. They have no feathers to rustle—only taut, wet-looking skin stretched over hollow cartilage, translucent enough that when they pass across the sun they look like drifting scraps of oiled paper. They never cry out. To signal one another across the wide spaces between towers, patches of chromatophores along their flanks flash in sudden, sharp ripples of ultraviolet and metallic blue—whole flocks blinking silent semaphore against the bleached sky before diving down to snatch up the soft, eyeless brine-slugs that congregate along the edges of the town's settling vats.

Down in the flooded plazas—open squares framed by distant transformer housings and marked with enameled municipal pylons—smaller life boils quietly in the warm shallows. Translucent, ribbon-like swimmers undulate in dense, braided currents along the submerged foundations, drawn to the faint DC magnetic fields leaking from buried grounding rods. Predatory arthropods with long, scissor-like forelimbs stalk the edges of these ribbons, their footfalls entirely cushioned by pads of spongy silica-flesh, striking beneath the water surface with rapid, hydraulic snaps that leave only widening rings of blinding light on the water.

The town and the basin have been together long enough to share an anatomy. Mineral crust climbs the iron pilings up to the high-water mark, flaring them out into smooth, bell-shaped pedestals of white stone. Old, decommissioned pipes have been left to calcify completely, turning into artificial ridges of travertine that reroute the sheet-flow into new shallow lagoons, which quickly fill with pink mats and five-legged grazers. Automated scraper-gantries inch along the active cooling troughs at a pace barely visible to the eye, shaving away the daily millimeter of mineral buildup with submerged diamond wires, dropping the pale chalky ribbons into the water where swarms of thumb-sized, porcelain-shelled scavengers immediately gather to consume the trace sulfates.

Clouds never quite form inside the caldera; the air is too hot to let the rising moisture gather into mist. Instead, the humidity hangs invisible until nightfall—not here yet, hours away—leaving the afternoon suspended in a state of dazzling, feverish clarity. Far across the flats, three miles off, a maintenance door on an automated pump-house swings open, a dark rectangle in the white glare, and then closes again without a click reaching across the water. A tram slides past on its elevated rail, casting a sharp, gliding reflection across the backs of fifty wading domes, which do not startle, only shift their weight smoothly in the hot current and flash a mute, shimmering ripple of pale violet across their skins as the shadow passes over them.
