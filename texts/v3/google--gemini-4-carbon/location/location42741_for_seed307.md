High on the newly thrust spine of the range, miles above the cloud-deck where the air thins to a dry whisper, a single gas-blister in the dark andesite has cracked open to the sky. The cavity is no larger than a cistern or a modest room. Its walls are young rock, unweathered and viciously sharp—freshly fractured basalt, needles of black glass, and jagged, overlapping plates of cooling slag that have never known frost-wedging or wind-polish. Plain, even daylight falls straight in through the ragged aperture overhead, illuminating every wet angle in crisp, shadowless clarity.

Inside this pocket, the heat is staggering. A hairline vent at the floor breathes up a continuous, invisible plume of geothermal exhaust so hot the air above it wobbles like glycerine. Where that scorching updraft meets the freezing boundary of the high altitude just below the rim, moisture crashes out of the vapor phase all at once. The walls weep scalding water. Heavy, slick sheets of it run continuously down the obsidian facets, gathering in shallow, trembling basins across the uneven floor, steaming faintly before condensing again inches higher. Nothing green grows here. There is no moss, no lichen, no hardy alpine grass—only thin, blistered skins of pale yellow sulfur-slime and rubbery gray mineral-blooms clinging to the submerged ledges where the water runs hottest.

Yet the alcove is packed almost to suffocation with animal life, and it is nearly silent.

At this elevation, the local rules of the world have worn thin, frayed by the raw, unhealed violence of the mountain’s recent upheaval. Acoustics fail first. A pebble dislodged from the rim drops onto a stone shelf and makes no clatter; instead, at the moment of impact, the air around it briefly darkens, absorbing the shockwave as a small, bruise-colored flicker before returning to normal. Water drips from overhangs, hits the pools below, and produces only the faintest, dryest *tick*, like two fingernails touching under a blanket. Even the furious, steaming churn of the vent-fissure registers only as a slight pressure against the eardrums.

Within this muted chamber, thousands of creatures crowd shoulder-to-shell across every slick square inch. There are segmented, knuckle-sized grazers with translucent carapaces that boil in layered heaps along the wet walls, scraping away the gray slime; flat, palm-wide leeches that undulate through the scalding runoff; and coiled, eyeless ambush-worms wedged into every razor-edged cleft. Above and among them move the larger things—lean, pale-fleshed organisms the size of terriers, multi-limbed and jointless-looking, with skin like boiled leather and clusters of black, bead-like eyes arranged around pulsing gill-slits. Dozens of them occupy this tiny room at once, climbing over one another’s backs, slick limbs tangled, constantly jostling for purchase on the knife-sharp ledges.

They build nothing. There are no nests, no woven mats, no stacked cairns, no carved niches. The stone remains entirely raw. Instead, the pale climbers pluck newly spalled flakes of volcanic glass directly from the cooling walls and hold them in their split, dextrous forepaws. 

All across the alcove, in eerie quiet, manual work is underway. One creature braces itself over a steaming puddle, gripping a triangular shard of obsidian between three fleshy digits, and uses it as a chisel—driving the point under the shell of a clustered grazer with brisk, practiced jerks until the armor splits. Another uses a long basalt needle as a skewer, probing deep into a hairline crack to hook a writhing worm. When a flake dulls or snaps against the young stone, the user drops it immediately into the hot wash and snaps a fresh razor off the nearest ridge. The floor of the pocket is carpeted inches deep in discarded, chipped glass and empty shells, shifting slowly under the weight of too many bodies, making no sound at all save a soft, wet hiss.

The brokenness of the place goes deeper than sound, and the animals treat the frayed physics as just another feature of the terrain.

Along the western wall, gravity slips sideways by thirty degrees; water streaming down from the rim suddenly sheets horizontally across the glassy face before dropping again, and clusters of small, hooked scavengers hang sideways off nothing, suspended in the invisible shear-zone while larger predators reach up with stone scrapers to rake them down. In the center of the floor, a shallow basin of scalding water no wider than a dinner plate reflects the sky from several minutes ago—a high, thin smear of cirrus that has already passed overhead—and when one of the pale tool-users reaches into the two-inch puddle, its limb sinks all the way to the shoulder, groping blindly in a deeper, displaced volume of water that isn't geometrically underneath it. It pulls back a thrashing, scaleless thing twice the width of the puddle itself, pins it to the hot rock, and begins methodically sawing through its hide with a jagged stone edge, steam rising off the butchered meat in thick white ribbons.

Near the top of the pocket, where the scalding air meets the cold blue drop of the outside world, space stutters. Drops of condensed moisture hang in stuttering vertical rows, repeating the same inch of fall three or four times before finally splashing onto the rock below. Here, the boldest of the climbers gather in tight, sweating knots. They search the air with outstretched paws until they find a seam—a faint, hair-thin line in midair where the daylight refracts wrong and the rock behind it looks misaligned. 

Working in pairs or threes, silent and frantic, they jam thick wedges of black stone directly into empty space. The wedges hold, biting into nothing, prying the seam open by half an inch. Out of that narrow geometric wound leaks a slow, viscous trickle of colorless fluid that smells sharply of ozone and wet iron, steaming violently as it hits the heat of the chamber. Immediately, the crowd surges forward, shoving and biting in dead silence, lapping at the leakage before the stone wedge splinters under the impossible tension of the air and the seam snaps shut again, dropping the broken rock into the boiling shallow below.

Down in the runoff, smaller things are already picking through the fragments, turning the freshly shattered splinters over in tiny, blistered claws, testing the edges for sharpness. Overhead, the ordinary afternoon sky sits flat and pale above the rim, indifferent, pouring clear light into a steaming cup of razor-stone where nothing speaks, nothing grows, and everything is busy.
