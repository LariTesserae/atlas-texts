The rock here broke recently—not in human days, perhaps, but recently enough that nothing has had time to round off its corners. The entire place is a single, steep-sided sink where a high mountain ridge pulled apart along clean, glassy fractures, leaving a chamber open to the sky no wider than a village green and roughly twice as deep. If you drop a plumb line from the jagged upper lip, it falls past sheer stepped faces of dark, unweathered basalt whose edges are still sharp enough to fray rope. There is no scree at the bottom to soften the angles, only angular slabs pitched against one another with wide, cold gaps of shadow between them.

Light enters strictly from straight overhead, and even at noon it arrives bruised and weak, strained through a permanent ceiling of high-altitude cloud that skims the rim without ever quite dropping down into the shaft. There is no rain, and no stream runs along the floor. Instead, the cold air holds a constant, Measured dampness. Moisture gathers as a gray sweat on the dark stone, beads along stretched fibers, and hangs in the air just short of fog, keeping the whole enclosure at the temperature of a cellar cut into ice-free stone.

Across this open volume stretches a town, though it has none of the bulk usually meant by the word. It is an urban grid reduced entirely to lines, nodes, and intervals, leaving nine-tenths of the air empty. Using split timber hauled from far below, wedged slate cantilevers, and miles of braided sinew and plant-bast cordage, builders have divided the chasm into distinct levels and thoroughfares. Narrow walkways of lashed poles cross from wall to wall with thirty or forty feet of clear, dim air separating one span from the next. There are suspended platforms no bigger than hearth-rugs, tiered racks of notched bone, wickerwork cisterns lined with pitch, and vertical ladders of horn rungs pegged into raw fissures in the rock. Everything shows the mark of chipped stone blades, bone awls, and hand-twisted twine—fresh pale wood shavings still cling to damp ledges where a strut was recently replaced—yet the layout is unmistakably civic. Main arteries of double-braided rope converge on central sorting platforms; slender wooden gutters run at precise, surveyed gradients from one wall-terrace to another to route collected condensation; tiny woven shelters hang at regular, disciplined intervals like lanterns in a vast dark hall.

The vegetation follows the same rule of wide spacing and extravagant growth. Because light is scarce and soil nonexistent, the plants do not crowd together in tangled thickets; they station themselves yards apart on the walls and rigging, giving each individual specimen a wide berth of empty cold air, and from those isolated anchors they grow immense and lush. Great translucent curtains of pale-green liverwort, heavy and slick as oiled leather, hang twenty feet straight down from the underside of the walkways, slowly curling and uncurling in unseen thermal drafts. Out of cracks in the sheer basalt spring slender, woody stalks no thicker than a thumb that reach horizontally into the void before bursting into broad, velvety rosettes the size of cartwheels, dark purple on top to drink the dim light and silvery-wet underneath. Other plants float upward: anchored to the floor boulders by hair-thin black runners, gas-filled bladders of mottled chartreuse skin bob at the ends of forty-foot tethers, swaying gently in the middle air between the bridges like tethered moons, spaced far enough apart that they never touch.

What fills this spare, damp architecture—what turns every thin rope, broad leaf, and sharp rock-face into a moving surface—is the animal life, which operates at a pitch of near-total silence.

Every structural line in the town doubles as a highway. Along the taut guy-ropes and wooden gutters flows a continuous, two-way procession of pale, soft-bodied creatures the size of human hands, shaped somewhat like flattish sea-slugs with fringes of feathery external gills along their flanks. They glide over a thin film of condensation without making a sound, passing over and around one another in dense, braided streams. Where two ropes cross, hundreds of them gather in slow, pulsing knots before sorting themselves onto new vectors. 

Look closer at the dark basalt walls between the spaced-out terraces, and what first reads as wet stone resolves into overlapping carpets of long-legged, radial crawlers—eight-limbed things with tiny central bodies no larger than berries and hair-fine legs spanning a foot across. Millions of them stand hub-to-hub on the vertical rock, swaying in slow, synchronized waves to comb microscopic water droplets and drifting spores out of the air. When a piece of loose stone flakes off a ledge, the carpet parts instantly and silently to let it pass, then closes back over the fresh scar before the shard has hit the bottom.

Through the wide, empty spaces between the hanging shelters and floating bladders, larger forms are constantly crossing. Translucent, boneless gliders—ribbons of muscular membrane bordered with rows of dark, pinprick eyes—launch themselves from the upper slate cantilevers and plane downward in wide, lazy spirals. They have no wings to beat; they steer by rippling their edges, hunting smaller, buoyant drift-larvae that rise continually from the damp clefts in the floor like reverse snow. Predation happens in midair without a snap of jaws or a flutter of panic: a glider simply overtakes a cluster of rising white motes, folds itself briefly into a pouch around them, and smooths flat again before landing with a faint, wet *slap*—almost the loudest sound the chasm ever produces—against the underside of a broad purple leaf, which dips under the weight and slowly rebounds.

Even the town’s primitive machinery is half-biological, overrun and driven by the fauna at once. The woven cisterns are coated on the outside with clusters of pulsing, shelled filter-feeders that drink seepage through the wicker and sweat clean water back onto the catch-trays below. Along the wooden walkways, colonies of velvet-furred, blind quadrupeds no longer than a finger huddle in dense, warm heaps around the lashed joints where wood meets stone, chewing away encroaching rot-fungi while larger, stork-legged amphibians step delicately over their backs on padded, suction-cupped toes, plucking individuals from the pile with rapid, noiseless tongues.

Nothing calls out. Nothing has chitin hard enough to click, or lungs built for barking or song, and the rock walls are too close and damp to carry echoes anyway. The air holds only a faint, pervasive texture of tiny movements: the microscopic hiss of millions of soft feet shifting on wet stone, the occasional heavy *drip* of collected cloud-water falling thirty feet onto a stretched hide collector, and the dry creak of a sinew cable tightening as a hundred pounds of pale, gill-fringed grazers migrate simultaneously from the east wall to the west.

When the town's builders move through the grid—only two or three visible at a time across the whole height of the shaft, wrapped in oiled skins and stepping barefoot along the narrow poles with stone scrapers and coils of fresh cord—they move at the exact tempo of the chasm. They pause to let a living wave of radial crawlers clear a ladder rung, scrape a surplus of gelatinous egg-masses into woven baskets, retie a frayed lashing with quick, practiced knots, and pass into one of the small hanging shelters, leaving the vast, dim intervals of air between the ropes to the drifting membranes and the slow, ceaseless traffic of the walls.
