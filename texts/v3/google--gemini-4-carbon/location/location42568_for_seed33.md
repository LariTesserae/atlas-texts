The air at this altitude is thin, cold, and smells faintly of bruised flint. It holds no moisture at all. If you breathe with your mouth open, the back of your throat dries to paper in seconds. 

You are standing near the upper rim of an abyssal drop, where a mountain range has been pared down by ages of dry wind into blades and needles of dark grey slate and pale chalk. The scale is vertiginous. Across a gulf five miles wide, the opposing face rises another several thousand feet before vanishing into a ceiling of bruised, plum-colored cloud that never produces rain. Everything sits in a perpetual, shadowless twilight, as if seen through smoked glass. There is no visible sun, only a diffuse, pewter luminescence leaking unevenly from the high mist—brighter in the troughs than on the peaks, though there is nothing down in the troughs to cast light upward.

Spanning this vast, empty volume is a city. Yet there isn't a single nail, hinge, gear, kiln-fired brick, or cut timber anywhere within it. 

The city is built of dry-stacked stone—billions of flat, grey talus shards fitted together by pressure and counterbalance alone—and it does not cling solely to the cliff faces. It steps right off the rock into the empty air. Great, sweeping causeways no thicker than a man's torso arc across miles of sheer drop without piers beneath them. Plazas the size of town squares hover half a mile out from the mountain wall, tethered by nothing more than slender, braided ribbons of stacked basalt that bow upward instead of sagging. Whole districts of hollow, wind-scoured towers hang upside down from the undersides of cantilevered stone terraces, their dry-laid masonry held snugly in place toward a local downward that belongs only to them.

The architecture is dense in layout—streets, stepped amphitheaters, switchback stairs, watchtowers, narrow alleys, bridged courtyards—yet almost entirely devoid of mass. Every wall is built as an open latticework, more hole than stone, like dry bone marrow or lace made of slate. You can look straight through seven blocks of tenements and still see the pale chalk streaks on the mountain five miles beyond. The emptiness swallows the structure; the city occupies the canyon the way a cobweb occupies an empty barn.

And the city is constantly, silently rearranging itself.

What passes for weather here is a tide of shifting gravity and dry electrostatic wind. You cannot see the fronts coming except by what they do to the dust and the buildings. A broad current will sweep up from the unseen floor of the gulf, cool and brisk, and as it passes through an overhanging district, the local rules of weight tilt thirty degrees to the east. A causeway does not collapse; instead, thousands of loose, dry-fitted stones lift quietly from its edges like scales on a pinecone, drift across twenty yards of empty gulf in a disciplined, flat ribbon, and click softly into place along the flank of a neighboring tower, extending a balcony that wasn't there five minutes ago. 

Because the air is so thin, these massive structural migrations make almost no noise. A whole courtyard can unpeel itself stone by stone and reassemble forty feet higher up with no more racket than a handful of dry beans poured onto a wool blanket—a muted, papery *tck-tck-shhh* that dies twenty paces away. The ancient erosion of the place comes from this ceaseless recycling. Every shard of slate has been fitted, lifted, tumbled through the dim air, and refitted so many millions of times that there are no sharp edges left anywhere. Every building stone is smoothed to the shape of a flat river pebble, polished by dry air and endless contact with its neighbors, powdered in pale dust.

Across the undersides of the arches and inside the hollow lattices grows the only stationary life: flat, black crusts of mineral-eating lichen or something simpler—dry, brittle sheets like burnt paint that drink static charge rather than water. When a gravitational front sweeps through and strips a wall away to rebuild it elsewhere, the black crust shatters into fine soot that hangs behind in the air, preserving the exact ghost-outline of the missing room for several minutes before the crosswinds pull it apart into calligraphy.

Through this shifting geometry move the inhabitants—or the scavengers, or the builders; it is impossible to tell which came first.

They are rare, spaced miles apart in the vast dimness, and completely adapted to an architecture that never stops turning inside out. High overhead, clinging to the vertical face of an inverted plaza, something the size of a hound but built like a flattened, dry husk of cartilage and grey felt detaches one of its six limbs, reaches across a yard of empty air where down is suddenly sideways, and catches a drifting ribbon of migrating floor-stones. It rides the stone stream across the gulf without moving a muscle, motionless as a dried leaf, letting the reorganizing street carry it half a mile toward a patch of black static-crust. 

Farther down in the twilight, deeper in the canyon where perspectives cease to agree—where two staircases run parallel to one another yet seem to descend in opposite directions—larger shapes move at a slow, deliberate pace. Tall, spindly things with jointed limbs like bundled slate needles walk calmly off the ends of unfinished bridges. They do not fall. They drop ten feet, hit an invisible seam in the canyon’s air, pivot ninety degrees until they are walking upright on the empty vertical plane alongside a wall, and casually pluck a smaller glider out of a rising column of chalk dust. There is no cry when the predator takes its meal; only a brief puff of grey powder and the faint, dry snap of brittle shell, swallowed instantly by the five-mile gulf.

Stand on a rim-side terrace long enough and you feel the dynamic working through your own boots. The cold stone floor beneath you thrills subtly, not vibrating, just loosening its grip on the mountain. A pebble near your toe slides uphill, pauses, turns on its edge, and waits. Two hundred yards out in the purple gloom, an entire empty avenue slowly folds along its centerline like the page of a book closing, shutting off the view of the opposite peak, while half a mile below, a dry cataract of silver talus pours smoothly upward out of the dark to build a new spire in the middle of nothing at all.
