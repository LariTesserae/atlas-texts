The basin sits a mile above the sea, so old that its surrounding mountains have worn down into smooth, whale-backed ridges of dark basalt and ochre clay. There are no jagged peaks left here, no sharp scree slopes or sudden precipices. Everything has slumped and settled into long, sweeping curves that stretch twenty miles from rim to rim. Yet this quiet, exhausted geology is entirely overlaid by the geometry of a sprawling, low-density city—an urban expanse that does not crowd together in towers, but spreads outward across the wet, warm plateau in vast, deliberate intervals. 

The ground is perpetually saturated. Warm water seeps up from deep thermal aquifers, not in violent geysers, but in a slow, lukewarm sweat that slicks the broad flagstone plazas and fills the shallow, stepped retention basins that divide the city's districts. Even under a clear, bright sky—the high-altitude sun casting sharp, normal shadows—everything glistens. Water sheets silently down the sides of cyclopean retaining walls, stained rust-red and mineral-white from centuries of continuous weeping. Between the paved avenues, the bare earth is a dark, heavy mud that smells faintly of iron and warm chalk.

What dominates the basin, before the eye even registers the scale of the infrastructure, is the sound. 

It is a continuous, deafening roar, physical and inescapable, vibrating through the soles of boots and settling in the chest. It comes from the Siphons—hundreds of immense, cast-iron and masonry towers spaced half a mile apart across the entire valley floor. Each tower is an industrial intake, drawing the heavy, moisture-laden air and subterranean steam down into subterranean turbines, or perhaps venting deep-earth pressure upward through fluted bronze baffles. The machinery turns at a glacial pace; great exterior flywheels, forty feet in diameter and slick with condensation, complete a single rotation once every two minutes. Yet the sheer volume of air and water moving through the subterranean labyrinth generates a colossal, resonant chord. It is not the frantic clatter of a factory floor, but a monumental, unchanging bass note mixed with the white noise of a massive cataract. People here—if any are out on the wide, empty causeways—do not speak outdoors. The air belongs entirely to the acoustic weight of the infrastructure.

Because the city is so spread out, walking from one structure to the next takes twenty minutes along raised viaducts of vitrified brick. Between the buildings lie wide expanses of drowned pavement, shallow reflecting pools only an inch deep, and open gravel flats where high-tension cables droop between oxidized steel pylons. The architecture is layered and anciently industrial. New riveted iron conduits are bolted directly into smooth, water-worn stone foundations whose original mortar dissolved and re-calcified centuries ago. Boiler houses the size of cathedrals sit alone in acres of wet silt, their arched windows unglazed, letting the warm wind blow straight through arrays of sweating copper pipes. Everything built has been here so long that erosion has claimed the city just as it claimed the hills; iron stairways have rusted into rounded, organic-looking ledges, and brick corners are rubbed smooth by centuries of blowing mist.

Life has only a tenuous foothold in this mineral warmth. There are no trees, no grasses, no soft green mats to absorb the sound. Only in the warm leeward crevices of the exhaust vents, where mineral-rich water pools in the carved gutters, do stiff, translucent-stemmed reeds grow in sparse, deliberate clumps. Patches of tough, mustard-colored lichen encrust the sunny sides of the iron flywheels, riding them slowly up into the glare and down into the splash-pits, hour after hour. 

Fauna is just as scarce, adapted entirely to the roar and the wet heat. Occasionally, a pale, flat-bodied crab the size of a dinner plate pulls itself out of a warm drainage canal to bask on a sun-heated transformer casing, its shell vibrating slightly with the hum of the current inside. High up in the latticework of the transmission towers, solitary, heavy-winged birds with slick, oil-dark feathers sit motionless for hours, watching the shallow water below for the blind, slow-moving eels that navigate the flooded street-grids.

The strangeness of the valley lies in its profound lack of urgency despite the overwhelming power on display. In most industrial landscapes, noise implies speed—pistons firing, trains rushing, smoke billowing. Here, the deafening volume accompanies near-total stillness. Plumes of white vapor rise from the masonry stacks, hit a thermal inversion layer five hundred feet up, and flatten out into slow-drifting, discrete clouds whose shadows crawl lazily across the miles of wet stone and spaced-out roofs. A sluice gate at the edge of a terraced reservoir might take six hours to winch open a single foot, its greased chains groaning at a pitch too low to hear beneath the overarching roar of the Siphons. 

As afternoon wears on, the clear highland light turns amber, catching on the millions of water droplets clinging to rivets, cables, and smooth basalt boulders alike. The entire wide valley shines like wet slate. The warm air smells of ozone, wet rust, and clean steam. Nothing hurries; water trickles down ancient grooves worn into iron plates, the giant wheels inch around their axles, and the city sits in its vast, deafening equilibrium, roaring steadily at the empty sky.
