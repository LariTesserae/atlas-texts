You hear the basin miles before you descend into it, and at first you mistake the sound for hydrology—some immense, unseen cataract grinding boulders in a gorge. Closer, the roar separates into trillions of discrete percussions: dry clicks, wet snaps, rasping plates, the high, glassy ring of rubbed chitin, and beneath it all a low pneumatic wheeze, as of countless small bellows pumping cold air. It is loud enough to vibrate in the teeth. Drop a stone here and you will see it bounce, if your eyes have adjusted to the gloom, but you will never hear it hit.

There is almost no light. Above the broad, shallow valley hangs a permanent ceiling of dense stratus, dark as oiled slate, holding out whatever weak sun or moon exists above it. What illumination reaches the floor is a bruisey, sourceless grey—just enough to show that the valley stretches out for miles in low, slack swells, free of cliffs or sharp ridges. Everything geological here is old and settled. Long ages of wind and intermittent seepage have planed the dark shale and limestone into whalebacks, shallow saucers, and smooth, meandering runnels. Nothing juts. Even the boulders have been licked down to low lozenges.

Nothing grows here, either. There is not a blade of grass, not a shrub, not a crust of lichen or a thread of pale cave-moss. Yet there is almost nowhere to set a foot without crushing something alive.

What looks in the murk like gravel shivers and parts when approached. What looks like the fluted ribbing of weathered bedrock turns out to be tier upon tier of sessile, calcified tubes, cemented to the stone and to one another, leaning into the valley’s steady draft with dark, feathery fans extended. The density is marine, though there is no sea—as if an abyssal trench had been drained of water while keeping all its crowding, its blind hunger, and its indifference to light.

Water arrives not mainly as rain, though a cold drizzle sometimes drifts across the flats for an hour and stops, leaving no streams behind. Mostly it comes as condensation. Cold air pools in the low basin, meeting stone that holds a faint, deep-seated mineral warmth, and every surface sweats. Moisture beads on shells, gathers in the smooth stone saucers into puddles no deeper than a thumb, and runs in thin, slick films down the flanks of the whalebacks. Mixed with fine dust settling endlessly out of the heavy sky and mineral salts leached from the shale, this dampness feeds a grey, tallowy biofilm—barely a biology at all, more a greasy tarnish on the rock.

Everything else in the valley eats that grease, or eats what eats it.

Across the stone flats move continuous, overlapping carpets of grazers: flat, segmented things the size of thumbnails and dinner plates alike, clicking forward on dozens of brittle legs, scraping the wet stone with radulae hard enough to score iron. Over millennia, their mouths and feet have contributed as much to the valley’s erosion as the weather; the shallow troughs winding between the rock swells are polished to a dull satin by fifty million bellies passing a day. Where two grazing shoals meet, they do not stop—they climb over one another three and four layers deep, a churning cobble of cold shells grinding together with a noise like shoveling coal.

Above and through them wade the larger forms. Spindle-legged striders—all joints and long, trembling feelers, their bodies no bigger than a fist held six feet in the air—pick their way across the living ground, stabbing downward whenever a shell cracks in the crush. Slower, heavier shapes bulldoze straight through the mats: pale, muscular slugs the bulk of calves, armored along their backs with shingled plates they steal from dead grazers and glue to their own hides with grey mucus. They leave behind brief lanes of bare, gleaming rock a yard wide, which the surrounding carpet of small bodies closes over again in seconds, like dark water healing behind a keel.

Even the air is thick up to twenty feet off the ground. In the near-dark, vision is useless, so the valley sees with sound. Millions of leathery, multi-winged flyers—some small as moths, others heavy and trailing long, weighted tail-whips—beat back and forth along the contours of the land, screaming continuously at pitches that cross the upper edge of hearing and spill down into audible shrieks and buzzes. The sessile tube-reefs click back to confuse them. Predators hunched in the shallow water-pans clap resonant, hollow claws together to stun passing flyers right out of the mist. The whole open expanse of the basin is an acoustic storm, a dense architecture of sonar pings, jamming trills, mating stridulations, and warning rattles echoing off the low cloud ceiling so that the sky itself seems to chatter.

The air smells sharply of wet slate, iodine, ammonia, and the metallic tang of crushed shell. It is cool—the kind of damp, steady chill that numbs exposed skin—except directly downwind of the largest mounds, where thousands of bodies knotted together in molting heaps generate a sour, localized warmth that steams faintly into the gloom.

Nothing is static, yet nothing about the valley’s larger form changes. A heavy flyer drops into the carpet, clipped by a sonic snap, and before its wings have finished twitching it is dismantled from underneath, plates carried off to armor a slug, soft parts vanished into a hundred clicking mouths, shell ground down by tomorrow into pale grit that washes into a saucer-pool to mineralize the next slick of grey tallow. Across miles of low, dark stone, the noise never drops, never swells to a climax, never sleeps—an ancient, deafening balance running at full pitch in the cold dark.
