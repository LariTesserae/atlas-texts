The air feels swallowed rather than breathed. It hangs heavy at body temperature and then climbs a few degrees further, saturated to the point where sweat cannot evaporate and simply joins the warm condensation already slicking every surface. Above, there is no sky—only an immense, lightless ceiling of vapor or void so thick that height loses its meaning, though the ground itself clearly occupies the bottom of something: a wide, sunken basin of warm black silt and shallow, sheeted water.

What little light exists has no source in the overhead dark. It comes up faintly through the water itself, a bruiselike, tarnished-copper luminescence trapped beneath two inches of warm brine, just strong enough to show the layout of the city.

It is unmistakably a city, organized along broad avenues, plazas, and terraced districts, yet its buildings stand terribly far apart. Between one structure and the next stretch whole acres of shallow water and glistening mudflat, connected by raised causeways of packed clay and river-smoothed cobble. Nothing here was cut by iron or lifted by crane. There is no glass, no wire, no mortar mixed with ash, no straight edge trued by a plumb line. Every tower, arcade, and multi-story block appears to have been pressed upward by bare hands or deposited layer by layer from mineral-heavy water over thousands of years, then worn back down into sleek, pillowed forms. Walls of dark adobe and porous travertine curve inward like ribs; staircases rise out of the shallows with treads hollowed deep at their centers, as if ten thousand bare feet walked them for centuries before vanishing.

Nothing lives in the water. No insects whine in the wet heat. No frogs call from the culverts, and no fish disturb the coppery sheen of the flooded boulevards. For vegetation, there are only scattered colonies of pale, leafless stalks—thick as wrists and waxy to the touch—rooted at the corners of submerged foundations, and occasional mats of dark, heat-loving moss clinging to the undersides of arches where steam collects.

Yet the city is constantly busy.

Every few minutes, a warm rain falls—not from everywhere at once, and not always downward. Across the wide, empty square at the basin’s center, a curtain of heavy drops lets go from twenty feet up, falling into the standing water with a brief, muffled hiss before stopping abruptly at an invisible boundary, as if blocked by a roof that isn't there. Three hundred yards away, along a colonnade of smooth, salt-streaked pillars, water sheets steadily *up* the stone, gathering from the flooded street, climbing the fluted channels of the columns, and pooling upside down along the vaulted ceiling before dripping back to the floor in slow, heavy beats.

The silence around these movements is profound and slightly pressurized, like the quiet inside an ear held underwater. Sounds do not carry across the open distances the way they should. A collapsing mud-wall half a mile off—giving way after centuries of slow saturation—drops into the lagoon without a splash, while the tiny, wet *tick* of a single drop detaching from an archway three streets away arrives right beside the ear, crisp and intimate.

The architecture itself participates in a slow, tireless circulation. Because the buildings stand so far apart across the open flats, you can watch their proportions shift if you look away and look back. This is not crumbling ruin; it is an established, ongoing metabolism of space. A solitary three-story bathhouse—all rounded lintels and weeping terracotta-colored mud—stands at the end of a long causeway. Walk half the length of that causeway through ankle-deep, bath-warm water, and the bathhouse is no closer, though its empty doorways have turned ninety degrees to face the eastern flats. Look down at the cobbles underfoot: they are ancient, worn satin-smooth by water, arranged in neat radial fans that belong to an intersection, though no cross-street meets the path here. A stone gutter runs alongside the walkway, carrying a brisk, warm current toward higher ground.

Inside the sparse structures, the breakdown of rule and measure thickens. Enter a low doorway barely tall enough to stoop through, and the interior opens onto a courtyard wide enough to hold twenty of the building's own exterior shells, floored in shallow, steaming pools partitioned by low dikes of polished clay. Steps lead down into the water and continue descending well past the level of the basin floor, fading into that dim, submerged copper glow, though from the outside the building sits flat on a two-foot plinth of mud. Water drips from the lintels in uneven rhythms—some drops falling fast and heavy like lead shot, others hanging elongated in the hot air, trembling for half a minute as they descend at the speed of sinking feathers.

Farther out, toward the edges of the basin where the ground rises slightly into broad, benchlike terraces of eroded limestone, stand structures that resemble cisterns and granaries—great bell-shaped domes with dark ocular openings near their tops. Steam breathes out of these openings in slow, regular exhalations, hotter than the surrounding air, smelling faintly of wet slate, boiled roots, and old salt. When the steam clears—not blown by wind, for there is little real wind here, only slow convective drafts rolling across the water—the domes have traded places along the ridge, or else two have merged into a long, windowless aqueduct spanning a dry hollow while water pours silently from one broken end into midair and vanishes before hitting the silt below.

Occasionally, one of the sparse, pale stalks growing near a foundation shudders, draws itself down into the mud, and breaches the surface ten yards away, leaving no ripple between the two points.

Everywhere, water is doing the work of inhabitants. It fills the sunken amphitheaters to the third tier, drains sideways through solid walls without leaving a seam, beads on the smooth clay benches as if someone warm had just stood up from them, and runs in neat, braided rills down the centers of wide avenues where nothing walks. The heat holds steady in the dark, pressing down on miles of spaced-out monuments and empty plazas, keeping the whole vast, worn-down settlement at a low, perpetual simmer—an ancient civic order still running its errands long after forgetting what shape space is supposed to keep.
