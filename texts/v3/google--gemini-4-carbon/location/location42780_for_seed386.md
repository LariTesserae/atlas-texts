At fourteen thousand feet, the air ought to be freezing, and a mile away—beyond the jagged, outward-tilted lip of the rim—it is. Out there, pale cloud-decks shear against bare grey scree. Inside the basin, however, the ground radiates a relentless, smothering heat. 

The landscape is a slumped, five-mile shelf of porous volcanic rock, cupped near the summit of an ancient massif and steeped in its own rising thermal water. There is no snow here, and no alpine scrub. Instead, the rock weeps scalding, mineral-sweet moisture from ten thousand hairline fissures, cooling just enough as it spreads across the terraces to turn the entire high-altitude bowl into a sodden, sweltering conservatory. The air is thin in the lungs—breath comes fast and shallow—yet heavy on the skin, saturated to the point of dripping. Sunlight falls through the thin upper atmosphere with stark, unfiltered clarity, throwing crisp shadows across glistening foliage before dissolving into pockets of low, drifting ground-steam.

Erosion has had ages to settle the basin into broad, stepped contours. There are no raw landslides or fresh lava flows; the basalt has been softened into rounded bolsters, shallow runnels, and wide, brimming pans lined with ochre silt and velvety green algae. Water is constantly in motion, though rarely in a hurry. It sheets silently over mossy stone lips, collects in knee-deep bog-meadows, and beads endlessly on the undersides of leaves. Every surface you touch is warm and slick, coated in a thin mucilage of thermophilic bacteria or padded with dense, saturated turf that yields six inches underfoot and immediately wells up with bath-hot water.

Because the air is both starved of oxygen and fiercely hot, the lushness here has a peculiar, muscular density. Trees do not grow tall—the wind shear near the upper ridges sees to that—so the canopy compresses itself into a tangled, chest-to-overhead-high thicket of sprawling, thick-trunked shrubs, giant bladder-ferns, and fleshy bromeliads the size of wheelbarrows. Leaves are rubbery, swollen with water, their surfaces grooved to channel incessant condensation down toward shallow, matting root systems. Pale, leafless orchids hang in heavy curtains from the undersides of leaning trunks. Beneath them, smooth-shelled, platter-sized snails slide over warm stone, and flocks of small, iridescent, flightless rails pick rapidly through the wet sedge, snapping up steam-flies and translucent amphipods that swim in the film of water covering the rock. Larger shapes move through the heat-haze of the middle distance: broad-backed, tapir-like browsers, dark-furred and slick with sweat and condensation, wading belly-deep through the flooded terraces, ripping up whole mats of warm water-weed.

There is not a single road, fence, foundation, or shed anywhere in the basin. No poured concrete, no cut timber, no pylons bolted into the stone. Yet the valley is heavily worked.

Half a mile down the middle terrace, partially obscured by plumes of white vapor and shoulder-high fern brakes, a sixty-ton thermal harvester inches across a flooded pan. It moves on six bulbous, articulated bladder-wheels of matte black vulcanized rubber—wide and soft enough to roll directly over the bog-mats and fleshy shrubs without tearing the root-skin. Behind it, the pressed ferns slowly right themselves, dripping, within twenty minutes of its passage. Three more rigs are visible across the miles-wide expanse of the shelf, spaced far apart like grazing cattle: massive, hull-sealed engines of brushed alloy, copper heat-exchangers, and articulated hydraulic booms. 

They make almost no sound. Engineered for hydraulic and magnetic-drive operation, their internal turbines are buried deep inside baffled, water-jacketed hulls. Up close, all you hear from one is a low, rhythmic pneumatic sigh—like a large animal exhaling through wet nostrils—followed by the soft, oily click of heavy valves cycling, and the slush of warm mire parting under its rollers. Long, segmented proboscis-pipes lower from their bellies into the deeper, hotter mineral vents, drawing up brines rich in lithium and dissolved salts, flashing the excess steam back into internal condensers, and storing the dense slurry in bloated dorsal tanks. Thin films of green moss and orange mineral scale already coat the lower chassis of the older machines; small pink frogs sit unbothered on their warm wheel-housings, riding them fifty yards across a marsh before hopping off into a clump of pitcher-plants.

Overall, the basin holds an eerie, cushioned quiet. The thick, wet vegetation swallows sound before it can carry across the open tiers, and the thinness of the high mountain air deadens whatever remains. There is no roar of waterfalls, only a ubiquitous, low-register murmur: millions of heavy droplets pattering onto waxy leaves, the constant *shhh-shhh* of warm water slipping over rounded stone ledges, the occasional wet snap of a browser breaking a succulent stem, and, from far across the sunlit green expanse, the faint, low hum of an electromagnetic pump spooling down.

Overhead, the sky is the deep, hard cobalt of extreme altitude. When a machine reaches capacity near the center of the shelf, a silvered, heavy-lift aerostat drifts silently down out of that bright blue—unmanned, winching a thick umbilical cable straight into the rising steam. There is a muffled metallic clack as the coupling locks, ten minutes of quiet fluid transfer while flocks of tiny yellow finches dart around the taut cable snapping at disturbed midges, and then the bag rises again, shrinking into the glare until it clears the dry, freezing rock of the outer rim and vanishes over the edge of the mountain, leaving the steaming green bowl entirely to itself.
