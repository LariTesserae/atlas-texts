There is no bedrock left here. If there were ever peaks, ridges, or granite bones holding up this plateau, three billion years of freezing rain and high-altitude gale have ground them down into a slurry long since swallowed by the roots. What remains is a flattened, miles-wide expanse sitting high in the thin, frigid upper atmosphere, composed entirely of deep, living, waterlogged turf. 

To look across it is to look across an immense, dim emptiness. There are no trees, no shrubs, no vertical obstacles to break the wind—nothing stands higher than an ankle, preserving a vast, desolate openness that stretches until the grey mist swallows the horizon. Yet the ground itself is total vegetation. Walking on it—if anything with two legs tried—would feel like treading on the taut, freezing skin of a colossal, water-filled drum. Beneath the surface mat of silver-green sedge and tough, rubbery lichen, the world is miles deep in woven root-felt, compacted peat, and circulating ice-water. 

Light doesn't fall from the sky so much as bruise through it. Heavy, low-hanging banks of cloud drag their bellies right across the turf, condensing immediately onto the cold fibers of the plants. Everything weeps. Every millimeter of foliage is beaded with near-freezing water, slick and gleaming weakly in the perpetual twilight. 

Despite the lack of a single brick, wire, or forged nail, the plateau sounds like a massive, poorly lubricated stamping mill operating at frantic capacity. 

The ground is violently, restlessly mechanical—not by human hand, because no one has ever built anything here, nor could they—but through a profound, localized rotting of physical law coupled with hyperactive botany. Beneath the flat surface, vast bladders of root-tissue rhythmically inhale and compress the bog water, acting as biological pistons. Every few seconds, miles-long veins of hardened, lignified moss constrict simultaneously, firing millions of gallons of black, tannin-rich slush laterally beneath the turf. You can see the shockwaves travel across the open plain: sudden, ruler-straight ridges rising three feet out of the flat wet mat and racing toward the horizon at sixty miles an hour with a deafening, wet *thump-thump-thump*, like a subterranean freight train. 

Steam—cold steam, frigid vapor blasted upward by sheer hydraulic pressure rather than heat—vents from puckered sphagnum valves with the shriek of factory whistles. Patches of black iron-lichen grow in wide, flat spirals across the ground; when the wind hits them right, they spin physically in their mud sockets, rotating like heavy, wet flywheels, throwing off sheets of dirty spray and generating a low, thrumming static charge that smells sharply of ozone and crushed celery.

Where the energy peaks, the geometry of the valley simply gives up. 

Because the landscape is so ancient—worn down past stone, past soil, down to the barest, flattest equilibrium—reality itself seems eroded thin here, worn threadbare like an old wet tarp. When two hydraulic root-waves collide beneath the bog, the surface doesn't just splash; it glitches. An acre of sodden moss will suddenly lift six feet into the freezing air as a flat, dripping slab, hover for three seconds with water pouring upward off its edges into the clouds, and then snap sideways twenty yards with a sound like cracking cast iron, leaving behind a square hole of absolute, starry vacuum that rapidly fills in with rushing bog-water and aggressively knitting roots. 

Distances across the open plain refuse to stay fixed. A puddle of oily, iridescent marsh-slime fifty paces away will abruptly magnify as if viewed through a thick lens, its ripples towering briefly on the horizon before snapping back to scale. Time stutters in the wet turf; in certain hollows, the falling rain hangs suspended in horizontal sheets, vibrating loudly like plucked steel wires until a shift in the wind drops a ton of water onto the moss all at once.

And through all this sparse, wide-open chaos, the animals thrive in frantic, staggering abundance. 

Because the landscape itself is sparse—flat, empty overhead, stripped of cover—the fauna provide the clutter, though they are spread across such a wide, churning plain that they never quite crowd it, moving instead like scattershot across a vast board. None of them seem bothered by the cold, the deafening mechanical roar of the roots, or the tearing of space. In fact, they feed on the industrial violence of the bog.

Flocks of thousands of heavy, flightless, barrel-chested birds—covered not in feathers, but in slick, overlapping plates of pale blubber and stiff bristles—sprint at terrifying speeds atop the racing hydraulic waves, riding the moving ridges of turf across the valley like commuters, plunging long, auger-shaped beaks into the pressurized vents to drink the mineral froth. When a patch of ground skips sideways in space, half a flock will vanish from one coordinate and burst out of the mist a quarter-mile away, never breaking stride, honking in harsh, metallic brass notes that echo across the wet emptiness.

Closer to the sodden mat, millions of segmented, translucent amphibians the size of loaves of bread haul themselves across the spinning lichen-wheels. Their internal organs glow with a faint, cold, electrical blue—charged by the static of the rotating plants. They gather wherever the reality-tears are worst. When a seam opens in the turf and gravity momentarily inverts, clouds of dark, buzzing marsh-gnats and pale, blind eels spill upward out of the ground water into the freezing air; the blue-lit amphibians leap into the inverted rain, snapping wildly, suspended mid-air in thrashing, tangled clusters before gravity slams back into place and drops them all slapping onto the wet moss. 

Further out in the dimness, solitary, long-legged grazers stand twenty feet tall on spindly, jointed stilts made of wet, calcified cartilage. They are spaced widely apart, lonely silhouettes against the grey expanse, keeping the density of the skyline bare and stark. As the ground heaves, pistons hammer beneath their feet, and freezing geysers hiss around their ankles, their knees pump like oil derricks, endlessly compensating for the rolling, glitching floor so their small, eyeless heads remain dead-level in the wind, filtering the blowing mist through baleen grates that clatter like looms.

There is no shelter anywhere. There is no high ground, because everything is already high ground, leveled off at the roof of the world and worn completely flat. The wind comes from every compass point at once, icy and soaked, smelling of peat smoke, grease, old snow, and split stems. Beneath your feet, the great green engine chugs, clicks, vents, and tears at the air—an ancient, endless, living factory floor working frantically under a dark sky to manufacture nothing at all.
