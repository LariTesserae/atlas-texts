There is no soil here, and if there was ever bedrock, it was dissolved epochs ago into a pale, milky silt that never settles. What holds this place up is root-felt: a yard-thick, buoyant mattress of living, interlocked rhizomes, rubbery stolons, and flattened vascular plates that flex underfoot like wet leather stretched over a waterbed. 

The whole world—or at least this enclosed, hamlet-sized basin of it—is an ancient, sunken courtyard no wider than three city blocks, cupped within a low, smooth lip of worn-down greenstone that rises barely twenty feet before giving way to a featureless, warm, white sky. Everything inside the lip is green, soaking wet, and wildly, restlessly alive, yet strangely spacious. You can see all the way from the northern sluice-wall to the southern curve without anything blocking your view. Because the ground itself *is* the forest, compressed and fused into a horizontal architecture, nothing bothers to grow into a clutter of bushes or tangled understory. Instead, the space above the root-mat is wide open, warm, and clear, punctuated only by widely spaced, deliberate structures that are simultaneously municipal infrastructure and giant botanical organs.

Thirty yards apart, rising from the slick, chartreuse floor, stand slender, hollow towers of braided, wood-like xylem. They are engineered things, though no saw or weld ever touched them. Billions of microscopic, programmable symbiotes—ancient tech now gone entirely native in the sap—direct their growth, forcing the plant-matter to extrude into precise, parabolic curves, spiral staircases with self-draining treads, and cantilevered balconies that hang empty over the wet plaza. And they do not stay still. The technology driving them is locked in a permanent, hyperactive state of revision. You can watch a tower’s flank ripple as its vascular bundles unbraid and re-weave to shift the weight of an upper gallery. Across the clearing, an arched aqueduct spanning fifty feet of open air shudders, splits down its length with a sound like a snapping hawser, and peels apart into two twin Causeways in the span of ten minutes, shedding gallons of warm, silvery water onto the turf below. 

Water is the constant medium of this turbulence. It does not rain from the bright, overcast sky; rather, the basin exhales and inhales water through its floor. Every few minutes, alocalized hydrostatic pressure wave rolls beneath the root-mat. You see the green floor swell by two feet in a broad, lazy dome, and then water sweats violently upward through millions of sphinctered pores in the plant-skin, flooding the depression ankles-deep in warm, slightly fizzing liquid that smells of crushed mint and ozone. Just as quickly, twenty yards away, a funnel-shaped bladder-drain dilates in the turf and sucks the sheet of water down with a tremendous, throaty *glug-glug-glug* that echoes off the distant perimeter wall. 

The noise is relentless, a bright, wet, mechanical-organic din. There is the constant slap and rush of shifting water, the groan and creak of wooden towers adjusting their own geometry, and the sharp, percussive *crack* of seed-relays firing across the open spaces. High up on the sparse pylons, translucent pods swell with pressurized gas until they detonate, flinging glittering, metallic-sheathed spores in flat trajectories from one tower to another—an airborne packet-switching network that conducts the city’s forgotten business over the heads of whatever walks below.

And there is plenty walking, skittering, and gliding through the open air between the towers. Because the architecture is so sparse, the fauna are completely exposed, turning the wide green plaza into a frantic, sun-warmed stage. Long-legged, wading creatures that look like skinless egrets made of bundled optic fibers stalk through the fizzing sheet-floods, stabbing their beaks into the root-pores to drink the data-rich sap before the drains can swallow it. Flocks of palm-sized, membranous gliders—half-moth, half-solar-sail—ride the warm thermal updrafts rising off the sweating floor, banking sharply around the shifting balconies in synchronized, shimmering clouds that click like castanets. When a bridge decides to dismantle itself, dropping ribbons of wet, nutrient-heavy pith into the shallow water, dozens of barrel-shaped, six-limbed amphibians haul themselves out of the drainage sumps, barking loudly like seals as they fight over thewarm, glowing debris before the floor reabsorbs it.

Walk toward the center of the basin and the age of the place becomes palpable beneath the frenzy. The perimeter greenstone, where it peeks through the overlapping lip of the root-mat, is worn as smooth as river soap, grooved by ten thousand years of the mat’s slow, rhythmic breathing. There are no ruins here because nothing is allowed to stay dead; anything that breaks is immediately digested and re-extruded. Yet the *pattern* is ancient, settled into a terminal, oscillating equilibrium. This hamlet-sized machine-marsh has lost whatever human inhabitants once walked its self-weaving balconies. The rooms grown high in the sparse towers have doorways proportioned for upright figures, and smooth, waist-high railings that still dutifully grow to catch a leaning elbow, hold that form for an hour while water sheets down their polished surfaces, and then untwist into a tangle of crimson, heat-dumping fronds when no one comes to lean on them.

The light is even, shadowless, and candid—the plain, bright daylight of late morning through high, thin mist. In this clear air, every droplet flung from a snapping vine, every iridescent ripple on the flooded green skin of the floor, and every frantic dash of the glass-boned waders stands out in crisp, isolated relief. Beneath your boots, the ground gives a sudden, warm heave; a hundred yards away, a spiral spire sighs, untwists ninety degrees to face the wind, and dumps a barrel of warm, sweet water from its crown, sending a flock of clicking gliders scattering across the open green room.
