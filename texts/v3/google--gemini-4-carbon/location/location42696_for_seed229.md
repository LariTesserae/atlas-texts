High up on the pale, chalk-white spine of a dry massif, the rock has been hollowed out by aeons of scouring wind into a deep, bowl-shaped amphitheater—a sunken pocket no wider than a cathedral nave. Around its jagged rim, the sky is a bruised, dusty violet, choked with high-altitude mineral haze that swallows the sun and reduces the light to a permanent, glowing copper dusk. There is no rain here. There has never been water in this stone. Yet the amphitheater is furiously, violently green-and-gold.

The bowl sits directly over a dry, warm fumarole—a vent exhaling a continuous, erratic gale of warm sulfur-rich gas, airborne silicates, and electrostatic charge from deep within the mountain’s crust. 

Because the rock floor is porous and treacherous, nothing grows close together. Perhaps only two dozen primary stalks exist within the entire sunken grove, spaced generously across the smooth, wind-dished floor so that wide arenas of swept white stone gleam between them. Yet these few plants are monstrously lush, overflowing their sparse allotments to fill the air above. Rooted miles deep into the dry, warm marrow of the mountain, they drink raw mineral dust and thermal kinetic energy instead of water. Their trunks are supple, metallic-looking reeds as thick as pillars, endlessly whipping and bowing in the turbulent updrafts. From these sparse columns burst colossal, hyper-dense canopies of dry, papery fronds, ribbon-leaves ten yards long, and swollen, translucent bladders filled with buoyant gases. 

The air inside the pocket is never still. The thermal vents beneath the stone fire in unpredictable, stuttering rhythms, turning the grove into a dry whirlpool. And because everything here is bone-dry, the chaos is deafening. 

The sound is a relentless, dry percussion: millions of crisp, silica-stiffened leaves clattering together like shaken sheet metal and split bamboo. Great hollow seed-pods knock against stone with the booming resonance of wooden drums. Every sudden gust strips away dead, golden-brown husks and sends them spiraling upward in frantic cyclones before the heavier mineral dust drags them back down. And beneath the clatter is the crackle. The constant friction of dry leaves thrumming in dry wind builds immense static charges; thin, hair-like filaments of blue-white electricity constantly snap and jump across the wide gaps between the sparse trees, filling the dim copper air with the sharp smell of ozone and toasted flint.

To live here is to ride the turbulence, and the bowl is teeming with creatures built like kites, sails, and grappling hooks. 

Because the ground between the huge stalks is exposed to sudden, blistering shifts in the thermal draft, the stone floor is mostly left empty, polished to a matte sheen by millennia of blowing grit. The abundant life stays aloft or clings to the whipping flora. Flocks of flat, ribless creatures—little more than muscular blankets of velvety, rust-colored hide—ride the boiling updrafts in dizzying, chaotic spirals, snapping up static-charged dust-mites and floating spores. Along the thrashing ribbon-branches, six-limbed, chitinous climbers with hooked, wooden claws clamber at frantic speeds. They have no fluid eyes to dry out; instead, their heads are crowned with feathery, copper-veined antennae that read the shifting electrical fields of the grove. They communicate over the roar of the wind by scraping serrated plates along their flanks, adding a piercing, rhythmic rasping—like heavy saws biting into dry gourd—to the thunder of the foliage. When a violent downdraft hits, hundreds of brittle-shelled gliders detach from the underside of the gas-bladders all at once, exploding outward like shrapnel across the empty spaces of the bowl to catch a calmer eddy near the stone walls.

In the lee of the amphitheater’s curved limestone walls, and wedged into the deep, dry crevices between the widest roots, are the scattered marks of hands. Whoever comes here—or lives tucked into the mountain’s dry pores—possesses only basic tools of chipped obsidian, bone, and cold-hammered native copper, yet they have learned to harvest the storm.

There are no houses, only thresholds and traps. Low, crescent-shaped windbreaks have been dry-stacked from heavy, flat slabs of stone, worn smooth at the edges as if built generations ago and constantly repaired. Lashed between neighboring rock spires with thick, braided ropes of dried plant-fiber are crude kinetic looms and static-catchers: wooden frames strung with heavy stone weights and rough copper wire that dance, spin, and clack wildly in the gusts. Some seem designed to snare the drifting, nutrient-rich bladders torn loose from the canopy; others channel the constant static sparks down into shallow pits filled with black mineral powder, fusing the dust into brittle, glassy fulgurites that have been partially chipped away by hand-axes. 

Carved directly into the sheltered underside of an overhanging cliff at the bowl's edge are deep, smoothed flutes and narrow tunnels bored into the rock. When the central vent heaves and the wind shifts eastward, the air slams into these hand-carved stone throats and lets out a low, bellowing, three-toned chord—a massive, primitive siren that cuts straight through the brittle roar of the leaves. Whether it is a gauge to measure the fury of the vent, a signal to call harvesters up from the deeper caves, or an instrument meant to herd the rust-colored gliders into fiber nets strung across the clefts, no one is currently tending it. A leather sling and a basket woven from stiff, glassy reeds sit wedged beneath a boulder, weighted down with a heavy cobble of iron ore so the wind won't take them, trembling slightly as the warm, dry gale screams overhead and another volley of blue sparks leaps across the dusky, thrashing canopy.
