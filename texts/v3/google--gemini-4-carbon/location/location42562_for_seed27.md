The ground is a single, unbroken shelf of pale, desiccated root-stone. There is no dirt here, no gravel, no underlying mineral bedrock within a thousand meters of the surface. What stretches across the wide, high basin is the petrified and semi-living vascular mat of an ancient, non-aqueous flora—fibrous, bone-dry, and worn completely smooth by ages of high-altitude wind. Walking across it feels like walking on tightly packed, polished driftwood, cold to the touch and faintly resonant, as though vast, hollow galleries lie beneath the crust.

Above, the sky is the color of bruised pewter, perpetually dim. A weak, distant sun hangs low like an unpolished coin, casting shadows that are long, crisp-edged, and curiously gray. The air is thin and sharp with chill, carrying zero humidity. When the wind drops from the surrounding, flattened ridge-lines, it brings no scent of dampness or decay—nothing rots here, because nothing has the moisture to rot. Instead, the air smells of dry static, crushed chalk, and volatile hydrocarbons, sharp like camphor and cold iron.

Though the flora constitutes the entire mass of the world, it does not crowd the basin. Centuries of fierce, dry scouring have eroded the vertical growth down to its absolute structural minimum, leaving huge expanses of empty, windswept root-floor between towering presences. Far apart—sometimes half a kilometer from one to the next—rise the spires. 

These are at once trees and municipal towers, the distinction erased by technology so old and precise it operates entirely inside the plant's genetic geometry. Originally cultivated by altering the vacuum-hardened lignins of the basin’s native flora, the towers grow as hollow, fluted columns of bone-white and slate-gray wood, seamlessly interlaced with veins of dark, non-reflective metamaterial. Over millennia, the relentless wind has sanded their windward faces into sleek, aerodynamic blades while pulling their leeward sides out into trailing, calcified fins. Wide, sweeping causeways—grown rather than laid, arching fifty meters above the basin floor—span the empty distances between the towers. They are thin as paper, impossibly rigid, and completely lacking guardrails, trusting entirely to subtle, localized gravity-shears woven into the living bark to keep travelers from blowing off into the pale void below.

Inside the towers, and suspended beneath the sweeping arches of the bridges, the urban infrastructure hums without wires or glass. Floors are formed from tensioned membranes of translucent leaf-chitin. Light within the settlements comes not from bulbs, but from slow, cold-burning chemical luminescence shifting beneath the woodgrain—soft bands of amber and ultraviolet drifting across walls like sluggish auroras, tracking the invisible movement of data and dry sap. Doors do not hinge; the rigid cellulose simply parts like woven silk when approached, snapping shut behind with a dry, crisp rattle.

Because everything is dry, hard, and stretched taut under structural tension, the entire valley acts as an immense, resonant soundboard. It is astonishingly loud.

The wind does not whistle here; it roars like bowing a gigantic cello. As the thin, fast air cuts through the perforated crowns of the tower-trees and across the tensioned bridge-spans, it generates deep, harmonic drones that vibrate up through the soles of the feet. Overlaid on this constant bass note is the brittle percussion of the ecosystem itself. Vast, low-lying fields of silver-gray scrub—stiff, glass-like fronds that feed directly on atmospheric static and dim radiation—clatter together in rolling, deafening waves whenever a gust sweeps the plain, sounding like millions of porcelain shards cascading down a chute.

Through this acoustic storm moves an abundant, restless fauna, entirely adapted to a waterless existence. There are no soft skins or wet eyes among them. Flocks of angular, six-winged gliders—built of dry, pleated cartilage and hollow carbon-lattice bones—launch themselves from the upper municipal balconies. They ride the violent updrafts between the sparse towers, hunting clouds of airborne spore-pods. When they dive, their stiff wing-membranes snap like bullwhips, cutting sharp, staccato cracks across the steady drone of the wind.

Down on the open, polished root-plains, tall, long-limbed grazers move in loose, striding herds. They look like mechanisms of articulated ivory and bundled wire, stepping cleanly on needle-sharp hooves that click loudly against the hardened substrate. They do not drink; they scrape up the static-charged mineral dust that collects in the shallow, eroded depressions of the root-floor, digesting it alongside clipped shards of the glass-scrub. Predators stalk them across the wide, exposed spaces without the benefit of cover, relying instead on strangeness of motion: low, multi-jointed things covered in light-drinking black bristles that flatten themselves seamlessly against the long, dim shadows of the afternoon, moving only when the clatter of the scrub reaches a deafening crescendo.

The whole basin is caught in a brisk, cold, relentless rhythm. Sap made of supercooled lipid compounds pumps upward through the tower walls with audible, rhythmic thuds, redistributing heat drawn from deep geothermal taproots miles below the dry crust. Spore-vents high on the urban spires suddenly blow open with the boom of small cannons, firing glittering, metallic-looking seed-flecks miles downwind to repair eroded bridges or establish new structural buttresses. Flocks wheel and scatter around these sudden silvery plumes, snapping up the stray genetic material in mid-air. Above the plain, along the seamless, unrailed causeways, pale figures wrapped in heavy, wind-stiffened cloaks cross from spire to spire, leaning casually at impossible angles into the gale, held steady to the singing wood by forces entirely unseen.
