There is no sand here. Nothing has been ground down yet. Where the bottom drops away from the upper shelves into the middle depths, every ledge is as crisp as the day it cracked open during cooling—black, vitreous basalt fractured into clean hexagons, ropes of frozen lava still glossy with an iridescent mineral skin, shards of volcanic glass sharp enough to part a drifting frond on contact. Even the water holds no silt. When a heavy stone shifts, no cloud rises to obscure it; it simply clicks against the raw floor with a clean, porcelain strike that travels a great distance through the warm gloom.

The whole basin sits miles above the world’s true oceans, cupped inside the jagged collar of a newly risen volcanic massif. Up at the surface—rarely visited, fifty or sixty feet overhead—the air is thin, cold, and choked with stagnant gray cloud that rests directly on the water like a wet wool lid, sealing out the sun. Beneath that bleak ceiling, submerged from rim to rim across a broad, valley-sized caldera, the water is as warm as blood. Heat leaks steadily from the young rock below, not in violent boiling geysers, but through miles of hairline fissures that breathe a slow, syrupy warmth upward, keeping the entire submerged highland at the temperature of a fever.

Light arrives reluctant and bruised. Before it reaches the upper terraces it has already passed through mountain fog and a drifting crust of pale, porous pumice rafts that knock softly together at the surface. What filters down is a dim, sourceless olive-amber twilight, bright enough to show the layering of things within thirty paces, then thickening smoothly into charcoal and deep plum across the wider span of the valley.

In that warm dimness, vegetation crowds every fracture. Because there is no soil, the plants grip the young rock with rubbery, acid-secreting holdfasts that etch their own shallow pits into the glass. Broad, leathery ribbons of rust-red and dark bronze rise twenty feet straight up, held taut by rows of translucent gas-bladders the size of fists. Between them grow denser thickets: branching, woody-stemmed water-shrubs hung with heavy curtains of moss-green filament, and wide, cupped leaves arranged in spirals to catch the faint downward drift of dead plankton and pumice dust. Where the growth is thickest, the plants touch and interlace, turning the open water into a series of green-shadowed rooms and vertical corridors. Nothing whips or thrashes. There are no waves here, and no tides—only the patient, massive rotation of warm water rising from the central clefts and sinking marginally cooler along the outer caldera walls, a current so slow that a shed leaf takes an afternoon to cross a courtyard.

The settlement grows directly out of the stepped basalt shelves, spanning both sides of a deep, water-filled gorge and climbing the flank of a bloated lava dome in tiers. It has the density and deliberate order of a town, though not a single piece of forged metal exists within it, nor any mortar ground from lime. Instead, the builders have pried free the natural six-sided columns of the cooling rock using fire-hardened wood wedges and heavy mauls of banded stone, then stacked them into crisp, interlocking walls, cellular dwellings, and wide cantilevered plazas. 

Because everything is underwater, weight behaves differently, and the architecture exploits it. Massive basalt lintels, too heavy for twenty hands to lift in open air, have been floated into place beneath harnesses of giant cured plant-bladders, guided gently onto notched pillars, and lashed down with braided kelp-cord before the gas is bled out to settle them. Some roofs are not laid down to keep rain out, but anchored down to keep them from floating away—broad canopies of buoyant timber and bladder-mats tethered by stone cables, straining perpetually upward like flat-bottomed clouds over the public squares.

Underneath many of these canopies, and tucked beneath stone eaves throughout the terraced wards, hang trapped pools of gas. They look from below like sheets of dark, trembling quicksilver set flush into the ceilings. Some hold breathable air hauled down from the surface in greased skins and poured upward into inverted troughs of pitch-sealed basketry; others collect the warm, sulfurous burps of the rock itself, channeled up from floor vents through chimneys of hollowed stem. Around these upside-down hearths hang tools and half-finished goods suspended on bone hooks so their tips rest inside the dry pocket above the waterline: bundles of fibrous bark curing in the trapped volcanic warmth, gourds of resin thickening out of reach of the wet, flaking-knives of black obsidian lashed to pale driftwood hafts.

Because travel moves in three dimensions, the streets are not flat lanes but cleared volumes. Guide-ropes knotted with pale pumice floats stretch across the dimmer avenues from gable to gable, marking thoroughfares at three different depths above the jagged stone. Woven screens of split reed divide household territories vertically as well as horizontally, swaying an inch to the left, holding there for a full minute, and swaying an inch back. Everywhere, the flora has moved onto the masonry. Young bladder-vines root in the unweathered chinks between stacked hexagons; velvety mats of maroon algae coat the older plazas, softening the razor edges of the quarries where the town’s own streets were cut out of the hillside.

Life beyond the leaf and the builder’s hand is scarce. The basin is too young, and too high, to have gathered a crowded menagerie. Now and then a solitary grazer—a shell-less, translucent slug the length of a forearm, pleated with gill-frills—glides imperceptibly across a basalt wall, scraping away the thin biofilm and leaving a gleaming black ribbon of bare stone behind it. Farther out, where the town’s outermost tether-lines vanish into the dim olive open over the central abyss, small schools of blind, finger-length silver fish hang motionless in a rising plume of warm mineral water, all facing down into the dark. 

Mostly, the valley keeps a muffled, pressurized hush. Water swallows small noises quickly and carries sharp ones cleanly. There is no wind, no rustle, no birdcall, no surf. For long stretches the only sound is a faint, rhythmic ticking: tiny beads of volcanic gas detaching from fissures deep in the gorge, rising past terraced walls and woven screens, tapping softly against the undersides of broad leaves as they climb toward the gray lid of the sky. Somewhere three tiers down, hidden behind a curtain of bronze fronds, an obsidian chisel strikes once, twice, against raw basalt—*tink, tink*—splitting off a fresh flake of glass whose edges have never known wear, before silence settles smoothly back over the warm heights.
