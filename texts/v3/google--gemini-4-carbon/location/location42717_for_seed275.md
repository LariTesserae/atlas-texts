You are standing at the bottom of a wide, subterranean basin, roughly the size of an inland valley, though there is no sky above—only the distant, vaulted ceiling of the earth, held aloft by sweeping, pale arches that are still growing. 

There is no rock visible anywhere. The floor, the terraced walls, the bridges spanning the central trench, and the ceiling miles overhead are entirely composed of a single, engineered, dry-matter flora. Locally, it is called *lignin-bone* or simply *the husk*. It does not drink water; it pulls heavy, dry carbon gases from deep tectonic vents and metabolizes them using radiant energy, weaving itself into rigid, pale-ochre lattices that resemble balsa wood crossed with fiberglass. Because the flora is young—planted only a few decades ago to hollow out and stabilize this cavern—there is no soil, no rot, no crumbled detritus, and no erosion. Every surface has the crisp, papery, unweathered geometry of a freshly unfolded wasp’s nest.

The air is brisk, hovering just above freezing, and profoundly dry. Breathing it feels like inhaling cool, clean linen. It smells faintly of static electricity, dry cedar shavings, and hot copper. 

Despite being deep underground, the cavern is flooded with bright, clear, shadow-casting daylight. Suspended along the high, vaulted ribs of the ceiling are miles of thick, quartz-sheathed light-pipes and heavy industrial arc-lamps. They hum at a frequency just below human hearing, pouring down a steady, noon-bright, slightly cool white glare. This artificial sun does two things: it lets the inhabitants navigate the town without headlamps, and it feeds the city itself. Under the constant, bright glare, the architecture is visibly active. If you watch the edge of a transit viaduct or the framing of an apartment block for ten minutes, you can see the pale, fibrous tendrils slowly braiding themselves tighter, extending outward into the empty air along thin, electrified copper guide-wires strung up by the city’s builders. 

Because the city is grown rather than stacked, and because the husk-plant requires vast volumes of circulating dry gas to build its mass, the urban layout is remarkably sparse. It is an open, airy metropolis of wide boulevards, yawning plazas, and vast vertical shafts, with buildings spaced hundreds of yards apart like solitary, geometric mesas. A five-story foundry sits alone on a broad, smooth plain of cream-colored root-plate; half a mile away, a cluster of residential towers rises like hollow, hexagonal reeds, linked by slender, tension-grown walkways that sway almost imperceptibly when the ventilation shifts. There is an immense sense of room here—empty, brightly lit, cool space waiting to be drafted into streets.

The industry that powers and directs this growth is everywhere, yet weirdly hushed. In a wet world, factories roar with steam, boiling vats, and hydraulic slam. Here, water is a corrosive contaminant, kept strictly sealed in minor laboratory flasks. The heavy machinery of the cavern runs on dry pneumatics, magnetic induction, and towering, unlubricated flywheel engines spun on graphite bearings. 

Across the basin, vast pneumatic mills inhale the dense gases from the floor vents and compress them into feed-lines, while automated looms draw endless miles of brass and copper guide-wire to steer the growing walls. Yet the whole valley operates at the volume of a library. You hear the soft, rhythmic *shhh-thump* of dry air-pistons half a mile off, the crisp, dry rustle of growing structural fibers sliding over metal jigs, and the faint, continuous crackle of static discharge along the iron grounding-rails embedded in the streets. Voices carry strangely well across the open plazas, flat and clear, without echo, absorbed instantly by the porous, papery floors.

Moving through the bright, sparse avenues and across the vertical faces of the factories is the cavern’s abundant wildlife, completely integrated into the industrial ecology. There are no wet-skinned amphibians or oiled birds here; the fauna is built of chitin, dry felt, and electrostatic down. 

Flocks of *cinder-moths*—creatures the size of dinner plates, with heavy, velvety grey wings—drift in slow, swirling thermals beneath the great overhead light-pipes, feeding on the microscopic, airborne spore-wax shed by the growing buildings. Along the copper guide-wires and exterior power conduits scurry thousands of *wire-skinks*: foot-long, segmented creatures covered in overlapping plates of dry keratin that click softly like fingernails as they run. They are tolerated, even encouraged, by the engineers because they graze on the wild, stray shoots of husk-flora that sprout out of alignment, pruning the architecture clean as they hunt the smaller, static-charged beetles hiding in the joints of the brickwork. 

Higher up, nesting in the warm housings of the transformer towers, sleek, sightless predators—long-limbed, pale-furred creatures resembling slender badgers with wide, papery ear-sails—listen for the dry *tick-tick-tick* of the skinks’ claws. When one drops from a gantry to make a catch, it lands on padded feet without a sound, scattering a brief, silent blizzard of dry white spore-flakes into the bright air.

A cyclic wind governs the day. Every few hours, the massive rotary louvers at the far ends of the five-mile basin pivot on silent magnetic rings, reversing the atmospheric draw to feed different sectors of the growing town. You feel it coming first as a drop in air pressure in your ears, followed by a cool, steady draft sweeping across the smooth, cream-colored plains. As the dry wind rises, the entire sparse city answers with a singular, low, papery sigh—millions of hollow, rigid plant-beams flexing a fraction of an inch, giant moths banking in unison under the blazing lamps, and the endless, shining brass guide-wires singing a single, faint, cold note before the air settles back into busy stillness.
