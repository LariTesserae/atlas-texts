It is no larger than a parlor—perhaps fifteen feet across and twelve deep—sunk below the grade of the low, sodden flats around it. You do not see dirt here, and you do not see stone. Every boundary of the hollow—the bowled floor, the curving walls, the heavy, rolled lip overhead that frames a circle of plain, even sky—is composed of a single, continuous mass of living wood and fused rhizome. 

It looks old in the way river-bed granite looks old. Centuries of warm, seeping groundwater have planed the knotted root-plates smooth, erasing where one trunk ends and another begins until the entire basin resembles the inside of a single green-black gourd, slick with constant condensation. There are no loose leaves, no dangling creepers, no clutter of undergrowth. Because the vegetation *is* the ground and the walls, the interior of the pocket remains startlingly bare. Between one smooth, wet curve of root-wall and the next, there is only warm, clear air and open room.

The noise hits first, and it never modulates. It is as loud as a boiler room at full firing. Deep beneath the root-pan—somewhere miles laterally or hundreds of feet down, entirely unseen—heavy industrial turbines or subterranean pumps drive a relentless, fifty-hertz mechanical tremor up through the water table, carrying stray industrial voltage and waste heat with it. Nothing built ever breaks the surface here; there isn’t a bolt, a pipe, or a shard of concrete anywhere in the hollow. Instead, the pocket has spent generations digesting the spillover of that invisible machinery until the biology itself has taken on the logic of an engine.

Touch the weeping floor and your fingertips buzz. The fused roots have drawn dissolved iron, copper, and zinc out of the low, saturated ground, plating their own bark in dull, metallic films to channel the stray earth-current. Water wells up steadily from hairline pores in the base of the walls—warm as bathwater—sheets smoothly across the shallow dish of the floor without carving a single new groove, and gets drawn straight down again by fist-sized intake bulbs spaced yards apart. When an intake bulb fills, a tough, calloused valve snaps shut with the sharp, pneumatic *clack* of a factory punch-press, followed by a pressurized hiss as excess vapor vents back into the air. Five or six of these valves exist across the wide floor, firing in an unhurried, overlapping cadence that hasn't sped up or slowed down in years.

Daylight falls cleanly through the open ring at the top—ordinary, uncolored afternoon light, neither shadowed by canopy nor bleached by glare. In that plain illumination, the spacing of everything inside the chamber looks deliberate, almost measured. 

Life is everywhere, yet nothing crowds. Across the slick, gleaming expanse of the floor sit dozens of broad, flat-bodied amphibians the size of saucers, their wet skin mottled with the dull gray-green of oxidized copper. They do not pile together or dart for cover. Each sits in its own wide territory of bare, humming wood, precisely two or three feet from its nearest neighbor, absorbing the electrical warmth through its belly while waiting for water-borne grubs to wash across its jaws. 

Look closer at the smooth walls and the same sparse, gridded geometry holds, though at a tighter scale. Thousands of heavy-bodied beetles with oiled, gunmetal carapaces cling to the vertical bark, arranged like rivets along a hull—always a thumb’s-width apart along the conductive veins of the wood, leaving wide stretches of wet, dark timber empty between the lines. None of them crawl. They keep their wing-cases locked half-open, fanning their inner wings in a continuous, blurred vibration that vents heat from their bodies and adds a high, metallic whine—like an idling dynamo—to the bass rumble of the floor and the sharp *clack-hiss* of the root-valves. 

Now and then, a faint blue spark no longer than an eyelash jumps from a beetle’s antenna to a mineral seam in the bark, releasing a sharp whiff of ozone that mixes immediately with the heavy, sweet smell of warm sap and wet moss. 

Nothing rots here, and nothing appears to grow. New tissue replaces worn tissue underneath the water-sheen at the exact pace the warm runoff polishes it away. A pale water-louse drifts out of a seepage pore, crosses eight inches of gleaming, vibrating wood, and disappears into a frog’s mouth without the frog shifting a millimeter out of alignment. Moisture condenses on the upper lip of the hollow, gathers into heavy drops, falls back to the basin, and enters the intake valves again. The light sits clear on the wet curves; the unbuilt room roars steadily on its stolen current, warm, deafening, uncluttered, and entirely finished.
