The shelf sits two thousand meters above the timberline, notched into the shoulder of a newly uplifted peak where the air ought to be freezing and thin. Instead, stepping over the jagged lip of the basin is like walking into the open mouth of a kiln.

It is a small place, no wider than a cathedral nave and walled on three sides by vertical slabs of blue-black stone so recently sheared from the mountain that their faces still hold the chalky, unweathered snap of fresh fractures. There is no scree yet, no softened gravel or rounded boulders—only crisp angles, razor-edged steps, and clean geometric fissures slicing across the floor. On the fourth side, the floor simply ends at a sharp drop-off, looking out over a vast, pale emptiness of high-altitude sky and the blinding white tops of clouds far below. 

Between the cold mountain air outside and the blistering air trapped within the basin stands no visible wall, only a faint vertical shimmer along the cliff’s edge, like heat rising off asphalt. Wherever the mountain wind pushes against that threshold, moisture snaps instantaneously out of the sky. It condenses along an unseen plane in beads and sudden sheets, falling onto the hot stone rim and running inward. The entire amphitheater is wet. Every sharp facet of dark rock gleams under a clear, ordinary midday sun, slick with warm runoff that gathers in angular pools and slips down narrow fracture-lines in steaming threads.

The heat and the water belong to the four objects scattered irregularly across the basin floor. They do not look like machines so much as typographical errors in the landscape. One stands near the drop-off: a slender, waist-high plumb line of matte, shadowless white material that pierces straight into solid basalt without a seam or drill-mark, yet sways slightly as if moored in liquid. Thirty paces back, wedged hoveringly inside a V-shaped cleft, hangs an open polyhedron of something resembling tarnished mercury, its struts thinner than wire. None of them hum, spin, or glow, though the air within an arm's reach of each is so hot it smells faintly of ozone and boiled quartz. When running water touches the base of the white plumb line, it does not splash or hiss into steam; the water simply ceases to be there, reappearing a few seconds later as a warm, fine mist three meters overhead, drifting lazily out in the bright sunlight to wet the stones all over again.

Because the rock is too young for soil, much of the basin remains naked, gleaming stone—broad, uncluttered expanses of wet black slate and sharp-shadowed ledges. Yet wherever the warm runoff pools in the deeper intersections of cracks, vegetation erupts with startling, extravagant lushness. The contrast is stark: ten paces of barren, steaming mineral slab, and then a dense, chest-high island of heavy foliage. These isolated thickets have no dead leaves and no woody bark. Everything is turgid, waxy, and swollen with hot water. Coiled fronds the color of bottle glass unfurl visibly over the course of an afternoon, dripping mucilage from their tips. Beneath them, bulbous, translucent bladders the size of melons crowd the crevices, filled with clear fluid through which the sharp stone underneath appears magnified and warped. Bright chartreuse mosses, thick as sheared velvet, trace the exact paths of the hottest rivulets, stopping cleanly to the millimeter where the rock dries in the sun.

Despite the constant motion of water and growth, the basin is remarkably quiet. The high-altitude wind that scours the ridges outside is sheared off at the invisible thermal boundary, leaving the interior air heavy, breathless, and still. Sounds inside the stone room stay small and distinct. There is the slow, asynchronous *plink* of condensation falling into warm rock pools; the occasional crisp *tick* of fresh basalt expanding in the heat; the soft, wet creak of a thick plant stem uncoiling against another. Every twelve or fifteen minutes, the air reaches a saturation point all at once. For ten seconds, the bright sunlight goes milk-white as fog flashes into existence across the whole shelf, muting even the drip of the rocks, before collapsing suddenly into a brief, warm, vertical rain that leaves the air diamond-clear again.

Life moves slowly across the gaps between the green islands. High on one sheer fracture-wall, a pair of pale, flat-bodied creatures the size of dinner plates cling to the weeping stone. They are translucent enough that their branching, copper-colored vascular systems show clearly against the dark rock as they glide upward at an imperceptible pace, grazing on the microscopic film of minerals left behind by the mist. Down on the floor, emerging from a tangle of bladder-vines, something long-legged and jointless—part whip-scorpion, part slick-skinned newt—picks its way delicately across an open expanse of scalding, sunlit stone. It lifts each foot high to shed the heat, aiming toward the mercury-colored polyhedron in the cleft. Where it steps out of a puddle, its tiny wet tracks steam once in the clear light and vanish before it has taken three more strides.
