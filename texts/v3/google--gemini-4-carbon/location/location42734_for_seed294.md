High in the gray, thin saddle of the mountain range lies a drowned basin that was never natural rock. Miles wide, rimmed by peaks long since ground down into low, rounded shoulders, the entire valley is an inundation of snowmelt trapped inside an ancient masonry containment. There is no raw bedrock left beneath the water—if there ever was any. For leagues in every direction, down through fifty or eighty feet of frigid, gin-clear liquid, everything is laid stone. 

The light is dim and pewter-colored, pressed flat by a permanent ceiling of cloud that sits directly on the surface of the lake, turning the boundary between air and water into a cold, silver smear. Yet beneath that dull skin, the water is extraordinarily clear, so cold that nothing rots and little suspended silt survives for long without being dragged down into the structural joints below. Looking down is like peering through thick, flawed blown glass into an endless drowned quarry, except the quarry was built, block by block, millennia ago, without mortar, metal, or engine.

Nothing down here holds a sharp edge anymore. The architecture—vast stepped terraces, miles of baffled causeways, sunken amphitheaters, and thick-walled cisterns—has been scoured for ages until the cyclopean blocks look like clustered river cobbles the size of houses. Pillars have worn through at their waists like hourglasses and toppled onto wide, dish-shaped plazas. Stairways have smoothed into undulating chutes. Where arches still stand, spanning broad trenches in the basin floor, they are thinned to delicate ribs of dark basalt and pale granite, polished slick as wet bone. There is no green anywhere. No kelp, no drowned sedge, not even a furring of algae; only the faintest, greasy blush of black and rust-colored mineral bacterial film clinging to the leeward hollows of the stone, starved by the bitter cold and the dim winter twilight that passes for day at this altitude.

What defines the basin is its violent, ceaseless motion—and its uncanny quiet. 

Because the entire sunken valley was constructed as some monumental, long-forgotten hydraulic maze—perhaps to divide mountain torrents, perhaps merely the foundation of an unimaginably vast highland settlement—the water never sits still. Invisible thermal springs deep beneath the lowest flagstones vent plumes of slightly warmer water into the near-freezing mass above, while heavy, glacial runoff pours continually over the smoothed lip of the western pass. The masonry channels, broken and worn out of true, catch these opposing currents and twist them into furious, silent Chaos. 

Under the surface, the lake is a storm of glass. Huge, invisible gyres spin across the broad plazas, abruptly collapsing and reversing direction when two subsurface torrents collide at a collapsed retaining wall. Whirlpools six yards wide form in seconds, spinning down into sunken courtyards, vacuuming up decades of pale stone grit in violent, twisting columns before snapping shut and vanishing. Heavy slabs of granite, worn nearly flat and balanced precariously on rounded fulcrums, suddenly tip and slide down smooth ramps as a shifting current shoulders under them. The water boils without bubbles, shearing against itself in visible, refractive ripples, tearing across the open expanses with hurricane force—yet from above, or even suspended within it, there is almost no sound. Because it happens entirely submerged, with no breaking waves against a rocky shore, the turbulence is eerie and muted. There is only a deep, low-frequency shuddering through the soles of the feet if one stands on the flooded rim, punctuated now and then by the dull, distant *clack* or booming thud of a two-ton lintel shifting in its socket half a mile away, the sound traveling fast and directionless through the icy medium.

The architecture is vast and sparse. Between one drowned bastion and the next stretches half a mile of bare, paved floor, swept utterly clean of debris except where sudden, spinning eddies drop temporary dunes of rounded gravel, only to snatch them away ten minutes later and hurl them over a drop-off. It feels less like a dense city and more like a sequence of immense, minimalist earthworks made entirely of fitted block—dikes, broad empty forums, solitary gatehouses standing out in the gloom with water surging sideways through their lintels like wind through high passes.

And the currents are full of life. 

With so much open space between the stone structures, the abundant creatures here do not crowd together; they are flung and carried across the gray expanse like birds in a gale, exploiting the chaotic hydraulics rather than fighting them. Great, pale, flat-bodied things—somewhere between ray and giant leech, translucent at their ruffled margins—ride the violent upwellings above the submerged walls, banking sharply on invisible shears of water, vanishing into the pewter gloom, then reappearing twenty feet lower as a downdraft sucks them into a trench. Schools of heavy, blind, slab-sided fish with skins like hammered pewter hang motionless in the brief dead-zones behind eroded buttresses, waiting until the current violently shifts; the instant their shelter becomes a scour-zone, hundreds of them let go at once, tumbling backward in a silvery, silent cascade until they catch the edge of another eddy quarter of a mile away.

Across the slick, ancient pavements, thousands of pale, long-legged crustaceans the size of dinner plates scuttle sideways in sudden, frantic migrations. They anchor themselves in the deep, smoothed grooves between paving stones when a heavy surge sweeps over, flattening their carapaces until they look like part of the cobbles. The moment the turbulence lifts or whirls away, they pop up onto high stilt-legs and sprint across the open flagging to graze on the sparse streaks of mineral film or strip the carcass of a battered swimmer slammed against a pillar by a rogue cross-current. Thick, muscular worms as long as a man’s arm anchor their tails in old drainage slits along the terraced walls, whipping sideways like heavy pennants in a hurricane, snatching smaller drifting creatures out of the racing water with hooked, jawed mouths that make sharp, clicking snaps—one of the few crisp sounds in the muted dimness, like twigs breaking underwater.

Here and there, the tops of the highest, oldest towers almost breach the surface, worn down to slick domes just two or three inches beneath the waterline. Over these submerged crowns, the water accelerates into glassy, standing swells that heave up into the freezing mountain fog, tremble violently without breaking into foam, and slide back down into the grey depths. Flocks of heavy-bodied, flightless diving birds—grease-coated, dark as wet slate, with paddle-wings and hooked bills—bob briefly in the freezing mist just long enough to gulp thin highland air before plunging straight back down into a spinning vortex, letting the downward corkscrew shoot them fifty feet deep in three seconds flat, shooting past smoothed stairways and round-shouldered monoliths on a hunt through a world that is entirely built, relentlessly cold, furiously alive, and nearly silent.
