At this altitude the air ought to be freezing, thin enough to starve a lung, yet it presses against the skin like the breath inside a boiler room. The plateau has no visible edge. It climbs into a bruised, pewter sky and stays there for thousands of miles—a continent heaved three miles above whatever passes for sea level on this world, still in the violent middle of its own arrival. Nothing here has had time to weather. Every ridge is a fresh fracture, obsidian-sharp and jagged as torn tin; every talus slope consists of blocks sheared minutes ago, their edges crisp enough to slice leather, completely untouched by wind-polish or the rounding patience of rivers. 

There are no rivers. Water exists only as a heavy, persistent sweat. Warm vapor exhales continuously from deep tectonic vents and immediately condenses onto cooler faces of slate and raw ironstone, coating the entire jagged highland in a slick, glistening film. Moisture beads on sharp rock angles, gathers in palm-sized pockets, trickles six feet down a basalt chute, and flashes back into warm mist before it can ever carve a gully. 

Above it all hangs a low, lightless ceiling of graphite cloud that swallows ninety percent of whatever sun sits behind it. The world operates in a permanent, murky dusk, lit mostly from within by the transient orange glow of friction-heated rock and the erratic blue snapping of native electrical arcs.

It should be deafening. Across every visible mile, the terrain is in a state of frantic, hyperactive upheaval—yet the landscape lies steeped in an uncanny, suffocating hush. Space here has lost its grip on acoustics. Sound waves travel ten or twelve feet and simply fold in on themselves, absorbed by some localized breakdown in the elasticity of the air. Half a mile off, an entire cliffside of glassy hornblende shatters upward, cycling like a colossal stamp mill, throwing splintered boulders a hundred feet into the gloom; from a short distance away, the catastrophe registers only as a faint, papery rustle and a warm shove of displaced air against the face. 

This is where the rules of the place fully give way. Nobody built anything here—there isn't a cleared road, a poured foundation, a purposeful wall, or the ghost of an architect anywhere across the continent—and yet the geology and the ecology are furiously, wildly industrial. Reality, fractured by whatever turbulence drives the plateau, cannot quite keep the categories of stone, machine, and organism apart.

Out of a raw, unworked fissure in a granite crag, six-ton columns of hexagonal basalt pump up and down in staggered, rhythmic succession, slick with natural black mineral grease that weeps from surrounding shale. Nobody milled them, and they power nothing. They are simply what rock does here when geothermal pressure meets broken physics. Great flanged reefs of native copper and magnetite extrude themselves from fault lines like bracket fungi, spinning slowly against one another on cushions of magnetic repulsion, generating thick, silent sheets of violet static that ground themselves into the damp earth. Here and there, a whole acre of jagged terrain stutters in time—heaving upward, fracturing into razor-edged plates, vanishing with a soft *pop* of displaced atmosphere, and re-extruding in the exact same configuration four seconds later, an endlessly looping geological misfire.

Directly atop this churning, reciprocating crust grows a dense, tangled riot of dark vegetation. Because the light is so poor, chlorophyll has gone nearly black, shading into deep maroon and oiled bronze. Lush, broad-leaved canopies crowd every square inch of stone that remains stable for more than an hour, their roots acting like hydraulic clamps, gripping moving pistons of rock and riding their strokes. Vines thick as mooring cables—armored in overlapping scales of flexible iron sulfide—bridge the chasms between quivering spires, drinking the warm condensation that sweats off natural cooling fins of extruded zinc. Huge, fleshy ferns uncoil in minutes where steam vents bleed dampness into the gloom, their fronds trembling not from wind, but from the silent, sub-audible thrumming of the crust below. When a tectonic linkage shifts and shears a stand of timber in half, sap the color and smell of hot castor oil spills over the fresh stone, and new shoots burst from the severed stumps before the old canopy has even finished sliding into the ravine.

Crammed into the tight, humid spaces under the dark leaves, animal life teems at a feverish density. Everything moves fast, navigating a habitat whose geometry rearranges itself from minute to minute, and everything moves in near-total silence. Swarms of heavy-bodied, fist-sized creatures with stamped, articulated carapaces of dull brass drop from branch to branch; they open triple-hinged wings that beat in a blur, lifting them through the gloom without a hum. Flocks of pale, eyeless climbers—six-limbed, muscular, slick with condensation—race along the undersides of copper-veined arches, grazing on mats of electro-synthetic moss that bloom wherever natural dynamos discharge into the damp rock. 

Down in the warm, grease-scented clefts between pumping stone shafts, thousands of segmented, yard-long scavengers pile three deep over freshly extruded seams of sulfur and warm tallow-clay, feeding frenetically before the seam snaps shut. Predators the size of wolves, built low and wide with pads of vulcanized, horn-like rubber on their feet to grip wet obsidian, stalk through the ferns. Occasionally one flickers—its hindquarters slipping sideways through a local tear in continuity, emerging two yards to the left of its shoulders without breaking stride, thoroughly adapted to a landscape where space itself has loose joints.

Climb to the razor-crest of a newly thrust peak—careful not to slice a boot open on rock that did not exist twenty minutes ago—and the sheer, terrifying expanse of the plateau opens out into the dimness. As far as vision carries before the bruised twilight swallows it, the land boils in dead silence. Miles away, a massive cyclone of warm mist and airborne iron filings rotates lazily over a sunken basin where the crust is tearing open and knitting shut like dark muscle. Beyond that, vast terraced tablelands ripple like conveyor belts, shedding whole forests into subterranean furnaces while fresh, lush canopies erupt from their trailing edges, glistening with sweat, flickered through with silent sheet lightning. There are no ruins, no beacons, no straight lines laid down by a mind—only an endless, fever-warm wilderness of living engines and jagged stone, assembling and disassembling nothing at all under a low, dark sky.
