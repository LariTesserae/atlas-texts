The plateau stretches further than the curvature of the world allows the eye to see, a vast, tilted table of newborn basalt and angular granodiorite standing three miles above the sea. There is no soil here to speak of, only grit, frost-shattered shards, and stone so recently thrust upward that its edges are still razor-sharp. If you run a gloved thumb along a ledge, the rock doesn’t crumble into clay; it slices the fabric. Water has not yet had the millennia required to carve riverbeds or soften the stepped cliffs. Instead, moisture gathers in wide, geometric depressions, pooling on impermeable stone until it spills over sharp sills in thin, unchanneled sheets. Everything gleams. Every dark facet of the rock is slick with condensation, weeping cold groundwater squeezed up from tectonic compression below, catching the pale, high-altitude daylight like millions of wet slate tiles.

Across this raw, glistening expanse stands a city, though it possesses none of the huddled density of older human places. Its structures are spaced miles apart, unified into an urban whole not by pavement or walls, but by precise alignment and invisible corridors of force. Rising straight from the wet stone are smooth, dark-gray monoliths and slender, needle-like spires five hundred feet tall, composed of a seamless metamaterial that sheds water without ever dampening. Between them, suspended forty feet above the boggy basins and sharp scree, run narrow, translucent causeways that flex slightly in the wind without making a sound, held aloft without pilings or cables. Here and there, wide, shallow rings of matte-white alloy rest lightly over bubbling thermal seeps, drawing thermal and barometric gradients directly into motionless, silent grids. There is no exhaust, no hum of transformers, no clutter of wires or pipes. The engineering is absolute and quiet, maintaining interior worlds behind seamless, unreflective glass while leaving the surrounding square miles of emerging geology untouched.

Because the plain is so vast, it manufactures its own weather right at eye level. Three miles to the north, a bank of silver fog is rapidly condensing out of seemingly clear air, dragging a curtain of sleet across a cluster of residential towers; ten miles east, broad shafts of crisp, afternoon sun strike the wet stone, turning the shallow sheets of surface water into blinding mirrors. The air smells sharply of wet flint, ozone, and cold snowmelt. 

Into this sparse, sprawling geometry, wildlife has poured in staggering numbers, treating the silent, hyper-advanced city simply as a peculiar range of warm cliffs. 

Where there is vegetation, it is tough, low, and fiercely opportunistic: wide mats of chartreuse cushion-plants wedged into clean tectonic cracks, rusty-red lichens painting the leeward sides of the dark monoliths, and sparse stands of ankle-high, silver-leaved willow clinging to the wettest margins of the overflow pools. Yet despite the thinness of the flora, the plateau teems with motion. Vast, shifting herds of heavy-shouldered ungulates—creatures coated in dense, greasy wool the color of wet ash—move constantly across the shining flats. There are tens of thousands of them spaced out across the horizon, scraping at the dense mats of moss and mineral-rich algae blooming in the standing water. Their split hooves click sharply on the unweathered basalt, a dry, scattered percussion that fades almost instantly into the thin mountain air. 

Above them, the sky and the city’s upper architecture belong to the birds. Flocks of broad-winged, slate-backed waterfowl rise and settle endlessly across the sheet-water basins, shimmering white and gray as they bank in unison through the shafts of cold sunlight. Along the suspended translucent causeways, rows of heavy, cream-feathered raptors sit shoulder to shoulder, entirely indifferent to the faint, rhythmic shimmering within the material beneath their talons—transit pulses moving invisible freight or passengers from spire to spire at ferocious speeds without displacing a molecule of the outside air. 

The quiet of the place is profound, amplified rather than broken by the sheer volume of life and weather. The thin, cold atmosphere swallows distance. A hunting pack of long-legged, pale-furred canids cuts silently through the labyrinth of freshly split boulders near a transit-ring, their breath blooming in rapid white puffs as they flank a lagging calf. Water drips steadily from overhanging rock shelves into clear, icy puddles. A sudden gust sweeps down from the higher ridges, driving parallel ripples across a mile-wide skin of two-inch-deep water and fluttering the heavy feathers of a hundred roosting hawks, then dies away. 

Out near the western horizon, the ground trembles—a brief, low-frequency shudder as the young plateau settles another fraction of an inch along a fresh fault line. Instantly, ten thousand wading birds lift three feet off the wet rock in a silent, pale wave, hang suspended for three heartbeats while the city's distant spires invisibly adjust their internal counter-harmonics to match the new geometry of the crust, and then settle right back down into the cold, gleaming water.
