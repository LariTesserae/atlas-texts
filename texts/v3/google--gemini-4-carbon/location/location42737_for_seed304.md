The settlement occupies a single tectonic split—a steep, jagged basin no wider than a town square, dropped thirty feet below the flat, featureless grey scrubland surrounding it. You do not see it until your boots are at the lip. Below, the space is crammed to absolute capacity with dry-stone architecture, wedged so tightly into the freshly fractured basalt that it is difficult to tell where the geological fault ends and the quarrying begins. 

The rock here is young. It has not had the millennia required to soften. Every ledge, step, and lintel is sharp-edged, dark, and flinty, sheared along clean crystalline planes. There is no mortar. Instead, the builders used weight and friction, fitting flat, sharp-cornered slabs of dark stone together in dense, interlocking stacks that rise three and four stories from the drowned floor of the pit. There are no streets, only shoulder-width slots, switchback stairs, and low, corbeled tunnels through the bases of dwellings. Roofs are flat slabs of slate that serve immediately as the courtyards and walkways for the tier above. Everything touches. A wall of one room is the retaining wall of a cistern, which is the chimney-back of a kiln, which is the foundation of a watch-post.

Water is the architect and the adversary here. The split cuts directly across a high, pressurized water table, and the raw cliff faces weep constantly. Cold, mineral-heavy water sheets down the dark rock, glossy and silent. Because the air is cool—the kind of damp, bone-deep chill that turns breath to faint vapor even at midday—nothing ever truly dries. 

To live in the pit is to manage the seep. Across every wall and packed into every crevice is an obsessive network of primitive hydraulics. Thousands of split-slate gutters, hollowed-out bone spouts, and pitch-sealed wooden flumes crisscross the narrow gaps overhead, catching the runoff from the cliff faces and routing it away from the sleeping quarters. Water is steered down stepped channels carved directly into the stairways, collected in brimming stone basins, and dropped through vertical shafts to turn small, heavy wooden paddles at the bottom of the cleft before draining into a dark, flooded sump at the lowest end of the basin. 

The light comes straight down from a flat, pewter sky, bright enough to cast crisp shadows in the upper tiers and reflect brilliantly off the wet stone surfaces. Because the basalt is wet, it acts like a dark mirror; the cold daylight bounces off slick roofs and brimming troughs, throwing wavering, watery reflections onto the undersides of stone lintels deep in the lower alleys. Down near the sump, the light turns greenish-grey, filtered through the mist of dripping flumes.

Despite the constant moisture, very little grows. The stone is too recently exposed to hold soil, and the water is too cold and heavy with dissolved iron and sulfur to encourage much beyond tough, specialized pioneers. In the rare corners where the channeling leaks and sits stagnant, thick mats of bitter-smelling black liverwort cling to the rock, alongside clusters of pale, rubbery bladder-fern that swell with absorbed water. Along the high rim, where wind hits the top tier, tough, wire-thin sedges grow out of woven baskets packed with hauled-in mud and hearth-ash—deliberate, sparse little gardens tended for cordage rather than food.

What makes the basin unusual—and explains why anyone would pack a town into a cold, weeping crack in the earth—are the calcified Skins. 

Scattered throughout the architecture, stretched over wooden frames or draped across stone racks in the brisk drafts of the alleyways, are broad, translucent sheets of pale amber membrane. They are harvested from the sump at the bottom of the pit. The water down there is rich in a strange, fast-forming mineral bacterial mat; left undisturbed for three days on the surface of still catching-pools, the cold water grows a tough, leathery pellicle half an inch thick. Pulled from the water with hooked poles, scraped with flint blades, and cured over peat-smoke, this biological-mineral skin shrinks tight and hardens into something stiffer than rawhide and nearly waterproof. The town is built of stone, but it is roofed, doored, bucketed, and clothed in this cured water-skin. Where the light hits a stretched awning of it, the alley below glows a faint, greasy gold.

The soundscape is hushed, insulated by the sheer density of the stone walls, which swallow wind and echo. There is no roar of a river here, only a vast, intricate percussion of small waters: a thousand simultaneous drips, trickles, and thin, steady pours hitting slate, wood, and taut membrane. Over this steady, quiet patter comes the occasional rhythmic *clack-thump* of a water-tipped wooden hammer resetting itself down in the workshops—simple, clever machines using filling buckets and counterweights to pound fuller's earth or crush soft ore without human hands. 

Animal life is scarce and furtive, adapted to the verticality and the chill. No birds nest in the pit, though occasionally a grey crow will land on the upper rim to peer down. Within the stone maze, the primary inhabitants—aside from the quiet, wool-wrapped builders who move sideways through the narrow passages—are pale, flat-bodied amphibians no bigger than a thumb. They cling to the wet undersides of the aqueducts, motionless for hours until a midge drifts past. Deeper down, in the dark pools where the pellicle grows, blind, bristle-legged crustaceans graze the underside of the forming skins, their faint scraping audible if you press an ear to a stone cistern wall. 

Everything is in quiet, perpetual motion. Water seeps from the earth, runs the channels, fills the tipping-buckets, pools in the dark, grows its thick pale skin, is peeled away by red-knuckled hands, and rises up the narrow stairs to shelter the stones against the next day's seep. The rock stays sharp, the air stays cold, and the town sits wedged in the earth like an iron wedge driven into green wood.
