The whole place fits inside the slumped, horseshoe-shaped hollow of an old mountain slump—perhaps three hundred paces across from rim to rim, cupped high on a windward slope where the clouds snag and tear themselves open. Yet within that tight amphitheater, everything is stacked, tangled, and furious with motion. There is no bare dirt here. If you drove a long iron spike down through the floor of the basin, it would pass through ten feet of cold, flooded root-mat, drowned timber, and living sphagnum before striking stone.

What looks at first like a dense, mossy thicket is actually a tightly packed vertical town, grown and lashed into the bones of dwarfed, thick-trunked cloud-cedars and sprawling mountain alders. There is scarcely a boundary between tree and house, infrastructure and canopy. Over centuries of settled habit, the inhabitants have pleated living branches together, grafting root to bough to form load-bearing arches, ribbing them with split-wood slats, and packing the gaps with turf, peat, and tough, fibrous sedge. Where dead timber is used—staircases carved from single notched logs, suspended boardwalks held up by braided willow cables, sluice-troughs hollowed from fallen trunks—it is immediately colonized. Within a month of being hewn by stone adze or soft copper chisel, any plank grows a coat of emerald liverwort, tufted lichen, and miniature ferns. Walking along a public catwalk feels like walking along the wet back of an ancient, breathing animal; the footing gives slightly, weeping clear, icy water around the soles of your boots with every step.

Water owns the geometry of the hollow. It arrives constantly—sometimes as silver sheets of slanting mountain rain, more often as fast, ragged mist blowing straight through the rooms and alleyways, condensing instantly on millions of waxen leaves and mossy eaves until every vertical surface runs slick. From the upper rim of the slump, dozens of milky, snow-fed streams spill straight into the canopy. The town does not fight this deluge; it routes it. Overhead and underfoot, a chaotic, clattering network of wooden flumes, woven reed-chutes, and living root-aqueducts carries water diagonally across the cramped airspace. Tipping-buckets made of hollowed burl wood fill, overbalance, dump their cold loads down lower troughs with a heavy *thump-slosh*, and right themselves, driving simple wooden paddles that turn grindstones or rhythmically pump bellows in cramped, smoky hearth-niches. 

The air is sharp and chill—the kind of damp mountain cold that pinks the knuckles and makes every breath, human or animal, puff white against the deep, saturated greens. Yet the light is surprisingly clear and candid. Because the basin faces outward over an invisible drop-off, the sky isn’t lidded by tall forest crowns; instead, clean, pale-gold mountain daylight pours sideways into the hollow whenever the cloud-rack parts, catching millions of falling water droplets, illuminating the translucent green edges of swirling fern fronds, and flashing off slick feathers and wet fur.

It is the animals, even more than the water and the wind, that keep the settlement in a state of roaring, ceaseless commotion. The town is not merely visited by mountain wildlife; it is thoroughly overrun, half-domesticated and half-wild in an indistinguishable, boiling soup of bodies. 

Every thatched roof-swell and grafted terrace crawls. Heavy, slate-gray mountainfowl with feathered legs roost in packed, muttering rows along the aqueduct rims, exploding downward in frantic, wing-slapping flurries whenever a pine marten or tame fishing-weasel darts along the rigging. Fat, woolly-coated rock-cavies stream through purpose-built wicker tunnels slung beneath the walkways, whistling shrilly in chorus whenever a shadow passes overhead. Up in the tangled briar-parapets, hundreds of glistening black corvids and emerald-throated mountain jays conduct deafening, territorial wars over drying racks of berries and gutted brook-trout, mobbing the broad-winged hawks that ride the updrafts over the rim. 

Down in the lower tiers, where the architecture sinks directly into boggy, rushing pools, broad-backed, shaggy water-goats splash along submerged log-ramps, shaking cold spray from their horns as herders in oiled leather capes shout and prod them with peeled hazel poles through narrow, woven hurdles. Even the pools themselves boil: dark, muscular char thrash inside submerged willow-traps, churning the brown peat-water to pale foam, while semi-tame otters slip over the slick boardwalks like wet shadows, rolling underfoot, stealing scraps, hissing, and plunging straight off the third-story balconies into the basins below.

Nothing stays still for five seconds. Gusts of sharp wind whirl into the horseshoe, flipping the pale undersides of the alder leaves all at once, swinging the rope bridges, blowing curtains of cold spray sideways from the overhead flumes, and sending cascades of trapped rainwater dumping off tilted moss-tarps onto the crowded decks beneath. When a trough jams with blown twigs and shed feathers, water spills instantly over the lip in a shimmering curtain; within moments, someone climbs out onto the slippery struts with a hooked pole, kicking loose a shower of bark and sending three dozen perching starlings shrieking into the air. 

The sound inside the amphitheater has weight and physical pressure. There is no quiet corner. Beneath everything lies the tireless hydraulic roar—water rushing down bark chutes, drumming hard on stretched-hide awnings, gurgling inside hollow trunks, splashing over stones. Layered thick over the water is the creak and groan of living wood flexing under wind and weight, the rhythmic wooden clack of water-hammers, the sharp ring of flint knapping and copper-peening from open-sided workshops, the barking of thick-furred herding dogs scrambling up vertical ladders, and above it all, a vast, continuous, multi-register din of screeches, whistles, bleats, wing-beats, and human voices calling sharply across the twenty-foot chasms of mist and dripping greenery just to be heard by a neighbor an arm’s reach away. 

Smells move through the cold air in sudden, distinct ribbons torn apart by the wind: crushed mint and bruised cedar needles, wet wool, sour bog-peat, sharp bird-lime washed instantly away by fresh rain, cold snowmelt, and the rich, resinous tang of damp alder smoke curling flatly out of turf chimneys before getting beaten down into the ferns. Everything is weathered, scarred by claws and cleats, worn velvet-smooth where hands grab wet railings, endlessly rotting at the bottom and endlessly sprouting, patched, woven, and born anew at the top.
