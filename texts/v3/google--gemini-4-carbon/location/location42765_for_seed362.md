There is no soil, and there is no steel that has not been swallowed. Across a basin wide enough to bend its own horizon, the world is composed entirely of compressed, dry root-plate and galvanized iron, fused together so thoroughly that you cannot strike a pick into the ground without drawing both dry white sawdust and orange rust. 

Overhead, there is no sky, only the Glare—a continuous, flat ceiling of industrial luminescence, miles high, burning at the exact, merciless brightness of a welding arc seen through pale glass. Shadows do not stretch here; light falls from every angle at once, bleaching the vast, low-lying tangle of the plain into shades of bone, zinc, chalk-green, and tarnished brass. Despite the brilliance, the air is startlingly cool, carrying the crisp, dry bite of an unheated warehouse in late autumn. There is no moisture to hold warmth. Nothing gleams with wet; nothing drips. 

What fills this continental floor is a dense, tireless forest of dry-matter flora that long ago hijacked the machinery built to process it. Or perhaps the machinery was grown from the start. Great, ribbed stalks of silicate-bamboo and pale, papery ironwood grow directly out of engine blocks, Seamless and terrifyingly vigorous. Vines thick as ship hawsers, armored in overlapping scales of dry, chitinous bark, loop through giant flywheels and pull them—not breaking the machines, but *turning* them. 

The landscape is intensely active, a continuous, low-elevation churn of biological and mechanical industry, yet it occurs in near-total silence. 

A row of stamping presses, stretching forty miles toward a shimmering, bleached horizon, rises and falls in steady, heavy rhythm. When the ten-ton iron rams drop onto beds of dry, fibrous gourds, they should deafen anyone standing near. Instead, the impact produces only a faint, muffled *thup*, like a fingernail tapping an empty cardboard box. The air here refuses to carry low frequencies. Heavy gears made of petrified heartwood grind together with immense torque, shedding drifts of pale, talc-fine sawdust, and the only sound is a dry, papery whisper. You can feel the vibration in your molars—a deep, industrial shudder running through the root-floor—while your ears register an eerie, library hush.

Because there is no water, the chemistry of the thicket runs on static electricity, dry salt-exchange, and light. Broad, leathery leaves, pale as parchment and cold to the touch, twitch constantly to catch the overhead Glare, flexing on ratcheting petioles. As they brush against one another in the dense, crowded understory, they build massive electrostatic charges. Every few minutes, a silent, violet-white sheet of static discharges across several square miles of canopy, causing billions of dry, brittle seed-pods to snap open in unison, releasing clouds of silver chaff that hang in the cool, buoyant air like metal filings.

Erosion here is the work of friction and wind. Over centuries of this ceaseless, dry motion, the hard edges of the ancient factory-blocks have been sanded smooth by blowing chaff and rubbing bark. Iron I-beams have been worn down into polished, saddle-backed curves where great, muscular creepers slide back and forth, hauling nutrients—dry pellets of sulfur and nitrates—along miles of corroded conveyor tracks. Beneath the active canopy lies a twenty-foot stratum of *duff*: a settled, established drift of powdered rust, shed bark-scales, stripped bolts, and desiccated husks, packed down into a firm, springy turf that swallows footsteps entirely.

And then there are the places where the scale and the endless, repetitive geometry of the factory-thicket cause space itself to slip a gear.

Because the landscape was built—and overgrown—in repeating modular units, distance loses its grip. Look down a straight maintenance trench choked with silver-white briars, and you will see a cooling tower three miles away. Walk toward it through the dense, dry brush for an hour, pushing past cold pistons wrapped in strangler-fig, and the tower is still three miles away, though you have passed its identical, numbered intake valve four times. 

In the middle distances, under the shadowless white glare, *parallax fails*. A massive, gantry-mounted harvester—now wholly colonized by pale, bloated tuber-vines that use its treads to crawl slowly across the plain—will appear to be fifty yards away, towering over the scrub. Then a dry runner-vine blows across its hull, passing *in front* of the machine despite clearly growing from a root-stock half a mile behind it. The eye aches trying to sort the layers. There are pockets of the thicket where the industrial repetition becomes literal: eight hundred square yards where the exact same twisted brass pipe, cracked in the exact same crescent, wrapped in the exact same six-leafed dry ivy, repeats every twelve feet. If you snap a brittle twig on one, the twigs on forty adjacent pipes snap simultaneously, releasing forty identical puffs of grey spore-dust in dead silence.

Animal life is scarce in this dry, churning immensity, reduced to odd, specialized custodians that look like misprints in the environment. Occasionally, something pale and flat—like a sheet of corrugated tin folded into the shape of a mantid—will unhook itself from a vibrating dynamo, drop into the sawdust drifts without a sound, and skate away on static-repulsion pads. High up in the bright, bleached rafters of the tallest canopy-cranes, flocks of small, papery things with the geometry of torn blueprints flutter from vine to cable, feeding on the electrical corona that gathers around copper burs. They have no voices; when startled, they scatter with the faint, dry rustle of turning pages.

Wind moves across the basin—not weather born of ocean or sun, but massive, continental pressure-drafts generated by the collective, rhythmic breathing of trillions of dry stomata and the tireless pumping of vine-driven bellows. A cool gale will sweep across the low expanse, bending the crowded, pale stalks in a single, silvery wave ten leagues wide, whipping up devils of rust-dust and mica-chaff that twist sideways into impossible angles before vanishing into the bright, flat air. Everything moves; pistons stroke, leaves ratchet, conveyors crawl, dust storms billow—all of it locked in a crisp, cool, dazzlingly bright pantomime, grinding onward without a drop of rain or a single loud noise.
