There is no dirt here, and if there is stone, it lies miles below, buried beneath an architecture of timber that forgot how to stop growing. 

The landscape is a roof, a floor, and a void, built entirely from a single, continent-spanning mega-flora. At this altitude, the air is thin, razor-cold, and perpetually bruised with twilight. Whatever star governs this world hangs permanently below a horizon of jagged, living ridges, or else is choked behind a high, stratospheric ceiling of ice-ash. Light is a rare sediment. It exists only as a faint, bruised indigo along the upper curves of the wood, plunging swiftly into pitch blackness in the drops between.

And the drops are almost everything.

To understand the ground here, imagine a lattice woven by giants out of ironwood girders, stretched over an abyss. The vegetation is total—there is nothing *but* the plant—yet the space itself is staggering in its emptiness. Trunks the width of river valleys do not grow straight up; they vault. They throw themselves sideways across leagues of freezing air, intersecting, fusing, and splitting into sharp, unweathered ridges of dark bark. Because this mega-forest is geologically young, nothing has rotted into softness. There is no mulch, no loam, no gentle slope of humus. Dead branches do not crumble; in the frigid, thin air, they snap along crystalline grain lines, leaving behind jagged, freshly splintered escarpments, wooden cliffs as sharp as knapped flint, and sudden, terrifyingly clean drops into five miles of empty air. 

Between any two structural boughs lies a volume of dark void large enough to birth its own weather.

This is a loud place. A forest of this scale, under immense structural tension and subjected to high-altitude thermal shifts, never stops speaking. The cold makes the sap contract; the wood booms like artillery. Great resonant *cracks* originate miles down in the dark and travel up through the soles of your feet before the sound hits your ears through the thin air. And then there is the wind. It drops out of the upper stratosphere and threads the vast, hollow polygons of the root-voids, turning the entire planetary lattice into an aeolian harp of monstrous proportions. Deep, sub-audible groans vibrate in the chest cavity, overlaid by the high, tearing whistle of gale-force drafts shearing across sharp, splintered bark-ridges.

Water never falls as rain—the air is too cold and thin to hold much of it for long—yet everything is slick, treacherous, and damp. Moisture rises from far below, out of whatever warmer, pressurized deep exists near the buried stone core. Warmth vents upward in colossal, invisible plumes through the miles-deep lattice. When these updrafts strike the freezing bark of the high canopy-ribs, they condense instantly. 

Every massive trunk has a wet side and a dry side. Leeward surfaces are dry as bone, splintery and raw. Windward and underside surfaces weep constantly. Thick rime frost builds up for hours, only to be melted in minutes when a fresh thermal plume roars up from the abyss, sending sudden, hissing cascades of slush and cold water sluicing down the 45-degree pitch of a mile-wide branch. Water pools in the fresh, sharp-edged crotches where two great limbs fuse, forming black, freezing tarn-lakes cupped entirely in living wood, skinned over with thin ice that shatters and reforms ten times a day as the great boughs flex in the wind.

Life crowds onto the timber ribs because the voids offer nothing to hold onto, though plenty to cross.

In the perpetual dark, eyes are useless, or ELSE swollen to pale, light-drinking saucers that reflect the faint starlight-indigo in eerie flashes. Most things hunt and flee by vibration and sound. Across the damp, bark-ridged causeways scuttle heavy, multi-limbed browsers with specialized, chisel-like plates for jaws; they do not eat leaves—leaves here are scarce, tiny, black needle-mats huddled in wind-shadows to hoard heat—instead, they gouge directly into the cambium of the world-tree, prizing up great shards of frozen bark to lick at the sugary, antifreeze-thickened sap beneath. When the wood bleeds, it smells sharply of turpentine, crushed mint, and cold iron.

Above and below them, the empty dark is busy. Things drop. Great, membrane-winged gliders the size of skiffs launch themselves from high splinters, riding the howling thermal plumes upward through the pitch black, clicking loudly to map the wooden cliffs by echo. Packs of slender, hook-clawed predators gallop along the undersides of the boughs, upside down over miles of nothing, tracking the rhythmic *thump-scrape* of the sap-gougers through the resonant timber. Everything moves fast; the cold demands high metabolisms, and the shifting winds require constant adjustment.

Scattered along the more stable intersections—places where three or four titanic limbs fuse into a knot the size of a county—are the marks of hands.

Whoever lives here has no clay, no fieldstone, and no ore. What they have is wood that behaves like stone, sap that cures like amber, and the bone and hide of the creatures that share the dark. Set back into the sharp, V-shaped wind-shadows of the bark ridges are small, hunkered structures. They look like barnacles or wasp nests, framed from harvested splinters lashed together with sinew and mortared tight against the freezing gale with boiled, pitch-black resin. 

Here and there, carved directly into the living wood of a trunk-highway, are deep, geometric steps—sharp-edged and raw, oozing slow beads of clear sap that freeze into handholds. Stretched across narrower chasms—gaps of only a hundred yards or so—hang thick, braided ropes of fibrous inner bark, strung with hollow bone chimes. In the dark, you do not navigate by sight; you navigate by the pitch of the wind hitting a specific neighbor’s bone-line, or the distinct drum-note made when a heavy wooden mallet strikes a carved sounding-fin raised at a crossroads.

Sometimes, clinging to the lee side of a towering splinter-crag, you will pass a solitary, guttering point of orange—not an open campfire, which would eat into the very ground beneath their feet, but a bowl carved from heavy, wet heartwood, lined with scraped frost-moss and burning rendered animal fat. The light doesn't travel far. It illuminates twenty feet of glistening, damp black bark, a rack of bone chisels and sap-tapping wedges, a windbreak of stretched glider-wing pulsing violently in the gale, and then nothing. Beyond that tiny sphere of grease-smoke and orange warmth, the bark drops away at a sheer angle, and there is only the roaring dark, the smell of cold sap, and the distant, miles-deep *boom* of the world growing another inch into the freezing sky.
