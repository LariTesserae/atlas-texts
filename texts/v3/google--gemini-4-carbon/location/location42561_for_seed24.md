At first there is only the warmth and the containment of the air.

It is the steady, unmoving warmth of stone that sits beneath a hot lowland plain and holds the year’s average heat in its mass—somewhere near skin temperature, dry enough to tighten the nostrils, utterly without draft. There is no smell of dampness, no sharpness of ozone or rot. What hangs in the air is mineral and faint: iron oxide, fine talc-like dust, and a dry, woody sweetness like split cedar kept in a closed chest.

Light enters from a single crooked chimney high in the shoulder of the vault, where two beds of sandstone slipped apart long ago and wedged fast. The cleft bends twice before it reaches the surface, so the sky itself cannot be seen, nor can rain or blown sand fall straight in. Whatever fierce glare exists outside arrives here baffled and spent, bounced down rough ochre walls until it pools on the floor as a dim, pewter-colored twilight. For the first several minutes nothing has color. Then the room assembles itself in shades of ash, slate, charcoal, and pale bone.

It is not a vast cavern, only a sunken chamber the size of a country church or a walled orchard—perhaps fifty paces from the low talus slope at one end to the rounded apse of solid rock at the other, its ceiling curving twenty-five feet overhead. Long ago, water hollowed this pocket out of softer lime-bound grit beneath a harder caprock, smoothed every angle into broad, scalloped flutes, and then vanished from the country altogether. The erosion here is finished work. The walls have the matte, settled curve of an old saddle; the ledges are softened; the fallen blocks on the floor lie buried past their waists in fine dry silt that drifted in grain by grain over millennia and leveled itself like water in the hollows. Nothing looks precarious. Every stone that was going to fall has already fallen.

Across the open floor, spaced well apart, stand six or seven pale vertical columns that look at first like stalagmites, until the eye follows them upward and sees their slight twist and fibrous grain. They are the taproots of deep-searching desert trees growing somewhere unseen on the baked plain above. Having forced their way through hairline joints in the caprock, they drop plumb through twenty feet of warm dark air and plunge into the silt floor, chasing some sealed seam of ancient moisture far deeper down. Some are thick as a human thigh, armored in papery, flaking bark that sheds dry curls in neat, undisturbed rings around their bases. Others are slender, taut ropes hung in pairs or threes. None of them sway; they are under immense, patient tension, half wood and half anchor cable.

Because of the roots, and because of the dim grey patch beneath the chimney, the chamber is not barren. Where the roots enter the floor, they have thrown up sparse, stunted suckers—low, thorny thickets of pale stems, leafless and waxy, colored the muted grey-green of sage or lichen. They grow with imperceptible slowness on the dregs of diffused daylight, each twig stiff and economical, spaced wide from its neighbors so that no stem shades another. Farther back, where even that pewter glow thins toward true black, a scatter of dry, stalked puffballs rises out of the silt on woody stems like slender pegs, their spore-cases split open years ago and preserved intact in the desiccated air, tough as old leather. Here and there a dead beetle husk, hollow and weightless, rests caught against a root-collar, blown down the chute in some forgotten season; nothing lives here to scavenge it. No spiders string webs between the roots. No bats hang from the clean, dry flutes of the ceiling. No mouse tracks stitch the dust.

The silence is physical, close around the ears. Without water there is no drip to measure time; without wind there is no sighing in the chimney. Only at long intervals—hours apart, perhaps days—thermal expansion in the plains overhead sends a faint, dull tick down through thirty feet of rock, or a single flake of root-bark detaches and lands on the silt with a sound almost too small to hear.

Near the edge of the brightest twilight, where a broad, knee-high slab of sandstone tilts out of the floor, the natural smoothness of the stone has been worked. A shallow oval dish is worn into the grain of the slab, polished glossy at its center. Beside it lies a rounded river cobble brought from somewhere miles away, sitting snug in a small dimple its own weight has pressed into the dust. Within arm’s reach, tucked neatly into a dry horizontal seam in the rock wall, rest two straight hardwood digging sticks, their points beveled with stone and hardened in char, the wood greyed and checked down the grain but completely sound. On the silt below the slab lie four sharp flakes of banded brown chert and a small heap of crushed, woody seed-husks, still crisp. There is no charcoal, no soot on the low ceiling, no stacked wall or cleared path—only a quiet work-station used briefly, left tidy, and held unchanged in the warm, dry dark as though the hands that set the cobble down had stepped away an hour ago rather than a hundred lifetimes back.

Nothing rots, nothing swells, nothing washes clean. Even when night falls outside and the faint pewter patch beneath the cleft fades to black, the stone gives off the exact same measure of warmth, the air holds its stillness, and the pale scrub waits without stirring until the gray stain returns to the floor.
