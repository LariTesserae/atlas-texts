If you dig down here, you will not strike stone. You will dig through warm, fibrous peat, and then through braided networks of pale, rubbery stolons, and below that into dense, water-logged timber-flesh that has forgotten which organism originally grew it, and further down still into compressed, anaerobic mats of ancient cellulose the consistency of wet leather. Somewhere miles beneath all of this lies an uplifted continental shield—a plateau worn down to a mirror-flat plain before complex eyes existed to see it—but nothing living on the surface has touched mineral earth in millions of years. The mountain range died of old age, flattened itself into a table high in the troposphere, and turned entirely into plant.

The air is warm, thick as broth, and astonishingly dim. At this altitude the sky should be thin and freezing, yet a heavy, ancient atmosphere holds the heat high against the ceiling of the world. Clouds do not pass over this plateau; they inhabit it. A permanent, pearlescent overcast sits directly on the landscape, turning noon into the color of tarnished pewter and bruising toward deep plum hours before actual nightfall. Because the light is so scarce, nothing wastes time being bright green. The foliage that makes up the floor, the walls, and the low, tiered roofs of the world is the color of oxblood, charcoal, dark olive, and wet slate—pigments tuned to drink every dim, scattered photon that filters through the vapor.

Water never truly falls here, because there is no violence in the weather to make it drop. Instead, the landscape simply sweats and collects. Moisture condenses continuously out of the warm fog onto billions of velvety, scalloped leaf-plates, gathering into heavy beads that roll down grooved stems and vanish into the living floor. Where the surface dips by even a few inches across twenty miles, the root-mat saturates and gives way to wide, shallow sheets of tea-colored water. These marshes have no mud. Their bottoms are firm, slick membranes of aquatic moss and submerged vascular pads that give slightly underfoot like the flank of a breathing animal.

Above the sponge-floor rises an architecture of slow, crowded tiers. Things grow wide rather than tall, spreading horizontal platters and ribbed umbrellas at waist-height, at shoulder-height, and thirty feet overhead, overlapping until they nearly touch. Walking requires a constant, gentle ducking and side-stepping between fleshy, buttressed trunks that are less like wood and more like bundled, turgid hoses. Everything is linked. Sap and rainwater are pumped laterally for miles through shared root-grafts; a dry spell on one horizon—if such a thing ever happens—is answered weeks later by a subtle slackening of pressure a hundred leagues away.

For all its stillness, the dimness is crowded with animals. You rarely hear them before you see them, because there is nothing crisp or hard in this environment to make a noise against. There are no dry twigs to snap, no gravel to scatter, no stiff leaves to rustle in a breeze. Sound dies ten feet from its source, swallowed by the wet, velvet surfaces and the fog. Yet if you stand still for five minutes, the dark shapes resolve themselves. Broad, flat-bodied grazers the size of calves—boneless or nearly so, moving on muscular, rippling skirts—slide up the damp trunks, rasping nutritious slime from the bark with scarcely a wet sigh. Overhead, stretching between the canopy plates, hang colonial web-drifters: translucent, veined sheets of flesh that comb microscopic spores and midges from the drifting mist, rippling in slow, rhythmic contractions. Long-limbed, warm-blooded climbers with oversized, oily eyes move hand-over-hand along the underside of the boughs, their fur matted with symbiotic algae so they vanish the moment they stop moving. Amphibious things with radial jaws sit half-submerged in the warm sheet-waters, motionless for days, waiting for velvet-shelled snails the size of fists to cross their margins.

When the landscape makes noise, it is hydraulic. A faint, deep *gurgle* beneath your heels as a root-bladder equalizes pressure. The periodic, heavy *plop* of a gallon of condensed water tipping out of a pitcher-leaf high above, tilting on its hinge and righting itself with a soft, leathery creak. Occasionally, a low, resonant hum vibrates up through the soles of your feet—not heard in the ears at all, but felt in the sternum—as something massive and subterranean shifts along the flooded galleries beneath the surface mat.

And scattered across the vastness, miles apart, are the marks of builders who used neither fire nor stone.

You might walk for three days through the dim, weeping galleries and think the world wholly untouched by mind, until you notice that the pooling water around your ankles isn't following gravity—it is being held in subtle, stepped terraces grown from braided surface-roots, coaxed over decades into low semicircular weirs. A little further on stands a shelter, though it has no cut timber and no thatch. Someone, generations ago, bound four young vascular stalks together at their crowns, scarred their inner bark so the tissues fused into a seamless, living dome, and pruned the interior hollow. Now it stands empty, still alive, still pumping dark sap through its walls, its doorway thickened into a smooth, healed lip of grey callus. Inside, the floor is dry—the roots beneath deliberately knotted to shunt groundwater aside—and hanging from small, horn-like galls on the interior wall are a few forgotten basins made of dried, cured gourd-rind, empty save for a velvet coat of mold.

There are pathways, too, if you learn how to read them: subtle depressions where the sponge-mat has grown denser and paler in response to centuries of patient, bare-footed traffic, curving gently between the dark columns to connect one high, dry hummock to the next. At rare intersections stand markers—not cairns, since there are no rocks, but tight, intricate knots tied into slow-growing aerial roots fifty or a hundred years ago, now swollen to the thickness of a human torso, holding their deliberate geometric twists high in the gloom like clenched fists. Whoever made them moved at the speed of the plateau itself. They planted a roof for their grandchildren; they bent a sapling to point toward sweet water and walked on before the bend took hold.

Out beyond the clustered trunks, where the broad marshes open up for dozens of miles at a stretch, the true scale of the place presses in. There are no peaks on the horizon to measure distance against, no ridges, no valleys—only the endless, level shelf of living dark, fading smoothly into warm, pearl-gray mist in every direction. Weather here is not wind and storm, but vast, continental breaths of barometric pressure. Over weeks, the fog thickens until visibility drops to twenty paces and the air grows heavy enough to coat the lungs; over the next month, it subtly lifts, raising the ceiling just enough for you to see five miles of dark, overlapping leaf-tables glistening like wet slate under a sky the color of ashes, stretching away across the roof of an ancient world that finished changing a very long time ago.
