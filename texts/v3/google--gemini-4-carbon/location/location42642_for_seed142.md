The plateau has no sky, only a depth of blackness so absolute that the boundary between high stone and empty air must be felt rather than seen. Yet the air is thin and breathlessly high—the unmistakable, chest-tightening altitude of a mountain roof—and it is perpetually, evenly warm. There is no wind to carry the warmth, no sun to radiate it. It rises steadily through the soles of one’s boots from the bedrock itself, an ancient, decaying planetary heat that holds the darkness at the exact temperature of a closed room.

Ages of dry, microscopic abrasion have ground the plateau down to its final, stubborn geometry. There are no sharp crags left, no talus slopes or loose scree. Everything has been smoothed into vast, undulating swells of dark, vitrified stone and basins of impalpable mineral flour that sits like heavy smoke in the hollows, undisturbed by any breeze. Nothing grows here. There is no soil, no lichen, no dry husk of a root; the chemistry of the rock is entirely sterile, locked in a state of post-erosional truce. 

Across twenty miles of this high, dark tableland spreads an industrial city, though it is built with such wide, deliberate spacing that a person can walk for twenty minutes along a paved causeway between one structure and the next. The architecture is monumental, windowless, and cast from fused basalt and unoxidized iron—great cylindrical silos, squat turbine housings, stepped ziggurats of riveted plate, and miles of elevated viaducts carrying sealed pipes as thick as tree trunks. Because there is zero moisture in the thin air, nothing rusts. Iron laid down ten thousand years ago retains the smooth, dull grease-sheen of the foundry, its edges softened only by the same unimaginably slow, dry settling that smoothed the hills.

The city is running, though it produces no light to work by. 

Vision here relies entirely on incidental luminescence: the dull, cherry-red glow of thermal expansion joints buried under iron grates; the faint, static-blue corona discharge bleeding off high-tension ceramic insulators; a pinpoint of amber behind a thick quartz sight-glass half a mile away, hanging in the void without context. These tiny markers map out the immense, sparse grid of the settlement. Between them, the avenues are velvet-black corridors paved in fitted stone blocks, swept clean long ago and never dirtied since.

Because the air is so thin, sound does not travel well, and what machinery operates does so at a pace that barely registers as motion. You do not hear clattering pistons or screaming steam—there is no water to boil. Instead, you feel a sub-audible, rhythmic pressure through the stone: the *thrum* of massive, dry-lubricated flywheels spinning in vacuum housings deep underground, drawing on the plateau's geothermal gradient. If you stand beside one of the great iron conduits, you might hear a faint, dry hiss—dense, heavy gases being shunted from a fractional distillation tower on the eastern rim to a storage dome three miles west. Once an hour, perhaps, a counterweight reaches the bottom of a shaft somewhere out in the dark, and a single, muted *clank* travels through the bedrock, arriving at your feet a second before the faint ghost of it reaches your ears.

The city’s avenues are not empty of life, only nearly so. The creatures that inhabit the warm dark are sparse and wholly adapted to the dry industrial equilibrium. Occasionally, crossing the faint red smear of light beneath a radiator bank, something the size of a hound moves on six silent, padded limbs. Their hides are greyish, mineral-crusted, and entirely dry, folded like heavyfelt. They have no eyes; their heads taper into broad, heat-sensing plates of pale cartilage. They do not hunt one another. They walk the miles between machines to lick the microscopic condensates of sulfur and metallic salts that weep from pipe flanges, or curl for days at a time against the warm casing of a step-down transformer, rising and falling at four breaths a minute. 

Higher up, clinging to the undersides of the overhead trestles where the static charge is thickest, hang solitary, radial things like brittle black umbrellas. They feed in some obscure, non-botanical fashion off the electromagnetic fields of the power lines, twitching a single rib once every few hours when the current shifts.

Nothing is being built, and nothing is falling down. There are no heaps of slag that haven't already settled into smooth, glassy dunes; there are no lights flickering out, because there are no bulbs to burn. The engines pump their dry, heavy gases in a closed loop from one end of the plateau to the other, equalizing pressures that never quite equalize, shedding a minuscule fraction of heat into the warm, thin air. If there were ever operators, they designed the machinery to outlast the need for eyes, and the plateau holds their work in a dark, quiet suspension—warm stone, smooth iron, and ten miles of breathless night between one faint red ember and the next.
