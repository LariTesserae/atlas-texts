The air here has the weight of damp wool, pressing hot and saline against the skin. You stand on a high plateau, a wide geological pan tilted subtly toward an unseen rim miles away, though you cannot see the rim because the light gives out long before the land does. Above, a seamless, low-bellied ceiling of charcoal-colored cloud swallows the sun, filtering it down to a bruised, pewter twilight that holds steady for hours on end. There are no shadows. Light simply clings to wet surfaces—a dull, oily sheen on stone, iron, and shallow water.

This is a city, unmistakably planned and massive in its footprint, yet the overwhelming sensation is one of staggering emptiness. It is an urban grid stretched to the breaking point, as if a standard industrial metropolis had been pulled outward from all four corners until every avenue became a windswept plaza and every courtyard expanded into a featureless plain. 

You look across an intersection paved in hexagonal basalt blocks. The street is four hundred yards wide. On the far side, half-dissolved in the hot mist, rises a solitary structure: a windowless, six-story condenser tower made of riveted iron plates and dark, glazed brick. Half a mile down the boulevard stands the next building, a low-slung turbine housing, and beyond that, nothing for a mile except a line of towering cast-iron stanchions carrying slack, dripping cables into the gloom. There is no rubble, no sign of ruin or abandonment. The emptiness is structural, deliberate. The city consists almost entirely of intervals.

Water is everywhere, yet it never gathers enough depth to drown the ankles. Because the plateau is high, hot, and sealed beneath an inversion of cooler upper air, the atmosphere is in a state of perpetual, silent weeping. It doesn't rain; the air simply sweats. Warm condensation beads on every iron rivet, trickles down the brick facades, and sheets across the vast expanses of basalt paving. Over centuries, this tireless, hot trickle has smoothed the city into an established state of wear. The sharp edges of the hexagonal flagstones have melted into soft pillows; shallow, braided channels have worn themselves diagonally across the avenues, guiding simmering, mineral-heavy runoff toward sunken bronze grates the size of house foundations. 

Nothing green grows in the cracks. The heat and the chemical tang of the water forbid leaves, roots, or stems. Instead, where the water pools longest in the depressions of the stone, there are broad, paper-thin smears of rust-orange and sulfur-yellow thermophilic slime. Closer to the iron foundations of the buildings, pale, blister-like crusts of mineral-eating fungi—hard as porcelain to the touch—ridge the mortar lines, thriving on the slow oxidation of the metal and the warmth radiating from the earth beneath the paving.

Despite the vast, hollow silence of the avenues, the city is awake and working. You feel it through the soles of your boots before you hear it: a slow, rhythmic thrum transmitted through the bedrock and the wet stone. Every few minutes, a massive valve shifts somewhere inside the distant condenser tower. There is a muffled, pneumatic *thump*, followed by a long, wet exhale as a plume of white steam vents from a copper stack near its roof, climbing twenty feet before flattening out and merging with the low dark clouds. Half a mile to the east, a solitary gantry crane, tall and skeletal as a winter tree, rolls slowly along elevated tracks, completely unhurried, carrying a single heavy cylinder of blackened steel across a vast, paved void. There are no operators visible in its high glass cab, though a faint, amber filament bulb burns steadily inside. 

Sound behaves oddly in the hot, damp emptiness. Without walls close enough to bounce an echo, noises simply drop dead into the mist. The hiss of escaping steam, the low gurgle of hot water dropping into a subterranean cistern, the occasional heavy *clank* of expanding metal—each sound sits isolated in its own pocket of space, surrounded by the profound hush of wet stone stretching away in every direction.

It takes a long time to realize the city is inhabited by anything other than its own slow machinery. Life here mirrors the architecture: sparse, resilient, and separated by immense distances. If you stand still in the middle of an avenue for an hour, letting the hot condensation soak your collar, you might see a movement near one of the steaming drainage grates. Something pale and multi-jointed, roughly the size and shape of a whippet but armored in translucent, wet chitin, emerges from the mist. It moves with a high-stepping, deliberate gait over the slick stones, pausing to scrape its mandibles delicately across a patch of sulfur-yellow slime. Near the warm exhaust louvers of a distant pumping station, two or three heavy, leathery shapes hang motionless against the dark brick, absorbing the vibrating heat like sleeping moths the size of coats. When one detaches and glides low over the flooded flagstones, its wings make no sound at all in the thick air—just a brief ripple reflected in the dark, steaming mirror of the street before the gloom swallows it up again.

Everything here has settled into a strange, hot equilibrium. The subterranean heat drives the engines; the engines vent the steam; the high plateau traps the cloud; the cloud weeps warm water back onto the endless paving, smoothing the stone by another fraction of a millimeter each century, washing the rust down into the grates to feed the deep boilers all over again. Walking from one streetlamp to the next takes twenty minutes, splashing softly through a half-inch of bath-warm water, guided only by the dim, amber halos suspended far ahead in the dark gray soup.
