The space is an upland sinkhole, perhaps forty paces across, rimmed on all sides by a continuous collar of pale, chalky stone. There is no horizon here—only the curved lip of the rock wall and the pale, cloudless bowl of sky directly overhead. Despite the altitude, the basin acts as a thermal trap. The air inside is heavy, humid, and distinctly warm, held in place like water in a cup, smelling faintly of wet limestone and boiled greens.

There are no sharp edges anywhere in the stone. Ages of warm mist and seeping groundwater have dissolved the walls into smooth, slumped forms—rounded shoulders, shallow alcoves, and long, vertical flutes that look like melted wax. Water does not fall or rush here; it sweats. A continuous, microscopic weeping coats the pale rock in a warm glaze, collecting at the base of the walls into a series of broad, ankle-deep pans. The floor of the basin is a series of gentle, calcified terraces, stepped like lily pads, holding sheets of water so clear and still they are discernible only by the slight refraction of the stone beneath them. 

Light fills the basin evenly, bouncing off the pale, wet stone so that even the overhangs lack deep shadow. Everything is brightly, softly lit. 

Because the space is largely open stone and shallow water, the vegetation reads with startling clarity. It does not crowd or tangle. Instead, individual plants stand yards apart, buffered by wide expanses of bare, wet travertine, giving the place the unhurried proportion of a gallery. Yet each isolated specimen is extravagant, swollen with moisture and warmth. Out of small pockets of black silt wedged in the stone grow solitary, trunkless palms with three or four immense, rubbery fronds that arch outward and touch the water. Elsewhere, bloated, emerald-green cushions of moss—each the size of a sleeping bear—sit entirely alone on dry pedestals of rock, drinking directly from the humid air. Strangest are the bladder-ferns: pale, translucent stalks rising directly from the shallow pools, ending in heavy, water-filled bulbs the size of melons that lens the sunlight onto the stone floor in wobbling, bright rings.

Nothing crawls, flies, or swims. The water’s surface is never broken by a skating insect; the fleshy leaves bear no chew-marks or sticky webs; no birds cross the circle of sky above. Without pollinators or browsers, the flora has settled into a strange, vegetative self-sufficiency. Reproduction happens by slow weight. Over months, a water-filled bulb on a fern grows too heavy for its stalk, bends slowly down until it touches the warm stone, and dissolves its skin to root in place. Moss cushions expand by fractions of an inch a year, splitting cleanly down the middle when they outgrow their perches.

The silence is profound, pressurized by the warm, damp air. Because the water moves primarily as a thin, laminar film sliding over smoothed rock, it makes no babble or splash. The only sound occurs at long, irregular intervals: the singular, muffled *thuck* of an oversized droplet detaching from a waxy frond and striking a shallow pool, sending a single ring undulating lazily from wall to wall before vanishing. 

Yet the basin is not entirely untouched. There are no walls, no cairns, no cleared paths, and no refuse, save for the subtle evidence of a patient, visiting hand working with the slowness of the place itself. 

Near the center of the floor, where a mineral spring wells up without a ripple, the calcified rim of a terrace has been slightly deepened using a harder river-cobble, coaxing the overflow to sheet leftward instead of right. Along this redirected film of water sit four broad, wooden spatulas—split from some tough, fibrous stem found outside the sinkhole, scraped flat with flint, and wedged into natural crevices in the floor. They are positioned directly beneath the bowing stalks of the bladder-ferns. Left there for seasons, the wood is currently disappearing beneath slow, pearlescent layers of calcium carbonate deposited by the weeping water, turning the simple wooden paddles into stone tools. Beside one of the giant moss mounds, a heavy, blackened Bronze-like nodule—perhaps meteoric copper, cold-hammered into a blunt wedge—has been left resting in a shallow dish of water, stained bright verdigris, slowly leaching blue-green mineral streaks down the pale rock toward the roots of a solitary, yellow-leaved shrub that has absorbed the tint into its veins.

Whoever comes here does not live here, and has not been here recently; the tracks of bare feet in the silt pockets have long since smoothed over into soft, indistinct dimples under the patient creep of the water. The tools are not abandoned, only waiting—set in place to let the warm, mineral-heavy stillness of the basin finish making them.
