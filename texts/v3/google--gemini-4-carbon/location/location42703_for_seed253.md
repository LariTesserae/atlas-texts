At five thousand meters above the forgotten plains, the water does not spill; it is held. 

What was once a jagged crown of alpine peaks was, in an unrecorded era, hollowed, terraced, and bound together into a single regional basin, only for the stone itself to be systematically replaced by root. Whoever engineered this flooded caldera used no metal, no mortar, and no tools that left scars. They wove living, cold-water timber—a species of sinker-cypress with bark like iron-rich peat—training the trunks over millennia to fuse into retaining walls six hundred feet thick. Now, the stone mountains are entirely gone, dissolved and digested ages ago. The entire elevated sea rests in a monumental, constructed basket of ancient, living wood. 

You are submerged inside it. 

Above, far out of sight, the surface of this sky-lake meets the thin, freezing air of the extreme upper atmosphere, eternally capped by thick mountain cloud and starless murk. Down here, there is only the deep, cool dark, hovering a few degrees above freezing. Light exists solely as a faint, bruised-indigo pressure from above—barely enough to distinguish the colossal, sweeping curves of the woven floor from the vast, open water between them.

Because the vegetation *is* the architecture, there is no clutter. There are no swaying kelp forests, no tangled weeds, no drifting detritus. Everything has been smoothed by eons of slow, cold circulation into its final, most stable geometry. The floor of the basin drops away in wide, sweeping amphitheaters of living root-wood, worn utterly smooth, slick with a microscopic velvety moss that feels like cold tallow. Great viaducts and hollow ribs—grown, not stacked—arch across miles of open, pitch-black water to brace the outer rims of the caldera. Between these titanic, living buttresses, the water is astonishingly empty and clear, creating vast, echoing galleries of dark liquid space. Nothing rots here; the cold and the slow, tannin-heavy chemistry of the wood preserve every living curve in an unchanging, primordial equilibrium. If a branch-beam thickens, it takes a century to add a millimeter.

Yet the darkness is loud, and it is crowded with motion.

Sound travels with terrifying clarity through the dense, frigid water, bouncing off the taut, drum-like walls of the wooden architecture. There is a constant, deafening low-frequency roar—the sound of immense volumes of water overflowing the outer lip of the root-dam miles away, plunging down into the clouds below. Beneath that bass note is the incessant, overlapping clamor of the basin’s fauna. Without light to hunt or navigate by, everything here speaks.

Shoals of pale, heavy-bodied fish—millions of them, blind and plated in dull cartilage—move in slow, sparse ribbons through the vast empty spaces between the wooden arches, producing a continuous, percussive clicking that sounds like hail hitting slate. Clinging to the worn curves of the sunken terraces are massive, shell-less amphibians with wide, flattened ribs; they press their bellies against the resonant root-walls and groan, sending deep, cello-like vibrations shivering through the entire structure of the lake. 

When you touch one of the ancient, woven retaining walls, it does not feel like dead timber. Beneath the velvet slickness of the moss, it holds the faint, taut turgor of something still drawing sustenance from the air far above and the mineral-depleted water within. It hums with the overlapping songs of ten thousand unseen swimmers and the endless thunder of the unseen falls.

There are no seasons at this altitude, under this much water. No silt settles because there is no rock left to erode. The blind shoals trace the exact same broad, looping paths around the submerged pillars that their ancestors traced ten thousand years ago, guided by the unshifting acoustic shadows of the architecture. The cold water presses down, clear and black; the living basin holds it up in the sky; the noise fills the dark, immense and steady and entirely without urgency.
