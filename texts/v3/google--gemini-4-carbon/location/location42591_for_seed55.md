The water is cold—four degrees above freezing, dense and heavy, pressing uniformly from every angle. Far above, perhaps seventy meters up, there is a ceiling of grey sky and winter chop, though down here that surface registers only as a faint, bruised luminescence at midday, fading swiftly into absolute black for the remaining twenty hours. 

Beneath this weight of water, there is no natural seabed. For hundreds of kilometers in every direction, the floor of this inland sea is entirely paved, terraced, and walled in hand-quarried stone. 

Before the water rose—millennia ago, when this low basin was dry land—an entire continent’s worth of labor went into reshaping the earth. There is no raw bedrock exposed anywhere, no unworked clay. Every surface is composed of dry-stacked slate, mortared fieldstone, split granite blocks, and broad cobble causeways. It was built with bronze chisels, wooden wedges, lever-frames, and sledges. You can still feel the faint, rhythmic pitting of hand-tools beneath the silt, though age and the tireless, slow massage of the deep currents have rounded every ninety-degree corner into soft, pillowed curves. Lintels sag smoothly into their jambs; wide, low staircases have worn into rippled ramps; retaining walls bulge gently outward like the flanks of sleeping cattle, settled into a permanent, ancient equilibrium that gravity can no longer disturb.

Because the ancient builders terraced a lowland plain, the topography has no dramatic plunges or soaring spires. Instead, it is an endless, low-relief labyrinth of sunken courtyards, broad avenues, miles of low-walled paddocks, shallow canals within the sea, and endless clusters of roofless, single-story foundations. It is a continent-sized stonework grid, drowned and smoothed to its final resting state.

Out of this stone carpet grows the forest.

It is a cold-water jungle, lush and dense, thriving in the dim twilight where terrestrial plants would starve. Anchored directly to the dressed granite and wedged deep into the mortarless seams between flagstones are millions of holdfasts—thick, gnarled knuckles of cartilaginous tissue as tough as cured hide. From these rise towering kelps and broad-bladed, leathery laminarians. Their stipes are thick as human wrists, flexing lazily in the weak abyssal drift, hoisting vast, ruffled canopies thirty meters into the gloom. 

Where the tall kelp parts over wide plazas and avenues, lower tiers of vegetation crowd the stonework. Dense thickets of deep-red dulse and burgundy sea-oak bush out from the tops of boundary walls, absorbing the last blue-green photons that filter through the murky column above. Every courtyard floor is cushioned in ankle-deep turfs of fine, filamentous dark moss and wiry black wrack. In the sheltered lee of collapsed granaries and low aqueducts, pale, calcified coralline algae encrust the cobbles in slow-growing plates ofmuted lavender and chalky pink, sealing the ancient masonry together like living mortar.

The space is thick with presence, yet easy to move through if one moves slowly. Fronds brush against neighboring fronds; leathery ribbons ten meters long trail lazily across the tops of lintels and drape through empty window-frames like heavy wet curtains. Yet there is nothing frantic in the density. Without wind or wave-surge, the vegetation hangs suspended in the cold brine, buoyed by small, thick-walled gas bladders the size of plums. 

Nothing swims here. There are no darting silver schools in the avenues, no crabs clicking over the thresholds, no worms burrowing in the drift-silt. Perhaps the water is too mineral-poor, or locked behind distant sills that severed it from a living ocean ages ago, or perhaps the sheer cold and dark thinned the blooded creatures out until the last shell dissolved into the stone. Only the very smallest, quietest things remain: the occasional slow-creeping limpet rasping microscopic film from a carved doorstep, a few blind, translucent amphipods drifting like dust motes between the kelp stipes, and pale, thumb-sized anemones clustered beneath the undersides of stone benches, waiting months for a stray piece of shed frond to drift into their reach. 

Without scavengers to tear them apart, fallen kelp blades lie flat along the cobbled streets for decades, slowly Turning translucent from the edges inward as cold-loving bacteria patiently dismantle them, enriching a fine, velvet layer of dark peat-silt that gathers in the corners of rooms.

The soundscape is muffled by the immense mass of water overhead, reduced to a heavy, enveloping hush. There is no roar of surf, no calls, no snapping claws. If you stay entirely still on the flagstones of an intersection, the primary sound is the vast, low-frequency pressure of the sea itself—a near-subaudible hum born of continental currents shifting miles away. Closer at hand, there are only soft, rubbery friction sounds: the *shhh-thump* of a heavy kelp frond sliding slowly across a rounded granite sill as the tide turns, the faint, dry rasp of calcified weeds touching one another, and, once in a great while, the dull, muffled *clack* of a flat slate shingle finally slipping from an ancient eaves-course and settling flat onto the silt below.

Time moves here with the patience of geology. A current takes four hours to push the canopy ten degrees to the east, holds it there in motionless suspension while the faint grey bruise of noon passes overhead, and then takes four hours to lean the entire continent of dark greenery ten degrees to the west. Over centuries, the holdfasts pry a fraction of a millimeter deeper into the cracks between the stones, while the coralline crusts thicken by a hair's breadth to bind them back together. The walls do not fall; the streets do not clear; the cold never breaks. The built world and the cold forest have locked arms in the dark, entirely finished with the business of changing.
