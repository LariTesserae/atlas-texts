There is no rock beneath the valley, and there has never been dirt. If you pry up a floorplate of galvanized diamond-tread at the bottom of the basin, you do not find gravel or clay; you find a sub-floor of freshly poured gray concrete, still bearing the crisp, splintery grain of wooden formwork that isn’t there, and beneath that, a dense weave of copper busbars, glazed white porcelain insulators the size of oil drums, and dark cast-iron housings packed together like organs in a chest. Go down six levels through access hatches whose brass hinges still carry bright spiral shavings from the drill bit, and there is only older, equally unweathered machinery—riveted boiler shells, counterweights, lead-sheathed cables—all the way down.

From rim to rim, the basin is four miles across: a broad, stepped amphitheater of tiered switchyards, gantry terraces, cooling towers, and viaducts climbing into thin, cold high-altitude air. The sky above is the fierce, dark cobalt of eleven thousand feet, bleaching to white glare toward the horizon. The light is relentless. It strikes millions of square yards of zinc plating, brushed aluminum, fresh mill-scale steel, and white ceramic, bouncing back up from the valley floor so brightly that shadows under the catwalks are lit from beneath by cold metallic reflection. Every edge is razor-new. Concrete corners have not yet chipped; punched steel gussets still hold sharp burrs along their undersides; not a speck of red rust softens a single bolt head. The entire valley looks as though the scaffolding was struck twenty minutes ago, though nothing here moves fast enough to have been built in a human lifetime.

Everything is running, and almost nothing makes a sound.

Across the eastern terrace, a line of forty stamping presses rises eighty feet into the glare. Their flywheels—each thirty feet in diameter, spoked in green-painted iron—are turning, but at the pace of the minute hand on a railway clock. If you line up the edge of a rim against a distant smokestack and hold your breath, you can watch a millimeter of cold air open between them. Massive steel connecting rods descend by fractions of an inch per hour. When a die finally meets its plate—which happens somewhere in the valley two or three times an afternoon—there is no crash. The air at this altitude behaves as if it has forgotten how to carry sharp transients. A hundred tons of drop-forged steel shearing through sheet metal produces a dry, localized *tock*, no louder than two billiard balls kissing in a carpeted room next door, and even that small sound dies within twenty yards. Wind moves steadily across the upper trusses, cold enough to numb bare ears even in the blinding sun, yet it passes through miles of cabling and perforated girders without a whistle, pushing silently against the skin like cool water.

The moisture here has nowhere to soak in, so it clings. Nighttime temperatures drop toward freezing, and when the fierce morning sun hits the valley, every shaded pipe belly, turbine casing, and underside of I-beam breaks out in a dense, cold sweat. Beads of water coalesce on cast iron, run down along the threads of three-inch bolts, and gather in shallow stamped pans, oil-traps, and the sunken dimples of floorplates. Here and there, tall banks of radiator fins breathe out thin, chalk-white plumes of vapor that do not blow away so much as hang in bright, sharp-edged slabs before condensing directly onto nearby handrails, leaving them slick. Narrow open sluices, rectangular and riveted, carry two inches of clear, ice-cold runoff along the contours of the terraces, cascading silently down stepped spillways into settling tanks below.

In the corners where this condensation collects alongside the pale yellow tallow wept by huge journal bearings, plants have taken hold. They are sparse and tough: stiff gray-green sedges sprouting out of bolt-cups, flat rosettes of waxy, silver-haired leaves wedged into expansion joints, and wire-thin creepers tracing the slightly warm copper conduits that run out from transformer banks. Their roots never rot because there is no soil—only braided mats of white fiber gripping grease, zinc dust, and cold water.

The animals, however, are everywhere.

Thousands of pale, heavy-coated birds—something like snowcocks or large, blunt-billed choughs with plumage the matte gray of primer paint—pack the tiered rows of porcelain insulators, sleeping in the bright glare or picking mineral crusts from the seams of boilers. Small, flat-bodied crabs the color of nickels sidle out of flooded cable-trays to scrape tallow from slowly turning shafts. And moving across the dense latticework of catwalks, pipe-bridges, and slanted buttresses are herds of long-bodied, slate-colored ungulates with wide, square pupils and horns corrugated like rebar. They step delicately over high-voltage busbars, drink from the steel sluices, and chew the greasy sedge and tallow-cake extruded from axle housings. Their hooves are soft-rimmed and rubbery; a herd of sixty passing overhead on a perforated steel grating makes only a faint, papery patter, like dry beans poured slowly into a sack.

Watch the herds for ten minutes, and the geometry of the valley begins to come apart.

A doe walks to the dead-end of a cantilevered steel beam jutting out three hundred feet above a sunken condenser pit. Two miles away, across the dazzling gulf of the valley, a broad concrete viaduct runs horizontally along the opposite wall. From the precise angle of the tall brass-and-mirror surveying towers that stand at intervals along the ridges, the raw end of the near beam visually overlaps the parapet of the distant viaduct. The doe does not hesitate. She lifts a forehoof, steps off the raw steel burr in the foreground, and sets her weight down on the concrete two miles away—shrinking instantly across the intermediate air without falling, without transition, her hindquarters briefly huge and sharp in the near light while her shoulders are already small and blue-hazes-distant on the far terrace. A moment later the rest of her follows, and she trots quietly along the viaduct.

The valley is dense with these structural disagreements, built directly into the fabric of the plant and operating under patient, unyielding rules. Whenever one of the great surveying towers completes a fractional rotation on its geared turntable—a cycle that takes roughly eleven weeks—new alignments lock into place across the basin, and old bridges sever cleanly into thin air. The animals know the schedules better than anything else could; flocks of primer-gray birds gather hours early at the end of a severed handrail, waiting calmly in the cold sun for a distant boiler-catwalk to drift behind it and become adjacent.

Other breakdowns have nothing to do with sightlines. Down in the denser middle tiers, where pipelines, chain-drives, ladder-cages, and exhaust cowls crowd within inches of one another, solid objects frequently share volume without distress. A four-foot riveted water main passes straight through the cast-iron flywheel of a compressor; as the wheel inches downward over the course of a day, its solid iron spokes pass seamlessly through the sweating steel of the pipe without leaking a drop, leaving only a faint, glistening smear of frost along the line of intersection. Nearby, heavy steel drive-chains emerge taut and greased from floor housings, climb forty feet into the blinding sky, pass over no pulley at all, and turn sharply downward around an empty cylinder of bright blue air that nonetheless takes the full, groaning load of forty tons, dimpling the links with invisible teeth.

There are pockets of misplaced interiority scattered out in the open glare. You can walk along an unroofed steel causeway under the noon sun and step directly into the lighting conditions of a windowless basement: for twelve square feet of open grating, nothing overhead blocks the sky, yet your coat turns dark gray, the blinding zinc floor drops to a dim, oily amber lit as if by a single weak filament bulb somewhere behind your shoulder, and the crisp high-altitude chill gives way to the stagnant, warm smell of shellac and old armature windings. A goat resting in the middle of such a patch will have its sunlit rump bleached white by UV glare while its head, two feet forward in empty air, lies in deep indoor gloom, blinking slowly at nothing.

Even motion does not always hold together cleanly. In the western switchyards, where dense thickets of vertical breaker-poles stand packed like bamboo, small mouse-like creatures with thick silver fur dart between the condensation pans. When they move quickly, they misregister—a faint, translucent second body trailing three inches behind the solid one, slightly blue at the edges, catching up only when the animal stops to drink and the two halves snap silently back into one.

Above it all, along the highest terraced rim where the air tastes thin and metallic as a coin, a colossal gantry crane spans a quarter-mile of tightly layered turbine halls. Its shadow falls sharp and black across a thousand overlapping stairways, insulators, and basking herds—except that the shadow does not match the crane. It is the shadow of a steep, forested mountain ridge that has never existed here, complete with the ragged silhouettes of wind-bent pines swaying slightly in a breeze that isn't blowing, sliding inch by slow inch across cold, bright, freshly stamped steel.
