At two thousand meters, the air ought to be cold, and thirty feet above the valley floor it probably is. Down on the deck, though, the heat is wet and fierce, radiating straight up from the living pavement like breath from a kiln. 

There is no rock here, and no soil. From one rim of the high basin to the other—a span of several miles under a clear, ordinary, pale-blue sky—the entire terrain is a continuous, constructed platform of tight-grained vegetal plate. Underfoot, the surface is divided into crisp, six-sided slabs of dense, horn-like cellulose, fitted together with the precision of masonry, though thin seams of bright green meristem still pulse between them. Every slab is canted at a slight, deliberate grade, channeling constant sheets of warm, clear water toward straight-edged gutters that score the basin floor in long geometric parallels. Everything has the hard, unweathered sharpness of something finished ten minutes ago: ninety-degree curbs, beveled sluices, unchipped corners gleaming wetly in the midday sun. Nothing has had time to wear down, and there is no lichen, no moss, no rot—not a single insect skittering across the wet seams, not a bird in the wide sky above.

For all the totality of this living stonework, the basin is startlingly empty. You can see for miles across flat, steaming esplanades where nothing rises higher than an ankle-deep retaining lip. Only at wide, irregular intervals—half a mile here, four hundred yards there—do the vertical structures break the plane, standing isolated against the distant white clouds like monuments in an abandoned fairground. 

None of them stay what they are for very long.

Half a mile out on the eastern flat, a cantilevered aqueduct—grown entirely from bundled, octagonal beams of pale yellow heartwood—is in the middle of extending itself over an empty plaza. You can see it moving. At its leading edge, thick, translucent shoots extrude horizontally from a collar of swollen green tissue, hardening and squaring off their own angles even as they push into open air. Inside the supporting piers, immense hydraulic pressures are at work; the sound carries clearly across the open water-slicked flats, a deep, rhythmic thudding like subterranean rams, followed by the high, sustained hiss of steam and sap venting through slit-valves along the underside of the arch. Suddenly, the cantilever outgrows its counterweight. A tremor runs back through three hundred yards of taut, green trusswork. With a noise like a cannon shot, a tension cable thick as a mast parts at the base; the entire forward span whips upward, splits cleanly along its grain, and crashes down onto the plaza in a spray of scalding water.

Almost before the echo finishes bouncing off the far side of the basin, the deck beneath the wreckage begins to react. Flaps in the hexagonal paving curl back on fleshy hinges; pale, digestive tendrils surge out over the fallen timber, dissolving the crisp beams into milky slurry that rushes away down the nearest sluice. Within twenty minutes the plaza will be swept bare again, hot and clean and empty, while two hundred yards away the deck is already blistering upward into a fresh, sharp-cornered plinth.

This is the steady state of the plateau: frantic, purposeless revision. Farther south, three tall, hollow chimneys of braided vine-wall lean inward to form a tripod, pumping geysers of mist fifty feet into the thin mountain air; where the hot vapor hits the colder upper currents, it condenses instantly and falls back in localized, drenching curtains of rain that strike the flat pavement with a continuous, drumming roar. A quarter-mile west of the tripod, a broad stepped amphitheater—every riser knife-sharp and slick with pale mucilage—is rapidly unbuilding itself, its tiers folding inward like origami as the turgor pressure drains out of its foundations, leaving behind a sunken square pool that immediately begins to fill from square-mouthed spouts along its rim. Elsewhere, solitary arches span nothing; staircases climb forty feet into clear daylight, terminate in a clean, saw-edged landing, and split apart down the middle to become twin buttresses bracing an invisible wall.

The heat comes from the work itself. Cellular division at this speed is exothermic, a vast, distributed furnace burning stored sugars to drive pistons of fluid and extrude tons of fresh structural lignin every hour. Touch a newly risen wall—pale cream, still soft enough to dent with a fingernail before the sun oxidizes it to mahogany—and it feels feverish, vibrating faintly with the rush of pressurized water climbing through parallel xylem conduits just beneath the skin. The water smells faintly sweet, like boiled cane and crushed green stems, without a trace of stagnant marshiness or decay.

Noise travels strangely across such an open, hard-surfaced expanse. Without windbreaks, foliage, or soft earth to absorb it, every sound stays distinct and loud: the constant *shhh-shhh* of sheet-water rushing down graded planes; the sharp, percussive *clack* of check-valves snapping shut inside subterranean conduits beneath your feet; the groan and creak of green wood bending under tremendous loads; the sudden, wet *whump* of a pressure-bladder rupturing inside a distant tower. It sounds like a shipyard or a loom-hall, full of urgency and heavy labor, which makes the visual stillness of the wide horizons—and the total absence of any moving creature—all the more disorienting. Nothing crawls out of the ruptured beams. Nothing drinks from the warm, geometric pools. Nothing walks the causeways that shoot straight as survey lines across the wet flats, meet at precise intersections, and then buckle upward into peaked roofs that shed rain onto empty tiles.

Toward the edges of the high basin, where the plateau meets the drop-off into the surrounding range, the construction grows steeper and more defensive, throwing up tiered parapets and angled wind-baffles against the colder gales rising from below. Even there, the boundary is absolute: crisp, mortised living bulwarks jutting out over raw mountain air, weeping gallons of excess water off their sharp eaves into the abyss. Looking back inward from those parapets, under the ordinary brightness of the afternoon sun, the whole miles-wide expanse gleams like wet slate—a sparse, steaming Draftsman's yard at the top of the world, drafting, demolishing, and redrafting an architecture for nobody at all.
