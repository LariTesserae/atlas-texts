There is no twilight here, no starlight, no faint fungal glow in the rot, and no indicator lamps on the machines. Light simply is not part of the physics of the place. Space has no appearance; it has only volume, resistance, warmth, wetness, and the time it takes a sound to leave a surface and come back.

The land is an immense, high tableland—a continental plateau lifted miles above lower, crushing atmospheric basins that never clear. Because of the altitude, the air moves fast and thin enough to breathe easily, yet it carries the relentless thermal exhaust of the depths below. Great wet updrafts crest the distant rims of the massif and roll inland for thousands of miles, cooling just enough against the stone to dump their moisture as perpetual warm rain, thick drifting fog-banks, and heavy, beading sweat that coats every square inch of the world. Everything is roughly the temperature of an open mouth.

Underfoot, the bedrock has been dissolved and smoothed over millions of years into an intricate, settled karst of broad stone bowls, rounded hummocks, and braided spillways. There isn’t a sharp edge left on the plateau. Even the steepest drops have been licked into bulbous, fluted draperies of stone by tepid runoff. Water stands in shallow, terraced marshes a thousand miles wide, spilling endlessly over smoothed lips of limestone and basalt into the next basin down, and the next, so that the ground itself produces a permanent, foundational hiss—billions of small spillages blending into wide, rushing sheets whenever a raincell passes overhead.

Nothing photosynthesizes, yet the plateau is choked with growth. The vegetation feeds on motion, heat differentials, and the mineral soup dissolved in the runoff. Where the wind pushes hardest across the rounded ridges, thick stands of kinetic flora crowd shoulder to shoulder: squat, muscular trunks anchored into rock fissures by cable-like roots, topped with wide, ribbed membranes of rubbery cartilage that thrash and shudder continuously in the warm gusts, converting mechanical flex into chemical energy. In the calmer, flooded depressions, dense thickets of heat-drinking fronds hang in heavy, slick curtains, their surfaces textured like velvet or gills to strip dissolved sulfur, ammonia, and trace metals directly from the condensing mist. To push through a stand of growth is to press between wet, lukewarm layers of bladder-weed, stiff rattling reeds, and hanging root-mats so thick they trap pockets of stagnant, musky air inside their tangles.

Because nothing sees, everything listens, vibrates, or smells, and the noise is staggering.

The wind and the spilling water are only the floor of the soundscape. Above that rises a dense, stratified racket of clicks, buzzes, low-frequency booms, and rapid-fire frequency sweeps. The air is thick with flying and gliding fauna—leathery, multi-winged things with broad acoustic dish-faces—zipping millimeters past soaked branches at full speed, mapping the tangled canopy through needle-tight beams of ultrasound. Larger wading and climbing animals communicate in lower registers that roll unimpeded through the wet foliage: hollow, percussive knocks made by snapping ossified throat-plates, or chest-rattling bass hums that travel equally well through air and ankle-deep water. 

Even the plants participate in the acoustic architecture. Over eons of coevolution with echolocating animals, the flora has shaped sound the way sunlit forests shape shadow. Certain fleshy creepers grow hollow, foam-lined baffles that swallow sonar completely, creating pockets of sudden acoustic deadness where soft-bodied grazers hide in writhing heaps. Other growths lift hard, concave dishes of rigid cellulose that bounce a flyer’s hunting-click back to it falsified—amplified and delayed so an empty cup of wet leaves rings out in the dark with the exact acoustic signature of a fat, segmented burrower, luring pollinators in to smear themselves against sticky spore-pads.

Scattered miles apart across this endless, roaring wet highland sit the machines.

There are no roads connecting them, no fences, and no roofs built for human comfort—only isolated, heavy industrial installations anchored directly into the ancient stone. A geothermal condenser tower rises out of a terraced marsh, forty stories of rusted, moss-furred cast iron and sweating copper pipework. Four miles east, an automated pump-jack the size of a barge straddles a mineral seep, its massive counterweights rising and plunging through the wet fern-mats on a six-second cycle: *clank-hiss, clank-hiss*, dayless year after dayless year. Overhead, spanning wide flooded valleys from pylon to unseen pylon, thick braided haulage cables hum under terrific tension; every twenty minutes, an ore-bucket rolls past high in the mist on greased steel pulleys, screaming briefly as its flanges take the guide-rail before vanishing back into the rain-noise.

None of this machinery carries a single bulb, dial face, or painted warning stripe. Built for a world without eyes, their exteriors are purely structural and tactile. Inspection hatches are marked with deep cast-iron notches and raised pin-patterns; exhaust vents simply jut out wherever pressure dictates, blasting plumes of hot, oily steam sideways into the whipping fronds. When an autonomous maintenance crawler moves between stations—a heavy, six-tracked rig smelling of hot grease, ozone, and wet iron—it navigates by spinning millimeter-wave radar horns and long, spring-steel whisker booms that whip and scrape through the brush ahead of its hull.

The ecosystem has long since folded the industry into its own rhythms. The great thumping pump-stations shake the bedrock in steady, predictable pulses that blind subterranean worms use to orient their migrations across hundreds of miles. Warm exhaust ducts are plastered three feet thick in shivering mats of heat-loving tubers, which in turn draw packs of long-limbed, crab-jawed scavengers that clatter expertly across moving piston rods, timing their leaps between strokes purely by the rush of displaced air and the whine of the bearings. High on the latticework of condenser towers, territorial ambush predators hang upside down in the deafening roar of pressure-relief valves, using the machine’s colossal wall of white noise to mask their own breathing while they wait with outstretched, sensory-haired forelimbs to snatch whatever the wind blows past the girders.

Weather rolls across the plateau on a continental scale—vast, slow-moving fronts of barometric drop that make the ears pop and set every membrane-tree for five hundred miles thrashing at a higher pitch. When a major front arrives, the warm drizzle thickens into horizontal sheets of tepid downpour that drown out high-frequency sonar altogether. For hours, or days, the flyers go to ground, clinging in dense, slick clusters under the undersides of stone ledges and warm engine cowlings. In that temporary blinding of the upper air, the ground-dwellers surge out of the flooded basins: heavy, multi-legged amphibians and plated grazers sloshing through the rising spillways in huge, jostling herds, snapping off mouthfuls of vibrating reed while the rain hammers on shell, leaf, weathered limestone, and unlit steel alike.
