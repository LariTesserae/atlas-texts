At the very top of the spire, the mountain stops being solid and becomes a cage. 

Six curved ribs of pale, wind-polished limestone lean inward like the fingers of an upturned hand, enclosing a space no larger than a village church. Outside those stone ribs, there is nothing for thousands of feet in any direction—only the blinding, cobalt drop of extreme altitude and the flat white floor of the cloud deck far below. Inside the ribs, there is almost nothing either. The center of the space has no floor. It is a vertical throat of empty air plunging straight down into the dark core of the peak, so that ninety percent of this bounded room is pure, dizzying void.

Yet the emptiness is ferocious. The hollow core of the spire acts as a chimney, drawing warmer air from the unseen valleys miles below and firing it straight up through the gap. Wind does not blow across this place; it erupts continuously through the middle of it. 

When wisps of vapor are sucked up the shaft, they hit the cold, high-altitude air of the lantern and condense instantly against the encircling rock. The ancient stone—smoothed over millennia into rounded, bone-like flutes and deep,settled grooves—weeps without stopping. Runnels of icy water sheet down the inner curves of the ribs, gather on overhanging lips, and drip into the abyss, only for the relentless updraft to catch the falling drops, shatter them into bright spray, and hurl them straight back up again. Water hangs in a perpetual cycle here: streaming down rock, tearing loose into the void, rising as mist, and plastering itself back onto the upper arches. In the clear, unfiltered daylight of the upper sky, a dozen faint, fragmented rainbows flicker and vanish in the central shaft every minute.

Because the rock never dries and the updraft softens the worst of the high-altitude freeze, the narrow perimeter of stone is chokingly lush, even while the space it frames remains completely hollow. Every inward-facing ledge, socket, and rib is upholstered in knee-deep, emerald cushion-mosses saturated like sponges. From these pads grow dense colonies of leathery rosette plants, purple-veined ferns, and long, ribbon-like vines with hollow, air-bladdered stems. Instead of hanging down, these vines stream upward in the constant draft, waving vertically like kelp in a tidal race, their pale undersides flashing in the sun. Small, bulbous orchids cling to the undersides of stone bridges, drinking the upward-falling rain.

The noise inside this stone crown is immense and unceasing. First there is the rock itself: millennia of grit-laden wind have bored smooth keyholes and narrow slots through the outer edges of the ribs, turning the entire peak into a monumental wind-instrument that hums a low, chest-rattling chord, punctuated by sharp whistles whenever a crosswind shears past the summit. 

Over that drone, the animal life screams to be heard. The hollow center is crowded not with matter, but with trajectories. Hundreds of dark, sickle-winged swifts hang motionless in the rising column of air, wings barely twitching, snapping up the clouds of fat, wooly-bodied midges and metallic beetles that get funneled up the chimney from warmer elevations below. The birds shriek in high, metallic staccatos that bounce sharply off the wet limestone. Along the moss-thickened ribs, colonies of short-tailed, rust-colored pikas dart between root-tangles, barking alarm calls that sound like struck flint whenever a shadow crosses the sun. Two or three broad-winged raptors—pale, heavy-taloned things adapted to thin air—circle tight inside the ribs, dropping folded like stones down the shaft and shooting back up moments later with a swift pinned in one foot, scattering red feathers that spin upward and catch in the inverted vines. Even the mosses click and rustle with pale, translucent land-crabs no bigger than thumbnails, grazing on algae in the streaming runoff.

Around the margins, tucked carefully out of the main blast of the chimney, are the spare, weathered marks of human hands. No one lives here—there isn't enough flat stone to lie down without rolling into the shaft—but people climb up to harvest what the chimney concentrates. Across a ten-foot gap between two eastern ribs, three peeled spruce poles have been lashed together with rawhide thongs, gray and iron-hard with age, forming a narrow catwalk slick with green slime. Shallow, curving gutters have been pecked into the limestone with stone hammers, diverting the weeping condensation into a pair of deep, natural potholes lined with Pitch-sealed basketry to hold clear drinking water.

Higher up, stretched across the slots between the stone fingers where the swifts exit into the open sky, hang loose nets knotted from sinew and tough, braided plant fiber. Most are slack and tattered, flapping violently like prayer flags in the gale, weighted at their corners with grooved river-cobbles carried up from another world entirely. Driven into hairline cracks along the safest ledges are dozens of carved bone pitons and wooden pegs, polished shiny where generations of hands have hauled themselves over the lip. Wedged beside one water-basin sits a cache no bigger than a breadbox: three chipped obsidian blades wrapped in oiled skin, a bundle of snares, and a hollow vulture-ulna whistle left wedged in a stone cleft so that it blows a single, thin, artificial note endlessly into the natural roar.

Nothing stays still. Cloud-shreds shoot from the floor and dissolve into blinding blue sky in the span of three breaths; sunlight slams against the wet moss, turns the rising mist brilliant white, then winks out as a stray vapor-bank swallows the spire, turning the cage into a roaring gray drum before blowing clear again five seconds later. Water pours down, sprays up, and clings; feathers and torn leaves spiral toward the apex and slip out between the stone fingertips into the vast, thin emptiness surrounding the mountain. The rock holds its ancient, eroded curve, slick and resonant, framing a loud, wet, furiously living knot of air suspended miles above the earth.
