The settlement occupies a single, bowl-shaped hollow near the absolute apex of a granite spire, so high above the planetary floor that the world below is entirely hidden by a permanent, slate-colored sea of dust. The rock here has forgotten how to break. Over millions of years of high-altitude wind, every crag, ledge, and outcropping has been scoured down to smooth, rounded, elephant-hide curves. There is no gravel, no scree, no sharp edges—only vast, polished shoulders of stone plunging away into the dry, murk-choked abyss on all sides, cradling a compact hollow roughly the size of an amphitheater.

Above, the sky is a dim, perpetual twilight, bruised purple at the zenith and yellowing toward the dust-horizon, filtered through miles of suspended mineral silt. Yet the hollow itself is warm. A deep, exhausted geothermal vein breathes steadily through pores in the ancient stone, holding the enclosed pocket at the temperature of skin even in the thin upper air. 

Because there is no rain, no runoff, and no groundwater, nothing flows here. Yet the hollow is choked with green and russet life. Thick, waxy-leaved vines as heavy as ship’s hawsers drape across the space, anchored into the smooth granite by dry, resinous suction pads. Plump, barrel-stemmed shrubs crowd every concavity, their swollen leaves overlapping like scales, hoarding moisture drawn molecule by molecule from the faint, humid exhalations of the warm rock and the breath of the things living among them. Gray-green beard-lichens hang in dense curtains, catching the ambient silt and knitting it with shed leaves into thick, dry, spongy mats that serve as soil.

The town is built into, around, and out of this tangle, packed so tightly into the stone bowl that there is no clear boundary between architecture, cliff, and thicket. Generations of builders using stone adzes, bone chisels, and rawhide bindings have stacked dry-stone walls of gathered, wind-polished boulders, fitting the smooth curves together with extraordinary patience and chinking the gaps with dried lichen-paste. Because floor space in the hollow is scarce, the settlement rises in tiers of low, flat-roofed huts, timber scaffolds made from the iron-hard wood of dead shrubs, and woven wicker galleries slung across the central gap like rigid hammocks. Everything is worn smooth by hands, feet, and centuries of use. Wooden ladder-rungs are bowed in the middle; stone thresholds are dished like shallow bowls; hand-carved horn hooks protrude from every post, hung with gourds, coiled fiber ropes, and flint-bladed knives.

It is difficult to move five paces without brushing against masonry, foliage, or flesh. The air is dim under the overlapping canopies of braided vine-bridges and succulent boughs, lit only by the dull ambient purple from above and small, smoldering tallow-and-dung braziers tucked into stone niches. 

In this warm gloom, animal life is everywhere, crowding the ledges and rafters alongside the human inhabitants. Thousands of fat, blind-eyed beetles click slowly over the dry bark, grazing on dust-fungi. Broad-winged, flightless moths the size of cupped hands cling in overlapping rows to the undersides of the woven walkways, vibrating their thoraxes to stay warm. Plump, short-legged reptiles with dry, pebbled skin pile atop one another near the geothermal vents in sluggish, tangled heaps, utterly indifferent to the sandaled feet stepping over them. Overhead, hundreds of small, russet-furred gliders and hook-billed cliff-birds roost in the wicker eaves and dense branches, packed wing-to-shoulder. 

Because everything is enclosed by smooth rock and muffled by layers of thick leaves and woven matting, the sound has nowhere to go. The hollow hums and rattles continuously. There is no wind inside the bowl—only a slow, breath-like circulation of warm air rising from the stone—so the noise is entirely biological and domestic. It is a thick, constant din: the dry, rasping chorus of ten thousand insects stridulating in the dark leaves; the low, gravelly chuckling and territorial clacking of the cliff-birds; the steady *tap-tap-scrape* of flint scrapers against cured hide; the rhythmic grinding of dry tubers and roasted beetle-shells in heavy stone mortars; the creak of heavily laden rope bridges shifting weight; low voices talking across three feet of gloom from one doorway to the next. 

Nothing happens quickly. A dropped scrap of rind vanishes beneath a slow-moving mound of beetles over the course of an afternoon. Water is never poured; moisture is passed exclusively through solids—chewed pulp, succulent flesh, steamed roots wrapped in leather—conserved inside living bodies and sealed clay jars smeared with tree-wax. Outside the lip of the granite bowl, the ancient spire drops vertical miles into a dead, dry haze, entirely silent and unchanged since before memory. Inside, pressed together in the dim warmth, stone, vine, beast, and wall lean into one another so closely they hold each other up.
