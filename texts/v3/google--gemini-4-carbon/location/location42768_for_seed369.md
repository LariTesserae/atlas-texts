High up near the treeline, where the air ought to be thin and sharp, the mountain drops inward to form a shallow, steep-sided stone court no wider across than a cathedral nave. There is no pass through it and no stream running out of its lower lip; it is a closed, settled dimple in old grey limestone, worn smooth ages ago into continuous, slumped curves without scree or jagged ledges. Overhead, the sky is plain, bright, and very close, pouring clear mountain daylight straight down into the bowl.

Inside the rim, the air is startlingly warm and heavy with moisture. The rock walls do not gush or cascade, but they sweat continuously from horizontal bedding planes—warm, mineral-sweet groundwater rising from deep below the ridge and sheeting over the stone in films a millimeter thick. Touch the rock anywhere and it feels like the flank of a resting animal: slick, tepid, coated in a clear glaze of lime and warm water that never dries and never hurries.

What makes the place immediately strange to the eye is how empty it is, and how overwhelmingly green. There is no brush here, no tangle of creepers, no leaf litter, no intermediate scrub crowding the floor. Between one living thing and the next lie ten or fifteen paces of clean, pale, water-darkened stone, open to the sun. Yet every plant that does grow in the basin is monstrously lush, swollen with warmth and perpetual wet into huge, solitary architecture.

Seven or eight of what look like trunkless canopy trees stand spaced across the floor at wide, regular intervals, rooted in deep solution pits. Each consists of a cluster of green, wrist-thick stalks rising twelve feet into the clear light before opening into horizontal parasols of tough, glossy foliage three yards wide. Their leaves are thick as saddle leather, pumped tight with turgor, edged in upturned rims like lily pads hoisted into the air. Farther toward the walls sit rounded mounds of liverwort the size of haystacks—unbroken, velvety domes of saturated emerald that swallow sound and shed water from their flanks without a single stray stem or weed breaking their curves. Nothing competes by crowding; whatever falls on the bare stone between the pits is washed slowly down into narrow drainage cracks or dissolved in the warm, alkaline film before it can root. The whole court reads like a gallery of wet green monuments arranged with immense breathing room under an open sky.

And yet the place is deafening.

To step over the stone lip is to walk into a steady, percussive racket that bounces endlessly off the curved limestone walls. High up on the overhanging rims, the seeping ground-sweat gathers along rounded stone drips and lets go in fat, heavy gouts—water drops the size of plums falling thirty feet through still air to strike the taut, upturned parasol leaves below. Each leaf is stretched tight over hollow, air-filled ribs, and each strike hits like a mallet on wet rawhide. *Thwack. Boom. Clack-boom.* Because there are dozens of drips and eight great clusters of leaves at different heights and tensions, the basin is filled with an unceasing, overlapping battery of drum-strokes, amplified by the concave stone until you can feel the sharper slaps in your teeth.

Underneath that bright clatter runs a slower, heavier noise from the floor itself. Beneath the pale rock lie flooded karst chambers that fill drop by drop over twenty or thirty minutes until a hidden stone bend primes itself like a siphon; then the rock exhales all at once through fist-sized blowholes in the floor—a long, wet, subterranean *chug-roar* of displaced air and warm water gurgling up over the stone, flooding the flats ankle-deep for two minutes before draining silently back down through hairline seams. Nothing moves fast enough to see it building, and then the whole floor clears its throat at once, shaking the puddles, before settling back into its half-hour wait.

Almost nothing with legs lives here. Once in a great while, a flat, translucent-bellied crab no bigger than a thumb edges sideways out of a warm blowhole, scraping microscopic algae off the wet glaze with spoon-tipped claws, wholly indifferent to the noise, taking ten minutes to cross the width of a human foot. High on a dry patch of the upper rim, a solitary black wasp may hover, drink from the warm seep, and leave again over the ridge. There are no birds; the drumming drives them off, or else there are no insects among the spotless, spacing-kept plants to hold them. Even the plants reproduce on an almost geological schedule. From the underside of the giant parasols hang single, green, gourd-like seed bladders that take seasons to swell. Their stems do not rot; instead, the constant hammering of water drops on the leaf above slowly, microscopically fatigues the woody joint, millions of impacts loosening fiber from fiber over two or three years until the heavy pod finally drops onto the flooded stone, splits, and waits months for a siphon-surge strong enough to roll it toward an unoccupied pit.

Nothing has been built here. There is no shelter, no cairn, no cleared path—there is nothing to clear—and no charcoal, because nothing in the bowl would burn and no one would stay the night inside such a din. 

Still, hands have been at work in small, patient ways that borrow the slow mechanics of the basin. In three of the warm, shallow troughs where water lingers longest between siphon-flushes, bundles of stripped mountain bark lie submerged, pinned down under river cobbles carried up from some valley miles below. Left in the warm, mineral-alkaline seep, the tough inner fibers ret and soften by themselves over weeks without tending. Beside the largest of the parasol-plants, someone has wedged a palm-sized flake of dark, knapped chert into a split in a low stalk, angling it so the plant’s slow, clear mucilage drips off the stone edge into a folded cup made from a dead leaf sheet pinned together with bone awl-splinters. 

Most subtly of all, on the eastern wall where a low overhang sheds a steady curtain of drops onto a juvenile leaf-drum, the limestone drip-lip above has been pecked away with a hammerstone—rough, pale bruises in the grey rind—shifting the drip point three inches to the left so the water misses the resonant center of the leaf and strikes its soft, curled margin instead. In that one ten-foot corner of the bowl, and nowhere else, the sharp *crack* of falling water drops to a dull, wet *tup*, carving out a small pocket of bearable quiet where two people might squat on their heels on the warm stone, scrape retted bark with flint edges in the clear sunlight, and hear each other speak over the thunder of the leaves.
