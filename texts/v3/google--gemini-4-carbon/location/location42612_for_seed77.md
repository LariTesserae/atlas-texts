As far as the curvature of the earth permits, the ground is an unbroken expanse of unbaked clay and packed, straw-bound earth, laid down in sweeping, stepped terraces that run for thousands of miles. There is no underlying bedrock exposed anywhere, no wild dirt, no accidental hill. The entire low-lying basin is a single, ongoing earthwork of continental proportions, freshly shaped and entirely devoid of machinery, metal, or builders.

The geometry of the place is crisp and raw. The low retaining walls that divide the shallow, mile-wide pans have sharp, unweathered ninety-degree corners, their vertical faces still bearing the faint, sweeping striations of giant trowels or smoothing-boards. Nothing has crumbled yet. No gullies have been cut into the embankments by time. Along the outer reaches of the plains, new mud bricks—each the size of a flagstones, dark brown and heavy with chopped reed fiber—sit drying in neat, staggered rows five miles long, shrinking slightly in the fierce heat and pulling away from one another to leave hairline gaps of shadow. 

The heat is immense and heavy, pressing down from a clear, pale-blue afternoon sky. Sunlight strikes the pale ochre and damp umber of the terraces directly, baking the top half-inch of the plains into a tight, drum-skin crust while the deeper mass of the mud retains a dark, malleable coolness. Because the earth is still curing on a planetary scale, it breathes moisture into the air. Shimmering columns of thermal vapor rise continuously from the damper flats. Five miles up, this rising sweat gathers into isolated, flat-bottomed clouds that drift lazily across the empty expanse. 

Every few hours, one of these clouds reaches saturation and collapses into a brief, localized curtain of warm rain. From thirty miles away, you can watch a grey pillar of water drag slowly across five or six adjacent mud-pans, darkening the pale clay to the color of wet slate, filling the shallow geometric catch-basins to a depth of two inches, and vanishing into steam again before it has traveled ten miles. 

Where the rain has recently passed, the landscape’s quiet, relentless activity accelerates. 

In the wet mortar between the drying tiles, and along the shallow drainage channels cut with plumb-line precision across the flats, sparse colonies of stiff, blue-green sedge and jointed horsetail rush upward. Their seeds are already mixed into the foundational mud. Triggered by the warm standing water, they sprout almost visibly, pushing thick, waxy shoots through the soft silt before the sun can bake the surface shut again. Elsewhere, wide, lonely patches of grey-green gourd vines unroll across the fresh brickwork, advancing several inches an afternoon, their broad leaves tilting edgewise at midday to survive the glare, their tendrils gripping the sharp, newly molded ridges of the earthen dikes. There are miles of bare, baking ochre between each patch of green, giving the plain an open, uncrowded rhythm—vast plates of tawny clay punctuated only rarely by dark, damp smears of water and low clusters of salt-tolerant reeds.

Nothing walks here. There are no tracks pressed into the soft mud before it sets, no burrows dug into the pristine corners of the stepped embankments, no insects whining over the warm puddles, no birds riding the thermals. The absence of animals gives the air a profound, heavy stillness. 

Yet the silence is never entirely complete; it is full of the minute, persistent sounds of raw earth adjusting to the sun. If you stand still on a dry causeway, you can hear the faint, rhythmic ticking of ten million square yards of mud shrinking as it cures, tiny tension-cracks snapping open like dry twigs beneath the surface. When a distant thermal sweeps past, there is the dry, papery whisper of stiff sedge blades rubbing together, or the soft *shhh* of dry clay-dust skating effortlessly across a smooth, miles-wide courtyard until it catches against a two-foot step. Where water sits in a newly flooded pan, the mud drinks it down with a faint, continuous fizzing of escaping air bubbles, millions of tiny pores opening and closing across the basin floor. 

The landscape is caught in the exact moment after creation and before ruin. The sun moves across the unobstructed sky, shifting sharp, clean-edged shadows from the western sides of the low walls to the eastern sides. Water evaporates, rises, condenses, falls, and runs along flawless, hand-smoothed channels that have never known a flood strong enough to scar them. In the dampened troughs, roots knit silently into the raw brick, anchoring the mud just as it hardens into stone beneath the blazing light.
