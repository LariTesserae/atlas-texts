The light hits you before the cold does, though the cold is absolute. At three miles above any theoretical sea, the air is thin, dry at its upper boundary, and stripped of haze, turning the sky a bleached, ferocious white-violet. The sun does not warm this plateau; it audits it, pouring down an unfiltered glare that rebounds off unweathered quartz veins, fresh fault-scarps, and miles of gleaming, grease-slicked carapace. There are no soft horizons here. The continent is newly heaved up from the crust—geologically infant, full of knife-edged basalt steps, raw hexagonal columns, and talus slopes of rock split so recently the edges would slice leather. 

Yet you can barely see the stone. There is no room left on it.

From one jagged horizon to the next, across four hundred miles of uplifted shield, the plateau is packed solid with bodies and growth, wedged into every fracture and stacked three deep on the flats. Nothing here was built—there isn't a straight wall, a forged bolt, a road, or a ruin anywhere on the landmass—and yet the entire landscape operates with the deafening, oiled, high-pressure certainty of a foundry floor.

The fauna are immense, sluggish, and overwhelming in number, pressed flank-to-casing like cobblestones. Most are variations on heavy, segmented, multi-limbed grazers and filter-feeders the size of locomotives or shipping containers, shelled in layered plates of biologically precipitated iron sulfide and dull, pitted nickel. They do not dart or roam. They inch. A herd the size of a terrestrial province migrates at the pace of creeping glacier ice, taking weeks to cross a single sharp-lipped ravine. Their limbs lock and unlock on hydraulic joints pressurized by thick, glycol-laced blood that refuses to freeze even at thirty below zero. Inside them, metabolic furnaces burn crushed pyrite and tough, fibrous flora at high heat, venting rhythmic plumes of sulfur-sweet steam through dorsal chimneys ringed in stiff, frosty cilia.

That exhaust is where the water lives. 

The sky itself never rains—it is far too cold and high—but down in the packed three-meter stratum where the animals and plants press together, everything is perpetually, greasily damp. Millions of biological vents exhale warm vapor into air cold enough to shatter rubber. Instantly, the vapor condenses against cold iron shells, sharp stone, and thick mats of vegetation, beading into slicks of warm brine, freezing at the edges into rime, thawing again as another massive flank grinds past, and dripping down into the dark, crowded crevices below. There are no rivers, only slow, steaming trickles of condensate threading through the crush of legs and roots, pooling in razor-rimmed rock basins before being sucked up by intake trunks.

Fed by this localized, industrialized dampness and the waste heat of a billion grinding bodies, the vegetation is startlingly lush. It climbs the flanks of the beasts as much as it clings to the young rock. Thick, rubbery bolsters of black-green moss—dense as felt to trap the blinding light without scorching—swallow every exposed ledge. Out of the moss erupt chest-high thickets of cable-fern and coiled, copper-sheathed reeds that vibrate in the acoustic wash. Where a beast lingers for a month, vine-like creepers with serrated, heat-seeking leaves latch onto its warm exhaust ports, weaving one animal to its neighbor until whole square miles of herd are stitched together under a thriving, frost-rimmed canopy of stiff leaves and blooming, oily-petaled flowers that smell of ozone and crushed pine. When an animal finally shifts a half-meter forward, the tearing of roots sounds like steel cable snapping.

The noise is monumental, continuous, and completely devoid of urgency. There are no calls, no cries, no wind-rustle. Instead, there is the continental roar of heavy, slow-moving mass and biological machinery. Across millions of acres, intake valves slap shut with the thud of drop-forges; digestive mills lined with basalt-grinding teeth turn over with a low, amplified rumble that travels through the soles of your boots; gas bladders hiss at ninety pounds per square inch. Overlying the bass grind is a ceaseless, stinging crackle. Many of the smaller organisms—blind, barrel-shaped symbiotes that pack the spaces between the giants' legs by the tens of thousands—generate high-voltage electrostatic fields to strip mineral dust from the air and arc-weld their own protective shells together out of shed iron flakes. Blue-white sparks pop constantly down in the damp shadows beneath the bellies, sharp flashes somehow still visible against the blinding white daylight overhead, adding the dry *snap-buzz* of a transformer yard to the wet chuffing of the vents.

Everything happens on a dial turned nearly to zero. Predation, if that is the word for it, takes seasons. Great, flat-bodied siphon-worms the width of threshing machines drape themselves across three or four iron-backed grazers at once, slowly boring through nickel plating with acidic, rotating radulae that advance a millimeter a day, tapping into the pressurized steam-bladders beneath while the host continues its glacial shove through the fern-mats, seemingly indifferent. When one of the titan-grazers finally dies, it does not collapse; there is nowhere to fall. Wedged upright by the bodies of its neighbors and held fast by woven creepers, its internal boilers slowly cool over a week. Frost claims its dorsal vents for the first time in a century. Immediately, millions of smaller, piston-jawed scavengers swarm up out of the damp dark between the rocks, methodically dismantling the hull plate by plate, smelting the metals down in hot, whistling gut-chambers, and extruding fresh mineral slag onto the sharp stone below—soil for the next dense wave of rubbery, frost-proof reeds.

Stand on an outcropping of raw, jagged obsidian—one of the rare spires steep enough to poke above the living, chugging blanket—and the sheer, saturated scale of it flattens perspective. The plateau stretches out until the curvature of the world drops it away, blazing bright under a cold, empty sky. Miles off, massive banks of white exhaust-fog hang low and stationary over the densest congregations, throwing prismatic halos back at the sun. Below you, with no gap large enough to set a foot down, the continent breathes, smelts, sparks, and grinds forward an inch an hour, deafening and patient and entirely wild.
