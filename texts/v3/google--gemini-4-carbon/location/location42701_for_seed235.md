The light is ordinary—the plain, unremarkable brightness of early afternoon under a clear, pale blue sky—and it falls across a mountain saddle twenty thousand feet up that feels like the inside of a laundromat boiler. 

Far below the outer parapets, so far down that distance flattens into haze, lies the top of a planetary cloud deck, level and white as salt. Up here the air ought to be freezing and thin enough to kill, yet it hangs heavy, wet, and blisteringly hot, pushing past forty-five degrees Celsius. Moisture condenses directly out of the clear sky onto every surface, slicking the rock in warm, continuous sheets. And over all of it, filling the entire high basin from ridge to ridge without dip or swell, is a sound so loud it presses against the eardrums like deep water.

It is the roar of a spillway, a jet engine, and a rockslide braided together and pinned at a single, unvarying volume. Nothing visible is big enough to make it. There is no central cataract, no volcano, no storm. The noise comes off the wet stone walls themselves, out of open doorways, up from shallow puddles in the courtyards. When a drop of warm water slips from the tip of a waxy leaf and hits a flagstone, it does not plink; its impact simply belongs to the general, bone-rattling thunder that already occupies every cubic inch of air. Your teeth hum with it. The broad leaves hanging across the alleyways do not even tremble.

The saddle and the surrounding spires are covered, for miles in every direction, by a dense, stepped city that was never built with tools.

From across the gorge—where three separate granite horns rise sheer out of the bright abyss—you can see the whole municipal sweep of it: packed residential tiers, wide switchback avenues, sunken plazas, buttressed retaining walls, covered arcades, and bridged chasms. Close up, there is not a single chisel mark, mortar joint, nail, timber peg, hinge, or shard of fired clay anywhere in the metropolis. The buildings are seamless extrusions of dark, porous basalt and yellow travertine that grew or precipitated directly into the geometry of rooms and thoroughfares. Walls rise two and three stories out of the living bedrock with the rounded, slightly slumped precision of termite mounds or cave draperies, pierced by rectangular windows whose sills have been smoothed by ages of hot runoff. Where a ceiling needs spanning, the stone either arches in ribbed vaults like the palate of a mouth or gives way to the fused, horizontal trunks of strangler trees whose bark has mineralized to the hardness of ironwood, locking into the rock on either side without seam or socket.

Everything is weathered into a long-settled truce with the water. Sills are dished at the center; gutters running down the middle of narrow lanes are worn to a greasy, satin polish; the risers of ten thousand staircases are softened at the lip, neither freshly cracked nor crumbling into ruin. It looks ten thousand years old and completely finished changing.

Plants crowd every horizontal ledge, pack the courtyards, and hang in heavy, glistening curtains from upper balconies. Because of the heat and the constant warm wet, the growth is lush and congested—thick bolsters of emerald liverwort, waist-high stands of stiff, paddle-leaved philodendrons, tubular crimson pitcher-growths brimming with hot rainwater, and mats of creeping rhizomes as thick as human wrists that braid over roof parapets and down into neighboring rooms. Every leaf is flawless. Not one margin is scalloped by a caterpillar; not one stem is bored by a weevil; no web spans the corners of the dark, steaming doorways. Aside from rare clusters of pinhead-sized, shell-backed limpets cemented permanently into the warm slime of the street drains—so slow they might as well be gravel—there is no animal life at all. No birds ride the thermals off the mile-deep cliffs. No flies hover in the greenhouse heat of the plazas. Reproduction here has no use for wings or mouths; runners creep over wet stone and stop, arrested at the exact boundary where rock, shade, and water met equilibrium long ago.

Walk fifty paces down any stepped street and the arrangement of the world comes quietly undone.

A broad, flooded promenade descends eastward along a cliff face, bordered by waist-high stone balustrades weeping rusty water into the void. You follow the runoff downhill past four crossroads, turn left through a low, windowless arcade choked with steaming fern, climb nothing, and step out onto the upper roof-terrace of the building that stood fifty feet *above* your starting point—looking directly down onto the empty promenade you just walked, where the same sheet of water is sliding eastward in the same unhurried ribbons. Look across the valley to check your bearings and you find the exact district you are standing in repeated twice more on neighboring pinnacles: not mirrored, and not a mirage, because a fallen, mineralized tree-trunk bridges the gap between two of them at an angle that shouldn't fit, and water from the lower pinnacle's gutter pours cleanly across the span into a cistern near the top of the higher one.

That is how the city stays wet without a river or a raincloud. The water is caught in closed topological loops across the broken heights. Warm streams sluice out of ground-floor thresholds, run briskly down mossy alleys, vanish under low stone lintels at the bottom of award-sloping wards, and well up again—at the exact same temperature and flow rate—from shallow basins on the highest sun-baked roofs half a mile upslope. No source ever drains; no low plaza ever floods past the ankles. Even the steam rising off the hot black flagstones vanishes three feet up without forming mist, returning its moisture instantly to the sweating walls around it.

The sun sits about forty degrees above the jagged western crest. It stays there. Shadows cast by the thick stone colonnades and hanging vines fall crisp and dark across the wet yellow sinter of the squares, edged with bright afternoon glare, never lengthening by a hair's breadth. A loose ribbon of bark peeling from a root-beam hangs half-curled over a puddle, stiff with hot moisture, neither rotting off nor growing over. In the blindingly ordinary daylight, miles up in the scalding sky, the whole vast, empty town sits locked at full volume—every gutter running, every leaf slick, roaring into the clear blue air forever without moving an inch.
