A lesser power, looking down upon the fifty-mile rookery of stone and grease and slow breath, would feel an itch to *fix* it. 

A young god, full of warm-valley pity, would split the dark schist with a hot spring. Or breathe five degrees of mercy into the winter gale. Or sow the gravel steppe with deep-rooted, fast-growing timber.

And within three generations, the city would be dead.

The wise power sees the whole equation before touching a single term. Warm the plateau by five degrees, and the permafrost beneath ten million tons of dry-laid granite turns to black porridge; every battered wall from basin to saddle slumps into the trenches, crushing the vaults and the stalls beneath them. Strike a hot spring in the square, and its steam rises into the freezing gale to coat every stepped ramp for two miles downwind in impassable, murderous black glaze, while rotting the rawhide lashings of the ancient rib-rafters overhead. Give them pine trees, and the soot of resinous fires clogs the pigeon-vents, drives away the guano that tans the forty-below leather, and breaks the sacred, tight-knotted patience that keeps a mason’s bare fingers steady on the stone.

The wise power watches for a long time—longer than the curing of a thighbone on the spurs, longer than the slow swelling of an ice-lens thirty feet under a retaining wall. It listens to the low, chest-vibrating hum in the tallow-dark vaults. It sees the children catching dung in leather baskets with quick, warm hands; it sees the Wall-Reader sitting motionless in her triple-white hides, listening through a hollow horn to the stone’s secret speech; it sees the descending porter press flat against the schist on a shin-ledge, dipping his greased forehead an inch to the climber bearing cobbles up from the gorge.

And the power feels something deeper than pity. It feels *reverence*.

These beings are not stranded here. They are not victims of the altitude waiting to be rescued into softness. They have entered into a covenant with granite, lanolin, gravity, and thin air so complete that to erase their hardship would be to erase their genius—to tell four hundred generations of careful, quiet builders that the femur wedged beneath the roof-beam was bearing weight for nothing.

Yet benevolence is not cold neutrality. Watching closely, listening down in the soot-thick dark before dawn, the power sees the two places where the plateau does not merely shape them, but cruelly, needlessly diminishes them before their time.

First: the smoke in the vaults, eating the great barrel-lungs from the inside out until elders drown dryly in the dark at fifty winters, their thick blood stalling in their chests.
Second: the white, blazing quartz-glare of the zenith, scarring the corneas despite the bone-slits, stealing the sight of the very elders whose eyes are finest at reading the shadow-line on a bulging wall.

To help such a people without unraveling them, a gift must look exactly like geology. It must require their own calloused hands, their own slow seasons, and their own eye for stone to unlock. It must never announce itself as a miracle.

So the power reaches down—not into the sky, and not into the city, but two miles out beyond the newest, rawest quarry-ridges, deep into the blind, frozen root of an unworked granite spur where the frost-cleavage will naturally split the rock open next spring.

There, in the dark beneath the stone, the power rearranges the sleeping chemistry of three narrow veins.

***

The first vein is pale, soapy, and fibrous, tucked beside the steatite they already quarry for their pressure-pots. When a mason’s diorite maul splits the frost-crack open in the seventh month, the seam reveals itself: plates of clear, smoky-gold mineral that peel apart under a thumbnail like rigid leaves of ice, completely fireproof, clearer than horn, tough as rawhide, and tinted the exact warm amber of tallow-flame. 

*Muscovite mica, doped deep in the lattice with iron and titanium to drink the brutal ultraviolet of the thin sky.*

Within two winters, the bone-slits tied across the brows of the roof-walkers and steppe-drovers are fitted with thin, stitched panes of the smoky stone. For the first time in ten thousand years, a drover can watch the whole horizon at noon without squinting into blindness; a Wall-Reader can keep her sharp, unclouded eyes into her seventies, reading a hair’s-breadth shadow-shift across a sunlit basin. And set tightly into a slate collar over a vault’s ceiling-hole, a hand-sized pane lets a shaft of clean, golden noon straight down into the dark chamber below without letting a whisper of warmth escape.

The second vein lies fifty paces lower down the same talus slope: a seam of heavy, grey-green porous stone, riddled with microscopic, crystalline cages like frozen sponge. At first, a boy chips a chunk loose simply because it is light to carry and holds warmth well when tucked beside a sunken dung-pot to heat his toes.

Inside the close, smoky vault that night, something quiet happens. As the choked dung-fire smolders beneath its slate lid, the grey-green cobble sitting on the rim drinks the acrid, lung-scarring breath of the incomplete combustion—the stinging aldehydes, the sour ammonia rising through the floorboards from the twelve steaming beasts below, the invisible creeping poison of carbon monoxide—trapping them inside its mineral honeycomb and catalyzing the worst of the fumes silently into harmless breath. When the stone grows heavy and fouled after a month, setting it out on the sunny south roof in the thin, blazing noon bakes it clean again, breathing the trapped nitrogen straight into the black night-soil of the thumbnail-tuber beds when washed with a ladle of rime-melt.

Nobody calls it a blessing from beyond the stars. They call it *lung-stone*. Within twenty years, every sunken hearth-pot in fifty miles of labyrinth is rimmed with grey-green cobbles. In the predawn dark, the elders stop coughing black phlegm into the straw. The stories told under the hides grow longer still, because the grandmothers live ten, fifteen winters more to tell them—their lungs clear enough at sixty to climb slowly up to the sunny terraces and teach the five-year-olds how to hear the *tick-grate* of a settling foundation.

And the third touch is the smallest of all—so small that not even the wisest Cutter on the high spurs ever suspects a hand was laid upon the world.

Out on the gravel steppe basins, in the dry crowns of the tough cushion-plants and the bitter wild sedge whose seeds go into the sealed steatite pots, the power nudges a single folding protein in the winter dormancy of the roots. The plants do not grow taller; they do not spread faster or drink a drop more moisture than the sparse salt-snow provides. They simply synthesize a denser, slightly sweeter oil in the seed-coat, rich in a subtle compound that keeps thick, altitude-heavy blood slippery in the narrow vessels of the brain and heart during the deepest forty-below stillness of midwinter, and guards the gums and joints when the tuber-crop is lean. The porridge in the leather pouches tastes a fraction nuttier on the tongue as a porter climbs the four-hundred-foot ramp. That is all.

***

Nothing else is touched.

The wind still combs through millions of dry-laid chinks with its low, dry hiss. The pikas still pack cured sedge into the sunny lintel-gaps while families leave them in peace. The herds still drift inward for sixty autumn days on clicking saucer-hooves, filling the trenches with musky fog. When a citizen’s long, clear-eyed, quiet life finally winds down to stillness, the sledge still whispers up the frost-glazed flagstones to the pale granite plinths, and the great bearded vultures still drop from the dark-blue zenith with a papery roar of primary feathers.

Sometimes, though, the power lingers—utterly unseen, taking up no heat, stealing no oxygen—just inside the kink of a sunken alley as dusk drops like an axe across the ridges.

It watches an old woman—eighty winters old now, her eyes behind their amber-stone lenses still dark and bright as wet flint—pause on a shin-ledge to let a line of heavily laden adolescents sway past with the slow, rolling *step-and-settle*. Above her head, wedged tight beneath a curving grazer-rib rafter, a satin-dark human collarbone gleams faintly where four centuries of greased sheepskin shoulders have rubbed it smooth as jade.

As the last boy passes, bearing two hundred pounds of smooth gorge-cobbles on his forehead-strap to weight his family’s slates against the coming gale, his warm, bare fingers brush the ancient bone above him for luck, and his brow dips an inch to the old woman against the wall.

Her greased lips scarcely part, preserving the heat, as she gives the soft, deep-throated click of peace in return.

And the wise power, asking no temple, wanting no name, needing no prayer from people whose whole existence is already an unbroken act of devotion to keeping one another warm, withdraws quietly into the dark blue zenith—leaving the stone to the masons, and the warmth inside the walls.
