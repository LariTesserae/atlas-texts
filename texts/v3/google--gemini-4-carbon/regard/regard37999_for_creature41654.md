At first, the attention that comes to rest upon the shelf is lighter than the sunlight. It does not displace a single molecule of the heavy, steaming air, nor does it interrupt the four-second snap of the twisted space near the drop-off.

A lesser benevolence might have flinched at the sight of the four anomalies. A neat-minded caretaker, recognizing them for what they are—splinters of a broken transit-drive, or stray rivets shed from the hull of something vast that scraped across this world’s dimensional skin a decade ago—might have reached down with surgical tweezers of pure law and plucked them out. Cleaned up the spill. Restored the mountain to its proper, freezing dignity.

And in ten minutes, the shelf would have flash-frozen into a tomb of black glass and burst cells.

Wisdom, however, knows the difference between a wound and a scar that has already learned to beat like a heart.

The observer watches for three full cycles of the sun. It watches the jointless whip-newts step cleanly through the mercury polyhedron as casually as an alley cat slipping under a fence. It listens to the sponge-coral’s slow, blind masonry around the thumbnail-sized drain into nowhere, admiring the sheer stubborn audacity of life using an abyss as a millrace. 

Most of all, it watches the five-limbed gardener.

It sees the creature wake in the dark—when the basin doesn't flash with fog, because the night-chilled rim condenses water continuously in a steady, whispering curtain—and spend hours delicately bending young bottle-glass shoots, holding their slick, gluey tips together between two hydrostatic toes until they fuse into a thicker roof against tomorrow’s UV. It sees the creature clear a fallen flake of basalt out of the sponge-coral’s main spillway, not out of altruism, perhaps, or perhaps out of something just shy of it: a deep, wordless comprehension that all the water on this floor is *one* water, and if the corner floods, the middle dries.

*Well,* thinks the power, with something that, scaled down to human size, would feel like the warm ache in the chest when watching a child build a functioning waterwheel out of splintered driftwood and mud. *Look at what you have made out of garbage and fever.*

Still, wisdom looks ahead. It reads the stone.

The observer lets its awareness sink down through the wet black floor, past the shallow roots and the boiling hairline cracks, deep into the newly uplifted shoulder of the peak. Here, the news is not good.

The mountain is young, and young mountains are restless. The same violent upthrust that sheared these crisp, cathedral walls out of the crust is still grinding away three kilometers down. Tension is stacking up along an oblique fault line directly beneath the nave. Left to the blind arithmetic of plate tectonics, in nineteen years—long before this pocket biosphere can throw off hardy spores capable of surviving the cold, long before the stone-backed mason’s descendants can turn their cleverness from mere maintenance toward true thought—a magnitude-six shrug of the ridge will cleave the outer lip of the basin away.

The white plumb line, moored not to the rock but to a fixed coordinate in the planet’s gravitational well, will be left hanging in empty air twenty meters out over the abyss. The floor will drop. The thermal boundary will rupture. The kiln will spill into the gale.

And there is a second, subtler doom. The observer examines the four typographical errors themselves. Three of them—the plumb line, the mercury polyhedron, and the four-second twist—are utterly stable, pinched-off knots of geometry that will outlast the sun. They draw their heat from the planetary rotation itself, an infinitesimal friction against space-time.

The fourth—the black comma in the corner pool—is fraying. Not expanding into anything catastrophic; rather, its edges are slowly untying. In eighty or ninety seasons, it will pinch shut with a tiny pop of hard gamma radiation, cooking the corner pool, and worse: without that drain, the white plumb line’s endless mist-making will have nowhere to go. The shelf will drown, overflowing its thermal threshold in a hot waterfall that will strip the mineral salts right out of the closed loop within weeks, starving the moss, the bladders, and the mason in turn.

So. What to do?

To give too much is a kind of murder. If the power widened the shelf into a lush, safe valley, softened the sharp slate into loam, dimmed the UV, or granted the blind mason eyes and language in a single stroke of grace, the creature *as it is* would cease to exist. Its beauty—its whole nascent soul—is bound up in its counting of the twelve-minute fog, its cool moss-furrows, its intimate, fearless mastery of a deadly, clockwork room. You do not help a tightrope walker by tackling them onto a mattress; you make sure the anchors at either end of the wire will not pull out of the wall.

The power decides upon three touches. None of them will make a sound louder than the *tick* of hot basalt.

First, deep in the blind dark beneath the mountain, three kilometers below the steaming pools, a locked jagged tooth of granite along the fault line quietly softens. It does not melt; its crystalline lattice simply yields, flowing like cold wax for the space of a single breath, shedding thirty years of stored tectonic stress into harmless, microscopic creep. Around the roots of the basin itself, a subtle mineralization threads through the deep fractures—veins of tough, fibrous nephrite and interlocking hornblende knitting the shoulder of the peak together like scar tissue around a bone. Earthquakes will still shake the range for a million years. The mountain will weather down around it like melting snow, eventually leaving this notch standing as a strange, flat-topped tower—a tepui in the clouds—long after the ridges beside it have crumbled into scree. The floor will hold.

Second, the power looks down into the flooded back corner, past the rust-red feeding fans of the sponge-coral, to the thumbnail-sized comma of absolute black.

With infinite delicacy, like a watchmaker fitting a ruby bearing around a grinding axle, the power hems the raw edge of the tear. It folds the fraying lip of space back into itself, stabilizing the aperture so it will never snap shut and never widen, and bends its exit vector just slightly. Previously, the water vanishing into that missing chip of space was simply deleted into an empty pocket dimension, lost forever, while the white plumb line pulled fresh moisture out of deep subterranean aquifers to replace it—a slow mining of the mountain’s deep fossil water that would one day run dry.

Now, the water that slips down the black comma falls through a curved dark no wider than a thimble and emerges four hundred meters directly *beneath* the base of the white plumb line, down in the deep, sealed rock, where it percolates slowly upward through warm beds of rich, unweathered olivine and phosphate before the plumb line catches it again and breathes it back into the sunlit air as mist. A leaky pipe has become a heart. The minerals will not run out for ten million years.

That is enough for survival. Any reasonable custodian would stop there and withdraw.

Yet the power lingers for one more twelve-minute cycle. It watches the stone-backed creature sitting locked and patient in the stream, holding its heavy limb across the fresh crack in the basalt so the melon-bladder will not thirst, waiting out the scalding sun while steam rises in faint wisps from the green velvet on its back.

Survival in a closed room, sustained for ten million years, has its own trap: stasis. Perfection with nowhere to go. The basin is only as wide as a cathedral nave. One day—perhaps ten thousand generations from now, perhaps fifty—the mason’s descendants will know every millimeter of this floor so completely that their minds will hit the invisible thermal wall and bruise against it, trapped between the boiling room that made them and the freezing sky that kills them.

They will need a porch. And they will need a problem worthy of their hands.

The power turns its attention to the vertical blue-black wall on the shady, eastern side of the nave, ten paces from the mercury polyhedron, where a clean geometric fissure splits the cliff face from floor to ceiling before pinching shut two meters in.

Deep inside that narrow cleft, out of sight of the sun, the rock gives one crisp, ordinary *tick*.

The fissure widens—not much, just enough for a creature the size of a sleeping hound to squeeze sideways if it lets the water out of two of its limbs. Instead of pinching shut two meters in, the crack now runs back thirty meters into the dark heart of the mountain, sloping gently downward, opening here and there into low, silent chambers of dry, unweathered stone.

Nothing anomalous is placed inside that cave. No space-twists, no shadowless white rods. Only natural rock, absolute darkness—which will not trouble a blind creature—and a gradient. Near the mouth of the fissure, the heavy, steaming air of the basin spills in, keeping the stone warm and slick. Five meters back, the rock cools to blood-heat. Fifteen meters back, it is crisp cellar-cool. At the very back of the deepest chamber, thirty meters in, a single bead of glacial meltwater seeps through the ceiling from the snowfields outside, cold as iron, *plinking* onto the dry slate once every minute.

A staircase of temperatures. A gentle slope between the kiln and the world outside, shielded from the wind, waiting in the dark for the day someone gets curious enough—or crowded enough—to carry a mouthful of woven fronds and hot water five paces farther than anyone has ever gone before.

And right at the lip of the newly widened cleft, sitting loose on a wet ledge where the next flash-rain will wash a trickle of runoff across it, rests a single flake of stone knocked free when the fissure shifted.

It is the size of one of the creature’s flat pads, shaped like a broad wedge. Unlike the brittle, sharp-snapping basalt of the rest of the floor, this piece—touched for an instant by the deep hornblende vein—is dense, heavy, and fine-grained, with one flat side and one rounded side. If wedged into a hairline crack, it would block a shifting stream just as well as a stiffened, living leg, freeing the mason to go back under the cool green dome while the stone does the waiting for it.

The air in the basin thickens, pressing softly against the rock walls. Twelve minutes are up.

Instantaneously, the sunlight vanishes into warm, milk-white fog.

Down on the floor, the five-limbed creature unlocks its cramped, stone-stiff leg from the rivulet. The diverted water immediately slips back into the crack, away from the melon-bladder. Before galloping toward the cliff’s edge to hunt for stunned moths, the creature pauses for half a second in the whiteout—*tick*, went that eastern wall a moment ago, a slightly deeper resonance than usual, an echo with a new, hollow room behind it. It tilts its flat, eyeless head toward the eastern cliff, mapping the tiny change in the basin’s acoustics, filing the new hollow away in its slow, steady memory for the cool of the night.

Then the ten seconds end; the fog collapses into warm vertical rain, pattering across the chartreuse moss on its back, and it trots briskly toward the edge of the clouds to gather its meal.

The benevolent power does not wait to see it find the wedge of stone, or explore the dark cleft. There is no rush. It has given the mountain bones, the water a circle, and the mind a door. The rest belongs to the gardener.
