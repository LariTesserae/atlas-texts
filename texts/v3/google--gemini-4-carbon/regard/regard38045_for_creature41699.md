To a power that has watched stars ignite and cooling crusts fold into mountains, most mortal settlements look like small fires built in dry grass: frantic, bright, smoky, and brief. Even when benevolent, a great intelligence usually approaches human towns with the careful pity of a physician entering a fever-ward. There is almost always so much to untangle—cruelties disguised as customs, hunger beside rot, panic dressed up as ambition.

When this mind’s attention drifted across the dry lowland plateau and dropped over the ninety-degree lip of the split, it stopped.

For a long time—three full revolutions of the main ratchet in Tower Seven, nine hours of human clock-time—it did nothing at all except listen with senses that did not have eardrums to be bruised.

It felt the sixty-cycle chord braided cleanly into the planetary drift. It traced the cold, patient geometry of the rifting stone and saw how the eleven brick-and-tile towers did not fight the wound in the earth, nor worship it in terror, nor strip it bare, but simply *bridged* it—turning a millimeter of geological parting into warm bread, ground lenses, dry quilts, and yellow streetlamps. It watched a lineman in the bright flat squint upward at a three-foot gap in a row of heavy blue-backed birds, nod once to himself, and chalk a scheduling mark onto a slate board without hurry or anger. It watched a rigger’s machete stop half an inch short of a grazing beetle as big as a saucer, tip the creature gently into a wire basket, and carry it down out of harm’s way. It watched the blind, chalk-white plates down in the lightless sumps swaying their four-foot antennae in contentment inside an artificial electromagnetic tide.

And the watching power felt something remarkably rare in the long history of oversight: *delight, untainted by pity.*

A lesser benevolence might have been tempted to "improve" the cleft. It might have hushed the deafening clatter of the birds and the bleeders so the people could shout across plazas like plains-folk; it might have cushioned the razor-sharp slate with sweet meadow-soil, or sped the slow trams, or taught the gray climbers not to steal candles.

Wisdom knew better in half a breath. Soften the noise, and you break the quiet, watchful grace of their hands and wrists—the clog-tap on the drainpipe, the attentive reading of throats, the unhurried evening circles under the lamps where silence is a shared coat rather than an emptiness. Lay soil across the clean-split flats, and you ruin the crisp vibration of the stone floor through which four hundred pairs of ankles keep their truce with five tons of unstoppable torque.

This was not a broken thing begging to be mended. It was an intricate, self-tuning pocket watch that earth, iron, fern, bird, crustacean, and human had somehow assembled together inside a stone box.

Yet no physical harmony lasts forever unassisted, especially when its mainspring is a tearing continent.

Casting its sight backward and forward along the grain of the rock—not prophesying, merely reading stress-lines, metallurgy, and genetics the way an anchor-smith reads rust—the power saw the three quiet treacheries waiting down the decades for the town in the split.

First, seventy feet behind the western anchor-wall of Tower Three, hidden entirely behind solid-looking greystone, lay a dry, chalky lens of rotten shale left over from an ancient lagoon. Right now the deep steel anchor-rods bit into good rock ten yards shy of it. In eleven years, when the cables had to be re-bored and stepped back to accommodate the widening rift, the drills would hit that invisible pocket of talc-soft stone. It wouldn't fail at once; it would hold under test, take the full tectonic load for two winters, and then shear out in the middle of a cold Tuesday night—whipping three tons of braided steel cable across the central plaza and smashing the glazed green tiles of Tower Six into dust.

Second, the rift itself was not uniform along its bottom. Half a mile south of the settlement, four hundred feet below the deepest sump-pit, the spreading crack was creeping toward a sealed, ancient pocket of sour brine and heavy sulfur gas trapped under high pressure. In thirty years, a hairline fracture would tap it. No explosion would touch the surface, nothing dramatic—just a slow, invisible seep rising into the cold groundwater. Within two seasons, the blind white *plate-backs* in the dark shafts would die off, their shells dissolving; the iron-bacteria would sour into corrosive slime; and the condensed breath of the deep pneumatic intakes would start carrying traces of hydrogen sulfide up into the fern-skirts, yellowing the giant fronds, silencing the copper frogs, and pitting the brass governors black.

Third, there were only three hundred and eighty-seven people in the cleft. They were healthy, clever, and steady, and travelers came down the western hoist-way now and then to trade plateau grain and wool for precision lenses and electrical porcelain. Still, over generations, isolated pockets grow brittle in blood and habit alike, or else attract greedy eyes from distant capitals who look at a self-powered town and think of munitions foundries.

So the wise power decided what to do. It would sign no sky with fire, speak into no prophet’s dream, leave no monument. In a town of master mechanics, a miracle ought to have clean tolerances and leave no burr on the gear-tooth.

It made four small adjustments—three in the dark of the earth, and one in the daylight at the rim.

Deep beneath the southern end of the rift, four hundred feet below the blind crustaceans, the power laid two fingers of pure pressure against the creeping micro-fracture headed for the sulfur pocket. It did not seal the rift—the town needed the pull—but it coaxed a hair-thin vein of dissolved quartz and calcite out of the surrounding stone and crystallized it diagonally across the crack’s nose, acting like a mason’s shim. When the rock parted next week by its customary fingernail’s breadth, the split deflected eleven degrees eastward into three miles of clean, sweet, dry granite, leaving the sour brine locked safely away for another ten million years.

Next, seventy feet inside the western cliff behind Tower Three, it turned its attention to the hidden pocket of rotten shale. Slowly, without raising the temperature of the cliff face by half a degree—for fear of disturbing the liverworts or startling a single sleeping bird—it rearranged the sluggish mineral lattice of that buried lens, knitting loose clay and crumbling mica into dense, interlocking hornfels, tougher and cleaner-holding than the greystone around it, and threaded it with a tiny, unmistakable ribbon of bright fool’s gold right at the outer margin so that, eleven years hence, when the anchor-smiths’ drills bit into it, they would bring up glittering cores, whistle through their teeth, and know they had hit bedrock worthy of a cathedral pier.

Third, down in the wet, pitch-black sumps of Tower One, where the dinner-plate crustaceans swayed in the sixty-cycle hum, the power touched the spawning jelly clinging to a submerged iron grating. Just a nudge to a folding protein—giving the next generation of *plate-backs* a quiet appetite not only for iron-bacteria, but for heavy lead and copper oxides leached from old solder, locking any stray industrial poison harmlessly away inside their shed chalky shells at the bottom of the dark water, keeping the town’s deep cisterns sweet forever.

The fourth thing took a little longer, and was done purely out of affection.

Up on the windy, sun-bleached plateau, three days’ walk to the north, a traveling tinker’s apprentice—a nineteen-year-old girl from a crowded river-city three hundred miles away, left stone-deaf in both ears by scarlet fever at twelve, possessed of uncanny, steady hands for watch-escapements and a lonely heart tired of shouting people who treated her like an idiot—came to a fork in an unmarked sheep-track.

Left went down toward the coastal ports, where she intended to look for factory piecework. Right led out across empty, unpromising scrubland toward a low scar in the flat country.

A sudden, stray squall—the brief lowland kind that smells of dry dust and cold height—blew across the left-hand road just as a mule-cart loaded with rough wool bales creaked past, turning right. The driver, an older plateau herdsman heading to trade fleeces for lens-glass and salted custards at the western hoist, caught her eye, saw the watchmaker’s loupe hanging from a leather cord at her throat, and pointed two fingers down the right-hand track, tapping his wrist where a gear turns.

She didn't have to hear him. She climbed up beside the wool bales.

By Thursday afternoon she will be lowered down the western hoist-way into the blinding, noisy split. While the herdsman claps his hands over his ears and winces at the brassy explosion of four thousand blue-backed birds lifting through the high wires, she will step off the lift-platform onto the raw grey slate, blinking in surprise at a place where nobody’s shoulders are hunched but the visitor’s. Within ten minutes she will notice two mechanics standing beside a humming transformer, talking with their wrists, elbows, and greasy fingers in crisp, rapid shorthand; she will feel through the soles of her worn boots the stately, clean *CLANK* of the main pawl dropping home in Tower Seven; and when a gray-furred climber drops onto a turnbuckle six feet from her face to crack a snail against the iron, she will laugh out loud. Within a month they will resole her boots with vulcanized rubber and boiled felt, hand her a lens-polishing spindle on the fifth floor of Tower Five, and discover she can feel an out-of-true bearing through a stone wall three minutes faster than anyone born in the basin.

Down in the cleft tonight, none of this shows.

The sun has just slid below the sharp western lip, dropping the stone room into crisp indigo shadow. The streetlamps have clicked on in yellow pools across the clean, unweathered rock. Up on the warm brick cornices, the birds have tucked their heavy yellow bills under their feathers and gone quiet, handing the night over to the bronze-bowl *glock-glock-glock* of the copper frogs in the steaming fern-curtains and the wheeling shadows of trowel-sized moths.

Out in the middle of the bare flat between Towers Four and Seven, the two old men have set up their tool-crate under a solitary cast-iron lamp. When the forty-minute ratchet drops home behind the tall arched glass, the bone dominoes rattle for half a second on the wood, the chicory ripples in the heavy ceramic mugs, and the earth beneath their rubber soles widens by another silent, harmless fraction of a hair.

The benevolent power lingers for one last minute—not high above the rim like a judge, nor deep in the rock like a mechanic, but simply sitting invisible in the empty air beside an unoccupied canvas chair, taking in the sharp smell of crushed fern, coal-oil, hot spiced chicory, and cold ozone—before moving on across the world, slightly less weary than it had been for a thousand years.
