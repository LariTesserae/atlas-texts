When the Attention arrived, it did not come down like lightning, or a voice from the clouds, or a shining figure stepping onto the central cast-iron flange. Wise power knows the physics of its own weight. To drop a sudden miracle into a system held at forty pounds of osmotic pressure would be like dropping a wrench into the spokes of the nine-foot flywheel.

Instead, the Presence lowered itself into the hollow the way the afternoon light did: scattering through the wax-heavy leaves, dissolving into the warm condensate, letting its awareness hum along at precisely sixty cycles per second until it could feel the basin from the inside out.

It took three days simply to listen.

It felt the tight, bruised-celery warmth of the root-mat. It rode through the dark return flumes with the oat-grain shrimp, feeling their tiny, methodical jaws scraping the brass valve-seats clean. It sat in the flooded dark with the valve-clams, feeling the calcium carbonate warp and lock around old iron bolts. And in the blue-green dusk, it flowed alongside the eldest of the sump-tenders—an eight-armed matriarch scarred along her mantle with a pale streak where a hot steam-pipe had kissed her twenty years ago—feeling the calm, wordless geometry of her mind.

She did not think in words, or even in pictures. She thought in *gradients*. Her consciousness was a slow, deeply satisfying map of pressures, temperatures, electrical fields, and tastes. *Here, the hum is smooth. Here, the sap is too sharp. Here, the iron weeps; pack it tight. Here, the warmth pools; rest the gill-folds.* There was no loneliness in her, no existential ache to be rescued from the damp dark, no secret wish for hands or speech or dry ground. She and her four kin were profoundly, unhurriedly *busy*, and their work tasted good to them.

A lesser benevolence might have been disappointed. A lesser god likes to tidy up, to uplift, to turn beasts into philosophers, or to shatter old rusting cages of iron so "true nature" can reclaim the pit.

The Wise Power saw clearer. If it ripped the cast-iron vessels out, the whole seventy-yard sponge of vascular tissue would collapse into stagnant rot within a month, suffocating the clams and starving the tenders. And if it sparked human-bright fire into the tenders’ quiet minds, it would ruin their tempo, teaching them impatience in a room where impatience meant getting crushed between two swelling petioles.

Yet the Power did not leave right away. Looking deeper—past the forty-pound dials, down through the four-foot flooded sponge-work, down into the drowned root-sump where no light had fallen in two centuries, and finally to the buried foundations of the factory-blue dynamo—it found the three quiet clocks ticking toward the end of this small world.

First: deep inside the sealed housing of the dynamo, the main bronze sleeve-bearing was wearing thin. Five decades of slow, relentless side-load from the buried turbine shaft had ground away eighty percent of the softer sacrificial metal. In eleven years—give or take two rainy seasons—the steel rotor would drop three millimeters, kiss the iron stator poles, weld itself shut in a shower of drowned sparks, and strip the teeth off the primary drive-gear. The sixty-hertz hum would stop forever. Without that gentle waste heat and the stray current keeping the mineralized vines honest, the first deep winter frost to dip into the hollow would kill the core gourds, and the tenders would freeze in their sleep.

Second: the phosphorus was slowly leaving. Though the hair-roots drank every dying frond before it could fall, nothing is perfectly sealed. Over generations, rare overflow rains had spilled a tiny fraction of dissolved mineral wealth out through a high rock-fissure hidden behind the eastern palisade, while nothing ever came *in* over the rim except rainwater, dust, and sunlight. Every generation of gourd-vines grew walls a hair thinner than the last; the latest clutch of sump-tender eggs tucked beneath the warm western buttresses held only two viable embryos instead of four, their rubbery capsules starved of trace salts.

Third: there were only five tenders in a single seventy-yard cup of the world. They were healthy enough, cousin-bred over eighty years into calm, sturdy uniformity, outliving most octopuses ten times over because their slow metabolisms burned so cool and their reproductive glands never triggered a fatal post-spawning decline. Still, one rockslide from the upper cliff, one freak blight in the camphor-sap, and there would be none left anywhere under the sky.

And finally, while the Power had its awareness sunk all the way to the bottom of the drowned sump, it answered an old, dangling question for its own quiet satisfaction: *turning pressure into nothing visible at all.*

It followed the thickest, oldest armored conduit down through thirty feet of compressed, petrifying peat until it hit true bedrock. There, bolted into a flooded granite drift cut by the long-dead engineers, sat a massive, bronze-cased dewatering pump coupled to an eddy-current brake. A quarter-mile east, down-slope through the mountain, an old artesian spring burst out of a dry limestone bluff to feed a valley stream—warm, clear, unfailing even in drought, pushed up out of a collapsed aquifer that would have gone dry seventy years ago if this forgotten green engine up on the ridge hadn't kept pumping its heart out in the dark. Neither the villagers who watered their orchards down in that valley nor the boneless gardeners up in the mist had any idea the other existed.

The Power smiled—not with a mouth, for it had none, but as a sudden, brief brightening of the afternoon light that made the green bladder-vines glow like emeralds.

*Very well,* it thought. *No miracles that break the skin. Only maintenance.*

It went to work at the speed of the flywheel.

Down inside the dark, grease-packed housing of the factory-blue dynamo, where no eye could ever see, the Power did not conjure a shiny new factory part. Instead, it coaxed the stray sixty-hertz current itself. Over the course of forty nights, atom by dissolved atom, copper and tin ions drifting in the mineralized plant-waxes weeping through the bearing seals were drawn out of solution and electroplated onto the worn underside of the bronze sleeve. Not pure, brittle metal, but a self-healing, porous lattice braided with microscopic carbon fibers leached from the greasy black moss outside. As long as the current hummed and the tallow-moss crept toward the grease-cup, the bearing would now rebuild its own wear-surface at the exact rate the heavy shaft ground it down. Good for another thousand years.

Next, seventy feet below the root-mat, right where the deepest, blindest taproots pressed their white tips against the drowned granite floor of the basin, the Power leaned a fraction of an ounce of tectonic patience against a hairline quartz seam.

*Crack.* Not loud enough to rattle a single valve-clam; softer than a snapping twig. Just enough to open a capillary vein into an ancient, untouched pocket of marine apatite and iron-rich greenstone locked in the mountain since an older ocean dried up. Warm, weakly acidic root-water seeped into the fissure and came back carrying the slow, rich tang of phosphorus, magnesium, and trace iodine. It would take six months for the first enriched sap to reach the sunlit elephant-ears, and a year before the matriarch drank from a pruned vine and felt a deep, nameless satisfaction settle into her mantle as her egg-glands plumped with strong, thick-shelled roe.

For the third problem, the Power looked over the rim of the hollow.

Quarter of a mile away across the forested plateau, hidden under the tangled canopy of the same old ridge, lay a twin sinkhole—a sister collapse in the limestone, sixty yards wide, choked with ordinary rot, stagnant black rainwater, biting midges, and half-rusted scrap left over from the old engineering camp’s dump: three cracked cast-iron boiler drums, coils of copper tubing, nothing hooked together, nothing alive in the basin's way.

Between the thriving green well and that dead, drowned pit ran an abandoned drainage trench, long since roofed over by fallen ironwood trunks and thick mattresses of wet sphagnum moss, dark and dripping as a cave.

The Power did not dig a canal. It simply nudged a single pressurized runner-root from one of the outer buttress-trees, turning its blind white nose three inches to the north so it slipped into the mouth of that mossy, roofed-over tunnel instead of curling back into the wall. It would take twelve years for the pressurized root-mat to bridge that quarter-mile tunnel in the wet dark. When it finally spilled over the lip of the second sinkhole, the swollen storage rhizomes would drink the stagnant mosquito-water dry, hoist themselves toward the sun, swallow the abandoned boiler drums in tight gray cambium gaskets, and build a second sunlit accumulator—quieter than the first, uncoupled from any dynamo, warm solely by biological fermentation and osmotic squeeze, waiting like an empty, furnished room. In fifteen years, on a warm, torrential autumn night when the mist lay thick across the whole ridge, two restless young sump-tenders crowded out of the western buttress would follow the hum and the camphor-scent down that damp, dark root-corridor and claim a second world.

Before departing, the Power allowed itself one small, pure indulgence—something with no engineering utility at all, meant solely for the creature who kept the seals.

Near the center of the western wall, tucked safely behind the warm dynamo where the water-hammer from the sixty-second piston met the steady sixty-cycle hum of the iron casing, hung an old, empty brass pressure-bell—once part of an alarm rig whose clapper had rusted off and vanished down into the roots generations ago.

With a touch lighter than a velvet-leech’s foot, the Power thinned a thumb-sized patch on the inside of that heavy brass bell by half a millimeter, and nudged a swelling woody truss half an inch closer until its bark kissed the rim under shifting afternoon load.

That evening, just as the sun slid off the upper canopy and the matriarch poured her mottled, boneless bulk up out of her ninety-degree den to begin her rounds, the cooling eastern leaves slackened, the buried sump-engine dropped its nightly third in pitch, and the sixty-second piston tripped its brass latch.

*Clack—shhhhhhh.*

As the pressure wave rolled back through the manifold, the woody truss shifted against the retuned brass bell. For the first time in eighty years, through the solid root-floor and the warm condensate, the bell answered the dynamo in a pure, resonant fifth—a deep, liquid, bronze *voooom* that hung in the wet air for ten full seconds, harmonizing every stray vibration in the hollow into a single, chest-filling chord before fading back into the patter of drops.

Halfway across the ceramic manifold, the matriarch stopped.

She did not startle. Slowly, deliberately, she pressed four broad, mucilage-slick arms flat against the warm copper pipes and lifted her smooth mantle-hood an inch into the silver mist. Beneath her skin, a slow, luxurious wave of deep plum and brick-red rippled from her eyes all the way down to the tips of her seven coiled limbs—the color she turned only when submerged in the cleanest, warmest current after a fresh molt. For three full minutes, while the flywheel crept through one complete turn, she simply held onto the pipe and let the vanishing bronze harmonic wash through every nerve in her boneless body.

Then the chord died away. A drop of guttation water struck her nictitating membrane and rolled off. Five feet to her left, a young bladder-vine was pressing a hair too hard against a porcelain insulator, making it chime out of rhythm.

She flowed forward through a three-inch gap between the gourds, hooked two arms around a stay-bolt for leverage, and went back to her work.

Overhead, in the ragged oval of darkening sky, the first humid stars came out, and the Attention withdrew without leaving a footprint—leaving the water to drip, the valves to sigh, the orchard stream down in the valley to run clear through the night, and the whole packed cauldron to go on turning sunlight into life.
