To look upon this drowned plain with mere pity—to wish it warmer, brighter, swifter, or louder—would be the mistake of a shallow mind. 

A lesser power, arriving here, might be tempted by theater. It might part the grey winter chop above and pour golden shafts of sun down into the avenues. It might warm the currents, breach the distant sills to let in schools of silver fish, or spark quick, bright fires of thought behind the blind mantles of the stone-movers. And in doing so, it would murder everything that makes this world a masterpiece. Warmth would strip the dissolved oxygen from the heavy brine and suffocate the copper-blooded grazers in an hour; bright light would breed fast, greedy algae to choke the slow laminarians; swift predators would shatter an etiquette built on two-hundred-year vigils and three-day meals.

The power that turns its attention downward through the seventy meters of cold press knows better. It recognizes peace when it sees it. It recognizes that the ancient, bronze-chiseled grid and its quiet inheritors have achieved something exceedingly rare in the universe: a dynamic, living stillness. Not stagnation, not death, but music played in a tempo so vast that frantic eyes mistake it for stone.

So first, the power listens.

For ten years—which down here is merely a long afternoon, time enough for an architect to re-shim one side of its spiral baffle and for three grazers to cross a market district—the presence simply rests in the water column, weightless and unseen, letting the subaudible *thrums* in the dry-stacked slate pass through its awareness. It learns the grammar of the double-beats. It watches the velvet-black arms weed the pitted backs of the travelers at slack water. It counts the thirty leathery eggs anchored beneath the stolen stable-lintel in the municipal cistern, feeling the slow, cabbage-sized heart beating beside them once every two minutes.

And because it is wise as well as fond, it looks beyond the avenues—out to the edges of the basin, down into the bedrock beneath the paving, and forward through the slow, grinding mill of geological time—asking only one question: *Where will the pinch come?*

Not an enemy; there are no enemies here. Only entropy, which moves even slower than the grazers, yet still moves.

Looking across five thousand years at a glance, the power finds three quiet griefs waiting in the dark.

First, the chemistry of the locked sea is slowly starving at the margins. Every chalky pink plate of coralline algae sealing a collapsed granary, every hardened chitin beak chewing through a kelp holdfast, every trace of copper in the blue blood and calcium in the stiffened hydraulic cartilage draws from a finite pantry. No great rivers scour raw mountains into this basin; the ancient builders paved the entire watershed down to the clay. In forty millennia, the coralline mortar will thin and crumble; the beaks of the architects’ great-grandchildren will grow soft and fray against the tough wrist-stipes of the sea-oak.

Second, the eggs in the cellar have nowhere new to go when they hatch, save where luck and blind stone-luck allow. When the thirty young uncoil from their leathery cases—perhaps twelve years from now—they will creep outward along the canals looking for loose stone to stack into their own first tiny dead-water traps. Most of the ancient city is mortared tight by pink crust or settled into permanent, interlocking equilibrium; fallen eaves-shingles and loose cobbles are rarer than food. Half the young architects wander too far into broad, monolithic plazas where every block weighs two tons and nothing can be pried free by small arms, and there, un-sheltered by baffles, they slowly starve in the clean sweep of the drift.

Third, the grid has fractures. Miles away, where an ancient fault-line sleeps beneath a terraced vineyard district, the bedrock has settled half a meter over three millennia, severing the stone-to-stone contact of a whole province. The grazers on the eastern side of that sunken seam press their warm flanks to the slate and send their low *thump-thump* down the wall, and the sound dies in three yards of wet clay. Two parishes live half a mile apart and believe themselves the edges of the world.

Having seen, the power decides what to do. It will leave no footprints, sign no name, demand no worship, and break no silence. It works at four degrees above freezing, moving no faster than the tide.

---

First, in the deepest, most forgotten trenches at the absolute center of the inland sea—far out where ancient quarry-pits drop twenty meters below the paved avenues, places even the grazers seldom bother to descend—the power reaches deep beneath the dressed floor, down through the sealed clay and into the cold crust of the earth.

It does not strike a hot vent. A boiling black smoker would scar the cold sea and breed alien worms. Instead, with infinite delicacy, it parts hairline fractures in a buried vein of mineral-rich basalt and limestone three miles down, guiding groundwater upward until it cools completely in the rock before ever reaching the quarry floor. 

What emerges between two colossal, bronze-wedged foundation blocks in the deep pit is not a plume, not even a bubble, just a cold, dense *seep*—barely three gallons an hour, the exact temperature of the surrounding abyss, heavy with dissolved calcium, trace copper, silica, and carbonates. The dense brine hugs the pavement like invisible mist, pooling along the gutters of the sunken quarry-roads and creeping outward with the four-hour lean of the continental drift.

No creature notices. Nothing changes overnight. Only, three miles downstream two winters later, a plate of muted lavender algae sealing a stable wall finds it slightly easier to lay down its winter rim. And inside the dark well of the plaza cairn, as the thirty grapefruit-sized eggs drink trace ions through their leathery skins, the tiny embryonic beaks forming in the jelly harden a shade darker, denser, truer black. The pantry is quietly restocked for the next ten million years.

---

Second, the power walks the broad, monolithic plazas three avenues away from the cistern—the barren stretches where the ancient builders laid cyclopean granite slabs too massive and tight-fitted for young, wrist-thick arms to ever pry apart.

Waiting until the twenty-hour dark is at its deepest and no sensory tendrils trail within half a kilometer, the power lays an invisible hand along the upper courses of a dozen ruined storehouses bordering those wide avenues. It does not topple the walls; the equilibrium of the bulging stone is sacred. Instead, it mimics the patient wedging of centuries-old kelp holdfasts. 

Here, a dry-stacked parapet loosens by two millimeters. Five minutes later, while the continental current leans west, a single forty-pound slate coping-stone slides softly free and settles into the velvet peat-silt with a dull, solitary *clack*. 

An hour later, half a mile north, three split-granite headers slip gently off a lintel that was already sagging, coming to rest in a neat, scattered cluster beside a shallow canal—blocks ranging from ten pounds to sixty, sized like progressive stepping-stones for growing strength. Over the span of six winters, at a pace indistinguishable from ordinary age, a faint trail of workable, flat-faced rubble appears across the unyielding plazas, spaced at intervals of three hundred yards, right where the weak abyssal eddy naturally bends around the old municipal district.

When the thirty eggs finally split their seams—not next year, nor the year after, but in their own unhurried season—the pale, thumb-thick hatchlings creeping along the mortarless cracks will find starter-shingles waiting in the silt just when their yolk-reserves begin to thin. They will stack three slates in the lee of a doorstep, catch their first drifting dust-mote amphipods, and live long enough to grow thighs thick as men's and build spirals of their own.

---

Last, and perhaps purely out of affection, the power goes out to the sunken fault-line under the drowned vineyards, where the severed wall stops the songs of the grazers.

Down in the three-yard gap where the ancient causeway snapped and filled with dead silt, burying the stone-path in sound-muffling mud, the power reaches into the rubble of a collapsed watchtower nearby. Moving no faster than the hydraulic crane-arm of an old architect—lifting, swinging four inches off the floor, making fussy, blind-seeming adjustments—it slides seven long, dense lintels of blue granite across the mud-choked rift. 

It beds the first deep against the fractured bedrock of the western terrace; it wedges the seventh tight against the sunken course of the eastern paddock wall; and between them, it shims the joints with flat, hard wedges of slate until the seven buried beams lock under the weight of the water into a single, rigid, unbroken bridge of stone beneath the silt. Then it smooths the dark bacterial snow back over the seam until nothing shows at all save an ordinary swell in the mossy turf, already indistinguishable from the work of the bronze-chiseled age.

The power withdraws upward ten meters into the swaying laminarian canopy and waits.

Four hours pass. The grey bruise of noon blooms faintly seventy meters overhead, turning the burgundy sea-oak along the wall-tops to the color of old wine, then fades back into absolute black. The current leans east, pauses in breathless suspension, and leans west.

Half a kilometer up the western vineyard terrace, two grazers meet in a roofless courtyard. One is massive, six feet long, its collagen saddle bearing a miniature forest of black wrack and chalky pink plates a century old; the other is middling, its pale sensory needles tasting the elder’s chisel-pitted flank. Slowly, the younger glides up onto the elder’s broad ridge, sealing its muscular sole down.

In the pitch dark, localized warmth blooms along their valves. Together, their flanks contract.

*Thrum-thump.*

The low, heavy pulse enters the dry-stacked granite of the courtyard wall. It races down the hillside through blocks laid four thousand years ago, reaches the edge of the ancient fault where for thirty centuries the sound has died in wet clay—and hits the buried blue-granite lintels.

Without losing a fraction of its velvet weight, the vibration crosses the seam, rings into the eastern paddock courses, and rolls onward through three miles of dark, silent masonry.

Far off in the eastern parish, paused beside an empty doorway draped in ten-meter kelp ribbons, a solitary old grazer—halfway through a three-day meal on a ghost-translucent blade—stills its cowled mouth. Through six feet of muscular sole pressed flat to the threshold, it feels a parish it has never known suddenly open up in the dark. The world has just doubled in size, and it is full of kin.

Slowly, taking four minutes to draw two gallons of freezing water into its gill-chamber so its lateral valves have full resonance, the solitary grazer presses its warm side hard against the ancient doorjamb and answers.

*Thump-thump.*

Back across the buried bridge the reply travels, passing beneath the swaying forest, vibrating faintly through the spiral stones of three distant cairns where vast, blind hearts beat once every two minutes over their quiet cellars, telling everyone who reads the stone that the road goes on.

High in the dark water column, the unseen visitor feels the answering beat pass faintly through the soles of its own borrowed stillness. Nothing here needs saving. Nothing needs waking. The stone holds, the cold keeps, and the second tenants have ten million years of afternoon ahead of them.

Without disturbing a single plum-sized gas bladder in the canopy, the presence lets go of the sea and rises through the winter chop into the grey sky, leaving the dark exactly as it found it, only slightly deeper in grace.
