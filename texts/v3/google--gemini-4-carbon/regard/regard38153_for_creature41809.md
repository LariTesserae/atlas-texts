A lesser benevolence would be tempted by generosity. It would want to widen the crooked slot in the shale so she did not scrape her ribs on the descent; it would want to thicken the pale mat in the trough so she could harvest twice as much and double the silver brought back to her quiet house; it would want to ease the stiff, damp-born ache that is beginning to settle into the small bones of her fifty-year-old wrists. 

Wisdom stays its hand. 

The attention that settles into the sinkhole does not arrive like light, which would wake green, hungry things that have no business here, nor like a breeze, which would dry the weeping curtains and throw off the slow math of the drip. It enters the hollow the way warmth enters stone—without displacing a single grain of silt. It lingers in the pewter twilight, taking the measure of the room. It reads the five-hundred-year-old peck-marks under their glaze of cream-colored calcite; it notes the tireless, delicate brushing of the soft-shelled crab keeping its joints free of stone; it traces the exact trace-chemistry of copper salt, cave-spore, and sulfur brine dripping from the three green pins; it follows the woman up the hemp rope and watches her rub warm oil into her calves beside a peat fire while outside the winter rain lashes the coastal grass.

It sees that this is not a place in need of rescue, nor a labor in need of improvement. What exists between the woman, the trough, the blind grazers, and the weeping rock is a conversation carried out at the speed of geology. To enrich this room would be to break it. More light brings moss; moss starves the ochre bacteria; without the bacteria, the limpets vanish; without the exact, lean balance of the pans, the *mother-of-brine* grows fat, porous, and useless against the grey rot in a fisherman’s pierced hand. Even easing the difficulty of the climb would be an injury in disguise—it is the narrowness of the slot, the slickness of the columns, and the discipline of the bad air that have kept greed and hurry out of this pocket for twenty generations.

And yet, looking further down and further ahead than human eyes or blind cave-eyes can reach, the wise power sees two quiet things coming.

The first is forty feet above, wedged in the slumped shale of the western rim. Just beneath the lip where her hemp rope bites into the stone four times a year, a hairline fracture has been drinking winter rainwater for six decades. Clay in the seam has swollen and gone slick. Left to the ordinary drift of gravity and frost-heave, three winters from now—perhaps during an empty February night, or perhaps on the spring equinox while a greased shoulder is wedged directly beneath it—a four-ton block of rotten shale is going to shear loose. It will not kill the hollow completely, though it will smash four fluted columns to chalky rubble and bury the slate platform, the clay jars, and the deep trough under dead rock, sealing the scoby away from air and hands until the village above forgets that fish-spine wounds were ever survivable.

The second thing is not in the stone, but in the house on the shelf above. The keeper has no daughter. Her sister’s child died of the winter croup twelve years back, before the cure was given fast enough, and the girl she has been quietly watching in the village—a cooper’s third daughter, small-boned, patient with knots, capable of sitting still for an hour watching tide-pools without throwing a stone into them—belongs to another bloodline. Pride and five centuries of habit sit heavy in the older woman's chest, arguing that a house secret dies with the house, even as her left knee catches and clicks on the climb out of the earth.

So the benevolent power acts—not from outside nature, and never faster than the sinkhole’s own clock.

Deep in the hairline seam of the western rim, where the winter water meets the underlying limestone shelf, it nudges the chemistry of a single trickle of seepage by less than half a percent. Instead of dissolving the bind, the creeping moisture begins to drop its load of dissolved carbonate directly into the rotten clay of the fault. Over the next thirty months, grain by invisible grain, crystal fingers of tough, fibrous aragonite knit across the shear-plane inside the dark rock. Nobody will ever see it. When the big January gale hits three winters hence, the four-ton block of shale does not even shudder; it has been quietly, permanently mortared back onto the bones of the coast.

Down in the hollow itself, the power touches almost nothing. It watches a heavy drop of water gather on an overhead curtain two feet left of the curing pins—a slow migration of ceiling-seepage that, in seven years, would eventually find the middle copper pin and glaze it over in stone, ruining the drip onto the hanging strip. With an infinitesimal breath of warmth—no more than the heat of a sleeping salamander’s belly—it coaxes a thumbnail-sized ridge of mineral crust to thicken by the breadth of an eyelash on the upside of the curtain. The water takes the easier path, sliding two inches to the right, back toward an empty pan where three calcite-limpets are waiting in the ochre silt. The pins will stay dry-necked for another two hundred years.

That is all it does to the stone. It leaves the carbon dioxide pooling in the corner basin to rise and fall with the coastal barometers, because respect for the room requires that the room remain dangerous to the careless. It leaves the crab to its endless grooming. It leaves the keeper’s aging knee untouched, because it understands human hearts: if her body felt twenty again, she would put off the hardest decision of her life until a sudden stroke on the windy moor took the knowledge out of the world forever in an afternoon.

Instead, on a grey Tuesday near the end of February, three weeks before the spring descent, it arranges one very small convergence up on the windy shelf.

Nothing grand—no vision in the peat smoke, no dream with a voice in it. Just a shift in the afternoon squall off the sea that drives the cooper’s twelve-year-old daughter under the lee of a dry-stone sheep fold out on the headland at the exact quarter-hour the older woman stops there to rest her basket of kelp and ease her clicking knee.

For ten minutes, neither speaks. The wind hisses through the thrift grass on top of the wall. Wedged in a damp chink of the dry-stone right between them, a common garden snail is slowly sealing its shell against the salt draft with a froth of mucus. The older woman watches the girl out of the corner of her eye. The girl does not poke the snail with a straw. She does not fidget, or chatter to fill the quiet, or wipe the cold sea-mist from her face. She sits on her heels, compact and balanced, breathing slow through her nose, watching the tiny, unhurried labor in the stone with steady, unsentimental attention until the film hardens to silver parchment.

In the silence behind the wind, the benevolent power simply holds the space still for three breaths longer than usual—warm, heavy, unhurried, smelling faintly of wet limestone—letting the older woman feel the exact shape of her own fatigue alongside the exact shape of the girl’s quietness, without fear or stinginess getting in the way.

The keeper looks down at her own broad, calloused thumbs, scarred white at the edges from forty years of hemp rope and bronze-scraping. Then she looks at the girl’s bare ankles, thin and tough above her wooden clogs, narrow enough to slip sideways between two crowded pillars without waking the water.

"Your father can spare you at the equinox," the woman says aloud, her voice flat and practical against the wind. It isn't quite a question. "Come by the back gate before first light. Don't wear wool, and don't wash your hands in lye the night before."

The girl turns her head slowly, looks at the woman who smells of mutton tallow and cold flint, and only nods once.

Far below their feet, sixty paces wide behind its choked slots of shale, a bead of warm gas detaches from the silt in the corner basin and rises—*blup*—through six inches of clear brine. The ripples spread until they touch the smooth black curve of the ironwood scoop, rock it by less than the width of a hair, and flatten back into glass. Satisfied, the wise power withdraws its weight, leaving the dark, warm room entirely to itself.
