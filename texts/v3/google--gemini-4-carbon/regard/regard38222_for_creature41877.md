A lesser benevolence, looking down upon this dry, silent basin, would have brought rain.

It is the first and clumsiest reflex of mercy: to see thirst and pour out water, to see crowding and offer expanse, to hear silence and fill it with song. A well-meaning hand would have gathered those miles-wide streets of afternoon cloud, thickened their flat bellies with grey moisture, and let a warm deluge fall across the baked ochre tips of the chimneys.

Wisdom stays that hand before a single drop can form.

Rain, here, would be an apocalypse. Heavy water hammering down upon a billion tons of sun-cured, unglazed silt mortar would turn the vaulted bridges to slick red gruel in an hour. The apartment-sized haystacks of the weavers—built to shed virga and hoard dry warmth, packed decades deep with dust and felt—would drink the storm like sponges, grow impossibly heavy, snap the coppery branches of the swollen trees, and crash down into the alleys, smothering ten thousand districts in wet, rotting thatch. The sudden flood, unable to drain quickly enough through the tight fractures of the young basalt, would drown the deep fungal gardens forty feet down, choking the blind miners in their shafts and severing the sole, delicate wick that draws life from the dark water table to the sun.

No. When the Wise Power turns its attention to this horizon-spanning city, it sees immediately that there is nothing broken here to mend.

And it sees something else: that the "being who lives in it" is not the supple six-foot monitor draped along the root-buttress, nor the high-shelled tortoise waiting patiently for the white latex to clot, nor the blind worker cementing the breach, nor the weaver flashing violent ultraviolet throat-feathers in the gloom. Those are cells, capillaries, peristaltic waves, and sensory nerves. The being that lives here *is* the city itself—a single, continent-wide metabolism made of clay, chitin, grass, sap, and bone, breathing once every twenty-four hours through a million dark throats, speaking to itself through the dry *thump* and *tick* of solid masonry, holding its own warmth against the cold stars.

To love a thing wisely is to love the exact shape of its endurance, not to wish it softer than it is.

So the Power descends—not into the sky, where there is nothing to touch, and not into the amber alleys, where even a ghostly footstep would send a lie rippling through the sensitive clay floor—but *underneath*. Down past the razor-edged coils of the flood basalt, past the damp, mushroom-warm chambers of the fungal combs, down into the dark, ancient sedimentary strata locked beneath the volcanic caprock, where the fossil water sits.

Looking along the long arc of geological time, the Power reads the only true vulnerabilities of this silent miracle.

First: the water beneath the basalt is finite. With no rivers crossing the sharp stone above and no rain reaching the ground, the deep aquifers are an inheritance from an older, wetter epoch before the lava poured out. Every night, when a million vents open at dusk and sigh warm dampness into the twilight—even with the gliders licking the masonry dry, even with the aerial roots drinking the breath—a fraction of that ancient water escapes upward into the high, dry atmosphere and drifts away across the curve of the world on those afternoon cloud-streets. Left entirely to physics, in twelve thousand years the capillary shafts will reach dry sand. The mortar will crumble without saliva; the chimneys will crack without repair; the great quiet body will mummify standing up.

Second: the magma plume miles below the crust, the mother of the young flood basalt, is not entirely dead, only sleeping. One more massive, continent-splitting effusion from those deep fissures would swallow the earth-city in fresh black glass before a single tortoise could clear an alley.

Third: the outer edges. Far away, beyond the blue-gray curve of the horizon where the basalt sheet finally thins and meets older, weathered lands, other ages will eventually bring loud, careless, fire-carrying things—or aggressive, wind-borne weeds and wet-world plagues—that do not know how to walk on ceramic-hard mud without shattering the roof of the world.

The Power decides to do three things, so quietly that not a single large-eared browser freezes in the dark, and not a single blind soldier sways its amber head out of rhythm.

Deep in the crust, twenty miles below the water table, the Power lays a cool, still palm against the restless mantle plume. It does not extinguish the Earth’s heat, for heat is lawful; instead, it gently turns and channels the rising magmatic pressure sideways, guiding it far out beneath a distant oceanic trench where fresh stone can be born harmlessly under miles of salt water. The young basalt caprock above is granted geological peace. It will never split open in fire again.

Next, in the dark strata between that deep heat and the roots of the termite shafts, the Power alters the subtle geometry of the subterranean stone. Far away—three hundred miles beyond the edge of the basalt basin, where distant mountain ranges *do* catch the monsoons—deep, porous sandstone seams lie tilted away from the dry plateau. With an infinitely slow, silent persuasion, grain by microscopic grain so that no tremor registers in the masonry above, the Power coaxes a deep, sealed syncline to open: a hidden, subterranean aqueduct running beneath the roots of the mountains straight under the volcanic shield.

No river will ever break the surface of the city. No marsh will ever rot its foundations. Instead, rainwater that fell fifty years ago on peaks unseen beyond the horizon will filter slowly through three hundred miles of dark, mineral-rich stone, arriving cool and pure fifty yards beneath the basalt fractures, replenishing the buried water table drop for drop at the exact rate the millón dusk-vents breathe it out. The inheritance becomes a pulse. Now the city can live not for ten thousand years, but for ten million.

And what of the surface? What gift can be given to a mind made of vibration, patience, and interlocking need, without spoiling its self-reliance?

Up on that raw, black pressure-ridge of buckled basalt—the highest point in the basin, not yet swallowed by red clay and jade-bushes—the Power lingers for one night between dusk and dawn, simply *witnessing*.

There is a kind of nourishment that comes purely from being beheld without interference. Think of a master weaver or an engrossed child working in a quiet room while someone who loves them sits by the door, asking nothing, changing nothing, merely keeping watch so the work will not be interrupted.

Along the jagged perimeter of the entire flood-basalt continent, where the black glass meets the outer world, the Power raises the raw volcanic rim by a scant few hundred feet of impassable, razor-coiled stone, starved of subsoil beneath so the termites will not bridge outward across it, and sharp enough to turn back the hooves, wheels, and invasive roots of whatever noisy empires may rise and fall in the outer lands over the coming eons. The basin becomes an island in time, ringed by dry, clinking ceramic cliffs and dry winds.

Then, just before dawn, down in the warm, dark lane where the copper-barked tree crushed the spire—where the six-foot monitor lies digesting its meal in the stillness, and the last half-millimeter of the eight-foot split is being sealed by the glistening, blind workers—the Power allows itself a single touch.

It presses an unseen palm flat against the base of the mended red spire, right where the sun-cured silt meets the flared collar of the root, listening through the solid matter the way the inhabitants listen.

Through the vast, unbroken masonry of thousands of square miles runs a single, sub-audible harmonic—slower than a heartbeat, warm, deep, and resonant, like the settling of bedrock that has found its eternal rest.

In the dark galleries forty feet down, the millions of pale workers pause their faint head-knocking for the space of one breath, antennae lifted into the mushroom-scented air, feeling the stone beneath them grow deep and sure. Along the root-buttress, the heavy monitor unhoods one dark eye in the gloom, feels the quiet certainty hum through its ribs, and lets its chin sink back onto the bark in profound animal ease. High in the suspended straw continents, a hundred thousand small birds tuck their bone-colored beaks deeper into their scapular feathers without waking.

Dawn hits the pale ochre tips of the chimneys. The eastern flanks warm. Silently, across five thousand districts, a million lizards shift three inches into the light, and the great, quiet city goes on building itself forever, watched over, untouched, and whole.
