A lesser kindness would look into that steaming cup of razors and try to mend it. 

It would cool the scalding draft to a gentle spring, smooth the obsidian needles so they could not pare away flesh, untangle the thirty-degree shear on the western wall, and stitch the bleeding seams of cold time shut. It would hush the frantic industry, restore ordinary sound to the air, and perhaps scatter hardy moss-spores across the cooled basalt. And in a week, every creature in the blister would be dead. Their hydraulic bodies would go slack without the pressure of the crowd and the heat; their cured hides would rot in temperate air; the blind grazers would starve without the iron-sulfide steam; the pale tool-users, suddenly deafened by noise and blinded by the loss of their shock-bruises, would perish in bewilderment on blunt, useless stone.

The power that pauses above the ragged aperture in the afternoon sky is older and wiser than pity. 

It does not descend as a shape, or cast a shadow across the shadowless clarity of the cistern. It simply rests its attention upon the rim, looking down into the silent, stippled purple flickers of ten thousand tiny violences, and recognizes what it is seeing. This is not a mistake of the mountain. It is a crucible. Out of raw catastrophic trauma—unweathered glass, boiling poison, torn geometry—life has not merely survived; it has made an orchard of the wreckage. More than that: up near the rim, shivering with internal frostbite, faceless animals are tearing open the fabric of sequence with chipped rocks so they can *see ahead*. 

To take away their struggle would be to insult their ferocity. Yet to leave the blister entirely as it is means an inevitable, meaningless end. The mountain is still settling. One millimeter an hour, the young andesite shifts. Within a season, or ten, one of two things will happen: a larger tremor will pinch the geothermal vent shut, freezing the oasis into a tomb of black ice within twenty minutes, or the fractured ceiling will collapse inward, crushing the entire silent nation before a single climber has time to learn what else can be done with a stone wedge and a half-second of borrowed future.

So the wise power reaches down—not into the blister, where its touch would feel like an alien law, but deep beneath it, miles down into the blinded, groaning root of the newly thrust range.

First, it finds the fault-lines feeding the hairline vent at the floor. Gently, with the patience of tectonic plates, it braces the deep throat of the conduit with pillars of dense, unyielding gabbro, locking the aperture open against the settling weight of the mountain. The scalding breath will not be pinched off in the night; the sulfur-slime and brass-shelled grazers have ten thousand years of guaranteed summer.

Next, it traces the mountain’s upward, unhealed stress—the hourly millimeter shift that turns the room black with absorbed groans. Instead of stopping the shift, which the climbers rely upon to spall fresh blades from the walls, the power redirects its vector. 

When the next hourly groan comes, the cistern goes pitch-black for one heartbeat as usual. Flakes of fresh glass pop loose into waiting three-fingered paws. Nothing inside the original room is broken or calmed. 

Except down near the floor, just behind the slick, crowded ledges of the eastern wall, an entire five-foot slab of young basalt parts cleanly along a newly guided fault and slips backward into the mountain’s flank, opening a black throat where solid rock stood a heartbeat before.

Steam rushes into the new cavity, curling and hissing without a sound. 

For three minutes, nothing approaches it. The pale climbers freeze in tangles around the breach, their violet gill-slits flaring rapidly, twenty bead-eyes per collar locked onto the strange absence of light-bruises inside the gap. Then one of the scarred, blurring elders—missing two fingertips, frostbitten around the mouth from decades of drinking cold ozone, flickering slightly ahead of its own limbs—drops down from the upper rim. It holds a thick, freshly spalled lance of obsidian in both split forepaws. Step by careful hydraulic step, bracing its cured hide against the razor-sharp threshold, it leans into the new room.

It finds what the wise power has hollowed out for them.

The blister is no longer a dead-end cistern; it is now the vestibule to a labyrinth of sister-blisters winding deep into the warm spine of the peak. 

The power has not made these new chambers easy, because easy things make dull minds. It has tuned them like instruments, stepping the brokenness outward in deliberate, survivable gradients. 

In the first new chamber, just past the threshold, there is space—simply *room*, three times the surface area of the crowded cistern, fed by a twin branch of the same scalding vent, wet and sharp and rich with fresh obsidian ridges, yet empty of rivals. And along its back wall, where the geothermal steam condenses against a wide, sloping shelf of dense, unfractured diorite, there is something that has never existed in their world before: a dry, warm overhang, flat enough for six hydraulic bodies to lie down at full length without sliding into boiling runoff, sheltered from falling glass and trampling feet. 

A place where an exhausted mind might, for the first time in ten thousand generations, discover what happens when consciousness lets go into sleep—especially a mind still humming faintly with cold, swallowed time.

Further back in the dark, for the boldest who will push deeper in the months and centuries to come, the wise power has left stranger gifts. 

There is a gallery where the acoustic failure is only half-complete: where a tapped stone still produces a faint violet bruise in the air, *and* a low, resonant hum in the rock—a Rosetta Stone of physics, waiting to teach creatures who only read shockwaves that vibration can carry across distance, that one climber tapping a chisel twice against a basalt rib can speak to another climber thirty feet away without touching it.

Above that gallery winds a chimney leading toward a sheltered side-pocket on the leeward cliff-face, shielded from the lethal mountain wind, where the scalding steam thins out into warm, misty twilight—a half-way threshold to the wider earth below the cloud-deck, plated with strange, softer stones like flint and native copper that do not shatter on the third strike, waiting for a woodcock-pawed mason to test what happens when a tool outlasts the task.

And near the roof of the first new chamber, where the geometry still frays against the high altitude, a single hairline seam hangs in the air. 

The power touched this seam last of all, altering its tension by a fraction of a degree. When two climbers inevitably scramble up the virgin glass walls tonight, working silently side by side to jam their stone wedges into empty space, the wedge will not immediately splinter. And the colorless fluid that weeps out will not burn their insides quite so viciously. It will run just a touch warmer, a fraction slower, giving the drinker not fifteen frantic minutes of survival-reflex, catching worms half a second early—but an hour of stillness on the dry diorite shelf below, where an unmoored mind, no longer forced to spend every drop of foresight dodging a neighbor's elbow, might look at the black stone in its split paw and see not just the next strike, but the shape of the blade before it is knapped.

Overhead, in the original cistern, the pale afternoon sky remains flat and clear. 

Down on the gravel of broken glass, young climbers are already surging toward the new breach in the eastern wall, testing the edges with calloused pads, carrying splinters of obsidian into the dark. Behind them, the elder who went in first has climbed onto the warm, flat diorite shelf. Its six slick limbs slowly uncoil, losing their fierce hydraulic tension for the first time since it hatched. Its violet gill-fringes beat slower, and slower still. Its bead-eyes disappear beneath tough gristle lids, and in the quiet dark behind them, untouched by razors, the first dream on top of the mountain begins.

Above the rim, the wise power withdraws its hand, leaving no footprint, no law, and no demand—only a door left ajar, and room enough to grow into whatever they choose to become.
