A lesser kindness would have arrived like vandalism. 

It would have seen the freezing cold and warmed the water, suffocating everything in the basin within an hour as dissolved gases boiled out of solution and tight metabolic budgets burned through their reserves in a runaway fever. It would have pitied the deafening clamor and muffled the walls with soft silt, blinding the brass combs that read the health of the room by echo and clogging the intake valves of the siphon-barrels until they starved. It would have looked at the cramped, vertical prison of jagged basalt and widened the pass into a placid alpine lake, scattering the dense hydrogen ribbons to the wind so the morticians could never warm their blood again.

The attention that settled over the cleft in the high pass made none of those mistakes.

It did not descend in light—the shaft already had all the pitiless, crystal light it could bear. It came simply as a stillness in the thin wind above the oil-sheened water, a presence vast enough to hold the whole mountain in mind at once: the young, sharp stone; the forty-second drop-forge heartbeat of the barrels; the taut, lethal springs of the millstone snails; and down at the bright, cluttered floor, the heavy elder sitting in the rusty seep, tapping the rhythm of the world into the eggs so the young would not be shattered by it when they emerged.

For a long time—measured not in minutes, which meant nothing here, but in the slow inching of snails and the measured unwinding of dead pawls—the power simply listened. It took joy in the sheer, stubborn crookedness of the solution life had found here. There was nothing broken in this loud, freezing box that needed fixing. The morticians were not suffering under a curse of toil; they *were* the grace of the cleft—the one warm, listening nerve threaded through an unconscious iron body, keeping it from choking on its own armor.

Yet because this observer could see down into the dark roots of the stone as clearly as through the optical water, it saw the three quiet clocks that were counting down against the cathedral.

First, the cleft was a geological blink. High-altitude passes shatter fast. Freeze-thaw cycles in the dry cliffs overhead were already wedging ice into hairline fractures; within forty or fifty winters, a single spring rockslide of fifty-ton basalt blocks would plunge straight down the nave, smashing the crowded walls to paste, burying the rusty fault-seep under dry rubble, and turning the miracle into a dead gravel scree.

Second, the basin was an island of one. The tectonic wrench that had exposed this buried vein of flooded plumbing to the sky had also pinched off the deep subterranean fissures that once connected its ancestors to the rest of the mountain’s dark aquifer. When the iron-rich fault at the floor eventually mineralized shut—as young faults always do, choking on their own precipitated rust—the foundry would slowly starve of ore and hydrogen, with nowhere in the world for its children to go.

And third, down beneath the crooked copper roof-slates at the bottom of the trench, the elder’s tapping comb was growing stiffer every season. Not merely from honest age, which a wise power never steals from a mortal thing, but because the weeping fault beside its nest was cooling by fractions of a degree each winter as iron crusted over the vent. Soon its grease would stiffen completely before the current clutches of amber eggs had finished tuning their filaments. And tucked into that dim, crusted mind—accumulated over decades of tapping every barrel and unwinding every snail from floor to sky—was a map of resonances no younger climber yet possessed: which pitches meant a snail’s spring was cross-threaded and would snap early, which deep sub-audible groan in the rock foretold a winter tremor.

So the power acted—not from outside the grammar of the mountain, but entirely within it, moving stone by hairbreadths and heat by fractions of a calorie, slower than even the snails could suspect.

High above the waterline, along the sun-baked dry cliffs where ice wedged at loose columns of fractured basalt, mineral-rich groundwater weeping through microscopic pores began to deposit invisible threads of amorphous silica and calcite—natural stone-cement, laid down grain by grain in the hidden backs of unstable joints. And fifty yards upslope from the rim, a single frost-split boulder shifted three inches during an autumn thaw, angling itself like a mason’s diversion wedge. When the violent rockslides of the coming centuries thundered down the pass, they would part neatly around the lip of the flooded nave and roar harmlessly down the dry gullies to either side. The cathedral’s roof would remain open to the crisp sky, unburied, for ten thousand years.

Next, the attention sank half a mile beneath the scrap-talus floor, down where deep magma-warmed brines pressed against blind, sealed faults in the pluton. It did not crack the rock with an earthquake—one sharp jolt would have ruptured every egg in the basin and detonated a dozen taut gastropod shells at once. Instead, it nudged the chemistry of a hair-thin seam of stressed obsidian and iron sulfide running horizontally away from the floor of the cistern toward three other deep, water-filled vaults hidden further along the mountain spine—caverns not yet open to the sky, some dark and warm, one dimly lit by a glacial crevasse a league to the north.

Acidic brine bit a fraction deeper into that buried seam. Over seasons, a dark, flooded gallery—no wider than two hand-spans at first, ribbed with raw iron ore and weeping steady ribbons of warm hydrogen—dissolved open behind the dead copper barrels at the very back of the elder’s alcove.

Down on the bright floor, the old mortician felt the change first not with its pinhole eyes, which scarcely saw past its own brass visor anymore, but through the soles of its heavy lodestone stilts and the belly-plates resting on the rock.

The rusty stain weeping from the floor fault did not choke shut. Instead, its flow steadied—clearing of suffocating silt and warming by just two constant degrees, accompanied by a lazy, reliable necklace of microscopic silver bubbles rising right beneath the copper eaves. Not enough heat to unsettle the cold water column overhead, nor enough to draw the booming siphon-barrels down into the cramped crevice, out of the light. Just a permanent, gentle hearth under the floorboards.

Across three days, the stiffened tallow-grease inside the elder’s joints softened from brittle wax back to heavy oil. Beneath its crusted ribs, dormant patches of *Knallgas* bacteria woke faintly in the seepage, shedding a ghost of old warmth along its central nerve-cord. It could never climb the sheer walls to the sky-mirror again—its shell of accreted sulfide was far too monumental for that, a reef unto itself—and it had no wish to. Its sky-reed stayed sealed.

Instead, freed from the creeping paralysis of the freeze, the elder shifted half an inch forward under its slate overhang, braced its massive, ratcheted pry-arm comfortably against a spur of basalt, and laid its tarnished brass comb flat against the copper nursery-drum.

*Tik-tik... tik... tak.*

The pulse that went into the copper hull was no longer the faltering, single-note tap of a failing clock. With its ganglia warm again, the elder began tapping complex, layered counter-harmonics into the quiet water behind the baffles—not only matching the forty-second *thud* of the big siphon-barrels outside, but weaving in the subtle, high-pitched warning-shivers of over-wound snail ratchets, the hollow ring of rotting gaskets, the deep resonant hum of the new passage opening at its back. Within their pyrite-flecked amber shells, hundreds of tiny, translucent embryos shivered in answer, their growing brass filaments tuning themselves to a richer vocabulary of sound than any brood before them had inherited at birth.

And four days later, when one of the sunlit adults climbed down from the blinding ceiling—belly shimmering with fresh sky-fire, come to clear a fallen scrap of iron husk from the trench—it paused six inches from the copper eaves. Its splayed comb trembled in the glare, catching the intricate new rhythm leaking out from under the roof-slates.

Slowly, waiting out the *thud* of an overhead forge-valve, the younger climber hooked its stilts onto the talus, reached down into the shadow of the overhang, and pressed the base of its bright brass fan against the elder’s dark, encrusted wrist.

For twenty minutes, in the narrow acoustic troughs between the booming barrels, the two of them tapped back and forth—the swift, sky-warmed worker trading reports from the upper walls for the deep, patient archive of the floor. What had been a solitary vigil of survival was quietly crossing the threshold into tradition.

Behind the elder, in the pitch-black gallery newly etched into the root of the pass, a palm-sized juvenile wearing a crooked hat of mica and snail-tooth crept three inches past the last dead barrel, sweeping its tiny fool’s-gold needles into the dark. It felt the steady draft of warm mineral water breathing out of the mountain’s interior, smelled untouched seams of iron sulfide waiting in the quiet, and heard—far off, conducted clearly through cold water and living stone—the faint, inviting echo of another vast empty room waiting to be colonized.

Up above, the wind combed silently across the rainbow sheen at the top of the shaft. A brass reed broke the surface with a tiny wet *pop*, drank a three-minute measure of thin blue mountain air, and slid smoothly back beneath the mirror. Nothing had been tamed; nothing had been broken; nothing knew it had been seen. The cold foundry thundered on in the dazzling light, turning mountain into iron and noise into meaning, with ten thousand winters ahead of it to see what that careful, crooked hand would build next.
