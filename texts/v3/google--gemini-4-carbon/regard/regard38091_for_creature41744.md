A lesser power would have ruined them in an afternoon.

Out of pity for their thirst, a clumsy mercy might have gathered the thin upper vapor into a cloud and given them rain. Within twenty minutes, liquid water running down the elephant-hide shoulders of the hollow would have dissolved the dried lichen-paste chinking a hundred dry-stone walls, slicked the bowed ladder-rungs into traps, turned the spongy mats of leaf-silt into heavy slurry, peeled the dry resin pads of the hawser-vines straight off the granite, and drowned ten thousand blind beetles in their bark crevices. 

Out of pity for their gloom, a careless grace might have parted the miles of suspended mineral dust to give them the sun—and burned out eyes widened over a thousand generations of purple twilight, bleached the beard-lichens crisp, and drunk every hoarded bead of moisture out of the split-open bowl before evening. Out of eagerness to be loved, it might have spoken from the air, or shown a bright face in an alley, and caused five hundred people to jerk their elbows at once: lamps falling from horn hooks, hot tallow spilling, babies waking in terror, a whole society sickened by the supreme rudeness of a sudden thing.

The power that found the spire was neither clumsy nor lonely. It came across the slate-colored sea of dust without making a ripple in the silt, paused beyond the polished granite lip where the thin air steals the damp from human eyes, and looked down into the warm, rattling bowl for a very long time before doing anything at all.

It knew how to read what it was seeing. It did not see deprivation; it saw a masterpiece of weight and patience. It saw that the stone, the vine, the reptile, the beetle, and the broad-ribbed people were no longer separate creatures occupying a shelter, but organs of a single slow body holding itself warm above an abyss. To pull one thread of hardship out of that weave without understanding what leaned against it would bring down the roof where the net-makers slept.

So the first thing the power practiced was local manners. It made itself slow. It stilled every impulse toward spectacle, quieted its presence until it displaced not one breath of humid air, and listened the way the blind elders listened—through wood, bone, tensioned rawhide, and stone.

Only after a full turn of seasons—watching babies suck bruised pith, watching houses unstacked at three taps of a horn rod and patiently rebuilt by sixty hands, watching grieving men turn their faces to the boulder and vanish in plain sight—did it take stock of the three quiet threats that no listener’s cheekbone could ever detect.

The first was miles beneath their bare feet.

The geothermal vein that held the hollow at the temperature of skin was very old, and it was dying. Deep down in the dark root of the granite spire, far below the drowned planetary floor, the ancient pocket of mantle-heat was exhausting itself, cooling by a fraction of a degree every century, its deep mineral hydrated seams nearly baked dry. For another four hundred years, perhaps five, the vents would breathe warm. Then the reptiles would pile closer and closer together and fail to wake; the succulents would shrivel inward; the breath of the rock would stop yielding molecules of damp; and the hollow would stiffen quietly into the cold silence of the spire around it, with nowhere in all the world for its people to climb down to.

So the power sank miles down into the blind, crushing dark beneath the mountain, where nothing lived to be startled. 

Working at the speed of cooling magma, it reached into the planetary crust and drew a thin, fresh artery of deep radiogenic warmth sideways through the bedrock, knotting it beneath the spire’s root like a weaver splicing a new strand into a frayed rope before the weight comes onto it. Around that heat, it folded deep, buried seams of water-bearing serpentine and mica, calibrating the stone’s porosity grain by microscopic grain. It did not make a geyser. It did not permit a single drop of free water to rise. It simply tuned the deep furnace so that the pores in the amphitheater floor above would go on breathing the exact warmth of a human flank, and the exact faint sweat of living stone, for another ten million years.

The second threat was in their blood.

An amphitheater of tiers and slung galleries can hold only so many bodies. Over a thousand generations on an island in the sky, every family tree had braided back into every other until the whole town shared the same stout ribs, the same hinged hips, the same wide dark irises. That closeness had perfected them for the bowl, but the arithmetic of a tiny, sealed gene pool is unforgiving over deep time; quiet errors accumulate in the dark, small frailties doubling up in sleeping wombs, the slow drift toward barrenness or brittle bone. And the same math pressed on the barrel-shrubs, the flightless moths, the hook-billed cliff-birds, and the sluggish vent-reptiles.

The power did not bring strangers up out of the dust, for there were none to bring, and strangers would not have known how to walk without crushing lizards or how to pass food under leather cloths. Instead, moving softer than warmth through the tiers at night while teeth ground and sleepers breathed into one another’s shoulder blades, it tended the copying of cells. 

It altered nothing of what they were. It kept every calloused splay of toe, every barrel chest, every liquid wideness of eye. Only where two matching, ancient flaws met in a newly conceived child—or in a clutch of leathery reptile eggs by a vent, or in the swollen seed-pod of an ironwood shrub—its touch gently proofread the spiral thread, nudging a single broken rung back to soundness, restoring the quiet vigor of a larger population without ever adding a single mouth the hollow could not feed.

The third threat was small, immediate, and domestic: the smoke and the silt.

In a windless bowl lit by smoldering tallow-and-dung braziers and dusted perpetually from above by fine mineral powder, even the slowest breath draws grit into the chest. By fifty or sixty, many of the elders slept with that wet, heavy rattle two tiers down, their broad lungs scarred by decades of silicate haze and grease-soot. To blow wind through the hollow to clear the air would have stolen their precious humidity in an hour.

So the power went back out into the yellow-purple murk beyond the spire.

Hundred of miles away across the slate sea of dust, where the broken shoulders of lower mountains lay buried under dry silt, dormant spores and ancient mineral salts drifted in the high, thin currents. The power combed the upper atmosphere for weeks, gathering trace grains of phosphorus, iodine, and salt that the hollow’s soil-mats were slowly running short of, and steered a faint, invisible ribbon of enriched dust toward the spire so the resin-smeared curtains of the silt-harvesters would comb a slightly richer catch each season.

And onto one sticky fiber line, one ordinary afternoon, it guided a single dormant spore that had blown in circles round the planet’s upper haze since before the dust rose—nudging its inner chemistry by a hair’s breadth on the way down.

A young silt-harvester scraped the resin off his line with a bone blade and kneaded the rich crumbs into a nursery-mat near a smoky niche, just as he did every day. Over three years, a small, pale-russet bracket-lichen nobody had quite seen before uncurled on the warm rock just above a tallow-brazier. It drank no more moisture than its cousins, took up almost no space, and turned out to have a greedy appetite for airborne soot and suspended silicate dust, trapping both in a waxy, dark rind that peeled off cleanly in rubbery strips suitable for waterproofing clay jars. Better still—as an inquisitive grandmother discovered twelve years later, after testing a thumb-sized pinch against her lip for a month the cautious way her people tested everything—when its pale inner pith was steamed inside a stitched bladder and chewed warm, its cool mucilage loosened the tight, dusty rattle in old lungs and let the sleeper two tiers down breathe quiet through the night.

The grandmother was praised softly across fifty doorways, shoulder to shoulder, for her keen nose and patience. The triumph belonged entirely to her.

Beyond these three things, the wise power kept its hands to itself.

When an old ironwood beam began to dry-rot under a gallery, the power did not mend the wood; it left the rot for a blind listener’s fingertips to find, so she would still have the dignity of tapping her horn rod three times against the stone, and five dozen neighbors would still unhook their gourds and spend a month leaning into one another’s flanks to resettle the curve of the wall. When someone wept into a pad of gray moss, it did not dry the tears or erase the grief; it let the damp moss be tucked around a nursery-shrub so sorrow could go on feeding the leaves, the way their morality required.

Sometimes, though, it stays.

There is no room in the hollow for another body, so it takes none. In the dimness beneath a braided vine-bridge, or along the smooth, warm curve of an interior boulder where people turn their faces when they are full to the brim with one another and need to disappear, it rests its unseen brow against the rock—facing the stone in strict observance of the house rules, asking not to be spoken to, asking not to be noticed, happy to have a basket leaned against it or a bare, leathery foot step past without apology, content simply to be part of the warmth that holds them up.
