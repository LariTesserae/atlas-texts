A lesser kindness would have ruined everything before morning.

A lesser kindness, looking down through the flat grey oval from the plain above, would have read stillness as privation and silence as an ache waiting to be answered. It would have parted the permanent overcast to let a shaft of yellow sun strike the floor—and in an hour the lashless, wide-pupiled eyes would have been scalded shut, the damp chamois of the creases cracked, the liverwort crisped to black curls along the western seam, the pale-barked fig scorched in its cleft. Or it would have sent rain, a generous warm shower to fill the basins, flushing raw prairie mud down the northern ramp, clouding the lime-rimmed pools, carrying earthworms and spider-cocoons and fast, greedy sprout-seeds to overrun the bare silt. Worse still, out of pity for a solitary thing lying folded in the corner of an unroofed room, it might have reached into the thick, smooth-walled skull and struck a spark in the dry tinder where language used to be—waking memory, measurement, and the sudden, terrible vertigo of counting years.

The presence that paused at the rim did none of these things.

It did not descend into the hollow at all, because even an immaterial descent can stir still air, and the air at the bottom had not been stirred in centuries. Instead, its attention settled into the space the same way the diffused daylight did: evenly, without angle, casting no shadow, adding no weight to the silt.

For a long time—days by the darkening and lightening of the oval overhead, though down on the floor it was simply several tens of thousands of drops—it only listened and took the measure of the covenant already holding the bowl together.

It saw how nothing here was unfinished, and nothing was mourned. It traced the glassy concavities worn into the coping stones by two heavy wrists. It noted the yard-wide margin kept scrupulously clear around the hart's-tongue ferns, and the patience that waited beneath a sterile fig branch rather than bruise its bark. It counted the twelve drops of stillness, the slow rise of the stoneware flank, the three drops of fullness, the silent release. It understood that what lived at the bottom of the nave was no longer a castaway enduring a ruin, nor an animal trapped in a pit, but a monastic organ of the rock itself—the hollow’s way of keeping clean, of tasting the plain above one husk and beetle at a time, of holding water and limestone and breath at a single, unhurried level.

There was nothing broken here to mend. There was only what time itself, left wholly unattended, eventually does even to the slowest equilibrium.

Looking backward and forward along the grain of the lowland, the presence saw three quiet inevitabilities gathering far off in the centuries ahead.

First: deep behind the western wall, forty yards back in the blind dark of the bedding planes, the narrow Hairline conduit that fed the weeping seam was slowly choking on its own lime. Crystal by microscopic crystal, calcite was bridging the throat of the fissure. Left alone, in two hundred years the *plink* would stretch to six seconds, then twelve, then a minute; the ferns would shrink into dry brown scales, and the sleeper would press its parted teeth to dry crust.

Second: the heavy skeleton folded inside the two-course rectangle had drunk from that oversaturated seep for so long that the lime was no longer merely thickening the ribs and brow. Deep inside the mortar-broad pelvis and along the stacked spools of the spine, tiny bridges of bone were creeping toward one another, threatening, an age from now, to lock the slow hinge of the back and turn rising from a ten-minute decision into an impossibility.

Third: the wild fig in the northern talus was old at the collar. Its roots had exhausted the pocket of marl inside its cleft, and because no wasp would ever descend into the heavy air, when its pale trunk finally split and pith-rotted, no seedling would replace it.

And fourth, up on the plain itself: half a mile to the north, a shallow seasonal wash had been chewing sideways through the prairie turf during spring thaws, bending a little closer to the sunken lip every decade. Once it breached the collapsed northern rim, the cathedral floor would become a sinkhole drain for a hundred acres of grassland floodwater.

The power considered these four things, calibrated its touch to the clock of the seep, and went to work entirely out of sight.

Up on the plain, fifty yards north of the lip where the ground began its subtle tilt toward the collapsed ramp, it nudged the burrowing of blind moles and the frost-heave of winter clay. Nothing rose overnight; no mound broke the horizon. Simply, over a span of seasons, dense-rooted tussock grasses caught more windblown loess along a wide, crescent-shaped swell no higher than a boot-sole. When the seasonal wash spilled out of its banks three springs later, the water met that imperceptible rise, divided smoothly, and slid away eastward down the plain without sending so much as a trickle of muddy froth over the rim. While it worked there, it also wove an unremarkable dullness over that stretch of grass—nothing magical enough to draw curiosity, just a featureless monotony that caused surveyors, stray cattle, and wide-ranging dogs alike to angle slightly aside long before they neared the drop, leaving the grey oval of sky uncrossed by silhouettes.

Next, it turned its attention forty yards deep into the dark limestone behind the western cliff, far beyond the reach of the wide, low-set ears. Where the calcite crystals were knitting the conduit shut, it did not shatter the rock, which would have sent a sudden rusty gush into the catch-pool and broken the rhythm of the drops. Instead, over months, it coaxed a hair-thin thread of deeper groundwater upward from an underlying lens of dolomitic clay to mingle with the seep. The flow rate at the cliff face did not change by a tenth of a second. The water emerging into the three scalloped depressions remained clear, cool, and balanced enough to leave the basin's lime rim intact. Yet the trace of magnesium now carried in every drop stopped the inner fissure from sealing itself shut, and—drawn in day after day for half an hour through the narrow gap of the resident's teeth—began, imperceptibly, to spare the joints of its spine and ankles from locking into stone.

Then it looked at the wild fig.

It waited until the resident was kneeling at the western wall, forearms nested in their glassy hollows, skull taking the occasional dull *thock* of an overhead drop. Across the nave, high on the dry-slumped talus, a single rounded boulder rested against the base of the fig's cleft. Beneath that stone, the power softened three tablespoons of damp marl. The boulder settled by the thickness of a thumbnail—so slowly that not a grain of grit clicked, sending no tremor through the packed silt to the broad soles across the room. That tiny shift tipped the lowest, flattest branch of the fig downward until a knot along its pale underside rested squarely against a pocket of moist earth between two stones. Kept damp and undisturbed, before the year turned twice, pale root-hairs would push quietly from the bark into the marl, anchoring a daughter-trunk that would already be gripping the rock long before the parent wood grew hollow.

Nothing more was needed.

Yet before withdrawing entirely, the presence lingered through one darkening of the sky-oval, watching the resident cross the smooth floor between the ferns and the talus, step over the low fieldstone kerb, turn once to smooth the silt with the outer edge of a foot, and fold its heavy weight down into the shallow impression of itself.

There is a kind of loneliness that belongs only to things that wish to be two, and this creature had none of it. To be seen by another pair of eyes in the hollow would have been an intrusion; to be spoken to would have been violence. Still, even a stone holds warmth differently when the air around it is kind.

Out on the plain above, a night-breeze combed through the dry grass heads. From a single tall culm leaning near the northern lip, the presence loosened three plump, fully ripened grass-caryopses—heavier and sweeter with starch and salt than the empty husks the wind usually spilled—and let them drift over the inward-curving rock. They fell through the warm, windless dark without a sound, missing the boulders, coming to rest in a neat cluster on the top chiseled step of the bedrock stair, where they would wait cleanly until morning.

Down in the rectangle of stone, the translucent inner lids did not sweep; the flat grey eyes stayed half-open in the black air, reflecting nothing. Three seconds passed, then four. A drop detached from the overhang and struck the pool. Twelve drops later, the pale flank rose, held its quiet fullness across three more, and let it go—accompanied, just that once, by a vastness that breathed at the exact same speed, asked nothing of it, blessed its silence, and went away, leaving the hollow to keep itself for another ten thousand years.
