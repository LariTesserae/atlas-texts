A lesser mercy would crack the roof. 

A lesser wisdom, looking in from the blinding blue heights of the granite spine, would see a prison: the coffined hand’s-breadth of sour air, the smooth-knitted orbits where eyes used to be, the gentle, necessary drownings in the fever-warm court below. A lesser god would feel pity—which is so often just a failure to translate another’s grammar—and let the sun in. And the sun would scald the violet grease to grey ash, bloom green violence across the blind masonry, speed the heartbeats of the folded ones until they starved in a week, and ruin a ten-thousand-year truce between flesh and stone.

The power that finds this place does not enter like a crowbar or a beam of light. It enters the way the mountain’s own memory does: along the crystal lattices of the granite, down through the weeping fractures, quiet as thermal conduction.

For a long time—years, even by the reckoning of the peaks outside, where glaciers calve and lichens eat an inch of rock a century—it simply listens. 

It rests its attention in the warm broth like one of the pale, four-fingered hands resting on a lintel. It reads the cistern on the cistern’s own terms. It feels the exquisite economy of the two-beat heart. It notes the saddle-shaped groove worn into the basalt porthole by four hundred generations of blind palms holding steady in the plume, and recognizes that groove for what it is: home, architecture, inheritance. It understands that these slow, dough-pale sovereigns are not fallen salamanders or ruined beasts waiting to be rescued back into quadrupeds. They have arrived. They fit their world as cleanly as a tongue fits a mouth.

And yet, because this power sees deeply into the root-plumbing of the dormant volcano and reads the long, patient math of limestone and heat, it also sees the two clocks that the blind palms cannot read.

The first clock is at the ceiling. 
Every century, the creamy stalactites grow a hair’s breadth down from the corbeled slabs, and the mineral sinter swells upward on the capitals. And more urgently than the stone: the ancient landslide choking the outflow drains is still settling, still compacting its clays into impermeable slate. In eight hundred years—only four lifetimes for the great, worm-ruffed matriarch—the water will rise that final hand’s breadth. The stagnant, metallic bubble under the roof will be pressed flat into nothing, forced out into microscopic rock-pores where no wide, doughy snout can follow. They have gills only in rumor, skin-folds good for waiting an hour, not forever. When the ceiling wets completely, the slow, liquid *whuff* will have nowhere to travel, and the sovereigns will hold each other down without meaning to.

The second clock is deeper. 
Down in the magma throat three miles below the baffled shafts, the old hearth is cooling toward sleep, its rising veins shifting slowly eastward inside the mountain’s spine. Left alone, in three thousand years the fever-warm court will drop to tepid, then to chill, and the sulfur will thin until the violet tallow starves, and the abbey will become merely a tomb of clear, cold water full of clean white shells.

So the wise power decides to act—not as a savior, and never as an interruption, but as a mason’s apprentice working on geological time.

It begins outside the walls, forty feet away inside the dark heart of the mountain, where the ancient builders’ main outflow tunnel lies crushed under ten thousand tons of fallen tuff and mineralized clay. 

It does not clear the tunnel. Clearing the tunnel would drop the water thirty feet in a day and leave the weightless, uncalcified elders crushed under the sudden, monstrous hammer of their own gravity on dry benches. 

Instead, precisely five inches below the current waterline—right at the lower lip of the greasy bacterial skin—the power coaxes a single hairline fracture in the retaining wall. It asks three quartz grains to slip sideways; it invites a weakly acidic thread of groundwater to kiss a seam of softer carbonate. 

Nothing gushes. Nothing even drips. A new weep begins, calibrated to the exact volume of the mountain’s deep inflow. The rising tide, which has been creeping upward at a millimeter a decade, simply stops. The hand’s breadth of air beneath the ceiling slabs is pinned in place forever—and more than pinned: twenty yards away, through a tortuous, dry capillary in the peak’s frost-shattered summit, a microscopic chimney opens no wider than a quill. No wind blows through it. No freezing draft touches the water. Only partial pressures equalize, molecule by lazy molecule, trading a whisper of stagnant carbon dioxide for a whisper of thin alpine oxygen once every few days, sweetening the ceiling-bubble by half a percent—not enough to wake them from their slow dream, just enough that an elder’s two-hundredth year comes a little less stiffly to the lungs.

Then the power turns its attention downward, and sideways.

Within the same volcanic spine, separated from the drowned abbey by ninety yards of solid, unquarried basalt, there is a natural cavern the ancient builders never knew existed. They stopped digging when they hit dense rock, never guessing that behind their eastern retaining wall lay a high, narrow fault-fissure, half-flooded with cold, sterile snowmelt, dead and dark as the inside of an iron bell.

The power goes down three miles to the shifting magma plumbing. With the touch of a finger on a cooling dike of gabbro, it leans gently against an embryonic stress-line, encouraging the migrating heat not away from the abbey, but slightly upward into the root of that neighboring, sterile fault-fissure—while keeping the old branch beneath the sunken court open and steady.

This takes fifty years. Above, in the drowned hall, the matriarch shed her skin-slime fifty times; one juvenile is permitted to hatch from a melon-sized egg wedged in a flute; a million plum-sized snails live and are lipped gently from pleated flanks and folded back into calcium. None of them suspect anything is changing.

Behind the eastern wall, in the secret fissure, the dead snowmelt slowly warms. Over decades, it rises from ice-melt to cool, from cool to tepid, from tepid to the exact temperature of blood and steeped broth. Sulfur seeps up from the newly nudged vein below, clouding the clear water with pale mineral milk.

Still, the two chambers do not touch. If the wall breached all at once, the pressure wave would strike those turquoise-stippled palms like a thunderclap, and the raw chemistry of unseasoned rock might sour the ancient soup.

So the power remembers how the builders worked, and how the folded creatures move.

Down at mid-water behind a crowded colonnade, where an old, blind sluice-niche ends against solid mountain rock, the masonry has already dissolved halfway back into raw basalt. Here, over the next thirty years, the stone softens along an old cooling joint. Grain by grain, dissolved by localized pockets of carbonic warmth, a passage opens through the ninety yards of intervening rock.

It is not a grand archway. It is narrow, crooked, and vertical—barely the width of a bucket, ribbed with smooth, rounded swells like knuckles and loaves, baffled with three slow dog-legs so no current can rush between the worlds. 

First, the water mixes at a crawl: a pint an hour, then a gallon. The greasy violet tallow creeps along the new ceiling, feeding on the fresh hydrogen sulfide waiting on the other side, stringing its dim, bruised-plum luminescence ahead of itself like a miner’s fuse taking ten years to burn through the mountain. After the bacteria go the drifting, ash-like crustaceans, twitching once every five seconds into strange, empty water. After the crustaceans go the parchment tube-worms, anchoring their grey quills in virgin cracks that have never known a chisel. After the worms go the blind, plum-sized gastropods, laying down a velvet road of mucus and rasping jaws—*scritch-scritch-scritch*—spreading the acoustic map of the world outward into what used to be silent stone.

And then one day—or night, though there is neither—the younger one, no longer smooth as lard now, carrying the first faint mineral warts of middle age along its spine, hangs among the crowded sleepers of the eastern colonnade.

Its four long, white limbs are stretched wide in the charcoal gloom. Its pore-stippled palms are listening.

And from the blind sluice-niche behind it, where for ten thousand years the sound of rasping snail-jaws has ended in a flat, dead wall of silence, there is a new geometry. 

The dry crackle of chitinous teeth does not stop at the back of the niche anymore. It bends left. It goes *in*. And riding back along that faint, warm exhale of water comes a tang of fresh, rich sulfur, unchewed limestone, and a subtle, vast hollowness—the acoustic signature of high, vertical stone where no echoes bounce because the snails are already carpeting it three layers deep, untouched, unharvested.

The creature waits six hours before moving. Why hurry? The stone is not going anywhere.

At last, it exhales two silver beads of hoarded air toward the ceiling, lets go of the ancient pillars, and pulls itself hand-over-hand into the blind niche. Where the builders’ mortised blocks end, its broad, doughy snout touches the smooth lip of the narrow fissure. 

It folds its cartilaginous ribcage inward with a soft, rubbery creak, compressing its barrel chest the way it has always done for choked sluice-channels, and slips into the seam between the old world and the new.

Ninety yards takes all afternoon. Along the way, its trailing fingers brush unfamiliar stone—not dressed by mauls, not squared and melted, just raw, pillowed volcanic ribs rubbed smooth by patient chemistry—yet shaped so kindly to the span of a four-fingered hand that it feels like memory. Halfway through, an erratic spark of pale ochre winks inside a stressed feldspar crystal, lighting its translucent belly for half a second, and goes out.

Then the seam opens.

Its outstretched left palm reaches forward expecting a wall, and finds open, blood-warm water. 

Slowly, weightlessly, the pale body drifts out into the fault-fissure. It anchors its hind feet against the lip of the doorway and stretches its long arms out into the dark, listening. 

The new chamber rises forty feet above and drops forty below—narrow, folded, rich with ledges and overhanging shelves of natural basalt, thick with the twitching ash of crustaceans that have multiplied unchecked for twenty years, upholstered with heavy, leathery drifters sleeping in virgin niches. And high above, trapped beneath an unbroken vault of native granite, sits a hand’s breadth of warm, breathable air, glowing faintly violet along its rim.

The creature hangs at the threshold for a day, its heart beating three times a minute, reading every contour of the annex through the turquoise-stained pores of its throat and palms. 

When at last it rises to break the greasy surface of this new sky, its nostrils part with the faintest wet *clack*. It draws in air that has never been breathed before, holds it, and lets out a low, hollow, liquid *whuff*.

The sound travels along the narrow vault, slips back through the crooked, ninety-yard seam in the rock, and emerges faint as a rumor beneath the corbeled slabs of the ancient abbey—where, three walls away, the great worm-ruffed matriarch pauses with her palm in the saddle-worn porthole, tilts her scarred, eyeless snout toward the eastern colonnade, and reads the sudden, quiet widening of the law.

Soon there can be four, and five, and seven. 

And the wise power, having signed nothing, having broken nothing, withdraws its touch from the mountain’s deep stone—leaving behind no monument to itself, unless you count that fifty years later, at the rough basalt lip where the narrow seam opens into the new cavern, a shallow, saddle-shaped groove has already begun to polish smooth under the gentle, patient grip of blind hands.
