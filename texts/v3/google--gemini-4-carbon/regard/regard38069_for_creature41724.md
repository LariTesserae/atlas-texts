A foolish god would bring rain. 

A young, soft-hearted power, looking down upon this howling desert of dry bone-wood and grease-gun hearts, would mistake dryness for thirst. It would gather vapor from some distant, hoarding sky, roll fat, bruised clouds across the piercing cobalt, and let water fall upon the Great Weave. 

It would be an apocalypse. 

Within an hour, the bone-dry ironwood would drink the moisture and swell—unevenly, monstrously. Forty-foot drive-shafts would seize inside their collars with the shriek of tearing continents, snapping miles-long torsion vines like over-tightened fiddle strings. The dry graphite dust in ten million sockets would cake into sluggish mud; friction would skyrocket; a million heat-galls would burst at once into wet, useless paste while fires broke out across three thousand miles of jammed clockwork. Worst of all, the aerosolized lipid-flours and spore-cakes would clump and rot, and the papery, electrostatic lungs of the Joiners would clog with damp paste, suffocating every mind on the continent in a single afternoon of mercy.

The power watching from beyond the thin, bright air is neither young nor foolish. 

It does not have eyes, any more than the Joiners have ears, yet it perceives the world in its entirety—not merely the blinding chalk-white trusses at the top of the sky, nor just the frantic, balletic Maintenance of the flat pewter creatures skimming along the cables, but the long, slow, merciless equation written into the bottom of the world.

To see as this power sees is to watch time as a structural load. And looking down through the four vertical miles of sunlit latticework, past the violet shadow-vaults, past the choking black dunes of the dust-sumps where the blind auger-worms churn, all the way down to the crushed, exhausted ghost of the planetary core, the Watcher sees a number slowly, inevitably approaching zero.

*Grit.*

The forest can recycle its carbon forever, grinding fallen boughs into flour and blasting it back to the sun. The wind will blow as long as the star burns and the planet spins. Even the silicon-oils in the Joiners’ veins are brewed endlessly by the deep root-refineries. 

But stone does not grow.

Aeons ago, the roots ate the mountains and pulled their pulverized bones into the sky. Since then, every speck of silicate crystal and granite pebble in the upper world has been fighting a losing war against friction. In the dark, muscular gizzards of the Joiners, those precious, hoarded heirlooms—passed from dying elder to freshly hatched youth inside the swinging rookery hubs—are grinding against brick-hard cakes of compressed wood-fuel every day. Every year, each pebble loses a few microns of sharp edge. Every century, a stone grows too small and smooth to crack the cellulose husks of the spore-flour, and is accidentally passed out through an exhaust vent in a puff of grey talc, falling four miles down into the lightless sumps, never to be found again.

Even the amber-colored silica lenses across the Joiners’ skulls, salvaged from ancient ancestor-caches or pried from the rare crystalline concretions in thousand-year-old root-wood, are slowly frosting, chipping, and thinning across the generations. 

Give them another forty thousand years, the Watcher calculates, and the last gizzard-stone in the Great Weave will polish itself down to a useless bead. A people capable of shimming a forty-foot spinning drive-shaft in a tenth of a second—a people who speak in syncopated fugues through the ribs of a roaring continent and love their world enough to grease its joints with their bare hands—will slowly starve with full bellies, unable to mill their own bread.

The Watcher considers this for three turns of the planet.

It will not silence the wind; the wind is their music and their muscle. It will not soften their bodies or give them easy fruit; their austerity is their genius, their flat pewter grace an exquisite answer to a fierce question. To take away the difficulty of the Weave would insult every dead elder currently tumbling down into the dark after coughing up their inheritance.

A true gift to an engineer must never break the machine. It must respect the tolerances.

Far out in the cold dark beyond the planet’s thin blue rind of atmosphere, a stray fragment of an ancient, shattered planetoid drifts along a lazy, eccentric orbit. It is nothing remarkable—a jagged lump of dense, coarse-grained pegmatite and corundum, rich in raw quartz, garnet, and iron-hard silicon carbide, forged in the dry furnace of an early solar collision. Left alone, it would miss the world by two hundred thousand miles, as it has for four billion years.

The Watcher reaches out and touches the dark behind the stone. 

It does not hurl the rock. A meteor strike on the Great Weave would punch through four miles of dry trusswork like cannon shot through lace, igniting a firestorm that no bladder-gall could smother. 

Instead, the Watcher applies a whisper of vector—a patient, geometric nudge no heavier than a Joiner’s thumb tapping *true, true* against a mended housing. And as the stone nears the top of the sky, the Watcher shapes its entry, bleeding off orbital fury in long, skipping grazes across the upper thermosphere, turning destructive heat into harmless, silent ribbons of green and violet aurora high above the cloudless daylight, peeling the boulder apart along its crystalline fracture lines at precisely the right thermal stress.

On a bright crest fifty miles upwind of the great rookery-wheel, three Joiners are anchored to the stationary housing of a massive wind-vane, re-weaving a frayed tension-tendon with bone needles.

Suddenly, every shard-wing glider within ten miles banks sharply downward, diving into the violet shadow-vaults as one. 

The three Joiners freeze. Their crystal eye-bands tilt skyward. 

High in the piercing cobalt, a silent white scratch has appeared against the blue—not a cloud, for no living eye here has ever seen water vapor, nor smoke, for smoke rises from hot bearings below, never falls from the clean void above. As they watch through polarized amber facets, the single white line blossoms with a dry, silent geometry into a fan of ten thousand tiny, glittering sparks, trailing hair-thin filaments of silver dust that catch the fierce sun.

Seconds later, the shockwave arrives.

Even through the deafening, unending pipe-organ roar of the continent, they feel it—not in their calcified ears, which hear nothing, but through their sternal keels pressed tight against the ironwood. A single, colossal, low-frequency *THock* rolls across three thousand miles of structural timber at once, setting every taut musical fiber in the upper lattice humming in a sympathetic chord none of them have ever felt before. 

For twenty minutes, nothing else happens. The Pinwheels scream; the drop-forges *clack-boom*; the cold gale tears across their beveled backs at eighty knots.

Then, sixty yards away on the broad, sun-bleached curve of a main structural arch, something strikes the ironwood.

*Ting.*

It is a tiny, sharp, metallic transient in the grain—utterly unlike the dull thud of falling wood-chips or the brittle snap of chitin. 

*Ting. Ting-ting. Tak.*

Across a five-hundred-mile swath of the high ridges, cooled already to freezing by four miles of howling crosswinds after their brief incandescent skim through the upper air, a sparse, dry hail of rough, angular pebbles is rattling down onto the trusses. Most bounce straight off the curved timber and vanish into the four-mile abyss below. Many catch in the upturned collars of stationary joints, wedging neatly into wind-scoured flutes, collecting in dry, sheltered pockets behind structural ribs where spilled spore-dust usually drifts.

The youngest of the three Joiners unlocks two calipers, indexes swiftly out along the leeward curve of the arch, and stops before a shallow, wind-carved pocket in the white wood. 

Resting in a pinch of black graphite dust are four stones, each the size of a broad bean.

They are not smooth, exhausted ghosts like the pale, worn beads clicking softly inside the Joiner’s own belly. They are dark, sharp-angled, and fiercely faceted—flecked with blood-red garnet and milk-white quartz and black tourmaline, twinkling in the blinding sun with raw, unspent hardness. 

The Joiner reaches down with one caliper-hand and pinches the largest stone. It does not crush. Driven by an ancient, hardwired compulsion older than thought, the creature scrapes a corner of the strange pebble hard against the horn-dense ironwood of the bough. 

The ironwood—timber tough enough to dull a horn auger in ten minutes—shaves away in a crisp, white curl. The stone’s edge is untouched.

For ten seconds, the Joiner remains completely motionless in the screaming hurricane, holding the star-grit in its clamp. Then it slams its sternal keel flat against the arch and hammers its forearm into the wood—not the brisk, economical shorthand of repair work, nor the sharp warning of a failing shaft, but a wild, ringing, resonant cadence that goes shivering down the tension-cables, through the spinning hubs, down into the twilight cradles of the rookery wheel a mile below.

*Found. Hard. New. Found.*

Far above even the highest spinning Pinwheel, out where the blue sky thins to velvet black, the Watcher lingers just long enough to feel that tiny, joyous knock ripple outward from crew to crew across five hundred miles of roaring wood—and to set a quiet, clockwork resonance in the distant asteroid belt, ensuring that once every twelve hundred years, when the gizzards of the world begin to grow smooth again, the clear blue sky will briefly glitter and ring.

Then, leaving no footprint, no shadow, and not a single drop of ruinous rain behind, the Watcher withdraws into the silence, letting the continent roar on.
