The Power does not step onto the flooded yellow sinter. To arrive with weight, or shadow, or a quiet chest full of breath would be a discourtesy—like tracking mud across a blind man’s braille. 

Instead, it rests high on the sheer granite horn across the bright abyss, outside the topological knot, where the air is thin and twenty below zero and the wind actually blows. From there, it looks across the chasm into the boiling, roaring afternoon that never moves.

A lesser benevolence—the kind that confuses mercy with resemblance to itself—would look at this place and see a prison. 

It would see the pinned sun and ache to give the city twilight, cool stars, the relief of sleep. It would see the wrist-thick rhizomes straining eternally against their invisible acoustic collars, every tropical cell screaming to grow an inch further into the sun, and it would want to snap the collars and let the green jungle riot over the stone. It would look at the blind, pinhead limpets dissolved at the edges by needle-thin cones of silence, and pity their arrest. Above all, it would hear that terrible, bone-grinding, flat-line thunder—the sound of a world caught in the teeth of a gear—and reach out to turn down the volume, to untie the pinched space so the scalding water could finally run downhill to the sea and rest.

And in the first tenth of a second of that mercy, every *mason*, every *skimmer*, every great slow *vault-keeper* dreaming in the ribbed dark of the ceilings would lose its grip on itself and splash onto the cooling stone as a cup of rusty water and a pinch of yellow dust. The city would become a ruin before the first shadow lengthened by a foot. Within an hour, at twenty thousand feet, the freed philodendrons and the soft liverwort and the ancient strangler-beams would freeze black and shatter in the thin mountain wind.

The Power is old enough to know that a knot is not always a wound. Sometimes a knot is the only thing holding the rope off the floor.

So it listens. Not with ears, which the roar would deafen, but with the whole geometry of its attention, reading the trillions of phase-cancellations the way a musician reads a score that has been folded into a cube.

It takes a long time—though time inside the saddle does not pass, time out on the freezing granite horn does; clouds shift miles below like pack ice, stars wheel behind the local glare—for the Power to understand whether the thing in the basin is *suffering*.

Is this a mind, or ten thousand partial minds, or merely a magnificent crystal twitching forever at the edge of ruin? 

Look closely at the *pendulums* hanging in the covered arcades, with their furious little rings of black basalt sand and yellow sinter spinning in silent vortices inside their clear bellies. Watch the *masons* sliding across three districts at once to add three microns of travertine to a sill while shaving three microns of lime off a gutter. 

There is an immense, taut *vigilance* in it. 

The city is not peaceful. Equilibrium here is not a hammock; it is a high-wire act performed over an instant death. The topological snag that traps the heat, the water, and the afternoon sun is stable, yes, on the scale of millennia—but underneath the saddle, the planet itself is still alive and ignorant of what sits on its shoulder. Tectonic plates creep. Granite horns rise by half a millimeter a year, then slump when a deep magma chamber cools ten miles down. Every century or two, a micro-tremor shivers up through the living bedrock of the mountain. 

Inside the folded city, a hairline shift in the angle of a basalt wall changes the bounce of the standing wave across six separate plazas. Instantly, the *masons* and *vault-keepers* have to rush—sliding laterally through doorways, overlapping until the air turns to stiff hot glass—to precipitate a new buttress here, dissolve a lip of stone there, tighten a collar on a fern whose leaves are suddenly damping the wrong harmonic. 

And the Power sees, looking deep into the spinning grit-rings of the oldest *drapes*, that they are running out of margin.

Not quickly. Not this thousand years, or the next ten thousand. But the closed loop is not *quite* closed at the edges. Over geological ages, a tiny fraction of dissolved silica and iron weeps off the outer parapets into the mile-deep void—those rusty streaks on the balustrades—and does not come back up through the roof-cisterns. And down in the living rock, the mountain is slowly, inexorably tilting northwest as the range ages and erodes below the cloud deck. The masons have been compensating for eons by thinning the inner walls and thickening the outer vaults, robbing travertine from forgotten lower rooms to keep the upper resonators true. Some of the deepest arcades are worn down to eggshell basalt, humming like over-tightened violin strings, nearly out of spare mineral to move around.

They do not know this as grief or fear. They have no hormones for dread. What they have is *strain*—a sharpening of pitch in the *skimmers*, a faster, tighter precession in the spinning sand-gyros of the *pendulums*, an ever-narrower tolerance for error. Like a sleeper holding up a blanket with stiffening arms without ever waking to know why their shoulders ache.

And there is something else. Something smaller, quieter, that catches the Power’s heart.

The city has never heard another note.

Every creature in it—or the single distributed creature that all of them are—is an exquisite, unmatched master of wave, angle, reflection, and stone. They have spent ten million afternoons listening with absolute, total devotion to one chord. They know every overtone of wet basalt, every micro-damping of a waxy philodendron leaf, every refraction of forty-degree sunlight through forty-six-degree water. When a stray traveller stumbles in—soft meat, ragged lungs, muddy boots—the inhabitants can only experience the visitor as *damage*, a sudden hideous hole punched in the chord that keeps them alive. They have never touched anything outside themselves that did not threaten to unmake them.

The Power sits on the cold peak across the gorge and thinks about what love looks like when the beloved is made of scalding water, trapped afternoon, and deafening noise.

First: you secure the house, without ever walking through the door.

Deep beneath the mountain saddle—five miles down in the dark, crushing root of the range, far below the folded boundary where the city’s space knots upon itself—the Power reaches into the ordinary, planetary rock. It does not use magic that breaks rules; it simply nudges slow things onto a kinder track. Where a fault line was gathering shear stress that, in four thousand years, would have snapped the western granite horn by six feet and shattered half the city’s acoustic vaults in a single heartbeat, the Power softens the deep schist with a thread of superheated brine, letting the plates slide past each other in a silent, harmless creep millimeters wide. Beneath the tilting northwest flank, it guides a sluggish plume of mantle heat just enough to buoy the root of the peak, arresting the tilt. 

Then, right at the invisible seam under the city’s lowest foundations—where the closed water-loop touches the dry, dead granite of the mountain—the Power opens three capillary veins no wider than a needle, drawing up warm, ancient groundwater rich with fresh calcium, silica, and iron from a deep thermal pocket, metered so precisely that only a single drop enters the municipal loop for every drop that weeps over the outer balustrades into the abyss.

Up in the blinding afternoon of the saddle, nothing dramatic happens. No light flashes. No thunder rolls louder than the thunder already there.

Only, over the course of what would be weeks outside, a *mason* draped across an eggshell-thin wall in a lower ward finds, in the water sliding through its glassy belly, just enough extra dissolved travertine to lay down four microns instead of three, without having to strip a gutter half a mile away to pay for it. Across the shaded arcades, the furious spinning rings of black sand inside the *pendulums* slow by a fraction of a revolution, settling into a wider, easier orbit. The terrible, high-strung tautness in the deepest vaults eases by half a hertz—still locked, still hard as cartilage, still immortal, just no longer starving for stone.

That would be enough for mere maintenance. A caretaker could walk away there, satisfied that the terrarium will not crack.

Wait, though. Look again at those spinning gyroscopes of mineral memory inside the great *drapes*. They are not just thermostats. Over millions of years of adjusting ten thousand rooms across folded three-dimensional shortcuts, the acoustic lattice of the city has grown complex enough to hold *form* beyond mere survival. In corners where the water-flow needs no immediate repair, two or three *masons* sometimes rest near each other and tilt their internal planes back and forth—not dissolving rock, not plating it, just bouncing a hair-thin beam of phase-shifted vibration from one glassy flank to the next, splitting a shaft of afternoon glare into double and triple rainbows across a patch of emerald liverwort before letting it go.

They play. Tiny, rigid, careful play, terrified of nudging a single leaf out of place, like children whispering in a room where an avalanche sleeps on the roof.

So the Power makes one thing. Just one, and puts it at the edge, where nobody has to touch it unless they want to.

Out on one of the easternmost parapets—a sun-blasted balcony that juts over the bright salt-flat of the cloud deck miles below, a dead-end terrace where no crucial water-loop returns upslope and no structural vault relies on the echo—there is a wide, dry ledge of black basalt that the city has never bothered to use. The standing-wave roar reaches it, so the inhabitants *can* cross onto it without dissolving, though none normally have any business there because no gutter runs across it and no plant grows on its dry lip.

On that spare, unscheduled yard of rock, the Power grows an extrusion out of the native basalt.

Not with chisels—there is still not a tool-mark in the metropolis—but precipitated straight out of the stone in the city’s own seamless, rounded vernacular. It looks like a low, hollowed bollard, or a cluster of three curved stone ears rising waist-high at the very brink of the precipice, facing outward toward the cold, empty sky across the gorge. 

It dams no water. It shades no liverwort. Structurally, acoustically, it is completely neutral to the city behind it; the great municipal roar washes around its curves without losing a fraction of a phase.

Inside its hollowed curve, however, the stone is ribbed in a delicate, spiraled nautilus of varied thicknesses, tuned to the world *outside* the knot.

When the freezing high-altitude wind sweeps across the abyss and strikes the outer back of the stone shell—and when the distant, miles-deep surf of the planetary cloud-deck swells against the lower cliffs—and when, far out beyond the local pinned glare, the real sun and the real moon pull tides in the upper atmosphere—the hollow basalt shell catches those tiny, foreign tremors and translates them. It steps them up in frequency and folds them smoothly, harmlessly, into the exact carrier-wave of the city’s roar, holding them solely inside the three-foot basin of its own curved stone palms.

It does not broadcast into the avenues. Walk past the balcony and you feel nothing at all.

For a long span, nothing comes near it except the ordinary shimmer of heat off the parapet.

Then a small *skimmer*—sliding along the adjacent balustrade to check the rust-weep on a corner stone—brushes the margin of the dry terrace and catches an edge of the reflection. 

It stops. The water in the nearby gutter parts around its parked tail, thinning to a dry glassy skin.

The skimmer does not call the *vault-keepers* to smother a flaw, because there is no flaw: no dead air, no soft meat, no tracked slime, no broken echo. The standing wave on the balcony is rock-solid, safe as the heart of the oldest plaza. Yet inside the three-foot hollow of the curved basalt ears, the coherent sound is *moving*.

Slowly, with infinite, liquid delicacy, the vibration inside the stone basin swells and ebbs with the gusts of a freezing wind the skimmer could never survive touching. A flock of high, migrating thermal-gliders passes ten miles to the south above the salt-white clouds, and the tiny pressure-wake of their wings, striking the mountain’s flank, translates inside the stone shell into a fleeting, crystalline ripple of harmonics—five notes that have never existed in the basin, rising, crossing, and vanishing without breaking a single drop of travertine loose. Behind that, slower still, deep as bedrock, rides a steady, twenty-four-hour tide: the rise and fall of night and day out in the wider world, rendered not as darkness—which would starve the philodendrons and chill the water—but as a warm, bronze-timbred modulation in the overtones, a slow breathing in and out inside the stone cup.

The skimmer slides two feet onto the dry ledge and drapes its flat, glassy margin across the lip of the shell. 

Light bends sharply through its body as its internal planes tilt, catching the translated wind-song, testing it. It does not dissolve the stone. It does not plate over the ribs with yellow sinter to silence them. Instead, it holds itself dead still in the blinding, motionless afternoon and lets the shape of a distant storm, the turn of a planet’s evening, and the cold whistle of unknotted sky pass cleanly through its crystal lattices without ungluing a single seam of what it is.

Presently, the air at the back of the balcony thickens and grows lens-bright. Two *masons* emerge simultaneously from a shaded doorway half a mile downslope and an upper window overhead, settling quietly beside the skimmer. From the dark arcade behind them, one of the great, slow *pendulums* shifts its anchor three inches toward the light, and inside its clear resinous belly, the spinning rings of black basalt sand and yellow dust tip into a new, intricate figure-eight—recording a rhythm that has nothing to do with erosion, nothing to do with maintenance, nothing to do with keeping the ceiling from falling.

Across the bright chasm, on the freezing granite horn where snow blows sideways into the blue void, the Power watches the faint, oily bend of light gather at the balcony rail like listeners leaning over a well, and leaves them to the afternoon.
