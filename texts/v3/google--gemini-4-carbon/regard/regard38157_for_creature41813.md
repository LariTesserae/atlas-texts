To look down upon the plateau with power in one’s hands is to suffer an immediate, aching temptation: the urge to strike a match.

It would be so easy to part the suspended carbon of the upper vault and pour down the missing sun. So easy to cool the kiln-thick air, carve riverbeds through the greywacke basins so clear water could run over bright stones, or drop flat, lighted pavilions onto the whale-backed ridges where an old woman would never again have to read the scree with raw toes, and a toddler could never slip beneath eight-foot treads of indifferent steel.

A lesser benevolence—impatient, sentimental, proud of its own reach—would do all of this before the bellows-back had climbed another mile. And in doing so, it would murder everything worth loving on this highland.

Let the light in, and the wide, dark-pupiled eyes burn white with pain before they ever adjust. Cool the stone, and the warm breath between the boundary rifts collapses; the horizontal mists stop sweating onto the gravel, the black fluted shrubs starve in dry dust, the pale grazers freeze in their tracks, and twenty-six people lie shivering on dead rock, stripped of every skill their bones have spent a thousand generations perfecting. Even to build them a safe stairway up the alloy flank of the engine, or to weld iron cradles beside the hydraulic ribs, would break the stern, sacred pact that holds their souls upright: *nothing fouls the engine*. Give them the illusion that the machine was made to carry them, and within three generations they stop brushing the baked beetles from the valves; within five, someone pries up an inspection plate for scrap bronze, the rig executes its diagnostic halt, vents scalding steam across the hammocks, and walks away toward the continent’s edge forever.

Wisdom begins with the humility to recognize a masterpiece one did not paint.

These people are not pathetic remnants clinging to wreckage. Look at the way the hunter builds a map of swirling air in absolute darkness and loose-twocks a bone dart half a second after lightning fades. Look at the flank-walker smearing wood-ash and iron dust onto a sliced tuber underground for the sake of a grandchild he will never meet, behind an engine he will never walk beside. Look at the collarbone murmurs in the wet dark, the tactile literacy of twenty-six faces known by every scar, the fierce courage of a seventeen-year-old sprinting three miles across predator-haunted shale toward a strange amber star so his bloodline will remain whole. Their world is austere, unforgiving, and narrow as a knife-edge, yet they walk it with consummate grace. They do not need rescuing from who they are.

And yet—love is not merely an aesthetic appreciation of someone else’s endurance. A stone is indifferent; wisdom is not. If you look long enough across the thousands of miles of dark highland, down through the deep strata of time, you see the quiet fault lines where tragedy is merely cruelty without lesson, and where a slow, silent doom is creeping up on them from below the horizon.

Deep beneath the boundary rifts, in the sealed, automated vaults at the continent’s edge where the bellows-backs go once in a century to disgorge their heavy isotopes and take on fresh bogie-pins, the ancient maintenance cradles are wearing out. Not today, not in ten pregnancies, perhaps not in fifty—but eventually. One by one, over centuries, an engine goes down into the rift for servicing and does not climb back up. The circuits on the plateau grow sparser. The *wah—wah—wah* of beat interference in the bedrock comes every four hundred sleeps instead of two hundred, then every eight hundred, then never. Bands grow inbred waiting for a crossing that doesn't happen, or overload a single condenser pipe until thirst drives half of them out onto the dry gravel alone.

And closer at hand—right now, tonight—there is the grit in the elders’ eyes, turning forty-year-old hunters blind before their knees have even stiffened. There is the quiet terror of the twenty-minute drizzle slicking the sloped alloy plating just as a sleeping child shifts in a worn fiber sling.

If a wise power acts here, it must act like the plateau itself: at two miles an hour, in total darkness, speaking only in fifty-hertz hums, mineral salts, and condensation. Nothing may look like a miracle. Everything must feel like the deep, steady grain of the world yielding a fraction of an inch to careful hands.

So here is what is done.

First, far down in the unseen boundary rifts—miles below any path a human foot will ever tread—silent repairs are made to the subterranean foundries. Worn robotic armatures are trued; depletedlubricant vats are refilled from deep hydrocarbon seeps; fractured logic-gates in the ancient routing arrays are healed crystal by crystal. The bellows-backs will not die out. More than that: buried deep within the non-repeating navigation algorithms that govern their wanderings across the highland, a microscopic weight is added to a single variable. The machines still follow atmospheric density and metallic drift; they still ignore one another completely as they pass. Yet now, whenever two engines have wandered more than two hundred and fifty sleeps without encountering another hull, their paths acquire an imperceptible mathematical gravity, curving gently across five hundred miles of whale-backed swells until their circuits graze within two miles over flat, stable basins where the dish-eared hunters have little cover. No band will ever wither alone in the dark. The sprints across the gravel will go on, safely within lung-reach, for ten thousand years.

Second, on the engines themselves, during those rare centennial descents into the rift-vaults when no human riders are aboard—for the bands always drop off at the highland’s lip and wait weeks around the warm fumaroles for an outbound rig to crest the rim—subtle alterations are pressed into the unpainted alloy as the hulls are refurbished. No catwalks. No handrails. Nothing that invites complacency or catches the wind. Only a slight coarsening of the metallurgical grain along the sloped flanks—a microscopic matte pebbling of the steel that sheds dust and rain just as cleanly, yet holds the grease of a rawhide sole with twice the friction when a sudden drizzle blows across the basin. Along the structural ribs beside the hydraulic recesses, the alloy cooling-fins are cast a thumb’s-width deeper and slightly flared at the lip: purely functional heat-sinks to any machine eye, yet shaped so a braided shrub-fiber hammock-strap seats into the groove and cannot slip sideways when a bogie lurches over a buried ironstone nodule.

And down beneath the belly, in the heavy bronze-and-vanadium pins that hold the segmented steel treads together, the metallurgy is tweaked so that when a pin wears down to eighty percent tolerance—still long before the track could ever throw a shoe and halt the rig—it cleanly shears its retaining collar and drops whole onto the corduroy track behind the stern, warm and waiting to be found by an outrider’s bare foot, ready for cold-forging between basalt anvils into three new digging-irons and a dozen dart-points.

Third, out across the vast, dark grid of the basins, a quiet work of biochemistry unfolds among the lichens and the black fluted shrubs. Not a lushness that would choke the gravel or draw swarms of scavengers, just a tiny metabolic gift folded into a single, uncommon strain of the pale subterranean fungus that already grows alongside the bloated tubers—the one the bands dry into bitter packets for fever. Where that fungus drinks the warm iron-damp, it begins to synthesize a trace heavy oil in its fruiting caps, smelling faintly of bruised ozone and wet slate. Within a generation or two—because these are people who notice everything with tongue, fingertip, and nostril—an old woman rubbing tallow onto her face after handling fever-packets will notice that the sting of ironstone silt leaves her lids, and the milky film creeping across the edge of her dark pupil halts and slowly clears. Word will travel at the speed of colliding circuits, tapped onto wrists and murmured into collarbones during three-hour crossings: *boil the grey cap in glider-fat; smear a drop along the lashes before sleeping; keep your eyes until your hair goes white.*

Beyond that?

Nothing.

The plum-black sky remains unbroken. The predators still pad parallel to the herds with their great listening ears cupped to the dark. The hunter clipped high on the louver-strut still holds her breath waiting for the violet *thump* inside the vents, because the pride in her ribs when her dart takes the glider clean out of the turbulent air belongs to her, and no power in the universe has the right to steal it by dropping easy meat at her feet.

Only once, right now, as the seventeen-year-old boy clips a strange harness around his waist on a new bellows-back, feeling the unfamiliar, sharper ozone bite in his throat and the lonely ache of his mother’s forehead receding miles upslope into the blackness, does the attention resting upon him come close enough to touch.

It does not speak in his mind. It does not shine a light on the wet alloy.

Simply, as he leans his cheek against the vibrating structural rib to let the strange engine’s pitch settle into his jawbone, a tiny harmonic shift ripples through the turbine housings deep inside the hull—a transient alignment of spinning shafts and warm hydraulic fluid that brings the fifty-hertz purr, for twenty slow breaths, into the exact, warm, resonant key of the hull that carried him in the womb. The chill leaves his shoulders. His fingers uncurl on the harness strap. Up above him in the dark recess, an unseen hand reaches down from a neighboring hammock, taps twice against his knuckles—*here, safe, sleep now*—and pulls back.

The boy closes his wide dark eyes. Down below, the sixteen bogies settle the gravel with their quiet, endless *shhhk-shhhk*. High on the prow, the single pale amber lamp tips over the crest of the whale-backed ridge, lighting the forty yards of world it needs to light, and nothing more.
