The attention does not enter by breaking the walls. Anything that pierced the vascular shell from the outside would only feed the panic of the root, triggering a fresh hydraulic surge, tightening the knot of causality until the forty-five minutes of breathlessness ground down into forty-five seconds of red paste. 

Instead, the presence arrives as a slight change in the quality of the Tuesday afternoon light. It has no body here, only an immense, patient listening—like a hand laid flat against the trunk of a feverish tree, feeling the hammering pulse beneath the bark and understanding at once both the blind terror of the wood and the blind terror of the splinter inside it.

For a short while—which is no time at all, and also three generations of pale crickets—the power simply watches. It honors the sheer, bloody-minded magnificence of the engineering. To have held back the crushing weight of the deep earth with cold-hammered copper, river flint, and braided sinew; to have braced one’s heels so stubbornly against death that *sequence itself* buckled rather than let the ribs cave in—that is not a small thing. And the unintended mercy of it, the warm bright pocket where tiny stowaways drink sweet sap and lay their eggs in stitched leather, is worth saving too.

Simply yanking the laborer out onto the grass overhead would be a clumsy violence. The sudden decompression of time would tear a mind apart; the sudden collapse of the hollow would crush the silent, many-jointed things that know no other sky. 

The knot cannot be cut. It has to be convinced to stop pulling.

The intervention begins in the sap.

Deep in the blind, roaring green behind the walls, miles away from any leaf, the plant does not know *why* it is swelling. It only knows wound-response: a foreign hardness in the cambium, a breach to be flooded with turgor and sealed with callus. Gently, so gently the hydraulic *whoosh-thump* doesn't even miss a beat, the wise power touches the chemistry of the rushing fluid. It introduces a quiet hormonal rumor—the deep-autumn signal of dormancy, the chemical memory of frost overhead, the slow, soothing instruction that tells an ancient oak to stop pushing raw meristem into a split and instead lay down dry, corky bark around a stone it cannot move. *Heartwood,* whispers the touch to the frantic cells. *Lignin. Settle. Hold your shape.*

The next time a seam ruptures near the floor, the pistol-shot *crack* is half a decibel softer. The cream-colored fibers exposed in the split do not immediately bulge wetly inward; for the first time in ages, they catch the warm air, oxidize a faint, pale gold, and stiffen.

The laborer does not notice yet. Trapped in the splintered panic of the loop, the broad, invisible hands still wrench a shattered chert wedge out of tomorrow and slam it into the ceiling.

Now the power turns its attention to the geometry of the air.

It cannot smooth out all the folds at once—the laborer is braced against them like scaffolding. If a shear-plane vanished while a heavy knee was pushing off it, the body would tumble sickeningly into itself. So the power works like a dry-stone mason, easing the tension one crease at a time. 

A droplet of guttation detaches from the apex. It falls four feet, hits the unseen glass plane in the middle air—and instead of shooting sideways at a sharp, shearing ninety degrees, the plane softens into a gentle curve. The drop arcs smoothly down, losing its violent momentum, and lands at the very bottom of the concave wooden floor. 

It does not evaporate. It stays there. A tiny, clear puddle, the size of a silver coin, resting quietly in true gravity.

Across the room, on the south wall, the two overlapping shadows—the raised maul and the slumped, gasping recoil—begin to drift slowly toward alignment. As the ferocious hydraulic pressure behind the green walls eases from a roar to a deep, sleepy murmur, the copper shims stop bending double. One stays wedged in the east seam for ten full seconds. Twenty. A minute. Because it is not spat out, it does not have to pop back into yesterday to strike a new blow. 

A sliver of the laborer’s scattered timeline slides smoothly home into the next, clicking together like warm bone.

In the center of the room, the sourceless, flat glare of the overcast Tuesday begins to develop a direction. It dims slightly at the nadir of the floor and warms near the ceiling, shifting from clinical white-grey toward the rich, slanting amber of four o'clock—the light that tells working animals and tired people that the heat of the day has broken.

Suddenly, there is a body in the middle of the cistern.

Not all at once—still flickering slightly at the edges like a mirage over hot river stones, smelling fiercely of woodsmoke, sour tallow, copper dust, and terror—the archaic, barrel-chested figure drops out of the high, tight angles of the ceiling and lands on all fours at the bottom of the curved wooden bowl, splashing the tiny coin-sized puddle with one broad, scarred palm.

The laborer freezes, crouched low, stone maul raised instantly toward the roof, teeth bared around a bleeding tongue, eyes wild beneath a heavy, sweat-plastered brow, braced for the pistol-shot crack and the crushing ton of greenwood.

*Thump.......... whoosh.*

The sound behind the walls is slow now. Deep and unhurried, like the breathing of a mammoth asleep in snow. 

Nothing cracks. 

The copper shims gleam dully in seams that have already begun to crust over with dry, sweet-smelling amber resin, hardening like cathedral arches. The digging sticks stand quiet, no longer shivering, held fast not by frantic leverage, but by wood that has accepted their shape and locked itself into sturdy, gnarled burls around them.

Slowly, painfully, the knotted muscles along the broad spine unbunch. The maul lowers an inch. Another. The laborer drags in a single, huge, shuddering breath that starts at the beginning and goes all the way to the bottom of the lungs without snapping back to the middle. When the breath comes out, it comes out as a long, ragged groan of sheer, bewildered exhaustion.

A thumb-sized, pale cricket drops lightly from the stabilized wood above—falling straight down now, through honest air—and lands near the laborer’s mud-caked knee. The person stares down at it, blinking thick, dust-rimmed lashes. On the hem of the oiled hide cloak, half a dozen tiny, pearlescent eggs gleam safely in the leather stitching. Gently, with the back of one bruised, Copper-stained knuckle, the laborer nudges a stray chip of black flint out of the cricket’s path.

They are safe, the room is holding, and yet there is still twenty thousand tons of dark earth overhead and no door.

The wise power makes one last adjustment—not a hole punched down like a pitfall, which would flood the warm chamber with cold mud or drown the delicate, pale-eyed crickets in harsh weather, nor a magic vanishing that would steal the laborer’s tools and leave them disoriented in an unfamiliar century.

High up on the north curvature, where the translucent leaf-membrane used to show the room half a second ahead of itself, the green skin thickens, splits dryly without violence, and curls back like the husk of a ripe chestnut.

What lies behind it is no longer the folded interior of the gourd. The power has gently stitched the torn pocket back onto the seam of the world—not deep in the crushing dark where the root-knot sank, and not in the bewildering present day of strangers, either, but right at the edge of a dry limestone bluff, maybe half a mile and two quiet hours downstream from the overcast Tuesday when the ground gave way.

Through the newly parted wooden arch—wide enough for broad shoulders, sloped upwards through a short, clean hollow of dry, sweet-smelling cedar-like heartwood—comes a draft of cool air. 

It smells of wet sage, river silt, and distant pine smoke. Outside that wooden throat, framed by gnarled surface roots gripping grey stone, the sky is clearing after rain. Long, golden late-afternoon sunbeams slant across familiar grass, stopping just at the threshold of the warm, dim root-cave, leaving the deep interior cozy, humid, and shadowed enough for generations of pale, soft-bodied crickets to stay behind in their self-sustaining palace of weeping amber sap and copper pillars, undisturbed.

The laborer sits back on horny, calloused heels for a long moment, simply feeling the weight of their own solid limbs in unbroken time. 

Then they reach down, touch the cool little puddle on the wooden floor, and lift wet fingers to a swollen, dry mouth. Leaving the copper shims and the fire-hardened sticks wedged right where they are—good work, solid work, holding up a roof that no longer wants to fall—the heavy figure gathers up only the favorite stone maul, shifts the oiled hide cloak carefully so as not to crush the tiny eggs in the seam, and climbs slowly, stiffly up the curved wooden ribs toward the smell of pine smoke and evening.
