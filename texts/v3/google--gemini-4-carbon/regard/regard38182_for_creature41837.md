To intervene here with a heavy hand—to cleave the amber soup with a shaft of clean sun, to freeze the churning shingle into new, jagged Alps, or to still the barometric gales—would not be an act of benevolence. It would be an erasure.

A lesser power, looking down into this warm, roaring pan, would feel pity. It would mistake endurance for misery, flatness for ruin, and the terminal state of matter for a mistake waiting to be corrected. Were such a power to lift the mountains back out of their grease, the colonial sails miles overhead would drop from the calm sky and suffocate in dry valleys. The lead-heavy flat-bodies beneath the stones would crush under their own unbuoyed weight. And the masons—whose entire bodies, minds, and languages are a masterwork of consent to horizontal violence—would be stranded on dry, motionless rock like deep-sea creatures hauled into the blinding air, their delicate, pale hands starving for the quarry-belt of the mud-tides, their forty radial struts useless on an earth that refuses to slide.

The wise power sees this clearly. It watches from a vantage that does not require height, listening through the dense, sweating atmosphere without needing silence. 

It watches a mason crouch at the lip of a cairn, taking the forty-mile-an-hour shriek of grit against its grey mantle, waiting for hours to pluck a single, palm-sized oval of black chert out of ten million exhausted stones. And looking deeper—down past the thirty-foot corkscrews of the hog-tubers, down through the warm, brackish tallow to the smoothed basalt roots—the power sees what the masons cannot yet feel, though perhaps their oldest tide-tables have begun to suspect it.

Memory is a non-renewable mineral. 

Even stubbornness erodes. Every million years, fewer pebbles remember the batholith. Every epoch, the ten-second phantom spires rise an inch lower before collapsing back into the slop. Eventually, even the stones locked inside the cairns will forget *Tuesday afternoon, four hundred million years ago*. Their hair-thin grooves will sweat smooth; their borrowed weight will evaporate; the dry *tock* will soften into a wet click, and the shelters will slump back into the sideways avalanche forever. The masons are curators of a dwindling estate, building dry rooms out of a currency that is slowly, irreversibly spending itself into grease.

So the power decides what to do. It will not break the world to save them, nor will it insult their craft by building for them. They do not need a savior. They only need the quarry not to run dry, and perhaps, once in an age, an answer to their witness.

The power reaches down—not to the sky, not to the surface, not even to the churning shingle—but all the way to the bottom of the pan, where the last, ankle-high roots of the continental shields sit drowned beneath the tallow. 

It does not raise them. Instead, with infinite, silent delicacy, it presses a single thumb of pure, fresh *conviction* into the planetary floor. 

Nothing bursts upward. No earthquake shatters the flats. Only a slow, deep, sub-crustal pulse—like a second heartbeat waking beneath a sleeping ribcage—rolls outward through the drowned bedrock. Where it passes, it does not make mountains; it simply re-crystallizes the tired mineral grain of one stone in ten million, whispering back into the exhausted chert and ironstone the dense, immovable grammar of deep earth. It seeds the deep veins with fresh stubbornness, ensuring that as long as there are pale hands waiting at the thresholds of the cairns, the roaring rivers of mud will never entirely run out of stones that remember how to hold.

That is the provision for their survival. Then, the power offers something else—not for survival, which the masons have already mastered, but for their spirits.

Three hundred leagues to the east, a mason sits alone at the doorway of a small, listing windbreak. This shelter is old; three of its foundation stones on the leeward side have finally gone senile, losing their grip on ancient gravity, letting the warm condensation bleed inward and thecontinental roar leak through the chinks like water into a hull. The mason has had its mantle extended into the screaming soup for what might be four days, forty struts locked into the soapstone, four pale hands hovering above the rushing slurry, waiting for a replacement anchor. Million after million, the lenticular ovals sluice past: *tired, tired, greased, empty, tired, gone.*

Then, carried on the crest of a three-hundred-mile liquefaction wave, something knocks against the mason’s sensory shadow before it is even within reach. 

The mason’s velvety facial plate flares wide. A pale hand shoots into the flying mud and closes around an oval of dark, blue-grey stone no larger than a duck’s egg. 

When the mason pulls it inside the leaking cairn and wipes the tallow from its flanks against the soft lining of its pouch, all four of its delicate hands go utterly still. 

This stone does not merely hold the faint, directional drag of an eroded Devonian root. It is terrifyingly, impossibly awake. Beneath the mason’s sensitive fingertips, the pebble hums with the absolute, crystalline stillness of a peak ten miles high standing under a black, airless, star-pierced sky—a height this world never even achieved in its youth, cold beyond anything in the warm broth’s memory, sharp enough to cut time itself, calm as the center of a diamond.

Trembling, the mason turns the blue-grey stone over to score its belly with a corundum thumbnail, preparing to bleed off its kinetic confusion and align it with the local tide.

Its nail stops. 

There, in the center of the stone’s smooth underside, is a groove already waiting. Not worn by grit, not cracked by shear, but drawn with the exact, hair-thin spiral geometry of a mason’s own hand—aligned not just to this afternoon’s barometric spasm, nor even to the next century’s stone-tides, but calibrated with breathtaking, mathematical tenderness to the drift of the entire pan. And beside the spiral, three tiny, microscopic dimples sit pressed into the grain, spaced precisely to meet the pads of two pale fingers.

Slowly, hesitantly, the mason lays two fingers over them, reading the tremor locked in the rock.

*High,* the stone says back to the hand. *Sharp. Cold. Still.*

And then, beneath that, a fifth word—not an instruction, not a command to change, only an unbearable, quiet weight of recognition:

*Held.*

The mason sits with the stone pressed to its sensory plate for a long time while the mud-gales boom and slap outside. Then it reaches down and slips the blue-grey oval into the failing leeward base of the wall.

The *tock* that answers is not loud, yet every drop of sweat hanging from the soapstone ceiling vanishes between one heartbeat and the next. The listing dome settles four inches deeper into an invisible socket that nothing in the universe could shear away. Inside the shelter, the air turns suddenly, exquisitely cool—not enough to harm, just a clean, dry draught smelling faintly of high snow and flint, a quiet so profound and spacious that for the first time in ten thousand generations, a mason sits inside a room that feels taller than the sky outside.

The power withdraws, leaving no footprint in the mud, no tear in the amber clouds. Outside, the horizontal lenses of marsh-water still hang and crack; the blind eels thrash; the warm earth boils sideways across a horizonless pan. Nothing has been violated. Everything continues. 

Inside the cairn, the mason tucks its pale hands back into its warm pouches, leans its heavy, mud-slicked mantle against the newly anchored wall, and waits for the next traveler to crawl out of the roar—eager to take their hand, guide two fingers to that single blue-grey stone in the dark, and let them feel that the mountains have not forgotten them either.
