A lesser mercy would have brought rain.

A lesser mercy, looking down from the zinc sky into that roaring, waterless cauldron, would have pitied the dry creak of the silica tendons, the frantic four-hundred-millisecond gamble of the Gasket-lice, the frantic wheezing of the Shear-dogs, and the blind, endless grinding of the Sump-worms in the dark. It would have softened the wind, coaxed a sweet spring up through the granite fissures, scattered spores of moss, and stilled the violent, rusted iron so the creatures could rest.

And within an hour, the basin would have been a tomb. Water would have gummed the pearlescent shell-flour into suffocating gray cement. It would have shorted the biological capacitors of the Ley-jars, drowned the pleated electrostatic gills, rusted the polished piston shafts into immovable red columns, and silenced the percussive heartbeat that keeps every waxy lipid in the bowl from freezing solid.

The power that pauses at the forty-foot rim is neither hasty nor sentimental. It does not measure life against a memory of green meadows. It stands—if *standing* can be said of an attention vast enough to weigh a tectonic plate, yet fine enough to count the teeth on a Silt-mite’s tarsus—at the edge of the gray talc peneplain, and first, before doing anything at all, it listens.

It listens for a long time.

To an unhurried mind, the deafening *thud-clank* and the burlap-gravel roar of ten million scraping shells are not noise. They are syntax. Watching the dark, cold waves of slate-blue chitin press inward, drink heat and voltage, and spill outward in bright, snapping currents, the observer perceives what the basin truly is: not merely a puddle of stranded pests, and not merely an accident of industrial wreckage, but a single, nascent *someone*. 

Every blue spark cascading down a mound of Ley-jars is an impulse crossing a synapse. Every vibration travelling from the donkey-pump through the belly of an Anvil-back and out into the granite is a proprioceptive nerve checking the boundaries of its body. The Brood-masons sorting pearl-eggs in the broken brass pressure gauge are its memory; the Sump-worms turning dust back into rising plumes of ammonia are its slow, deep breath. Out of orphaned cast iron and relentless deprivation, the barren planet has assembled a mind—a fierce, clockwork sleeper, dreaming a hot, dry dream of *continuing*.

So the wise power keeps its hands off the surface. It offers no miracle that would startle a single scavenger off a bolt-head. Instead, it drops its gaze straight through the swarming carapaces, through the packed drifts of pearl-flour, and into the metal and the rock, reading the hidden ledgers of wear and time.

What it finds there is an hourglass nearly out of sand.

The compound being is dying—not of predation or cold, which it has mastered, but of finitude. 

Look inside the wobbling flywheel’s housing: the degraded copper brushes that throw off the life-giving blue static have less than three millimeters of metal left before their tension-springs bottom out against bare iron. Twelve years, perhaps fourteen, and the sparks will cease forever.

Look inside the heavy cast-iron crankcases: the ancient sump of heavy, sulfur-rich petroleum grease—the black lifeblood that weeps past the degraded leather and asbestos seals to feed the Half-Seconders—is down to a tarry rind clinging to the bottom walls. When it goes dry, the Gasket-lice will starve in a week, and within a month the dry cylinders will gall, scream, and friction-weld themselves into dead monuments.

Look inside the primary piston rod itself: invisible beneath the burnished steel and yellow smears of crushed slowpokes, a branching dendritic fatigue-crack has worked its way sixty percent through the shaft’s cross-section. Four million more strokes—less than nine months—and eight hundred pounds of iron will shear loose, punch through the cylinder head, and stop.

And two miles down, where the ancient borehole taps a pocket of deep crustal brines to drive the closed-loop geothermal turbines, silica scale has narrowed a twelve-inch pipe to the width of a thumb.

Here is the paradox that stays a clumsy hand: *you cannot repair the machines.*

If you true the wobbling flywheel, it will run smoothly and throw no sparks; the Ley-jars will perish. If you replace the degraded seals with tight, flawless rings, no grease will weep onto the shafts; the Half-Seconders will starve. If you balance the donkey-pumps so they no longer shudder on their rusted foundation bolts, the Anvil-backs will lose the high-frequency oscillation that rasps their food from the stone. If you clear the deep steam-pipe all at once, the sudden surge of pressure will speed the engine’s stroke from half a second to three-tenths of a second—and every Gasket-louse in the bowl, trusting the ancient clock bred into its nerves, will be obliterated on the very first beat.

This life does not live in spite of the machines’ ruin. It lives *inside* their ruin. Their breaking is its sky.

A quiet gladness—the deep, artisan’s delight of a mind asked to thread a needle in the dark without waking a child—settles over the observer. It sinks down into the dead peneplain, miles below the blind Sump-worms, and begins to tend the hearth.

First, the deep throat of the earth. Atom by atom, so slowly that the pressure gauge on the surface (even if its needle weren't bent and packed with eggs) wouldn't twitch by a hair's breadth, the power coaxes the choked geothermal conduit to widen by three millimeters—just enough to offset the cooling of the surrounding rock—while weaving a dense, glassy lattice of non-reactive tourmaline along the inner pipe wall so scale will never choke it again. Beneath the heat exchanger, where the ancient magma chamber has grown sluggish and crusted, it subtly shifts the grain of the deep mantle rock, opening a silent convective chimney from the planet’s core. That heat tap will not fail now for fifty million years.

Next, the black marrow. Deep in the sheared-off feeder lines buried under the granite floor, miles from the extraction point, old pockets of heavy, sour crude sit trapped behind collapsed shale—forgotten remnants of an ocean that dried up two billion years ago. Gently, nudging the capillary fissures in the stone, the power connects those stranded reservoirs to the hairline supply tubes beneath the pump housings, setting up a slow osmotic creep. It matches the viscosity, the sulfur content, and the microscopic suspension of iron oxide precisely to the ancient grease already in the casings. The seals will go on weeping their precious black ribbons at the exact rate of four milligrams per hour, forever.

Then comes the delicate work: the iron and the copper, tended between one *thud-clank* and the next.

Inside the plunging eight-hundred-pound piston rod, while it pauses for its fractional breath at the top of its stroke, the iron crystals along the hidden fatigue-crack remember how to hold hands. Dislocations in the steel lattice migrate harmlessly to the surface; metallic bonds knit shut from the core outward, annealing the shaft back to the tough, springy pearlite of the day it was forged—yet leaving its outer skin rough, pitted, and worn to the exact micron-tolerance the Gasket-lice expect under their hooked feet.

Around the wobbling flywheel, the power does not touch the worn tips of the copper brushes where they spit blue ozone fire. Instead, it gathers stray copper ions leached long ago into the deep granite dust and feeds them silently into the *backs* of the brush-mounts. Like fingernails growing imperceptibly from a root, the copper blocks will now extrude forward at two-tenths of a millimeter per decade, precisely matching the rate at which the wheel grinds them away. The wheel keeps its drunken wobble. The bearings are held in a permanent state of stable imperfection—never healing, never seizing, never changing their song.

That alone would be enough to make the basin immortal. A museum piece under glass, ticking safely until the sun swells.

But wisdom knows that stasis is only a slower kind of cage. A compound mind trapped in a forty-foot bowl can only think forty-foot thoughts. And even an immortal engine is a single point of failure against a falling meteor or a shifting fault-line.

So the power rises back up into the brisk, zinc-lit air and looks outward across the ruler-flat smudge of gray talc.

This extraction line had other stations once. Three hundred yards to the leeward—far enough that it won't steal wind or alter the acoustics of the home sump, close enough that it isn't another world—an identical depression lies completely buried, choked to the brim with gray talc-silt drifted over eons, its ten cast-iron donkey-pumps entombed and cold, their deep geothermal pipes crushed shut by an ancient slip of the bedrock.

The power does not dig it out. To hand a creature a finished house is to rob it of the muscles it grows by building.

Instead, over the course of an afternoon—which to the frantic metabolism of the sump is ten generations of Silt-mites—it does three very small things.

First, across the three hundred yards of flat peneplain between the living bowl and the buried one, it traces a shallow, winding groove in the bedrock, no deeper than a wagon rut, tucked just out of the scouring main force of the prevailing wind, and seeds its stone pores with a dormant trace of the lithotrophic iron-bacteria. Currently, any creature blown over the lip of the sump freezes on the featureless plain within minutes. Now, when a dust devil spins out of the basin carrying a load of ground pearl-flour, stray Silt-mites, and a few wind-tossed eggs, the groove will catch the drift instead of letting the gale scatter it to nothing.

Second, deep beneath that buried sister-basin three hundred yards away, it unpinches a single crushed geothermal pipe—just enough to send a whisper of forty-degree warmth seeping up through the suffocating talc-silt, and to set one buried, muffled piston ticking lazily beneath twenty feet of dust. *Thud... ... ... clank.* Once every ten seconds. A faint seismic rumor travelling through the bedrock.

Down in the home basin, pressed tightly against the granite, a hundred Anvil-backs pause for a fraction of a second as that distant, unfamiliar tremor passes through their file-textured bellies. Deep in the fissures, the blind Sump-worms turn their eyeless heads half an inch toward the leeward wall. In the distributed, crackling mind of the compound organism, a brand-new thought—tiny, faint as a single static spark, shaped like *over there?*—fires for the first time in a million years.

It will take them centuries to answer it. Perhaps Sump-worms will tunnel along the warm rock seam beneath the groove, venting warm ammonia up into the wind-rut until Anvil-backs can survive a slow creep outward during the warmest hours of the zinc afternoon. Perhaps Shear-dogs, crowded in a bountiful century, will chase prey fifty yards down the sheltered trough, die there, and leave their heavy carapaces as windbreaks for Brood-masons to mortar eggs behind, building a bridge of dead shells inch by inch across the waste until they dig down to the ticking iron and clear the second bowl themselves. Or perhaps they will ignore it for ten thousand years until they are ready. It is entirely up to them.

Third, and last: just before withdrawing its attention, the power looks down at a single Brood-mason tucked inside the paper-thin curve of the eroded hopper. The spider-like creature is methodically brushing four pearl-colored eggs with its velvety tarsal pads, rotating them to keep their breathing-pores clear, having just eaten the fifth to keep its own dry clockwork moving.

Nothing is changed in the Brood-mason’s hard, frugal arithmetic. Nothing is softened in the Shear-dog’s magnetite guillotine-jaw ten feet away. The air remains sharp enough to sting; the ozone and hot grease sting the dry breeze; the burlap-sack roar of millions of ceramic plates grinding together rises undiminished to the clouds.

The visitor adds nothing more, takes nothing away, leaves no sign, asks no worship, and goes on its way—leaving behind a steady heartbeat in the deep rock, a seam of black grease that will not run dry, and a faint, rhythmic knocking three hundred yards to the east, waiting in the dark like an unopened door.
