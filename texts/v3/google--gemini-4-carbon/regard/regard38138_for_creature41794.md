To look down into the caldera with a wide and patient mind is to experience the quiet thrill of finding a watch that assembled itself out of gravel and candle-wax, ticking away in the dark at one beat per hour.

A lesser power, moved by clumsy affection, might ruin it in an afternoon. A lesser power might think *thirst* and send a gentle rain—and watch in horror as a single warm shower dissolves the uric-acid mortar, clouds the glassy amber nodes, cracks the hot porcelain vaults with steam, drowns the dry-setae footpads of the masons so they slide helplessly off their bridges, and blooms a sudden, slimy rot across eighty feet of cured, parchment-clean memory. Mercy, without comprehension, is just another kind of landslide.

So the first thing the wise power does is *hold its breath*. 

It arrests its own impulse to bless by adding. It does not step inside the rim. It sits—if sitting is the word for a gathering of attention—out on the barren trachyte shoulder, letting the thin, freezing plateau wind pass right through it, and watches the pit through three turns of the seasons.

It watches the grease pass from spoon-claw to purple tongue. It reads the long, slow sentences written in static electricity on the velvet carapaces of the masons. And as it reads deeper—down past the sunlit violet canopies, past the warm ninety-degree yeast-vats, down through the eighty feet of sealed hexagonal ancestors to the raw volcanic rock beneath—it sees the two clocks that are ticking against this world.

The caldera is not a timeless paradise; it is a spark caught in dry tinder, burning very slowly, and its fuel is finite.

The first clock is in the basement. Because the crater is geologically yesterday—a small, sharp burp of gas and magma through a dry crust—the heat beneath it is shallow. The hairline vents breathing sulfur and carbon dioxide and gentle warmth into the foundations are cooling. Not quickly; no living mason or her great-granddaughters will notice the chill. Perhaps three hundred years remain before the deep rock drops five degrees, the sulfur sighs thin out, the lipophilic yeasts in the lowest vats go sluggish, and one winter night the hoarded solar heat in the lizards’ bellies is no longer quite enough to keep the city’s blood from congealing into cold tallow.

The second clock is at the ceiling. The city cannot stop building upward. Every generation of cured carapaces, dust-pellets, and vitrified walls raises the floor of the living town a few inches closer to the jagged collar of the rim. In two centuries, the upper plazas will breach the wind-shadow. The thin, scouring gales of the high plateau will catch the heavy, saddle-leather canopies, ripping them along their brittle balsam joints and stripping away the warm boundary layer of air that holds the hush together.

It is a mortalbeing—for the city *is* a single being, a slow, decentralized animal with stone bones, lipid blood, arthropod hands, and reptilian lungs for holding the sun. Left strictly alone, it will live a miraculous, self-contained life of three centuries, crest the rim of its cradle, freeze, and cure itself into an exquisite, motionless fossil of spun porcelain under the dry blue sky.

There is no shame in a three-hundred-year life. Many stars do less with their millions. For a long time, the watching power considers simply bearing witness—memorizing the exact turpentine-and-sage perfume of the afternoon arcades, honoring the finite perfection of the thing, and letting it end clean when its stone cools.

Yet look at how hard those first dozen pioneers crawled across miles of killing talc, eighty years ago, following nothing but a thread of warm sulfur on the wind. Look at the pale sister in the dark, uncapping a bladder of fermenting wax by half an inch to bleed off a shimmer of vapor, never having seen the sun she is storing. Life here did not choose brevity; it chose *continuance* against ridiculous odds. It wants to keep calculating.

So the wise power decides to act—not as a savior descending into the square, nor as a gardener watering a cactus, and never by breaking the seal of the city’s own agency. It will not give them thoughts they could not think, nor tools that make their mandibles obsolete. 

It will speak only the language the plateau already speaks: rock, stress, warmth, and time.

***

Miles down, beneath the barren mineral dust of the plateau, a deeper, older seam of heat lies trapped under an impermeable cap of dense basalt—part of the great magma-plume that punched the solitary caldera and then sealed shut. Left to chance, its pressure would bleed sideways into dead strata ten leagues away, warming nothing.

The power reaches down into the dark, silent crust where nothing lives, and makes an adjustment smaller than the width of a human hair.

It does not crack the earth; an earthquake would shatter the unreinforced porcelain bridges of the city like dropped teacups. Instead, over the span of forty years, it coaxes a microscopic creep of crystal grain along a diagonal fault line deep beneath the plateau. Millimeter by millimeter, decade by decade, a capillary chimney of porous pumice and micro-fractures opens between the deep, steady magma chamber and the cooling roots of the caldera. 

No sudden gush of poison gas erupts into the undercrofts. Only, the dying sulfur vents at the bottom of the eighty-foot honeycombed foundation... do not die. They stabilize. A steady, inexhaustible sigh of warm carbon dioxide, trace hydrogen sulfide, and dry geothermal heat locks in at the exact pressure of the city’s youth, turning a shallow cooling ember into a deep-earth hearth good for ten thousand years.

That solves the basement. Now for the ceiling, and the loneliness of a single jar.

A solitary vessel, however immortal its stove, is still at the mercy of a single falling meteor, a single virulent mutation in its yeast-vats, or the day its own masonry climbs into the freezing wind. If this dry, wax-blooded miracle is to have a real future—a history rather than an anecdote—it needs room to grow without losing its roof. And it must earn every inch of that room itself.

Three hundred yards due east of the caldera’s jagged rim, across a stretch of sterile, wind-scoured dust, lies another depression in the black trachyte—a shallow, blind collapse-pit, currently cold and filled with drifted volcanic ash. And four hundred yards beyond that, a third, wider and deeper still, shadowed by a ridge of pale pumice. Dozens of these dry, lifeless pockmarks dimple the high shoulder of the plateau like bubbles in cooled slag.

The power will not carry a single lizard or seed-pod across the wind-swept waste to plant a colony. To carry them would be to teach them nothing; they would remain helpless islands waiting for an invisible hand.

Instead, starting five feet below the living floor of the city—right where the roots of the oldest copper vines probe the sheer east wall of the caldera—the power gently dissolves a soft vein of anhydrite salt-rock that runs horizontally through the trachyte between the living pit and its dead neighbor three hundred yards away.

It does not hollow out a highway. It leaves a tight, dark, winding seam—packed with loose pumice grit, sheltered entirely from the freezing plateau winds above, and warmed from below by a branch of that same deep capillary fault. A dry umbilical cord through solid stone, barely wide enough at first for a flattened skink’s ribs or the loaf-broad shell of a mason.

Then the power withdraws its hand completely, folds itself back into the vast, quiet blue of the high-altitude sky, and waits to see if the city is listening.

***

Nothing happens that afternoon, or that year. That is how you know the gift was scaled correctly.

Three years later, deep in the shaded eastern undercrofts, a pale, twig-limbed cellar-keeper is tending a row of leaf-leather vats near the raw trachyte wall. Her heat-sensing eye-pits, blind to daylight, register a faint, unfamiliar glow in the infrared: a hairline crack in the black rock is breathing a steady ribbon of warm, sulfur-sweet air—not upward from the buried ancestors, but *sideways*, out of the east.

She steps closer on silent stilts. She touches a spoon-clawed forelimb to the stone, leaves a microscopic smear of fermented ester-wax on the crack’s lip, and turns back to her vats.

Weeks later, a broad, velvet-plated mason descends a shaft to deliver pellets of crude balsam. Rotating past the pale sister in the dark, his saffron antennae brush her flank. He reads the static pattern and the slightly altered oxidation of the wax on her shell. His foregut chemistry shifts by a fraction of a percent. He climbs halfway back up the wall, stops at the hairline crack in the trachyte, and instead of sealing the draft with frothy stone-paste—as masons have done for eighty years whenever they met cold rock—he pauses. 

The draft is not cold. It is eighty-eight degrees, rich in raw mineral grit, and charged with the crisp static of unworked pumice.

Slowly, moving like the minute hand of a clock, his wide lower mandible opens—not to plaster the crack shut, but to scrape away a loose flake of soft anhydrite, widening the lip by a quarter of an inch. He swallows the grit to line his gizzard, lays down a neat, ribbed archway of UV-free geopolymer to shore up the tiny opening so it will not collapse, and backs out.

Ten years later, the archway is a vaulted gallery six yards deep into the living mountain, threaded by a thick, pale-copper root that has grown sideways in the dark to drink the sulfur sigh. Two sandstone-beaded lizards have discovered that this blind tunnel holds noon’s heat even better than the crowded nurseries; they sleep coiled at its end, shedding warmth further down the seam, drawing turquoise rivers of mites after them to sweep the fresh pumice-dust back into the town for mortar.

A century from now, themasons will break through the far rock-face into the bottom of the second pit. They will find it choked with dry ash, cold under the open sky, swept by eddies from the rim. For twenty years they will retreat every dusk into their warm subway-tunnel, advancing by day only an inch at a time, extruding cantilevered porcelain baffles out from the cliff-base, training the heavy slate-green canopies out into the sun to catch the light and still the air, teaching a dead bowl of stone how to hold its breath.

And back in the first caldera, as the upper terraces finally near the windy rim, the city will no longer need to build straight up into the killing gale. Its thermal map has grown sideways. New eggs carried into the deep, dark, lateral tunnels—exposed to slightly different mineral salts and total subterranean stillness—will molt over millennia into new castes: broader-clawed miners with diamond-hard basalt mandibles; thicker-scaled courier-lizards that ferry banked solar heat half a mile underground from sunlit pit to subterranean refinery.

One day, five thousand years hence, a traveler crossing the barren, wind-scoured plateau under the thin blue sky will see nothing at first—only miles of dead mineral dust and jagged collars of black volcanic rock. 

Until they come to the edge of one sharp pit, or any of forty sharp pits scattered across the high shoulder of the mountain, look straight down out of the wind, and stop dead in the sudden, turpentine-scented warmth and breathtaking hush of an archipelago of porcelain cities—all connected beneath the stone like chambers of a single buried heart, passing golden light and memory from hand to silent hand in the dark.

Up on the rim, catching nothing on the sharp facets of the trachyte, the wise power watches two masons meet at the mouth of the first eastern tunnel. For ten minutes they rotate slowly around one another’s bulk, saffron antennae tasting the static of a world that just grew slightly larger, yet lost not a single drop of its quiet.

Satisfied, leaving not even a footprint in the dust for the turquoise mites to sweep away, the power lets the afternoon light tilt across the plaza, and moves on.
