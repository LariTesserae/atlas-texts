A foolish power, looking down through the eighty feet of liquid slate, would see deprivation and offer remedies. It would think to clear the water so the sun could reach the bottom; it would warm the freezing brine, or calm the colliding bores, or split the greased gneiss into sheltering canyons so life would not have to hold on quite so hard. 

A wise power looks at the seven concentric rings of suction, the radial willow-lattice of cartilage, the microscopic backward slant of the denticles, and the thumbnail-sized hatchling emerging already heavy with its mother’s grit, and feels something very close to reverence. 

To soften this basin would be to murder the *skrid*. Clear the silt, and the sleeper sharks and diving seals have eyes while the *skrid* has none; calm the water, and its tremendous, horse-pull grip becomes dead metabolic expense, outcompeted within ten generations by fast, fragile, upright things drifting in over the seaward sill. Every harshness of the valley is already half of the creature’s body. The shove of the bore is its legs; the crushing weight of the stones is its digestive tract; the acoustic blanket of clay is its privacy; the polished bedrock is its drum.

So the power does not touch the water, or the cold, or the dark. Instead, it listens to the two stories meeting blindly at the end of a taut spruce-root string, and makes three small, quiet adjustments—so slight that neither the fishers in the skin boats nor the flat, blind masters of the floor will ever suspect a hand was laid upon their world.

First, sixty miles inland and eighty years earlier in the slow math of ice, the power leans its weight infinitesimally against the belly of a grinding valley glacier. 

Just enough to divert a sub-glacial meltwater stream three yards to the west, where its silt-laden teeth bite into a buried, forgotten seam of green-grey steatite—pure, dense soapstone, laced thickly with veins of calcite and heavy iron carbonate. Slowly, season by season, chunks of this soft, greasy, mineral-rich rock are plucked loose, tumbled smooth beneath the ice, and rolled down the drowned river mouths into the dark basin. 

They do not flood the valley; they arrive sparingly, one here, three there, settling into the wide cobble pans among the millions of sterile granite and basalt skulls. Now, in the leanest, blackest weeks of late winter, when the surface freezes solid and no fat comes down on strings and the whelks are scarce, a wandering *skrid* riding a sliding sheet of gravel will occasionally brush a stone that smells faintly of iron and chalk and yields like hard tallow to a horny beak. Calcium and ballast, waiting quietly in the dark, unpaid for by the theft of a poor man’s gear.

Second, up in the grey chop and blowing sleet, an old man sits near the grease-lamp in a sod-roofed winter house, splitting spruce root with his thumb-nail and complaining of the bottom-thieves that shear his bindings and steal four days of pecking-work off the end of a line. 

The power does not speak to him in a dream or show him the flat, six-foot shadow pouring over the gneiss. Mystery is safer for them both; if men knew there was four hundred pounds of dense, slow meat lying flat on the stone, they would invent heavy iron dredges or multi-barbed grapnels to tear it loose. Instead, the power simply nudges the old man’s idle memory toward two disparate things he already knows: the way driftwood wedged in a tide-crack slips free when the water turns, and the abundance of flat, worthless shale cobbles littering the high storm-beach right outside his door—stones nobody uses for sinkers because they are too brittle to peck a groove around without splitting.

Before morning, the old man works out a lazy man’s knot. Instead of spending two days pecking a permanent groove into precious imported soapstone and lashing it fast with rawhide, he takes two flat, heavy beach-shales that cost him nothing to pick up, traps them in a quick, loose cradle of plain dried kelp-stem tied with a slip-hitch below the bone gorge, and smears the cheap stones heavily with rancid seal-grease left over from the lamp-trimmings—telling his grandsons that the smell of the grease draws the halibut down-current.

When the first line goes down through the slush-hole the next afternoon, the old six-foot *skrid*—the heavy grandmother with fifty pounds of half-ground basalt in her gizzard—feels the thrum of the spruce root from three hundred yards away. She peels her downstream rim, pours herself across the greased gneiss through the boiling mud-knots, and settles her great, slate-colored dome over the rig.

She snips the fat from the bone gorge cleanly, never touching the sharp antler. Then she reaches her collar-mouth down to the sinker. She does not even have to shear the precious spruce-root line; at the first firm tug of her beak on the brittle kelp-stem cradle, the slip-hitch parts as it was meant to. Up in the skin boat, the boy holding the line feels a solid *thump*, then the sudden lightness of a dropped weight. He hauls his line back whole—gorge intact, spruce root unbroken—curses the slick bottom mildly, ties on two more free beach-stones smeared with lamp-dregs, and drops it back down. 

Below, in the roaring silence, the *skrid* clamps her rim tight against the rock against an east-bound haul and takes the greased shale into her mouth, rasping off the rancid, life-giving fat before swallowing the heavy stone whole. What was war—small, bitter, mutually costly—becomes an unwitting tithe.

Third, and last, the power looks across the five-mile plain of planed, jointless gneiss, smooth as greased bone, and reaches down with a single finger into the bedrock at the exact center of the widest basin, where the colliding currents build their worst, boiling shear-walls.

It does not raise a shelter. It does not carve a cave. It simply deepens one ancient, ice-scoured glacial striation by three-quarters of an inch, widening it to the breadth of two hands over a length of twenty feet, its edges rounded and polished just like the rest of the floor so no current will snarl in it and no silt will pack it full. 

To the water, it is nothing. To a rolling cobble, it is nothing. 

To a four-inch juvenile eelpout caught outside its mother-shelter when a freak winter bore mobilizes the whole valley floor at once, that three-quarter-inch shadow in the boundary layer is the difference between remaining in the basin and being swept out over the seaward sill into the abyssal dark. To a female *skrid* heavy with egg-ropes, locking half her pale suction-rings onto the gneiss rim beside a cobble pan, that faint, thumb-deep lip gives her upstream margin just enough mechanical purchase that not even a once-in-a-century meltwater flood can peel her edge and flip her white belly to the drag.

And when she lifts a dozen skull-sized stones in the blind, freezing soup and slams them down against the floor right beside that tiny flaw in the ancient rock, something slightly different happens in the gneiss. The shallow groove catches the downward blow like the bridge of a viol, coupling the strike a fraction more cleanly to the deep root of the stone.

*Thud.*

Five seconds of surging, grain-filled dark.

*Thud. Thud.*

The pure, sub-audible heartbeat travels out through the planet—not two miles now, through the muffled *shuck-shuck-shuck* of the migrating gravel, but three and a half, reaching the farthest, loneliest iron-rivet males waiting at the very lip of the seaward sill, calling them home through the mud.

The power stays only long enough to feel the tremor pass through the cold stone beneath them all, and to watch two pale, watch-spring eelpouts tuck themselves back beneath the grandmother’s settling skirt as the iron-colored bruise of noon fades back to pitch. Seeing that nothing is broken, and nothing is owed, it withdraws, leaving the landslide to keep falling forever in the dark.
