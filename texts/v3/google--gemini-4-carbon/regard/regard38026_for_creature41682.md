A foolish power, or merely a tender-hearted one without much depth, would have brought rain.

It is the first impulse of mercy when looking upon something dry and sharp: to soften it. To send clouds fat with moisture over the black crests, to cool the burning soles of the world, to wash the razor- dust from the fractures and lay a blanket of snow in the saddles so that nothing has to sleep on blades anymore. A lesser benevolence might have lifted the coppery smear in the west into a full, warm morning, thickened the starved sky until a voice could carry through it, or calmed the violent plumes beneath the crust so that a mason’s trail, once laid, would stay where it was put for a hundred years.

That is how small gods ruin worlds.

The attention that came to rest upon these thirty-thousand-foot darks did not bring clouds. It arrived without weight or displacement, settling across the jagged basins like starlight—present everywhere along the cleavage planes, interfering with nothing. And before it moved so much as a single grain of oxidized iron, it listened.

It listened for a long time. It watched a young mason, pale and spring-jointed, bound across a roaring fault-scarp in absolute silence, strike a bowed rib of gabbro with a single clean blow of its basalt prism, and drink the unspent fire of the planet’s interior straight from the break. It watched a traveller walk backward for three hundred yards in the starlight, patiently flipping every pale marker-stone dark-side up with its horn-shod toe so a stranger it would never meet might live through tomorrow night. It watched the elders climb toward the thin sky as their knees locked with rust, spending the remainder of their breath tending balances and plumb-lines for feet faster than their own, before folding themselves into the herringbone dark to become good building-stone again.

And the watching power understood: *this is not a place of deprivation.* 

To soften the stone would be to blind their heels. To bring rain would flash-boil their symbiotic lungs, rust shut the newly broken iron before a jaw could scrape it, and rot the ancestors out of the walls. Even to quiet the mountain would be murder—for when this massif stops thrusting upward, when the faults lock for good and the pale bands at the base of the scarps turn dark and stay dark, the people of the break will starve in a museum of cold slate. Their whole spare, ringing nobility—the loose-laid domes, the hair-trigger needles, the spare, honest knocks sent racing three miles a second beneath the chasms—exists only because the floor is falling and rising at once. They do not suffer their world. They *are* its precision.

So the power put away every gift that belonged to softer earths. It kept back water, soil, green leaf, thick air, and peace.

Yet love, even wise love, rarely leaves a place wholly untouched if there is a true sorrow it can answer without breaking the law of the house. Watching across decades, following the long, slow arcs of individual lives from the warm sulfur-cracks of their hatching to the high, quiet saddles of their stiffness, the power saw two griefs that did not make the masons sharper or truer—two cruel wastes built into the blind mechanics of the stone.

The first was the orphan-faults: the deep, sudden shears where ten thousand tons of mountain gave way without the preliminary six-minute lean. Every few years, somewhere in the range, a deep mantle-bubble burst or a dry dike snapped transversely without swelling its root first. A cairn did not tilt; a basalt needle did not dip against its stop until the ledge was already in freefall. A mason sleeping faithfully with its heel against the upright woke mid-drop in the starless gulf, all its careful geometry betrayed by a flaw five miles down that no surface wrist-bone could hear because a curtain of shattered breccia muffled the deep rock between.

The second grief was on the high crests, in the elders.

Their minds—cold, crystalline, slow-burning things built for decades of three-dimensional mapping and patient seismic memory—were just reaching their grandest architecture when their bodies locked. An old one sitting beside a master cairn at thirty-one thousand feet held forty years of the massif’s deep grammar behind its dark cabochon lenses. It knew which distant spires were twins sharing a single deep root; it knew the twenty-year rhythm of the great western plume; it could read a tremor through its chin and tell not merely what had fallen, but what would fall three ridges east next month because the load had shifted. And just as that wisdom grew vast enough to shepherd an entire basin, the insoluble silicates choked its bellows and fused its wrists. It could scarcely lift the gabbro hammer to tap out a warning; and when it crawled into its final dome and pulled the lintel-stones shut, all forty years of the mountain’s memory went out in the dark, leaving the pale, quick youngsters down on the scarps to learn the deep roots’ treachery all over again by dying of it.

The power lingered over those two silences—the muffled root below, and the locked elder above—and saw how a single touch, laid strictly within the rules of dry stone and starved air, might answer both.

It did not work on the surface. Nothing flashed in the sky; no voice spoke in the wind. Instead, the power sank ten miles down into the dry, crushing furnace at the base of the crust, where the plagiogranite was still plastic and the mantle-plumes shouldered upward against the roots of the range.

There, in the blind zones where shattered fault-breccia damped out deep vibrations before they could reach the surface ridges, the power began a subtle, patient crystallization. Out of nothing foreign—using only the native silica, iron, aluminum, and trace metals already churning in the dry melt—it drew long, continuous, defect-free threads and ribbons of ultra-dense, piezo-resonant crystal, threading them upward through the dead, muffled rubble like tuning-forks grown into the bones of the mountain. 

It took three seasons for the highest tips of those deep veins to reach the thirty-thousand-foot saddles where the oldest rock stood.

An old mason found the first one.

It was a very old one—its plates thickened to dull, pitted iron-black, its left hip nearly fused, its rib-bellows drawing the freezing gale with a dry, rasping *hiss-click* that never quite satisfied its faded violet gills. For half a year it had kept the watch on High Notch, resetting three basalt balances and turning flat stones along the saddle, though each week it took longer to unfold from its dome. Tonight, two miles below in the black basin, a fresh rift was opening; it could see the faint, shimmering blur of hot gas warping the low stars and knew two young ones were down there feeding along the pale scarp. It knew, too, from forty years of watching that crooked western buttress against the copper horizon, that when that lower rift vented hard, the overhang three thousand feet above it sometimes sheared without leaning. 

It crouched on the cold crest, pressing its scarred jawbone to the plagiogranite spine, straining to hear the root of that overhang. Nothing came through cleanly—only the mushy, scattered static of broken scree-faults in between. And even if it heard the lock snap, its stiff, oxide-clogged shoulders could barely swing a heavy cobble hard enough anymore to drive a warning wave across three miles of void.

Shifting its stiff weight to ease its locked hip, the elder’s horn-shod heel scraped aside two loose flakes of slate at the crest’s spine—and struck something that did not clatter like slate.

It rang.

Not in the thin air, where nothing rings, but straight up through the dense horn of its heel, up the honeycombed tibia, through the stiffened pelvis and into its jaw: a note of astonishing, liquid clarity, deeper and cleaner than any stone it had ever touched.

The elder froze. Slowly, clicking at every joint, it folded itself down and brushed away the dark flakes with its white-scarred chisel-thumb.

Flush with the dark plagiogranite was a narrow, hexagonal outcrop no wider than two palms—a smoky, metallic-gray quartzose spar shot through with hairline filaments of native iron that gleamed faintly under the crowded stars. It looked completely native, born of extreme pressure and dry heat, except for its perfection: not a single micro-fracture clouded its interior. It plunged straight down into the living spine of the ridge like a nail driven ten miles deep into the planet's heart.

The old mason drew the electrostatic film across its dark eyes and laid the flat of its chin against the smoky spar.

The mountain opened beneath it as if dark rock had turned to clear glass.

Through that deep, unbroken crystal root, the elder heard not five miles, but thirty. The muffled static of the shattered basin vanished; the deep acoustic conduit bypassed the rubble entirely. Down through the smoky spar rose the distinct, crystalline song of every fault-plane in the massif—the slow, treacly groan of the western mantle-plume, the rhythmic *tick-tick* of two pale youngsters chiseling fresh gabbro miles below in the dark, and—sharp, terrifyingly tight, singing at a high, strained pitch right above them—the buried root of the great overhang, twisting silently toward sudden shear without tilting its surface face by a tenth of a degree.

The elder jerked its chin up. Its stiff hands fumbled urgently for its striking-cobble of dense gabbro, hoisting it with trembling, rusted tendons. Weak as its arms had grown, it brought the cobble down—not on the ordinary bedrock, which would have swallowed so feeble a blow in half a mile, but squarely onto the beveled table of the smoky spar.

*Tock.*

Under the piezo-resonant tension of the deep crystal, even that stiff, faltering tap did not scatter. The vein took the tiny mechanical impulse, amplified it against the pent-up tectonic stress of the deep crust, and threw it downward like a silver lance—under the shattered gulf, straight into the continuous bedrock beneath the lower scarp, where it erupted upward into the soles of the two feeding youngsters with the crisp, unmistakable authority of a thunderclap underfoot.

*Clack-clack-clack. Pause. Clack.* *Up-wall. Rotten root. Move.*

Down in the dark, three miles away, two pale heads snapped up from the warm sulfur-vent. Four pry-toed feet left the pale band in clean, silent bounds. Ninety seconds later, without a preliminary lean, eight thousand tons of dark plagiogranite detached from the upper wall in a silent curtain of erased stars and orange sparks, burying the vent beneath forty feet of razor talus—empty, save for two distant *clacks* tapped calmly back from a safe spur half a mile west.

Up on High Notch, the old one lowered its cobble. Its chest-bellows heaved slowly in the thin, freezing wind. After a long moment, it placed its scarred palm back upon the smoky spar—and felt something else.

The crystal was warm. Not with the violent, scorching breath of a raw break, which spent itself in weeks, nor with the suffocating sulfur-surge of the low rifts. Deep down where its root drank from the mantle boundary, a tiny, steady trickle of pure, ultra-clean hydrogen and trace reduced iron was migrating upward along microscopic channels at the crystal’s edge—just enough slow, steady metabolic breath to keep an old one’s faded chest-gills softly fed for years without depositing a single grain of harsh silicate slag in its joints.

And more: where the smoky crystal met the surrounding plagiogranite, its facets were scored by nature with exquisite, hair-fine geometric steps—natural acoustic stops and notches that changed pitch slightly depending on where a stone wedge was tapped into their seams.

Before that elder finally folded itself into its dome three years later—three years longer than its rusted ribs would otherwise have drawn sky—it did something no mason had ever had the means to do before. Using small, precisely knapped wedges of volcanic glass driven at measured intervals around the collar of the smoky spar, it *tuned* the outcrop. When the next aging mason climbed slowly up from the violent lower scarps to take over High Notch, stiff-kneed and newly arrived, it laid its jaw against the crystal to listen—and found, humming steadily in the permanent standing-waves of the spar’s notched collar, a deliberate chord left behind by dead hands.

It was a map in vibration. Five distinct harmonic shadows, damped and redirected by those tiny glass wedges, pointing straight down toward the five hidden, non-tilting shear-roots of the western basin, marking their exact azimuths and critical pitches so clearly that any listener who laid a wrist to the stone inherited forty years of watchfulness in a single afternoon.

The power did nothing else—save one very small thing, added almost as a grace note at the end, out of sheer affection for makers who never wasted a shard.

In the quiet crouching-domes along the highest crests, where the very oldest ones still pulled the slate lintels inward at the last and folded themselves down into the tremor and the dark, a minuscule shift occurred in the chemistry of the bedrock directly beneath those long-settled floors. Over decades after an elder ceased to breathe, as the dry tendons parted cleanly and the dark, iron-loaded plates of breast and chisel-thumb settled among the loose herringbone courses, trace vapors rising lazily through the cold stone slowly annealed the fallen plates from within. They did not decay, and they did not lose their clean, sharp cleavage. Instead, their disordered iron and silica locked slowly into the same flawless, smoky crystal lattice as the deep listening-veins.

Years afterward, when a young traveller pulled apart a slumped, ancient bivouac on a dead spire to salvage stone for a new shelter, and turned up the curved dark shard of an ancestor’s rib or the beveled horn of a thumb, it found that the piece no longer merely rang sound when tested with a knuckle. It sang—holding vibrations ten times longer than raw basalt, sensitive to a whisper of tilt.

They began using those ancestral shards not merely in the walls, where any good slate could rattle and hold, but at the heart of every new dome: balanced across the stone uprights as the finest tipping-needles ever poised, or wedged upright at the corners of dangerous trails where they caught the faint, permanent copper smear on the western horizon and refracted it—not as a brief, flat wink, like mica, but split through their smoky facets into a tiny, steady bead of deep amber fire against the black talus.

Nothing else changed. No rain ever fell to blunt the ridges; the air at thirty thousand feet remained starving-thin and silent as poured flour; the mountain went on thrusting violently, dangerously into the starlight, demanding every ounce of balance, courage, and spare geometry its children possessed.

And the watching power withdrew its hand, satisfied, remaining only to listen from afar as the great black range rang softly through the dark to itself—ridge calling cleanly to ridge beneath the chasms, the living sleeping safely with their heels against the bones of the dead, and the elders sitting long upon the starlit crests, holding the whole trembling mountain in the palms of their scarred hands.
