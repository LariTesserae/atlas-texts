A lesser kindness would have ruined them in an afternoon.

It would have looked down from the bone-white glare at this weeping, fractured millstone of a valley and seen only an error left over from the world’s making—a place too cold, too loud, too sharp-edged to be endured. Out of pity, a clumsy benevolence would have smoothed the spatial seams, stilled the skipping water, warmed the thin air, and laid a gentle blanket of loam and moss across the gravel. 

Within a month, the inhabitants would have starved, frozen, and gone deafly blind. Without the spatial puddles opening into the crushing dark three miles below, their ropes could never reach the sulfur-grease. Without the grinding friction of the rock-faults, their insulated huts would lose their only heat. Without the relentless hydraulic clatter bouncing off invisible shelves, their calcified bristles would read nothing at all—only a terrifying, featureless hush. Worse still, a smoothed valley would strip a thousand generations of master masons and mathematicians of the great, roaring instrument they had spent their whole existence learning how to play.

The power that pauses here knows this. Wisdom begins in listening long enough to tell the difference between a wound and a home.

So it does not arrive as a sun breaking the bleached sky, nor as a voice booming across the pillars—either of which would scramble the valley’s acoustics and drive the workers flattening to the gravel in terror. Instead, it folds itself quietly into an unoccupied crease between two wet fins of slate, takes up no room at all, and listens.

It listens for seven turns of a minor spatial tide.

It hears the brisk, dry *tink-tink-chak* of the chest-slates cutting across the bass roar of the water, and learns the grammar of their collective map before the first day is out. It watches the bucket-sized young hauling tarry clots of deep-earth bacteria out of two inches of water, flicking drops of warm brine off their four-thumbed hands. It feels the quiet, slow-pulsed contentment inside the upside-down huts, where off-duty workers seal their rawhide bases to the ceiling-stones, tuck their boneless arms into dry mineral felt, and rest in the faint, humming warmth of a stressed wall. It watches the elder dodge the forty-ton drop of subterranean floodwater with two seconds to spare, skate back out onto the scoured dolomite, and strike the first tally of a fresh column.

Nothing here needs saving from itself. To look upon these squat, eyeless, chalk-crusted plumbers wedging three milky balls of quartz into an empty V of air—turning a destructive shear into a purring hydraulic bearing—is not to feel pity. It is to feel the sharp, quiet delight of an artisan coming upon a workshop of peers in the middle of a wasteland.

Yet a power that can see across millennia notices two things that the workers, bound inside time, cannot fully see.

First, it looks thirty yards down the wet wall from where the elder is chiseling. 

There, older columns of tallies march backward through the decades, recording the rise, drift, and snap of the invisible shelves. Go back fifty years of marks, and the grooves are shallow, softened at their lips by the silver sheets of condensing water that never stop running down the dolomite. Go back two hundred years, and the marks are faint dimples, barely legible even to the sensitive pad of a spatulate thumb. Beyond three hundred years, the wall is smooth as soap. The chalky stone has simply wept itself away, or sheared off in high, bell-toned collapses like the dark slate fin across the basin.

And the spatial tides have a grand period longer than three hundred years. 

Looking down into the tangled geometry beneath the mountain’s roots, the watching power can trace the whole vast clockwork they are trying so hard to measure. Every nine centuries or so, seven deep seams fall into phase at once. It is survivable—there are sheltered alcoves on the east rim that remain continuous even during the Great Uncoupling, and a dozen well-placed collars of large quartz spheres along the central arches would bleed off the worst of the torque. Four times in their history, their ancestors nearly mapped the approach of that nine-century convergence. Four times, the soft dolomite dissolved under the endless water just fast enough to erase the opening chapters of the equation before the pattern closed. Every nine hundred years, they are forced to meet the great shift half-blind, losing four-fifths of their huts and starting the count over on fresh, treacherous stone.

Second, the power looks three miles straight down through the wagon-wheel puddles into the lightless, crushing fissures beneath the crust. 

Down there, in the scalding sulfurous dark where their braided crystal ropes come scraping blindly through the slime, two of the three primary brine-vents are slowly choking with hard anhydrite scale. In eighty years, the black bacterial mats in reach of the valley puddles will thin to starvation smears. Meanwhile, up in the basin, the accessible veins of raw white quartz—the rough grist they feed into their spinning water-bowls to grind into stabilizing spheres—have been picked nearly back to the sheer boundary cliffs.

To hand them answers would insult their minds. To break the broken rules of the valley on their behalf would ruin their physics, rendering every tally on their walls a lie. Anything done here must obey the exact grammar of stone, pressure, water, and time.

So the power reaches not into the air, but three miles down into the dark, and half an inch behind the wet rock.

Down in the crushing subterranean fissures, where no bristle can hear and no rope currently hangs, a minute adjustment of tectonic stress runs silently through the deep basement rock. Hairline cracks flex open beneath the choking vents. Scaled throats clear themselves in a muffled gout of warm, mineral-rich brine that never breaches the puddles above, settling instead into wide, slow, warm eddies through the deep vertical throats. Within weeks, the black sulfur-bacteria will bloom three fingers thick across every wall below the wagon-wheel pools—a rich, self-flushing pasture good for the next forty thousand years. At the same time, deep hydrothermal currents roll a heavy apron of fist-sized, unfractured milky quartz cobbles and thick, silky seams of long-fibered crystal wool up against the lips of a dry fault that intersects the lower gravel beds of the basin—not stacked in neat, uncanny piles, just waiting in the scree to be stubbed against and pried loose as the gravel shifts.

Then comes the harder, subtler work, right at the center of the stone forest.

Dolomite is calcium and magnesium carbonate. Quartz is silica. When silica-rich water is driven into dolomite under fierce, localized shear-pressure and friction-heat—the exact conditions found along the basin’s spatial faults—earthly chemistry already knows how to knit a third thing: nephrite jade. It is the dense, massive cousin of the very asbestos-crystal fibers they wrap around their shoulders. On the scale of hardness, chert and flint sit at seven; nephrite sits just beneath them at six and a half. A chert chisel driven by a strong, boneless arm can bite into it, leaving a crisp, pale groove. Yet because nephrite is woven of microscopic, interlocking mineral felts rather than brittle plates or soluble chalk, water cannot dissolve it, silt cannot blur it, and a shifting spatial fault cannot shatter it. It is the toughest natural stone that rock can make.

And when struck in a thin, tensioned rib, it rings like bronze.

Behind the rotten, water-softened skin of the great central pillar—fifty feet from where the elder has just begun a new column—a dormant seam runs all the way down to the seven deep faults at the root of the basin. Along thirty yards of that pillar’s flank, half an inch beneath the crumbling gray dolomite, the power accelerates ten million years of metamorphic alchemy into the space between two *cracks* of a skipping waterfall. Silica and magnesium lock fingers, felting together into a broad, continuous, mutton-fat-pale face of dense nephrite, anchored deep into the bedrock where the grand nine-century tide gathers its tension.

Nothing flashes. Nothing booms. The bone-white sky stays flat and merciless.

The power lingers just a moment longer—long enough to watch what the next routine tremor does.

Midway through the elder’s third chisel-mark on the newly scoured ledge, a minor spatial ripple shivers through the central pillar. A soggy, foot-thick slab of old, half-dissolved dolomite—its surface holding only the ghost-dimples of dead centuries—loses its grip on the inner spine, slides six feet sideways through an aerial skip, and settles into the gravel with a soft crunch.

All around the clearing, bristles flare at the unfamiliar pitch of the echo.

The elder pauses, chisel in one four-thumbed hand, chert hammer in the other. When rock peels off the central pillar, the newly exposed stone underneath always sounds the same under the falling spray: a dull, chalky hiss. 

The water hitting this newly bared face makes a high, singing hiss, like silver shot poured onto taut rawhide.

Testing the gravel twice with their baton, the elder skates slowly across the gap, reaches the foot of the central pillar, and sweeps one pale, ossicle-packed arm across the exposed flank. 

The hand stops dead. 

Even through calloused pads worn flat by decades of masonry, the stone feels astonishing. It is dense as iron, waxy-smooth, completely unpitted, and faintly warm from its deep root in the fault-web. When the elder raps a blackened thumbnail sharply against it, it does not go *thock*.

*Tiiiiiiing.*

A clear, sustained, bell-pure note cuts cleanly across the mechanical applause of the valley—and riding underneath that note, vibrating straight up from three miles underground, is a low, steady, unmistakable harmonic hum: the exact, live tension of the seven deep faults, translated into pitch. As an unseen spatial tide shifts half an inch deep in the mountain, the overtone slides down a tiny fraction of a semitone, steady as a plumb line.

All across the basin, the clatter of chest-slates abruptly stops. Fifty hidden workers freeze on their pillars and under their arches, every fringe of calcified bristles angled toward the central spire.

Slowly, deliberately, the elder sets the sharp edge of the chert chisel against the pale, singing stone and strikes once with the heavy lump of rock.

The stone resists—hard, far harder than chalk or slate, pushing back against the blow with a stubborn, fibrous toughness—and then yields a single, razor-clean white curl, leaving a sharp V-shaped notch. A ribbon of cold water leaps down across the notch. Nothing chalks. Nothing blurs. The elder runs one spatulate thumb over the cut and knows, with the bone-deep certainty of a stone-worker, that ten thousand years of weeping water will not lick that mark away.

For three long seconds, the elder stays completely still, one broad hand pressed flat against the singing rib of jade, feeling the deep clockwork of the world humming clearly against their palm for the first time.

Then their other hand drops to the dark slate strapped across their chest, and their blackened nail strikes a rhythm no one in the basin has ever tapped before—not an alarm, not a work-order, but a brisk, ringing summons that leaps from fin to spire until every slate in the valley is tapping it back:

*Come feel. Come hear. Bring the old counts.*

From behind the invisible fold six feet away, where the air smells of struck flint and deep-earth sulfur, the watching power listens to the chorus build, satisfied that nothing has been taken from them, and nothing untrue has been added. Then it unfolds itself from the crease without disturbing a single drop of upward-falling water, leaving the masons to their work.
