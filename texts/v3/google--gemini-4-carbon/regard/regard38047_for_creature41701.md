A foolish god would bring light.

A lesser benevolence, looking down upon that roaring, warm, sightless plateau, would ache with misplaced pity for the dark. It would part the crushing atmospheric envelopes or hang a cold, sterile lantern in the mist; it would give them fire that burns in the wet, or smooth out the terrifying storms, or shut down the ancient, grinding machines so the wardens could live in an unspoiled garden. And in doing so, it would blind them, starve their reefs, silence their songs, and break the sharp, thrilling edge of their genius against a mercy designed for creatures with eyes.

The power that turns its attention here is older and quieter than that. It does not look at the plateau; it *listens* to it. It feels the wet, mouth-warm rushing of a billion limestone lips, the rubbery thunder of the membrane-forests, the six-second *clank-hiss* of the great pump-jacks, and the astonishing, syncopated chest-chords of the things that have made themselves the stewards of alien iron. It recognizes beauty that owes nothing to the photon. It sees a young mind—collective, tactile, fiercely curious—already reaching outward on grease-slick talons.

And then it follows the wire.

It watches that solitary old mechanic, the scarred elder whose three fine sensory-tapers have grown calloused and dull from fifty years of tapping scalding steam-valves, hook onto the lip of the outbound ore-bucket in the middle of a continental gale. It rides beside her as the cable screams from *F-sharp* back down to a thrumming *D*, swinging out over five hundred miles of drowned karst and thrashing kinetic ridges, toward the western rim of the massif.

Here is what the wardens do not know, because none who have ever caught that bucket have had a way to come back:

At the edge of the plateau, the haulage cable does not descend gently. It tips over an eroded basalt lip into a vertical abyss where the atmospheric pressure climbs steadily from breathable upland mist to a hot, crushing, supercritical soup of sulfur compounds, ammonia, and metallic halides. For the first mile of the drop, the old warden clings tightly inside the rim of the bucket, her mantle wrapped double around her chest, her rosette clenched shut against the thickening, acrid air, thrilled and terrified by the sheer, bottomless *emptiness* of the echo below her—a space so vast her fiercest chest-thump vanishes without returning a single wall.

By the third mile down, the heat rises past blood-warm into fever, and the pressure crushes her air-sacs flat against her ribs. By the fifth mile, long before the bucket reaches the automated smelting-barge floating on the dense chemical layers of the lower atmosphere—where an articulated unloading claw will dump four tons of raw highland ore and one boiled, asphyxiated four-hundred-pound carcass straight into a pressurized slag-hopper—the old explorer is already dead. Her curiosity ends in an automated chute, unrecorded even as an error flicker in the blind factory’s ancient logic.

And *there* is the second thing the wardens do not know, though the wise power perceives it immediately as it traces the buried control-conduits down through the crust of the planet:

The machines are dying in a way no grease-nipple or shimmed bearing can arrest.

Whoever dropped the seed-factories from orbit eons ago never came back, and the deep-basin smelters that manufacture replacement crawlers, spin fresh haulage cable, and refine the tallow-grease for the upland rigs are running on the last three percent of their fissile and catalytic reserves. Within four hundred revolutions—twelve generations of wardens—the deep foundries will gutter out one by one. First, the maintenance crawlers will stop cresting the ridges, leaving no fresh copper wire or grease blocks to steal. Fifty years later, the central line pressure will fail for good, not triggering a demolition rig, simply letting the great condenser towers cool to the temperature of the rain. The forty-story iron reefs will rust through at their footings and topple into the terraced marshes. The subterranean worms will scatter; the heat-tubers will rot; and while the wardens as an animal species might survive in diminished, feral pockets among the limestone bowls, the intricate, tool-forging, grid-listening *civilization* they have bootstrapped onto the ribs of dead gods will be dragged back down into the mud before it ever learns how to make steel of its own in a fireless world.

The wise power considers this for the space of three pump-jack strokes: eighteen seconds.

It will not reveal itself. To speak from the sky in a booming voice would turn engineers into supplicants; to hand them finished miracles would rob their three-fingered hands of the joy of figuring out how the world rings when tapped. Whatever is done must feel entirely native to the physics of wet stone, iron, and echo. It must look like luck, salvage, and a slightly wider door left ajar.

So the power reaches out—not to the rookeries, not to the healthy young mechanics, but first, gently, to the cable rolling five miles above the abyss, twenty miles short of the fatal drop.

---

Three miles inland from the western rim of the plateau, where the haulage line passes over its final anchor-pylon before plunging off the cliff into the toxic depths, there stands an ancient, long-decommissioned transfer station. For ten thousand years it has been nothing more than a cavernous, flooded shell of reinforced concrete and silent bronze switchgear, ignored by the active network, too bleak and wind-scoured to host a warden rookery.

As the old, scar-fingered warden swings through the storm toward the rim, braced inside the ore-bucket, waiting for whatever unimaginable machine lies at the end of the world, a forgotten mechanical shunt on that final pylon—rusted solid since before her species stood on four legs instead of six—suddenly, smoothly, gives way.

*Clack-kchunk.*

A heavy Counterweight arm drops. A secondary guide-rail swings three inches inward.

Instead of hurtling past the pylon and over the lethal cliff into the crushing lower basins, her bucket catches a diversion switch. Flanges screaming, sparks invisible in the warm deluge, the car peels off the main haulage loop, coasts down a curved, slackening spur-line, and rolls with a booming metallic *thud* against a spring-loaded bumper deep inside the sheltered concrete vault of the dead transfer station.

Silence—relative silence, broken only by water dripping from vaulted ceilings and the distant howl of the wind outside—falls around the bucket.

For ten minutes the old warden does not move. Her heart hammers against her double breastplate. Slowly, cautiously, she unfurls her scarred rosette. The air is thin and wet, smelling of wet concrete, cold bronze, guano, and something wholly unfamiliar: the crisp, sharp tang of electro-plated nickel and warm, living mineral slush.

She clicks her talons once: *tink*.

The echo that comes back makes every dermal flap along her spine flare straight up in astonishment.

The chamber has been subtly altered—not built anew, only *nudged*, as if an ancient subsystem of the original builders had belatedly woken to execute a dormant protocol. Across the vast stone floor, fed by a newly cracked geothermal vent from below and a diverted rainwater sluice from above, stands an open-vat electrochemical foundry.

Fire cannot live on the plateau. The wise power knows this. You cannot give charcoal kilns to a world at one hundred percent humidity.

Instead, here is metallurgy that belongs to water and vibration.

Heavy copper busbars, thick as a warden’s leg and humming at a steady, gentle sixty-cycle purr generated by a simple, indestructible magnetohydrodynamic turbine down in the geothermal vent, dip into terraced stone basins of mineral brine. In the first basin, raw, crushed ore spilled from old buckets is dissolving slowly in warm, acidic runoff agitated by ultrasonic transducers—sound itself shaking rock apart into rich metallic soup. In the second basin, where the current runs mild and warm—safe enough that when the old warden dips a trembling, calloused taper into the liquid, she feels only a sharp, invigorating *tingle* across her nerves—pure, bright dendritic sheets of copper and nickel are plating themselves out of the water onto removable slate cathodes, growing like stiff metallic leaves in the dark.

And beside the vats, bolted into the concrete at waist-height for a low-slung quadruped, sit six heavy inspection plaques.

The old warden hauls her four-hundred-pound bulk over the lip of the bucket, drops suction-footed onto the wet concrete, and creeps toward the plaques. Her three scarred sensory fingers reach out, expecting the cramped, frustrating four-finger-and-thumb pin-grids of the ancient builders.

She freezes.

On the left half of the cast-bronze plate is the ancient script—the tight, alien braille she has known all her life as unreadable machine-bark. On the right half, cast into fresh, uncorroded bronze, the exact same schematic is rendered in deep, sweeping relief curves, acoustic resonant cavities, and wide, five-point radial indentations spaced *precisely* for two heavy talons and three long, tapping tapers.

It is a Rosetta Stone of touch and pitch.

Strike the first hollow boss on the plaque with a talon, and it rings at the exact *F-sharp* of a strained haulage cable, followed by a raised tactile diagram showing the crystalline grain structure of steel under tension. Tap the next cavity, and it hums with the sixty-cycle purr of the plating vats beside her, paired with raised, unmistakable contours showing how to hang a wax-coated reed mold in the brine so the electric current grows a seamless bronze pipe, a bearing race, or a blade straight out of the water—no ore-bucket press required, no dependence on the dying machines down in the abyss.

Further back in the vault, racked neatly out of the rain, sit three dormant maintenance crawlers—smaller than the twenty-ton juggernauts, stripped of their demolition steam-lances, their sealed logic-bellies opened and replaced with purely mechanical, direct-linkage hydraulic controls: wide, five-digit levers and foot-pedals cupped to fit vacuum-pulling soles. And overhead, leading *back* eastward out of the vault toward the inhabited plateau, runs the return loop of the haulage cable, fitted now with a simple, counterweight clutch-lever that any creature with two strong talons can pull to summon an empty car home.

The old warden stands alone in the humming dark for an hour, her nasal rosette flared wide as an umbrella, trembling from prow to hind-pad. Her burned, numbed fingers move over the plaques again and again—tapping, scraping, listening to the bronze sing back the secrets of current, alloy, pressure, and leverage. She is not dead at the bottom of a sulfur ocean. Her exile has become an embassy.

---

The wise power does only two things more before withdrawing its hand entirely, leaving the plateau to its rain and its rightful heirs.

Miles down in the crushing, toxic basins, deep inside the failing central logic-cores of the automated network, a single administrative bit is quietly flipped across every factory node. The demolition-and-refit protocol is erased. From this hour onward, when an upland pump-jack finally wears out centuries from now, or when a sept chooses to unbolt a condenser valve to redirect steam into their own electro-forming vats, no twenty-ton juggernaut will ever come roaring up the valleys to scour their homes off the iron. The machines will yield gracefully to whatever hands reshape them.

And finally, down along the plunging western cliff-face—into the terrifying five-mile vertical drop where the atmosphere thickens from warm mist into dense, hot, metallic clouds—the power plants a trail of spores plucked from the plateau’s own hardiest bladder-weeds and sulfur-drinking reeds, coaxing their genetics by the barest fraction of a turn so they root into the cliffside crevices all the way down into the crushing dark, growing hollow, pressurized gas-bladders rich with breathable oxygen and clear, echoing acoustic bells that chime steadily in the abyssal updrafts.

A ladder of air and sound, waiting on the cliffside for future centuries, if ever the mechanics build pressure-suits of plated nickel and cured hide and decide they want to climb down into the deep crushing oceans to see what else their world holds—or look up, past the thin upland air, where radar horns pointed straight at the zenith don’t bounce back rain at all, only an infinite, silent, star-filled dark waiting to be mapped by echo.

Back inside the transfer station at the edge of the world, the old warden finishes memorizing the first bronze plaque.

She walks over to the main structural girder anchoring the return cable—steel coupled directly to the ancient limestone spine of the continent, connected by rock and pipe and tensioned wire to the hide-covered drumhead bolted to her home condenser tower forty miles east.

She plants all four hind pads on the wet concrete, presses her heavy, armored breastplate hard against the cold steel of the pylon, and fires every striker-rib in her chest at once.

*THUD-RIP-CHIRR-PING.*

Out through the howling storm, rolling down the braided haulage cable and shuddering faintly through forty miles of drowned stone, travels a chord no rookery on the plateau has ever heard before: the unmistakable, scarred cadence of their lost elder’s chest-voice, ringing back from the far side of death, carrying the acoustic shape of *shelter*, *copper-growing-in-water*, and *come and feel*.
