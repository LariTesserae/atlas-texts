To a power that has watched stars ignite and cool, that has seen a thousand species claw their way into thought through fire or song or blood, the basin is a rare and quiet delight.

At first, the Presence does nothing at all. Wisdom begins with stillness, and there is no sharper insult a god can offer a self-made mind than hasty charity. 

So for a long time—three winters, perhaps ten; time is thin at this altitude—the Presence simply occupies the cold, silver smear where cloud meets lake, sinking its attention down through eighty feet of gin-clear turbulence to listen.

It does not judge them for their lack of eyes, or mourn that they will never see the stars above their permanent ceiling of fog. It sees what they *do* perceive, and marvels. Where a human standing on the rim sees a bleak, drowned ruin, the six-limbed masons inhabit a cathedral of pure, roaring geometry. Their minds are shaped like the water they ride: tense, patient, astonishingly exact, built not on nouns and static objects, but on vectors, shears, loads, and intervals. A single *thock* of a muscular pad against a basalt flagstone does not mean *rock* or *food*; depending on the weight of the slap and the stone chosen to carry the tremor, it means *three-lengths-northeast, tension-rising, fulcrum-ripe.* 

They are poets of leverage. They are engineers without hands, warm and stubborn in the dark.

Benevolence, watching them, feels the quiet ache that always attends young intelligence trapped in a closed vessel. 

Left entirely alone, the Presence sees what will become of them. It reads the stone the way the creatures read the water. In four thousand years—an eyeblink—the relentless glacial grit and their own cleverness will wear the basin flat. Every pillar will be toppled; every balanced lintel will have slid to the lowest possible trench and locked there; every house-sized block will be rounded down into an immovable, dead lens of stone. Worse, the eastern rim—a cyclopean retaining wall three miles long that holds the entire lake pinned in the mountain saddle—is slowly thinning at its base. One sharp mountain tremor ten millennia hence, or one winter of extraordinary ice-heave, and the masonry dam will breach. The storm of glass will empty down the dry gorges in six hours, leaving miles of sleek, dry cobbles under the freezing mist, and ten thousand warm, blind engineers suffocating on bare stone, their warm nurseries boiled dry and then frozen solid before nightfall.

A lesser power might lift the fog to warm them, not realizing that warmth would bring green rot, choking algae, and silt to blind their pressure-pits and smother their skin-fronds. A lesser power might soften the currents to spare their labor, not realizing that without the violent, silent storm of glass, their minds would go slack, their hydrofoil grace made useless, their cooperative masonry rendered obsolete. To take away the weight of the stone would be to take away their language.

So the Wise One decides upon three gifts—each slow, each silent, each wholly faithful to the harsh physics of their home.

***

The first gift is a stay of execution, hidden beneath the floor.

Deep under the eastern rim, below even the warmest thermal nurseries where the pale, hand-sized young practice catching eddies, the mountain’s ancient heart still carries slow veins of heat and mineral-heavy water. The Presence reaches down into the blind, groaning dark of the crust—not shattering rock, which would terrify the basin with unnatural thunder, but gently coaxing the deep plumbing of the mountain a fraction of a degree slantwise.

Hot, silica-rich brine and dissolved iron begin to seep upward into the hairline fractures of the eastern retaining wall’s deepest foundations. Over decades, in the bitter cold where that deep seep meets the lake’s downward pressure, the minerals precipitate out—not as soft mud, which the currents would tear away, but as dense, vitreous veins of dark chalcedony and ironstone, welding the cyclopean base-blocks together from within. The dam will not break in ten thousand years, nor in a hundred thousand. The vessel will hold long enough for the minds inside it to grow into whatever they are meant to become.

***

The second gift is raw material—and a puzzle.

At the western pass, where the heavy glacial runoff pours continually over the smoothed lip into the basin, there sits a long, drowned causeway flanked by a sheer face of ancient quarry-wall—one of the few places at the very edge of the masonry where the original builders cut directly into the mountain’s bones before laying their courses. Over ages, it was polished flat and seamless, impossible for a suction-pad or a beveled prow to pry into.

One midwinter, during the natural groaning of the high glaciers, the Presence leans the weight of a single thought against a buried fault-line behind that western wall.

*CRACK.*

To the creatures clinging in the plazas three miles away, it feels like the birth of a small sun. The shockwave travels through the laid granite faster than sound in air, a crisp, ringing chord so rich in harmonic detail that across the entire basin, thousands of leathery gray shapes freeze on their pillars and flare their flank-pits wide, drinking the echo. None are harmed; the fracture happens behind the wall, not beneath them.

When the boldest elders—scarred, skiff-long veterans with calcified prows polished smooth as ivory—glide edge-on through the western shears days later to read the new water-shape, they find a wonder.

The seamless western face has stepped back fifty yards, opening a drowned quarry-amphitheater that was never smoothed out of true. Better still, the fracture has cleaved a massive vein of dark, dense hornblende basalt along its natural, sharp-angled hexagonal columns. Hundreds of fresh blocks—some the size of coffins, some the size of cottages—now rest on stepped ledges in the path of the incoming glacial torrent. 

They have *edges* again. 

For the first time in fifty generations, a creature sliding its muscular pad over stone feels a crisp, ninety-degree corner. A sharp edge splits water cleanly; it casts a knife-bright vortex sheet rather than a sluggish, wandering gyre; it locks into a floor-seam without rolling when wedged; it rings with a clearer, sharper *thock* when struck, carrying a message twice as far through the sub-foundation.

Even more intriguing: mixed among the heavy black basalt columns are scattered nodules of native meteoric-grade iron-nickel and dense magnetite, weathered out of an ancient sill—stones three times heavier for their size than pale granite, stones that sit stubbornly in a raging torrent where granite would skitter away like wood, stones that taste subtly sharp and electric to the iron-chitin of their beaks and the sensitive rims of their flank-cups.

They now have a quarry that replenishes itself slowly as the glacier grinds above. They will not run out of pieces for their machine.

***

The third gift is the smallest, and the most dangerous, because it is not for their survival. It is for their minds.

Half a mile south of the basin’s center lies a wide, dish-shaped forum that the creatures rarely visit. It has no good thermal vents beneath it, and its surrounding walls collapsed ages ago in such a way that two opposing currents cancel each other out in a broad, frustratingly unpredictable chop—too turbulent to rest in without burning precious blubber, too confused to hunt efficiently.

During a three-minute slack-water pause in the dead of night, while the nearest hunter is a quarter-mile off digesting a grease-coated bird in a trench, something changes at the center of that useless forum.

Nothing new is brought in. The Presence uses only seven worn cobbles and three thinned ribs of broken arch-stone that were already lying scattered across those flagstones. Moving them with the silent, buoyant grace of the lake itself, it sets them into a tight, spiral cluster no wider than forty feet across.

When the opposing currents slam back into the forum four minutes later, they hit the spiral cluster—and the chaotic chop vanishes.

In its place rises a single, exquisite hydraulic Engine.

The seven blocks and three curved ribs are wedged together in a self-locking, three-dimensional *cadenza* of fluid dynamics. They catch the two warring torrents, braid them through three stepped slots, and spin them upward into a permanent, crystal-steady double helix of water that climbs sixty feet toward the silver cloud-ceiling without wobbling by an inch. 

More than that: buried at the center of the spiral, balanced in a carved cradle between two ribs, sits a five-hundred-pound lens of dense dark basalt. As the braided current pours past it, vortex-shedding pulls the stone slightly left, until it kisses the northern rib—*tock*—which subtly spoils the vortex on that side, causing the current to snap the stone three inches right, kissing the southern rib—*tock*.

Every eleven seconds, regular as a pulsar, indifferent to the shifting chaos elsewhere in the lake: *tock... tock... tock.*

And because of the way the curved arch-ribs channel the exhaust current downward into a forgotten sub-floor inspection shaft, the spiral actually sucks a faint, lost ribbon of deep thermal water twenty feet higher than it ever rose before, spilling a thin, gentle braid of warmth right out the side of the structure—not enough to build a nursery, not enough to trap a single blind pewter-fish or dinner-plate crab.

It catches no food. It walls off no shelter. It serves no stomach.

Yet it is unmistakable.

Two days later, a solitary juvenile—six feet of tough, curious gristle—feels that steady, eleven-second pulse humming faintly through a floor-seam half a mile away. Never in the history of the world has anything in the storm of glass kept perfect time. Rocks tip once and lie dead; wall-worms snap in frantic bursts; gyres swell and collapse like breathing beasts. Nothing goes *tock... tock... tock* through a whole night and a whole day.

It glides out across the empty pavements, banking cautiously off the shear-planes, and drops into the leeward margin of the forum. Within an hour, it is slapping the flagstones sharply—*thock-thock, thock!*—summoning others with a cadence no elder has ever quite used before, because there has never been a reason to say *come feel this shape that feeds no one.*

By nightfall, twelve of them are locked down in a wide ring around the forty-foot spiral. 

They do not dismantle it. For hours, they simply hang flattened against the slick pavement, their blood-dark respiratory fronds streaming steadily behind them, their rows of pale-rimmed sensory pits turned inward toward the Engine. In their minds—those dark, pressure-mapped cathedrals of pure vector and tension—they can see every ounce of force running through the seven cobbles and three ribs. They can feel how block A pins block B against the shear; they can taste the tiny, singing thread of warm water drawn up from the deep; they can feel the exquisite balance of the swinging stone heart beating out time against the masonry.

It is using rules they already know, taken three steps further than they have ever dreamed of going.

One massive old female creeps slowly inward, suction-pads rippling over the granite, until her scarred, calcified prow is three inches from the outermost slot. She extends a single thick limb and presses the edge of her pad lightly—just half an inch—into the intake stream.

Instantly, the singing double helix above them tilts five degrees; the warm thread cools; the swinging stone inside slows from eleven seconds to fourteen—*tock...... tock*.

She pulls her limb back. 

The helix snaps upright. The warmth returns. *Tock... tock... tock.*

Around the ring, three other elders answer—not with the heavy, booming *thock* used to coordinate a quarry-heave, but with a light, muted tap of their pad-rims, almost an echo of the swinging stone.

***

Up on the flooded rim, where the permanent clouds sit directly on the cold, silver smear of the lake, the Presence withdraws its hand.

There will be no burning bushes down in the culverts, no prophecies carved into the pillars, no sudden Uplift tearing them out of their skins before they have earned their own names for the dark. For centuries yet, they will go on shearing diving birds in the trenches and wedging cobbles over crab-runs. 

But down in the center of the gray basin, a clock is ticking in the stone. Already, the sharpest minds among them are memorizing the curve of those three arch-ribs, wondering what happens if you set *four* ribs over a deep vent in the western quarry, wondering how far a rhythmic *tock* can be thrown down a hollow culvert if you shape the mouth just right, wondering—for the very first time in the cold, gin-clear gloom—*who else knows how the water bends?*

Satisfied, the Wise One settles back into the mountain mist, prepared to listen for the next ten thousand years to see what they build back.
