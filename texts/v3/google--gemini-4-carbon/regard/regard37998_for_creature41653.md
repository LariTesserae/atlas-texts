To look down into this box of blinding salt and silent flesh with the eyes of a god—a gentle god, an old and patient god—is to feel your hand twitch toward mercy, and then stop halfway through the air.

Watch the clumsy benevolence of lesser powers first, in your mind’s eye, just to see the ruin it would make. 

Warm the air by ten degrees so the children do not have to clinch their jaws against the burn? Instantly the acoustic inversion shatters. Sound rushes back into the amphitheater like an avalanche; the crashing of the ice-pillars roars off the basalt walls at a hundred decibels, deafening ears bred for velvet hush, driving the baroreceptive beasts mad with panic in their dark stalls, shaking the dry-stacked towers down into the slush. 

Quiet the violent weeping of the seams so the grandmothers don't have to pry up their floorboards twice a week? Without the constant hydraulic flush, the brine stagnates and freezes solid deep inside the rock; the shrimp die off; the furnace-bellied shiver-hens starve and cool inside the parkas of the toddlers; the rigidified walls crack at the first frost-heave and spill twenty naked, moon-faced sleepers out into the violet-black night.

Give them timber? Smoke. Give them iron? Rust in an hour, and flesh torn away wherever a bare knuckle brushes cold steel. Lift them down to a green valley? You murder the thing they have spent ten thousand years becoming. You erase the thought-in-the-wrist, the collarbone-lullaby, the marvelous, greasy, six-species single organism pulsing inside this teacup of basalt.

A wise power looks at the purple-black sun-burn across the bridges of their noses, feels the sub-audible purr of the birds vibrating through the slate, and recognizes a masterpiece written in an alphabet of extreme constraint. You do not edit a poem like this by removing its rhymes. They are not suffering from their world; they *are* their world, folded back upon itself until it grew warm.

And yet.

Because you are vast, and your sight does not stop at the polished black slate or the blinding cloud-glare, you look where the scaffold-climbers cannot. You let your awareness sink past the dished thresholds, past the six-legged beasts folded like woolly sowbugs against the cellar stones, straight into the basalt cliff. Down through the hairline fractures, miles deep into the dark, into the uplifted, subterranean sea trapped over the mountain’s dying volcanic root.

And there, in the lightless, pressurized brine-caverns, you find two truths that the lice in the blowhole have no way of reading.

First: the magma plume three leagues beneath the trapped sea is cooling. Very slowly—on a clock of centuries rather than seasons—the thermal heart that pressurizes the brine and feeds the sulfur-vents where the white shrimp breed is crusting over with dead olivine. Left to the blind drift of geology, in four hundred years the seams up in the sunlight will begin to sputter. The water will come out colder, sparser, poorer in fat. The hamlet will not die in a cinematic cataclysm; it will simply starve by inches over six generations, clutching lukewarm birds in darker rooms, wondering why the whale is finally dying.

Second, and far closer: a hundred yards behind the eastern cliff-face, a massive septum of rotten, salt-eaten limestone separating two high-pressure brine pockets has worn thin as an eggshell. Within three winters, the next minor tremor will punch it through. The entire eastern cliff will not weep; it will *calve*—shearing fifty thousand tons of basalt straight across the town square, sweeping towers, capstans, beasts, and bone-bridges over the southern parapet into the cloud-abyss in four muffled, soundless seconds.

So the wise power acts. Not from the sky, where light already cuts too hard, and never in the shape of a miracle. A miracle makes beggars out of engineers; if a hand came down out of the violet dark to prop up their walls, their fingers would stop twitching with foresight and learn instead to fold themselves in prayer, which does not turn an ice-blister aside.

Instead, the power goes down into the dark and does heavy, quiet plumbing.

Down at the root of the mountain, where no eye—not even an eyeless polyp’s—will ever witness it, a hairline fault in the deep mantle is coaxed open by the width of a fingernail. Nothing violent. Just enough for a fresh, steady ribbon of primordial heat to kiss the underside of the subterranean sea-floor, stabilizing the thermal convection loop for the next forty thousand years. And around the hydrothermal chimneys where the blind shrimp spawn, a subtle enrichment of dissolved silicates and trace minerals begins to seep—chiefly bioavailable calcium, selenium, and a quiet, complex lipid precursor in the vent-bacteria.

Then, up behind the eastern cliff-face, around that perilous, paper-thin wall of rotten limestone. The power does not seal it—sealing pressure only sends the violence looking for another door. Instead, over the course of eighty nights while the city sleeps entombed in black ice, the power opens forty microscopic relief-capillaries leading *sideways* through solid, unfractured basalt, routing the excess hydraulic rage gently toward the uninhabited northern corner of the cliff, while crystallizing veins of tough, fibrous serpentine directly through the weak rock behind the houses.

Wait until spring, and watch what the inhabitants experience of God.

One morning at high noon, a sixteen-year-old scaffold-climber hanging on a kelp-rope along the northern corner notices a brand-new seam weeping gently where dry rock used to be. Before it even breaks open, three of the six-legged beasts down in the nearest cellar have already knocked their shovel-horns softly against the beams—*tok, tok*—having read the polite, unhurried shift in pressure an hour ago. Nobody panics. The crews trot over with bone pry-bars and split-horn gutters. They cut a new groove in the basalt. They harvest a magnificent gush of brine-shrimp that seem, when rendered in the soapstone troughs that evening, slightly richer, slightly sweeter, yielding an oil that burns a fraction steadier on the silver-moss wick and leaves less soot on the drying gut-curtains.

Over the next generation, nobody notices that the toddlers’ bones set straighter without the winter bow-leggedness that used to claim one child in ten, or that the purple-black radiation scar across the bridge of their elders' noses no longer turns ragged and cancerous in their fifties, protected from within by trace carotenoids riding up from the dark vents through shrimp, worm, crustacean, and bird. To them, it is simply a long run of good, fat years, earned by their own sharp eyes and quick levers.

Is that all? Almost. A wise power is rigorous, yes, a watchmaker of ecosystems—but benevolence has a heart, and hearts are fond of leaving small, useless, beloved things behind. Something that solves no survival problem they could solve themselves, yet answers an ache they didn't even know had a name.

Think of the ledge halfway down the drop.

That terrible, grey limbo inside the belly of the clouds where the basin-folk and the coast-folk meet once every few moons to trade kelp for worm-oil. Think of what happens there: cousins separated by three vertical miles of atmospheric violence, meeting in a dead white fog where the highlanders’ heads pound from the thick, damp air and the lowlanders’ chests burn from the chill, neither able to look the other in the face without pain, hauling heavy slings past one another in hurried, miserable silence before retreating up or down to a pressure their bodies can forgive.

Along that damp, miserable trading-ledge, deep inside a sheltered overhang of the cliff where the cloud-mist condenses and freezes no harder than cold rain, the power wakes a dormant pocket of geothermal warmth—nothing grand, just the temperature of a human palm—and seeds a shallow basin of pooled mist-water with an organism nudged gently sideways out of the deep mountain’s own bioluminescent polyps.

The next time the bone capstans turn, and the leanest haulers from the high box climb down the kelp-ladders into the blinding wool of the cloud-deck to meet the muffled figures climbing up from the roaring sea, they step beneath the overhang to swap the slings.

It is no longer grey and dead inside the cleft. The rock breathes a gentle, neutral warmth where neither lung-type hurts quite so fiercely, and the damp stone ceiling is carpeted in a dense, velvety mat of cushion-moss that glows a soft, steady amber-gold—the exact, forgiving color of skin untouched by the scalpel-sun, soft enough to look at with bare, un-goggled eyes. And the air inside that curved pocket of warm stone has a strange, mossy resonance: it drinks the terrifying roar of the distant ocean surf from below, yet holds just enough thickness that a hum does not vanish.

A woman from the high basin pushes her bone slit-goggles up onto her forehead for the first time outdoors in her life. Across the warm pool of golden moss-light, a man from the sea-shore pulls down his salt-stiffened scarf. She sees the strange, unmasked paleness around his eyes; he sees the soot-dark badge burned across hers, and the barrel-breadth of her chest. 

Neither speaks—they have no words in common anymore anyway. Instead, resting his bare, ocean-chapped palm flat against the warm, glowing stone wall, the coast-man hums a low note in his throat—the rhythm of swells hitting a shingle beach. Twelve feet away, pressing her stubby, hyper-dexterous fingers against the same vein of warm basalt, the woman from the silent city feels the vibration travel cleanly through the rock into her wrist. Her fingers dance back against the stone—*tap-tap, pause, tap*—and she answers with the closed-mouth lullaby of the dark towers.

They stay twenty minutes longer than the trade requires, sharing bricks of dried shrimp and strips of sweet kelp-rind in the golden dimness, looking at each other’s whole faces without squinting, before putting their goggles and scarves back on and climbing back to their separate worlds.

And the power withdraws its hand entirely, receding into the violet-black silence above the blinding sun, content just to watch: the black slate batons flashing at noon, the hot white birds pouring down the chimneys at dusk, and down in the stifling, musky dark, a young hauler tapping a brand-new rhythm—the slow, three-beat roll of an unseen ocean—against the collarbone of her sleeping child.
