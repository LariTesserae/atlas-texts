A lesser benevolence would have seen a trap. It would have looked at the bricked-over drains, the fouled glass, the blind, flattened life wedged between cast iron and boiling copper, and felt pity. It would have cracked the vault to let in the sun, or purified the heavy water, or lifted the sprawling creature out into some wide, clean ocean—and in doing so, murdered an entire, murmured liturgy of consensus in a single afternoon.

Wisdom, however, recognizes a miracle of grace even when it smells of wet rust and warm tallow.

When the Power turned its attention downward—passing effortlessly through the dry, grinding strata of whatever new world currently occupied the crust overhead, slipping past forgotten sub-basements and crushed transit tubes until its awareness pooled in the warm, green-black gloom of the shaft—its first act was simply to be still. It did not arrive as light, for light here was strictly measured out in amber blisters and three-yard halos of violet corona, and any brighter radiance would have blinded the subtle electrical sight of the custodian and scalded the shade-loving hornwort.

Instead, the Power entered the basin as an appreciation of its physics. It listened to the *plink, plink* of the soot-grey ceiling sponges turning explosive gas back into water. It felt the minute-hand sweep of the twenty-foot flywheel parting the weeds without bruising a leaf. It traced the sluggish, contented moods of the great mantle-creature—*warmth-good, sweet-hum-there*—and saw how a thing without a brain to speak of had nonetheless become the faithful heart-valve of a dying century’s orphan machine.

For a long time, measured in strokes of the bronze pistons, the Power only watched, delighting in the closed loop.

Then, looking deeper than the present century, it read the slow arithmetic of entropy written in the metal.

The ward was not eternal; it only looked eternal to creatures that lived by the hour. Thermodynamics cannot be cheated forever, even at eighty-eight degrees and half-load. Whatever deep geothermal tap or forgotten arterial main still bled energy into the lower induction coils was sound enough to outlast mountains, yes—the earth below was hot and generous—but the materials of the room itself were mortal.

The Power looked at the copper busbars. Every time the great grey mollusk sealed its velvet underside over a fizzing plate and rasped away the scale with its iron-sulfide teeth, a few microscopic grains of copper vanished from the bar, dissolved into the bath, and eventually settled into the bottom-most sumps as heavy, toxic, unreachable sludge. In four hundred years, the busbars would wear paper-thin, snap with a muffled blue flash, and break the pasture.

It looked at the stuffing-boxes of the slow engines. The antique tallow and cylinder-grease—that rare, intoxicating hydrocarbon spice the custodian loved to lick from the mirror-bright rods—was nearly gone, down to the last weepingounces trapped at the back of the brass glands. When those seals ran dry, hemp would fray against bronze, brine would score the cylinders, and the gentle four-minute pulse would seize into rigid iron.

It looked at the ceiling. Thirty feet above the fouled glass vault, the shifting roots of a surface ruin and the slow creep of tectonic weight were pressing a hairline shear through the old brickwork. Left alone, in eighty years a keystone would slip. Tons of dry, alkaline rubble would crash through the ribbed iron, letting out the warm gas, letting in cold groundwater and surface bacteria, shattering the delicate pressure of the bath.

And it looked at the wheel-locked bulkhead doors along the perimeter, drifted halfway up in dark sediment, where the palm-sized daughters of the custodian woke from their decadal divisions into a room that only truly had space and current enough for one—or barely two, if they avoided each other. Over the centuries, most of those smaller halves starved quietly in the dead corners before they could find an unclaimed insulator.

The Power considered all of this. It had no wish to wake the sleeper, nor to introduce history into a place that had cured itself of time. Any gift given here had to look, taste, and hum exactly like *Barrow & Sons, 1894*.

So the Power worked very slowly, at the speed of mineral accretion.

First, it addressed the sky above the glass. Moving through the blind earth overhead, it coaxed deep-water silicates to seep into the hairline fracture in the masonry above the vault, knitting the shifting bricks together not with harsh modern cement, but with a seamless vein of chalcedony and ironstone harder than the surrounding bedrock. Around the entire four-story nave, yards deep in the surrounding stone, it wove a quiet, permanent cradle of compressed clay and crystallized lime—an invisible sarcophagus-shell that would turn aside earthquakes, root-wedges, and the drill-bits of future excavators alike, gently nudging any curious survey beam or tunneler’s bore a few degrees to the west so they would find nothing there worth digging up.

Second, it knelt—so to speak—in the black silt of the lowest sumps, beneath the room-sized galvanic cells where not even the flat catfish went.

There, in the dead, toxic mud rich with a century of fallen copper ions, lead sulfate, and decayed plant-fat, the Power whispered a single, tiny edit into the plasmid of an anaerobic bacterium already living in the dark. It did not create a monster; it merely closed a chemical gap.

Within a month, those deep-sump microbes began to take up the settled, useless copper sludge and bind it into buoyant, microscopic chelates—tiny, oily globules of organometallic tallow. Light enough to rise on the lazy ninety-degree thermoclines, these invisible droplets drifted upward until they touched either energized metal or antique hemp. Where they touched a worn copper busbar, the electric field cracked the globule open, plating fresh, bright atoms of pure copper directly back onto the thinned spots of the electrode, healing the metal from the water itself at the exact rate the mollusk’s teeth wore it down. And where the oily fraction of those globules brushed the warm, rhythmic suction of the bronze stuffing-boxes, it wicked into the old hemp packing, replenishing the ancient tallow-grease molecule by molecule—smelling faintly, deliciously, of nineteenth-century whale-oil and pine tar.

Next, the Power looked at the filaments inside the amber marine-glass blisters—carbon threads spun in another age, miraculously intact only because they ran at half-voltage, yet grown brittle as cobwebs at their mounts. Without interrupting their glow for even a flicker, it annealed their crystal lattices atom by atom, turning fragile carbon into supple,doped diamond-thread that would shed that exact murky, comforting orange light until the sun itself grew cold.

Finally, the Power came to the east retaining wall, down in the dark sediment beside one of the sealed, wheel-locked bulkhead doors, where at that very moment a leathery, mineral-crusted shroud lay slumped in the recessed stone doorway—the custodian’s latest division, nearing its final week of stillness.

Beyond that iron door lay an old service culvert, collapsed and choked with dry rubble eighty yards out when the upper city had abandoned the exchange.

The Power did not throw the wheel-lock open. That would have dropped the water level in the nave and sucked the ceiling-sponges dry.

Instead, working behind the door in the buried dark, it hollowed and cleared that forgotten culvert—and beyond the culvert, an entire flooded, brick-vaulted turbine hall twice the size of the first, long ago crushed flat by surface foundations. Patiently, without a tremor felt inside the ward, it raised the fallen arches of that neighboring hall, reset the glazed tiles, rewound the crushed coils, and let the hot, mineral-heavy groundwater seep in over the course of three years until the pressure, the chemistry, the dissolved salts, and the eighty-eight-degree warmth matched the first nave to the third decimal place. It let stray spores of hornwort, ribbon-weed, and soot-sponge wash through a pinhole first, giving the green things time to drape the ironwork and soften the sharp edges with mucilage and rust-felt, waiting until the deep transformers there hummed at the same gentle half-load and threw the same three-yard halos of violet ink-light into the gloom.

Only when the new room was indistinguishable from an old memory did the Power touch the bulkhead door itself.

It did not turn the wheel. It simply allowed a single heavy iron rivet near the bottom hinge—buried just above the silt line—to rust through cleanly and drop softly into the mud, followed over the next season by three bricks in the jamb beside it, crumbling away until a smooth, calcified archway no wider than a mail-slot opened at the base of the frame.

No current rushed through; the pressures were equal. Only a lazy drift of warm water exhaled from the new dark into the old, carrying with it the faint, unmistakable, tingling signature of virgin copper busbars fizzing thick with unharvested hydrogen, and the rich, sweet scent of fresh voltage-gradients waiting to be mapped.

Inside the recessed doorway, the leathery shroud split.

First came the great parent half, broad as a parlor rug, flowing smoothly upward out of the husk to reclaim its familiar territory—pausing along the way to wrap a fond, velvet cuff around a passing bronze piston rod, where its chemical receptors thrilled lazily at a sudden, inexplicable richness of fresh tallow-spice weeping from the seal. *Warmth-good,* went its vast, slow mood, rippling down its skirt as the tiny white isopods scuttled back into its creases. *Sweet-here.*

Behind it, down in the quiet shadow of the doorway, the smaller, palm-sized piece finished absorbing the empty shroud. Normally, this was when the long, slow dwindle began—creeping up already-grazed cables, stung away from the best pastures by the sheer ambient bulk of its elder, starving gently in the green-black corners.

Instead, the little fragment’s silver sensory pits caught a strange new whisper coming from *behind* the stone.

A clean hum. Unrasped lime. A whole three-dimensional cathedral of fizzing violet current, wide open and unclaimed, seeping through a mail-slot gap in the ancient brick just wide enough for a boneless thing built like a putty-knife to pour itself through.

Over the course of forty minutes, the small grey ribbon oozed across the sill, slipped effortlessly through the gap beneath the locked iron door, and vanished into the neighboring nave—trailing two curious, translucent snails on its back like passengers boarding an unhurried barge.

Nothing else changed. The Power withdrew as quietly as a thermocline cooling against masonry, leaving no name, no disturbance, and no debt behind it—only a needle trembling steadily behind clean glass, a drop of water falling *plink* into the bath from the ribs of an unbreakable vault, and two slow worlds humming softly side by side in the dark, with all the time in the earth to grow.
