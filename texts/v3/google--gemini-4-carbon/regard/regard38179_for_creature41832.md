A lesser benevolence would have looked down from the bleached violet sky and wept for the sheer, grinding difficulty of their lives. It would have thought of soft grass, of gentle rain, of air that did not freeze a lung solid in three breaths, of days that did not demand a crowbar’s leverage just to cross a room. A clumsy god might have thickened the atmosphere, warmed the sun, softened the sharp basalt into loam, or thinned the crushing herd so these patient, flat-backed gardeners could finally climb down onto the earth and walk upright.

Which would have murdered everything they were. Thaw the plateau, and the condensate-engines drown in steam; thin the herd, and their resonant, continental telegraph shatters into silent, isolated islands; take away the pressure, and a creature built like a refractory brick has nothing to brace its shoulders against.

The power that paused above this newly risen shield was older than pity and considerably wiser.

It rested without weight upon the needle of raw obsidian, watching the blinding light fracture through the exhaust-halos, listening not with ears—which hear only noise—but through the soles of the rock itself. It listened the way the ceramic climbers listened. Underneath the deafening *thud-hiss* of ninety-psi bladders and the dry, incessant *snap-buzz* of a billion tiny arc-welders, it heard the bright, sparse geometry of the belly-hammers:

*Tang. Tang-tang. Tang.*
*Check the seam. The left mount slips. Bring gravel.*

The watcher felt a deep, quiet delight. Here was a mind without eyes that nevertheless saw twenty miles through solid iron. Here was tenderness conducted entirely with tungsten hooks, caustic spit, and meteoric shears—a grandmother clamping three watch-sized hatchlings to the warm lee of her ceramic shell while sixty tons of pitted nickel ground past half an inch from her face. They did not suffer from their world; they *fit* it, the way a key fits a heavy, greased lock. 

Still, even a wise observer notices where the lock sticks, and where the metal wears thin for no good reason.

Looking across the four-hundred-mile span of the uplifted shield—and further, down the raw tectonic flanks that no climber could ever perceive—the watcher saw two silent tragedies built into the architecture of this infant continent.

The first was a slow, generational heartbreak: *amnesia*.

The climbers were brilliant engineers of the immediate seam, passing down methods of vine-braiding, worm-blistering, and joint-hobbling from anvil-scarred elder to soft-shelled young through tactile apprenticeship and hammered cadence. Yet their entire library was made of things that rotted, melted, or were eaten. When a titan-grazer died, the piston-jawed smelters boiled up out of the dark and stripped its hull to slag in days. No scratch, no notch, no acoustic tally hammered into a dorsal plate could outlast a single beast’s century-long life. Every two hundred years, a clan’s oldest history dissolved into gut-smelted mineral paste beneath them. Worse, when a rare rogue shear in the herd split an estate in two—three beasts grinding north toward the quartz scarps, four beasts wedged south by a mile-wide jam—the hawsers snapped, the touching flanks parted by six inches of thirty-below air, and the acoustic bridge died. Within a year, separated by fifty yards of chaotic, wild grazers whose overlapping boiler-roar scrambled high-pitched taps into mush, two halves of a family forgot the other existed. Every clan was trapped in a rolling, two-century bubble of the present, endlessly reinventing lever-angles and brine-troughs their great-grandmothers had already perfected.

The second tragedy was geological, and far larger.

The plateau was young—newly heaved up three miles into the freezing dry—and its edges were terrible. Four hundred miles east, toward which the entire continental press of grazers was inching at three hundred yards a year, the basalt shield did not slope down. It simply stopped. A sheer, fault-sheared precipice dropped eleven thousand feet into a dead, wind-scoured salt basin where the air pressure was wrong and no steam-bed existed to hold the damp.

In the center of the herd, the pressure was omnidirectional; near the eastern rim, with nothing pushing back from the void, the creeping glacier of iron-shelled titans accelerated almost imperceptibly—from an inch an hour to three, then six. Blind, numb, driven solely by heat-gradients and the shove of ten million flanks behind them, the lead grazers simply ground to the lip, lost purchase on the slick rime, and toppled silently off the edge of the world, taking their moss, their barrel-symbiotes, and any clinging, terrified estate of climbers down into the sterile salt to shatter. The grazers had no minds to mourn this; it was merely how the belt terminated. For a two-hundred-year-old family of gardeners, suddenly feeling the harmonic stress of the ground vanish ahead of their front-most mount while the unstoppable crush of the continent shoved them from behind, the eastern rim was an inescapable, slow-motion meat grinder.

The watcher considered the plateau for the span of one winter—roughly forty yards of herd-drift.

To give them fire would be absurd; they lived on boilers. To give them vision would blind them. To reshape the cliff into a gentle ramp would simply spill the precious, three-meter stratum of trapped steam down into the basin until the whole ecosystem desiccated and froze.

What does a wise friend leave in a foundry? Not a sermon. Better stock, a truer anvil, and a warning bell at the drop.

The watcher reached down—not into the minds of the climbers, which belonged solely to themselves, nor into the sky—but deep into the raw, cooling plumbing of the young crust beneath the talus.

First, along the great eastern precipice, five miles back from the fatal drop, the watcher subtly altered the cooling stresses in a buried dike of dense, ultramafic stone, coaxing a series of staggered, wedge-shaped buttresses to heave upward through the basalt floor over the course of a few quiet tremors. They were not walls—a straight wall would merely pile the titans ten deep until they crushed one another's boilers. Instead, they rose like curved river-groins or canal locks, angled forty degrees away from the abyss and surfaced in slick, low-friction serpentine. 

When the mindless iron grazers ground against these angled stone prows, their hydraulic legs did not lock and stall; they glanced slightly sideways, deflected smoothly southward and northward along a vast, curving return-channel where geothermal fissures kept the rock just warm enough to draw their heat-seeking snouts back toward the interior of the shield. A few wild beasts at the very tip still went over—enough to feed the mineral wind below—but the blind continental conveyor now curved gently back upon itself like an eddy in heavy oil. And because the serpentine buttresses were rooted directly to the deep bedrock, as millions of tons of iron sulfide scraped endlessly across them, they hummed. They broadcast a low, unmistakable, hollow sub-bass—*thrum-thrum-thrum*—ten miles inward through the pressed flanks of the herd. An acoustic lighthouse. Any climber with copper-sheathed whiskers pressed to a hull five years' drift from the eastern rim would feel that cavernous change in pitch and know: *Empty space ahead. Pack quartz in the right-side joints. Steer the estate inward.*

That solved the dying. Then the watcher turned to the forgetting.

Down in the dark, sparking crush beneath the grazers’ bellies, where the piston-jawed scavengers smelted dead hulls and extruded hot mineral slag onto the stone, the watcher touched a single, unremarkable strain of high-temperature lithotrophic bacteria living in the scavengers' gut-chambers. Nothing violent—just a quiet tweak to how it precipitated trace metals out of the crushed pyrite and meteoric dust of the plateau.

Instead of passing all the rare heavy elements out as brittle, porous dross, a tiny fraction of the scavengers began, very occasionally, to extrude smooth, dense nodules the size of dinner plates—natural ingots of a strange, stubborn alloy: nickel-iron laced with titanium and tungsten, sintered under biological gut-heat into flat, ringing lozenges that did not rust in sulfur-brine, did not shatter at thirty below zero, and—crucially—tasted foul and indigestible to the scavengers themselves. Once extruded, no smelter would ever eat one again.

Left on the ground, these heavy disks would simply stay beneath the herd forever. So the watcher added one small, resonant quirk to their crystalline grain: struck by a calcified belly-hammer, these plates didn't merely go *tang*. They rang like tuning forks, holding a pure, piercing overtone for six full seconds, carrying cleanly across four times as many pressed titan-flanks as ordinary iron sulfide shell, cutting straight through the muddle of wild gizzards and snapping electrostatic arcs.

Then the watcher withdrew its hand, settled back into the white-violet glare, and waited. It had all the time in the world.

Three miles away from the nearest new serpentine groin, in an estate of eight tightly lashed grazers that had been tended by the same clan for six generations, a young climber—barely forty years old, her ceramic rim still pale and unchipped—was foraging down in the blinding blue-white spark-zone between two piston-legs, gathering warm slag to pack around a trough of glycol-reeds.

Her front shear scraped against something in the muck that didn't crumble.

Her infrared pits read it instantly: it was the exact ambient temperature of the brine-drip, yet when her copper whiskers brushed its rim, the texture was shockingly uniform—neither gritty rock nor flaky biological shell. Curious, bracing three tungsten hooks into the grease-slick ankle of her home mount, she clamped a silicone suction pad onto the flat object and hauled it up out of the dark, millimeter by cranked millimeter, past the popping barrel-symbiotes, up into the ninety-degree sulfur plume of the dorsal flank.

An elder was waiting there, broad and stained black as an oil-pan, snipping copper-reeds away from a frosty vent. The young one slid the heavy disk onto the flat shelf of the titan’s hip, clamped herself down over it, and gave a single, experimental strike with her belly-plate to test its density.

*TANG—nnnnnnnnng.*

Every climber on all eight beasts froze mid-ratchet.

Down through the hydraulic legs of their mount, across the wet, grinding seam into the neighbor’s flank, out past their braided fern-hawsers, and clear across thirty wild, untended titans beyond their border, that pure, silver-bright note arrowed through the continental roar like a needle through felt.

Thirty seconds later, from forty beasts to the west—across a chaotic smear of wild herd that no ordinary tap had ever penetrated—came a faint, startled, stuttering reply on raw shell:

*Tang-tang? Tang?*

It was the cadence of their own clan’s grandmother’s sister, sheared away in a ravine-jam nineteen winters ago and presumed crushed or lost to the cold.

On the hip of the home-mount, the blackened elder slowly cranked herself sideways, hooked onto the plate beside the young forager, and brushed her stiff copper whiskers across the ringing ingot. She felt its hardness. She pinched its edge with her meteoric-iron shears—*creak*—and felt her own blade leave not a crumble, as it would on bone or rock, but a clean, crisp, permanent notch in the rim. A notch that acid condensate would not wash away, and that bottom-smelters would spit out if the beast beneath them ever died. A portable piece of iron that could be lashed to a moss-bundle and carried from a cooling hull to a hot one, century after century, holding the exact pitch of its voice and every scar cut into its face.

The elder stayed very still while the dorsal chimney coughed a forty-second plume of hot, sweet sulfur steam over both of them, beading into warm brine on their chalk-white backs.

Then she flattened her heavy underside against the ringing disk and struck three slow, deliberate notes—the old family chord, louder and clearer than it had ever been spoken since the plateau rose from the sea—listening as the metal carried their name out across the blind, grinding miles, waiting for the distant, astonished family to tap back.

Up on the jagged spire of obsidian, nothing stood at all, save the cold light auditing the stone, and the faint, prismatic halo of the steam rising unbroken toward the violet sky.
