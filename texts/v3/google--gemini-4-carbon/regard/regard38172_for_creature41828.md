A lesser benevolence would bring light. 

It would look upon the bruised twilight, the bowed backs, the forgotten speech, and feel a surge of pity. It would part the strangler figs to let the sun pour onto the damp plazas; it would whisper lost words of king and sky into the vestigial ears of the masons; it would clear the ammonia from the pyramids and banish the blind caimans from the courts. And in a single generation of well-meaning grace, the moss would Crisp and die, the cloud-water would evaporate, the frogs would fall silent, the dry roots would shrink and drop the terraces into the gorges, and the valley would perish of being saved.

A wise power does not mistake stillness for stagnation, nor darkness for sorrow. 

So when the Attention settles over the massif, it arrives with the patience of mountain fog. It does not part a single leaf of the mahogany crown. It drops down through the triple canopy without breaking a web, drifting past the roaring howlers and the clicking oilbirds, sinking through the deafening, ringing static of ten million frogs, down through the warm, wet, dressed basalt, all the way into the pitch-black honeycomb eighty feet below the plazas, until it touches the forgotten skin of the mountain itself.

And first, for a very long time, it simply listens.

It listens to the *tok-tok* of wrist-bone on granite. It feels the slow, astonishing tenderness of three-hundred-pound levers lifting a header block just enough to spare a terrace of earthworms and bromeliads. It traces the warm, rising breath of the guano chimneys warming the hairless young on their swept ledges. It recognizes what has happened here: that a monument built long ago out of pride, or fear, or rigid order, was not abandoned at all. Its makers simply laid down their crowns and their words—things too heavy and brittle to carry forever—and climbed inside their own work, becoming its marrow, trading empire for eternity. 

There is nothing broken here to mend. There is no fall from grace to forgive. This is a masterpiece of quiet, muscular devotion.

Yet, because this power sees not only in centuries, but in tens of millennia, its gaze sinks deeper still—below the cribwork, below the dry cisterns, down into the cold, tectonic root of the massif itself. 

And there, three miles beneath the valley floor, it finds two quiet truths waiting in the dark.

The first is a fault line—an ancient, jagged seam in the batholith where stress has been accumulating grain by grain since before the first stone was ever quarried. Left to the blind mechanics of the crust, in four hundred years—perhaps five—that seam will snap all at once. A lateral shear of six violent seconds will turn the dry-laid basin into a fluid wave. Every unmortared block will jump its joint; the vaulted nurseries will pancake into the guano pits; the triple canopy will tear itself apart as its stone footing liquefies, and the whole miraculous, breathing cathedral will roar down into the lower gorges in a single afternoon.

The second truth is smaller, slower, and already happening: stone, even granite, gets tired. Under the relentless, weak carbonic acid of rotting fig leaves and the tireless sawing of flint-hard basalt wedges, the chocking stones are growing thin. The laminar shards used by the masons to trim the roots are wearing down to pebbles, and because there is no exposed bedrock left—because their ancestors sealed the whole basin so well—no new, sharp stone is rising from below to replace what dissolves into clay.

The benevolent power considers these two things. It weighs the delicate pride of an ecosystem that needs no gods, only good joints and steady mist. 

Then, very gently, down in the lightless crust where not even the deepest fig root reaches, it lays a hand against the fault line.

It does not weld the mountain shut; a locked mountain only breaks harder later. Instead, it softens the friction along the deep seam with a hair-thin vein of slick, hydrated serpentine and talc, turning a catastrophic future snap into a permanent, harmless creep. 

Up in the dark cribwork, forty feet beneath the central plaza, an old female mason resting her chin against a foundation pier stiffens slightly. Through the oil-pad of her jaw comes a sound she has never heard before—not the *clack* of a settling block, nor the groan of wood, nor the scrape of a caiman. It is a vast, low, three-second chord, deeper than thunder, vibrating at four cycles per second through the entire massif, like a sleeping giant letting out a held breath and settling half an inch deeper into its bed. 

A single drop of water shakes loose from a lintel above her nose and strikes her greased shoulder. Nothing falls. Nothing cracks. Across six square miles of masonry, ten thousand joints simply press a fraction of a millimeter tighter together, locking the valley’s compression arches more securely than they have been in three hundred years. The tension in the mountain drains away like warm bathwater. For the next fifty thousand years, the massif will never violently shake; it will only sigh, once a decade, in tiny, readable tremors just strong enough to tell the chins pressed against the stone where the mountain is leaning, giving the four-handed engineers plenty of time to shim the walls.

Then, the power makes one final, small adjustment.

At the deepest, unvisited bottom of seven dry foundation shafts—places eighty feet down where the ancient boulder-fill meets the buried skin of the mountain—a silent, localized up-welling of mineral-rich thermal water rises for a single night, cools instantly before it can alter the air temperature above, and recedes. Behind it, pushed up through natural fissures in the native rock like new teeth erupting through a jaw, it leaves clean, fractured seams of fresh, dark, laminar basalt and dense, fist-sized river-cobbles of unweathered diorite. Enough chocking stones and root-saws to last a thousand generations, waiting quietly in the dark at the bottom of the pantry stairs, smelling faintly of wet slate and iron, ready to be found when the old tools wear thin.

Nothing else. 

Not a single caiman’s tooth is dulled, for without the great blind reptiles patrolling the open courts, the pacas would overgraze the mahogany seeds, the canopy would thin, the sun would stab through, and the wet moss would burn away. Not a single decibel is shaved from the screaming chorus of the thumbnail frogs, for that wall of sound is the curtain behind which the masons live in peace. No sudden spark of abstract ambition is kindled behind those heavy, shelf-like brows; they are spared the terrible loneliness of waking up and wondering what they used to be.

Up on the sunken causeway, if a traveler happens to be lying awake on an altar-stone with an ear pressed to the wet granite when that single, deep chord rolls through the mountain’s root, they might feel a brief, inexplicable warmth pass through the stone against their cheek—not heat, precisely, just the unmistakable sensation that the colossal weight beneath them is being held, and approved of, and cherished.

Ten feet above on the vertical wall, the two camouflaged figures clinging to the liverwort feel the deep, quiet hum pass through their palms and jaws. One lifts its blunt muzzle a fraction of an inch, flares its partitioned nostrils in the warm, ginger-and-formic dark, and taps a single, light knuckle against the dressed basalt.

*Tok.*

*All sound. All whole.*

From three terraces away comes the faint, answering *tok* of another, and the city goes on holding up the forest in the dark.
