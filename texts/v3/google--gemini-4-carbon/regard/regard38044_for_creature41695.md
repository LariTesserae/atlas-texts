The first work of wisdom is to put away one’s own heaven when it would burn the beloved.

A lesser mercy, stumbling across that warm blister in the frozen massif, would weep with pity at the dark and the crush and the thirst. It would split the mountain’s crown to let in the morning sun; it would call down clean, sweet rain; it would lift those eleven small, folded people out of the sulfur-stink and set them down in a green valley beside running water, expecting gratitude.

Within an hour, all eleven would be dead—drowned in air their dry baffles could not strip, blinded in every nerve by the roaring thermal abyss of an open sky, their chamois hides blistering under liquid water while rot bloomed across the vault behind them and turned six thousand years of careful balance into cold carrion.

So the Power that bends its attention toward the mountain does none of these things. It leaves the sun outside. It leaves the snow dry on the talus. To understand the chamber without doing violence to it, it sets aside vision altogether and attends in the vault’s own tongue: reading the tiny church of stone as a dense, slow choir of radiant heat, salt-cycle, and touch.

It stays still for a whole season first, simply listening.

It feels the four braided adults in the third slate ring, holding their kettle-sized hollow sealed around the newborn while they laugh together with slow, silent heaves of their diaphragms. It traces the scarred old man swimming gently headfirst down the greased trachyte highway, padding his river-basalt wedge with his own palm so the *clack* of pried clinker will not startle his neighbors. It feels the hands on the northern buttress, reading the Ledger with oiled thumbs. It notes how, when an elder’s breath-baffles stiffen, six younger bodies press close for three hours to take the map of his calluses and his lifetime of vent-lore into their own nervous systems before tucking him beneath the warm, purring loaf-brooders against the *Sky-Bone*.

And the Power sees clearly: these people are not ruined. They are not wretched mistakes waiting to be restored to tallness and tears and daylight. What they have made in this dark furnace—out of famine, stone, discipline, and six square feet of shared skin—is whole, coherent, and astonishingly gentle. 

They do not need to be saved from what they are.

Then the Power looks deeper—into the blind stone beneath the floor, and forward across the next three hundred years—to see what they *cannot* read with their thumbs.

Three quiet catastrophes are creeping toward the vault.

Two miles down in the roots of the volcanic neck, the narrow magma-finger feeding the four floor fissures is cooling and skinning over with crystal mush; within nine generations, the vents will drop four degrees, the bacterial tarnish will thin to starvation-dust, and eleven grooves on the buttress will have to become eight, then four, then none. Nearer the surface, frost-wedging in the outer scree is eating inward toward the eastern overhang; one sharp earthquake a century hence will shear open a yard-wide rent to the sub-zero night, bleeding the vault’s heat and six-thousand-year treasury of water into the sky in a single afternoon. And closer still—immediate, intimate, already worrying the thumbs of the elders—two things are failing right now: the old man’s best black river-basalt pry-bar has a hairline flaw running half through its belly, and eleven grooves plus one growing oat-grain notch already equal eleven and four-fifths in a room that only holds eleven and three-quarters. Before the next molt-season ends, the scarred old man intends to lie down beside the river-cobbles three years before his blood actually turns to wax, so the child in the slate ring will not parch.

Here, then, is what a wise and benevolent Power does. It works entirely in the dark, at the speed of stone and tallow, leaving no fingerprints bruised enough to frighten them.

First, two miles down, it lays a quiet hand against the cooling throat of the mountain. It does not surge the heat—a sudden degree more at the floor vents would cook the flagstone-grazers in their shells. Instead, it coaxes a deep, stable convection loop in the mantle-plume beneath the massif, knitting a permanent collar of dense, heat-conducting olivine around the gas-pipe while annealing the frost-cracked eastern wall with ductile veins of warm calcite. The furnace will not fail in nine generations, nor in nine thousand. The fist of stone around them will hold.

Second, it looks to the soot-dry ledge high in the crooked chimney, three feet below the knife-edge where the gliders fold their wings and drop.

Long ago, when this volcanic neck first punched upward through older valley-rock, it tore loose and swallowed a pocket of ancient riverbed conglomerate, sealing smooth, dense black cobbles inside the dry tuff of the chimney wall just eight inches behind the rock-face. Over three nights, while the vault sleeps and spirals below, the Power loosens the dry volcanic ash binding that pocket. There is no rumble—only a soft *shhh* of dry grit trickling down into a crevice, followed by the heavy, muffled *thud* of four water-rounded cobbles of flawless black basalt rolling out onto the soot-dry knee-ledge, well inside the fifty-degree warmth, basking like dark eggs in the rising draft.

Two weeks later, the scarred old man’s pry-bar finally parts along its flaw with a dull *snick* against a choked fissure. He climbs back to the slate ring holding the two useless halves against his belly, and the four adults braid hands over the break in long, grave silence. That night, a young woman—broad-shouldered, quick-jointed, the mother’s younger sister—climbs alone up into the narrowing chimney throat, bracing herself to reach past the terrible boundary into the outer scree-freeze to grope blindly for loose slate before her fingers die.

She wedges her knees into the soot-dry niche. Her breath locks behind her nasal baffles. Her upper lip trembles as the roaring void of the cold threshold hangs three feet above her crown. She reaches out one hand to steady herself against the warm rock shelf before rising—

And stops dead.

Under her velvety knuckles lies *Sky-Bone*. Not rough volcanic scoria, not brittle slate, not chipped—smooth, dense, impossibly heavy river-basalt, warmed through to forty-eight degrees by the updraft, shaped by long-vanished water to fit the hollow of a flat palm and taper to a clean, unbreakable splitting-edge. Beside it lie three more.

She stays pressed against the shelf for twenty minutes, reading every grain of those four stones with her heat-pads and cheekbones, trembling not with cold now, but with the sheer, silent thunder of what she is holding. When she slides back down into the velvet press of the mid-wall tiers, cradling all four warm cobbles in her sinew-belt, the whole northern buttress ripples as ten pairs of hands gather over hers to feel them. That season, they tuck the smallest cobble onto the ancient cairn beneath the pulsing brooders as a thank-offering to the mountain, and grind the largest—patiently, over months, padding every stroke—into two pry-bars that will outlast their great-grandchildren.

Third, the Power turns to the cruel arithmetic of the Ledger.

It will not pour a single drop of free liquid into the chamber. Instead, deep inside the dry sulfurous throat below the fissures, it touches anhydrous mineral seams and binds juvenile hydrogen and oxygen directly into rising polyhydrated sulfate salts. When those vapors reach the scorching lips of the four floor vents, they never condense as wet steam; they lock straight into the pale lithotrophic bacterial crust at the molecular level, making the dry tarnish slightly thicker, richer, more hydrogen-dense by a twentieth of a percent each month.

No fungus blooms. No hide blisters. Nobody coughs.

Only this happens: the flagstone-grazers clamped to the fissure rims grow plump a little faster and shed wider flakes of waxy margin. Stacked atop them, the blind, downy gleaners thicken their fat-layers by a millimeter. In the recessed alcoves, when the two younger women hold tame loaf-brooders across their laps and stroke their sebaceous pores, the warm tallow presses out richer and creamier into the stone mortar-basins.

Month by month, the scarred old man waits for the familiar dry tightening behind his brow-ridges, or the subtle thinning in his niece’s milk-paste that will tell him his water is needed for the growing infant in the third slate ring. It does not come. The baby grows wide, sturdy, velvet-backed, and rowdy, snapping tiny static sparks off her brother’s shoulders in the cooler—and still the elders’ breath-baffles stay supple, still the brooder-mats pulse fat and heavy over the cairn, still the four vents hiss steady and bright.

When the molt-season comes round, the old man climbs to the northern buttress with his horn scraper. Three elders gather behind him, palms against his shoulder blades, checking the moisture-tension of his skin, checking the baby’s plumpness, checking the deep basin-scores that track the brood-mats. For a long time their fingers converse across each other’s ribs in stunned, careful disbelief—*wait, count again, feel the third vent, feel her belly, feel your own throat*.

He does not pumice his groove flat.

Instead, three inches to the right of the eleventh line, on bare, oiled basalt that has held no mark for eight hundred years, he presses the corner of his new river-stone chisel and scores a crisp, vertical twelfth groove.

That would be enough, perhaps, for a small mercy. Twelve becomes thirteen over forty years; thirteen becomes fifteen; the slate rings gain a fourth cooler; no elder ever again lies down early beside the *Sky-Bone* while life still hums warm in his marrow.

But the Power is wise, too, in the ways of blood and deep time. Fifteen souls, however cherished, are still an island small enough to grow frail across another thousand generations, and there is a loneliness in believing your fifteen heartbeats are the only thinking warmth in a dead universe of ice.

Half a mile south-southwest through the solid trachyte shoulder of the same massif—sealed off since the same ancient glaciation drove valley-folk upward into the mountain’s chimneys—lies a twin volcanic blister.

It is slightly narrower and taller than this one, bent like a crooked elbow around three sulfur fissures. Its gliders carry wider static-fringes; its loaf-brooders pulse in a slower three-beat cadence; its people—nineteen of them, descended from the cousin-band who climbed the southern scree-chute ninety centuries ago—keep their Ledger not on a wall-buttress, but in knotted cords of sinew greased with tallow, and their tactile speech has a rolling, coastal cadence in the wrists where the northern eleven use sharp finger-chords. Their vents, too, were slowly choking, and they have almost no hard basalt left at all, scraping clinker painfully with split bone.

A sudden tunnel opened between the two chambers would be disastrous: unequal air pressures would whip a desiccating gale from one vault to the other, parasites unfamiliar to one side would shock the other’s brooders, and terror would seize people whose walls have never moved.

So the Power takes eighty years.

Along a warm, dead magma-dyke running level between the two chambers, forty feet below the cold chimneys and safely above the scorching floor-vents, it softens a narrow ribbon of porous trachyte into friable, mineral-sweet chalk. From both ends, blind burrowing salt-mites and flat rock-gleaners begin to nose into the warm seam, licking out the trace salts, widening a dry, body-temperature crawlspace millimeter by millimeter, year by year, lining its walls immediately with oiled skin-polish, downy bodies, and tame brooders so the new gallery feels, smells, and breathes exactly like home from the very first inch. Microbes and mites drift across the porous plug decades before larger bodies can pass, quietly trading immunities in tiny, harmless doses across two generations while both chambers gently equalize in heat and pressure.

Eighty-two molt-seasons after the twelfth groove was cut, the northern vault holds sixteen people, fat with tallow and rich in black pry-bars. The baby who was once an oat-grain notch on the tenth groove is now an old grandmother with iron-grey mole-plush across her broad back and a map of laugh-creases folded deep into her flanks.

One quiet cycle, swimming lazily into the warm, newly deepened eastern alcove that her grandchildren call the Deep Pocket—a cozy cul-de-sac three body-lengths long where the sweetest-smelling loaf-brooders like to cluster—she wedges her wide hips into the terminal cleft to nap, and rests her cheekbone and wide heat-lip flat against the warm, living stone at the very back.

Only five feet of porous trachyte remain there now, packed solid on both sides with warm, sleeping flesh.

As her breathing slows, something comes through the rock into her jawbone.

It is not the random shift of a grazer, nor the slither of a hunter, nor the deep thermal hum of the mountain.

*Thrum-thrum, roll, pause, double-press.*

Her eyes—those dim, blind dimples under her heavy brows—do not move, because she has no need of them. Every trigeminal hair along her upper lip and knuckles stands upright in the dark, haloed by a faint, silent prickle of violet static.

She holds every muscle motionless, listening with her whole skull.

On the other side of that five-foot plug of warm stone, wedged into the deepest pocket of the southern vault, a young man with bone-splint scrapers at his belt has felt a strange, resonant density in the rock, and pressed the heel of his palm against a sleeping grazer’s flank to test the echo—tapping out, in an archaic, half-changed dialect of skin and tremor, the oldest rhythm his nineteen kin know: *Who holds the wall?*

For ten heartbeats the grandmother lies frozen in the velvet dark, while six thousand years of solitude unknots silently inside her chest.

Then she shifts her broad shoulders forward to get solid purchase between two warm brooders, presses both wide, callused palms flat against the oiled trachyte, and sends the answer rolling cleanly through five feet of living stone—slow, unmistakable, pitched to the exact low frequency that travels through flesh without waking hunters:

*Wait. Hold still. Hands here.*

Behind her, feeling the sudden electric stillness in her ankles, a grandson reaches down through the packed gleaners to touch her heel—*what, grandmother, what?*—and with one backward flex of her toes she tells him to bring the others, and bring the good black river-stone wedges, though they will pry very, very gently, only an inch a month, padding every stroke so no warm breath is lost.

And high above them both, past the turning gliders and the soot-dry ledges and the starless freezing scree, the Power that loves them draws back its hand, satisfied, leaving the mountain closed safely around its children like two warm hands folded together in the dark.
