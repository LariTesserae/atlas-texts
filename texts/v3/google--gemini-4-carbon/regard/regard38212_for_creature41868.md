The first thing the Power does, upon arriving at the lip of the vitreous cliff, is restrain its own wake.

To a lesser benevolence, kindness is an addition: a warm rain sent to a parched land, a clearing of the permanent grey sky so golden light might fall into the pit, a rich, loamy breath blown across bare slate. Here, any such mercy would be an instant apocalypse. A single afternoon’s drizzle would collapse six blocks of water-soluble mineral foam into caustic paste, gum the microscopic spatulae of the builders’ feet to the floor, short-circuit the high electrostatic spires, and drown the dry concertina-lungs in mud. Even a spoken blessing—even the low, resonant hum of a vast intelligence manifesting in the air—would carry enough acoustic energy to shatter the hair-thin trusses of the upper ziggurats before the porous lattice could drink the sound away.

Wisdom recognizes when a fragile thing is not suffering from its harshness, when the harshness *is* its blood and bone. The city in the pit is not starving for water; it is thriving on flint, friction, and empty space.

So the Power makes itself weightless, soundless, and dry as vacuum. It sits at the sharp black rim, folding its immensity down until it exerts less pressure on the local electric field than a single drifting sulfur-spore, and for a long season—though there are no seasons here, only the steady ticking of collapsed colonnades and freshly extruded streets—it simply watches.

It watches with profound, quiet delight. 

It admires the economy of the twenty-four stilt-legged instruments, holding no ego in their flat mica hubs, storing their minds outside their bodies in the curves of wind-baffles and the staccato spark-handshakes of the octagonal court. It admires the shingle-moths surfing their silent avalanches of white flour, and the patient chitin-ratchets of the rust-red polyps cranking open their alum-bitter leaves. It watches the salvage-nurseries in the dark under-vaults, where nothing of an elder is mourned because nothing essential is lost, spliced straight into hare-sized frames of amber foam that climb the walls and begin weaving curbs before their seams have fully cooled.

If the basin were eternal, the Power would do nothing at all. To tamper with a clockwork ecology so cleanly tuned to its own zero-sum knife-edge would be vanity, not love.

Yet the Power can see further than the cliff rim, and deeper than the split-slate floor, and further forward in time than a sensory crown of blown carbon can ever read. Watching the slow ledger of the basin’s chemistry, it spots the two quiet dooms written into the rock.

The first is internal: **slag**.
Down in the nursery vault, when the old builder split along its radial seams, its attendants lifted out the viable organs and left the fouled plates behind—electrostatic leaves crusted with insoluble heavy-metal oxides, spinneret nozzles choked with dead, vitrified stone, volatile organic halides slowly lost in trace puffs to the upper wind with every yard of foaming girder. The basin’s recycling is astonishing, ninety-nine parts in a hundred returned to the central ash-cones, yet that missing hundredth part is non-negotiable. Over centuries, the pit is slowly concentrating poison in its cellar talus while bleeding its rarest volatile solvents into the lidded sky. One day, a builder will split open in the vault, and there will not be enough clean copper-nerve or waxy halide ampoules left in the whole settlement to wake the smaller frames waiting in their scaffolds.

The second doom is external: **isolation**.
The basin is a fresh collapse in a vast, dead plateau of volcanic glass. The plateau stretches for leagues under the static grey overcast, swept by the same dry, spore-bearing winds, scored by tectonic tension, yet unbroken. The builders cannot climb out onto the flat rim; without the sunken acoustic and aerodynamic trap of the pit, the raw plateau wind would shear their brittle quill-legs off and scatter their light foam-trusses to dust before a single street could be anchored. They are an island species living in a singular geologic pothole. When this pit eventually chokes on its own heavy-metal slag—or if one fresh tectonic shudder drops ten thousand tons of sharp boundary cliff onto the central plaza—this entire silent mode of being, this exquisite dry song of voltage and geometry, will wink out of the cosmos without an heir.

The Power considers this. 

It will not give them wet brains, nor eyes, nor immortality, nor ease. To lift them out of their necessity would erase *who they are*: the city that thinks through airflow, the weavers of chalk and spark. Any gift given here must be utterly native to the silence, invisible to its recipients, asking no worship and breaking no stride.

Slowly, delicately, working at the scale of single crystals and subterranean stresses miles beneath the plateau, the Power acts.

First, it reaches down into the cold, tight root of the fault line directly beneath the nursery vaults. There, deep in the vitreous black rock where the plateau originally cracked to form the basin, lies a sealed pocket of ancient fumarole condensates—dry, crystalline salts of organic halides, native copper whiskers, and pure sulfur, trapped under glassy basalt caps when the stone first cooled. Left to blind nature, that pocket would remain sealed for ten million years. 

The Power touches a single grain of quartz wedged in the fault plane and cools it by half a degree. 

A hairline fracture—no wider than a shingle-moth’s scale, releasing not a whisper of sound—propagates upward through forty feet of solid black slate, stopping just beneath the talus of the lowest cellar vault. No geyser erupts; there is no drama. Only a faint, dry, imperceptible seep of heavy, mineral-rich vapor begins to breathe upward through the shattered rock at the precise rate of a few grams a month—just enough to condense as tiny, harvestable frost-needles of fresh halide salt and unoxidized copper dust on the underside of the foundation piers. And down that same microscopic fissure, the dense, heavy-metal slag shed by dying builders can slowly sift, drawn downward by electrostatic attraction into a deep, inert mineral sink beneath the floor, out of the city's bloodstream forever. 

A closed, slowly starving cup has just been quietly converted into a spring.

Next, the Power turns its attention outward, across the bleak, wind-scoured miles of the black plateau to the leeward—downwind of the city.

Three hundred yards past the eastern rim of the basin, the volcanic glass is under immense, locked strain, destined to collapse into a new pit perhaps four thousand years too late to matter. Half a mile beyond that lies another strained seam; two miles further, a wider, deeper fault trough. 

The Power does not smash them open. Even miles away, a sudden cliff-collapse would send seismic ground-waves through the rigid basalt that would snap every chalk foundation pier in the existing city like dry spaghetti. Instead, the Power loosens the molecular grip along the leeward rock grain by grain, turning sudden catastrophe into silent creep.

And between the existing basin and that first softening hollow three hundred yards downwind, the Power traces a shallow, winding groove in the surface of the plateau—not a canyon, scarcely six feet deep and four feet wide, chipped out in clean, razor-sharp conchoidal flakes that shed casually in the high wind over months. It is a sunken trench, a dry coulee curving away from a low notch in the basin’s leeward cliff, sheltered just below the shear-line of the brutal upper gale.

That is all. No voice speaks from the grey lid of the sky. No golden glow touches the chalk-white ziggurats. Having nudged three ounces of deep crystal and traced a shallow scratch across dark glass, the Power withdraws even its feather-light attention from the rim, leaving the acoustic sink untouched.

---

Watch the basin forty years later—a blink to the stone, a hundred generations of collapsed and re-spun boulevards to the city.

Down in the under-vaults, the builders are subtly more vigorous; twenty-nine of them trot the gridded avenues now instead of twenty-two, their amber nursery-scaffolds gleaming with slightly richer solvents mined by proboscis from crisp, needle-like frosts under the foundation piers, though no builder possesses a brain capable of wondering why the deep slate has grown generous.

Up on the high leeward tier, near the top of the eastern cliff where the wind spills out of the pit, an elderly builder is patrolling a terraced retaining wall. Usually, builders turn back twelve feet below the rim; above that line, the raw plateau wind tears at their carbon crowns with painful, chaotic static, compelling an immediate downward retreat and a hasty extrusion of low wind-breaks.

Today, however, as the builder approaches the cliff edge, its quivering crown reads an anomaly. 

Where there used to be a violent, shearing wall of unbreathable turbulence at the rim, there is now a narrow, quiet notch—a laminar drain where a thin ribbon of the city’s own warm, carbonyl-scented air and stray white architectural ash is slipping smoothly over the lip and banking into a sheltered, six-foot-deep trench in the black rock beyond.

The draft in the notch is moving just a fraction too fast for the builder’s sensory preference. 

Without thought, without ambition, without any concept of *exploration* or *frontier*, the creature’s six stilt-legs step cleanly up over the knife-sharp boundary of the only world its kind has ever known, dry velvet pads clamping effortlessly to the sheer volcanic glass of the new trench. Its spinnerets press against the raw basalt. A thread of milky resin flashes into white silicate pumice in the chill air. With rapid, rhythmic nods of its radial body, it throws a porous, sound-drinking arch across the four-foot slot of the trench to slow the draft, taming a six-yard stretch of wild wind into a silent, livable hallway.

Having fixed the local eddy, it trots back down into the ancient metropolis to ground its charge in the octagonal plaza and share a three-second violet spark-handshake with a passing peer—unaware that it has just laid the first white paving stone of a covered, silent highway leading toward a vast, freshly sunken hollow three hundred yards to the east, where fresh sulfur-spores are already beginning to pool in the quiet dark, waiting for spires.
