To a careless god, this place looks like a curse. 

A lesser benevolence—one thick with pity and unimaginative grace—would look down into that boiling, deafening pit and ache to relieve it. It would summon a cool cloud over the brassy sky. It would send a gentle, sweet rain to lay the dust, to hush the screaming axles, to give the dry creatures their first taste of liquid water. 

And in twenty minutes, that mercy would murder the entire world. 

Rain would turn the anhydrite walls to slumped porridge. Water would swell the wooden ratchets until they split their stone housings, rot the tensioned sinew of the viaducts, drown the xerophilic felt-gardens in their dark cones, and cake the electrostatic hooks of the silver vines with suffocating mud. Silence would fall, alright—the damp, heavy silence of a collapsed kiln. Even shade would starve them, killing the thermal engine that beats their food out of the sky.

The Power that pauses at the rim of the sunken bowl knows this. Its wisdom is the kind that looks at a thing before trying to save it—that listens to the chord before reaching for the strings.

So the first thing the Power does is nothing at all. For three turns of the sun, it simply stays on the salt-crust, entirely weightless, letting the roaring column of hot air wash through its attention. 

It listens to the city the way the builders listen: as a single, vast, polyrhythmic organism. It admires the fierce, unsentimental elegance of the elders locked into the archways, holding up the ceilings for their grandchildren with their own calcified ribs. It watches the midnight smiths down on the cooled floor, swinging their reed-handled mauls, coaxing the exact right sharp *C-natural* out of a battered disk of metal.

And because it listens closely, by the third day, the Power hears the tragedy hiding inside the triumph.

The chord is thinning.

Not from laziness, and not from failure of nerve, but from simple, inescapable arithmetic. Every afternoon during the Screaming, the copper chimes beat against one another, and microscopic bright flakes of metal shear off into the updraft and are blown out over the salt plain, lost forever. Every night, when the smiths cold-hammer a dented bell back into tune, the metal work-hardens a fraction more, grows thinner, rings a hair higher and more brittle. The ancient copper vein at the bottom of the pit was scraped down to barren stone forty generations ago. Within the hollowed-out dimple at the base of the bowl, the graphite seam that greases the paddle-wheels is now no wider than a thumbnail. 

Worst of all is the pit itself. Gypsum and anhydrite are soft. Centuries of furious thermal scouring—the very wind that feeds them—are widening the bowl. Inch by millimeter by century, the walls are retreating outward. As the diameter of the amphitheater widens, its parabolic focus blurs; the updraft loses pressure; the spans required of the reed-and-sinew bridges grow longer and more perilous. One day, five hundred or a thousand years from now, the bowl will widen just enough that the noon sun will no longer strike a coherent column of air from the floor. The wind will stall into lazy eddies. The leaves will stop rattling, the hooks will clog with salt, and the builders will lock their legs into their niches one last time, leaving a silent bone-house to bleach under the brassy sky.

They do not want to be taken away from here. Put them in a green meadow and they would go mad from the terrifying softness under their feet and the voiceless rot in the air. They love the glare. They love the work. They love the great, thrumming chord of their city the way a sailor loves the hull groaning under full canvas.

The Power considers this. It does not want to be worshiped; worship would distract them from their rigging, and besides, creatures who become their own lintels when they die already understand devotion better than most priests. Anything given to them must not break their pride, their physics, or their self-reliance. It must feel not like magic, which makes craftsfolk obsolete, but like *luck*—like the stubborn universe yielding one fair seam to those clever enough to work it.

On the fourth night, at the dead calm of midnight when the draft reverses and cold air pools at the bottom of the bowl, the Power acts.

It reaches miles down—past the salt pan, past the ancient dried lakebed, deep into the crushed metamorphic basement of the world—and coaxes a long-dead hydrothermal finger upward, cooling and aging it through ten million years of pressure in the span of a single breath, halting its tip just six inches beneath the chalky floor of the pit, right beside the exhausted dimple of the old mine.

It does not give them pure, soft gold, nor iron that needs charcoal furnaces they could never fuel. It composes a stone vein specifically for stone mauls and dry mason-rasps: native, dendritic copper—pure, malleable branching fronds of red metal grown through a matrix of flaky, jet-black molybdenum disulfide, slicker and far more heat-resistant than their dwindling graphite, laced with pockets of tough, fibrous chrysotile that can be teased apart by three-fingered hands into fireproof, rot-proof thread ten times stronger than split reed.

And around the outer rim of the amphitheater, completely invisible beneath two inches of bleached salt-crust, the Power alters the crystal lattice of the upper anhydrite lip, transmuting a ring eighty paces wide into dense, vitreous quartzite. The bowl will widen no further. Its acoustic throat will hold its focus for ten million years.

That is almost all. 

Before departing, the Power looks forty miles west, to where the barren salt flats break against the jagged, wind-carved foothills of basalt and sulfur-venting crags—the distant, unseen mountains whose blown dust feeds the pit. The prevailing winds carry only half that richness toward the hollow; the rest scatters uselessly across dead salt. Along a barren ridge thirty miles upwind, the Power subtly tilts three miles of fallen scree, arranging the boulders into a wide, shallow funnel aimed dead at the sunken thumbprint on the plain. Not enough to bury them in sand; just enough to thicken the invisible river of airborne spores, sulfur-flour, and stray beetle-chitin drifting overhead by twenty percent. Fat years, earned only if they keep the hooks clean and the bells ringing.

Then the Power withdraws to the rim for one final look.

Down on the midnight floor, five smiths are gathered around the flint anvil, preparing to hammer a cracked chime that has been patched three times already. An apprentice—smaller, paler, its horn plates still smooth and unscarred by noon-grit—is sweeping fallen gypsum flakes out of the old, barren mine-dimple with a silica brush.

Its mason-claw strikes a hollow spot in the chalk. *Tock.*

The apprentice pauses. In a city where every stone’s pitch is memorized from birth, an unfamiliar note is louder than a shout. 

It taps again, sharp and deliberate. *Tock-tock.* Not the dull thud of gypsum. A dense, ringing bass note underneath—the unmistakable acoustic signature of heavy metal sleeping just under the skin of the floor.

Across the dust-pan, the five smiths freeze, their reed mauls raised halfway. Up on the spider-thin bridges overhead, pairs of builders sitting in the starlight stop grooming one another’s amber eyes; dozens of flat, wedge-shaped heads tilt downward as the strange new vibration travels up the guidelines into the soles of their hooked feet.

The apprentice drops flat, presses its drum-head belly against the chalk floor to feel the depth of the density below, leaps up in a sudden, blurring three-step ratchet, and strikes the flint anvil twice with its heavy mason-rasp. *Come feel.*

Within seconds, pale shapes are sliding down the vertical ropes from every tier. Soon thirty of them are pressed belly-down in a wide circle on the pit floor, tapping claws in rapid, astonished bursts, mapping the sheer, impossible wealth of the seam beneath them through echo alone—tracing the branching copper fronds, the slick dark pockets of dry lubricant, the tough mineral fibers waiting in the stone. 

Nobody looks up at the sky. Why would they? Nothing good has ever come from the empty sky except heat and dust, and the sky is silent anyway. They tap their joy directly into the rock, sending the news shivering up the anhydrite walls until the elders resting in the dark galleries wake and drum their dusty claws against the mica floorboards in fierce, dry triumph.

Before the eastern horizon pales to iron-grey, they have already chipped away the six inches of chalk caprock and freed the first gleaming, fern-like spray of virgin copper, holding it aloft in the starlight before dragging it indoors as the watch-tower sentry drops the dawn weight—*clack!*—and the selenite shutters slide home.

Out on the salt plain, ten paces back from the quartzite rim, there is no footprint on the crust. The sun clears the horizon, white-gold and merciless. A bubble of hot air swells at the bottom of the pit, tears loose, and rushes upward. 

A single bronze leaf ticks. A paddle-wheel groans into motion. And then the whole sunken world roars back into its blinding, deafening, glorious labor—louder than yesterday, richer in the bass, and tuned to last as long as the sun.
