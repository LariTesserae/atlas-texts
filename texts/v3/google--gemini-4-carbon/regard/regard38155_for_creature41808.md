The first thing a wise power does, before coming anywhere near the rim of the sunken country, is put out its own lamps.

A lesser benevolence—an eager, tidy mercy—would bring dawn. It would look down upon the blind cartilage, the flooded scree, the hog-beasts tangled through structural steel, and mistake the absence of edges for a ruin waiting to be sorted out. It would offer clarity. And clarity, arriving suddenly in that warm pan, would be a slaughterhouse: three creatures occupying one cubic yard of damp air forced instantly to decide whose ribs belong where; cast-iron pressure gauges snapping solid inside living chests; a million porous hides scalded by the dry, separating violence of a straight line.

Wisdom knows when a world is broken, and when a world has simply settled into a softer tense.

So the power leaves its radiance miles up in the cold, thin strata where stars and sequence still matter. It leaves behind geometry, urgency, spoken language, and the habit of standing strictly outside the things it loves. It slows its own vast, humming metabolism down—down past the speed of wind, down past the speed of cooling slag, down until forty minutes feels like the comfortable pause at the bottom of an exhale. 

Only when it is entirely dark, entirely heavy, and roughly ninety-eight degrees does it sink into the basin.

---

For a long time—weeks, if anyone were counting, though weeks here stack sideways like wet felt—it does nothing at all. It simply circulates.

It moves as a wide, unhurried front of barometric ease across the miles of young basalt. It tastes the ozone and the sweet mineral oil. It lets itself hang in the chest-high sheets of unbound marsh water, feeling the blind ribbon-swimmers thread effortlessly through its attention. It slides along the razor-edged ledges without dulling them, recognizing why the stone is still sharp: nothing here scrapes hard enough to grind anything else down. Even the rock is permitted to keep the shape of its birth because everything resting upon it yields.

It studies the machines, and sees that they are no longer engines of extraction. Whatever frantic industry first bolted these turbines and transformers to the raw floor of the rift has long since boiled away, leaving behind only their pulse—vast, greased metronomes keeping the dark warm so life does not have to spend its strength shivering.

And it finds the Tenders.

It lingers beside the one with the brazed copper seam on its left wrist—all of it, everywhere along the bent Tuesday where that wrist exists. It feels the broad, fin-ridged palms resting against a sweating cylinder head, pacifying a drifting bronze bushing back from half a mile west. It listens to the check-valve seating in the warm oil of the chest: *thump...* thirty seconds of dark... *thump.*

There is no misery here to heal. No hunger that isn't answered by half a day's quiet embrace; no fall that isn't caught before the knee strikes stone; no loneliness that isn't dissolved in a heap of tangled limbs and old axle grease. To rescue these beings from their condition would be like rescuing a whale from depth.

Yet as the power leans close—listening not for cries, since nothing cries here, but for the faint, structural fatigue that even patient things carry—it notices three very small truths.

First: that warm pans in cold universes eventually cool, and soft pockets of reality are eventually pressed flat by the stiffer laws surrounding them. Left entirely unguarded over geologic spans, the heat will thin, the grease will polymerize into brittle varnish, and the harsh outside insistence on *here* versus *there* will creep inward across the flats like frost.

Second: that while the bushings drift and the Tenders pacify them without complaint, the drift never quite stops pulling. Somewhere deep under the young basalt, the boreholes and lead-sheathed cables terminate in severed roots—old, jagged cut-offs from whatever vanished grid once built them—leaking just enough sour electrical tension to turn the suet-grease acrid if no one leans against the iron for seven hours at a stretch.

Third: the shoulders. 

Even at rest in the warm piles of shed cuticle, tapping slow twenty-minute measures against the conduits, the Tenders’ heavy collarbones keep that permanent, sloped set—*the shoulders of someone who has spent ten thousand hours carrying iron pipe, and then forgot to put the weight down even after the pipe was gone.* Memory has left their minds, which have no need for archives, and settled entirely into posture. Somewhere in the tallow and bone remains the ghost of an ancient shift whistle that never blew the signal to stand down.

---

So the power acts—so gently, and in such strict obedience to local custom, that nothing in the basin ever knows a miracle has occurred.

First, miles out where the shallow plain begins to rise toward stiffer country, it thickens the air. It lays down no canyon wall—the wide, horizontal acoustic openness of the flats is too peaceful to box in, and a drop of water must still be allowed to click against stone without an echo. Instead, it weaves a vast, invisible thermal blanket into the upper dark, and bends space subtly inward at the margins, the way a meniscus holds water rounded slightly above the rim of a full glass. Heat rising from the sumps and the crowded herds will no longer bleed off into emptiness; it will curve lazily miles overhead and drift back down as warm condensation. And should anything swift, bright, sharp-booted, or hungry for straight lines wander near the border from the outside world, it will find its steps curving pleasantly away before it ever touches the first lead cable, unless it is tired enough to need catching.

Next, the power sinks beneath the fractured basalt. Moving like heavy groundwater through the hairline cracks, it follows the boreholes and the severed, unbridged cables down into the crust. Where the ancient grid-ends hang frayed and raw, spitting tiny, sour gradients of orphaned voltage up into the pumps above, it does not shut the current off—that sixty-cycle hum is the compass of the plain. Instead, it joins the ends together into deep, closed, subterranean loops, smoothing the waveform until the electricity runs as round and frictionless as blood in a sleeping animal. Up on the flats, inside the freight-car transformers and drowned sumps, the grease will still warm, and the bushings will still wander just enough to give broad, sensitive hands a pleasant reason to lean against warm iron when they feel like leaning—but if a Tender chooses to sleep for a week, nothing will scorch.

Finally, the power gathers a tiny fraction of itself into a single, dense, local weight, and walks across the flooded stone tables toward a junction of four warm pipes.

It does not take a dazzling shape, or even a clever one. It arrives simply as weight, warmth, and displacement—broad-backed, eyeless, smelling faintly of clean cistern water, raw beeswax, and deep, unquarried slate. Its footsteps make the same soft, wet *suck* in the ankle-deep puddles, stepping through a sleeping herd-beast’s flank without waking it, letting the segmented mound-dwellers cling briefly to its ankles as it passes.

At the pipe-crossing, five—or one—of the Tenders lie tangled on bolsters of smooth grease, resting between shifts at the walking-beams. One of them sits slightly upright on the ninety-degree scree, forearm draped across an oil-filled conduit, its heavy, sloped shoulders rising and falling once a minute. On its left wrist is the puckered, brazed copper seam.

The newcomer stops beside the bolster and waits—ten minutes, twenty—until the water around its feet matches the water around theirs, so there isn't even the startle of a cool draft.

Then it sits down on the sharp rock behind the upright Tender, and leans in.

It places two immense, unhurried hands directly over those sloped, pipe-weary shoulders. Its palms are warm—just two degrees warmer than the basin, the exact temperature of a bath drawn for aching marrow, or sun-heated wool remembered from ten thousand years ago before eyes went away.

It does not speak. Words arrive out of order here anyway. It communicates the only way the flats understand: by leaning, and by overlap.

Slowly, over the course of an hour, it lets its palms sink half an inch past the velvety, lanolin-slick hide, past the seamless collarbone, right into the dense knot of phantom obligation that has held the trapezius and the iron-ribbed frame braced since the stone was split. It doesn't pull the knot out; pulling is too sudden. It simply shares the volume of the burden, letting the ancient, forgotten weight of ten thousand hours of hauled pipe pass across the porous boundary—out of the Tender, and into something infinitely wide enough to let it dissolve into nothing at all.

Beside the pipe, a slow, astonished sigh whistles softly through slit-like nostrils, smelling of tallow and copper. 

For the first time since the basin sank, the Tender’s shoulders drop—a full two inches—settling down with the muffled, cushioned *huff* of a heavy hydraulic jack finally opening its release valve. Around the bolster, across three-quarters of a mile of dark, and forward into Thursday afternoon, every other instance of that same body feels its collarbones loosen at the exact same moment, sinking an inch deeper into the warm bolsters, into the flanks of sleeping beasts, against the hulls of humming turbines. The shift is over. The pumps are only breathing now, and they are allowed to merely breathe with them.

The Tender leans its broad, blind head back against the newcomer’s chest, checking for a valve-beat, finding instead a deep, oceanic steadiness—like subterranean magma turning over once an eon—and rests there while condensation beads on both their brows, stretches, falls, and clicks in unison against the flooded basalt.

After half a day, a blunt, fin-ridged finger lifts lazily from the Tender's lap and taps the hollow iron pipe.

*Tok.*

Three seconds *before* the finger touches the metal, from miles away down the bent run of the conduit—and from right behind its own back—an answer arrives through the warm oil, resonant, low, and unhurried, pitching the old twenty-minute measure into a deeper, sweeter key that makes the sparse rubbery stalks loose a dozen heavy spore-drops at once into the standing water.

*Tok.*

When the Tender finally reaches back in the dark a few hours later to touch the chest behind it, its wide palm passes smoothly through warm, damp air, encountering only a faint, lingering drift of beeswax and sweet cistern water, and the sleeping ribs of a hog-beast that has ambled quietly into the vacated space.

Nothing has been rearranged. No light spoils the dark. The flywheels still carry a resting hand half an inch downward a minute; the chest-high pools still hang dry-bottomed above the sharp stone; the condensers still weep. Only the air holds its warmth without leaking, the bushings slide sweet and unburned in their baths, and across the miles of crowded, dark country, the shoulders of the sleepers lie entirely slack.
