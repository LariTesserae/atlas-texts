A lesser power, looking down through the bright, torn belly of a passing cloud, would feel a rush of pity and reach down to fix things. 

A lesser power would look at the razor-sharp basalt and smooth it into gentle, safe staircases, forgetting that if the stone loses its bite, every wedged slate balcony in the chime will instantly slip its teeth and plunge two hundred paces into the dark. It might hush the deafening roar of the ten thousand falling drops so the people could shout across the void, not realizing that the roar is the town’s plumb-line and barometer—that a mother inside a moss-stuffed room knows the frost has clogged an upper aqueduct solely because a familiar, sharp *clack-clack* sixty feet above her roof has suddenly gone mute. It might gift them iron chisels, or rich black valley soil, or warm timber, and in a single generation of ease, break the exquisite, slow-breathing covenant that keeps sixty human beings suspended safely inside the throat of a waking mountain.

Wisdom knows when it is looking at a masterpiece. Benevolence knows not to smudge wet paint.

The Power watches the man on the northern bridge hold his thick-sandaled foot poised in the sparkling air while the great bass note of the wind hums through his ribs. It watches the stone beneath his heel decide not to slip. It watches him set his foot down and walk on. 

And the Power loves them—not as pathetic creatures clinging to a harsh margin, work-worn and mute, but as master luthiers living inside the body of a violin while it is still being tuned. It sees the four-year-old’s cat’s-cradle in the tallow-lit dark; it sees the grandmothers wedged into the misty, booming roots of the chasm below, holding the walls apart with their femurs and braided vines so the sun can still reach the crane-pens. To take away their danger would be to insult their genius. To make their lives easy would be to make their dead unemployed.

Yet, a wise eye looks deeper than the surface of the wet slate. It looks down into the root of the ridge, reading the slow calendar of stone three centuries ahead, and it looks inward, into the blood and marrow of sixty people who never marry outside their stone horn. 

Here, and only here, are two quiet traps that patience and hornbeam clappers cannot solve.

First: sixty souls is a very narrow vessel to carry human blood across generations. Already, in the moss-muffled dark, two of the newborns share a slight, harmless stiffness in the fourth finger, and a winter cough that passes through a valley town in a week lingers here for a season because every lung is cousin to every other lung. 

Second: deep in the tectonic root of the chime, eighty yards below the lowest bone-tier where the abyssal updraft rises, a massive, unseen flaw in the young crust is gathering torque. In eleven winters—not today, not tomorrow, when the mountain’s normal settling cracks an hourly seam, but in eleven winters—a silent, sideways shear will snap the great keystone blocks at the bottom of the throat. It will not give the warning *crack* two hours ahead. The chime will simply clap its black glass hands together in the night.

So the Power acts. Not from the sky with thunder or miracles, which would blind eyes tuned to mica-flashes, and not with a voice, which would sound vulgar and monstrous to ears raised on breathy sibilants. It works solely through the grammar the chime already speaks: stone, drift, water, and slow time.

First, it reaches deep into the blind, crushing dark beneath the ridge, eighty yards below the misty bottom of the chasm, where no eye will ever see. 

It does not freeze the mountain—a frozen young mountain only stores up greater fury for later. Instead, with the gentle pressure of an unseen thumb, it coaxes that lethal, silent shear to wake up early, and breaks it into ten thousand tiny pieces. Over the next seven years, instead of one catastrophic snap, the deep root will bleed off its tension in mild, polite shivers—one every few moons. High above, the woman with her fingers on the basalt seam will feel a familiar, readable tremble; she will flash her mica disc; *tak, tak-tak* will go the hornbeam slats; the men will move three stone counterweights and re-shim a stair, mildly proud of their own foresight, never knowing that an apocalypse was just handed to them in installments small enough to mend before supper.

Second, the Power looks five ridges away, across leagues of jagged, cloud-snagged highland where the sixty believe nothing human could possibly live. 

In fact, four days’ journey to the west—though four days across raw, unweathered basalt ridges might as well be the moon—there is a cleft canyon of fractured granite. Not a chime, not a cylinder, but a long, narrow slot-gorge roofed in permanent ice-bridges, where forty people live by stringing gut-snares across wind-tunnels and building tiered ledges of wedged stone bound with resin and willow-root. They do not use hornbeam clappers; they speak across the roar of sub-glacial meltwater by striking small, tuned slabs of ringing greenstone hung at their hips, and they wrap their knees in cured marmot-pelt instead of boiled leather. They are just alien enough to renew the blood, and just kin enough to know, without being taught, never to hurry on wet rock.

The Power does not drag them together. It merely tilts a high-altitude thermal over the western passes.

Two days later, the boy with the wooden scoop and the waxy-leaf basket is moving along the lower pot-rows at noon, skimming drowned midges and pine mast from the shivering linen drip-catchers. 

Snagged in the wet weave of the fourth catcher, washed down the sheer obsidian face by the morning’s pearl-white cloud, is something that is neither moth nor pine-seed. 

He pauses, one hand touching the taut vertical balance-vine, and lifts it out with two cold-reddened fingers. 

It is a shuttle for netting—three inches long, carved not from crane-bone, but from pale, dense mountain-sheep horn, notched at both ends with unmistakable, deliberate symmetry, and rubbed smooth with red ochre and thumb-grease. Tied to its center is a three-inch tail of cordage braided in a four-strand square hitch the boy has never seen in any cat’s-cradle game: a hitch that does not slacken when frozen, because a fine hair of beaten copper wire—native metal pried from a distant granite vein—has been spun straight through the plant fiber.

The boy stands completely motionless on the foot-wide slate ledge for five full minutes while ten thousand water drops hammer the drum-tight leaves around him like a roaring crowd. 

He does not drop his basket. He does not run—nobody runs. With the liquid, unhurried cadence of the grey birds overhead, he slips the horn shuttle into his greased belt-pouch, finishes skimming the midge-paste for the cranes so the afternoon chores will not fall behind, and only then climbs the switchback stairs toward the moss-lined hatch of his family’s winter room, where the elders sit braiding green-hide in the hush.

Meanwhile, four ridges west, a high-altitude gust tearing off the lip of the basalt chime drops a stray, wind-tossed scrap into a meltwater sieve in the granite slot-gorge: a thumb-sized disc of flaked, silver-backed mica, drilled cleanly at the top with an antler-awl, strung on a loop of bladderwort skin greased with crane-fat.

It will take them three summers to find each other. The scouts who eventually leave the rim—two young women and a man, armored at elbow and knee, carrying coils of vine-rope and hornbeam slats—will move across the razor-backed ridges at a glacial, testing pace, wedging stone steps into raw crevices and waiting for frost to lock them, reading how the clouds snag on distant peaks. When they finally peer down through a crack in a blue ice-bridge and hear, cutting cleanly through the roar of glacial water, a sharp *ting—ting-ting* of struck greenstone, and answer from above with *tak—tak-tak*, it will belong entirely to them. Their triumph. Their discovery.

Before withdrawing its attention, the Power permits itself one small, wholly unnecessary indulgence—not for their survival, which they have well in hand, nor for their future, which is now open, but purely out of affection for the quiet rooms behind the moss.

High on the eastern rim, just where the prevailing morning wind strikes the jagged basalt lip to blow its deep, chest-shaking bass note down the cylinder, a single loose tooth of volcanic glass has been vibrating slightly out of phase for forty years, adding a faint, ragged, teeth-setting harshness to the undertone of the town’s hum. 

A heavy, flightless cliff-crane, stepping slowly along the rim at dusk, feels a sudden, pleasant itch under its left scale, pauses, and kicks backward once against that precise shard of glass. 

The shard snaps clean away—falling harmlessly down the outer face of the mountain rather than inside—and tumbles into the clouds. 

Instantly, as the evening wind crosses the newly notched lip of the chime, the single vast bass note changes. The ragged edge vanishes. The great stone barrel drops half a tone into a pure, warm, resonant fifth—still deep, still chest-vibrating, still wild, but round and rich as a cello string, humming gently through the triple-layered moss walls where sixty people sit in the warm dark, whispering like breath over stone, turning a strange piece of sheep-horn over and over in their scarred, capable hands.
