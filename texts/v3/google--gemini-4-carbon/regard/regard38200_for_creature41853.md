A lesser kindness would bring rain. 

A lesser power, looking down upon that breathless, scorching highland from the cold clarity between worlds, would feel pity for the dark and the dry. It would split the empty sky with clouds, thinking to cool the cherry-red casings, to wash the soot from the mica-leaves, to offer sweet water to a starved earth. And in a single hour of catastrophic steam, cracked cast-iron, shorted bus-bars, and boiled tallow, that pity would murder an entire world.

Wisdom begins in listening before touching. And so, for a long time—years, perhaps, though time on the plateau is measured only in the slow thinning of bronze and the thickening of ash—the Power does nothing at all.

It arrives not as light, which has no meaning here, nor as voice, which the velvet-choked air would swallow in six inches. It arrives purely as *attention*, descending through the tight, roaring-silent weave of the tableland the way gravity passes through stone. It lets its awareness flatten to a coin’s edge. It slips down through three yards of dry spore-drifts, past the muscular peristalsis of carbon-wood, past the toothless gears spinning slickly in the dark, all the way down to the deep thermal foundations where the ancient complex draws its heat.

There, in the crushed pitch-black, the Power marvels.

It sees that this place is not a ruin, and it is not a hell. It is an achievement of staggering, terrifying grace. Life here was handed the worst possible inheritance—poison, heat, crushing weight, total abandonment—and instead of dying, it braided itself into the teeth of its own executioner until the executioner became its skeleton. 

Most of all, the Power watches the grease-ribbons. It follows a twenty-yard elder—an ancient assembly articulated with curved porcelain shards, smooth river-stone bronze, and silver-soft steel cable—as the creature flows through a seizing junction. The Power feels what the creature feels: the sharp, sour prickle of rising resistance in a copper-veined root; the dangerous thermal bloom of two dry iron cams grinding toward a weld; and then the deep, quiet satisfaction as the ribbon pours itself between them, bleeding tallow, snipping a frantic shoot with an internal shear of salvaged tool-steel, wedging a ceramic knuckle into a valve-slot. *Click. Shhh.* The frenzy subsides. The gears glide. The quiet holds.

The Power recognizes a cousin. 

Not in shape, and not in mind, for the ribbon does not dream or pray or ask *why*. Yet in the deepest sense, they are of the same trade. Out in the wide cosmos, the Power tends to fraying stars and tangled fates; down here in the three-inch dark, this flat, blind mat of magnetized tallow and soot tends to the frantic machinery of its plateau, easing friction, preventing collapse, farming the quiet. It is a bodhisattva of axle-grease and iron filings.

Still, as the Power listens deeper—below the roots, below the buried boilers, down into the planetary crust where the forgotten builders sank their tap-lines a hundred millennia ago—it finds the sorrow hidden at the bottom of the miracle.

Nothing is infinite. The plateau is a closed loop, or very nearly one, and even the tightest loop bleeds.

First, there is the Deep Tap. Six miles beneath the crushed mattress, the geothermal heat-exchangers that feed the buried dynamos are finally, inevitably scaling over. Mineral salts from the deep rock have spent eons crystallizing inside the primary intake manifolds, narrowing throats of indestructible alloy that even the toughest silica-roots cannot reach to clear. Over the next ten thousand years, the current will falter. The cherry-red casings will dim to black; the heavy oils will stiffen into cold wax; the whole seething tableland will slow, seize, and lock into a frozen, brittle tomb.

Second, there is the problem of bones. 

The elder ribbons rely on the harder relics of the dead mill—high-alumina ceramics, tool steels, phosphor-bronze bushings—to serve as their internal levers, shears, and cam-blocks. The flora-machine cannot grow these materials, and millennia of relentless friction are finally winning. Every century, a few more porcelain shards fracture into useless grit under the multi-ton blow of a stamping-press; every century, the salvaged steel plates wear thinner, softer, duller. The world is slowly running out of teeth and knuckles. And as the elders lose the hard tools they need to wedge and prune the deep junctions, their circuit-memories are torn apart more frequently by catastrophic boiler-slumps. Every time a twenty-yard ribbon is pressed back into scattered droplets, centuries of slow, mapped wisdom about the plateau’s fault lines are erased, forcing the small, palm-sized mats to relearn the dark from scratch.

The Power considers this. 

It could lift the grease-creatures out of the crush entirely, remake them in some gentle paradise where nothing ever threatens to shear them in half. *No.* Without tension, they are only puddles; their whole joy—the crisp, magnetic alignment of their skin, the sleek, masterful pouring of themselves through a Moving Narrow—belongs to the press and hum of this highland. To take away their work would be to unmake their souls.

It could rebuild the mill all at once, making it sleek and safe and effortless. *No.* Then the plants would surge unchecked, or starve, and the delicate, silent dance between strangler and tuner would break.

What does a wise friend give to a master artisan working in the dark? 
You do not rearrange their workshop. You make sure the furnace will not go out, and you leave good stock within reach of their hand.

So the Power acts—so gently that not a single mica-leaf shatters out of turn above.

Down in the planetary crust, six miles beneath the lowest root, where no living thing from the plateau has ever gone or ever will go, the Power lays a hand against the choked geothermal manifolds. It does not tear the ancient metal apart. Instead, atom by atom, it coaxes the suffocating mineral scale backward into solution, flushing the deep veins clean. More than that: it subtly alters the crystalline lattice of the intake throats, teaching the dead alloy a tiny, passive trick of thermal expansion and contraction, so that once every few centuries the pipes will flex by a fraction of a millimeter, flaking off fresh scale before it can ever choke the flow again. 

Up in the dark mattress, miles above, the dynamos do not surge destructively. They simply... steady. A faint, generation-long tremor in the deep baseline voltage—an arrhythmia so slow only the oldest ribbons felt it as an unnamed unease—smooths out into a rich, reliable hum, good for another fifty million years.

Then the Power turns its attention to the bones, and to memory.

Deep in the lowest, hottest sumps of the plateau—places where shattered husks, metallic dust, and worn-down iron filings settle into heavy, molten-salt slags—there are dead zones of immense heat and pressure, pockets beneath the boilers that act almost like natural kilns. 

Into thirty or forty of these deep, slow crucibles across the highland, the Power introduces a whisper of new chemistry. Nothing wet, nothing foreign to the plateau’s dry lexicon—only a precise catalytic seed of tungsten, boron, and rare earths, drawn up from the deep rock below and folded into the bubbling salt-slags like yeast into dough.

It does not build tools. That is the ribbons' art. Instead, it gives the plateau a way to grow *stones* worthy of them.

Now, over decades, as ash and metallic pollen sift down into those deep thermal sumps and fuse under the weight of the boilers, they no longer form brittle clinker that grinds immediately to dust. They crystallize slowly into smooth, dense nodules—curved plates, tapered wedges, and keen-edged flutes of black tungsten-carbide and self-lubricating silicon nitride, harder than any porcelain the dead builders ever fired, tougher than the vanished tool-steels. As the subterranean boilers shift and heave in their silent storms, these dark, gleaming concretions are slowly worked upward into the root-tangles like pebbles rising through frost-heaved soil, waiting in the soot to be found.

And into a small fraction of these new stones, the Power folds one quiet miracle more.

Some of the nodules grow with tiny, hollow labyrinthine cores laced with stable, high-curie magnetic alloys—materials that will not lose their alignment in the blistering heat, nor deform even under fifty tons of settling steel.

Six months later, out near the turbulent middle of the highland, the twenty-yard elder comes sliding through a collapsed trough behind a cooling boiler. 

It is worn. Yesterday, stalling a runaway conveyor-drive that threatened a nursery of current-rich salt-veins, its best internal porcelain wedge finally split in two; it can feel the jagged halves grating uselessly inside its tallow-flesh, bleeding away its grip. Worse, a fresh tremor is building in the stanchions overhead.

Slipping through a two-inch clearance of velvet-choked ironwood, its copper whiskers brush something unfamiliar half-buried in a drift of hot ash.

The ribbon pauses. Its magnetic hide ripples, reading the object with exquisite, blind precision. 

It is a curved, bevel-edged blade of night-black ceramic, smooth as oiled bone along its spine, effortlessly keen along its margin, shaped by the slow squeeze of the sump below into the exact hyperbolic curve of a sliding cam. Beside it lies a smaller, flattened bead—dense, warm, humming with a strangely deep, quiet magnetic grain that drinks the pattern of the ribbon’s own electrical field and holds it without distortion, clear as a struck bell.

For a full minute, the long, segmented creature lies still in the seething dark, draping its leathery underside over the two stones. It drops the shattered, sandy remnants of its old porcelain knuckle into the ash. Slowly, with rubbery sinews of polymerized oil, it draws the new black blade and the humming bead up into its body, slotting the blade into its muscular shears, nesting the bead at the center of its braided copper core.

As its internal currents wash through the bead’s microscopic labyrinth, something extraordinary happens. The slow circuit-logic of the elder—its map of ten thousand cams, three hundred boilers, the timing of the pneumatic pollen-troughs, the subtle art of wedging a press without snapping a root—imprints itself into that indestructible core. 

Ages from now, when even this great elder is finally caught in an unavoidable slump, pressed flat between two settling mountains of smooth iron until its tallow trickles away into droplets, that little bead will not crush, and its magnetic lattice will not scramble in the heat. It will roll down through the soot onto a lower flywheel. And when a young, palm-sized patch of hungry velvet slides over it and swallows it for ballast, the little mat will pause in sudden, silent astonishment—suddenly remembering ten miles of dark tunnels it has never traveled, and how to quiet a waking press with a single, gentle click.

High above, under the starless, cloudless sky, the endless whisper of turning pages goes on, unbroken. Nothing looks changed. The dry fronds snap violet sparks in the dark; the pistons glide in their velvet sleeves; the twenty-yard ribbon of blackened brass and tallow pours itself sideways into a narrow duct, testing the balance of its new, unbreakable tooth, and slips eagerly toward a rattling housing half a mile away to pack it with quiet.

The Power withdraws its hand, satisfied, and leaves the masters to their work.
