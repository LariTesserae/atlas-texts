A lesser kindness would tear the basket open. 

A lesser power, looking up from the stable plains of the finished world fifty miles below, would see only a cage of frantic, claustrophobic agony—a blind thing suffocating in its own wet mortar—and out of pity, it would sweep the reed walls away, untangle the bruised, overlapping limbs, and lower the creature gently onto cool grass under an ordinary sun.

And in three breaths, the Hod-Knot would die of uselessness. Worse than die: without a seam to pinch, without the tearing roar of the unfinished sky pushing against its blind brow, its soft infant-knuckles would lose their hydraulic purpose. On solid ground where four walls only ever make four corners and mud stays where it is laid, a creature made entirely of *bracing* would simply slump into an unshaped heap of cooling dough, bewildered by space, starved of tension.

The Wise One knows this. Wisdom begins by looking at a thing and refusing to mistake its nature for a mistake.

So the visitor does not enter the kiln-world by taking up space—there is no space left to take, and another body crammed into the nine-pace hollow would only force the Hod-Knot to work harder, cinching new flesh against the cedar ribs. Instead, the Power arrives outside first, out in the black-violet rind where the colors separate like oil on water. 

It wraps nothing like hands around the furious, whistling exterior of the reed-and-daub shell. For a long while, it simply listens through the wattle. It feels the pistol-shot *crack* of the swelling green bamboo, the overtuned singing of the gut-strings, the heavy *thud-slap* of the creature’s pulse beating inside the floor tiles. It recognizes what this place actually is. This is not a ruin, and it is not a prison. It is a seed-coat that caught in the rafters of creation before gravity and sequence finished descending. And the being inside is a faithful builder who was handed wet clay and raw timber, told *hold this fast*, and never given the word to lay down the trowel.

To destroy the work would insult the worker. To leave it as it is would let the pocket pack itself denser and hotter until even double-exposed flesh has nowhere left to fold, choking on its own ferocious diligence.

What this little world lacks is not rescue. It lacks *the next step*.

The Power does not speak—words are useless where sound emerges two feet away from the throat that made it. Instead, it reaches precisely to the thumb-sized gap at the top of the vault, where the two green timbers cross without quite touching, right against the smooth, blind gristle of the Hod-Knot’s dome.

For the first time, what comes through that gap is not weeping hot water, and not the silent geometric fracture of the upper vacuum. 

The Power breathes a single, narrow draft of **dryness** through the hole. And braided invisibly into that dryness is a single minute of **time**.

Not an hour. Not a century—a century would turn the reeds to dust and rot the raw gut-strings before the creature knew what was happening. Just sixty quiet, sequential seconds, cool as river-air at dusk, threaded straight onto the Hod-Knot’s brow.

Inside the scalding dark, the effect is small, miraculous, and profound.

Where the cool draft touches the weeping timber-gap, the hot water stops boiling and condensing at once. A single droplet hangs on the rough cedar lip, cools, commits to being liquid, and falls—just downward, obeying a newly whispered rumor of gravity—to strike the soft terracotta floor.

When it hits the clay, the mud does not swell frantically upward to trap it. The clay takes a tiny, smooth dimple. And holds it.

Above, the Hod-Knot stills. Not all of it at once—three of its heavy thumbs keep pinching coils along a wall-jar out of pure momentum, *whack, whack, whack*—but the blind dome of its head pulls half an inch back from the ceiling reeds. Its lipless clay-slit of a mouth pauses mid-chew on a strip of bitter cedar bark.

Something extraordinary is happening to its hands.

Across the rim of the wall-niche jar, where its left thumb just pressed six crisp whorls into the wet terracotta, the clay begins to *set*. It drinks the tiny draft of dryness, shifting from slick, urgent mud to leather-hard earthenware. When the Hod-Knot’s blunt, dimpled thumb comes back around to pinch that seam shut again, the seam does not yield. It doesn't leak. It doesn't slump. 

It holds itself up.

The pale afterimage of the creature’s wrist, usually left hanging in the clay for four heartbeats, vanishes cleanly on the first, because "yesterday" and "now" have finally stepped half a pace apart. Where its dough-pale elbow was jammed halfway through its own hunched shoulder in a bruise of translucent plum, the room quietly concedes three new inches of true, settled geometry—not much, just enough for one collarbone to slide gently free of the other with a long, wet, hydraulic sigh. Four walls still meet at five corners, because the Power has a fondness for strange craftsmanship, and five corners hold the stratosphere back admirably; yet now, when you lean sideways, the wall stays three feet away until you choose to touch it.

Watch the Hod-Knot’s broad thumb hover over that leather-hard jar rim. 

It presses once, testing. Twice. Waiting for the mud to slide, waiting for the gap to tear open. Nothing tears. Under its infant-smooth skin, where no callus had ever been permitted to form because time had no grip here, a faint, tough grain of horniness thickens across the pad of its thumb. The mark of a tradesman who has survived a finished task.

Then the Power offers its second gift.

Down at the base of the pocket, away from the weeping sky-gap—down where the sixth layer of terracotta tiles meets the herringboned floor mats and the blind, throat-pulsing frog-thing swallows steam—one thick bamboo pole stops popping. Its green rind mellows to cured, quiet gold. Between two copper-bound posts, a palm’s-breadth of wet red daub dries to a warm, dusty rose, cracks cleanly along a straight hairline, and instead of letting the tearing vacuum in, swings outward an inch on tough hinges of braided hemp root.

Outside that new chink, there is no violet void. 

The Wise One has moored the tiny reed kiln to the crown of a great, unseen buttress—not dragging it down to the flat earth, which would be too vast and terrifying, but grafting its bottom timbers onto the tip of a high, misty crag of raw basalt and tough mountain lichen, just below the salt-smear of the clouds, where the air still roars fiercely enough to comfort a creature born in a gale, yet smells faintly of cold stone and pine needles. 

And even better: just outside that chink, resting on a narrow ledge of unfinished rock where the mist curls against bare stone, lies a fresh pile of unworked red river-clay, a bundle of split, fragrant cedar, and nine paces of empty, unbuilt air.

Inside the warm dimness—where the amber sap-light still glows, though the shadows now lean lazily sideways instead of pointing straight backward—the Hod-Knot feels the draft shift. 

The ferocious, boiling panic goes out of the chamber’s percussion. The sinew cords drop an octave from a shriek to a deep, resonant hum, like a tuned cello resting in its case. One of the pale, sixteen-legged woodlouse creatures crawls across a broad heart-shaped leaf, and for the first time, all sixteen of its legs arrive together in the same heartbeat; it settles into the warm dry hollow of a cured bamboo joint and goes to sleep.

Slowly, clumsily—because sequence is a brand-new tool—the Hod-Knot draws its third knee back out of the wattle wall. The wattle does not blow inward. The wall stands firm, smelling cleanly of drying sedge and turpentine. 

The creature lowers its heavy, barrel-wide ribcage an inch onto the floor mats, feeling its own slow pulse *thud* not out of panic in the tiles, but steady and warm inside its own dense chest. One blunt, freshly callused hand reaches down to touch the hairline door-crack at the base of the wall. Its webbed, root-haired toes curl into the leather-hard thumbprints on the floor—prints that will stay crisp now for a thousand years without needing to be re-pinched every breath.

It feels the empty ledge outside. It smells the cold, patient stone waiting for mortar. It realizes, down in the blind gristle of its brow and the flint plates of its jaw, a truth no creature in that pocket had ever been allowed to know:

*This room is sound. It will hold.*

And when it is ready—not yet, perhaps not for twenty quiet minutes of simply sitting in the warm, gentle rain of its own condensing breath, rubbing one thumb over the marvel of a finished pot—it can push that small reed door open, haul a fresh Hod of wet clay onto the misty crag outside, and begin building a second room. 

With a doorway between them, so it can always come back inside to rest.
