To look down into this warm, drumming cup of stone is to look at a truce so rare and finely tuned that even a god’s breath might knock it out of plumb. 

A lesser power, moved by pity or fondness, might be tempted to help. It might soften the crooked bone in the nephew’s ankle until he walked straight again, or smooth a stair into the slick northwestern wall so an aging woman would never slip, or hush the deafening leaves so the two of them could speak without leaning shoulder to shoulder. 

A wise power looks longer, listens beneath the *thwack-boom* of the canopies, and stays its hand. 

If the nephew’s ankle had not been crushed under the scree, he would be down on the cold ridges with the other thirty-year-old men, running meat across the shale, and his aunt would be working the troughs with someone faster, louder, and less patient, or working them alone. It was the limp that brought him into the warm water; it was the drumbeat that taught them how to pitch their voices beneath the world’s noise; it is the slickness of the coral-seam that keeps the careless and the greedy up on the dry rim where they belong. Every hardship at the edge of this bowl is part of the lock that keeps its peace intact. To fix any piece of it from above would be to insult the forty-year-old hammer-bruises on the eastern overhang, and the quiet pride of two pairs of pale, chalk-ringed feet.

So the power does almost nothing at all. Almost.

Deep beneath the mountain—far below the flooded travertine chambers where the blind eels whip sideways out of the siphon-pull, down in the dark, crushed roots of the ridge where ancient fault-lines pinch the rising thermal waters—rock is never entirely still. Mountain hearts shift. A hairline tremor in winter, no stronger than the shrug of a sleeping shoulder, can pinch a warm conduit shut or crack open a deeper, sulfurous vein, turning sweet alkaline ground-sweat sour with brimstone or cold as snowmelt in a single season. Many high thermal bowls die that way, leaving behind dry, chalky skeletons of dead moss and silent stone.

Down there in the black, miles beneath the soles of their feet, the watching presence lays one quiet finger against a creeping seam of granite. A winter shift that would have sheared the warm spring sideways in six years’ time is eased into a harmless, hair-wide slip along a dead bedding plane instead. Up in the court, three feet under snow-steam, a single siphon-flush comes four seconds late one January midnight, and then the twenty-eight-minute heartbeat resumes as if nothing had ever threatened it. No crab loses its hold; no bighorn ewe along the rim even lifts her head.

Only two other touches are permitted, both so small they look entirely like the mountain being itself.

The first is down in the crooked larch camp on the leeward shoulder, where the pair sleep. Two hundred paces uphill from their lean-to stands an old, lightning-struck subalpine larch, half-dead for a century, its heartwood soaked through with amber pitch until it weighs like iron. In the third week of August, when nights turn sharp and dry twigs grow scarce around the hollow, an afternoon gust—just slightly better aimed than the wind usually bothers to be—lays that dead trunk softly down into a bed of heather, splitting its resinous belly open into clean, stove-length slabs without snapping a single living sapling around it. It is five summers’ worth of quick, hot, smokeless evening fire, waiting twenty steps from their hearth for an aunt and nephew tired from carrying wet fiber up out of the rock. They will think themselves lucky, test the fat yellow splinters with their thumbnails, and thank nothing louder than the wind.

The second touch waits inside the diagonal seam on the northwestern wall—the ladder of ancient horn-coral nodules they climb down barefoot every morning. 

Limestone dissolves, even slowly. Under forty summers of warm, millimeter-thick seep and the rub of calloused big toes, the lowest and most crucial coral boss—the one the nephew has to trust all his weight to when swinging his stiff left leg down onto the basin floor—has worn smooth as soap at its upper edge. By next spring, slick with morning algae, it would have let a wet foot skate free.

All through one dark November, while the human camps are miles away down in the river gorges and the stone court boils alone with white winter fog, the seep along that single diagonal inch runs a fraction richer in dissolved silica than lime, and a hair cooler. Grain by microscopic grain, while the unseen leaves boom in the steam, the water eats a sixteenth of an inch of soft grey matrix away from around the fossil’s collar, exposing a fresh, sharp-ribbed crown of ancient coral beneath—rough as a rasp, solid as bone, waiting in just the right spot for a crooked ankle next May.

And near the southern wall, in that deep empty solution pit toward which the woman nudged a split green seed-pod with the side of her bare foot two summers back, the pod’s single pale root-hook finally catches hold in a tiny pocket of marl that the half-hour floods somehow never quite manage to scour out. By the time the woman’s iron-grey hair is white, and a younger cousin with stiff knees or a quiet temper is being taught where to stow their greased boots under the wasp-ledge, a ninth cluster of green stalks will be rising toward the light, stretching tight its own saddle-leather drumhead to take up the beat.

The wise power asks no shrine, leaves no vision in their sleep, and alters not one note of the thunder. It simply sits for a moment on the dry upper rim in the clear mountain daylight—like the solitary black wasp pausing to drink at the warm seep—listening with deep, unhurried pleasure to the *thwack*, the *boom*, the soft *tup* in the eastern corner, and the low, private murmur of two human voices speaking plainly inside the roar, before leaving them entirely to their work.
