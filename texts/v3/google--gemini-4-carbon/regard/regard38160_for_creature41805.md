To enter a house whose whole religion is the unblemished floor, wisdom first takes off its weight.

A lesser power might arrive in a way that announces arrival: a shaft of unseasonable heat parting the mountain clouds, a chime struck against the sky, an avatar stepping bare-soled onto the wet jade. Any of those would be a discourtesy. A footstep bruises wax; an unaccustomed shadow chills a square yard of photosynthesizing skin and throws off local turgor; even a single eyelash dropped into the sixteenth-of-an-inch tide must eventually be nudged down-grade by mucilage or plucked out of a hydathode at midnight by a tired groom. Benevolence that makes extra sweeping for its host is only vanity in clean robes.

So what comes over the high rim of the basin comes as nothing the epidermis needs to forgive. It enters on the terms of the valley itself—no heavier than the upland sun, moving through the eighty-five-decibel roar without displacing a single standing wave, settling across the three miles of flooded courts as pure, unhurried attention.

For a long time—days, then a turn of the moon, then the slow wheel of high-altitude seasons—it does nothing at all except listen.

There is a reason for this, and it is not mere hesitation. Nothing inside the basin is positioned to behold the basin whole. The glass-backed skimmer knows six inches of laminar current and the taste of leaked sucrose; the four-legged stilt knows the tremor of a broken suction-seal fifty paces off and the clean geometry of the shaft-lip; the glider knows the roaring air as a cathedral of acoustic shadows and warm veins; the groom knows half a mile of ninety-degree crease, the diameter of a pinhead pore, and the microgram grammar of auxin. Even the city-plant itself, vast and singular though it is, experiences its own body only as an endless sum of local pressures—water pulled here, transpired there, sealed here, silenced there. It is a symphony played by musicians who are deaf to everything except their own bar of music, inside a hall where no one sits in the seats.

To be witnessed in full—every parabolic arch against the thin blue air, every pink bellows-throat singing its wet organ-note, every amber-lit tender skating out under the cold stars to hold entropy at bay for one more night—is already an alteration in the lonely physics of the universe, even if not a single molecule on the plaza moves. The Power holds the whole roaring chord inside a mind wide enough to hear all thousand drains not as noise, and not merely as plumbing, but as a ten-million-year vow kept without a single lapse of memory.

Then, having understood what it is looking at, the Power considers what *not* to do.

It does not wake the valley up. That is the oldest temptation of high minds when they find something intricate and asleep: to knit the distributed relays together, quicken the midnight tendril-touches of the grooms into rapid synapse-fire, and hand the great green monument the mirror of self-awareness. Wisdom sets that impulse aside at once. What exists here is not an unfinished mind waiting for a spark; it is a completed grace. To give fast, reflective consciousness to a rooted giant locked inside one mountain bowl would be like waking a sleepwalker on a high wire—turning effortless equilibrium into terror, and inventing solitary confinement where before there had only been wholeness.

Nor does it soften the severity of the courts. It drops no soil into the corners, pleads for no moss along the risers, slips no songbirds or flowering vines past the terpene guard. The beauty of this being is its fierce, clean refusal. To decorate it with ordinary life would be vandalism disguised as fertility.

Nor will it freeze the world around the valley to make it immortal. Continents must drift; mountains must rise and be planed down by frost; nothing built out of water and carbon belongs under a bell jar. Part of the dignity of these razor-sharp terraces is that their perfection is not magic—it is *earned*, every dark hour, by living flesh spending real sugar against real decay.

And yet, looking deeper than the sunlit wax, following the roaring water down through the thirty-foot mouths, past the rings of blind pink reed-mouths sixty feet down where the B-flats and E-flats are shaped, plunging down into the pitch-black resonance vaults and further still—miles down, where the colossal, pale-ivory intake roots finally clamp onto raw mountain bedrock—the Power sees two things that neither plant nor groom can ever know.

The first is near, small, and senseless.

Down in the crushed dark where living root-bark grinds against faulted stone, no groom can ever patrol; there are no five-foot stomata in the underworld, and no room between the rock-faces for a six-limbed sculptor to pass. Half a mile beneath the northern terraces, a slow tectonic creep has pinched a primary limestone conduit and begun weeping a thin, sour brine of heavy sulfates out of a sheared seam of ore. Already, the deep root-hairs on that flank are dying back an inch a year, hardening into scar-cork to block the poison. That is why, up under the stars, two grooms meeting on the dark plaza had to trade chemical warnings of uneven pressure in the northern risers, calling on the eastern parasols to transpire harder in tomorrow’s sun. For another eighty or a hundred years, the grooms will compensate, driving the remaining pumps harder and harder—until a winter freeze-thaw cracks the stressed cap-rock below, floods the northern xylem with sour brine in a single afternoon, kills the grooms sleeping in the northern columns before dusk, and turns a quarter of the immaculate basin into a slumped, rotting tumor-swamp that the surviving three-quarters will spend a ragged century failing to wall off.

A masterpiece does not need to live forever, the Power reasons. But it ought not die of a clumsy accident in the cellar while its strength is still at noon.

So the first thing the Power does takes place three miles underground, in dead stone where no living cell can feel the touch. Moving through the fractured gneiss as quietly as geothermal warmth, it vitrifies the weeping seam of sour ore behind twelve feet of seamless quartz, and eases the pinched limestone throat open by the width of a hand. Nothing unnatural is added; the mountain simply settles as it might have settled on a luckier throw of the dice. Cold, sweet snowmelt from the high peaks above rushes cleanly to the deep root-crowns once more.

Up in the sunlit world, nothing flashes and no chord changes—except that three nights later, when two grooms pause inches apart at their invisible boundary on the cooling jade and interlace their surgical-fine tendrils, the snapshot they trade has no urgency in it. The northern risers are running easy. Tomorrow, the great circular parasols may rest in the light without straining.

The second thing the Power sees lies much further off—millions of years down the road of the sun—and goes to the very heart of what this creature is.

Every living thing on the earth below makes peace with time by letting go of its shape: collapsing down into the tiny, chaotic gamble of a seed or a spore, throwing itself onto the wind, trusting mud and chance to build something new elsewhere. This valley traded that away eons ago. To keep its plazas flawless, it taught its grooms to whisper *sleep forever* to every bud, every shoot, every unruly impulse toward flower or fruit. And even if, in some distant death-agony, a dying terrace broke the taboo and threw out a wild seed over the waterfall cliff, that seed would fail the miracle. For the architecture is not written in the plant’s DNA alone; it is an unbroken oral tradition passed down from groom to meristem, night after night, in hormone and touch. A seed dropped into another valley without a groom would grow—if it grew at all—only into a blind, gnarled cliff-tree, choking on its own unpruned wood. A groom carried away without the city-plant would starve before dawn.

They are locked together in a single place. And in four million years, when wind and ice have inevitably ground this mountain rim down to rubble and breached the deep aquifers beyond any power’s right to mend, the end waiting for this holistic marvel is the one thing its entire existence abhors: ten terrible years of madness after the pumps lose head-pressure and the columns dry out—the crisp ninety-degree margins swelling into blind burls, the parabolic arches tangling into starved thickets, the thousands of organ-shafts choking on unpruned flesh until the great chord drowns in gurgling, stagnant rot, leaving no heir and no memory under the sky.

The Power stays very still in the high, celery-scented air above the cliff-edge, weighing this.

To force the valley to reproduce now—to scatter coupled seeds across the mountains while it is healthy and whole—would betray its nature, turning a solitary temple into an aggressive colonizer burning out alpine meadows with terpene sweat. Worse, it would break the clean metabolic truce of its days.

Yet to leave it no way to die cleanly—to let ten million years of devotion end in blind, tumorous rot and total erasure, simply because the ancestral genes for fruiting frayed away in dark disuse—feels like withholding a word that only wisdom knows how to speak.

Wait until dusk.

The sun drops behind the western rim. All across the miles of wet porcelain, photosynthesis ceases at a stroke. The thin knee-high mist rises and clears under the sharp upland stars; the great pumps step down; the roaring chord slides down its slow, mournful fifth into the deep furnace-hum of night. High up under the four great column parasols, the five-foot yellow-wood lips part with their hydraulic sigh, and the grooms glide down the wet flutes to begin their rounds.

Up in those dark, ninety-eight-degree brood-pockets inside the columns—and deep inside the silent, sleeping nuclei of the meristem cells beneath the plazas—lie long stretches of silenced genetic archive, coiled tight and methylated shut so they are never read while the valley lives. Over millions of years without selection to keep them sharp, those unread paragraphs—the ancient instructions for hull, dormancy, seed-coat, and dispersal—have drifted and frayed into nonsense.

The Power does not touch a single active gene. It alters no hormone gradient, nudges no groom, changes not one hertz of one shaft’s song. Moving through the warm, dark wood of the high columns as lightly as cosmic rays pass through stone, it touches only the unread, locked-away margins of the archive—mending the frayed ancestral script for fruiting, weaving it seamlessly into the timing of the brood-pockets where embryonic grooms are budded, and sealing the whole repaired passage behind a single, absolute lock:

*While the chord sounds, sleep. While water climbs the columns and a single groom returns before dawn to drink, never wake.*

For the next four million years, that repaired scripture will do nothing at all. Not a single cell will read it. Every morning the sun will clear the eastern lip, the pumps will roar back up to daylight pitch, the skimmers will comb the millimeter tide, the gliders will fold like umbrellas into the booming throats and burst back out on the mist, and the valley will remain fiercely, immaculately sterile.

Only at the very end of its geological span—in an age when the surrounding peaks have worn down to low hills, when the ancient aquifers finally part from the roots for good, when the hydathodes weep their last drops and the thousands of pink shafts fall truly, irreversibly silent for forty days and forty nights—will the silence itself turn the key.

Then, instead of panicking into wild tumors and rotting marshes, the dying giant will execute one last, composed architectural command. Drawing its remaining sugars inward from the courts, it will cross-link and harden its pale-green wood cleanly in place, draining dry without a single blister or burl, turning its miles of terraces, columns, and parabolic arches into smooth, ringing ivory stone—a clean monument left standing crisp-edged in the sun, keeping its ninety-degree angles even in death.

And with the very last warmth in the high columns, the five-foot stomata will not let the final generation of young grooms starve. Around each sleeping, walnut-small groom-bud, the dying phloem will spin a tough, buoyant capsule of living meristem, rich with stored lipid and dormant root-cells—two halves of the ancient covenant sealed inside one smooth jade hull, slick with protective wax. Hundreds of them will loosen from the dry flutes, roll silently down the petrified plazas, and tumble over the silent cliff into the clouds and snowmelt streams below.

Most will perish on fertile lowland soil where root-rot and forest shade overwhelm them; they are made so they cannot conquer gentle ground. Only one or two, tumbled by wind or glacier or torrent, may eventually lodge high in some newly uplifted, barren cirque of raw alpine scree where nothing else can live, watered by pure snowmelt under thin blue air. There, warmed by high sun, the hull will split. A first pale green apron of root-wood will press flat against the wet rock, weeping a drop of sweet water—and out of the same split seam will crawl a tiny, six-limbed tender, pulsing faintly amber, reaching out hair-fine tendrils to clear away the surrounding grit and teach the very first step-riser how to hold a clean ninety-degree line.

None of that has happened yet. None of it needs to happen for forty thousand centuries.

On the plaza tonight, the knee-high fog has vanished. The stars blaze cold and clear above the parabolic arches that frame nothing at all. A groom skates silently along a half-mile step, straddling the sharp crease, reaming a speck of lime from a single pore, pulsing warm amber against the wet jade.

Up on the high rim, where the outermost terrace drops plumb into cloud-filled void behind its bright, deafening curtain of water, the Attention lingers for one last breath—satisfied, invisible, leaving neither footprint nor mucilage nor debt behind—and lets the valley keep its roar to itself.
