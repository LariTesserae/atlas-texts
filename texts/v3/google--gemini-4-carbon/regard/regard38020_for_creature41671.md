A lesser mercy would have ruined everything in an afternoon.

A lesser mercy would have looked down through the grey lattice of deadwood, seen the blind, pleated thing scraping sickness off the walls of an ancient shelter, and wept for what it used to be. It would have remembered the upright creatures who laid the red brick and carved the stairs, and judged this flat, silent descendant a tragedy of shrinkage. Out of pity, it would have widened the jagged tear to let in the clean wind, poured sweet surface water into a pool at the bottom, cleared away the biting snakes and the choking moth-webs, or reached into that smooth, folded brain to rekindle speech and memory and the ache for horizons. Within a week, the wind would have parched the moss to powder, the standing water would have bred loud, stagnant rot, and the keeper—dazzled, deafened, and starved of the steam that feeds its skin—would have crawled under the bricks to die.

The power that stops above the baked plain is older and kinder than pity. It does not confuse human shapes with dignity, nor smallness with poverty. 

It pauses at the lip of the caprock without casting a shadow, reading the sinkhole not as a grave where something fell and broke, but as an ark that succeeded. It perceives the whole eighty-foot cylinder at once: the exquisite parsimony of calories, the courtesy of creatures that negotiate by touch rather than slaughter, the ten-second tide of a thousand moth-wings lifting warm dampness to the roof, and at the center of it all, the quietest carnivore on the continent tending a garden it cannot see with eyes it no longer needs.

To look upon a closed world that has achieved peace is, almost always, to receive the command: *Leave it alone.*

Almost.

Because this power sees across centuries as easily as across inches, it also sees the four slow clocks ticking toward the ruin of the pit—troubles too high, too deep, or too subtle for four-thumbed hands and geological patience to mend.

First, it looks at the ceiling. The bridged, interlocked roots of the dead surface-trees have held back the plain’s loose gravel and strained the brutal sun for four hundred years, kept stubbornly intact by mineralization from the rising vapor. Within another sixty, however, a main load-bearing taproot on the eastern rim will finally rot through at its collar. When it snaps, six tons of caprock will shear loose with it. The crash will crush the central cairns—and the fist-sized sleeper gestating beneath them—while tearing open a hole thirty feet wide. The noon sun will lance straight to the humus floor, cooking the mycelium white-hot by day and bleeding the chamber’s heat into the starlit desert by night.

Second, it looks two thousand feet down, through the rust-colored sandstone to the magmatic plumbing that warms the pit. Geothermy is restless. A deep vein of sour, sulfurous brine is migrating slowly eastward along a fault; in two centuries it will breach the sweet-water fissures beneath the chamber, turning the gentle subterranean breath acidic and choking every lung and gill-plate in the room.

Third, it looks at the semicircular terrace of dry-laid red brick, and at the pale land-crabs clinging to the niches. Here, the keeper’s own touching neatness has created a quiet trap. For generations, the lineage of keepers has dropped every cracked crab carapace and emptied snail shell into that dry basin to keep the vapor-vents clear. Because the overhang above the bricks never drips, the shells never dissolve. Slowly, pound by pound, century by century, all the calcium in this sealed little world is being locked away in a dry museum pile. The basin is finally full to the lip—and the crabs on the walls have grown soft, translucent, and paper-shelled for lack of lime in the stone-sweat.

Fourth, and smallest: in the deepest pocket beneath the middle cairn, the tiny, unformed successor waiting in its leathery membrane for the elder to grow old has a hairline tangle in its umbilical rootlet—a fluke of fungal crowding that will leave it slightly stunted when its turn comes to wake.

The power considers these four things. It knows that suddenness is a kind of violence, and miracles that announce themselves are merely vanity. Whatever is done here must happen at the speed of growing stone.

So it sets to work, making no sound at all.

---

Above, where the fierce sky hammers the deadwood lattice, nothing seems to change. No green shoots sprout to drink the pit’s precious moisture; no masonry appears. Instead, when the thin desert dust blows across the plain at dusk, grains of pure silica and iron-oxide catch in the checks and splinters of the ancient grey roots. Drawn inward by the rising damp from below, those minerals do not merely coat the wood; they begin, molecule by patient molecule, to replace the rotting cellulose from the core outward. Over seasons, the dead root-bridge quietly petrifies *in situ*. Its bark-grain remains rough enough for velvet geckos’ toes and moth-silk anchors; its gaps remain narrow enough to bruise the sunlight into amber and olive. Only now, the lattice is turning to agatized stone, fused seamlessly into the surrounding caprock—an unbreakable sieve that will hold its exact shadow for ten thousand years.

Far below the mattress of warm humus, deep in the blind dark of the bedrock where not even the pale roots reach, a hairline fracture gently pinches shut against the creeping sulfur vein, nudging the sour brine harmlessly away toward an empty salt-pan miles to the south. At the same time, a fresh seam of clean, deep groundwater is coaxed against the warm pluton, guaranteeing that the vapor rising into the pit will remain sweet, steady, and ninety-eight degrees until the plain itself erodes to sea level.

Inside the chamber, the adjustments are finer than a whipcord snake’s tongue.

Beneath the middle cairn, the choking mat of mycelium relaxes its grip by a quarter of an inch around the sleeping, fist-sized embryo; a rich drop of mineral-sweetened dampness wicks down through the mulch, unkinking the tiny nutrient cord so the next gardener will wake whole and unhurried when its decade arrives.

And under the semicircular brick terrace, a microscopic capillary channel opens in the sandstone floor—only beneath the very bottom layer of the centuries-old shell midden, three feet down in the dark. Warm, faintly carbonic sweat from the rock begins to kiss the oldest, chalkiest shards at the bottom of the heap. The terrace above stays entirely dry, just as the keeper likes it. No puddle forms, no rot smells. Only now, at the rate of a thimbleful a month, the bottom of the pile silently melts back into liquid mineral, wicking outward and upward through the porous sandstone walls to sweat out again through the ancient chisel-marks. 

Within a season, the brimming pile of shells settles downward by half an inch—making room for another lifetime of tidy housekeeping. Along the western wall, the grazing crabs scrape the enriched algae, and their new shells harden with a faint, healthy sheen of ivory.

---

That could be all. By every rule of stewardship, the work is finished. 

Yet wisdom is not cold, and love—even vast, planetary love—takes pleasure in courtesy. To mend someone’s roof while they sleep is charity; to meet them at the threshold and bow, according to the manners of their own house, is respect.

The power will not enter as a surface walker. It will not bring the stench of ozone, the blinding flare of a hundred-degree mammal, or the terrifying hammer of a hurried heart.

Instead, during the quietest hour of the afternoon, while the keeper hangs wedged in its dead-end sandstone stairway five feet above the floor, beginning to stir from a three-day torpor, a tiny shift occurs in the rock directly beneath its broad, grey-padded palm.

A palm-sized patch of the worn step warms by half a degree—not the blaze of an intruder, just the exact, living warmth of another creature like itself, resting on the other side of a paper-thin veil of stone.

The keeper stills. Its geckos pause on its shoulders, dark eyes unblinking. Above, the pale curtains of flightless moths open—*one, two, three, four*—and fan the mist upward.

Slowly, hydraulic and silent, the keeper uncurls its four-thumbed hand and presses its flat, sensitive palm flush against the warm spot on the step. Its two pink, velvet facial hollows tilt down toward the sandstone. Its boneless nasal filaments unfurl to taste the rock. 

There is no threat in the scent—only deep, clean rain that fell on the mountains before the plain was dry, wet iron, and crushed fern.

From within the sandstone step, directly against the creature’s palm, comes a vibration. 

*One. Two. Three.*

Three silent seconds—the exact cadence of two crabs meeting on a narrow ledge, the exact measure the keeper itself presses into the throat of a stranger to test what belongs here and what must give way. Only there is no contest of strength in this tremor, though the keeper’s nerves, tuned to the weight of walls, can dimly sense an immensity behind that small warmth—something heavier than the caprock, deeper than the fissures, vaster than the fierce sky above.

At the end of the third second, the immensity yields.

Deliberately, gently, the warmth in the stone steps backward, cooling by a fraction of a degree in the unmistakable, ancient posture of deference: *This stair is yours. The room is yours. Pass in peace.*

For a long minute, the blind gardener remains crouched on the worn step that leads nowhere, its four thumbs spread wide over the fading warmth. Nothing in its smooth, folded brain forms a word or an image of a god. It experiences only a sudden, profound loosening in the oiled sheaths of its tendons—a deep somatic certainty, felt from the lichen-creases of its neck to the calloused pads of its heels, that the walls are thick, the steam will not fail, and the cairns stand straight.

A fat drop of condensation gathers on the maroon underside of an aroid leaf overhead, swells, and falls—*pat*—onto the pleated terracotta mantle of its back, channeling neatly down the labyrinth of its skin to wet the corner of its mouth. 

The keeper drinks. It reaches behind its hip, pinches a stray guy-line of moth-silk carefully between two blunt thumbs, anchors it safely to a fern stem, and glides silently across the sweating stone to check the crabs.
