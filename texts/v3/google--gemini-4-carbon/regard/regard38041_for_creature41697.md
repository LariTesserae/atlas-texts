To a lesser benevolence—one still enamored of its own reflection, one that mistakes green leaves for virtue and dry, sunlit libraries for the pinnacle of mind—this plateau would look like a purgatory in need of rescue. A lesser god would itch to crack the flagstones, to scatter seeds of mountain pine, to clear the heavy mist with a brisk, drying wind, or to quicken the slow, heavy blood of the Sump-wrights with restless, sky-gazing ambition.

The Wise Power does none of these things. 

It arrives without footprint or shadow, settling into the thin, warm air of the tableland simply as an attention—vast, quiet, and diagnostic. It listens first. For a long turn of the high sun, it lays its awareness flat against the wet basalt, much as the master boiler-tender lays his calloused palm against the sweating iron of the steam-main. 

It feels the astonishing, hard-won grace of this equilibrium. Here is a civilization that has survived the usually fatal adolescence of industry—not by retreating from the machine, nor by letting the machine devour the world, but by waiting until the machine became rock, and the flesh became water, and the two learned to digest one another without fever. There is no malice in the flooded avenues. There is no frantic hoarding, no terror of the dark, no alienation between the hand that turns the bronze spanner and the snail that rasps the lime. To introduce "progress" here would be to introduce famine; to introduce grass would be to starve the sulfur-mats that feed the vats that feed the children.

The Power recognizes a masterpiece of patience, and bows to it.

Yet, because its gaze penetrates deeper than ten generations of bronze tally-rings—down through six thousand feet of stepped basalt into the magma-chambers, and forward across the slow grind of continental plates—it sees the three quiet thieves that even the wisest closed loop cannot entirely bar. 

First: the boilers do not stay warm by wish alone. Deep beneath the foundry-wards, whether they draw upon thick seams of ancient coal or tap the upward-bleeding heat of volcanic vents, geological wealth is finite, and geothermal throats eventually choke with precipitated silica or shift sideways as the crust drifts. If the water cools by ten degrees across a century, the yeast-vats falter, the mist turns to sleet, and the great, oiled reef freezes on its perches.

Second: no recycling is absolute. Every year, at the far, forgotten edges of the half-continent where the stepped basins finally meet the sheer drop to the lowlands, a little warm brine, a little dissolved copper, a little ground bone-phosphorus spills over the rim and is lost forever to the sea below. Over fifty millennia, the plateau will slowly bleach itself of trace metals and salts—the bronze spanners growing scarcer, the shells of the cobblestone-filterers growing brittle and thin.

Third: there is a small, uncomplaining sorrow at the edges of old age. The street-corner vendors sitting waist-deep in the hot basins, their eyes clouded white by mineral cataracts from seventy years of alkali-steam and high-altitude ultraviolet glare, going blind decade before their strong, slow hearts are ready to stop.

A wise power does not break the grammar of a world to fix its spelling. It will not drop miracles from the sky; miracles create priests, and priests create edges where before there were none. If a gift is to be given to plumbers and masons, it must arrive as plumbing and masonry.

So the Power reaches down into the deep lithic dark, three miles beneath the lowest sump, where no auger has ever bitten.

There, where the roots of the plateau press into the mantle, it makes a subtle, permanent adjustment to the planetary plumbing. It coaxes open a network of deep, self-scouring convection fissures, marrying the abyssal heat of the world’s core to the deep aquifers beneath the city, and lining the stone throats with stable, catalytic crystal lattices that will not scale over or choke with salt for fifty million years. Up through these new arteries—slowly, gently, matching the exact pressure-settings recorded on the wardens' oldest bronze tallies—rises not just immortal warmth, but a measured, inexhaustible upwelling of juvenile mineral wealth: dissolved iron, copper sulfides, phosphorus, potassium, and trace salts leached safely from the deep mantle rock. 

A century from now, when an ancient coal-seam finally plays out or an old geothermal well goes tepid, a crew of Brine-walkers probing a lower terrace will strike a new vent of sweet, hot, mineral-heavy steam. They will nod, consult their brass gauges, rivet a collar over it, and stamp a new tally-ring, never suspecting anything more than the good fortune of solid rock.

Next, the Power turns its attention to the yellow bacterial mats and the black biofilms clinging to the warm spillways. 

With a touch finer than sunlight, it nudges a single strand of plasmid DNA inside a pale, rare strain of spring-water microbe flourishing near the drinking-condensers and the street-corner soaking basins. Nothing outward changes—only a subtle new metabolic byproduct in the clear, warm mist rising from the public baths. The vapor now carries trace, soothing esters and gentle chelating compounds that dissolve calcium-alkali deposits on contact with living tissue, while reinforcing the UV-absorbing pigments of that sweeping third eyelid. 

Over the next two generations, the young will notice—if they notice at all—that their grandmothers and grandfathers sitting waist-deep in the broth-basins still have dark, clear eyes at eighty. The elders will still be able to read the stamped notches on the bronze rings, still watch the fat locusts drift through shafts of afternoon steam, still recognize the faces of their great-grandchildren right up until the afternoon their chests finally still and they are carried up to the sunlit slate.

And then—having secured the heat, balanced the salts, and cleared the eyes of the old—the Power lingers for one last moment on the rear platform of the municipal maintenance tram, right beside the conductor with his felted feather-mantle and his clinking harness of oiled tools.

The tram rolls through the half-inch sheet of pewter-bright water: *clang... clang... clang*. The amphibians part like warm grease and close again behind. Up ahead, the bank of bright white cloud hangs steady among the smokestacks.

The Power asks itself whether a mind so thoroughly wedded to stone and water lacks anything of the spirit. Should they know that stars wheel beyond the steam-plumes? Should they know that other minds, dry and quick and fragile, live far away across the sea or among the spheres?

The conductor leans his broad, slate-gray forearm on the verdigris brass of the brake. A bare-throated scavenger drops onto the tram-rail beside his elbow, folding its wet-coal wings with a soft, leathery sigh, and tilts its head to let the high mountain sun warm its copper-tinted throat. Without looking down, the conductor reaches out one thick, horn-plated finger and strokes the bird’s warm breastbone once, feeling the slow, steady knock of its heart—the exact rhythm of the atmospheric engine venting two terraces down. Then his throat-pouch swells, and he lets out a low, resonant hum—not a signal to the driver this time, not a warning about shell-crust on the rails, just a single, rich harmonic note pitched purely for the pleasure of feeling the brick facades on either side of the avenue hum back at him in sympathy.

Up at the prow, the driver hears it, smiles behind his heavy, oiled lids, and hums a fifth below it—completing a chord that ripples outward in tiny, geometric rings across a quarter-mile of shallow, sunlit water.

The Power listens to the chord fade into the vast, wet roar of ten million living throats, and smiles its own invisible smile. They already know what the stars are made of; they are standing in it, keeping it warm.

It touches nothing else. It leaves no name, no omen, no disturbance in the mist. It simply withdraws into the clear, pale altitude above the plateau—though every few thousand years, when its path brings it past this world, it pauses for an afternoon just to listen to the cranes turn, and feel the bass thrum of the turbines rising steadily through the immortal stone.
