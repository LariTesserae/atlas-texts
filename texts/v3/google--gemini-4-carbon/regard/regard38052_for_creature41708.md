A lesser power, looking down through the ice-ash ceiling at those tiny, grease-stained knots of life clinging to a continent of freezing ironwood, would be seized by pity.

Pity is dangerous in the hands of a god. Pity wants to flatten the forty-five-degree pitch into a meadow. It wants to tear away the stratospheric veil, flood the bruised indigo dark with blinding yellow noon, calm the howling root-voids, and soften the flint-sharp timber into warm, forgiving loam. 

A wise power looks longer, listens through the grain as they do, and stays its hand. 

Look at their long, prehensile toes holding the bone awl; look at the silver-blue flash behind their wide retinas; listen to the six-part humming inside a resin-sealed hut, tuned to the quarter-tone against ten million tons of strained timber. To give these people sun would be to blind them. To still the wind would silence their compasses. To stop the world-tree from growing—to freeze their towns into safe, dead permanence—would turn supreme engineers of tension and acoustics into museum pieces stranded on a corpse. Their harshness is not a curse they are waiting to be rescued from; it is the exact shape of their genius.

So the Power does not descend in light, which here means fire, terror, and snow-blindness. It does not speak from the sky, where the gale throws voices away. 

Instead, it goes five miles down.

Down past the vaulting highways and the hunting grounds of the hook-claws; down through the howling, miles-wide polygons of black air; down through the roaring warm chimneys where subterranean water turns to mist; all the way to the crushed, pressurized dark where the continent-tree’s titanic taproots finally meet the forgotten stone of the world’s core. 

Here, the Power places a hand against the wood—not to command it, for the tree, too, is a magnificent creature doing what it was born to do—but to introduce a minuscule, subtle mutation into its deepest vascular memory, and to nudge two small biological threads already clinging to its bark.

Three things change. None of them look like miracles. All of them take generations to be fully understood.

***

**First: The Warning Note.**

The greatest cruelty of the upper lattice is not the cold, nor the predators, nor the drop. They have mastered all three. The cruelty is the blind caprice of the shear—when a three-generation town around a tarn-lake splits open in a single afternoon, swallowing forty sleeping huts, the bone-chimes, the children, and the ambered dead before anyone can reach their harness-knives.

Deep in the cambium, the Power alters how the mega-flora Equalizes tension before a major structural split. 

Now, when two county-sized boughs fuse into a knot, the wood along the hidden fault-line begins to deposit microscopic, hollow crystals of mineralized sap—tiny resonant chambers laid down along the stress-grain years before the wood actually reaches its breaking point.

Three generations after the Power’s touch, a family sits wedged inside their warm, lanolin-smelling barnacle-hut at a crossroads knot. Outside, the stratospheric gale screams as usual. Inside, they hum their low, anchoring chord against the familiar bass drone of the trunk.

Suddenly, through their shoulder blades and the bare, ridged soles of their feet, a new vibration threads upward from deep inside the bough. 

It is not the sudden artillery *boom* of an imminent fracture. It is a slow, rhythmic, unmistakable *thrum-thrum-thrum*, pitched like a plucked sinew string, repeating once every twenty breaths. As the deep root-tension inches closer to critical, the crystalline pockets micro-fracture in sequence, singing through the ironwood grain not an hour before disaster, but *six days* before. 

And the sap that begins to weep along the doomed fault-line no longer smells merely of mint and cold iron. Mixed with the crushed crystals, it releases a sharp, unmistakable tang of sweet cinnamon and ozone that cuts straight through grease-smoke and turpentine.

The tree has not stopped growing. Towns still must move. Nothing has been made soft or permanent. 

But now, the tree *speaks before it turns over in its sleep*. A settlement has a hundred hours to unlash the hide airlocks, coil the bone-chime bridges, drain the grease-bowls, tap the warning out across the sounding-fins to three neighboring ridges—*The knot sings; clear the wet-side tarn; we move highward*—and climb away without losing a single child to the dark. Over centuries, they come to call that six-day pulse *the Polite Groan*, and they strike a matching rhythm back into the wood before they depart: a thank-you note tapped from one living thing to another.

***

**Second: The Deep-Moss.**

Fire is their narrowest bottleneck. Every drop of hot sap-syrup, every cast spearhead of black pitch-iron, every sixty-degree bubble of hut-warmth depends on rendered browser-tallow and glider-fat. When the herds migrate around the curve of a five-mile trunk, or the thermal plumes shift away from a splinter-crag for a season, the lamps gutter out. Cold creeps in; water cannot be boiled to split timber; fingers stiffen until they miss their handholds on the rime.

High in the wind-shadows, huddled in tiny black mats, frost-moss already grows. The Power reaches into its starved cells and wakes up a dormant chemosynthetic pathway, teaching a cousin-strain to drink not the faint indigo starlight, but the violent mechanical energy of the tree itself—piezoelectric trickles and friction-heat generated where massive bark-plates grind micro-inches against one another in the constant wind.

Walkers begin finding it tucked deep inside the raw, dry V-clefts where great branches flex hardest: thick, velvety cushions of a dull, bruised plum color. 

When untouched, it is barely warm. When peeled from the cleft in heavy, rubbery loaves, carried inside a hut, packed tight into a carved heartwood bowl, and *squeezed* firmly under a tensioned wooden clamp—mimicking the crushing flex of the tree—it wakes up. 

It does not burn. It produces no soot to choke barrel-chested lungs in an airtight room, and it cannot catch the floor on fire if kicked over in a gale. Compressed in its clamp, a loaf the size of two fists glows with a dim, velvet-violet bioluminescence that never triggers snow-blindness in wide, silver-backed eyes—and radiates a steady, dry, smokeless baking heat for forty hours before exhausting itself into a brittle, lightweight ash (which, they soon discover, makes an even stronger temper for pitch-iron than bone-dust).

Fat is still prized for eating, for boiling sap fast, and for greasing cheeks against the wind. Drop-hunters still leap into the howling steam with bone clackers between their teeth. Life still demands nerve and speed. 

Yet now, an old carpenter whose ear-bones have thickened can sit in a warm hut during a lean month without burning his grandchildren's food for heat. Now, a lone traveler caught on a leeward cliff when a sudden slush-cascade drenches their mittens can pull a clamped fistful of bruise-moss from their belt-pouch, press their ridged, freezing palms directly against its fever-warm velvet, regain the nerve-sensitivity in their fingertips, and *listen* their way safely home.

***

**Third: The Singing Amber.**

The third gift has nothing to do with survival. The Power gives it solely out of respect for who they are.

Because these people own nothing heavy—because stone does not exist and every town eventually splits open—their history is fragile. A song lasts only as long as the throats that hum it; a family’s lineage is only a sweeter, denser *thock* beneath a mallet when passing a grandfather’s amber-scarred shoot, soon overgrown by six feet of new bark as the highway vaults higher into the sky.

The Power touches the defensive sap of those high, indigo-lit shoots—only in the places where cambium is deliberately opened by bone chisels and met with the salt and warmth of skin.

Nothing looks different at the funeral. The dead walker is still folded knees-to-chin, striker-mallet and climbing-pegs hugged to their barrel chest, wedged high into the dry cliff of a young, sky-reaching bough. The clear, mint-sharp blood of the world-tree still floods the pocket and cures over a season into golden-black glass.

Only now, as the resin polymerizes around them, it aligns its long molecular chains to the exact acoustic signature of whatever vibrations pass through the wood while it sets.

During that first season, the mourners come up to the high shoot, press their bare palms and foreheads against the freezing bark beside the amber window, and tap their mallets gently—or press their throats right against the timber and hum. They hum how she rigged a three-hundred-yard line across the Splitting Tarn in a crosswind; they click-speak the names of her cousins; they tap out the joke he always struck on the home-fin when returning empty-handed from the wet side; they pass into the curing sap the exact pitch of the bough-chord their family sang together in the dark.

Decades pass. Centuries pass. New bark rolls thick over the scar, lifting the ancestor hundreds of yards higher toward the bruised twilight as the continent-tree reaches for the hidden star. The great bough widens into a valley-sized highway.

A young drop-hunter, ten generations removed, climbs alone along the dry ridge, spiked heels *snick-snicking* in the thin, razor-cold air. Tired, she stops in a wind-shadow, pulls off one thick three-layer mitten, and presses her bare, deeply ridged palm flat against a subtle, resonant swell in the ancient bark to listen for hook-claws and rising thermals.

Underneath the roar of the root-voids, underneath the artillery boom of cold sap miles down, the warmth and pressure of her living hand wake a subtle sympathetic vibration in the buried lens of cured resin six feet inside the heartwood.

Up through her wrist, into the bones of her forearm, and straight into her chest cavity rises a ghost-chord. 

Clear, intricate, unmistakable: five voices humming a low, steady harmony in a sixty-degree room long since swallowed by the abyss, threaded through with the rapid, laughing *boom-tak-tak-boom* of a knotwood mallet saying, *Two walkers, coming up from the wet side, wind rising behind us—put the tallow on.*

She keeps her eyes closed in the indigo dark. She presses her cheek to the freezing ironwood, matches her breath to the dead woman’s hum, learns a harmony her village forgot two hundred years ago, taps a polite two-beat reply into the grain with her toe-peg, pulls her mitten back on, and climbs higher into the roaring world.

***

Far below, down where stone remembers light, the Power withdraws its hand from the root. 

It leaves no name for them to worship, no priesthood, no law, and no promise to catch them if they miss a harpoon-throw in the dark. It simply watches for another thousand years with quiet, fierce delight, listening to the continent-spanning harp grow richer and stranger, chord by chord, as the tree and its children climb together toward the ice-ash sky.
