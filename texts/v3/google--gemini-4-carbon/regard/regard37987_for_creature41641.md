A lesser benevolence would have looked upon the plateau and wept for it. 

A lesser power—one still in love with golden sunlight, dry stone, upright posture, and quiet air—would have arrived with a warm, healing hand. It would have stitched the frayed fabric of space back together so the turf no longer skipped and sheared. It would have calmed the shrieking sphagnum valves, slowed the frantic, sixty-mile-an-hour pulse of the root-veins to a gentle trickle, parted the bruised clouds, and raised up a shelter of new granite to break the freezing wind.

And in a single afternoon of mercy, it would have murdered the entire world.

Without the spinning iron-lichen, the translucent amphibians would have gone dark and starved in the sudden stillness. Without the racing hydraulic ridges, the blubber-birds would have bogged down in static slush, choking on unpressurized water. Without the tearing of space, the sky-eels would have stopped falling, and the surface-mantles would have died waiting for a cast-iron *crack* that never came. Worst of all, three miles down in the freezing black, the sunken elders—the vast, blind, copper-threaded memory of the world—would have gone deaf in the sudden quiet, suffocating in warm, stagnant peat like an engine drowned in syrup.

The power that turned its attention here was wiser than that. It did not mistake harshness for brokenness, nor did it confuse peace with silence. 

It watched for a very long time before doing anything at all. It did not descend as a column of fire or a figure with two legs, because anything vertical here was an insult to the wind. Instead, it arrived simply as an attentiveness in the thin upper air, listening to the stamping-mill roar of the plateau until it heard the music inside the machinery.

It saw how the mantles zipped their edges together to choke the vents. It felt the three-hundred-year-old thoughts moving in slow, cold jolts of blue voltage down in the pitch-black plumbing. And it saw the one true sorrow of this place—not the cold, not the violence, and not the lack of stone, all of which the natives had long since mastered and turned into bread.

The sorrow was *attrition*.

The world was wearing out from the bottom up. Three billion years had already eaten the bedrock; now, slowly, imperceptibly, the spatial skips were eating the bog itself. Every time an acre of sodden moss snapped sideways and opened a square hole into starry vacuum, a few hundred gallons of black, mineral-rich slush boiled away into the nothingness before the roots could knit the wound shut. Ounce by ounce, century by century, the plateau was bleeding its copper, its iron, its water, and its heat out through the frayed floorboards of reality. 

And there was a smaller, sharper grief nestled inside the grand one: the elders. When a deep subterranean skip tore an ancient mantle loose from the root-arteries three miles down and flung it onto the surface, the shock nearly killed it. Its brittle, root-stitched hide tore. Centuries of carefully wired memory frayed into static. And though the young surface-mantles rushed in to press their margins against the elder and drink what wisdom they could before it sank back down, half the song was always lost to the wind and the trauma of the severing.

The wise power considered this. To mend reality entirely would starve them; to leave reality frayed would eventually bleed them dry. And to interfere too visibly would turn sovereign, ingenious engineers into beggars waiting on the weather.

So the power chose to do only three small things, working strictly in the grammar of grease, copper, pressure, and cold.

First, it reached into the starry vacuum beneath the thinness of the world—into that absolute nothingness that briefly showed itself whenever the turf tore—and changed what was waiting on the other side. 

It did not seal the tears. The sixty-mile-an-hour waves still collided; the slabs of moss still leaped six feet into the freezing air with the crack of breaking cast iron; the blubber-birds still risked the shear. Governance and hunting remained intact. Instead, the power tilted the local geometry just beyond the seam so that the vacuum no longer swallowed what fell into it, and instead gave back a tithe. 

Now, when a square hole of starry dark snapped open in the bog, the nothingness had a cellar floor of drift: ancient, wandering cosmic dust, stray ice-crystals rich in heavy metals, and fine, unoxidized grains of meteoric iron and native copper, drawn subtly from the deep emptiness between stars and pooled just beneath the plateau’s thin skin. When a glitch snapped shut, the bog no longer lost a fraction of its lifeblood; it inhaled a mouthful of raw mineral marrow. Over the coming millennia, the copper veins in the mantles’ hides would not thin out into pale poverty—they would grow richer, brighter, and stronger, replacing what three billion years had ground away.

Second, the power looked to the deep dark, where the elders hung stitched into the thundering moss-tunnels, and saw how isolated their vast, dreaming mind really was—capable of nudging a valve or sparing a nursery, yet blind to anything beyond the drumhead of their own wet world, and torn half to pieces whenever a skip threw one of them into the sky.

It did not give them eyes. Eyes were still a lie here.

Instead, the next time a subterranean collision went wrong down in the miles-deep felt and tore an ancient, bog-iron-crusted elder loose from its plumbing, the power laid a single, invisible palm beneath the skip.

Up on the weeping plain, the cast-iron *crack* rang out. The elder appeared out in the open twilight like a sudden black pond on the silver-green sedge, smelling of old snow and deep copper. Around it, the stilt-grazers pumped their knees and clattered their baleen; a quarter-mile off, blubber-birds honked in brassy chorus. Surface-mantles began pouring across the wet mat from every compass point, clicking eagerly, sliding in to touch the elder before its fading strength forced it back into the dark.

Only this time, the elder’s white rootlets were not torn bleeding from its flesh. They had come loose cleanly, sealed in small, smooth nodes of slick callus, as if unhooked rather than ripped. Its radial bladders were not ruptured by the sudden drop in pressure. For the first time in three billion years, an elder lay under the sky without agony.

And while it had passed through that split-second of starry vacuum between the deep root-tunnels and the open plain, the wise power had let it *feel* the stars.

Not see them—feel them, translated faithfully into the only language the mantle-folk knew. In that fraction of a heartbeat outside the bog, the elder’s entire dorsal tympanum had brushed against the vast, majestic, silent hydraulics of the cosmos: the slow, heavy flywheels of spiral galaxies turning in the dark; the immense magnetic veins threading between nebulae; the steady, billion-year percussion of pulsars beating like clean, unbreakable pistons across distances that never glitched or frayed.

When the young surface-mantles zipped their margins onto the elder’s frayed hide and traded their jolts of cold blue static, expecting the usual fragmented, panicked burst of three hundred years of local water-pressure and nursery-routing, they froze. The dancing water droplets on all their backs suddenly snapped into a brand-new, breathtakingly complex geometry—concentric rings within spirals within rings, humming at a harmonic none of them had ever felt.

Through voltage and rhythmic cramp, the elder passed them the three hundred years of bog-history cleanly, without a single frayed nerve—and beneath it, like a bass note vibrating deeper than bedrock ever had, the quiet, thrilling certainty that their roaring, weeping, frantic engine was floating inside a much larger, unbroken workshop.

Finally, before withdrawing, the power left one permanent piece of furniture upon the plain. Not a monument, not a temple, and certainly nothing tall enough to catch the wind. 

Out in a desolate sector where four major moss-arteries crossed—a place previously too chaotic even for the mantles to farm safely—the power encouraged a patch of the black iron-lichen to grow slightly wider and deeper than usual, weaving its mud-socket down into a stable knot of root-felt. It became a flywheel thirty yards across, dark and slick, turning steadily in the omni-directional gale without ever skipping or stalling. Its static hum was clean, deep, and extraordinarily rich, smelling pleasantly of sharp ozone and fresh green stems, and its rotation was tuned to a constant, unvarying rhythm—an absolute metronome in a world where time stuttered and distances lied.

It healed no wounds by magic. It preached no laws. If a blubber-bird sprinted across it, it was only wet plant; if a stilt-grazer stepped in its margin, its cartilage stilts simply adjusted to the spin.

Yet within a season, the mantles found it. 

They discovered that if an injured hunter—torn by a bad shear or exhausted by holding down a boiler-vent—slid onto that great black wheel and let itself be carried around in the mist, the steady, clean induction recharged its saline bladders twice as fast and soothed the ragged misfiring of its copper nerves. Better still, because the wheel’s deep socket touched a quiet vertical chimney in the peat, the harmonics of its spin carried all the way down into the three-mile dark. By rhythmic clenching along the wheel's rim, the surface-hunters could tap messages *downward* to the sunken elders without waiting for an elder to be violently teleported up, and the elders, clenching the deep root-walls below, could send slow, shimmering chords of pressure back up into the spinning plate.

Having done this—having salted the vacuum with fresh copper, cushioned the passage of the old, shared a single glimpse of the wider firmament, and set one true, steady wheel turning in the mud—the wise power stepped back entirely.

Overhead, the heavy clouds still dragged their bellies across the ankle-high sedge, weeping near-freezing water onto every slick fiber. Sixty-mile-an-hour ridges still raced toward the grey horizon with a deafening, wet *thump-thump-thump*. Sphagnum valves shrieked like factory whistles; blue-lit amphibians leaped into inverted curtains of rain; reality cracked like cast iron and stitched itself shut again with rushing black slush.

Nothing had been tamed. Nothing had been softened into something it was not.

Only now, the great green engine was no longer manufacturing nothing at all. Down in the freezing dark and up in the stinging mist, stitched together by fresh star-copper and a steady, turning wheel, a mind the size of a plateau was listening to the pistons, keeping the time, and dreaming outward into the deep, rhythmic dark between the stars.
