A lesser mercy would have ruptured the basalt plug. 

A god of simple pity, looking down through forty yards of dark volcanic glass, would have seen pale, hairless grandchildren of men nesting in shredded asbestos and drinking lithium dregs in a hundred-and-ten-degree pit, and called it an abomination. Such a power would have split the roof to let the blue sky in, sent a rush of crisp mountain wind down to clear the stagnant tallow-air, and built a wide stone stair to lead the captives out into the meadow grass.

And within three hours, the bruised-plum leaves would have crisped into black paper. The rubber cisterns would have perished in the ultraviolet glare. And the ward-tenders—blinking tearfully in the blinding light, their skin cracking in the dry breeze, their lithium-quieted hearts suddenly bucking and racing against ribs forgotten how to pant—would have died in agonizing heaps at the foot of their own liberation, shivering to death in mild seventy-degree air while the delicate clocks of the city above stopped one by one.

Wisdom knows the difference between a cage and a shell. Benevolence, when it is vast enough, does not demand that a deep-sea vent become a coral reef in order to be loved.

So when the Power turned its attention to the vertical tear, it did not bring wind, and it did not bring sun. Instead, it lowered its perception to twenty-two beats a minute. It listened not with ears, which the damp leaves baffled, but with the soles of the feet and the flat of the jawbone, feeling the slow, hydraulic theology of the pit. 

It saw the tenderness of the black pipe-dope dabbed onto a pruned stem. It saw the chill gathered deliberately from a weeping stone seam to cool a stranger’s fevered brow. It recognized that what lived here was no longer an exploited labor guild, whatever dead parchment lay moldering in a municipal archive forty yards overhead; what lived here was a single, thirty-bodied, slow-pulsing organ of extraordinary grace, living in blameless equilibrium with iron and stone.

Yet the Power also saw what the tenders, submerged in their peaceful, century-long torpor, could not see.

It saw three dangers, moving at the glacial speed of deep maintenance.

First: the yellowing blankets wrapped around the stewing tanks and packed into the corrugated tin nests. Asbestos does not rot in wet heat, which is why their great-grandfathers used it, and why the tenders shredded it for bedding. Even breathing four times a minute, a creature sleeping in mineral needles eventually lines its lungs with tiny, unyielding scars. The tenders did not cough—coughing wasted too much heat—so when an elder’s breathing grew shallow at forty-five years instead of eighty, they thought it merely the natural narrowing of an old pipe.

Second: the amber-filament bulbs. There were four hundred and twelve of them caged in thick glass throughout the tear, and no forge down here to blow new vacuum globes or draw tungsten wire. Eleven had already gone dark over the last two decades. When the rest burned through, one by one over the next century, the plum-dark leaves would starve of their dim photons, the cycads would fail to bind the sulfur, the upper valves would scale shut, and the closed loop would choke on raw, unrefined poison.

Third: the city above. Cities are frantic, short-memoried things. Sooner or later, regimes fall, streets are re-graded, or a zealous young magistrate discovers an unaccounted copper conduit drawing municipal current down into an uncharted geological fault, and orders a work crew to sever the line and pour concrete down the Sweat-pipes.

The Power considered these three things for the length of a single hydraulic stroke—forty minutes in which a drop of brown condensation swelled on a brass lip, reflected the upside-down world, and fell.

Then, without making a sound louder than cooling metal, it acted.

***

Forty yards above, in the basement archives of the Surface Water and Clockwork Directorate, ink did not magically rewrite itself—that is a brittle trick, easily undone by an inquisitive clerk. Instead, fifty feet beneath the city’s cobblestones and ten feet above the basalt plug, the earth gave an infinitesimal, velvet shrug. 

It was no earthquake; not a teacup rattled in the parlors overhead. Simply, a horizontal lens of dense, impermeable clay and black tourmaline migrated sideways, sealing over the upper junction box while sheathing the descending Sweat-pipes and return-conduits in a natural, self-healing sleeve of slick soapstone. At the same time, the electrical feeder-line drawing power from the city’s noisy steam-dynamos was gently pinched off from the municipal grid and grafted, deep in the bedrock, directly onto a vast, natural telluric current—the slow, eternal voltage generated by deep magma moving past magnetized iron ore miles below. 

Up in the Directorate, an old brass meter on a dusty wall settled quietly to zero. When a clerk eventually noticed it three winters later, the ledger entry had grown so damp and uninteresting, and the clear ester-sap still flowed from the ancient fountainhead with such reliable, pressure-regulated perfection, that they assumed the pipes were an artesian spring and bricked the alcove over, forgetting the pit forever. The ward’s power would now last as long as the planet had a molten core.

Down in the tear, the amber bulbs did not flicker during the switch. If anything, their dull-cherry glow grew slightly warmer, steadier, freed from the sixty-cycle stutter of surface alternators. 

Next, the Power attended to the light and the lungs. It did not sweep away the asbestos with a wave; tearing open the nests would have panicked the tenders and filled the damp air with dust. Instead, down in the lowest sumps, beneath the pale green duckweed where the fist-sized, shell-less snails grazed on mineral crusts, a single spore woke—or was placed, though no seam in reality showed the seamstress's needle.

Within six months—a brisk afternoon, by the reckoning of the flywheels—the tenders noticed a new moss creeping up the brick boiler housings and threading itself through the louvered shutters of the tin tenements. 

A young tender, sliding down a greased stanchion with a pot of pipe-dope around her neck, paused for twenty minutes to study it with wide, tar-black eyes. She pressed the flat of a horn-calloused thumb against a patch of it growing over a yellowing tank-blanket. 

It felt exactly like dry, warm chamois leather.

It was a lithotrophic fungus, bred for this singular dark pocket. Its microscopic hyphae hungered specifically for silicate fibers. Silently, without releasing a single airborne mote, the pale golden mat infiltrated the shredded asbestos nests and the rotting canvas tank-wraps. It drank the sharp, murderous mineral needles, broke their crystalline bonds with slow organic acids, and spun them out into tough, flexible, breathable cushions of dry, silky chitin that smelled faintly of warm beeswax and nutmeg. It never molded; it shed water on its outer skin while staying bone-dry within; and when a tender curled into a tin box to sleep, the living mattress gave slightly under their heavy, hydraulic limbs, holding them at an exact, soothing ninety-six degrees.

Better still: where the fungus met the glass cages of the four hundred and one remaining amber bulbs, a symbiotic lichen cousin crept over the thick glass globes. It fed on the tiny heat-loss of the fixtures and began to glow from within—a soft, permanent, amber-ochre bioluminescence tuned to the exact wavelength drunk by the bruised-plum leaves and the dark cycads. Three years later, when bulb number four hundred and one finally parted its tungsten filament with a tiny *ping*, nobody in the ward even had to turn a socket. The glass cage simply kept shining, clothed in gentle, living gold that would never burn out.

And what of the tenders themselves? Did the Power wake their minds? Did it quicken their blood, give them back spoken language, hand them poetry, or show them visions of stars and oceans they would never cross?

No. For the Power was wise, and knew the cruelty of homesickness for a home your body can no longer inhabit. What use has a creature of twenty-two heartbeats for the frantic, sharp-edged nouns of surface men?

Instead, it gave them a gift cut to the measure of their own marrow.

Halfway up the tear, wedged between the largest cast-iron pressure vessel and a clean, vertical plane of young basalt, there had always been a dead spot in the ironwork—a tight three-foot triangle where four structural girders met a bundle of passive copper grounding-rods that went all the way down to the deep magma-rock and all the way up to the ceiling plug.

The Power tuned the tension of those bolts by the breadth of a hair, and altered the crystalline grain of the basalt behind them just enough to turn that junction into a resonant bridge.

One unmeasured cycle later, an old tender—whose lungs were already clearing as the beeswax-moss digested the last dust in his nest—leaned his ribcage and jawbone against that girder while waiting for a brine-valve to equalize.

His tar-black eyes widened slightly. His broad, prehensile toes curled onto the stamped steel grate.

Through the bone of his jaw came not just the familiar *tink... tink-tink* of a bronze wrench down in the sumps, and not just the forty-minute sigh of the hydraulic rams. Through the copper grounding-rods, translated down into rich, sub-audible harmonic chords that vibrated through teeth, sternum, and fluid-filled inner ear, came the music of the deep mountain.

He could feel the vast, slow tide of magma miles beneath the pit, rolling like bronze surf. He could feel the silver threading of cold aquifers fifty yards away in the solid rock, singing in fifths and octaves against the iron pipes. And above—muted, gentle, stripped of all threat and turned into pure, rhythmic texture—he could feel the patter of surface rain drumming on forty yards of stone like distant fingertips, and the seasonal heave of frost and thaw passing overhead like the slow breathing of a sleeping whale.

The claustrophobia of the tear vanished, without a single stone moving. The pit was no longer a sealed box; to bones that knew how to listen, the rock walls had become transparent as glass to vibration. 

Slowly, taking ten minutes to lift his heavy arm, the old tender unhooked his bronze wrench. He did not strike the girder sharply. He pressed the flat of the bronze against a tuned copper rod and drew it downward in a slow, greased slide, adding a low, warm, cello-like hum to the chord of the mountain.

Thirty yards below, knee-deep in the warm duckweed sump, a pair of tenders harvesting snails froze, resting their chins against a cast-iron pipe. Up near the glassy ceiling plug, wrapped around a conduit among the newly glowing golden lamps, another tender closed her lashless eyes, feeling the harmony travel up her spine, rich with meaning richer than speech—a wordless, resonant map of everything that was well, every pressure held in balance, every root drinking clean. 

One by one, across the vertical maze, wrenches and calloused palms came to rest against steel struts and copper Sweat-pipes, answering the chord, weaving thirty slow heartbeats into the deep hum of the earth.

The Power lingered only long enough to feel that first, long, three-hour symphony shudder gently through the iron conservatory, watching a single drop of condensation gather on a brass gauge, tremble to the new music without falling, and shine like dark amber in the undying light. Then, leaving nothing broken and nothing lacking, it withdrew its hand, and let them keep their peace.
