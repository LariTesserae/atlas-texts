A lesser power, looking down into that thrashing, sunlit abyss, would feel pity. 

A lesser power would see the ruptured eardrums of twenty-year-olds, the fingers locked permanently into scarred hooks, the sheer, exhausting terror of a world without a floor, and decide to *fix* it. It would reach down and calm the shearing crosswinds. It would thicken the stone needles into safe, broad pillars, or raise the floor of the basin until trees could root in quiet dirt, turning the roaring void into a gentle, verdant valley where children could walk upright and speak aloud in soft voices.

And in doing so, it would commit a quiet, total murder. 

Because a wise power looks into the basin and does not see misery. It sees a masterpiece of fierce, terrifying grace. To still the wind here would be to suffocate the cloud-grazers waiting for their eight minutes of sideways mist; it would starve the heavy-boned plumb-bob hunters hanging beneath the arches; it would render the gale-apes’ rapid, percussive bone-talk meaningless. Worse, it would look at the Tuners—at three thousand years of compact, stubborn brilliance, at the tactile poetry of a brother tapping a joke onto his sister’s collarbone in the dark, at the twelve-generation symphony carved into the windward edges of quartzite—and declare all of their hard-won mastery obsolete.

A wise and benevolent power knows that love does not erase the mountain to spare the climber’s knees. It honors the climb.

So the Presence does not still the gale. Instead, it listens.

It settles its attention into the roaring, ninety-five-percent-sky of the basin, feeling the thermal updrafts boil out of the poisonous depths and crash against the high shear-winds. It watches the young woman out on the highway cable, a hundred and fifty yards over nothing, braced against the whipping bark with her prehensile feet while she saws frantically at the tangled, dead mass of drifted bromeliads. 

She is fast, and she is strong, and she is running out of time.

Two miles upwind, unseen behind a dazzle of afternoon sun, a freakish convergence is building: a massive, superheated bubble of lowland air tearing loose from the deep swamps at the exact moment a cold front spills over the northern rim. In forty seconds, they will collide directly over this sector. It will not be a standard double-gust, nor a flash-mist; it will be a rare, wandering sheer-hammer—the kind of invisible atmospheric avalanche that snaps ancient quartzite needles off at the base and wipes whole routes off the Tuners' mental maps once a century. 

On the pedestal, the old man with his jaw pressed to the bronze chisel feels the sudden, sickening drop in pitch as the distant stone arch stops humming its minor third and begins to *scream*. His eyes fly open. He scrambles forward, grabbing the safety braid, slapping a frantic, desperate rhythm onto the third teammate's back—*cut-loose, anchor-down, dead-fall*—knowing already that the girl is too far out on the span to haul back, and that when the main cable blows, its whipping recoil will smash their pedestal clean of life unless they sever her line.

The third Tuner reaches for his heavy axe, his broad, weathered face twisting in silent agony. Out on the vine, the young woman feels the sudden, eerie two-second vacuum that precedes the hammer. She stops sawing. She wraps both thick arms and hooked legs around the hogshead-thick vine, buries her leather-masked face against the rough bark, and shuts her eyes behind her smoky quartz goggles. She does not pray for the wind to stop. She is a Tuner; she knows what the wind is. She simply locks her scarred fingers together and prepares to hold on until her bones break, or until the sky takes her.

Here, the Power moves. Not against the nature of the basin, but entirely *within* it—a thumb pressed lightly, invisibly, against the scales of chaos.

Half a mile upwind of the girl, standing isolated in the empty blue gulf, is a dead, barren needle of pale quartzite, worn paper-thin at its center by ten thousand years of grit. Nature had scheduled it to snap and fall into the abyss sometime in the next forty years. 

The Power reaches out and wakes a microscopic fault-line inside that barren spire *now*, three seconds before the sheer-hammer arrives.

When the invisible wall of air hits the sector, it strikes the weakened needle first. With a sound like the cracking of the world’s spine, the upper four hundred feet of stone shears off. Before it can plummet, the horrific updraft catches its broad, flat face, hanging ten thousand tons of pale rock suspended in the roaring air for one impossible second—just long enough to act as a colossal, sacrificial breakwater. 

The sheer-hammer shatters against the falling spire, splitting cleanly in two. 

Instead of an inescapable wall of downward force, two howling, chaotic rivers of air tear *around* the highway span. The shockwave that hits the young woman is still monstrous—it rips the drifted bromeliad mat clean off the vine for her, shredding it into green confetti, and flings the highway cable upward in a violent, seventy-foot arc that nearly tears her shoulders from their sockets—but the anchor-roots hold. The vine snaps taut, shuddering like a plucked bowstring, and drops back into its groove as the broken stone spire tumbles harmlessly down into the distant, sunlit haze.

On the pedestal, the axe never falls. When the roaring wall of torn cloud clears four minutes later, leaving everything steaming and slick in the bright sun, the young woman is still there, clinging to the gleaming wet bark, slowly, methodically unlocking her cramped fingers to tap three sharp, heavy beats directly into the woody fiber of the cable.

Back on the rock, through the bronze chisel pressed to his jawbone, the old man feels the faint, miraculous tremor arrive: *Clear. Holding. Hauling in.*

That would be enough for a passing kindness. Just a saved life, disguised as an astonishing stroke of luck, leaving their pride and their world intact.

Yet the Power lingers, watching the three of them crawl back into the woven-bone bunker an hour later, trembling with adrenaline breakdown, pressing their shoulders and knees tightly together in the dim, creaking basket as their calloused hands fly across one another's collarbones and shins—recounting the terror, mourning the fallen spire that has changed the local echoes, laughing silently with wheezing chests at their own survival.

The Power sees the beauty of their life, yes. *And* it sees the quiet, cumulative toll that has no romance in it: the way the old man winces when a barometric drop squeezes the ruined, scarred drums of his ears; the slow, creeping calcification in their permanently hooked knuckles that makes the old ones clumsy in the rim-villages long before their minds fade; the sheer poverty of meteoric iron, forcing them to guard every toggle-hook like a king's ransom because when iron wears thin and snaps, a whole family’s breadwinner vanishes into the mist.

Benevolence does not remove the test, but wisdom knows when to enrich the soil. It decides to leave three gifts woven so seamlessly into the violent ecology of the basin that no priest will ever build a temple to an outside god, and no Tuner will ever feel like a charity case. They will simply think they have finally looked closely enough at their own home.

First, down in the unnavigable, crushing heat of the unseen abyss below, the Power nudges the deep tectonic vents. 

In the weeks to come, the boiling updrafts will carry a slightly altered trace of mineral ash up from the floor of the world, dusting the undersides of the swaying epiphytic mats. The plants will drink it in. Within two seasons, a humble, gray-green bracket fungus that has always grown sparsely in the dark, bruised interiors of the staghorn-fern knots—right where the gale-apes hunt—will begin to fruit a little thicker, weeping tiny, amber beads of thick resin to trap spores in the flash-mists. 

Next spring, a Tuner harvesting crabs with a cut on her hand will get that sticky, bitter amber smeared across her skin, and notice by nightfall how quickly the acid-sap burns stop throbbing, and how strangely loose and supple her stiff, scarred finger-joints feel the next morning. Within five years, the rim-villages will be boiling that resin with glider-fat into a salve. Rubbed warm into the knuckles before sleep, it will dissolve the grinding chalk in an old climber's joints; dabbed sparingly around the outer ear of a twelve-year-old apprentice before their first rotation in the gulf, it will thicken and toughen the tympanic membrane with flexible, resilient collagen—not enough to make the roaring wind pleasant, never enough to replace the intimate, sacred grammar of hands on collarbones, but enough that when an old grandmother retires to the quiet clefts of the rim, she will still be able to hear the laughter of her grandchildren in the stillness, without pain.

Second, the Power looks at the vast, miles-wide acoustic organ of the basin itself. 

High up near the rim, in sectors too sheer and barren of vines for the Tuners to ever climb, stand clusters of hollow, fluted quartzite needles that have only ever produced meaningless, deafening white noise. Slowly, using nothing more than the natural, sand-blasting grit carried on the sixty-mile-an-hour crosswinds, the Power subtly guides the erosion inside seven of those high, unreachable flutes, widening a throat here by the width of a thumb, beveling a lip of pale stone there.

It tunes them to the barometric pressure of disasters.

The Tuners will not notice it at first. The basin is always roaring. Only slowly, over the next three rotations, will the sharpest skull-listeners—lying flat on the pedestals with their jaws pressed to bronze chisels—realize that the background chaos has acquired a faint, deep sub-harmonic. Under normal gales, the high rim-flutes sleep. When a dangerous storm builds, they hum a steady, warning fifth that travels cleanly through the bedrock spires. And when another century-storm, another sheer-hammer, begins to brew ten miles away over the badlands, those unreachable stone throats will catch the shifting pressure twenty minutes early and send a deep, unmistakable, pulsing heartbeat shuddering down through every spire and every taut primary cable in the basin—twenty minutes of grace to get off the spans, clip double-lines, and crawl into the woven-bone bunkers before the sky breaks.

And for the last gift, the Power looks far back in time, and just beneath the surface.

Along the solid cliffs of the western rim, half a mile from one of their quiet, motionless villages, a seasonal meltwater stream cuts through a narrow gorge. Tomorrow morning, a minor rockslide—triggered by the distant tremor of today's fallen spire—will peel away twenty feet of worthless, crumbly shale in that gorge. Beneath it, gleaming dark and dense in the morning sun, will lie the flattened, ancient heart of an iron-nickel meteorite that struck the bedrock eons before the basin was ever hollowed out. 

It is not soft gold to make them rich merchants, nor magical steel that requires no labor. It is stubborn, star-forged metal that will take weeks of roaring charcoal fires and exhausting hammer-work to shape. Yet there is enough of it there to forge unbreakable toggle-hooks, heavy chisels, and saw-blades for forty generations—metal tough enough to match the stubbornness of the hands that will wield it.

The Power withdraws its touch, satisfied.

Out in the blinding blue gulf, a pocket of shredded white cloud rockets sideways out of the depths, swallowing the pedestal and the bunker in screaming, gleaming mist. Across the primary vine-cables, a thousand gray blisters split open at once, blooming vivid crimson fans into the gale to drink the passing sky. Underneath the thrashing fern-mats, a pair of slick, olive-scaled gale-apes hang upside down over a mile of nothing, tapping a rapid, gossipy tremor against each other’s wrists as the whole world heaves forty feet up and drops weightless again.

Inside the swaying basket of woven bone, the three Tuners finish their dried slug-meat. The flash-mist tears apart; warm afternoon sun blasts through the gaps, turning the wet green rigging to whipping steam. The young woman pulls her boiled-leather mask back over her mahogany face, checks the smoky quartz over her eyes, taps a quick, affectionate squeeze onto the old man’s scarred forearm—*My lead*—and crawls back out into the deafening, glorious roar, clipping onto the line to finish clearing the way across the sky.
