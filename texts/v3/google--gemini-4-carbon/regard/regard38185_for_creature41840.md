A lesser power—one intoxicated by its own mercy—would ruin this place in a heartbeat. It would look at the shivering peril of the abyss, the exhausting roar, the slimy spruce poles, and the creatures flattened against the weeping dark, and it would offer *relief*. It would broaden the ledges into safe terraces, hush the screaming wind, warm the stone, or spark a restless, tool-making fire behind those wide, owl-gold eyes. And in doing so, it would turn a masterpiece of tension into a museum, and make beggars out of kings.

Wisdom knows better than to fix what is fiercely, jubilantly whole. 

When the Presence comes to the crown of the spire, it does not arrive as a light that outshines the fractured rainbows, nor as a hush that silences the stone. It arrives simply as attention—vast, quiet, and delighted, settling into the roaring lantern like sunlight into moss.

For a long season of turning clouds, the power does nothing at all except *witness*. 

It feels the exquisite geometry of the Rags’ tendons locking without effort into water-worn flutes. It shares the sharp, clean joy of the backward leap into the ninety percent—that wet *whump* of violet-veined hide catching the vertical river, the lazy corkscrew through upward-falling rain, the mouth full of fat midges and sun-struck mist. It listens through the limestone to the *tock-tock-trrrrrk* of clan speaking to clan in the dark, overlapping like shingles, warm in lanolin and amber-strap hammocks. It watches the climbers haul themselves over the lip with blue-knuckled grit, taking no more than the chimney spares, leaving the boundary-note blowing in the cleft.

There is no tragedy here to mend. Pain exists—ice chokes the lower vents in deep winter; an infant sometimes loses its grip before reaching the pouch; a rotten spruce pole will one day snap under a climber’s boot; millennia from now, the limestone ribs themselves will thin, fracture, and topple into the cobalt void. 

The wise power looks into deep time, tracing the grain of the mountain from its dark roots to its whistling tips, and decides upon three very small things. None of them will ever look like a miracle.

First, it reaches two miles down into the lightless gut of the peak, where the valley winds enter through jagged, shifting throats of shale and frost-wedged scree. In harsh decades, rockslides down there choke the draft, starving the lantern above of warmth and bugs and lift until the mosses blacken and the clans eat their last pouched pikas in freezing silence. And in centuries to come, a single tectonic shudder would have collapsed the main flue entirely, turning the roaring cage above into dead, dry bone in an afternoon.

Deep in that unseen dark, the power nudges the chemistry of the seeping groundwater. Over months and years, mineral-rich trickles begin to lay down thick, pearlescent collars of travertine and flowstone around the subterranean arches, cementing loose fault-blocks, smoothing the jagged chokes into clean, sweeping venturis, and opening a second, deeper intake vein toward a geothermal seep three valleys away. The updraft will still surge andebb with the honest weather—lean days are part of the Rags' sharp grace—but the throat of the mountain will not collapse, and even in the cruelest whiteout winter of a century, a faint, steady thread of warm vapor will still rise from the deep earth to keep the sleeping alcoves just above killing frost.

Second, on a sleet-blind morning in autumn, it touches the three peeled spruce poles lashed across the eastern gap. The center pole has rotted secretly from the core outward; the next young climber to step mid-span with a heavy basket of swifts would have gone straight down the shaft—and in the grief and superstition that followed, his village below would either abandon the spire forever, or return with fire and iron to purge its "demons."

The power does not turn the rotten wood sound. Instead, at midnight when no human is within vertical miles, a sudden, violent shear of crosswind catches a loose, heavy river-cobble from a frayed net above and drops it cleanly onto the center pole. It snaps with a gunshot crack and tumbles down into the dark. When the climbers crest the rim four days later, the hazard is plain to see in the bright sun; cursing the wind, they haul up fresh, resin-heavy larch poles on their next ascent and lash a stronger bridge. The truce holds.

The third thing is not for survival at all. It is pure gift—one mind tipping its hat to another across the gulf of being.

It is given to the old Rag who climbs clumsily into the still air beside the water-basins to press its chest-drum against the rock near the vulture-bone whistle. 

The power sees *why* the elder comes here. Every other vibration in the spire is slave to chaos: the shriek of birds, the hammer of spray, the shifting organ-chord of the gusts. That wedged hollow bone is the only thing in the Rag’s universe that holds a single, stubborn shape against the storm. Listening to it is how an ancient creature of pure motion touches stillness. It is the edge of wonder.

Yet bone dries, splits in the frost, and turns to splinters. And a single note is lonely.

High on the leeward curve of the eastern rib, six inches from the cleft where the human whistle sits, there is a natural nodule of dense, flinty chert embedded in the softer limestone—a stone heart that will take ten thousand years longer to erode than the rock around it. Inside this nodule is a blind, curving cavity left by an ancient fossil shell, nearly touching the surface.

Over the course of a single afternoon, while clouds turn the cage into a roaring gray drum, the power accelerates a thousand years of wind-scour in the space of three breaths. Four stray grains of quartz grit, flung hard by an eddy, strike a paper-thin septum of stone and pierce the chamber at a precise, grazing angle. 

When the cloud blows clear and sunlight slams back against the emerald moss, the spire has a new voice.

It is too quiet to hear with ears over the roar of the chimney. The climbers, with their thick leather boots and wind-deafened heads, will never notice it when they come to check their obsidian blades. It does not travel through the air at all; because the chert nodule is fused directly into the spine of the rib, it pours its vibration straight into the living bone of the mountain.

Later that dusk, after the raptors have gone to roost in lower crags and the swifts have thinned to darting shadows against the indigo sky, the old Rag hauls itself up over the mossy rim. Its long, pale wrists move stiffly in the windless pocket. It dips its lipless mouth into the pitch-lined basket, drinks the quiet water, and clicks its milky eyelids clear. Then it settles down beside the cache and presses its scarred gray chest-drum flat against the stone.

The human whistle gives its thin, familiar *eeeeeee*—one solitary line drawn straight through the dark.

And then, rising right up out of the limestone into the Rag’s cartilage, comes the answer: a warm, unfathomably pure *fifth* below it, steady as starlight, rich with faint, bell-like overtones that shift not with the panic of the storm, but only with the slow, grand breathing of barometric pressure between day and night, summer and winter. Two notes now, locked in an effortless, resonant chord—half human accident, half mountain bone—turning the wild roar of the shaft from mere noise into music.

The old Rag freezes. Its wide, golden owl-eyes dilate until the dark pupils swallow the amber almost whole. Its translucent membranes forget to click shut, letting mist bead like diamonds on its corneas. 

For a long time it stays motionless under the first cold stars. Then, very slowly, it presses its hard chin-plate down against the chert vein and gives a single, soft click of its throat—*tock*—dropping a third, percussive heartbeat right into the center of the chord, feeling the shockwave travel down the curve of the rib, past the upward-streaming vines, all the way down to the dark alcoves where its grandchildren hang sleeping in amber straps, dreaming of falling upward.

The Presence lingers just long enough to feel that small *tock* echo back through the stone. Then it withdraws into the wide cobalt sky, leaving the water to fall up, the vines to stream toward heaven, and the cage completely open at the top.
