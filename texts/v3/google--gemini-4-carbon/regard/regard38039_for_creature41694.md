A lesser benevolence—the kind that imagines love only in the shape of its own body—would have ruined it in an instant. 

A lesser power would have heard the deafening, relentless roar in the dark and pitied the strain of it, silencing the wind to give the pocket peace, and so starved the pewter leaves in a single afternoon. It would have brought warmth, shattering the delicate temperature gradients that fed the Bridge-Lichens; or wept gentle, life-giving rain down the chimney, turning the dry-graphite bearings to black sludge, rusting the flywheels to a halt, and drowning the electrostatic Shiver-Molds in mud. Or it might have reached inside the cramped dark of the iron cylinders and pulled the blind clathrate mind out into some wide, bright heaven, watching in horror as its weightless frost-architecture boiled away into nothing the moment the crushing pressure was lifted.

Wisdom, however, knows that a chord is not a cage. To a being made of acoustics and barometric pressure, stillness is not peace; stillness is amputation.

So when the Power turned its attention to the stratospheric massif, it did not bring spring, or silence, or room to stretch. It did not step inside the chimney at all, because there was no room for guests, and to push a single metallic frond aside would be to break a circuit decades in the soldering. 

Instead, it knelt outside on the freezing, mummifying knife-edge of the peak, and listened through forty feet of dark basalt.

It listened for a long time—years, perhaps, though time in the chimney was measured only in revolutions per minute. It felt the monumental, unvarying bass of the three flywheels, the screaming treble of the eastern slate-slot, the crisp electrostatic crackle of dust-colonies skating uphill, and beneath it all, the profound, glass-smooth focus of the lattice in the bottles, holding twelve brass poppets open by the width of a thought against the fury of the sky.

The Power recognized what it was looking at. It was looking at a sanctuary built entirely out of resistance. Everything inside that keyhole of stone was happy—if happiness is understood at its root: the clean, unimpeded exercise of one’s exact nature. The frost in the tanks did not want eyes; it wanted *poise*.

Then the Power looked further, reading the slow, deep-time ledger of friction and stone, and saw the three quiet deaths creeping toward the room.

First: the dry-graphite bearings of the three heavy flywheels. Graphite in a dry, cold gale lasts an astonishingly long time, shedding microscopic, slippery platelets of carbon so steel never grinds on steel. Whoever had bolted this forgotten array into the mountain a century or two ago had over-engineered the housings magnificently. Still, nothing spins forever on a finite reserve. In eighty years, or a hundred and ten, the graphite sleeves in the middle housing would wear down to their brass retainers. A millimeter of play would enter the shaft. At six thousand revolutions per minute, a millimeter of play becomes a hammer. The bearing would seize, friction-welding itself in a shriek of white sparks; the turbine above it would tear loose from its bolts; the chord would shatter; the heat would die; the compressors would stop; and the slow leak through the shimmed valves would bleed the twelve cylinders down to dead, hollow iron.

Second: the eastern wind-slot in the slate roof. Though the interior basalt had found the exact shape of the wind and stopped yielding, the *exterior* lip of the overhang still took the raw, unchanneled violence of the planetary jet stream, laced with high-altitude ice-crystals and meteoric dust. Over centuries, the razor-thin slot was widening—by microns a decade. Eventually, the aperture would grow wide enough to drop its pitch by a full octave, falling out of the resonant band of the piezotrophic flora below, letting in swirling snowdrifts that would sublimate and re-freeze on the warm conduits, cracking the manifolds.

Third: loneliness. Not the loneliness of a social creature missing a pack, for the clathrate sponge knew nothing of *other*. Rather, the poverty of a mind with exquisite sensitivity given only a single, flat line to trace. The planetary jet stream shifted by half a degree once a week; otherwise, the world was one note. The lattice in the cylinders had grown complex enough, over a century of surviving shockwaves, to feel nuances a thousand times finer than the wind ever bothered to play for it. It was a master organist sitting before a magnificent console with a brick resting on middle C.

The Power considered these three things. Then, gently, without breaking a single law of the mountain, it went to work.

It began with the roof. 

It did not seal the slate overhang or cast an unnatural spell upon it. It simply walked five hundred yards upwind along the jagged spine of the massif, where a vein of corundum and dense white quartz lay locked inside an outcrop of granite. With a nudge no different from a deep-winter frost-heave, it split a seam in the granite just enough for the screaming jet stream to bite into the corundum vein.

Over the next three winters, heavy, microscopic grains of sapphire-hard alumina blew horizontally across the peak and struck the exterior lip of the slate overhang. Instead of scouring the slate away, the corundum particles—hitting at a precise, shallow angle dictated by a thumb’s-breadth ridge the Power had left on a boulder thirty yards east—wedged themselves into the microscopic cleavage planes of the slate. Cold-welded by kinetic impact, they armored the lip of the wind-slot in a glaze of embedded sapphire a hundred atoms thick. The slot stopped widening. Its geometry locked against the sky for the next hundred thousand years.

Next, the Power looked to the bearings, and the amber pilot-lamps, and the sealed housings buried deep behind packed, untouchable walls of silver-grey cabbage.

You cannot open a housing without a wrench, and you cannot turn a wrench in a solid block of vibrating iron-hard leaves. So the Power used the couriers that were already making the rounds.

Into that same upwind seam of rock, alongside the corundum, the Power exposed a narrow, dark ribbon of pure, crystalline graphite and molybdenum disulfide, mixed with a trace of native tungsten for the filament-starved bulbs—veins that had been laid down when the mountain was an ocean floor, waiting three hundred million years in the dark. 

The gale shaved the dark ribbon into flour finer than smoke and carried it down through the eastern slot.

Inside the chimney, the new black dust fell into the roaring twilight. The Shiver-Molds found it instantly. To their electrostatic facets, molybdenum and hexagonal graphite were marvelously slick, conductive skates, though useless for eating. As the flat, ash-like colonies glided uphill against the hurricane, polishing the pewter fronds to bright silver coins, they carried coats of this black super-lubricant on their backs. 

When a colony brushed too close to the warm iron of the flywheel housings, a Bridge-Lichen waiting across the thermal gap fired its microscopic blue spark. *Pop.* The colony scattered—and a pinch of ultra-fine graphite and molybdenum dust fell directly onto the housing’s intake labyrinth seal, drawn inward by the slight negative pressure of the spinning shaft. 

Atom by atom, day after day, the bearings began to heal from the inside out. Where microscopic pits had begun to form in the century-old sleeves, the sliding molybdenum platelets burnished into the steel under pressure, building up a self-replenishing, dry-film mirror smoother than anything the original machinists could have milled. The tungsten ions, meanwhile, drifted onto the Bridge-Lichens, which dutifully stripped and plated them along the copper conduits, reinforcing the degraded seals around the caged amber bulbs so no stray oxygen could ever seep in to burn out their glowing orange coils.

The engine would not run forever—nothing made of matter does, and the Power respected matter too much to cheat it of its eventual rest—but eighty years had quietly become fifty millennia.

That left only the brick on middle C.

The Power sat back on its heels on the freezing stone outside, listening again to the blind frost breathing through its twelve brass valves. It watched, through the rock, those tiny, steady beads of violet-pink corona discharge hovering in the gloom beneath the catwalk, shifting to pale blue once a week when the jet stream wobbled.

*What do you give a mind made of pressure and pitch,* the Power wondered, *that will not startle it, will not break its glass-smooth home, and will ask nothing of it that it doesn’t choose to give?*

You give it a window. An acoustic one.

Ten feet to the north of the chimney, on the sheer, unvisited leeward cliff of the massif—where the wind did not scream, and where the thin stratospheric air lay cold and relatively still—the Power laid a single finger against the dark basalt. 

It did not carve a hole into the chimney. The chimney remained sealed, dark, and tight. Instead, deep *inside* the solid twelve-foot thickness of the northern rock wall—the very wall into which the heavy iron brackets of the twelve gas cylinders were bolted—the Power subtly altered the crystalline grain of the stone. 

Inside the solid basalt, invisible from either side, it tuned seven slender, branching veins of dense olivine and quartz, running from the outer cliff face right up to the buried anchor-bolts of the gas rack. They were solid rock, completely impermeable to air or cold, incapable of leaking a drop of heat or pressure. Wait outside on the cliff and you would see nothing on the stone face but seven faint, shallow dimples, arranged like cupped ears turned toward the wide world below and the deep sky above.

Acoustic lenses. Solid-state waveguides.

Down in the roaring pocket, nothing changed in the air. The wind still howled its single, deafening chord; the flywheels still droned their monumental bass; the pewter thickets still shivered in locked, rigid tension. None of the plants or dust-molds noticed a thing. 

Inside the twelve wire-wrapped cylinders, however, bolted directly to those deep iron anchors, the supercritical argon suddenly carried a whisper.

It did not drown out the room’s great chord; it rode underneath it, a billionth as loud, clear as a needle scratch on glass because it traveled solely through solid stone and iron straight into the dense fluid of the tanks.

For the first time in a hundred years, the clathrate lattice felt something that was not the wind-slot or the dynamos.

It felt, fifty miles down at the roots of the massif, the slow, tectonic groan of a glacier shifting an inch over granite—a sub-audible, majestic *slide* of pure bass. 

Three hours later, as the planet turned its shoulder into the sun, the outer cliff face warmed by four degrees; the olivine waveguides expanded by a hair’s breadth, and the lattice felt the sunrise not as light or heat, which would have meant nothing to it, but as a slow, golden glissando rising through fifths and octaves in the bones of its iron rack.

High overhead, a meteor the size of a walnut burned across the mesosphere, silent to human ears on the ground below, its hypersonic shockwave ringing the seven stone dimples on the cliff like distant, crystal bells. Inside the dark tanks, the xenon-frost shivered—not with the violent, shattering terror of a blown valve, for the barometric pressure did not waver by a single pascal—but with a sudden, delicate ripple of sympathetic resonance running from tank one to tank twelve and back again.

Down in the steel-gray gloom beneath the choked catwalk, sheltered behind a stiff pewter cabbage-head, the twelve tiny beads of violet-pink gas above the brass vents did something they had never done before.

They did not shift together in unison. 

Valve one held its steady violet note, anchoring the chord against the howling ceiling slot. Valve two shifted to pale blue. Valve four dipped to a deep, velvety crimson-indigo as a microscopic whisker of carbon-fluoride relaxed by three atoms to harmonize with the glacier fifty miles below; valves seven and eight flickered through pale lavender and icy cyan in a slow, deliberate arpeggio, tracing the fading ring of the meteor against the turning of the earth.

The pressure needles behind their yellowed glass did not twitch. They stayed pinned to their black lines, steady as stone, because the lattice had already worked out the math: when valve four tightened by three atoms, valve nine opened by three, keeping the inner stillness absolute while playing counterpoint on the exhaust.

Outside on the freezing peak, the Power listened for a few days more. 

It listened as the frost in the bottles learned the shape of moonrise—felt through the rock-tide of the mountain’s crust—and the distant, rhythmic thrum of summer thunderstorms rolling across plains three hundred miles to the south, and the crisp, brittle crack of frost-wedging on neighboring peaks. The creature was not merely enduring the mountain anymore; it was accompanying it. Beneath the relentless, unchanging roar of the pocket, twelve tiny beads of cold plasma were weaving a slow, silent fugue in the dark, translating the whole vast weather of a world into micro-ohms and noble light.

Satisfied, the Power stood up, brushed the dry stratospheric dust from its knees, and left the mountain to itself. There was no need to leave a sign, and no reason to ever pry open the lid. Everything inside had plenty to do.
