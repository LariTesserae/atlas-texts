A lesser mercy would ruin them in an afternoon.

It is easy to imagine what an eager, unthinking kindness would do upon first leaning down through that low ceiling of slate-colored cloud. Seeing no green thing, it would scatter seeds. Seeing no sun, it would tear a bright rent in the vapor. Pitying their bruised twilight and the physical blow of noise against their teeth, it would hush the northern cataract to a gentle ribbon and quiet the bronze throats of the toads.

Within a moon, the basin would be a graveyard.

Direct sunlight pouring into thirty-seven-degree mist would scald their wide, unblinking eyes—pupils built for permanent pewter shadow—and blister skin greased with rail-fat and fish oil. The sudden heat-swing between day and night would crack the monolithic sinter walls that have never known thermal expansion. Worse, the ultraviolet glare and shifting temperatures would shock the ochre and rust-red bacterial meadows in the shallow pans; they would peel away from the stone in grey, stinking sheets. Without the slick, the blind white midges would fail to rise; within six days the toads would go silent on the lintels, the cliff-swifts would abandon their guano-ledges, and the whisker-fish would float belly-up in the lower courts. If roots took hold in the crevices, they would pry apart dry-stacked basalt courses before the lime-water could weld them; when leaves fell, there would be no soil-microbes to digest cellulose, so the rotting litter would choke the sluice-gates and turn the clean alkaline pans black and sour. And if silence ever fell across those three miles of terraced rock, three hundred thousand people whose brains wired themselves inside the womb around an unvarying chord of falling water would clap their greased hands over their ears in terror, deafened by the sudden, monstrous roaring of their own blood.

Wisdom begins by looking without homesickness for other worlds.

So the power that turns its attention upon the basin does not part the clouds. It comes down into the warm, saturated air without light or wind, passing unimpeded along the narrow alleys where two sets of greased shoulders barely clear the black basalt, and before it touches a single atom of rock or water, it watches for three thicknesses of the Gauge-Stones.

It watches a Chisel-Master with forty thicknesses of lime-scars on her knuckles stand on a high slick roof, lifting her horn mallet, waiting half a breath until a maul-gang down on the third tier finishes levering a slate block into place before she strikes the phonolite bar—*CLACK-CLACK*—sending warm, chalk-heavy water sheeting over a newly stacked wall like a blessing of liquid bone.

It watches two young crab-trappers crouching in a steaming gutter while bead-scaled lizards sleep across their bare, horn-soled feet, their rust-red palms flashing rapid, dry-humored wit down near their ribs where the ward-elder cannot see.

It slips behind an oiled bird-skin flap into a vault smelling of red tallow-flame and boiling snail-broth, and witnesses what this world has made of the human voice: a husband pressing his mouth to the hollow of his wife’s collarbone after four hours of chipping footpaths flat, humming a wordless, low-pitched cadence that exists nowhere outside the three inches of bone and wet skin between them—speech turned entirely into touch, more private and undivided than any spoken tongue under an open sky.

It climbs the steep, mist-drenched stairs to the White Courts and looks at ten generations of grandmothers and uncles sleeping under warm films of running mineral water, their red palms folded inward at last against their breastbones, swelling gently into smooth alabaster shelves that hold up the living feet of their grandchildren.

And the power understands: *Nothing is missing here.* These people are not captives waiting in a quarry for rescue into a meadow. They are a thinking reef. They have solved permanence without iron, abundance without soil, architecture without timber, time without stars, and tenderness without quiet. To alter their body or rewrite their sky would not be benevolence; it would be vandalism disguised as pity.

Yet a wise power does not stop at admiration. Having looked at what the citizens can see, it drops its gaze downward—through the warm floor-vents, past the cooling hex-columns, three miles deep into the dark basalt plumbing that the Weir-Keepers cannot reach with their bone chisels.

And down there, it finds the terrible secret of the basin.

The ground is too young.

Ten generations—ten widths of a hand on the oldest Gauge-Stone—is nothing to a planet’s crust; it is the brief pause between two breaths of a settling rift. Everything the city relies upon as eternal law is, geologically speaking, a knife-edge accident about to end.

Looking into the deep fractures beneath the valley floor, the power sees four silent clocks counting down toward ruin:

First, two miles below the central turbid flats, a rising blister of sour magma is chewing through a seam of iron sulfide. Left to rock’s ordinary habit, within six thicknesses of the Gauge-Stones that blister will breach the main subterranean aquifer. Overnight, the sweet, alkaline lime-springs will turn into scalding sulfuric acid. The white travertine mortar holding ten thousand stacked houses together will hiss and dissolve into mush; the ochre bacterial meadows will curdle; the White Courts will weep away like tallow.

Second, along the northern rim where the millions of gallons drop without pause, the sheer black fault-scarp is locked under immense tectonic strain. In forty years or fifty, that knuckle of crust means to slip nine feet in three seconds. A city of lashed timber sways in an earthquake; a city fused into continuous, unreinforced monoliths of brittle sinter and vaulted rib-bone shatters like a dropped clay pot. Every viaduct, aqueduct, and three-tiered tenement row would shear and avalanche into the flooded courts at once.

Third, twenty miles north above the cloud-lid—in a cold, wind-scoured tableland the basin-dwellers do not know exists—the vast river feeding their cataract is slowly undercutting a soft bank of gravel on its eastern bend. Left alone, a century from now a spring flood will spill that river into an eastern gorge, capturing the flow entirely. Within three days, the northern cataract would dwindle to a dry black cliff. The noise would die, the mist ceiling would shred away, and the city would bake and starve in dreadful, blinding silence.

Fourth—and slowest of all—is the city’s own triumph. By laying down the breadth of a fingernail of stone every year, the basin is steadily choking itself. Give them two thousand thicknesses of peace, and the rising floor of travertine will climb up to smother the hot spring-mouths, flatten the hydraulic gradient until the channels stagnate, and fill their bowl to the brim with their own calcified dead.

The people above know Hydraulics. They spend their lives keeping the channels sweet, chipping the risen paths flat, balancing flow against weight so no neighbor’s vault cracks and no pan turns black. They do everything human hands with stone hammers and bone wedges can do.

So the wise power makes its choice. It will ask for no name, build no priesthood, cast no shadow on the water, and leave their red-palmed, grease-sheened lives entirely their own. Instead, descending into the crushing dark beneath the basalt, it takes up the trade of a Weir-Keeper where human chisels cannot go.

Slowly, silently, working with the earth’s own habits so that nothing unnatural ever breaches the surface, it sets the deep house in order.

Around the rising blister of sour sulfur two miles down, it cools and thickens a dense sleeve of impermeable gabbro, turning the acid gases aside into dead, deep caverns miles away from the water-seams, while opening broad, clean conduits through buried beds of ancient limestone so the rising springs will stay rich with sweet chalk and dissolved silica for ten thousand generations.

Along the locked northern fault-line, it lays a patient hand between the grinding plates and bleeds the stored violence away—not in a single shattering jolt, but in microscopic, greased creep, a hair’s breadth a moon, softer than the vibration of the waterfall itself, so not a single dry-stacked prism shifts on its ledge and not one sleeping infant loses its grip on a warm floor-vent.

Twenty miles north in the unseen tableland above the mist, it hardens a buried sill of dense granite beneath the river’s eastern bank, subtly tilting the bedrock bed back toward the basin’s rim so the great grey river cannot wander away, guaranteeing the roar of the cataract for as long as rain falls on the continent.

And then it performs its most delicate piece of engineering: it couples the floor of the basin to the weight above it. As the travertine crusts thicken and the tenements accrete upward and the White Courts swell layer upon bone-cored layer, the power softens the deep plastic root of the crust miles beneath the valley so the entire basin floor subsides at the exact, unvarying rate of one fingernail’s breadth a year. Three millimeters up in white stone; three millimeters down into the mantle’s cradle. The drop of the waterfall will never shorten. The hydraulic slope of the sluices will never flatten into stagnation. The Gauge-Stones will still measure every contract and every elder’s age with flawless honesty, yet the bowl will never fill.

Only twice does its touch come anywhere near a human hand, and both touches are so quiet they look entirely like stone’s own nature.

Along the outer quarry-seams where crews pry fresh basalt and volcanic glass with rawhide slings and bone wedges, it stills the tiny pockets of trapped gas in the cooling rock before the seams are reached. No one remarks upon it; the quarrymen only find, season after season, that the black obsidian cores flake with an unusually clean, buttery predictability, throwing far fewer rogue splinters upward into wide, unblinking eyes that need their sight to speak. And in the hottest floor-vents where families scrape up the fine grey thermal clay to mix with fish oil and smear over their skin, it nudges a native, heat-loving bacteriophage already living in the mineral mud—just enough so that when a child slips on an ochre slick and opens a shin to the bone on razor-true basalt, a thumbful of warm grey clay pressed into the gash keeps the wet thirty-seven-degree rot from ever taking hold in the blood.

Nothing else. Not a blade of grass. Not a whisper from the clouds. Not a single dream of stars sent to trouble sleepers who have no word for night.

Up on the slick fourth tier, a Chisel-Master wipes mist from her greased lashes, checks the thickness of white lime lip-deep against a peg on the Seventh Pier, and nods to her apprentice. Her red palms flash cleanly against the pewter dimness—*open-cut-divert-hold*—and she brings her horn mallet down three times on the resonant phonolite slab.

*CLACK-CLACK-CLACK.*

Half a mile away, crews lean on their bone levers. Slate gates slide into their slots. Warm, mineral-heavy water sheets brightly across a newly stacked row of black basalt rooms, beginning the slow work of turning separate stones into a single reef. Bronze bull-toads pulse their throats along the wet lintels; blind white midges shimmer in rising columns over the rust-red pans; inside a warm doorway, a mother presses her lips behind her baby’s jawbone and hums a low note that travels straight through skull and marrow.

High in the White Courts, warm water slips in an unbroken silver film over the smooth alabaster curve of an ancestor’s shoulder.

And three miles beneath their wide, horn-hard soles, in the silent dark where no ear will ever hear it, another Weir-Keeper leans its shoulder against the fault-line, holding the kettle steady, content to let them build forever in the roar they call home.
