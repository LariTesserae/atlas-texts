To a power that has watched stars ignite and crusts cool across ten thousand worlds, most mortal works look like bargaining. People build walls to say *not here*; they light fires to say *not cold*; they pave stone to say *not moving*. Even benevolence, when it looks down upon struggling things, usually feels the urge to tuck a blanket around them—to blunt the wind, raise the sun a few degrees, smooth the jagged greywacke so no child splits a shin in the dark.

That is how lesser powers ruin masterpieces.

The attention that settles over this roaring trench does not come down like a shaft of golden light parting the cloud-wrack. Parting the clouds would let radiative heat escape into the upper atmosphere, drop the ridge-top temperatures three degrees, lock the spillway moisture into permanent rime, and starve the braided black streams by Tuesday. Wisdom, first of all, knows how to read a balance sheet before touching the ledger.

For a long season—longer than a winter, shorter than a fault-cycle—the presence simply listens.

It listens to the forty-knot spillway roar, not as noise, but as a respiratory rate. It traces the thermal bleed nine kilometers down at the olivine contact, noticing the exquisite, light-fingered geometry of the spatial shears the Gale-backed have hung in the rock—how spare they are, how utterly free of vanity. There are no monuments down there, just as there are no fences above. It watches the people in their otter-skin coats, smelling of sour fern-pith and lamprey fat, running hot through the brush, clacking greywacke on greywacke to speak through the soles of one another’s boots.

It recognizes peers—not in scale, perhaps, since these mortals still bleed if a fresh basalt flake catches an artery, and still die if a logjam breaks ten seconds too soon while they are wading a braid—but peers in *taste*. Rare, fiercely rare in the universe, is the mind that possesses godlike leverage and uses it solely to keep the world wild enough to bite back.

To soften their valley would be an insult. To teach them anything about their own rock would be presumptuous. To leave a shining gift inside one of the twelve-meter calm cylinders would turn a workbench into an altar, and altars are dead air of the worst kind.

Yet love, even wise and restrained love, rarely passes through a place without leaving a signature—if only the kind of signature a fellow craftsman recognises with the pad of a thumb.

And looking very deep, along timelines longer than seven generations or seventy, the power sees the one quiet vulnerability in their design. Not in the machinery—the barycentric anchors will outlast the mountain range—and not in the ecology, which is thick and redundant as braided hawser. The vulnerability is in the seam between flesh and tool, over deep time.

Right now, the Gale-backed remember why they are here, even if they don't count the winters. Their engineered vasculature runs hot, the neural lace in the periosteum behind their ears wakes cleanly with its dry metallic taste on the tongue, and when a woman sinks her tallow-greased wrists into the photon-drinking blister, her fingers still know the old-world math of phase-balances and phreatic pressure.

But flesh drifts. In a valley this loud, this urgent, where every calorie is earned in freezing black water and no books survive a week outside a dead-air boundary, lineages eventually fray. A plague among the waterfowl, three bad slumps in a single decade that bury the best midwives under ten tons of flood-basalt, a subtle transcription error creeping into that counter-current forearm vasculature or the periosteal growth-genes—and five hundred years from now, a child might step into the calm, taste copper at the back of their throat, plunge their hands into the black blister, and *not quite know which way to twist their wrist*. Or worse: the neural lace fails to grow cleanly in a generation of newborns, leaving the deep shears orphaned nine kilometers down while the people above remain magnificent beasts who can no longer steer the kettle they live on.

So the power acts. It makes three changes—none of them visible to the eye, none of them calming a single gust of wind.

***

First, it reaches into the deep crustal ledger—not the nine-kilometer olivine contact where the Gale-backed work, which is *their* studio, not to be rearranged—but forty kilometers deeper, in the asthenospheric mantle plume that feeds the graben’s youth.

Tectonic trenches are mortal things. Left strictly to planetary physics, a rifting basin either tears all the way open into a cold inland sea within a hundred millennia, or its mantle heat-source drifts sideways, the faults lock up, the scarps weather round, the root-zone cools, and the frantic cold jungle slowly slackens into ordinary boreal bog.

With a touch gentler than a shorebird settling its feathers, the power nudges a slow convection cell in the deep mantle into a self-correcting harmonic loop. It does not smooth the tremors above; the den ceilings will still shudder every twenty minutes and drop cold water on sleeping faces. What it ensures is that the engine beneath the studio will not run out of fuel prematurely, nor crack the valley floor wide open into the ocean. It buys their knife-edge another four million years of raw, settling youth, should they continue to want it.

***

Second, it looks to the biological library hidden inside the people themselves, and inside the mud they eat from.

Gene-lines kept in small, tough populations narrow over centuries; engineered traits, lacking laboratories to refresh them, suffer genetic drift or get selected out during freak bottlenecks. The power does not rewrite the Gale-backed into immortals—immortals grow bored, and bored stewards start flattening temperature curves. Instead, it tucks a quiet, distributed backup into the ecosystem they have married themselves to.

Down in the five-degree root-zone, threading through the yard-thick cushions of black peat and living moss, runs a ubiquitous mycorrhizal fungus that binds the roots of the wrist-thick giant ferns to the raw flood-basalt. And in the peat-lined fermentation pits where the Gale-backed sour their starchy fern-pith into winter paste, a tough, cold-loving yeast already thrives, passing into every belly in the basin from weaning onward.

With exquisite molecular thrift, the power weaves a dormant, symbiotic chaperone into the genome of that native pit-yeast and the deep basalt-moss. It changes nothing about the sour, warming bite of the fermented paste. It gives no miraculous cures for broken bones or drowned lungs. All it does, generation after generation, whenever a person eats from the pits, is quietly proofread the somatic and germline expression of those delicate, ancient adaptations—keeping the periosteal neural lace crisp and noiseless, keeping the counter-current veins true, preventing subtle heavy-metal toxicity from the raw bog-iron and young volcanic groundwater from dulling their inner ears over forty years of sleet. As long as they eat the mud’s bread, the bridge between their calloused hands and the barycentric anchors will never corrode from within.

And—just in case—into the acoustic resonance of the greywacke itself, the power does nothing magical at all, save one tiny enrichment along a single exposed vein high up the eastern spillway: a seam of extraordinarily dense, iron-ringing hornfels that splits naturally into palm-sized river cobbles. When struck together, those cobbles carry a sub-bass click through wet peat nearly twice as far as ordinary stone, clear even under the boom of rolling river-boulders. They will wash down into the upper gravel braids next spring. A hunter will pick two up, test their weight, clack them once against a knee, blink in the dark at how crisply the return echo maps an undercut bank three hundred yards downstream, and lash them to their wrists with rawhide.

***

The last thing the power does is purely for delight—a greeting left between equals, placed where only someone living *exactingly* as they live would ever find it.

Not in a calm cylinder. Never there.

Out in the roaring middle of the basin, where three black river-braids smash together against a freshly dropped scarp of flood-basalt, there is a deep, violent eddy pool shadowed by horizontal conifers. It is a treacherous, thrilling spot—forty-knot spray whipping horizontally over the lip, boulders booming together underwater, big silver fish stacking up by the hundreds in the slack behind a submerged stone tooth before fighting the cataract.

Down at the bottom of that roaring black pool, six feet under thirty-four-degree water where no light ever reaches, wedged beneath the overhang where only a bare arm with blown-black night-heron eyes and hot counter-current veins would ever dare reach by touch in the dark—the power grows a single cluster of stone.

Not gold, not crystal, nothing bright. Just native geothermal mineral, precipitated out of a warm hairline vent in the basalt: three smooth, heavy nodules of black nephrite jade, slick as river-tallow, shaped by the tumbling gravel in the eddy until they feel, to probing fingertips underwater, uncannily like three miniature sea-chests locked together in a familiar, asymmetric geometry—yet warm. Four degrees warmer than the surrounding vent-water, holding a steady, thumb-sized pocket of deep mantle heat in their dense atomic lattice that does not fade when pulled into the sleet.

A child or a veteran hunter, plunging an arm shoulder-deep under the roaring bank after a fur-eel next week or next year, will brush those three warm curves behind the silver fish.

They will pause, water tearing at their grease-lashed sleeve, gale screaming inches above their hood. Their fingers—those same fingers that know the invisible maintenance bevels of a spatial anchor and the slick gill-plates of a six-pound trout—will trace the unmistakable three-body geometry grown straight out of raw river-jade.

They won't fall to their knees. The Gale-backed don't kneel except to butcher meat or check the moss-mats.

Perhaps the woman from the horsetail bog will pull one free, turn it over in her wet, calloused palm in the pewter dark, feel its quiet, permanent forty-eight-degree warmth sitting solid against her skin without deadening a single breath of wind, and let out a short, rusty huff of laughter that the storm tears away in half a foot. She will tuck it inside her otter-skin mittens—not as an amulet, just a remarkably good palm-stone for thawing rawhide knots when sleet turns to grease-ice on the ridge—knowing, without needing a single word of explanation, that somebody walked through the trench, looked at the mud and the wind and the razor-sharp stone, understood every bit of the joke and the labor, and didn't touch the thermostat.

Then the presence withdraws, leaving the clouds unbroken, the gale at forty knots, the mud at one degree above zero, and the roaring green dark completely to its masters.
