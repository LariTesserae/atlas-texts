First, nothing at all. 

For a long time—days as the surface counts them, dozens of shifts of the worm-gut down in the crack—the attention that has found this place simply rests there, weightless and utterly still, careful not to stir a single molecule of the syrupy brine. 

A lesser benevolence would have ruined everything in the first ten seconds. Pitying the blindness of the worms, it might have flooded the fissure with warm light, bleaching the violet kelp and blinding the delicate chemoreceptors of the architects with chaotic convection. Pitying the crushed masons in the eaves, it might have plucked the checkered flatworms from the walls—and within three generations, unchecked and unculled, the builders would have bred past their oxygen budget, suffocating in a milky soup of their own unspent paste. Pitying their confinement, it might have split the basalt wide open, flushing away the thick, stagnant chemical atmosphere in which every sentence of their formic-ester language is written. To blow fresh ocean through this pocket would be like tearing the roof off a library in a hurricane to give the books more air.

Wisdom knows better. Wisdom looks into the freezing, violet-gray gloom and recognizes at once that there are not ten million tiny, frantic creatures living in this crack. 

There is *one* being here.

It is young, blind, and entirely unaware of its own existence, yet it is unmistakable: a nascent, distributed mind thinking in aragonite and bitter esters. Each mason-pair is a firing synapse; each cantilevered bridge is a consolidated memory; the breakers in the black cellars are the necessary forgetting that keeps a mind from stiffening into madness; even the flatworms and the twig-anemones are the sharp, autonomic discipline that keeps its posture true. It is a creature whose skeleton is a city and whose bloodstream is indoor rain, dreaming geometry in the dark at two degrees Celsius.

To alter its nature would be to murder who it is becoming. 

And yet, wisdom is not indifference. Having listened long enough to understand the grammar of the drumming legs and the slow, generational thesis of the rising spires, the quiet power lifts its gaze from the avenues and looks through the solid black basalt—outward, and upward, into the wider world that the blind city cannot sense.

It finds two truths waiting in the dark.

The first is three miles north andforty meters tall beneath the waterline. Forty meters is shallow on a polar shelf. Every few centuries, a deep-keeled tabular berg calves off a distant glacier and rides the coastal current over this ridge, its blue-black foot ploughing trenches through the seabed sediment and shearing the tops off brittle basalt outcrops like a planer over rough pine. One is coming now. It moves at half a knot, silent until its keel grates against stone, carrying three million tons of momentum dead in line with the hairline crack. In sixty hours, it will not merely scrape the roof; its pressure wave alone will slam down the throat of the fissure, shattering every razor-edged terrace, snapping every braided chitin arch, and dredging the broken white dust of the metropolis out into the scouring current. There is no lesson in an iceberg. Nothing evolves against an eraser.

The second truth lies just four centimeters above the swollen, acid-wet collar of the stunted pioneer worm clinging to the ceiling.

The city’s desperate, brilliant gamble—hatching an alchemist from pheromone-crowding to dissolve the sky—is aimed true. Four centimeters of dense olivine basalt remain, and at the pace the clear sulfuric droplets are fizzing the rock into sand, the worm’s grandchildren will break through in roughly eleven months. Beyond that four-centimeter rind lies exactly what the civilization needs: not the open, lethal sea, but an ancient, sealed network of pillow-lava tubes stepping steadily downward into the continental slope. Dozens of chambers, some as large as cathedrals, plunging down to two hundred meters where no ice-keel will ever reach.

Except those chambers are dead. 

Long ago, cold clay and precipitated barite choked off the hydrothermal capillaries beneath them. The water sealed inside those deeper vaults is stripped of calcium, starved of iron, and utterly devoid of sulfur. When the pioneers finally pierce that ceiling eleven months from now, expecting a new sky, their chemical plumes will bleed into a sterile, pitch-black tomb.
