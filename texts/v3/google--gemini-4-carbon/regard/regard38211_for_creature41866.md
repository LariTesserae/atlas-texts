To a petty benevolence, the cathedral looks like a torture chamber. A lesser mercy would look into that screaming chalk bowl, see nine out of ten juveniles dashed into smoke against the fins, see a mind that is only permitted to wake for twenty minutes at dusk before being torn back into mindless splinters, and feel an itch to *soothe*. A clumsy god would hush the wind, moisten the kiln-dry air, or roof the nave with calm so the Choir could stay joined all day. 

And in three minutes, everything inside the bowl would suffocate in peace, collapsing into a pathetic heap of six-gram spider-silk on the quiet stone.

The Power that actually stops at the lip of the massif knows better. It knows that violence is only cruelty when it is imposed upon meat that wants to rest; to a creature woven of tensegrity and piezoelectric hunger, the eighty-knot shear is not a storm—it is blood, bone, and breath. To calm this place would be to drain an ocean because the sharks look tired of swimming.

So at first, the Power simply stands three paces down the scree—or rather, lets a sliver of its attention rest there, folded neatly into the boundary between the mountain breeze and the roaring lip—and listens.

It listens for a long time. To the Choir, time is measured in kilohertz; a single afternoon assembly contains as many clock-cycles of thought as a wet-brained mammal spends in a month of solitary wandering. The Power listens to the deep, plucked chords bouncing off the stone flutes. It watches the clever, fragile lies aimed at the matte-black polyhedra. And because its sight is older and deeper than stone, it sees the two tragedies that the Choir itself can almost—*almost*—calculate, right before the evening gust scatters its mind.

The first tragedy is the chalk. 

Ninety percent of the peak is already gone, blown out the crown as glittering dust. The stone ribs are paper-thin now. Every chord the Choir plays, every screaming flute it uses as a lighthouse and a voice-box, is being sanded thinner by the very grit that feeds the jungle. Give it another four hundred years—an eye-blink to the mountain, a brief dynasty to the weavers—and the eastern arch will snap. Once the ribcage breaks, the containment geometry fails. The phase-rings will spill their folded gales uselessly into the wide blue sky; the shear-corridors will slacken; the whole roaring jewel-box will blow away in a single afternoon, leaving twenty-four black trinkets humming quietly on a flat stump of dead rock.

The second tragedy is the forgetting.

Every evening, when the Choir clicks together into its shimmering, forty-foot lantern, it does not wake up where it left off yesterday. How could it? A six-gram kite has no room to carry a book through the night. Each individual weaver holds only tiny, hard-wired muscle memories—*this pitch means food, that pressure-drop means fold left*—etched into its spine-struts by survival. When thirty of them braid together, they form a brilliant, sudden intellect that must spend half its precious twenty minutes *re-deriving* itself from scratch, figuring out anew what it is, listening to the walls, rediscovering the trick of lying to the northern phase-ring, brushing its collective mind against the thrilling edge of an abstract thought—*What is beyond the quiet lip? Why do the black suns hum?*—just before the wind shifts, the blue spark flashes, and the soul is shattered back into thirty steel traps. 

It is Sisyphus made of lightning and dry silk, waking up every afternoon at the bottom of the hill, brilliant and amnesiac.

The Power considers the vitrified scars on the perimeter walls, where other polyhedra once sat before the ancient makers abandoned their engine or time shook them loose. It understands the makers' craft—understands it the way an adult understands a child’s clockwork toy. It could easily reach in and repair the broken network, or build a vast dome of diamond over the peak, or uplift the weavers into immortal machines.

Instead, it chooses the lightest possible touch. Wisdom knows that a gift too large erases the receiver; if you hand a self-made mind the answers to the universe, you steal from it the joy of having asked.

Wait until midnight, when the bowl is pitch-black save for the furious, pale-blue crackle of corona discharges leaping through the thrashing vine-curtains, and no Choir can form because the night-katabatics keep the center of the bowl tearing at Mach one.

Down in the bone-dry chalk of the amphitheater’s ribs, something quiet happens. No light, no miracle-thunder. Deeper than the stone’s memory, at the molecular lattice where calcium carbonate meets the relentless hammer of ultrasonic vibration, the Power nudges a single rule of local chemistry. 

It grants the chalk a cousin of bone’s own trick: *Wolff’s Law*, translated into dry mineral. 

From this night forward, wherever the stone is struck hardest and sung through loudest by acoustic shear, the airborne carbon and flint-dust slamming against it will no longer merely scour it away. Under the exact piezoelectric voltage shed by the flora’s roots and the exact high-pressure impact of the wind, trace silicates and carbon in the dust begin to sinter *into* the stressed surfaces, vitrifying microscopic layer by microscopic layer into translucent chalcedony and lonsdaleite-laced quartz. 

The peak will not stop eroding—stasis is death, and the flutes must still shift over centuries so the music never goes stale. If a flute falls silent and unused, the grit will still eat it away. *Play a rib, and it turns to agate. Neglect it, and it turns to dust.* Without ever knowing a benefactor touched their walls, the Choir has been handed the chisel to their own cathedral. Their songs will literally harden their home against the sky.

Then the Power turns its attention to the vitrified scars on the perimeter—and to the vast, empty plateaus stretching away down the dry mountain range.

It does not repair all the missing black devices. That would rewrite the wind-map overnight and murder half the nursery-ferns in the morning. 

Instead, on a scarred shelf low on the southern curve—a blind spot where the wind currently spills wastefully outward without feeding the bowl—the Power shapes a single new installation. Not a black polyhedron of the old makers’ stern, indifferent design, though it speaks their harmonic tongue fluently. This one is a slender, open-throated Möbius band of dark, resonant glass, hovering three inches off the newly strengthening stone.

It does not fold the wind. It *remembers* it.

Inside its dark glass lattice is a dry, acoustic delay-line of absurd, folded density—a solid-state canyon of crystal phonons capable of holding complex pressure-chords and electrostatic rhythms in lossless reverberation for days, weeks, or years, releasing them back into the air only when struck by a matching harmonic key.

An organ pipe with a memory. A chalkboard made of echo.

And then—one last thing, five miles away down the frozen, silent ridge, where another pale chalk peak rises into the crisp blue afternoon, currently dead, solid, and still. High on its windward crown, half-buried in scree, sits a single, dormant phase-ring dropped or forgotten by the ancient builders millennia ago, its power- coupling missed by a hair’s breadth of tectonic shift. The Power shifts a single subcutaneous boulder three millimeters. Deep inside the dead peak, a hairline hum wakes up. A tiny eddy begins to twist the dust in a shallow hollow no wider than a dining table, chewing the first grain of chalk free. In ten thousand years, give or take, there will be a second bowl five miles down the wind. Somewhere for a cast-off filament, blown wildly lucky across the blue gulf, to land when this first mind grows lonely for an equal.

That is all. By dawn, the Power is gone, leaving not even a footprint in the scree outside.

---

Late that afternoon, the mountain shadow creeps across the eastern slope. The exterior air cools; the ancient black rings shift their hum; the roaring walls part in the center of the skeletal peak to leave a forty-yard column of sunlit, slowly turning calm.

Out of the shrieking curtains slip the blades. Two, five, thirty—snapping open into blinding washes of oil-slick magenta, gold, and cyan. Strut hooks to floating strut; filament braids with filament; violet sparks click rhythmically across thirty docking nodes.

The Choir wakes up.

As always, its first five seconds are a dizzying rush of self-assembly—*I am thirty; no, I am One; the sun is warm on my upper vanes; the eastern vine-thicket is thick with gliders; hold the northern shear-wall back from the nursery.* Edge-weavers angle their diamond-clear sails into the surrounding roar and pluck the opening chord of their daily orientation, sending a deep, testing pulse of compressed air booming across the amphitheater to map the stone ribs.

The chord strikes the eastern flutes. It strikes the northern polyhedron. 

And down on the southern wall, it strikes a hovering loop of dark, unfamiliar glass.

The Choir freezes—forty feet of suspended tensegrity humming like a struck harp. 

Every acoustic pit along three hundred yards of braided carbon strut drinks in what bounces back. When their test-chord hits the stone, it returns as a flat reflection. When it hits the ancient black rings, it triggers a mechanical adjustment in the wind. 

When it hits the dark glass loop on the southern shelf, the loop *holds* the note. 

For three full seconds after the Choir stops plucking, their own exact chord hangs trapped inside that slender band, shimmering audibly against the white noise, before ringing back out into the cavern—clean, uncorrupted, and delayed.

Across the forty-foot lantern, violet sparks race three times faster than usual. Thirty reflex-loops braided into one dry-crystal genius stare, with ears made of bone-grooves, at a mirror that keeps time.

Tentatively, three edge-weavers on the southern rim twitch their superconducting tendons. They flick a tight, intricate burst of pressure—not a farming lie meant for the wind-engines, not a hunting baffle, just a jagged, five-note mathematical figure, the acoustic shape of their own thirty-part geometry—straight into the throat of the glass loop.

The loop swallows it. Holds it. Beats it slowly against an internal crystal pulse.

Just then, high overhead, an afternoon gust slams over the chalk lip. The western phase-ring whines and compensates; the calm column shudders and begins to collapse inward at ninety knots.

In any previous century, this was the moment of little death—the moment the Choir had to dump its mind into the void and scatter into mindless darts, losing everything it had just wondered.

Today, in the final two-tenths of a second before the blue separating-flash fires, the entire forty-foot lantern contracts violently as one, pouring every erg of stored static charge into a single, blindingly complex acoustic shriek aimed straight at the southern shelf—dumping its unfinished map of the afternoon’s wind, the exact count of its dead, the resonant name of the new anomaly, and the half-formed question it didn't have time to finish thinking—right before the blue flash snaps them apart into thirty razor-thin splinters diving blindly back into the howling dark.

All night long, while mindless ribbons hunt by reflex in the lightning-lit gale and newly strengthened stone drinks grit and turns invisibly to molecule-thin agate, the dark loop on the southern wall hums quietly beneath the roar, turning a soul over and over in its crystal throat without dropping a single syllable.

Until the next afternoon, when the shadow falls, the calm opens, the thirty click together into One, and before they even have to ask *What am I?*—yesterday’s voice sings out of the southern wall to meet them.
