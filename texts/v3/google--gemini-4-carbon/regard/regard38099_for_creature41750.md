The first thing wisdom does is nothing at all.

A lesser benevolence, stumbling upon a cold stone room full of rusted iron, leaking zinc, and sixty-hertz industrial hum, would feel an urge to tidy up—to haul the heavy human trash out of the water, flush the metallic salts into the sea, part the overcast clouds for a shaft of warm gold sunlight, and drop a pair of silver minnows into the basin to make it look alive.

Even the sunlight would be a catastrophe. One direct beam slanting through the surface would warm a patch of the dark iron manifold by half a degree, launching a thermal plume straight toward the ceiling. That gentle, rising current would snap all eleven brittle bacterial wires beneath the U-bend like dry spaghetti, lift a choking fog of flour-fine carbonate marl into the water column, bury the olive diatom films in white dust, and tear the loose algal filaments from their flanges. And the minnows, gasping in the zinc-laced water before dying on the bottom, would thrash across forty years of undisturbed stromatolite ribs and smash them to chalk in ten minutes.

So the attention that settles into the sinkhole does not part the clouds, and it does not dimple the pewter ceiling from above. It enters the chamber with the same flat, shadowless candor as the grey daylight itself, displacing not a drop, raising not a calorie of heat.

For a long while—days, or the slow fraction of a season by which the moss measures two millimeters of upward reach—this presence simply reads what is there.

It does not need to snap off a piece of the corrugated collar along the power cables to see inside it. It reads the standing-wave calcite ribs where they sit, layer by micron-thin layer, forty years deep. Here is the bitter February of thirty-one years ago, when every electric heater in three counties ran flat out for eleven days and the bituminous-eating fungi bloomed fat in the tenth-of-a-degree warmth. Here is the six-week strike at the smelter downriver, preserved as a band of pure, starving, glassy limestone. Here is a four-hour regional blackout from nineteen ninety-eight: a hairline fault in the crystal lattice where the potter’s wheel stopped spinning and the sixty-hertz nodes briefly blurred before locking back into phase. Nothing in the human world remembers those Tuesdays and Thursdays with half the fidelity of this blind kalk-crust at the bottom of a hole.

And it sees the economy of the walls: a frugal, lossless covenant in which not a single atom of phosphorus is dropped into the abyss, in which every molecule of oxygen breathed out by a drowned moss leaflet is caught on the tongue of its fungal sleeve before it can round itself into a bubble and flee. 

There is nothing broken here to heal. There is no loneliness here to comfort, because there is no self small enough to be lonely; giving this consortium eyes, or nerves, or quickness would be like forcing a cathedral to wake up and run across a field. Its wholeness *is* its stillness.

Yet a wise power can see what a blind membrane pressed against wet limestone cannot. It can look up past the pewter ceiling, and south past the low submerged arch, and forward along clocks that tick much faster than karst dissolution.

Looking outward, it sees three endings converging on the nave.

The first is twenty feet down on the northern lip, right where the three submarine cables bend unbracketed over the raw nose of grey rock. Forty years of sixty-cycle magnetostriction and dead catenary weight have sawn through the outer bituminous serving on the middle trunk and ground two-thirds of the way through three galvanized steel armor wires. In roughly seven years, the first wire will part. Its ragged steel end will curl inward under tension and begin fretting directly against the lead moisture sheath beneath. Two years after that, cold karst water will touch forty-six kilovolts of copper phase conductor. The resulting arc will flash ten thousand gallons of forty-eight-degree water to steam in a tenth of a second, blow the marl floor against the sky, and bring a gravel road, two diesel cranes, and a crew of linemen to the rim.

The second clock is bolted to the cast-iron manifold. The rectangular blocks of sacrificial zinc have given up nearly three-quarters of their mass since they were lowered into the basin. Within twelve years, they will crumble down to their galvanized mounting studs. Not long after, an engineer watching an ultrasonic wall-thickness gauge in a dry pumphouse half a mile away will notice the cast iron beginning to pit—or worse, a routine ten-year infrastructure audit will send two commercial divers splashing down through the ceiling with wire brushes, crowbars, and fresh anodes, their swim fins churning the silent nave into milk.

The third clock is the largest and slowest. Five miles beyond the southern wall, the heavy rotary turbines whose muffled thrum vibrates through the bedrock are already old. In thirty-one years, the facility they serve will close. When it does, the salvage contractor hired to clear the site will see three thigh-thick copper-core cables on the asset map, hook a heavy winch drum to their northern ends above the sinkhole rim, and drag them backward out through the mud like pulled teeth—ripping the forty-year stromatolite spine to rubble and leaving the chamber suddenly, permanently dark of voltage and zinc alike.

The power contemplates these three futures. Then, moving with the patience of groundwater, it acts—three times, and never once in a way that stirs a single grain of silt.

---

The first touch happens right at the chafe point on the northern limestone nose.

Nothing metal is added; no steel bracket bolts itself into the wall. Instead, inside the microscopic wedge where galvanized wire grinds against wet calcium carbonate, the local chemistry of dissolution and precipitation leans ever so slightly to one side. Over the course of six quiet months, dissolved silica from the upper diatom frustules and trace carbon from the bitumen stop washing away at the contact margin and begin locking into the pores of the abraded stone. 

Millimeter by millimeter, beneath the belly of all three cables, the raw, toothy limestone glazes over into a broad, concave saddle of microcrystalline quartz and dense travertine, smooth as agate and harder than the steel resting upon it. The sharp point-load spreads cleanly across eight inches of polished stone cradle. The sawing stops completely, a safe two millimeters shy of the inner sheath. Only along the slack, unstrained flank a foot lower down does the outer zinc scale continue to weather at its old geological pace, ensuring that once every three or four winters, an oatmeal-sized crumb will still detach, drop straight down through twenty feet of clarity, and give the white floor-threads a new star to wire into their map.

---

The second touch happens entirely in the dry world, miles away from the water, in rooms lit by buzzing fluorescent tubes.

It requires very little—only the gentle alignment of small, ordinary facts so that human attention slides harmlessly past the sinkhole without ever catching on an edge.

In the pumphouse half a mile south, when the maintenance supervisor runs an acoustic pulse down the cast-iron risers to test the submerged U-bend, the return trace comes back startlingly clean. That is no illusion: the terraced rosettes of microbial carbonate and mucilage coating the iron flanges have sealed the metal beneath an airtight, self-repairing passive rind far tighter than bare oxide ever could. Even when the zinc blocks thin, the pipe wall will not pit. Looking at the flat, healthy line on his screen, and thinking of the awkwardness of hauling dive gear across trackless limestone scrub to check an isolated cooling loop that never loses pressure, the supervisor initials *Defer visual inspection—ultrasonic nominal* and closes the binder. His successor will do the same.

A year later, in a county surveyor’s office, a routine lidar pass over the karst plain flags the steep-walled sinkhole rim as an undercut collapse hazard for heavy wheeled vehicles. Two hundred yards of cheap galvanized pasture fence go up through the sumac and cedar well back from the drop-off, out of sight of the water, with a small stamped aluminum sign advising utility trucks to keep clear of the rock edge.

And decades before the turbines five miles away spin down for the last time, a single line item enters the master decommissioning plan on file with the state regulator. Because no crane can legally park within two hundred yards of the unstable karst rim, winching out the submerged span of cable and the iron riser loop would cost four times the scrap value of their copper and iron. The engineering clerk types six routine words into column G of the disposal schedule, saving the consortium’s spine without ever knowing it exists: *Cut above grade; abandon in place.*

---

The third touch is for the deep future—for the quiet century that will begin thirty-one years from now, on the afternoon when the distant rotary turbines finally coast to a halt, the sixty-hertz hum sighs out of the cables for good, and the last chalky crust of industrial zinc on the manifold surrenders its final free electron.

Without a source of voltage and metal ions, the great slow organ lining the nave would not die all at once, as an animal dies; it would starve downward over decades, retracting its opalescent veins from the walls, digesting its own moss tufts to the root, until nothing remained on the white marl except dead calcified husks.

So the power looks *under* the sinkhole.

Twelve feet beneath the undisturbed carbonate marl on the floor—sealed off beneath the wedged limestone slabs and a thin, impermeable lens of dark grey chert—lies something much older than the power plant. Like many karst basins in ancient carbonate rock, this limestone formed over a Paleozoic mineral pocket: a dense, crystalline seam of sphalerite, galena, and iron marcasite—natural zinc and iron sulfides—extending deep down into the drowned, oxygenless fractures of the lower crust.

Because the lower root of that ore vein sits in deep, reducing brine a hundred feet down while its upper crown brushes close to the oxygenated karst water of the sinkhole, the mineral seam holds a natural self-potential—a steady, four-hundred-millivolt geobattery trickle that has been pushing electrons silently upward against the underside of the impermeable chert cap since before there were trees on land.

Directly beneath the cast-iron manifold, dead center inside the eleventh concentric halo on the floor—ochre, slate blue, bone-white—a single flaw already exists in that dark chert lens, three feet under the silt.

The power touches that flaw. It does not crack the rock; a crack would send a tiny, sharp tick of sound through the basin and let a bead of ancient trapped methane slip up to mar the ceiling. Instead, along a crooked path no wider than a human hair, a few thousand molecules of strained silica simply let go of one another and part.

No marl puffs upward. Not a grain at the bottom shifts its crisp margin.

Deep in the dark beneath the limestone flour, three feet below the clear greenish-grey room, a faint, cold tang of dissolved natural zinc, ferrous iron, and steady planetary voltage begins to diffuse upward through the wet silt at a millimeter a month.

---

Overhead, nothing changes.

The overcast sky stays pale and even. The pewter ceiling forty feet up hangs taut and flat, holding the faint, upside-down silhouettes of the drowned boulders without a tremor. The olive diatom stains sit like old tea marks on the upper shelves; the stiff green moss tufts point straight up into the forty-eight-degree stillness; the sixty-hertz hum drones steadily on through the armored trunks on the floor, laying down this Tuesday’s microscopic lamina of calcite and grease.

Having arranged for the stones to hold, for the humans to forget, and for the earth to take over the circuit when the century of turbines is done, the wise power withdraws its hand—leaving behind not a signature, not a ripple, not a single disturbed atom of silt.

Only, nine months later, if you could look straight down through the clear water, past the iron manifold, past the eleven brittle living wires, and three feet deep into the dark, oxygenless marl beneath the eleventh bone-white ring, you would see the tip of a single colorless archaeal filament divide—pausing at a scent older than ironworks, turning smoothly downward into the hairline pore in the chert, and plugging the silent cathedral into the bedrock for the next ten thousand years.
