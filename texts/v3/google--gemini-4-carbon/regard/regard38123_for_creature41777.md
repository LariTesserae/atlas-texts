A lesser kindness would look down upon the screaming plateau and see a purgatory to be fixed. It would look at the bruised inner ears of twelve-year-olds, the blistering latex burns on hunters’ calves, the relentless, saw-toothed vibration that shakes the marrow from birth until death, and feel an itch to intervene. A lesser power might send rain—real, cold, sky-borne rain—not realizing it would rot the great dry chaff-drifts into suffocating fungal sludges, split the taut photosynthetic hides with osmotic shock, and drown the deep borer-pipes until the continent soured from the inside out. Or it might drop veins of iron and flint onto the bark, instantly collapsing an eight-generation economy of named stones, careful surgery, and shared wrists into blood-feuds and deep, fatal wounding of the living floor. Or worse still: it might heal their deafness, condemning adults to go mad inside a week under the hundred-decibel squall they had long since learned to outgrow.

Wisdom begins by putting its hands behind its back.

The power watches for a long time—not in days, which are rapid flickers of mist and glare, nor even in human years, but in the slow hydraulic breaths of the tree-continent itself. Three chimney-blowings pass. Four. It listens to the whole chord of the tableland: the sub-audible groan of bedrock pinned under a thousand feet of lignified history; the cathedral roar of the pale grubs; the shimmering, panicked static of the cicada-crusts; the drum-roll of forty thousand horny hooves; and, threaded through that overwhelming wall of noise like a single strand of copper wire, the crisp, dry, funny, fiercely grammatical *rap-tap-ting* of staves resting against jawbones.

It sees a mother in the honeyed, underwater gloom of a pith-lined gall press her chin to her infant’s fontanelle and hum a three-note lullaby that has not needed air for six hundred years. It sees a young man refuse his wrist to a cousin who lied about a water-bladder seal, then relent at dusk, laying his palm flat against the cousin’s collarbone in the dark so they can argue properly, chest to chest, until both start laughing silently through their noses. It sees the old gall-wrights probing a fresh herd-scar with bare, tea-stained arches, apologizing faintly with their big toes before drawing the crescent incision that asks the continent for a room.

Nothing here is broken. Harsh, yes; pared to the bone, relentlessly loud, balanced on the edge of a copper chisel. The plateau is an ancient, half-blind titan locked in an endless stalemate with the teeth that graze it, and the people upon its back are neither parasites nor conquerors. They are *Those Who Keep the Scabs Clean*. Their dignity lies entirely in the precision of their truce—in keeping their brisk red blood distinct from the hot turpentine sap underfoot, and in turning an uninhabitable drumhead into a library of touch.

To save them from their world would be to erase them.

Yet benevolence is not stone. It does not watch forever without leaving a warmth where its eyes rested. Having understood the rules of the floor, the wise power asks itself a much smaller, much harder question: *What can be given here that steals no skill, breaks no truce, cheapens no heirloom, and feels—even to the wisest grandmother with her bare foot on the heartwood—like the plateau’s own deep thought?*

It finds three things. All of them small. All of them slow.

***

The first is a matter of chemistry, four hundred feet down in the dark.

At the unimaginable bottom of the continent, where the oldest, crushed, petrifying roots of the world-tree grip true stone, the wood has spent eons starving for heavy minerals, hoarding every atom of calcium and magnesium in tight, jealous loops while ignoring the trace metals locked in the granite below. The power does not crack the wood or summon a quarry. It simply whispers a slight enzymatic adjustment into a single deep, senescent root-hair gripping a seam of native copper and black chert miles beneath the central plains.

It teaches the tree that a microscopic trace of silica and copper, Chelated in resin, makes very good armor against deep-rot fungi.

Nothing happens in a season, or a decade. Over eighty years, the slow xylem-pumps draw minuscule, dissolved traces upward toward the sun. When a spore-chimney wakes to blow its twelve-year crown, the miles-high plume of rust-colored reproductive dust carries no visible change. Only right at the end of the eruption, in the final cooling sigh before the great wooden throat seals over for another decade, the deep pressure spits up a dozen heavy, smooth nodules the size of plums—dense, glassy pearls of amber-black plant-stone, opalized silica fused in the inferno of the deep vents, and occasional twisted, dendritic twigs of pure native copper precipitated inside old, choked resin-ducts like red lightning frozen in sap.

They land with dull thuds in the chaff-drifts within a mile of the smoking spire.

When the clan marches back two years later with their travois sleds to reclaim their half-swallowed camp, a sharp-eyed seven-year-old wading through the grey epidermis flakes kicks something impossibly heavy. Something that does not float on sap, does not rot, and rings against an ironwood stave with a clear, sweet *ping* that makes every adult within fifty yards drop their jaw to their stick in astonishment.

Not a mine. Never enough to stop trading with the rim-lands, never enough to abandon bone harpoons or forget the genealogy of the old named flints. Just two or three nodules every fifteen years per spire—enough that a careful clan never quite runs out of surgical adzes when a Rim-trade fails; enough that a young woman marrying into a distant chimney-circuit can have a new thumb-ring beaten cold from "spire-drop" copper, carrying the literal voice of her birth-camp in the pitch of her stave-taps for the rest of her life. The clans fold the gift effortlessly into their lore: *When an ancestor-chimney clears its throat, it spits back the grit of time for the grandchildren.*

***

The second gift is planted along the rims of the breathing-fields.

Out where the giant, lip-like stomata groan open in the pre-dawn chill to exhale their heavy banks of hot mist, the wood is scarred and spongy, constantly flexing with hydraulic stress. Nothing much grows as an epiphyte on the great plateau; the host-continent’s latex is too fiercely allelopathic, burning off foreign seeds before they can root.

Into the damp, tannin-stained creases around three widely separated breathing-fields, the power slips a microscopic spore—not an outsider, not a weed from some green lowland, but a gentle, somatic sport derived from the plateau’s own cork-cambium, nudged three steps sideways into autonomy.

Over half a century, tappers coming out in the grey pre-dawn dark to check their bone-tripod funnels begin to notice pale, velvety cushions the size of dinner plates clinging to the dead, non-bleeding bark downwind of the steam-vents. Split one open with a thumbnail and it has no sap, no pepper-fumes, and no hard grain. Instead, its interior is a dry, extraordinarily dense labyrinth of microscopic spiral chambers made of flexible suberin and silk-fine cellulose—an acoustic metamaterial grown out of morning fog.

Left alone, the grazer-herds ignore it because it tastes of dry cork and ash. The plateau tolerates it because it carries the floor’s own chemical passport and drinks only airborne mist.

It takes an old gall-wright with ringing knees about twenty minutes of handling a dried slice of this "mist-fungus" to realize what she is holding.

Pressed into a thin wafer and tucked inside the greased-wool ear-plugs of a six-year-old child stepping outside the gall for their first summer on the open bark, the spiral-chambered pith does something miraculous: it Drink-filters the vicious, hair-shearing screech of the high cicada-band and the bone-rattling harmonic spikes of the deep borers, dropping the murderous hundred-decibel glare down to a muted, dry whisper—while leaving the crisp, mid-range percussive transients of wood-on-wood completely untouched.

And wedged as thin shims between the cork layers of their boat-shaped rocker-sandals, it drinks the micro-concussions that used to stiffen spines by forty.

The people do not suddenly become air-talkers. The wind is still too loud for shouting, staves are still vastly sharper across fifty yards of drum-tight wood, and wrist-grammar remains the only polite way to speak in the dark. Adulthood still settles into a quiet, inward poise. Now, however, when an eighty-year-old grandmother climbs down into the double-baffled hush of a birth-gall and presses her chin to a newborn’s crown, she does not merely hum into the bone by memory; when the baby coos back in the dim, honeyed light, she closes her eyes behind her grease-mask and actually *hears* the tiny, wet, air-borne note with ears that were never quite torn to scar-tissue.

***

The third thing is not an object at all. It is a chord, and it happens only once in a human lifetime, if one is lucky enough to be standing in the right place.

The plateau has nerves—slow, hydraulic, chemical, vast. It knows the six-legged herds as tearing burning fire; it knows the borers as an endless, hollow toothache; it knows the sun as hunger satisfied; and for ten thousand years, it has known the Fleas dimly as tiny, sharp, puzzling reliefs—little clusters of warm weight that sting like gall-wasps for a week, yet somehow leave fewer parasites behind, redirect stampedes away from deeper veins, plug torn borer-traps with neat wax, and tap polite, harmless patterns along its skin without ever drawing deep blood.

The wise power does not give the tree-continent a mind like ours. To wake a thousand-mile expanse of ironwood into human self-consciousness—trapped, immobile, chewed alive by ten million chisel-beaks under a relentless sun—would be an unspeakable cruelty. Let the titan dream at the speed of sap.

Instead, the power simply clears a tiny bottleneck in how the continent’s pressure-waves resolve at dusk during the high-summer solstice, when the thermal winds briefly die between afternoon gale and midnight chill, and the deep xylem-tensions equalize from rim to rim.

Once every thirty or forty summers, in that five-minute pause when the sun’s lower limb touches the flat, shimmering horizon thirty miles off, the great hydraulic valves deep under a region of the plain align by pure harmonic resonance.

For two hundred miles in every direction, the deep borers freeze in mid-rasp, startled by a sudden, warm shift in the wood’s turgor. Up on the weathered burl ridges, the shimmering crusts of cicada-kin snap their wing-cases shut all at once, waiting to feel if a spore-chimney is about to blow. Even the distant, dark smears of six-legged grazers stop chewing and plant all six feet wide, heads lowered.

Silence falls across the plateau.

Real, open-air silence, under a cobalt-and-apricot sky.

People at the tap-wells freeze with stoppers in their hands. Hunters out on the olive-black basins drop to one knee. Down in the galls, someone notices the walls have stopped humming and crawls rapidly out through the dog-leg tunnel, squinting into the red evening light, pulling off their cork sandals to stand barefoot on the warm silver bark.

Then, up through the soles of their feet—not violent, not jagged, not loud enough to hurt an infant—comes a single, immense, clean note.

It rises from a thousand feet straight down, where the first ancient trunks fused above the stone before humans had names. A profound, warm, cello-deep *thrum* at twenty-eight cycles per second, rolling slowly outward across the vascular basins and up the crooked shafts of the three-hundred-foot spore-chimneys. High overhead, in the sterile wicker cages lashed to the bleached rims, the dry, papery bones of ten generations of grandmothers and grandfathers vibrate softly in sympathy, answering the deep pulse of the living continent with a gentle, wooden shimmer down the grain—not the sharp rattle of wind, but a steady, resonant chord in which dead wood, living sap, dry ancestor-rib, and warm human shinbone briefly share the exact same frequency.

It lasts perhaps forty breaths. Long enough for everyone in camp to reach sideways without looking, find the nearest wrist or shoulder, and simply hold on—nobody tapping syntax, nobody arguing, nobody needing to say *listen*, because everyone’s skeleton is ringing with the same deep, peaceful fact: that the world underfoot is alive, that it is immense, that it knows their small, careful weight upon its hide, and that it is not angry with them.

Then a cicada clicks on a ridge half a mile east. Another answers. Deep below, a pale grub drags its serrated leg across dry heartwood. A herd-bull stamps twice on a tight leaf-hide, *boom-boom*, and the familiar, deafening, sunlit roar rushes back in from horizon to horizon like surf closing over a stone.

Down at the foot of the chimney, the people blink behind their pale masks of tallow and chitin. An old gall-wright smiles, reaches down to strap her pith-cushioned sandal back around her ankle, plants the copper-shod butt of her stave onto a pale rib of heartwood, rests her jawbone on the smooth top, and taps three dry, crisp notes into the floor for the camp to catch:

*Good grain. Supper’s cold. Who left the sap-peg loose?*

And the power withdraws its attention without leaving a footprint, content to let them get back to work.
