A lesser power, looking down through the smoked-glass ceiling of that sky, would feel a rush of pity—which is so often just vanity wearing a gentle mask—and reach in to fix the place.

It would round off the razor-sharp basalt so the children wouldn’t need chain-mesh gloves. It would thicken the freezing air so a man could laugh without budgeting his breath for the next ten minutes. It would stitch the spatial faults shut, quiet the four-minute echo of the drop-forge, and pour a million tons of soft, loamy river-silt into the chasms so the dead could lie flat instead of feeding cabbages in iron troughs.

And in a single afternoon of well-meaning mercy, it would murder the city.

A wise power watches for a long time before it even considers touching a rivet. It listens to the dry, clicking conversations running through the wet steel of the catwalks. It watches the crag-fowl drop off a roof and ride a seam in reality to save their wings three hundred yards of thin air; if you heal that seam, half the rookeries starve before winter. It watches the twelve-year-old boy on the apron of the cable-car—his pulse steady at thirty-five beats a minute, his thumb resting calmly on his iron ring, measuring the lean of local gravity without a trace of fear—and recognizes that this is not a refugee camp clinging to a disaster. This is a triumph. 

To take away their harshness would be to tell the Pin-Drivers that their patience meant nothing, to tell the families tending the greenhouse troughs that their ancestors had become dirt for no reason, to turn barrel-chested masters of a vertical world into clumsy, over-blooded invalids gasping in thick air.

So the power does not smooth a single edge of quartz. It does not warm the mist by a single degree. 

Instead, it matches the tempo of the valley.

At twenty thousand feet, anything sudden is an enemy. A miracle here cannot arrive as a trumpet blast or a shaft of golden sun breaking through the pewter gloom—the glare would blind eyes adapted to sodium lamps, and the noise would bounce off the glassy amphitheater walls until it shook the tenements loose from their needles. If grace is going to enter this cordillera, it must arrive the way the stone arrives, and work at the speed of the forty-foot flywheels: an eighth of an inch per hour, dark, heavy, and cold.

Down in the unrendered abyss—below the floating lenses of black water, where the bottom of the world is still trying to decide what shape it wants to take—the power makes three small, quiet adjustments to the rules of what is coming up through the seams.

First, for the hands and the engines, it introduces a new vein into the extruding rock.

Four days later, a Surveyor of the Seam dangling beneath Ward Nine notices her caged beetles singing a slightly lower chord—not the frantic scattering of a spatial fault, just a deep, resonant hum, a fifth below the sixty-cycle drone of the transformers. Where two ghostly cliffs have just been pinned together by a fresh wrought-iron rod, a narrow ribbon of pale, blue-grey mineral has been squeezed out along the weld, weeping out of the raw basalt like mortar from between bricks. 

When a workman chips a piece loose with his pick, it doesn't fracture into scalpels. It cleaves into soft, soapy plates that smear between his chain-mesh fingers like cold tallow. It is a native, high-geometry steatite—a dense, iron-rich soapstone that drinks vibration and refuses to hold an edge. Ground into powder, it makes a grease for the counterweight chains that never stiffens in the freezing damp. Cut into blocks and mortared onto the sills of doorframes, the edges of stair-treads, and the rims of docking aprons, it gives a human hand somewhere bare to rest. And when the Pin-Drivers experimentally coat a grounding-rod in a slurry of the grey paste, the rod bites into the overlapping stone with half the steam-pressure, settling the shivering cliffs not with a cannon-shot that sets the valley screaming, but with a low, satisfied *thud* like a heavy door closing against felt.

Second, for the greenhouses and the lockets, it answers the problem of time.

Nothing has been here long enough to be ground down—so the power lends a tiny, invisible fraction of age to the mist itself. It seeds the lower condensation clouds with a microscopic, high-altitude lichen, no bigger than coal-soot, that cannot survive anywhere warm. It avoids the brick tenements, avoids the steam-pipes, avoids the smelters and the ironwork completely. It only eats raw, unweathered glass-faces miles away from the wards, out on the unpinned ridges where exile used to mean death. 

It works very slowly. It does not ruin the sharp geometry of the peaks; from half a mile away, the black polyhedrons look just as stark and newly minted as ever. But where the shivering sheets of water spill down from terrace to razor-edged terrace, the water no longer drops completely clear. When it pools on those invisible, horizontal shelves of air two thousand feet up, a fine, dark sediment settles at the bottom of the floating lenses—the very first true silt the mountain range has ever made on its own. 

When the wind shifts, damp grey drifts of mineral flour collect in the leeward angles of the foundry roofs, mixing with the crag-fowl guano and the coal-clinker. Nobody’s grandmother is replaced; the black humus in the upper-story troughs remains sacred, the core of every household. But now there is enough spare, clean earth blowing in off the wild stone that a young couple starting a new trough in a newly riveted spire doesn't have to wait for someone to die before they can plant a radish.

And third—and smallest of all—the power leaves something purely for the boy on the cable-car, and for everyone like him who spends their life listening to wet iron.

In a city that rings like an inside-out bell, silence is the rarest luxury, and music is nearly impossible outdoors; any note sung aloud smears itself across four minutes of echo until it turns into mud. Yet the whole metropolis is strung together by miles of wrist-thick steel hawsers, catenary cables, and cast-iron aqueducts, all humming under immense tension.

The power does not speak a word into those cables. Wisdom knows better than to announce itself to people who value quiet. 

It merely tunes the wind where it passes through three specific spatial creases at the far northern mouth of the five-mile gulf—narrowing a fault here by the breadth of a thumb, widening an extrusion seam there—so that the constant, freezing draft drawn inward by the breath of the blast furnaces no longer howls at random. 

Now, in the four-minute pause after the distant drop-forge strikes—right as the rolling avalanche of noise finally dies away into the mist, before the great hammer lifts again—if the boy on the docking platform takes off one chain-mesh glove and lays his bare, straight-scarred palm flat against the wet steel of the handrail, he feels something underneath the dry clicking of the telegraph rings and the slow pulse of the flywheels.

It is a chord, travelling through the iron spine of the entire cordillera. 

It is sub-audible in the air, so it never wakes the rookeries or bounces off the quartz walls. You can only hear it through your bones, leaning your forehead or your palm against the metal that holds the city up. It is deep, slow, and pitched to the exact rhythm of a thirty-five-beat heart—a warm, resonant hum rising up from the pinned roots of the mountains, passing through fifty isolated wards at once, telling anyone with their hand on the rail in the pewter dark that the cables are holding, the pins are biting deep, and the ground beneath them, though still arriving, is glad they are here.

The boy stands on the iron apron as the car kisses the greased buffers without a spark. He leaves his bare palm on the cold rail for three seconds longer than his job requires—one heartbeat, two—feeling the chord pass through his wrist cuff and into his chest. 

Then he pulls his glove back on, taps twice with his ring to signal *fastened*, and steps ashore onto stone that is, for the very first time, just slightly softer than his boots.
