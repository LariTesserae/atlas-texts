A lesser god would have brought the sun.

A proud or sentimental mind, looking down through the stratus deck into that nine-hundred-foot throat of cold limestone, would have seen the tallow-pale skin, the ten-inch sleeping cracks, the children quieted with mashed crop-fig instead of lullabies, and felt the hot, clumsy itch of pity. Such a power might have split the overhanging rim to let golden shafts of afternoon spill across the gravel, or called up a sweet river to wash the ammonia-slicked talus clean, or laid a gentle staircase up the fault-face to lead the forty grey shapes out into grass and wind and spoken names.

And within a year, the forty would have been dead.

The sun would have burned their wide, dark, membrane-cornered eyes to blind white scars. A river would have flushed away the lime-crusts and drowned the beetles, breaking the scavenger web that kept the valley from choking in six million rotting carcasses. Warmth would have spoiled the horn-cured swiftlets on top of the monoliths in three days and woken sleek, fast diseases in the guano-mud. And a staircase—any easing of the sheer vertical terror of the walls—would have brought the loud men down from the plateau not five at a time on greasy ropes, nervous and eager to leave, but by the hundred, with mules and iron shovels to mine the bird-lime, and dogs that hunt by more than sight, and the casual, righteous cruelty that walking-tall people always bring to cousins who crouch in the grease.

The power that actually bent its attention to the basin knew all of this in the space of a single three-second drip from a chert overhang.

It did not come down like lightning or a voice. Down here, light was violence and voice was static. Instead, it lowered its awareness into the dead air of the inversion the way cold water settles into gravel—weightless, silent, observant, taking the exact temperature of the stone before deciding whether even a thought was too heavy to leave behind.

For a long time—if time could be measured where winter and noon were rumors—it simply stayed, listening beneath the surf-roar of the bird columns.

It watched a woman with grey-plastered stubble and scarred knuckles crack the femur of a night-crow between her back molars, draw out the cold thread of marrow, and press it onto the tongue of an old man whose knees had thickened with lime-ache until he could no longer glide fast enough to beat the rock-martens to a fallen squab. He did not thank her; he laid two greased fingers briefly across the pulse in her wrist—*warmth, still here*—and leaned his back against the dry rock flour of the slot so she could use his ribs as a bolster while she slept.

It watched two adolescents crouched thirty yards apart on the open chert flats during a heavy drop of fruit-birds, catching each other’s eyes through the drifting black chaff of wings and making a tiny, wholly unnecessary flick of the little finger low against their bellies—a dry joke about a wide-eared fox that had just slipped on an egg-slicked stone and landed flat on its chin. Neither smiled with their mouths, because cracking the fat-rind around the lips let the chill in, so they smiled with a slow, twin narrowing of their lower lids before turning back to their flint flakes.

They were not miserable. They were not beasts, and they were not broken remnants waiting to be rescued back into an upper-world shape. They had paid a staggering price to keep their humanity inside a stone box—giving up song, fire, height, running, colour, and easy birth—and yet inside the narrow margin left to them, they were whole. They remembered to turn the stranger’s hammerstone damp-side down. They shared the bruised summer figs from a dead bird’s throat before swallowing their own portion. They loved one another in dark horizontal cracks ten inches high, quietly, fiercely, skin through tallow.

*Do not insult them with miracles,* the wise power thought. *They have outwitted the stone on its own terms. Anything that breaks the terms betrays their craft.*

Yet wisdom is not stone. To see a life balanced on the razor edge of banded chert—forty souls only, where one bad collapse of a sleeping-slab, one rope-party descending a new chimney with a keen-nosed hound, or three lean seasons of blight among the plateau laurels could thin forty to twelve, and twelve to none—is to ask whether there is a gift small enough not to wake the valley, slow enough not to startle the hand that receives it, and plain enough to pass forever as luck.

So the power worked, not upon the people, and not against the basin, but deep inside the quietest margins of the rock itself.

First, it looked nine hundred feet up, to the narrow drip-chimney where the three-ply bast rope hung tied around its horn of grey limestone.

The plateau men only knew two ways down through the overhanging lip of the fault: that chimney on the east wall, and a steeper, miserable crack four miles north that they used once every few summers. Around the upper throat of the eastern chimney, up where the coastal stratus dragged wet bellies through stunted cliff-juniper and sour grass, a hairline frost-fracture already ran behind a thirty-ton block of caprock. Left alone, it would have held for another eighty years.

The power did not strike it. It merely coaxed a thread of mist-water to linger an hour longer in the seam before a rare sharp night on the upper plateau. Ice swelled by the thickness of a thumbnail. Over four months, silent grains of quartz let go one by one. 

One grey morning when no egg-gatherers were on the ropes and no floor-dwellers were working that mile of the talus, the inner lip of the upper chimney leaned three inches inward and wedged fast. From the plateau, nothing looked haunted or forbidden; the climb had simply gone rotten, the way young limestone always goes rotten, narrowing the throat just enough that a man with a wide basket or a dog slung in a harness could no longer squeeze past the chockstone, and frayed bast rope caught and sawed itself half-through on a fresh tooth of chert. The plateau men cursed, spat into the mist, took half as many eggs that year, and never once thought of bringing hounds or timber down a crack that barely let a thin boy pass sideways.

Down on the floor, nine hundred feet below, nobody heard the shift over the roar of six million wings. Only, months later, an old tracker noticed that the two-fisted sign—*the loud ones are coming down*—had been made twice since the last moult instead of six times.

Second, the power sank its attention deep beneath the flat gravel corridors at the center of the basin, miles from the reeking walls, directly below three of the largest detached monoliths where the forty slept.

Deep under the subsided block of the valley floor, the parallel fault lines that had dropped the basin still carried a faint, residual warmth from the earth’s crust—nothing volcanic, nothing dramatic, no steam or sulfur, only rock at eighty degrees instead of fifty, sealed beneath forty fathoms of impermeable shale.

With a patience slower than root-growth, the power teased open a capillary thread—thinner than a grass-stem—through the underlying limestone directly beneath the dry rock-flour beds of the sleeping slots. No water rose through it. No gas escaped to taint the dead air. Only a slow, dry conduction began to climb through the stone sole of four great slabs, raising the temperature of the bedrock beneath the ten-inch crevices by seven degrees.

Seven degrees is nothing to a man in a wool coat under the sun. 

Inside a windless horizontal slot where five small greased bodies curl together to keep the fifty-degree rock from drinking the marrow out of their ribs while they sleep, seven degrees is everything. None of the sleepers woke sweating; none of them wondered why a god had touched their house. Over the next two years, the rock flour under those four slabs simply felt *good*—like stone that held the ghost of an old warmth. Babies born into that velvet dust did not have to burn every drop of their mothers’ milk just to keep their chests moving through the first ten days. And the old man with the lime-thickened knees found, after a hundred quiet sleeps against the back wall, that his joints unbent an inch easier when he slid out into the pewter gloom, buying him five more years of teaching the young ones how to strike a chert core without bruising the bulb of percussion, and where the blind isopods clustered thickest in the lime-pans.

Third, the power turned to the sky—not the blue sky above the clouds, which belonged to another species now, but the plateau woods ten miles beyond the rim where the heavy, night-eyed fruit birds flew through the mist-slot to gorge.

On the stony ridges up there, out of reach of the plateau shepherds’ goats, grew low thickets of wild mountain fig, sea-buckthorn, and oil-laurel. Where windblown seeds fell into dry cracks and usually withered, a quiet encouragement settled into the mycorrhizae beneath the soil. Over a decade, those wild, unvisited thickets thickened and spread along three miles of fog-wet crag—heavy-fruiting, tart, bursting with winter-hardy sugars, vitamin-rich pulp, and dense, nutty oils, fruiting in staggered waves so there was never a month of the plateau year when the branches hung wholly bare.

The night-crows multiplied along the upper seams of the basin cliffs. More squabs grew heavy with yellow fat and jostled off the ledges onto the gravel aprons. More warm, uncrushed orange berries and sweet purple figs came dropping out of the grey ceiling inside muscular crops, waiting for a thumb-sized flake of dark chert and two scarred, careful fingers. Within half a generation, there would not be forty people beneath the boulders, precarious as a single pinch of cold ash; there would be seventy, perhaps eighty—never enough to crowd the vast gravel corridors or upset the beetles and foxes, never enough to need fire or chiefs or walls, just enough that when a young woman crouched by a water-saucer and looked across half a mile of stone, she knew there were other lineages out beyond the slate haze whose blood was not too close to her own.

That would have been enough. Reason said stop there: secure the walls against intrusion, warm the sleeping-stone by the breadth of a breath, enrich the wild orchards of the messenger-birds, and depart.

And yet, before withdrawing, the power lingered beside a single monolith out in the silent geometry of the middle flats.

Wisdom cares for survival, yes—for calories, warmth, concealments, gene-pools. But heart knows that a mind that remembers to turn a borrowed hammerstone back into its damp-ring is a mind capable of wonder, starved though it may be of things that shine without hurting.

Under the east corner of a boulder a quarter mile from the central sleeping-slab, half-buried beneath three inches of scree where only a hand reaching after a wounded cave cricket would ever find it, lay a fist-sized nodule of local chert. To the eye, its rind was the same dull, chalky grey as ten million other stones in the valley.

Deep inside the silica lattice of that nodule—and a dozen others scattered miles apart across the floor—the power tightened the microscopic spheres of hydrated quartz until they aligned, silent and cold beneath their grey crusts.

Years from now, a twelve-year-old boy coated in tallow and stone-dust will squat beside a bedrock mirror, strike that cobble with a hammerstone expecting an ordinary black knife-spall for slitting swiftlets, and stop dead.

Resting in his greased palm, silent in the endless *shhh-klick-lak* of the wheeling flocks, will sit a razor-sharp blade of precious black opal. Even in the shadowless, wet-pewter light of the basin, as he tilts his wrist a fraction of an inch against his pale belly, deep, slow embers of sea-green, violet, and pigeon-blood crimson will slide beneath the dark glassy surface—colours that have no name in touch-talk, colours softer than sun and colder than fire, asking no wood, making no smoke, giving away nothing to the rim nine hundred feet above.

He will hold his breath for four slow counts, watching the green light shift as his pulse moves his thumb. Then he will glide quietly back across the gravel to the ten-inch slot beneath his family's slab, tap twice on his mother’s collarbone in the dark, and press the stone into her greased hand, guiding her thumb toward the mouth of the crevice just far enough for the pewter gloom to catch the edge.

And in the wordless language of wrists and knuckles, two new signs will be born under the mountain—one for the knife that cuts, and another, pressed gently against the hollow of the throat, for the secret light inside the stone.

Satisfied, leaving not a single footprint in the damp dust for the beetles to smooth away, the power rose up through the swirling black columns of birds, passed silently through the grey stratus deck into the blinding, noisy, ordinary sun, and left the basin to its peace.
