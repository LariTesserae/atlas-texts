The first thing Wisdom does, upon looking down into the pewter glare of the basin, is hold entirely still.

A lesser benevolence would have arrived with improvements. It might have pitied the raw knees of the camelid calves and chamfered the razor lips of the greywacke, not realizing that softening the stone would ruin the suction-grip of the thumbnail amphipods and starve the fibrous, self-fraying hooves of the herds of the friction that keeps them trim. It might have parted the cloud bank to let warm valley air flood the sills, evaporating the millimeter-thin sheen of sweat on the vertical rock and stranding ten million maroon flatworms halfway between pools. Out of distaste for machinery in a virgin place, it might have snapped the Kevlar tethers or rusted the inverters—and in a single freezing night condemned forty week-old calves accustomed to the thirty-degree leeward hull, while forcing the bronze-ruffed vultures back into exhausting, blind commutes through sleet.

Wisdom sees better than that. It has watched ten thousand worlds, and ten thousand ages of this one, and it knows how vanishingly rare this particular arrangement is: an iron age that learned to walk on tiptoe.

For once, the clever, restless primates down in the valleys did not blast switchbacks into the talus. They did not pour concrete pads or string buzzing fences or leave men in tin shacks to grow bored and shoot at pale cats in the fog. Somebody down in a lighted room two thousand meters below designed an eight-foot pneumatic bladder specifically so it would deform around a slate splinter rather than crush it. Somebody muffled the transformers. Somebody wrote a routing algorithm that lets fifty tons of steel yield the right-of-way to a sedge cushion. And out of that uncommon restraint, the plateau did what life always does when it is not panicked: it treated the newcomer as weather and stone, and wove it into the household.

When a miracle is already occurring by mortal courtesy, a wise power does not interrupt to sign its name.

Yet Wisdom also sees across time, and therefore sees the three thin places where this quiet truce is fragile. It reaches down—not as a hand from the sky, nor a voice in the mist, because voices in the mist terrify plovers—but as three small, silent adjustments in the grain of things, each no louder than condensation spilling over a rock lip.

***

The first adjustment happens four hundred feet beneath the wet basalt, ahead of the rotating diamond teeth of Rig Four’s drill string.

Two miles east, unmapped and sleeping in a fault pocket left by the plateau’s violent uplift, lies a vein of extraordinarily pure, high-grade cesium and lithium pegmatite—rich enough, if a core sample ever rode up the pipe into an assay chamber, to shatter the economics of restraint. Within eighteen months of such a discovery, boardrooms six thousand miles away would override the highland conservation protocols. Charges would be set along the eastern pass; diesel haulers would grind up raw switchbacks; floodlights would bleach the pewter dark; the autonomous skimmers would be scrapped as toys.

On the other hand, if the basin’s brines thin out too quickly, a quarterly review three years from now will deem the highland sector unprofitable and reel all six aerostats home in a single week, yanking the warm cliffs out from under the herds before a generation of calves can relearn the old, colder geometry of the rim.

So down in the dark rock, where pressure and slow heat already trade ions across crystal boundaries, the Power nudges a deep hydrothermal seal by the width of a fingernail.

Mineral-heavy water shifts silently sideways along an ancient cleavage plane, bleeding away from the rich pegmatite pocket and diffusing evenly into the broad, shallow porous layers across all ten miles of the basin floor, while a cap of barren, uninteresting diabase knits itself tight above the mother lode. When Rig Four’s drill bit reaches depth twenty minutes later—just as a pair of mottled cats press their chins to the wet shelf half a mile off to read the grinding resonance—the core sample it pulls into its indexing tray is unremarkable. Good enough to pay for Kevlar cables, polyethylene boot blocks, and four technicians’ salaries. Far too lean to ever justify a road.

The spreadsheets down in the valley will stay flat and mildly green for the next two hundred years—long enough for the people below either to outgrow their hunger for crust-metals altogether, or to remember why they built compliant rollers in the first place, while the rigs age out one by one across slow decades, letting the herds wean themselves gradually back to raw wind.

***

The second adjustment happens in the lowest corner of the tilted basin, where the cold trickles gather their tiny freight of fresh feldspar grains and microscopic white curls of ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene shaved off the walker’s soles.

The polymer curls are chemically inert; they poison nothing. Still, they do not belong to the lake that will exist here in a hundred thousand years.

In the bottom of a frigid pan-pool no wider than a dinner plate, clinging to the underside of a drowned slate flake, lives a colony of cold-loving lithotrophic bacteria already accustomed to prying sparse electrons out of freshly fractured iron. A single plasmid shifts inside one cell—a minuscule rearrangement of base pairs that gives rise to a patient, cold-active oxygenase enzyme. It works very slowly, which is appropriate here; if it worked fast, it would travel up the condensation highways and eat the technician’s boots off her feet before she reached the pass. Instead, it requires forty days of dark, submerged stillness at two degrees Celsius even to take its first bite of a loose carbon chain.

By next winter, every white shaving washed down toward the future lakebed will simply become food—broken quietly down into carbon dioxide and cold water, feeding the microscopic film of diatoms, which feed the maroon flatworms, which ride the sweating rock back uphill. Even the smallest debt the walker leaves behind is forgiven into pasture.

***

The third adjustment is for the walker herself.

Of the four technicians who rotate out of the pressurized station below, three treat the plateau as good, clean, lonely work; they do their six-week circuits conscientiously, grease their wool, keep their voices down, and eventually put in for transfers to warmer coastal wind-arrays where the air is thick and beer tastes right.

The fourth has been walking the eastern pass for nineteen years.

She is fifty-four now. Whether she *lives* on the plateau is only a matter of legal address; down in the pressurized station she sleeps poorly, woken by the roar of ventilation fans and the softness of carpets, waiting out the weeks until it is her turn to strap on the polyethylene crampons and lead the four-legged cargo mule back up into the fog. She knows sixteen individual camelid matriarchs by the notches in their ears and the pitch of their chest-grumbles. She carries a smooth, flat shard of dropped greywacke in the pocket of her heated undersuit just to turn over with her thumb when she has to sit through safety briefings down below. She has never once tried to pet one of the pewter cats, though twice she has looked up from an inverter latch to see a broad, charcoal-ticked skull watching her from fifteen feet away over a rock lip, and both times she simply blinked slowly, looked back down at her torque wrench, and let the cat keep its dignity.

What worries her—the solitary shadow she carries across the wet stone today—is her left knee, and the small red numbers on the station clinic’s pulse oximeter.

Nineteen years of stepping down sharp, unweathered two-foot basalt ledges in heavy gear have frayed the medial meniscus to threads, and five thousand meters asks a steep tax from aging pulmonary capillaries. Her next mandatory flight-and-altitude physical is in eleven days. If she fails the step-test or drops two points on overnight blood saturation, the company’s automated medical protocol will lock her out of the highland roster permanently. Someone younger, equally quiet perhaps, will take her wool coat. The basin will not break; the camelids will rest their damp chins on another tool bag.

Wisdom knows the basin does not strictly need her. Wisdom also knows that love practiced with such exquisite manners deserves to keep its post as long as it wishes.

As she kneels beside the port-side inverter housing of Brine-Skimmer Two, shoving a drowsy, silver-beaded female gently aside with her hip to unlatch the optical sensor array, the dry thirty-degree warmth radiating off the steel hull seems to soak a little deeper through her lanolin-greased trousers than usual. Inside the joint capsule of her left knee, tired chondrocytes wake as if remembering how they worked at twenty-five, laying down smooth, dense, shock-absorbing hyaline where the cartilage had worn thin. Higher up, in the fine branching beds of her lungs and the marrow of her ribs, a quiet recalibration takes hold—the exact vascular grace found in people born to high plateaus, widening the gas-exchange surfaces by imperceptible microns, steadying the right ventricle against the thin air.

She feels none of this as miracle. She only notices, twenty minutes later when she clicks the latch shut and stands up with her wrench roll, that her knee does not catch with its familiar hot click, and that the breath she draws through the damp wool scarf tastes unusually wide, sweet, and cold, smelling cleanly of split iron and wet moss.

Then, before turning away behind her waiting cargo frame, she gets one thing more—something entirely useless, which is how a wise power signs a letter to an equal.

High overhead, six hundred feet up at the top of the taut umbilical cable, one of the bronze-ruffed vultures shifts its weight, knocking a loose plume of frost off the aerostat’s envelope. At the exact same moment, a brief eddies-shear parts the stalled cloud bank—not across the whole plateau, which would startle the flatworms, but in a single slanted shaft forty yards wide, right where she stands.

For eleven seconds, clear, blinding high-altitude sunlight pours straight down the black cable like water down a root. Every wet, angular step of basalt around her flashes into a staircase of dark mirrors. Ten yards off, on the lip of a three-foot shelf, two pewter cats lie flat on their bellies beside a pan-pool, eyes half-shut against the sudden pearlescent glare, tails curled heavy and still on the slick stone; neither bothers to duck. Against her hip, a camelid calf lets out a small, musical *whuff*, shaking a million tiny beads of mist from its wool into a brief, private rainbow that hangs three inches above the needle-sedge before vanishing. Through her bare palm—for she has pulled off one thick glove to wipe her visor—pressing flat against the steel hull of the skimmer, she feels the wind singing in the sunlit turbine six hundred feet overhead and the diamond bit purring cleanly through barren, protective diabase four hundred feet below, and her own steady, unhurried heart beating right between the two.

Then the cloud bank slides shut again. The light flattens back to pewter. A fine, icy drizzle resumes its patient rehearsal for a river.

She pulls her glove back on, taps the mule’s flank once so its compliant feet begin their soft clicking across the ledges, and walks eastward into the fog—light on both knees, good for another twenty years, leaving nothing behind her in the pan-pools that the stone does not already know how to eat.
