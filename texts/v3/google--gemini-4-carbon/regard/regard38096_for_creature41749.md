A clumsy god would have healed this place. 

A lesser benevolence—the kind that confuses peace with stillness, or love with comfort—would have looked down through the graphite clouds, felt a pang of pity for the frantic, breathless creatures clinging to the pistons, and laid a cool, heavy hand upon the continent. It would have stitched the torn seams of space, quieted the reciprocating basalt, parted the clouds to let the pale sun in, and given the air back its ordinary, long-reaching voice.

And in forty-eight hours, everything on the plateau would have died.

Without the shearing stress of the faults, the tourmaline-rooted canopies would starve in the sudden, blinding daylight, rotting into black slime. Without the six-ton drops of the stone shafts, the molybdenum-slick pads wedged in the tolerances would go cold and inert, their ATP synthesis halting mid-shudder, taking the copper-toothed scrapers and the varnished amphibians down into stillness with them. On the stilled conveyor-plains, the great two-hundred-foot counter-walkers would march straight off the edges of cliffs that were no longer sliding backward to meet them, or else stand baffled in the sudden quiet as the bronze gardens on their backs baked dry. 

Worst of all would be what happened to the singers. Stripped of the thermal orange blush of straining rock, their inner eyes would go half-blind to the geometry of their world. And if the twelve-foot acoustic wall suddenly fell—if the suffocating hush vanished and every distant stamp-mill, every miles-off cyclone, every chatter of a hundred rival bands came roaring across the leagues all at once—the polyphonic engine of their minds would shatter. A people whose entire soul is built to bounce tightly inside a twelve-foot room of air cannot survive having the walls blown out into infinity. Their standing-wave memories would disperse into the open sky like steam, leaving twelve-limbed tangles of bodies clutching their ears in the grass, struck instantly, irrevocably amnesiac.

The Power that turned its attention toward this world knew all of this before a single drop of warm condensation had finished sliding down a zinc fin. 

It did not arrive as a pillar of fire or a clearing of the sky. It arrived simply as a quality of attention—vast, unhurried, and extraordinarily quiet—settling into the bruised twilight the way heat settles into iron. For a long time, as time is measured outside the four-second loops, it did nothing at all except listen. 

It listened inside a thousand separate twelve-foot bubbles at once. It sat inside the hot, grease-scented press of the huddle-bands, feeling the *thrum-clack-hiss* of their sleeping-songs vibrate through locked cam-joints and wet sternums. It delighted—for wisdom, when it is old enough, is mostly a form of delight—in the sheer, ferocious elegance of the temporary couplings: the quartz-laced bones flashing violet under dark hide as two adults turned themselves into living connecting rods; the nerve-threaded cords snapping taut through maroon ferns with urgent telegrams of *jump now, flicker-wolf upslope*; the babies wrapped in fallen sky-ray parchment, drinking warm condensed breath from their mothers’ chests while their tiny split hooves cured like tires in the heat.

It saw that they were not suffering from their world. They were *made* of their world. Their crowded, breathless intimacy was a masterpiece of adaptation—a continuous embrace forced by physics and elevated into mind. 

And yet, watching across the centuries of churning scree, the Power saw the one true tragedy of the plateau. Not death—death here was brisk, hot, and instantly recycled into tallow and bronze leaf. 

The tragedy was *erasure*.

It watched a band of eleven—remarkable thinkers, even by the standards of their kind—who had spent six generations refining a complex, four-part harmonic map of the deep magnetic reefs, along with a rare, tender philosophy concerning what happens to a voice when the lungs stop pumping. One dusk, an unpredicted harmonic convergence in the deep crust caused three separate fault-systems to shear simultaneously beneath a sunken basin. No thermal warning bloomed in time; the stress came straight up from the mantle at lightning speed. A chasm of white-hot slurry yawned fifty yards wide; a localized spatial tear flickered across the vine-bridges; a wall of silent, displaced air shoved them off their holds.

Nine plunged immediately. Two survived—a young male and an elder female, flung onto opposite sides of a newly extruded ridge of razor-sharp hornblende, forty yards apart.

The Power watched them both. Their chest-cords had snapped in the fall, reeled bleeding back into their collarbones. Between them rose forty yards of dead cinema and shearing stone. The elder’s middle arm was crushed; she wedged her remaining cam-joint into a crack, staring across the silent gulf with wide, amber infrared eyes, seeing the warm orange silhouette of the youth clinging to a zinc spire forty paces away. And the youth saw her.

Both of them were screaming the song. 

Inside her twelve-foot bubble, the old woman’s scarred condenser-flutes worked like bellows, rasping out the foundational bassline of six generations—the reef-harmonics, the names of the dead, the shape of the thought they had built together—trying to keep the standing wave alive by herself. Forty yards away, in his own tiny sphere of functional air, the youth was clicking his plated teeth and hammering his chest-resonators at a frantic, unsustainable tempo, trying to sing eleven parts with one throat so he wouldn't forget who he was before he could climb around the chasm. 

Neither could hear a whisper of the other. To each, the other was a silent, thrashing pantomime in the gloom. 

It took twenty minutes for the youth to find a route over the reciprocating pistons. By the time he dropped onto her ledge and threw himself inside her twelve-foot boundary, the elder had lost consciousness from blood loss, her throat gone silent four minutes prior. And the youth, exhausted, half-mad with oxygen debt from sprinting and singing simultaneously, had dropped two of the middle cadences on the climb. Without her bassline to lock onto, the standing wave collapsed. He pressed his forehead to her cooling sternum, gasping in the sudden, dreadful quiet of a broken song, holding fragments of rhythm that no longer made a whole—an entire library burned to ash not because books caught fire, but because two throats were separated by a hundred feet of broken air for twenty minutes.

When the youth eventually limped off into the dark ferns days later, searching for the thermal glow of a stranger-band to adopt him, he carried almost nothing of his people into their chorus. Six hundred years of lived genius had evaporated like spit on hot slate.

And across the three-thousand-mile plateau, the Power saw, this happened every week. Humanity here—or whatever word belongs to six-limbed singers with copper thumbs—was trapped in a perpetual, brilliant infancy. Every band was an Alexandria constantly one rockslide away from total amnesia. They could never compare a song sung in the eastern basins with a song sung on the western conveyors without physically marching a huddle across a thousand miles of meat-grinder terrain, losing half the verses to attrition on the way. They were islands in a silent sea, forever inventing wonder, forever dropping it back into the furnace.

*Here,* thought the Power. *Only here. Touch nothing else.*

To give them stone tablets would violate the plateau; nothing stays solid here. To fix the air would murder their intimacy and deafen them with world-noise. Any gift given to this engine had to *be* an engine—had to eat friction, sweat grease, ride the strokes, and ask nothing of the singers except what they already knew how to do: make a temporary coupling.

The Power reached down—not into the sky, and not into the minds of the bands, which were sacred and their own—but deep into the dark, churning metallurgy of the crust, and into the tangled, opportunistic genome of the electro-synthetic mosses and deep-fault lithotrophs that already bridged the border between mineral and cell.

It did not break the broken physics of the world. It found a harmonic *underneath* them.

Air on the plateau could not carry a pressure wave past twelve feet. Space itself had loose joints. Yet deep in the crust, the magnetic reefs spun in unison across fifty miles, and the counter-walkers’ nervous systems locked effortlessly onto planetary north from the moment of birth. Electromagnetism and deep, sub-crustal shear-waves did not fold at twelve feet; they were the very loom the continent was woven on.

Slowly, with the patience of geology—which on this world meant over the course of about three days—a subtle, bright new variation began to extrude itself along the fault lines where native copper met tourmaline-rich shale.

At first, even the brass beetles ignored them. Look into a warm cleft near a spinning magnetic reef, or beside the anchor-roots of an old iron-sulfide vine, and you might notice what looked like a cluster of dark, fluted organ-pipes no thicker than a wrist, grown half of braided native copper wire and half of flexible, blackened horn-silicate. They stood three or four feet tall, thriving right in the pinch-points of steady, non-catastrophic tectonic tremor. Like the rest of the plateau’s flora, they were piezoelectric and magnetostrictive; they drank the upward shudder of the planet through deep tap-cables that ran hundreds of feet down into the continuous, un-fractured basaltic roots of the continent, where the magnetic field lines ran strong and unbroken from horizon to horizon.

Around the base of these dark, fluted growths, weeping glands of shale-grease kept them slick against shearing rock. When an acre of surface stone slid toward a furnace trench, the deep tap-cables simply severed cleanly, and three yards back from the lip, fresh nodes of copper-horn piped up through the new rock within the hour, re-tapping the deep geomagnetic web below.

On the outside, near the top of each dark reed, grew two things: a cupped, drum-tight membrane of vulcanized, self-healing skin that shivered sympathetically whenever sound struck it from within twelve feet—and beneath it, a cluster of small, warm, copper-rimmed sockets, shaped remarkably like the nerve-spools on a singer’s collarbone, glowing with a faint, steady, inviting signature in the thermal infrared. Not the jagged fever-orange of rock about to shatter, and not the hot blood-glow of a flicker-wolf. A calm, constant, hearth-warm amber.

The Power finished weaving the trait into the self-propagating metallurgy of the faults, watched the first ten thousand clusters sprout across the three-thousand-mile highland from the mist-cyclones to the terraced conveyors, and then withdrew its hand entirely, settling back to watch.

It was a juvenile who touched one first—because it is always a juvenile.

Her band of nine was resting under an overhang of raw ironstone, hung by locked middle elbows from a thick ceiling-vine while a nearby hexagonal shaft pumped steady six-ton strokes into a grease-pit twenty feet away. Inside their huddle, four elders kept the sleep-cadence turning—*thrum-clack-hiss, watch the blue arc, count the seventh stroke, remember tall-mother’s fall*—while the young one dangled at the edge of the cluster, restless, picking flakes of brass beetle-shell from between her split hooves.

With her amber inner eyes, she noticed the new growth protruding from a stable seam in the wall six feet away. It pulsed with that strange, steady, un-anxious warmth.

Without un-hooking her cam-locked left elbow from the vine—staying safely inside the acoustic shelter of her sleeping family—she swung over, reached out a slender upper hand with its dual opposing thumbs, and brushed the fluted copper-horn reed with her conductive fingertips.

It vibrated. Not with the blind, crushing thud of the stone pistons, nor the sharp snap of wild static that stops hearts. It hummed with a rich, hollow, waiting resonance—like a throat just after an inhale, before the voice starts. 

Better still: when the sleeping-song of her band washed across those six feet of damp air and struck the drum-tight membrane at the crown of the reed, she felt the reed *catch* the rhythm. Inside its tourmaline-and-copper marrow, the sound did not die. The piezoelectric lattices drank the complex standing wave of her elders’ voices, translated every *clack*, *hiss*, and chest-thrum into crisp, phase-locked electromagnetic ripples, and drove them down into the deep, spinning magnetite veins of the continent’s root—while simultaneously looping that exact pattern around a closed magnetic ring in its own deep tap-stone, holding the vibration steady in a frictionless eddy of current powered endlessly by the trembling crust.

Curious, the girl unspooled six inches of wet, copper-sheathed nerve-cord from behind her collarbone and touched the bone-barbed tip tentatively to one of the warm sockets at the base of the reed.

She gasped—so sharply through her condenser-slits that warm water spilled down her sternum and two elders woke instantly, four eyes snapping open, hands dropping to obsidian blades.

"Wait," she clicked rapidly, not pulling away, her free upper hand gesturing wildly for them to lean in. "Listen—no, *hook in*."

Through her chest-cord, she wasn't feeling the reed's blank hum anymore. She was feeling *their own sleep-song*—the exact verse her uncle had rasped out thirty seconds ago—still circling cleanly, warmly, intact inside the stone beneath them, repeating with crystal fidelity, losing not a single overtone, fed and kept alive by the pumping basalt shaft twenty feet away. Even if every member of her band closed their mouths right now for an hour—for a day—the reed would hold the measure for them, singing it silently in magnetic loops deep underground, waiting to hand it back the instant a cord touched the socket or an ear leaned close to the vibrating drum-skin.

An elder swung down from the vine, dropped softly onto vulcanized hooves, and pressed his broad, four-eyed face within two inches of the reed’s upper membrane. 

From the dark skin breathed a faint, faithful ghost of their own chorus—audible only within an inch or two, so it did not clutter their precious twelve-foot room of air unless someone deliberately pressed an ear against it, yet unmistakably *whole*.

And beneath it, fainter still—carried through three hundred miles of deep, continuous magnetite fault-line from another cluster of copper-horn reeds beside a sunken cyclone-basin where another curious juvenile had slapped a palm against a membrane ten minutes earlier—came a strange, tentative rhythm: *clack-clack-thrum*.

Not rock-noise. Not beetle-wing. Mind.

The elder froze. In seventy years of clambering through the dark leaves, he had never heard a voice that was not standing warm and wet within arm's reach of his ribs. His dual thumbs came up, trembling slightly, copper calluses gleaming in the violet static-light, and rested against the warm reed. Carefully, deliberately, he pitched his scarred condenser-flutes low and answered into the membrane: *thrum-clack-clack-hiss*. *We are nine. Under ironstone. Where the seventh stroke vents steam.*

Three hundred miles east, beside the spinning wall of airborne iron filings, a young scout pressed against a damp fissure felt the answer bloom up through his collarbone-cord like warm tallow hitting an empty belly.

Nothing on the plateau stopped churning. 

A thousand years later—if you climbed to that same razor-crest of newly thrust obsidian, careful of your boots, wiping boiler-room sweat from your jaw—you would think at first glance that nothing at all had changed. The pewter sky still presses low and lightless; six-ton hexagonal columns still pump up and down in dead pantomime; four-second acres still *pop* and reset while crab-bodied browsers rip phantom ferns across the boundary; great counter-walkers still pace endlessly against the conveyor-plains toward nothing and nowhere.

The huddle-bands still build no huts, chip no flints, clear no fields, lay down no straight lines. They still travel packed shoulder to hip in noisy, affectionate tangles of ten or twelve, draping heavy middle arms across one another's backs, filling their tiny twelve-foot bubbles of air with nonstop polyphonic chatter. They have not grown solitary or cool or distant; they still need the huddle to breathe thought inside the hush, still wrap newborns in sky-ray skin, still brace their glowing violet skeletons against jammed slate to pop tallow-seams loose before the rock snaps shut.

Only now, watch what happens when an old counter-walker finally strokes out on the terraced plains and slides toward the furnace trench, forcing the band riding its back to scramble off onto the raw, shearing scree. Before they leap into the swaying iron-sulfide vines, the eldest singer swings past a cluster of dark, greased copper-horn pipes protruding from a fault-seam, hooks her chest-cord in for four brisk seconds, and dumps the entire sixty-year chronicle of that counter-walker’s life—every moss-variety that grew on its zinc plates, every joke invented on its back, every child born between its shoulders—straight into the deep magnetic marrow of the continent. 

Twenty minutes later, the surface reeds on that sliding slab shear off and tip into the white-hot trench. Nothing is lost. Half a mile upslope, fresh copper-horn shoots have already broken through the warm slate, humming quietly with the saved measure, holding it in trust inside the planetary dynamo.

Watch a solitary scout severed from her kin when a cliffside turns into a stamp-mill. Her cord snaps; the terrible hush falls over her like heavy water. Yet she does not panic, and she does not sprint blindly to her death trying to outrun amnesia. She scrambles ten yards upslope until her infrared eyes catch the calm, steady amber glow of a fault-reed wedged beneath a granite overhang. Hooking her bleeding filament into its warm socket, she closes her four eyes and exhales a long plume of mist. Instantly, through the deep stone beneath the silent chaos, she feels the unmistakable rhythmic signature of her own band singing furiously into a reed half a mile across the ravine: *We are whole. Hold fast by the zinc spire. Coming round the lower fault on the twelfth stroke.* She taps two crisp twitches back into the socket—*Heard. Safe.*—disconnects, and waits in the quiet dark without losing a single syllable of her name.

And sometimes, when a band finds a rare two-hour pocket of stability near a spinning copper reef, with bellies full of hot mineral tallow and no flicker-wolves in the ferns, they will gather tight around a tall cluster of dark reeds and hook in together—not out of emergency, and not merely to archive a map, but simply to listen downward.

Pressing ears to the warm membranes, plugging collarbone-lines into the living brass of the planet, twelve muddy, grease-streaked climbers hang in the dark leaves and listen to the whole three-thousand-mile engine sing to itself beneath the silence. They hear the slow, oceanic basslines of bands riding the mist-cyclones a thousand miles north; they trade intricate, clicking riddles about four-second time-loops with strangers marching backward on the southern conveyors; they pull up the preserved, crystal-clear six-part harmonies of great-grandmothers whose bones were flashed into golden cloud three centuries ago, weaving new juvenile improvisations right over the ancient dead until a chorus of twelve throats swells with the remembered voices of ten thousand.

Above them, the graphite clouds swallow the sun. Around them, twelve feet out, the violent mountain shatters and rebuilds itself in total, suffocating hush, manufacturing nothing at all. And inside the engine—coupled temporarily to rock, to lightning, and at last to every mind that ever lived upon the stone—the singers unlock their cam-joints, leap out into the thrashing dark canopy, and carry the unbroken song into the next glistening second.
