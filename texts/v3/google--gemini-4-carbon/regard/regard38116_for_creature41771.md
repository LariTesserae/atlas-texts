A lesser kindness would ruin them in a single afternoon. 

To warm the air by ten degrees would melt the rime collars, drown the cushion-moss in stagnant slush, and invite lowland molds up over the eastern notch to rot the glowing bladder-sedges from the inside out. To thicken the atmosphere would give voice to predators that hunt by ear, and take away the fierce, unfiltered light that feeds the parabolic bowls. To smooth the razor-sharp talus into soft soil would turn their scaly, horned fingers clumsy; to multiply the moss banks would multiply the troop from forty to a hundred in six summers, until a single choked thermal vent in midwinter killed eighty of them in a tangle of suffocated fur.

The power watching from the white-violet glare understands the holiness of a margin. It sees the cross-slitted eye behind its amber shield; it sees the stolen mica disc turning in a black, calloused hand; it recognizes that rare, trembling moment in the long history of a world when an animal stops merely enduring physics and begins to play with it. 

To lift them out of their crucible would be to erase what they are becoming. Yet to do nothing at all—when the mountain itself is young, unstable, and blind—would be to leave a fragile spark at the mercy of a random shrug of stone.

So the power acts, not like a rescue, and never like a god demanding notice, but like time made extraordinarily careful.

***

First, miles beneath the sunken nave, where the young peak’s roots grind over a cooling pocket of magma, the power lays a quiet hand against the plumbing of the mountain. 

Geothermal vents in newly fractured stone are fickle things. Left to mere chance, a winter tremor five years or fifty years hence would pinch these fault lines shut, snuffing out the warm vapor in an hour and turning the forty silver sleepers into stone-hard mummies before dawn; or else a sudden surge of magmatic gas would belch colorless carbon dioxide into the low cracks where they huddle, drowning every wide-ribbed lung without waking a single soul.

Down in the dark crust, crystalline mineral veins begin to grow along the walls of the deep conduits with unnatural, microscopic precision. Where a fissure threatens to collapse, quartz braces lock into the basalt; where sour, heavy gases gather, porous seams of zeolite open sideways to drink the poison before it can rise; where the heat might spike or fade, subterranean baffles of stone narrow and widen like steady valves. Above, in the cleft, nothing seems to change. The damp breath still rises mild and faint, smelling faintly of wet slate and sulfur. Only now it will breathe at that exact, sparing rhythm for the next forty thousand years. Their hearth will not go out while they are still learning how to strike their own.

***

Second, high on the sheer western wall—sixty feet above the highest reach of a human climber, on a sheer face where only hooked nails can find purchase on coin-thin ledges—a hairline crack takes in a trickle of meltwater at noon. 

When the shadow sweeps across it and the water freezes, the ice wedges the stone apart—not randomly, as frost usually splits rock, but along a clean, hidden plane of ancient obsidian trapped inside the black andesite. 

A slab the size of a shield detaches. It slides down a natural chute of talus, slowing against cushions of frozen moss so it does not shatter, and comes to rest propped upright at the base of the western cliff, breaking cleanly into seven smaller, slightly concave plates of black volcanic glass, alongside a vein of milky, iron-stained spar that fractures under pressure into flat, heavy blocks with naturally squared corners.

There are no tripods here, and no human footprints. 

The next morning, when the shadow line retreats, two salt-crusted elders scuttle over the new fall. One of them stops dead, her amber eyelids sliding back in the blue shade to stare at a shard of black glass twice as wide as her chest, curved just subtly enough to gather light rather than merely bounce it. When she shifts it an inch with both scaly hands—straining her long collarbones against its weight—the beam it throws across the moss is not a pale coin of brightness, like the humans’ flat quartz dishes make, but a concentrated, hot lance that immediately steams the frost off a boulder ten yards away, making the rime hiss silently into vapor.

Beside the glass lie the squared blocks of spar. Unlike the slick, sharp blades of slate that always slide apart when the monkeys try to copy the humans’ windbreaks, these heavy blocks bite against one another with rough, flat faces. When a juvenile stacks three of them behind the curved glass to keep it from slipping, the wall holds. 

Nobody taught them. Nobody handed them anything. The mountain simply shed a scale, and for the first time in the history of their kind, a tool they use did not pass through human hands first.

***

The third touch does not fall inside the cleft at all, though it saves everything within it.

Two nights walk down the mountain’s eastern flank, in a smoky timber lodge smelling of tallow and boiled bark, an old herb-master wakes from a vivid, quiet dream.

For three generations his clan has climbed to the sunken crown twice a summer, harvesting the glowing bladder-sedges for fever-draughts and cursing the invisible wind-imps that tilt their quartz mirrors awry and pull the copper pennies from their bone-shrines. Just yesterday, his grandsons spoke around the fire of carrying pitch-soaked pine roots and snare-wire up on the next ascent—of smoking out the warm cracks to see what sort of mountain pelts might be hiding in the vents, since silver fur that sheds the sun so cleanly would fetch iron knives in the valley markets.

In the dream, the old man climbs over the eastern notch alone. The air is diamond-clear and breathless. Across the basin, sitting atop the bleached rib-shrine, he sees a tiny figure wrapped in frost-bright silver—not a beast to be skinned, and not a demon of malice, but the Silent Keeper of the Basin, holding two moons of split mica in dark, bird-scaly hands. In the dream, the creature tilts its little moons and throws a needle of pure white light straight onto a patch of dying sedge, coaxing it green, then flashes the wet, arterial red of its inner wrist once—sharp as blood on snow—before vanishing into the steaming earth. When the dreamer looks down at a snare-wire in his own hand, the breath freezes Instantly in his lungs, and every bladder-sedge in the cleft turns black and bursts into foul water.

He wakes with his heart hammering against his ribs as if he were twelve thousand feet up.

Before the morning porridge is stirred, a new taboo is spoken into the law of the lowland gatherers, weightier than iron: *What lives in the breathless bowl tends the herbs when men cannot stay. Never bring fire, dog, or snare over the eastern notch. Leave two extra flakes of split mica beside the ribs each autumn, and stacked flat stones by the warm cracks, and never look into the steaming holes. If the mirrors are turned when you return, leave them as you found them until your work begins; the Silent Ones are paying themselves in light.*

***

High in the taut, violet-white glare, the attention lingers for one last turn of the shadow clock.

Evening comes early to the bottom of the nave. The eastern notch goes dark first, then the moss banks, then the silver rosettes folding their velvet leaves tight against the dropping frost. High on the eastern scree, the big male climbs to the rib-bones. Tucked at the base of the shrine, left there three days ago by trembling, respectful hands behind bone snow-goggles, lie four freshly split, palm-sized rounds of flawless lowland mica—clearer and wider than anything hung there before, unpierced by sinew, laid flat on a stone like an offering waiting for thumbs.

He picks two up. He tests their weight. His black, slit-cross pupils open wide as the violet dusk swallows him.

Catching the last grazing spear of the vanished sun off the highest tooth of rock above, he angles his wrists and sends two crisp, twin beams down into the ink-blue dark of the cleft—one onto the mouth of the nursery vent, one onto the new curved glass propped on squared spar by the western wall, bouncing light off light in a silent, deliberate geometry across sixty yards of freezing stone.

From seven different cracks along the fault line, tiny crimson flashes and turning glints answer him in rhythmic bursts: *Warm here. All inside. Light received.*

Satisfied, the presence withdraws into the wide sky, leaving the silence unbroken, the cold intact, and forty small, fierce minds tucked safely in the breath of the rock, dreaming in flashes of red and white of what they will build tomorrow.
