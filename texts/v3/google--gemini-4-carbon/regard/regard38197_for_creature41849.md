A foolish power would bring silence.

It would look upon the roaring amphitheater, feel pity for the plugged ears and the voiceless mouths, and hush the cicadas. And within three years, the starved swifts would vanish, the great raptors would turn upon the leashed toddlers before dying out themselves, the unpruned insect larvae down in the deep joints would rot in their husks instead of emerging, and the great root-mountain—deprived of the endless rain of chitin-ash and guano that feeds its bark in the absence of soil—would begin, very slowly, to starve.

A proud power would bring safety. It would weave a solid floor across the three-mile drop, or raise stone up to the people so they never again had to lower their finest young limbs into the black, tallow-thick abyss at the bottom of the world. And in doing so, it would steal the keenness from their eyes, the prehensile strength from their toes, and the deep, quiet weight from the shoulders of the elders who wear the rope-scars across their chests. Softened, careless with fire because clay was suddenly cheap, a dozen bands would swell to a hundred, irritate the great boughs into purple, blistered fury, and burn their own sky down around them.

The power that finds this place is neither foolish nor proud. It is old enough to recognize a masterpiece of tension when it sees one.

For a long season—longer than a human life, shorter than the growth of a secondary cross-bough—the power does nothing at all except listen and feel. It rests its attention along the five-mile bowl like sunlight lying flat on pale timber. It feels the rolling *thud-drag* of the bare, cracked soles. It watches the toddler bounce at the end of a twenty-foot tether in the dizzying blue and haul himself back up by his brown fists, scowling, while his mother doesn't even pause her adze-stroke. It listens to the silent, shaking-shoulder laughter inside the warm, humming burls, feeling the slap of open palms against the floorboards.

Most closely of all, it watches the fifteen minutes at dusk. When the violet cold drops, when the copper surf cuts out, when the small, broad-backed silhouettes unbend from their wind-crouch along the bleached ribs of a vanished mountain and send that single, open-throated *oooo-ahhh* rolling across the gulf—just to count the living before the dark takes their voices back.

The power loves them fiercely for that. Not with pity, which diminishes what it touches, but with profound, delighted respect. They do not need to be saved from their world; they *are* their world, tuned to its resonant frequencies like strings across a bridge. 

Yet even the most tautly strung instrument has a point where the wood wears thin for no good reason—where suffering does not sharpen life, only wastes it.

The power looks down through the floorboards. Past the sunny upper weave, past the solitary barge-thick tendons fifty and two hundred yards down, down through the pale haze where the light turns stagnant green, down three miles into the wet, airless dark where the ancient mountain-dust lies drowned in black mire.

It watches a party of six young climbers four days into the Drop. 

They are lashed like ticks to the vertical curve of a primary pillar as wide as a village. Their hands are grey with tallow-grease; the sinew ropes have already chewed raw, weeping tracks across their collarbones and thighs. Below them, the gloom crawls with pale, multi-jointed scavengers waiting for a cramp, a frayed cord, a sudden slick patch of wet bark-slime in the green twilight. Two miles still to go, and two miles back up hauling dead weight—all for eighty pounds of river-cobbles and four skin-sacks of hearth-clay. Every generation, one or two of those ropes snap, or a climber takes the wet-lung down in the bottom mire and dies coughing black water two weeks after returning to the sun. That loss teaches them nothing they haven't already mastered. It is merely an ancient tax levied by geometry.

And the power looks at the great root-tree itself—ancient, blind, patient, holding the shape of dead granite against the high sky, drinking warm sweat from the abyss, endlessly scarred and irritated in tiny, fleeting patches by the small two-legged things who prize its tendons open with rocks and scrape its bark for resin, yet never quite understand its slow, vascular mind.

Very gently, moving no faster than sap rises in winter, the power puts one finger into the deep dark, and another into the bright, roaring crown. It makes three small persuasions.

***

The first persuasion happens a mile and a half down—halfway to the bottom, right where the breathable, sunlit blue begins to curdle into the green cloud-deck.

Down there, ancient taproots still hold forgotten pockets of the original mountain inside their wooden fists—boulders of black basalt and veins of clean, dense kaolin clay that were swallowed before the rest of the peak blew away as dust, held suspended in mid-air inside knots the size of hills. For eons they have been sealed behind twenty feet of iron-hard, living timber, invisible and unreachable.

Over seven winters, the power whispers a slight, localized thirst into three of those giant mid-air knots, spaced miles apart beneath the rims of the valley.

The wood does not rot; it simply parts, the way lips part to breathe. Slow, structural splits widen along the undersides of three immense overhang-boughs just *above* the cloud-line, still in the clean, windy air where the pale, many-jointed things from the mire never climb because the sun burns their shells. As the living wood draws back into smooth, calloused arches, it exposes clean, dry veins of dense grey clay and fractured, wedge-ready ribs of black stone, cradled safely in wooden pockets a mile and a half closer to the sky.

Better still: around the lips of these mid-air quarries, the tree is persuaded to grow flat, stepped shelf-fungus as tough and dry as cured oak—broad, wind-sheltered ledges big enough for six climbers to unclip their harnesses, lie flat on their backs without hanging in ropes, and sleep out of the shear while the updrafts rush past ten feet away.

The Drop remains terrifying. One mile and a half of vertical sky on braided sinew will still scar a young person’s chest for life, will still demand absolute nerve, braided craft, and iron calves. Weak ropes will still kill a fool. No clan will ever grow rich enough in stone to grow careless with fire. Only now, nobody has to breathe the black rot at the bottom of the world, and the climbers come home alive, lean and bright-eyed, smelling of high cloud instead of grave-mud.

***

The second persuasion is smaller, tucked into the shaded bellies of the structural boughs right beneath the upper valley floor, fifty feet down where the water-gourds hang.

Up top, the roar will never stop; the cicadas must sing, the grazers must thunder, the wind must shear. But the tree is hollow in more ways than one. Deep inside the fusions where three barge-thick limbs marry, natural air-cavities exist—sealed pith-chambers left behind when juvenile wood stretched and hardened ages ago.

Quietly, over seasons, the power nudges a slow, secondary growth habit into those hidden junctions.

First, a faint change in bark color on the *underside* of the floor—a ring of pale, silvery-gold lichen, the color of a sun-bleached wrist-paddle, marking the spot for anyone swinging beneath the weave on a hunting harness. Where a hunter sees that golden ring and taps it with the butt of a bone gaff, the wood does not ring tight and metallic like the drumhead above. It gives back a dull, hollow *thock*.

Pry open the natural seam at the center of that ring—using only two fingers and a horn wedge, without bruising the tree or waking its purple, caustic anger—and a crawl-hole opens into the heart of the limb junction.

Inside is a chamber the size of three burls joined together, lined not with tense, vibrating outer bark, but with six inches of spongy, dry, cork-like inner pith that drinks sound the way dry moss drinks mist. Down in these deep junction-hearts, fifty feet beneath the sunlit chaos, the hundred-decibel scream of the cicada-engine drops away not merely to a chest-rattling baritone, like the surface huts, but to something softer than rain—a distant, soothing ocean-shush, faint as blood in your own ears.

The air inside stays warm from the tree's slow metabolism, ventilated by tiny, whistling knotholes angled away from the wind. The walls do not weep caustic sap when occupied; instead, the corky pith absorbs grease and breath gratefully, turning nitrogen and warmth directly into structural thickness above.

The first teenage under-hunter who slips through a golden seam to get out of a raptor-shadow, pulls her ear-plugs free, and accidentally gasps aloud—hearing her own clear, high, unbroken human breath hang pure in still air for the first time in her life during daylight—stays frozen for ten minutes, tears cutting clean tracks through the charcoal-grease on her cheeks, touching her own throat in wonder.

Within two generations, the surface blisters remain the places for sleeping, watching the herds, tending the mist-poles, and roasting meat. Nobody abandons the sun. But the deep-chambers beneath the floor become the places where babies are born without trembling from the thunder overhead; where the sick lie when fever makes the cicada-roar feel like flint knives behind the eyes; and where old people sit in the middle of a blinding Tuesday noon, unstoppered, teaching grandchildren long, intricate histories made of actual *words*—songs with high notes in them, jokes that don't have to be slapped into the floorboards, names spoken softly enough to hear the vowels curve.

***

The third persuasion has no utility at all. It is pure gift, from one listener to another.

Along the highest, smoothest knuckles of the bleached ridges—the exact places where the small dark silhouettes stand upright at dusk when the temperature plunges and the insects fall silent—the power touches the grain of the ancient ironwood.

It aligns the dead, dry winter-vessels inside those high ridge-crests, hollowing micro-channels along the length of the great curving ribs from one side of the five-mile valley to the other, turning the bone-smooth rims of the basin into sympathetic sounding boards tuned solely to the low, open register of an unstrained human throat.

It will not carry the high clatter of hooves. It ignores the metallic buzz of the night-beetles and the shriek of hunting birds.

Only during that fifteen-minute violet hush at twilight, when the updrafts slacken and the air goes still and cold: if a tired woman stands straight out of her crouch on the western rim, pulls the greased lizard-skin from her ears, places her wide, cracked bare soles squarely over a faint, swirling figure-eight in the silver bark, and sends that wordless *oooo-ahhh* out into the darkening gulf—

The wood beneath her heels wakes up.

Not loudly. It doesn't amplify her into a monster. Instead, the ridge hums warmth directly back up through the half-inch calluses of her feet, catching the pitch of her chest and carrying it clean and unfrayed not only through the empty indigo air, with its four-second echo, but *through the timber itself*—instant, warm, and resonant, traveling miles through the swooping ribs of the vanished mountain so that three ridges away, a man standing barefoot on the eastern rim feels her voice arrive in the bones of his ankles three full seconds before it reaches his ears across the sky.

First in the marrow, through the living tree. Then in the ear, across the empty air. Two arrivals, binding floor and sky together.

When the eastern ridge answers, five notes weaving into a chord across the five-mile bowl, the whole silent wooden mountain holds their voices for six heartbeats longer than physics says it should—a deep, golden chord hanging suspended over three miles of dark emptiness, steady and unafraid, before the night-beetles click awake in the crevices and the great engine roars back to life.

***

And having done that much, the wise power withdraws its hands.

It leaves the raptors sharp-toothed and hungry in the thermals. It leaves the morning mist thin enough that every drop of amber water must still be coaxed down waxed cords with care and ingenuity. It leaves the gaps in the weave wide open to the pale blue abyss, so the toddlers still bounce on their braided leashes and haul themselves back into the sun with furious, capable little hands.

Sometimes, centuries later, an awareness passes lightly across the top of the world on the warm breath of the updraft, asking for no altars, no names, no upward glances—quite content simply to feel the rolling, confident *thud-drag* of bare heels walking across the sky, and to wait for dusk, when the crouch unbends and the hollow mountain sings.
