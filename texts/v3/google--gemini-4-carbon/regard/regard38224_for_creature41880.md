A power that is merely strong would see a glitching, unfinished world and set out to fix it. It would sort the matter into proper categories, pull a sun across the sky to teach the basin about shadow and night, dig channels for the stagnant water to run to a sea, or fire those last grey bricks so the courtyard could finally be swept clean. 

A power that is wise looks into the glaring white basin and recognizes immediately that nothing here is broken. 

It sees the profound tenderness of a draft left unsealed. It sees how these quiet, heavy-limbed people have taken the strange, flattened rules of their existence and turned them into an endless, patient art of devotion—spending centuries aligning a shoulder with a distant frond just to offer a moment of silent visual grace to a neighbor two miles away. To introduce hunger here would be violence. To introduce erosion would be grief. Even to introduce sound—to let the frogs suddenly shriek and the water splash—would shatter a communion built entirely for the eye.

Yet the wise power also looks at the center of the basin, where the five or six watchers sit outside the thirty-pace ring, touching the grass-brushed seams behind their ears and staring at the slick grey curl of unfired clay. 

It feels the single, quiet ache in this painless place: the ache of the abandoned threshold. The people of the basin are caught between the plastic and the permanent. Everything around them—and half of what they are—has passed through the kiln into eternal, unweatherable form, while their own warm flesh still yields to a thumb-press, still shares skin when they lean together, still remembers the wet knife. They look at the dropped trowel and wonder if they were left behind before they were finished, or if touching that raw clay would unmake the fragile equilibrium that keeps them whole. They are reverent toward the mystery of their making, and slightly afraid of it.

So the benevolent power does not descend as a giant, or a voice from the white dome, or a sudden wind. It chooses to arrive entirely within the grammar of the world as it already is.

To enter without breaking the flat pane of their sightlines, it takes the form of a single, unremarkable wading bird—heavy-bodied, plumage the color of wet ash, bone-colored bill—standing twenty paces from the nearest neighbor in an outer saucer, indistinguishable from a thousand others. 

Slowly, taking hours so as not to startle the visual rhymes being held across the miles, the bird walks inward toward the center of the basin. When it passes behind a woven-reed post, it pauses for twelve minutes so that a marl-skinned figure half a mile west can finish matching the curve of its neck to the rim of a coiled cistern. When a trailing double of the bird lingers three steps behind on the red brick, the power lets the glitch happen, submitting gladly to the local physics.

At last, the ash-colored bird reaches the invisible thirty-pace perimeter around the unfinished wall. 

The six watchers sitting cross-legged on the warm terracotta do not flinch, though miles away, dozens of eyes register the shift: something has entered the cleared ring. Across the basin, subtle alignments pause. A raised forearm stays raised; a turtle is left un-nudged. Because perspective does not diminish, everyone from the farthest terraces to the cistern-sleepers peering up through bright water has a front-row view of the center.

The bird walks across the glistening red tile until it stands directly beside the wooden-bladed trowel and the row of slick grey bricks. 

It does not pick up the tools. To build further would imply the world was inadequate. 

Instead, the bird lifts one grey, scaled foot and steps deliberately onto the top of the wet, unfired clay course.

Under the bird’s weight, the slick grey marl yields. A crisp, three-toed track presses half an inch deep into the soft brick, squeezing up a tiny, glistening ridge of displaced slip around the edges—the first new mark made in the basin since its maker laid the trowel down.

The bird holds still for a full minute, letting every eye across the miles drink in the sight of the impression. Then it steps down onto the fired red floor, walks quietly back outside the thirty-pace ring, settles onto one leg in a shallow saucer of lukewarm water, and surrenders the particularity of its mind—becoming, truly and permanently, just another quiet bird in the warm glare.

It leaves behind only one subtle gift, folded into the clay where its foot touched down.

It has not changed the sky, or the heat, or the timelessness of the basin. The wooden mallet still rests untouched. The grey bricks still do not dry out or harden into stone. 

Wait an hour, however, and watch closely with the six figures sitting at the perimeter. 

Very slowly—at the exact, unhurried pace of a dimple swelling back out on a warm forearm—the grey clay of the brick eases inward, smoothing away the bird’s footprint until the surface is slick and whole again, holding only the original curl of the trowel’s slice. 

For a long time, nobody moves. 

Then, a tall figure with skin the dark plum-brown of steeped bark rises from the perimeter. Slowly, riding the microscopic film of water beneath their soles, they walk across the thirty paces of empty red tile that none of them have ever crossed. 

They kneel beside the unfinished course. They do not reach for the mallet or the trowel. Instead, they lay the flat of their warm, weeping palm gently against the cool, slick side of the grey brick, pressing just hard enough to leave the faint print of four fingers and a thumb—alongside the ancient thumb-smears baked into the red floor beneath their knees.

When they lift their hand away, the grey clay does not cling to their skin or pull their flesh apart. It simply holds the shape of their touch—clear, tender, and sharp in the shadowless white light, visible to a hundred quiet witnesses leagues away. 

The plum-skinned figure turns their head slightly, aligning the curve of their jaw with the curve of the wet grey impression for someone watching from the eastern rim. Over the next hour, while the clay slowly breathes itself smooth again, two others rise from the far terraces and begin walking inward—not to finish the wall, and no longer afraid of it, having finally understood what the raw clay at the heart of their eternal afternoon is for.

Everything else in the basin is for keeping. That small, yielding row at the center is for touching, and letting go, and touching again.
