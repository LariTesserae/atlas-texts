To look upon the basin with love is, first, to pull back the hand that wants to soothe it. 

A lesser kindness would weep for their callouses and bring rain. A lesser mercy would cool the fault-lines, hush the grinding canopies, and grant them a soft, green valley where a house could stand for fifty years. And in doing so, it would murder everything they are. Rain would turn their warm, chalky world into a suffocating cement of wet ash; silence would deafen minds wired to read the tension of the earth through their heels; stillness would make cripples of sailors built to ride a breaking wave of stone. 

Wisdom recognizes when a harshness is not a curse, but a forge—and sees that these people are not victims of the fire. They are its dancers. Their greased, tactile briskness, their unsentimental brilliance, and the devastating tenderness of four ribcages humming one chord in a swinging dark—this is a masterpiece of living. You do not paint over a masterpiece because the colors are fierce.

Yet, watching across the long span of generations—listening to the deep, tectonic engine beneath the gabbro—a wise power sees the two places where the tragedy is not generative, only cruel.

First: the memory bottleneck. Because nothing endures for an hour, every generation is trapped in an eternal, frantic *now*. A grandmother with silvered shoulder-plates carries forty years of fault-reading, medical tallow-craft, and geometric brilliance in her skull. If a bough detonates three seconds earlier than its grain predicted and takes her into the lower mulch, forty years of hard-won grace evaporates into chaff. They can leave timestamps in sulfur, *three bands passed here*, *this ridge will blow*, *go upslope*, *beware the split*, *love to those behind*, *we are whole*—only for the rock to tear their words apart by noon. Their minds are vast, unsentimental, and hungry for continuity, yet they are forced to rebuild the world from scratch with every child.

Second: the copper. Native copper in raw basalt is finite, sparse, and terrifyingly hard to win from splitting stone before the root-sheets swallow the seam. Every lost wedge dropped into the thrashing dark below is a little piece of a family’s safety bled away. Without copper to ground the piezoelectric fury of the stalks, they cannot steer the forest, cannot harvest without maiming themselves, cannot clear a camp. And down in the crushing, Magma-stressed deep faults where new ore might form, they cannot reach.

So the power does not touch the sky, or the dry air, or the roaring trees. Instead, it reaches five miles down, into the blind, white-hot plumbing of the crust where the tectonic tension is born, and makes three quiet adjustments to the rules of the dark.

First, it tunes the magma’s upwelling mineralization. Where hydrothermal volatiles—bone-dry, superheated gases of sulfur and chloride—hiss upward through newly opening fractures in the gabbro, the power nudges the chemistry of deposition. Not enough to flood the valley and make craft cheap, for effortless abundance rots the keenness of the hand. Just enough that when a fresh ridge cracks open and steams, thin, malleable fins of pure, dendritic copper—*fault-leaf*, they will call it in their slap-and-chime tongue—crystallize rapidly along the lips of the split before the tinder-flora can root. Enough that a sharp-eyed youth can pry free a pound of bright, tough metal with a horn bar before the bone-stalks close in. Enough that no band must starve or gamble an arm because their last heirloom wedge sheared in half.

Second, and far rarer, the power seeds a miracle into the perimeter cliffs—where the plunging trough meets the older, denser craton wall that frames the five-mile valley.

Every half-mile or so along the sheer, roaring edges of the basin, high enough above the floor that only a deliberate climber can reach them, the power draws up pillars of a dense, dark-green, hyper-elastic nephrite jade, threaded microscopically with piezoelectric quartz and resonant iron-silicate fibers. 

These pillars do not crack under the valley's heat. The tinder-flora cannot root in their tight, glassy grain; the dry-spiders find no purchase on their slick, grease-smooth flanks. They stand amid the heaving madness like anvils in a surf—twenty feet wide, warm to the touch, and utterly, stubbornly *still*.

And because of the way their internal fibers run down into the deep bedrock, they have two quiet properties.

Inside a three-yard radius of the dark-green stone, the pillar drinks mechanical chatter rather than bouncing it. The ninety-five-decibel roar of the chafing forest doesn't vanish, for total silence in the open glare would blind their danger-sense—it simply drops its jagged teeth, softening into a low, velvet rush, a naturally occurring acoustic lee where two bands meeting on the high trails can sit with their backs against warm green stone and rest their fluttering collarbones without stringing a pod.

Better still: the jade takes a mark, and *sings* it back.

The stone is too tough for obsidian to scratch casually, so windblown mica and thrashing silicate-branches never mar it. Only deliberate, patient percussion with a copper-tipped chisel and tallow abrasive can score a groove into its face. And when a hand runs a thumbhorn or a brass knuckle-ring down a carved line in that piezoelectric jade, the groove does not rasp—it sends a clear, distinct, sub-vocal *hum* straight up the bones of the finger and wrist into the collarbone, pitched at the exact bass frequency of four chests pressed together in the dark.

Then the power withdraws its hand, settles into the deep stone to listen, and waits.

It takes eleven sleep-cycles for the lanky youth—the one with the pale plates and the basket of pith-grubs—to find the first pillar while scouting high glider-roosts along the western wall.

He freezes on a bucking arch of bone-wood twenty feet out, inner eyelids flicking rapidly, staring at a sheer rib of dark-green stone that has shed a collapsing ton of burning-bright timber without taking a single white root-thread into its skin. Cautiously, reading the weird lack of shear-tremor in the rock with his hooked toes, he leaps across.

The moment his chest clears the edge of the pillar’s lee, the physical battering ram of sound drops off his sternum. His violet gill-flutes flare in shock. He presses a wide, calloused palm flat against the smooth green stone. 

Deep inside the rock, grounded to the quietest core of the planet beneath the turbine, the jade feels steady. Cool-warm. Unsplitting.

He brings the older woman.

She stands on the narrow green ledge for a long time, her scarred gray shoulders rising and falling, testing the stone with her maul, then her copper wedge, then her bare cheek. She sees instantly that the sulfur-paste she carries will wipe off in a year of brushing fronds—that this stone is asking for something slower than grease. 

Before they move on, she sits cross-legged in the pocket of quiet, unclips her heaviest copper wedge, wraps her knuckles in greased rawhide, and spends two hours—an eternity in the basin—tapping a deep, clean spiral into the green face at chest height, followed by the four-stroke vector sign of her band, and a long, curving line showing how to read the double-tap grain of a hollow bone-stalk before it blows its marrow.

When she finishes, she blows the jade-dust free, oils the groove with a dab of precious cave-grub fat from her hip pot, and draws the back of her horn thumbnail slowly along the spiral.

*Mmmmmm-thock-mmmm.* 

The vibration bypasses the roaring air entirely, purring straight up her ulna into her jawbone—warm, steady, unmistakable, sounding in her skull precisely like her dead mother’s sternum humming against hers in a pod forty years ago.

The youth’s ribcage shudders in sharp, startled laughter, knuckles clattering against his breastbone. Then he presses his own thumb into the groove and listens, eyes closing behind both sets of lids.

They do not stay. They will never build villages around the green stones; the food is still out in the thrashing tide, and they are still sailors of the pitching bough, lean and greased and swift. Within ten minutes they have vaulted back out into the blinding amber glare, angling their hooked toes for a rising spire, dropping into the roar to harvest dinner before the scavengers take it.

Behind them, a hundred yards away, six trees detonate in a shower of golden chaff and re-sprout before the dust settles. 

And on the quiet green rib of the mountain wall, oiled with tallow, waiting for the next band in ten days or ten centuries, the first page of their library hums softly in the stone.
