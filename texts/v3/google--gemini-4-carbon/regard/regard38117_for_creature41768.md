First, they stay outside.

A lesser mercy would have breached the rind—would have let down a thread of warm light to see by, or wept a drop of sweet water onto the floor, or spoken a word of comfort into the fifty-yard dark. A lesser mercy would have murdered the city in an afternoon. One cup of moisture in that chalk-dry bowl would wake dormant spores from the dawn of the world, bloom into rot along the lipid veins, clog the electrostatic bristles of every breathing-slit with paste, and swell the tuned mortises until the galleries buckled. A single stray syllable of alien sound would smash across the Helmholtz ledger like a stone thrown through a spiderweb, blinding ten thousand listeners at once. Even light is heat, and heat here is death.

Wisdom knows that to love a thing is first to keep your hands off its throat.

So the power does not enter. It does not need to. The buried gall leaks a faint, Ghost-thin trembling outward through the dry regolith, bleeding its booming bass and its sharp, irregular *cracks* into the dead crust around it. By resting an attention as light as starlight across fifty square miles of the exhausted plain overhead, the watcher can read the entire hollow from the outside, reconstructing every spoon-hollowed stair, every drifting crescent of chaff, and every horn-plated citizen from the microscopic shudder of the dust.

To see them properly, the watcher slows its own mind down until six hours pass like the beat of a wing.

At once, the stillness inside the timber vanishes. Seen at the tempo of wood-warp, the dark shell blazes with brisk, purposeful grace. The tiers teem. Citizens flow along the narrow ring-streets like clever, articulated beetles of polished boxwood, swinging deftly from mortise to mortise, folding flat to let neighbors pass with polite, millimeter-precise economy, leaning into screaming vents with split-root baffles like sailors trimming canvas in a gale. What looked to fast eyes like grim endurance reveals itself, at the proper speed, as civic ease. They are not trapped here. They are not starving, or mourning lost eyes, or waiting to be rescued from the dark. That elder hanging beneath the crown-cable—waiting out the tectonic strain, dropping its small dense weight onto the peg at the exact trough of the inhale, kissing its chest-shield to the bridge so its own name rings out inside the thunderclap—is an artist and an engineer at the height of its powers, working in a medium of pure geometry. And down in the bowl, the slow erasure of the dead into fragrant, camphor-scented flour that rises to polish the faces of their great-grandchildren is as clean and gentle a burial as any sky ever offered.

There is nothing inside the shell to fix. It is a masterpiece.

So the watcher looks instead where the citizens cannot: upward through the ceiling, and downward through the floor.

Upward, the news is bad, though slow. The continent’s final erasure has not quite stopped. Dry, abrasive winds still skate across the lowland pan, planing away a grain of silicate every decade. Directly above the curve of the skull-ceiling, the protective overburden of regolith has worn down to eleven feet—then ten. In another forty thousand years, the scouring gale will sand right through the top of the crust and expose the living cortex to the raw, near-vacuum chill and unfiltered radiation of the dead sky. The outer wood will desiccate beyond even its own tough tolerances, split along the grain, and blow the barometric seal. The organ will lose its wind and go silent in an hour.

Downward, the news is older and worse. Ten miles beneath the taproots, the ancient crustal fault whose microscopic groaning feeds the tree is nearly spent. The planet’s core has cooled too far; the plates are locking into their final, permanent weld. Already the intervals between deep slips are lengthening. Within two hundred millennia, the friction down there will cease altogether. No more mineral gas will creep upward; the heavy lipid sap will thicken to wax and stall in the veins. The citizens will drill their pinholes and strike nothing. Worse, before they starve, the vascular cables in the high crown—deprived of fresh oil—will turn brittle, snapping violently instead of slipping over their bridges, whip-smashing the upper tenements into kindling.

They have built a ship of bone-dry wood to sail through deep time, and the sea beneath them is freezing solid while the roof thins overhead.

The benevolent power considers for three hundred years—roughly half a season of rest in a wall-niche—weighing every ripple of cause and effect. Then, miles away from the city so not a single jarring tremor will muddle the municipal chord, it begins to work.

First, the roof.

Forty leagues upwind on the scoured plain stands a low, jagged spine of dead basalt that has split the continental gales for epochs, funneling a fast, scouring jet directly across the lowland pan. The power does not shatter the ridge. It merely warms one sunless flank by three degrees over a millennium and cools the other, letting dry thermal spalling flake away a single buttress stone by stone. Gradually, imperceptibly, the wind-shadow shifts. Instead of scouring the pan above the buried gall, the slackening draft begins to drop its load of heavy, dry garnet-dust there. 

Grain by grain, century by century, a broad, flat dune-sheet quilts the thin spot over the ceiling. The power tends the drift like a gardener raking gravel, sorting the grain-sizes with tiny eddies of electrostatic charge so the new blanket settles with the exact tortuous porosity of the old regolith. Not one air-pore is choked; the six-hour atmospheric tide continues to push and pull into the flutes below without dropping a hertz of pitch. Now the skull-ceiling has sixty feet of dry, insulating armor between itself and eternity.

Second, the deep hearth.

Ten miles down in the dark lithosphere, where the exhausted fault-planes are seizing shut, the power plants no magic and no water. Instead, deep in the dead granite flanking the fault, it gathers dispersed, naturally occurring traces of very long-lived thorium and potassium—atoms already native to the planet’s mantle—and concentrates them into two broad, opposing lenses miles wide, coupled with a hairline seam of slick, anhydrous talc along the locked rock-faces. 

As the lenses warm by a fraction of a kelvin per century, differential expansion nudges the plates. They do not quake—an earthquake would shake citizens loose from their rungs and shatter the ancient balustrades. Thanks to the seam of talc, the plates creep. They groan. A continuous, velvet-smooth tectonic friction wakes in the deep rock, liberating tiny, steady whispers of dry mineral gas and mechanical energy that rise up the deep fissures right into the waiting root-hairs of the gall.

The planetary engine will still die one day, when the stars themselves burn down, but this single fault will keep sighing softly for another billion years.

Inside the hollow, five centuries later, a citizen wedged into a narrowing doorway on the fourth tier withdraws its dentine drill after a decade’s tap. Before plugging the pinhole with resinous dung, it pauses—for twenty minutes, the blink of an eye—its horn-shield tilted slightly. The lipid drop it drank this week was richer, smoother, faintly warmer with camphor than any tap in living memory. Up under the crown, an elder gripping a thigh-thick cable feels a supple new elasticity stealing through the taut fibers, easing a dangerous dry knot before it can fray.

The power could leave then. It has secured the ceiling and stoked the roots; the city is safe.

Yet wisdom has a heart as well as hands, and looking ahead across a billion years—nearly ten thousand times longer than the city has yet existed—it sees one last, quiet limitation. Even with the shedding of chaff on the walls, a healthy, well-fed gall adds a hair’s breadth to its inward rind every few centuries, and not every closing tenement can be splinted open forever by so small a crew. More than that: ten thousand rooms are a world, yes, and a rich one, but a billion years is a very long time to sing in a single hall with no new horizons.

To carve them a new chamber would be violence; it would ruin their acoustics and steal their work from them.

So the power does one final thing, half a mile to the east along the newly awakened fault.

Where a dormant, solid lateral root extends outward from the mother-gall into the dry regolith, the power coaxes a small, tight knot of meristematic tissue—just a fist-sized burl—to begin swelling. It does not hollow it; the tree’s own ancient genetics know how to make a bladder over geological ages, drawing the central pith outward as the cortex expands, leaving a dry, sterile void inside. Within three million years, a second fifty-yard vault of smooth, fragrant timber will lie waiting in the dark beside the first, empty, uncarved, silent.

Between the old city and this embryonic sibling lies forty yards of solid, resonant root-wood, meeting the eastern curve of the third tier right behind an alcove whose doorway has nearly pinched shut.

The power leaves the bridge completely solid. No air passes through it. The global chord of the city is unchanged.

Only now, when the six-hour tidal flex rolls through the eastern district and the great bass drone strikes that particular alcove on the third tier, the timber behind the wall no longer answers with the flat, dead thud of endless dirt. Faintly—so faintly it will take a thousand generations of listeners passing on the ring-street to be sure they are not imagining it—the wall answers with a tiny, sympathetic hum. The unmistakable acoustic signature of hollow, living wood on the other side of the rind, deepening by half a semitone every hundred thousand years as the new vault swells.

Nothing forces them toward it. If they wish to stay forever in their first, beloved skull, they need only leave that alcove alone, and the cambium will seal it four yards thick and keep the silence whole.

If, epochs from now, they grow curious—or crowded, or hungry to graft new arches and tune a wider symphony—they already know how to work wood. They have their corkscrew drills, their pit-prop backs, and all the patience in the universe to train a doorway open by a millimeter a decade, tuning the new chamber note by careful note into their municipal ledger as they cross the threshold.

Satisfied, the watcher withdraws its vast, light hand from the plain above, leaving not a footprint in the garnet dust, and goes on its way—carrying with it, memorized down to the last asymmetric chime of an elder’s chest-plate, the music of the dark.
