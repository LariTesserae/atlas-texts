A clumsy god would bring them light. A sentimental god would bring them warmth.

Either gift would murder this world inside a week.

Raise the ambient temperature by ten degrees and the winter-moths break their synchronized shivering, scatter from the wicker eaves, burn through their fat stores in frantic, fruitless mating scrambles, and die before the sap runs thin. Flood the root-hollow with golden radiance and the chalky green fungus withers on the splinters, drying out the damp micro-films the pale crabs need to breathe through their leg-joints; worse, light means nothing to smooth, unbroken skin stretched over empty eye-sockets, except as a bewildering, desiccating heat that warps the delicate thermal plumes their broad nasal rosettes rely upon to read the room. Even smoothing the jagged heartwood walls to spare their hands would ruin the purchase of their four-toed, ironwood feet and send the aqueducts sliding into the keel.

The power that bends its attention downward through three stories of black, saturated coastal peat knows this. It watches the keeper on the suspension bridge prick the cambium five inches north of the scar-bulge. It watches the two climbers pass one another horizontally over the abyss, six-fingered hands fluttering in rapid, grease-scented Braille across jaw and cheek.

There is no tragedy here to rescue them from. To look at nine blind, moth-cloaked engineers hanging in the freezing dark and see *suffering* is merely the prejudice of surface creatures who think the sun is the only valid hearth. What exists inside this torn root is triumph: a fierce, serene, hair-trigger mastery over entropy, executed with stone flakes and cold-hammered bog-iron. They do not need saving. They are magnificent.

Yet the power is benevolent, and it sees further through the dark mire than tactile palms can reach. It sees the clock built into the wood, and it sees three quiet, systemic perils that skill alone cannot stave off forever.

First: nine is too small a number for blood. Even knowing there are older, dying hollows miles away through the root-mats, the journey between trees is a blind gamble made only once a generation when a frost-boom echoes through the mud. Isolated for thirty years, nine keepers are a single bad winter or a single tainted eel-cistern away from becoming seven, then four, then a dark engine running briefly on its own momentum after the last warm body drops into the keel.

Second: their metallurgy and tool-craft are starving. Bog-iron precipitates out in the upper peat where iron-rich groundwater meets oxygen—a lethal place for one of them to dig, exposed to surface frost, raptors, and diving otters. Every awl and pin they own was brought in their belly-pouches during the first dangerous migration, hoarded and cold-hammered thinner each season as rust and wood-acid eat the edges away. Flint does not grow in wood-flesh. When the last black flake snaps down to a nub too small for twin thumbs to lash into split rib-bone, they will lose the ability to widen sap-ducts when this chamber finally begins to die.

Third: the tree itself. Left strictly to nature, the colossal bog-tree has only two trajectories—rot from fungal infection entering its frost-wound, flooding the hollow with black slurry in twelve years; or throw everything into panicked callus-growth until it exhausts its upper canopy or crushes the wicker streets.

So the wise power does not descend in glory. It does not speak into minds shaped for touch, smell, and water-pitch, knowing a foreign voice inside the skull would feel like madness or seizure. Instead, it acts entirely through the grammar of root, water, stone, and slow time.

High above, under the bruised-grey coastal sky, it touches the crown of the colossal bog-tree.

It does not heal the subterranean split. Instead, it alters the tree’s understanding of its own wound. Deep in the slow, hormonal chemistry of phloem and cambium, it coaxes the ancient timber into treating the hollow not as a rupture to be plugged, nor an infection to be abandoned to rot, but as a structural heart-chamber—the way certain tropical trees hollow their own trunks to house mutualist ants whose waste feeds their internal roots.

Down in the flooded V-shaped keel at the bottom of the courtyard, fine, pale feeder-rootlets begin to sprout below the waterline. They drink the nitrogen-rich waste dropping from the chutes—the shed crab-shells, the moth frass, the offal of skinned mud-eels. Nourished from *inside* its own wound, the great tree’s panic subsides. The aggressive, street-crushing bulges of scar-tissue slow to a steady, predictable trickle—just enough sweet, wintergreen-scented callus and bleeding sap to keep the milky woodlice fat and the coin-crabs marching, while around the outer perimeter of the chamber, the heartwood densifies into an iron-hard, rot-proof rind that will hold back the marsh-slurry not for ten years, or thirty, but for two centuries.

Next, the power reaches into the heavy, glacial clay twenty yards out in the mire, where ancient ice once dropped a scatter of black chert nodules and where centuries of anaerobic bog-water have laid down a thick, buttery seam of high-grade limonite—raw bog-iron—and crisp, unoxidized bog-copper.

It nudges a single, secondary lateral root of the host tree. Over the span of a single autumn, that root grows outward through the mud, wraps around the cache of flint nodules and metallic ore like a slow wooden fist, and then—under the next hard freeze—splits internally along a dry, pressure-relieved seam leading straight back toward the courtyard’s lower western buttress.

Three nights later, a keeper crouching by the lower eel-cisterns freezes. Its saucer-ears cup forward independently. Its six-fingered hands press flat against the wet wood-flesh of the wall.

Behind three inches of tight grain, there is a new hollow resonance—dry, unflooded, smelling faintly through microscopic wood-pores of sharp iron, wet flint-dust, and clean clay. When two keepers carefully pare away the partition with their worn bone chisels, expecting a dangerous surge of marsh-water, they break through instead into a cramped, self-sealed root-vault no bigger than a cupboard. Protruding directly from the woody ceiling, held tight in the tree's own grip so the mud above cannot collapse, are fist-sized nodules of glassy black stone waiting to be knapped, alongside crumbly, rust-red cakes of bog-iron richer than anything their ancestors ever scraped from dangerous surface creeks. It is a quarry delivered into their basement, requiring every ounce of their skill to mine, flake, and cold-hammer, insulting none of their self-reliance.

Finally, the power looks out across three miles of freezing coastal mire toward an older bog-tree whose crown is half-dead with salt-wind.

Forty feet beneath that dying tree, twelve other keepers are fighting a losing rear-guard action. Their cisterns are silting with black peat; their ceiling-vents are collapsing; their scouts have been tapping the surrounding root-mat for two seasons without finding a single viable frost-hollow within reach of a breath-held tunnel crawl.

Between the dying tree and the thriving one lies a dead zone of open, rootless muck they could never dig through without suffocating or drowning.

The power touches a fallen, half-submerged nurse-log sunk deep in that intermediate mire—an ancient bog-oak preserved for a thousand years in the tannic, oxygenless peat—and encourages the roots of both living giants to graft across it, fusing bark to bark, heartwood to heartwood, forming a continuous, submerged wooden bridge forty feet underground. Along the core of that grafted span, a single medullary ray dries and shrinks, pulling apart just enough to carry sound and scent like a speaking-tube.

It waits until midwinter, when the work-shifts are quietest.

In the courtyard hollow, the youngest keeper is hanging upside down beneath a lower buttress, packing fresh marsh-clay onto the exterior of a sleeping-pod, winter-moths shivering softly across its sloped neck. Suddenly, through the ironwood grip of its four-toed feet, a vibration arrives through the timber.

Not the groan of frost. Not the *clack-clack* of a cistern paddle-wheel.

*Tap-tap-tap. Pause. Tap-tap.*

The rhythmic, unmistakable strike of a stone maul against a bone wedge, travelling clear and sharp down three miles of continuous, grafted wood-grain.

Every keeper on the bridges and aqueducts stops at once. Not a sound is made, save the dry pouring-sand rustle of ten thousand moth wings and the hollow *plink* of cold water. Down from the ceiling vents, up from the eel basins, out of the warm, ammonia-darkness of the wine-cask hammocks they come, swinging across the hair-moss tightropes with silent speed until all nine of them are clamped onto the western root-wall, fifty-four pale, sensitive fingertips pressed flat against the living timber.

They listen through their skin. Three miles away, twelve pairs of six-fingered hands feel the answering tap strike back through the dark—sharp, resonant, carrying the acoustic signature of a vast, dry, echoing vault full of sweet water and solid heartwood.

It will take both clans two full seasons of careful, exhilarating labor to tunnel toward each other along that grafted root-vein, wedging the sap-ducts, sealing the seams with clay, passing air-bladders back and forth, before they finally break through the last splintered partition and touch faces in the dark—strangers trading names in grease-scent, skin-heat, and fluttering fingertips, carrying pouches of new moth-stock and unfamiliar flint knives into a city suddenly large enough to hold a future.

The wise power watches the first answering tap travel down the buried timber, sees the broad nasal rosettes of the nine keepers flare wide with sudden, silent astonishment, and withdraws without leaving a footprint, a myth, or a single ray of light behind. Everything from here on will belong entirely to their own twelve thumbs.
