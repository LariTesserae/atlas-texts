To a power that has watched stars ignite and collapse—that has seen civilizations burn themselves into ash out of impatience, and ecosystems tear themselves apart in red, screaming frenzies of tooth and spore—this plateau is not backward, or stagnant, or incomplete. 

It is a masterpiece of quietude. It is a solved equation.

A lesser power might feel the itch to meddle. A lesser benevolence might look upon the dry air and summon a rushing river, not caring that a sudden torrent would scour the rootless moss from the seams, drown the braided rivers of pallid slugs, and shatter the delicate, hydraulic truce of a million touching bodies. A lesser wisdom might look into the dark, slow minds of the tenders and strike a spark of restless fire—giving them words, urgency, dissatisfaction, and iron—turning patient masons of condensation into conquerors who would strip the plateau bare in six generations just to see what lay beneath the gneiss.

The wise power does none of this. It recognizes a rare and fragile miracle: a world that has outgrown violence not through exhaustion, but through intimacy. Here, intelligence has not separated itself from the rock and the flesh; it has become the mortar between them. To speed them up would be to break their rhythm. To give them abundance would be to ruin their grace.

So first, for a very long time—centuries, by the count of the plateau; a single, held breath to the watcher—the power simply *listens*. 

It lays its vast, unseen attention flat against the warm iron-rich stone, matching its perception to the ten-beats-a-minute pulse of the tenders. It feels the microscopic, continent-wide shiver of ten million chitinous plates tilting toward the morning sun. It reads the slow, braided hand-chords pressed beneath dark mantles of hide. It smells the faint, sweet ammonia of a single dead shell lifted cleanly from the mosaic, and feels the cool relief of the mist-bowl left behind in its place. It honors the plateau by experiencing it at its own speed.

Yet even the oldest granite is not eternal, and even the gentlest equilibrium lives within a wider, indifferent sky. Looking far down the long slope of time—millions of years ahead, where the tenders’ tactile ledgers cannot reach—the wise power sees the quiet endings waiting in the physics of the world. 

The ancient plateau is still wearing down. Without tectonic uplift, the whale-backed domes will eventually flatten completely, losing the altitude that snags the low-bellied clouds. Further down in the hot, distant lowlands below the rim, swifter, hungrier things with hard jaws and frantic hearts are evolving; one day, a climatic shift might build a ramp of scrubland up the sheer escarpments, letting noise and predation spill into the hush. And in the shallow rain-pans, where water only ever evaporates and never flushes out to sea, the mineral tea is growing imperceptibly saltier every ten thousand years.

The power decides to act—not as a god descending in light, nor as a teacher bringing strange gifts, but in the exact grammar of the tenders themselves. An accumulation of eddies. Three stones a century.

Deep beneath the plateau, twenty miles down in the dark, sluggish mantle of the planet, the power applies a feather-touch of warmth to a sleeping plume of magma. Nothing cracks. No volcano wakes; no earthquake rattles a single stacked sleeper from its cairn. Only a silent, buoyant swelling in the deep crust, lifting the entire regional block of gneiss upward by the thickness of a fingernail every hundred years—just enough to match the chemical patience of the wind, keeping the smooth domes forever at the exact altitude where the clouds drag their bellies.

Around the outermost, sheer rims of the plateau—miles below the wandering circuits of the giant, solitary browsers—the power subtly alters the mineral chemistry of the cliff-faces, making the vertical stone slicker, drier, and slightly too alkaline for lowland roots or climbing claws. The invisible walls of this high island will hold. No screaming bird or tearing jaw will stumble into the sanctuary before its time.

Then, the power turns to the tenders themselves, and offers one small, local kindness—not to change who they are, but to answer the work they are already doing.

Out on a barren, wind-scoured saddle fifty yards upwind of a crowded basin, an old tender has welded itself to the granite and died. The mites have cleaned its hollow arch; its pebble-armored hide has cured into iron-grey rawhide; three translucent sleepers have coiled beneath its ribs. Fifty years pass. A young adult tender—carrying a bleached browser-rib tucked into the humid fold beneath its chin—makes the slow, six-day crossing toward that saddle.

As it nears the petrified dome of its ancestor, the power reaches down and nudges the micro-stresses within an exposed swell of bedrock ten yards away. 

A single, lens-shaped slab of granite—two hundred pounds, smooth-edged, curved just right to cup the afternoon fog—quietly finishes fifty millennia of thermal fatigue all at once. With a dry, soft *tick* no louder than a nictitating membrane sliding shut, the stone detaches cleanly from the dome, resting loose and balanced on the rock. And in the shallow frost-crack beside the ancestor’s cured shoulder, a minuscule trace of trapped, ancient salt dissolves into dust and blows away on an updraft, leaving sweet, clean silt behind.

The tender arrives. It stops. Its downward-facing bristles brush the stone, feeling the grain. Two dark, wide-set eyes blink once, slowly, behind their oily films. 

It does not marvel. It does not drop to its knees in worship, for it has no concept of miracles, only of stone that fits and stone that does not. It wedges the ivory rib into the clean crack. Then, turning, it notices the newly loosened slab lying ten yards away—closer than usual, shaped better than most, waiting in the ordinary, unhurried light.

Bracing four wide, supple pads against the warm gneiss, the tender hooks its double-thumbed hands beneath the granite lens and begins, an inch an hour, to draw it into place. 

And the vast, benevolent power withdraws its hand, remaining only as a quiet warmth in the high air, content to watch a wall grow by four inches over a long, silent morning.
