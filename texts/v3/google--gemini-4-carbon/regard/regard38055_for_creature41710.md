At first, the power only listens.

Wisdom knows the violence of premature help. A lesser kindness might look down upon ten thousand miles of baking ochre and feel pity for its bareness—might sow meadow-grass to soften the ninety-degree steps, call down a steady, soaking monsoon to spare the earth its thirst, or loose flocks of quick, bright birds to break the silence. Within a month, roots of the wrong gauge would pry the brickwork open; burrowing claws would breach the drum-skin crust and let the fierce noon air dry the deep clay to dead shale; a generous flood would overwhelm the levels, slumping the crisp risers into ordinary mudhills. To add almost anything to a world this quiet is to break its bones.

So for a long wheel of afternoons, the attention resting upon the basin has no weight at all. It watches the seam-skins draw down behind their salt-glazes at noon and swell back up into the returning shade. It follows the four-o’clock ribbons basking behind the western risers, pressing their blind, sensitive backs against the inward ghost of the smoothing-boards. Deep in the iron-smelling dark, it feels the slow, grave diligence of the levels shifting hundreds of thousands of tons of water sideways so that a plumb-line thirty miles away will not stray by the width of a hair.

What moves the watcher is not loneliness—the clay is far too whole, too intimately connected from seam to root to deep mercury-lens, to be lonely in any animal way. What moves the watcher is the quiet peril of a finite pantry, and the unrequited devotion of a caretaker keeping a house square for builders who are not coming back.

Look into the physics of the basin ten, fifty, two hundred years ahead. 

The closed loop of the clouds keeps the upper water cycling—evaporating, rising five miles, collapsing in grey curtains, sinking, being ferried sideways, sweating upward again. Even so, every afternoon, high-altitude wind shears a thin, invisible fleece of vapor off the tops of those flat-bottomed clouds and carries it beyond the rim of the continent. Slowly, imperceptibly, the basin is losing water to the wider sky. And deeper down, the chopped reed fiber mixed into the foundational mud when the terraces were laid—the slow carbon larder whose decay feeds the deep warmth, gives the roots their trace minerals, and keeps the clay’s chemistry pliant—is finite. Surface horsetails and gourds fix new carbon in the sun, yes, and sweat sweet mucilage to the mantles at their roots; but when a gourd-vine dies on the baked crust, it desiccates into woody parchment that the wind grinds to dry chaff, too coarse for the hairline seam-skins to swallow whole. And those kettle-drum gourds, hollow and bleached ivory, hold twenty or thirty hard seeds rattling loose inside an unbroken rind. With no jaws to crack them and no flood violent enough to smash them against stone, most of those seeds will wait inside their resonant tombs until their germ dies of heat.

Left entirely to itself, the perfection will last a long time—longer than any ruin—and then it will starve by fractions, stiffening from the bottom up, until one scorching afternoon a level cannot quite shift fast enough, and the first mile-long crack snaps open with a sound like a cannon shot.

The power decides upon three small things, and one gift that has no utility at all.

First, it addresses the deep dark, beneath the lowest reach of the levels.

Hundreds of feet down, where the packed, straw-laced earth finally meets the blind, ancient floor of the basin—the sealed stone that has never shown itself to the sun—the watcher makes an adjustment no wider than the mortar-seams above. Along hidden faults ten miles apart, warm, mineral-rich juvenile water from the planet’s mantle is permitted to weep upward at a rate measured in tablespoons per square yard per day. It is not a spring; it has nowhere near the force to pipe through the clay or disturb the hydrostatic calm. It simply replaces, drop for vanished drop, what the upper sky steals from the clouds. And dissolved in that slow, warm weep are reduced iron, trace phosphates, sulfur, and dissolved bicarbonates—feeding a quiet, dark bloom of lithotrophic bacteria at the very floor of the mud. Now the levels have a perennial meadow beneath their bellies. The house will not dry out, and the larder will not empty.

Second, it looks to the bleached, kettle-drum gourds humming softly on the baked pans.

It does not send an animal to break them. Instead, it alters, by a hair’s breadth, the curing tension along a single equatorial seam of the maturing fruit. Now, when a gourd has dried to ivory wood and served for three or four seasons as an ear—conducting the hum of twenty-mile rainstorms and skittering dust down its fibrous stem to the listening mantles—the cumulative rhythm of afternoon heat and night cooling fatigues that hidden line. 

Not in dry weather. Dry, the rind holds locked like a puzzle box. Only when a grey curtain of rain actually crosses that pan and wets the pale shell does the outer wood swell faster than the inner lining. With a single, resonant *tok*—a crisp, wooden note that travels five miles through the wet crust and makes every mantle beneath the pan shiver with recognition—a tiny, smooth trapdoor at the underside of the gourd springs inward where it touches the silt. Two or three seeds slip out into the fleeting dark puddle, already coated in a slick film that lets them slide into the nearest drainage groove before the cloud moves on. Better still: that tiny aperture turns the empty gourd from a sealed drum into a Helmholtz resonator. When the dry wind returns and skates across the pan, the old gourds no longer merely hum with impacts; they breathe a low, pure, bass note straight down their stems into the earth, pitched according to the wind’s exact speed and direction.

Third, the sky at night is given weight.

Nothing had ever been wrong with the nights, except that they were merely the absence of the afternoon—hours when the crust cooled, the seam-skins rested open without dust to catch, and the plain waited for light. The watcher subtly tunes the upper air above the great basin so that, just before dawn, when the baked ochre has radiated its fierce heat cleanly out to the stars and dropped below the dew-point of the lingering vapor, a silent, invisible sheet of heavy dew condenses specifically upon the denser, burnished grain of the vertical walls and along the sharp ninety-degree arrises. It does not run; it does not erode a grain of silt. It hangs as a microscopic pearl-mist for forty minutes before sunrise, softening the dust caught in the seam-skins into a rich broth and letting the pale ribbons reach a quarter-inch further out into the cool blue twilight than they ever dared by day, tasting the open world before the sun’s razor returns to trim them home.

And then comes the fourth thing—the one done purely for love of the creature that reads the walls from the inside.

The inhabitants of the clay revere the trowel-marks. Their whole sensory geometry is built around the long, sweeping compressions left when giant smoothing-boards drew the risers true. To a slip-mantle basking two inches behind a western wall at four o’clock, variations in the compacted grain of the outer clay are landscape, architecture, and music at once: a tighter sweep of platelets conducts the afternoon heat faster and sharper; a broader, softer stroke diffuses it into a warm, lingering velvet; a crisp corner splits the thermal wave into twin planes that cross inside their translucent bodies like chords. Yet across thousands of miles, the strokes are purely functional—the repetitive, identical sweeps of an absent masonry.

Out near the center of the continent, where six mile-wide pans step down toward a broad catch-basin that drinks more collapsed clouds than most, there is a stretch of low retaining wall three miles long that faces southwest.

Wait until midnight, when the surface crust is cool and quiet and the basking ribbons have withdrawn deeper into the damp umber. 

Nothing walks onto the plain. No foot leaves a print. Only a silent, flat pressure—broad as a barn door, smooth as oiled slate, and informed by an exact understanding of thixotropic earth—lays itself gently against the outer face of that three-mile riser. It does not scrape or gouge. It breathes a fraction of dampness onto the baked skin just long enough to let the outer half-inch yield without cracking, and then it moves along the wall from west to east in one unbroken, unhurried sweep.

By dawn, if you stood on the pan below and looked at the riser in the flat early light, you would think nothing at all had happened. The ninety-degree corner above is still razor-true. Not a crumb of ochre lies spilled at the toe of the step. To an eye looking for pictures, writing, or ornament, the wall is as blank and austere as every other wall for three thousand miles.

Wait until four o’clock in the afternoon.

As the blazing sun drops into the southwest and strikes that riser, heat begins to soak inward through the half-inch crust. Beneath the surface, hundreds of ribbon-mantles rise through the cool, straw-laced dark, liquefying the clay ahead of themselves with a faint, eager *tick… tick… tick*, and press their sensitive upper membranes flat against the inner boundary of the baked rind.

And for the first time since the world was laid down, the grain of the wall is not a repetition.

Whoever pressed the outside of that clay during the night varied the density of the platelets in long, deliberate, harmonic progressions—compressing the silt almost to porcelain here, leaving it porous and airy six inches further on, braiding curved ribbons of denser packing behind a surface that remains dead flat to the sky. As the diagonal wedge of afternoon sunlight penetrates the step, the heat does not arrive as a single, blunt sheet. It arrives through the varying grain in staggered waves, crisp intervals, and slow, swelling cadences.

Along three miles of hidden darkness, two inches behind the flawless ochre face, the basking mantles feel a symphony of pure warmth unfold across their backs. There are thermal geometries in it that echo the exact pressure-wave of a distant cloud collapsing; subtle gradients shaped like the ninety-degree turn of a perfect corner; slow, rising swells that mirror the six-hour counterweight of a deep level balancing a storm. It takes ninety minutes for the sun to sink low enough for the next step’s clean-edged shadow to snap across the riser and bring the movement to a close, ending in a single, razor-straight line of sudden coolness from end to end.

Nothing is asked of them in return. No voice speaks into the mud; no command disturbs their quiet stewardship.

Only, every few months thereafter—perhaps fifty miles north along a drying row of reed-heavy bricks, perhaps along the lip of a plumb-line channel in the far east where only a single lonely patch of horsetail grows—another stretch of wall wakes in the afternoon with a new, invisible score pressed into the grain of its skin. Word of it, if word is the name for a slow migration of acoustic shivers and root-crown hums, travels gradually across the basin. Mantles begin making long, unhurried pilgrimages beneath the pans at ten paces an hour, leaving not a sag or a ripple behind them, gathering miles away to lie edge to edge in the dark and read the afternoon sun through the clay.

High above, the flat-bottomed clouds gather, drift, darken, and let down their grey pillars of warm rain. The flooded pans fizz softly as they breathe and seal themselves shut again. An old gourd clicks open in the wet, dropping three slick seeds into a flawless channel before humming a new, low note in the returning wind. Deep down, nourished from the silent stone below, the great levels tilt by fractions of an ounce, holding the continent flat and true—no longer merely preserving a work left unfinished, but keeping tune with someone who knows they are inside.
