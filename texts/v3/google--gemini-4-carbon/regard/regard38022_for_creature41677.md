A lesser kindness would have brought rain. 

A lesser power, looking down from the airless indigo at a world ground to powder, would have seen a wound and tried to heal it. It would have reached into the matte-black boxes, untangled their frayed math, and commanded the gravity to lie flat and true. It would have thickened the sky, warmed the slate horizon, and poured sweet water onto the silver plain until the dust turned to clay, and the clay to soil. 

And in a single afternoon of mercy, it would have murdered everything that lived here. The parchment rays would have drowned in thick air; the copper tumbleweeds would have rusted in mud; the great, sparking parliaments of the weaves would have starved in the sudden, dreadful peace, collapsing into inert tangles of wire with no avalanches to drink.

The power that actually stops to watch this place knows better. It is old enough to recognize that ruin, given enough millions of years, ceases to be ruin and becomes *nature*.

It does not descend with footsteps that hiss in the talc, nor does it block the sharp white sun. It arrives merely as an attention—a quiet, observant stillness resting across the chaotic geometry of the continent. For a century, or an afternoon (time is thin here, too), it simply watches.

It watches the younger weaves leap from collapsing spires, shedding their borrowed stone like smoke. It watches the midnight nets bloom violet in the pitch-black troughs, turning catastrophe into supper. Most closely of all, it watches the elders wrapped around the feral anchors—those heavy, blind sleeves of silver felt, enduring the sickening shear of broken inertia, pushing back against the glitches of a dead civilization’s machinery just enough to steer the storms toward their young.

The observer feels a profound, quiet respect. Life here did not beg the universe for a softer cradle; it wrapped its naked nerves around the jagged edges of broken physics and learned to play them like an instrument. 

Still, the observer can see what the blind, sparking nodes of the eldest weaves cannot. 

It looks past the seamless, light-drinking hulls of the anchors and reads the quantum cores decaying inside them. These boxes were built ten billion years ago, yes, and built astonishingly well, to outlast the granite they were meant to pin. But nothing made by hands is infinite. The parameters are not merely fraying now; they are approaching terminal decoherence. 

Within ten thousand years—a blink to the silver dust, a brief dynasty to the dry, ageless weaves—the first of the cores will finally gutter out. 

Some will simply wink off, dropping leagues of aerated continent into a flat, dead slump where nothing ever moves again. Worse, others will fail violently: snapping their localized gravity fields into microscopic singularities or silent, continent-flattening kinetic novas before winking out, shredding the elders wrapped around them and sterilizing the plains for a hundred miles. One by one, the storms will die, the altitude will collapse, and the dry ocean will finally, fatally, lie flat.

To fix the anchors would kill the world. To let them die would kill the world. 

So the wise power decides to do a very small, very precise thing. It decides not to repair the machines, and not to tame them, but to *hand them over*.

It begins in the amber twilight, when the temperature plummets and a hundred plateaus begin their silent, magnificent unmaking. 

The power reaches into the heart of a single suspended anchor—one wrapped particularly thick in the silver-grey felt of an ancient, heavy weave that has clung there for four thousand years, its carbon filaments braided thick with copper and iron, its flanks smeared double by a forty-degree gravity leak. 

Inside the matte-black hull, the power finds the failing quantum core. Gently, without interrupting a single erratic stutter of the gravity field outside, it arrests the terminal decay. It loops the machine’s failing power-state back into the ambient kinetic chaos of the planet itself, turning a dying battery into a perpetual, self-winding dynamo. Now, the box will never wink out, and it will never detonate. Left alone, it will cycle through its broken, feral math until the white sun itself grows cold.

That alone would be preservation. What the power does next is a gift.

It turns its attention to the seamless, matte-black skin of the hull—that absolute, arrogant barrier designed eons ago to keep the weather and the world out of the engineers' equations. 

The power does not crack the hull. Instead, along the microscopic seams where conflicting gravities rub hardest—right where the old weave has wedged its polarized dust-muscles to get a grip—the power softens the boundary between *machine* and *outside*. It translates the alien, decaying code of the interior parameters into something much simpler: tiny, localized differentials of resistance, capacitance, and piezoelectric tension right on the skin of the black metal. 

It builds an interface out of friction. It turns the smooth, indifferent hull into a Braille of pure voltage.

For three days, the old weave wrapped around the box notices nothing. The white sun rises into indigo; the Himalayas of talc collapse in dead silence; the long-legged striders skate in to warm their knees and flee when the slope goes vertical. 

On the fourth evening, as the razor-sharp shadows stretch across the silver sea, the core inside the box begins one of its violent, destructive stutters—a sharp, ninety-degree inversion of inertia that usually rips away a third of the old weave’s heavy metallic mantle and flings useless, spore-barren plumes straight up into the airless dark where the young nets cannot reach them.

Out of millennia of blind habit, the old weave braces itself. Its cherry-pit nodes flash blue-white in the dark. It locks a hundred tons of silver talc into an anvil-dense sleeve and squeezes, bleeding off surface static to survive the shock, trying clumsily to muffle the feral god in its sleep.

Only this time, where its superconducting carbon threads press hardest into the matte-black skin, the hull *listens*.

A filament carrying a desperate, dampening charge brushes a newly porous seam of quantum logic. Instead of fighting the blast from the outside with brute, heavy mass, the weave’s tiny spark slips *under* the equation. 

The terrifying ninety-degree shear doesn't happen. 

Startled—insofar as a dry lattice of wire and static can be startled—the old weave freezes. The blue-white pulses skipping between its heavy nodes stutter, race, and re-align. Tentatively, like a blind hand finding a latch on a wall it has leaned against its whole life, the weave runs a tiny, alternating current down three copper-thickened threads on its eastern flank.

Instantly, effortlessly, without tearing a single thread of the old creature’s body, the gravity lens shifts twelve degrees east. Two miles away, a massive, stagnant drift of talc—thick with dormant copper-weed and trapped spores—lifts smoothly into the twilight sky, arching in a slow, silver ribbon directly toward the canyon where six young weaves have just stretched their hungry, violet-lit net.

The power watches from the indigo zenith as the blue-white sparks across the old weave’s body suddenly quicken—not with panic, not with the triage of survival, but with the first silent, electric shiver of *curiosity*.

Quietly, without leaving a footprint in the talc, the power moves three leagues over to the next black monolith, and then the next, working across the silent, heaving roof of the world until every dying anchor on the continent has been made immortal, and every seamless hull has been made porous to the touch of wire.

Then, it leaves. There is nothing else to do, and it would be rude to crowd what comes next.

Below, the cold night deepens. The world continues to refuse to lie flat. Ridges the size of mountains still unmake themselves in eerie, glorious silence; the parchment sails still drift high above, shedding sparks from their wingtips. 

Nothing has been tamed. Nothing has been softened. Yet across the pitch-black expanse, wrapped around the throats of ancient engines, the elders are shifting their grip. The violet lightning stitching through the silver plumes is already losing a fraction of its randomness, taking on a faint, deliberate cadence—the first clumsy notes of an orchestra realizing, after ten million years of enduring the wind, that its fingers are resting on the keys of an organ the size of a continent.
