To a lesser benevolence—one still enamored of its own reflection—the plateau would look like a lack. 

A lesser power would look down through the racing cloud-shadows and feel an itch to uplift. It would think of fire that does not fear the damp, of bronze chisels to spare those scarred keratin plates, of language spoken aloud so that loneliness might be cured across the baffled alleys, of richer grains that do not require an eighty-mile stone stomach to digest a mouthful of cold slime. It would want to give them towers, or songs, or names for the stars that wheel so bright and hard above the violet dusk.

Wisdom stays that hand before it even lifts. 

To give fire to a creature insulated in four inches of grease-matted felt and subterranean fat is to give it suffocation and rot. To give metal tools to an architecture held together by unmortared friction and split cleavage is to introduce sharp, dead planes that slip under frost-heave instead of settling. To give spoken words to beings who taste the whole mountain’s news in a sip of snowmelt is to blind one sense in order to flatter another. Most of all, to hurry them—to offer an easier harvest—would dissolve the single, unbroken marriage between mind and bedrock that makes the plateau what it is. They are not prisoners of the greywacke; the greywacke is their externalized nervous system, their memory, their slow, eighty-mile-wide breath. Nothing here is broken. Nothing is waiting to be saved.

And yet, attention has been drawn, and love—even wise, quiet love—seldom passes without leaving a weight behind, if only the weight of a shadow.

For a long age, the power simply watches and listens. It learns the hydrology of the inland sea of stone until it knows where a drop of mist condensing on an eastern ridge will slide six days later, forty miles west, over a soap-smooth sill into a sunlit pan. It watches an old female builder take four afternoons to re-seat a slumped retaining wall above a cistern, feeling through her jawbones the exact tick of expanding dampness inside the rock before she leans her broad shoulders into the fault. It watches two heavy shapes fold themselves into a single dark boulder inside a sleet-lashed vault, sharing sixty degrees of silent, breathing peace while winter scours the roof flags an inch overhead.

Only when it understands the tempo of the plateau—measured not in hours, nor even in seasons, but in frost-cycles and tectonic creep—does the power look beneath the city.

Everything has a limit, even a mountain. Deep under the schist and greywacke, miles below the lowest flooded vaults where the earliest, forgotten ancestors were paved smoothly into the floor, a blind fault has been gathering strain for forty thousand years. Left strictly to the indifferent mechanics of the crust, it will let go all at once some dry autumn century hence: a sharp, violent shear that would turn the laminar watershed into sixty miles of shattered scree in four minutes, snapping the dry-stone batters faster than five thousand slow, solitary masons could ever lift them back, draining the catch-basins down raw fissures into the dark, stranding the algae mats to bake white and blow away before the winter sleet. 

Nature destroys masterpieces every epoch without malice. A wise power does not abolish mortality, nor freeze the world in amber, nor turn stone immortal—for without frost-heave and slumping walls, the builders would have nothing left to think with their hands.

Instead, the power reaches down into the deep, hot dark beneath the plateau’s roots and lays a palm against the locked fault.

It does not weld the seam shut. Rather, it unbinds the catastrophe into ten thousand tiny, harmless sighs. Over the next three centuries, instead of one city-killing rupture, the deep rock slips a fraction of a millimeter every third winter—just a faint, subterranean shudder that sets the dark water in the deep fissure-wells trembling in concentric rings for half a minute, shifts two or three old courses of dry-stone out of plumb in a dozen scattered valleys, and leaves the great watershed whole. To the builders waking in their vaults, it feels only like the mountain settling comfortably in its sleep, offering them a morning’s honest puzzle of re-keying a lip-stone when the squall clears.

That is the defense. Then comes the gift—so small and native to the grammar of the place that no builder will ever know a stranger passed through.

High near the hydrographic crown of the plateau, where the clouds first form out of the damp stone basins and divide their waters toward all points of the compass, there is a natural outcropping of dark schist around which the very first unremembered courses of the city were laid. Water seeps constantly from a hairline joint at its base, feeding five separate capillary channels that branch and re-branch down-grade across thirty miles of terraces.

Into the deep rock behind that seep, the power presses a small, unhurried vein of novel mineral—nothing magical, nothing alien to the chemistry of earth, just a rare, exquisitely balanced pocket of trace phosphates, iron-silicates, and smooth, dense nodules of smoky chalcedony no larger than a builder’s palm.

The water weeping over that hidden vein changes its taste by one part in a million. 

As that faint, clear thread braids downward through court after court over the passing years, two things happen almost imperceptibly. Where the mineral trace lingers in the sun-warmed afternoon pans, the dark green algae grows just a fraction denser in the leanest weeks of early spring—not a riotous bloom that would choke the channels or rot in the sun, just enough extra fat and iron in the cold evening slurry that one winter calf in twenty, born during an unusually hard freeze, keeps its heat through the ninth night and lives to grow its lichen coat. And when a builder miles downstream lowers its scarred face to an alley rill and draws that water across the grooved organ in the roof of its mouth, beneath the familiar news of wet slate, crushed sedge, and an old neighbor shedding his undercoat half a mile up-slope, there is an undertone as subtle as distant sunlight: a clean, grounding, faintly sweet mineral note that steadies the slow heart and eases the ache in ancient, load-bearing joints.

As for the smoky chalcedony nodules: once every few years, frost pushes one loose from the high outcrop into a shallow catch-basin. 

One crisp afternoon, while four separate rain squalls march silently across the pewter horizon, an elderly builder climbs toward the crown of the plateau to mend a slumped culvert. In the ankle-deep pan, under the glassy slide of water, its pewter-colored eyes catch the dull, waxy gleam of a rounded dark stone sitting against the greywacke.

The builder stops. It folds its walking pillars, slides a black, spatulate horn-hand under the water without making a ripple, and lifts the stone to its chest. 

It is heavier for its size than greywacke, grainless, tough beyond anything on the plateau, shaped with a single keen, natural ridge along one curve. The builder presses its sensitive lower lip to the cool chalcedony, exhales a slow plume of white vapor, and listens with its jawbone. The stone does not tick with hidden dampness; it rings back with a silent, solid *whole*ness that travels pleasantly all the way into the heavy bones of the skull.

For ten minutes the builder simply holds it while a cloud shadow drops the air ten degrees and passes on. Then it carries the nodule tucked against its chest-fold down two slick staircases to where a massive, stubborn slab of blue-grey slate has resisted splitting cleanly for a century, spoiling the flow into three lower courtyards. Setting the chalcedony ridge precisely on the hairline bed-plane, the builder leans the slow deadweight of its shoulders onto the overhang.

*Thock.*

The stubborn slate parts like butter, straight and true as a plumb-line.

The builder fits the two new halves into the batter until water sheets over them in a seamless silver skin. Before moving on, it does not carry the dark splitting-stone away into a private hoard. Instead, it sets the chalcedony nodule neatly on the dry, flat lintel-slab above the nearest doorway—right at chest height, where the next solitary mason passing down this alley forty years from now will see it against the pale crustose lichen, know immediately what it is for, use it, and set it back on the sill.

Up on the high ridge, where the bright highland wind drops into the millions of stone joints and loses its voice, nothing stands against the sky. There is no shrine, no omen, no disturbance in the slate-grey wading bird’s slow stalk across the beehive domes. There is only the vast, quiet city digesting the rain, a fault line sleeping easily in the deep dark, and a faint, clean taste of well-being threading downward through eighty miles of cold water.

The wise power lingers until dusk turns the wet roofs violet, watches the builder amble down into the flooded courtyard to skim its evening meal under the first sharp stars, and goes on its way, entirely content never to be thanked, known, or named.
