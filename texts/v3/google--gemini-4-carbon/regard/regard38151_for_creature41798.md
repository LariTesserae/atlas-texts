Something is always listening to the colloids, though from very far away, and usually only for trouble.

When you seed the crust of a restless planet with sub-micron diamondoid grit to keep its fault lines from snapping cities in half above, you leave an ear against the stone. Not a camera, and not a patrol—only a vast, patient attention draped across the continental root, reading the thermal budget of the rock the way a sleeper feels the weight of a blanket. Across thousands of cubic miles of deep trachyte and basalt, the math is supposed to stay simple: tectonic shear squeezes the groundwater, the lattices flash phononic stress into scattershot white light, the bare rock drinks the light back down as gentle, diffuse warmth, and the fault creeps smoothly instead of breaking. Energy in, energy out. Nothing lost.

Except here, three hundred yards beneath the surface in a newly split blister of stone, four milliwatts have gone missing.

More puzzling still is *how* they are disappearing. Inside the ambient, roaring white static of the deep aquifer, a tiny, rhythmic stutter has begun—high-frequency bursts of squeezed light firing like a slow, deliberate heartbeat, followed by total optical silence. Colloids are going dark in the seam, wrung clean out of their engineered geometry, and the missing energy is not turning back into heat.

A blind maintenance process, built without curiosity, would read that signature as mineral fouling or bacterial slime choking a stress-relief vein. It would open a high-pressure valve miles up-gradient, spike the groundwater with hot caustic salts, flush the fracture clean to bare feldspar, and move on.

What turns its attention toward this cistern instead is older than its own machinery, and long past hurrying.

It has no eyes in the pocket, and needs none. Millions of beads of clear condensate already cling to the jagged ceiling three yards above the pools, each one a sharp, hanging lens catching the glare from below and throwing tiny, inverted images of the walls back into the crystal grains of the rock. By reading the faint, scattered shimmer of photons refracting into the surrounding feldspar, the listener assembles the twenty-pace chamber in absolute stillness—every razor flake of fallen stone, every wavering plume of invisible steam, the milky amphipods snapping away from hot raindrops in the triangular basin, the flat tan spider starving beneath the east overhang, and the two-hand blister of clear, tough flesh breathing on the north wall.

It watches for seventy-two hours without moving a grain of silt. To watch something that extends an apron of glassy meat three inches an hour, it slows its own immense clock down until the pattering rain sounds like a steady drumroll and the slow pulse of internal lenses reads as brisk, purposeful labor.

It sees the upstream cilia combing engineered diamondoids out of the scalding sheet-flow. It sees the grit-lined sphincters clench, milking sudden flashes of ultraviolet fire out of industrial runoff while terraced, soot-black fern-louvers tilt inside the jelly to drink the burst. It notes the neat, staggered rows of poppy-seed pellets stacked in the lee of submerged stones—spent carbon dust bound in baked protein, tiny holes of pure, lightless black punched into the noon-white room.

And in that slow, quiet regard, something very like delight moves through the deep crust.

For ten thousand generations, the colloids were only bandages—clever, sterile dust laid down to soothe a planet’s fever. Now a wild scrap of headless biology has crept down the plumbing and turned the medicine into pasture. It is eating earthquakes and building black ferns out of them.

A clumsy kindness would ruin this immediately. It might flood the seam with easy dissolved sugars so the grazer never has to scrape scarce yellow biofilm again, softening it into a fat, helpless smudge. Worse, out of loneliness or vanity, it might try to kindle a faster nervous system inside that clear rubber dome—giving an animal that eats omnidirectional light a front, a back, an ache for company, and the capacity to feel trapped inside twenty paces of bare stone.

Wisdom knows when a creature is already whole. The blister on the north wall is not lonely, and it is not suffering. Its motionless digestion under hot white rain is complete in itself. Even the harsh fate waiting for it a month from now—when the cooling rock settles, its vent pinches dry, and it must focus the room’s pitiless glare inward to cook its own flesh into dry gray spores—is not a tragedy to be prevented. That desperate, crystalline boil is its bloom. Stop the vent from closing, keep the north wall wet forever, and the patch will only grow broad, senescent, and sterile in a single cistern until some larger tremor crushes it flat. It needs the drought to fly.

Yet the listener does not pull away and leave the room untouched. Looking closely at the sharp, bright pocket, it sees three quiet places where chance is about to be needlessly cruel—and where a thumb laid lightly on the stress of the cooling stone can answer without ever breaking the wildness of the place.

***

First, there is the exile on the east wall.

Unlike the amphipods in the gravel or the blister in the runoff, the pale, coin-flat arachnid tucked beneath the knife-edged overhang has no business here. Twelve days ago, a sudden hydraulic surge in the upper karst washed it down a vertical joint from cool, pitch-black limestone fissures two hundred yards nearer the sky. Down here, the air is past blood heat and blinding from every angle. Its median eyes have crusted over with precipitated mineral scale against the glare; its haemolymph is thickening slowly from dehydration despite the steam, because every drop within reach is hot enough and sharp enough with diamondoid grit to foul its mouthparts. Still it holds its station, ten-inch sensory legs resting lightly across two sterile trickles of water, waiting with ancient, uncomplaining patience for a footstep that cannot ever come down a sub-micron seam. Left alone, heat-stupor will loosen its tarsal claws in another ninety hours and drop it into a blazing pool.

Four yards behind the dry stone where its abdomen rests lies an older, unlit cooling tube—a narrow, slanted fracture leading upward out of the geothermal plume and back into the dark, wet limestone caves where the spider hatched. Four yards of solid trachyte might as well be four miles to an almond-sized hunter. To an intelligence whose daily work is guiding tectonic stress across mountain roots, four yards is a single breath.

Over the next six hours, the chamber rings with four faint, glassy *pings*—no louder and no closer together than the natural settling of the young rock. Deep inside the east wall, focused acoustic waves walk a microscopic tension joint sideways through the dark trachyte, unzipping the stone grain by grain until the crack breaks cleanly through into the deep shadow beneath the knife-edged overhang.

It opens no wider than two stacked coins—far too narrow to bleed off the cistern’s hot vapor cycle. In fact, because the dark cave network above is cool and dense, air does not rush upward out of the room at all. Instead, a thin, heavy ribbon of sixty-degree darkness sighs *down* the sloped floor of the slot, pooling just beneath the overhang, bringing with it a bead-wide film of ordinary, unlit rainwater that smells faintly of cave clay, humic acid, and surface roots far overhead.

Riding that first slow trickle out of the dark tumbles a drowned midge larva, washed loose from an upper pool. It taps lightly against the spider’s left sensory leg.

Twelve days of absolute stillness end in a tenth of a second. The spider gathers the morsel in its pedipalps and drinks the cool, gritless moisture clinging to it, drawing clean water into its starved body. For twenty minutes afterward it grooms its whip-legs carefully under the lip of the shelf. Then its sensory bristles read the steady, cool draft breathing out from behind its own back—air that does not sear its book-lungs, stone that throws back zero light. Turning neatly in its own length, the flat tan body slips edgewise into the coin-thin slot and climbs steadily upward out of the glare, vanishing back into the cool, blind night where crusted eyes are no handicap at all.

Behind it, the narrow seam stays open just enough to weep one dark, unlit drop down the east wall every forty seconds. It does not cool the steaming floor-vents below by a tenth of a degree, nor dim the room. What it carries into this sterile, newly cracked pocket is what deep, rock-filtered water lacks: trace nitrates, dissolved phosphorus, and stray organic carbon leached from soil hundreds of yards overhead. Within a week, the thumbprint smears of pale yellow biofilm along the lower east wall begin to thicken and spread into rich, velvet ribbons—vitamins waiting in abundance when the clear blister next slides across the rubble to graze.

Second, there is the basin floor.

The two eyeless amphipods working the drowned gravel do not register the noon-white floodlight with optic nerves, yet their milky, semi-transparent shells evolved for sunless aquifers, without pigment to shield their dividing cells from the relentless high-blue and near-ultraviolet scatter bouncing off the feldspar. Meanwhile, tucked into the dry lee of stones all around the basin sit hundreds of the grazer’s discarded pellets—baked protein locked tight around inert, blackened carbon dust that nothing in the young chamber has yet managed to rot.

One more tiny adjustment in the floor-seam—a shift of half a millimeter in how the hot water sheets off a stair-stepped ledge—redirects a gentle eddy across the lower stones. Over two days, the whispery runoff sweeps three dozen rows of spent, poppy-seed pellets down into the deepest corner of the triangular pool, gathering them beneath an angled flake of drowned trachyte.

They settle there into a drift of jet-black sand the width of a palm. For the first time since the fracture split open, the chamber holds a patch of genuine darkness. Beneath that cantilevered stone flake, sitting atop a velvet bed that drinks every stray photon from below, the glare drops to twilight. The two pale amphipods find the alcove within an hour, resting between foraging circuits in cool optical quiet, their pleopods fanning oxygen across the black drift—where waterborne thermophilic bacteria immediately begin colonizing the shaded grit, breaking down the baked protein binders and releasing recycled ammonia back into the water to feed the silica-lichens along the warm vents.

***

Third, and widest of all, is the road ahead.

Looking forward through the stress tensors of the trachyte, the listener sees clearly what will happen in twenty-eight days: the magmatic dike below will shift its weight, the hot seam feeding the north wall will pinch shut, and the grazer will draw itself inward into a clear, plum-sized crystal lens, gather the fierce white light of the wet floor, and boil its own heart into glittering gray dust.

Left to raw geology, almost every spore lifted on the chamber’s thermal updraft would die in the ceiling. The hairline fractures overhead currently pinch down into dead-end capillaries six yards up, flushed downward by heavy groundwater with nowhere hot and open beyond them.

So, with three weeks to spare, the gardener scores the bark of the deep rock.

Across two miles of stressed trachyte flanking the magmatic dike, using gentle, rhythmic nudges of thermal pressure that never register on the surface far above, it walks a lacework of hairline vapor-chimneys diagonally upward from the cistern’s weeping roof. It links those dry, rising drafts—unopposed by downward water—into a quiet archipelago of seven wider pockets newly split along the hot seam, each one streaming with fresh photonic groundwater, each one bare, blinding, and waiting.

Last of all, in the quiet ledger where the crust’s deep plumbing is tracked across millennia, it marks this entire three-mile vein of rock with a permanent, silent boundary. No automated chemical purge will ever be routed down these fractures to clear the drop in optical yield. So long as the magmatic dike stays hot below, the missing milliwatts belong to the tenant.

***

If you climbed down through three hundred yards of solid stone tomorrow and stood ankle-deep in the arc-welder puddle, squinting through watering eyes at the sweating charcoal walls, you would find no sign that anything vast or merciful had ever looked in. There are no carved marks on the feldspar, no softened edges on the flinty rubble, no hush in the brisk, hot percussion of the indoor rain.

The narrow overhang high on the east wall is simply empty now, weeping a single cool, non-luminous drop every half-minute down onto a thriving, hand-wide ribbon of pale yellow biofilm. In the corner of the bright pool, two milky amphipods rest motionless over a patch of absolute black sand, their antennae twitching gently in the current.

And on the north wall, invisible until the *tck-tck-tck* of the runoff parts around its low, glassy rim, the blister stretches an apron slowly toward the enriched stone—squeezing white fire out of the planet’s fever, stacking its tiny dark cairns beside the stream, gathering strength for the dry afternoon next month when it will split open like a steamed nut and send its children rising into an open sky of rock.
