A lesser kindness would have reached down and turned the machines off.

A lesser god, looking into that bruised twilight bowl from the quiet cold above the clouds, would have pitied the scarred, pearlescent skin of the Line-Tenders, mourned the ruptured ears of their ancestors, shuddered at the blind scissor-things waiting among the basalt knives, and offered silence as a cure. It would have stilled the turbines, smoothed the jagged rock into gentle pasture, thickened the people’s fragile bones, and set them down upon safe, silent grass.

And in doing so, it would have murdered an entire world. To take the roar from a people whose ribs are woven to ripple with it—whose language is light and shadow-pressure, whose grace is built out of leaning forty degrees into thunder—would not be salvation. It would make Rim-Walkers of them all: heavy, broken things gasping in an unbearable quiet, stranded on dead dirt, grieving a sky that used to hold them up.

The Power that paused above the pewter clouds was older than pity, and far more attentive.

It did not announce itself. An announcement large enough to cross twenty miles of acoustic shear would either shatter three residential districts or blind half the canopy. Instead, for three local years—a span unmarked by seasons in that constant, sweaty dusk—it simply listened. 

It laid its vast, weightless attention along the non-baryonic filaments like a citizen pressing a jawbone to a wall. It learned the grammar of amber-and-cyan wrist-flicks. It felt the polite *ba-dump* of shade-taps across crowded evening plazas. It followed the grease-slicked Line-Tenders down into the blurred hell beneath the tiers, watching their calves swell with micro-embolisms and their corneas cloud while they hacked dead, calcified arthropod shells off a humming main-line so four hundred sleeping children three hundred feet above wouldn't drop into the dark. It watched the funerals—the indigo throats, the unison snap of twenty shades, the terrible plummeting dark. And it watched the machines buried deep in the vitrified crust below: ancient, orphaned emitters built by minds long gone, grinding away on degraded feedback loops, brute-forcing the sky into solidity with sloppy, jagged harmonics that tore at living tissue only because nobody had serviced their phase-aligners in six hundred years.

The builders had designed the scaffold to hold weight. They had never bothered to make it gentle, because they had not planned on anyone living inside the chord.

When the Power finally moved, nobody in the basin looked up and saw a miracle. Miracles are abrupt, and abruptness in a standing-wave city kills people.

It began down in the dark, beneath the basalt splinters, deeper even than the blind scavengers went.

Into the buried vaults where the colossal emitters hummed in their cracked housings, something moved like warm oil through stone. It did not replace the engines; it *tuned* them. Over the course of forty days—so slowly that not a single sleeping hammock jerked, not a single bead of suspended mist dropped out of its lattice prematurely—it smoothed the microscopic burrs off the containment shears.

High in the seventh tier of the central district, an old Baffle-Cutter named Vess—his eyes milky at the edges, his knuckles knobby with grey cartilage—paused with his chin pressed against a primary strut. He frowned, his collarbones flaring a faint, puzzled yellow.

The roar had not grown quieter. Had the volume dropped, the floor beneath his wide heels would have gone spongy. The titanic brass-and-organ thunder was all still there, holding up the miles of emerald bladder-vine and invisible avenues. Yet the *teeth* were gone from it. Beneath the primary load-bearing wave, there had always been a vicious, sub-audible parasitic rattle—the ultrasonic jaggedness that crazed kneecaps, detached retinas in the deep under-halls, and turned blood to foam. 

Over six weeks, that jaggedness simply dissolved. The chord resolved. 

Where the ancient machines had been screaming in a harsh, mechanical cluster-tone, the basin slowly shifted into a rich, consonant, mathematical suspension—still immense, still physically solid, still loud enough to hold a municipality in empty air, yet rounded at the microscopic edges so that it passed through wet marrow and eye-jelly like warm water rather than broken glass. Young Line-Tenders dropping beneath the lowest tiers on their silk tethers came back up after two-hour shifts blinking clear, unclouded eyes, staring in disbelief at calves that did not ache, whispering across the plazas in cascading green ripples of bewildered laughter down both arms.

Only one small convenience broke: for a week, nobody’s tuning-fork kettle would boil. Without the ugly, tearing high-frequency shears, the hanging gourd-bladders stayed lukewarm. There was a brief, frantic flurry of white-blue irritation across kitchen membranes throughout the basin—until a teenage girl in the eastern reaches noticed that where three of the newly consonant harmonics braided together near the ceiling struts, they formed tight, walnut-sized knots of pure thermal resonance, steady and clean as candle flames, marked by a faint golden shimmer in the mist. Move the kettle six inches left into the gold knot, and glider-stew simmered faster than before without scorching the gourd. Within three days, the hand-cant for *hearth* had a new gesture: a cupped palm tipped slightly upward toward gold.

Then the Power turned its attention to Down.

It did not fill the basin in. It understood that a sky-people need depth beneath them the way sailors need fathoms; take away the abyss, and you take away the meaning of balance, the pride of the jump, the translucent beauty of ten canopies stacked in glowing twilight.

Instead, it addressed the cruelty of the bottom.

Down among the razor-edged basalt fractures, where the air was thickest and the emitters thundered directly overhead, a microscopic spore began to drift out of the warm, mineral-free trickles at the basin rim—something that looked entirely native, cousin to the aero-moss and the bladder-vine, yet engineered with quiet, patient genius to love weight and darkness and stone.

It settled on the fresh volcanic glass. Feeding on the raw iron and magnesium of the young rock and drinking the violent acoustic energy right at its source, it grew—not upward into weightless sheets, which the crushing near-field roar would have shredded, but sideways and thick, knitting over the razor splinters in deep, spongy, velvet-black mats ten feet thick. Within a season, the raw knives of the floor were gone, swallowed beneath a vast, dark, resilient carpet of *bass-peat*—a springy, living cushion that drank falling impact the way the locals' heel-pads drank vibration.

And out of that black carpet rose the catch-sails.

Fifty feet above the newly padded floor, anchored to the deep rock by braided, rubbery cables, enormous cup-shaped membranes of deep violet flora unfurled across the dark chasms like wide, overlapping lotus leaves, each a hundred yards across, buoyed at their rims by rings of warm gas. 

The first time a teenager slipped on a slick patch of condensation during a harmonic squall in the fourth tier—missing his grab-line, plunging silent and flailing through six tearing emerald canopies while forty neighbors froze in strobing cyan horror above—they waited for the awful, final disappearance into the black teeth below.

Instead, two hundred and fifty feet down, they saw the distant violet expanse billow downward like a giant lung inhaling. 

The boy hit a tilted, slick, elastic curve of wet membrane that yielded twenty feet under his slight weight, sleighing him sideways in a long, cushioned arc that bled his momentum off into a chorus of booming, drum-like thrums, spilling him bruised, winded, and smeared with harmless purple mucilage onto a broad, spongy shelf where the acoustic pressure was gentle enough to breathe—and right beside an ancient maintenance pylon whose vertical climbing-rungs reached straight back up to the first tier.

He climbed home four hours later, glowing pale, shaky amber from head to foot, smelling faintly of crushed plums. The entire district didn't sleep that night; three thousand people crowded the invisible edges, snapping their shades in rolling, miles-wide ovations of rhythmic quiet that echoed off one another’s backs like warm rain.

And what of the dead?

The funerals did not have to stop. A people should not have their rites stolen from them. When an elder died now, their kin still washed the light from their skin, still gathered with indigo-lit throats at the un-gridded gaps, still snapped twenty shades open to make a chimney of quiet and let the body go.

The body still fell through the sighing green silk. Yet instead of smashing upon sterile obsidian to be scissored apart by blind vermin, it dropped gently into the deep, warm folds of the violet catch-leaves, which curled slowly inward around silent, un-breathing weight, lowering it down into the rich black bass-peat below. There, warm luminescent mycelia took the hollow bones and knit them cleanly into the floor—sending up, six weeks later, tiny, drifting lanterns of pale blue spore-light that floated lazily up through the tiers on evening thermals, brushing past sleeping hammocks and glowing green bedrooms before dissolving into the canopy to feed the very skin-bacteria the grandchildren wore on their arms.

Down was no longer hell. Down had become root, and cradle, and return.

Last of all, the Power went out to the ragged margins of the bowl, where the Rim-Walkers dragged themselves across wet gravel on crutches and bellies, gasping in the thin, quiet gravity, scraping salt from stone cracks with bleeding fingers.

It did not herd them back inside; exiles and heretics and restless minds need an edge to walk, or a city becomes a cage.

Instead, along the raw lip where the standing waves frayed into ordinary air, five-mile-long ribbons of crystalline mineral began to sweat out of the seeping runoff fissures, hardening along the stone trails in smooth, resonant tracks. They were passive acoustic waveguides—veins of tuned quartz-analogue grown straight into the rock, catching the stray, leaking thunder of the basin and channeling it outward into gentle, waist-high corridors of localized, humming buoyancy that wound up the crags like invisible mountain streams.

A Rim-Walker woke one damp dusk wrapped in her cured vine-leathers, braced herself for the sickening, bone-bending misery of hauling her seven-foot frame over a ridge toward the salt pans—and felt a warm, familiar hum press softly beneath her ribs. 

She stood up without her crutches. The air along the salt-trail held twenty percent of the city's lift—just enough that her honeycombed femurs didn't bow, just enough that her supple, cartilage-laced chest had something friendly to lean against while still leaving the vast, quiet pewter sky open above her head. And there, pooling in smooth, newly weathered bowls along the quartz-veined stone, the mineral runoff gathered clear and rich with dissolved salts and trace metals the weightless jungle starved for, waiting to be scooped up without a single cut palm.

The Power lingered one day more above the clouds, checking its work.

Looking down from the rim, a stranger with a stone in their hand would have sworn nothing at all had happened. The twenty-mile hollow still roared like ten thousand brass turbines against a cathedral organ. The empty air still shimmered with hairline grids and violet pinpricks; the harmonic squalls still wrung sudden geometric galaxies of warm mist out of nothing; giant, weightless curtains of chartreuse vine still thrashed in a wind made of pure noise, while pale ribbon-gliders surfed invisible troughs and long-limbed neighbors argued across fifty yards of void in blazing, rapid-fire poetry of cyan and gold.

Nobody built a temple. Nobody knew there was anything to pray to. They were pragmatic descendants of maintenance crews; they assumed, in long, flickering debates over simmering stew-bladders, that some ancient self-repair subroutine deep in the planet’s crust had finally finished a six-century diagnostic cycle, or that the jungle and the machines had simply grown old enough together to reach an understanding.

That suited the Power entirely. Wisdom has very little use for worship, which tends to make tall people kneel. It much preferred watching a seventy-year-old Line-Tender with clear eyes step casually off nothing onto nothing, flick a drop of spit across a humming plaza to watch it spin silver, flash a slow green wave of pure amusement down both arms to his granddaughter, and lean forty degrees into the thunder that held his world aloft.

Satisfied, the Power withdrew into the quiet between stars, leaving the chord to ring.
