To a lesser power, this kiln-hall might look like a trap—a blind, claustrophobic box begging for a window, a spring of cool water, a breath of wind, or a voice to wake the sleepers. A clumsy benevolence would crack the woven ceiling to let in the sky. And in doing so, it would murder everything inside. Rain would turn the bone-ash heads of the Masons to blind slurry; wind would snap the brittle, dry-fitted mortises of the air; rot would find the pale oak beams and the bran-stuffed ribs; and the sudden, terrible tyranny of ordinary Euclidean space would crush twenty folded alcoves into a single ruin of broken brick and suffocated hares.

The wise power sees this immediately. It looks upon the yellow-ochre basin not as a prison, nor as an unfinished sketch waiting for a soul, but as a complete and gentle liturgy. It recognizes the rare sanctity of a world without predation, without decay, without grief—a universe sustained entirely by patience, warmth, and joinery.

Yet the power also sees the quiet, mathematical peril creeping toward the kiln-hall’s distant future. 

Because nothing dies here, and nothing leaves, the room is slowly, inexorably compacting. Every bark-hare that fires itself into a tile, every lime-cube that grows a leaf, every Mason that mineralizes into a load-bearing pillar adds to the sum of craft. The Masons can pare the air and fold new creases into the timber, tucking ten yards into an inch, and they are masters of this origami. But even folded space has a grain. Fold a dry sheet of parchment too many times upon itself, and the fibers begin to strain. One day—perhaps ten thousand seasons from now—there will be one brick too many, one angle too acute. A dry-stone seam will shear. The silent pressure of accumulated craft will have nowhere left to tuck itself, and the peaceful thicket will lock solid, petrifying every breathing flank and boxwood elbow into a single, dense, immovable block of terracotta.

So the power decides to act. Not to change the nature of the room, and never to startle its makers, who would only try to mend a miracle as if it were a crack in the wall. 

To touch this place without breaking its rules, the power makes itself very small, very dry, and entirely true to plumb. 

It does not enter from above, for there is no above. Instead, down in one of the deepest, oldest creases of the hall—where three mineralized caryatid-Masons hold up the weight of six overlapping ceilings—the power lets a single, tiny thing come to rest inside a split-hazel pouch that was hung on an oak peg long ago and forgotten.

Days pass in the toasted-chaff warmth before a living Mason comes that way. 

This Mason is old. Its chamois knees are stiff with absorbed silt; its boxwood shoulders creak a fraction slower than they used to; its felted wool cowl is heavy with white lime-dust. It has come down through the triangular gap beneath a cistern wall, investigating a faint, chalky tension in the floor. Four pumice-worms have settled into a corner and begun firing themselves into stone all at once, and the surrounding herringbone tiles are groaning silently, bowed tight with nowhere left to fold. Every nearby timber beam is already packed ten-deep with tucked alcoves; every grass cord in reach is knotted taut.

The Mason kneels. Its sedge plumb-lines swing out from its blank plaster temples, trembling violently as they read the congested geometry. The chert beads knock together with a dry *tick*. There is no seam left here to pare. 

Methodically, unhurriedly, the Mason reaches into its own pouch for a hornbeam wedge, finds none sharp enough, and turns its smooth brow toward the forgotten pouch hanging on the ancient oak peg behind it. Its plumb-lines point straight to the hazel weave, pulled by an exquisite, irresistible structural truth sitting inside.

The Mason unties the dry grass drawstring and reaches in with its heavy stoneware hand. 

What it draws out is not flint, nor slate, nor hornbeam. It is a small, three-sided wedge—a bevelled prism no longer than a finger-joint—made of a pale, warm substance the room has never held before. It looks like seasoned ivory or river-smoothed soapstone, yet it is bone-dry and faintly porous, drinking the static heat of the Mason’s palm until it feels alive. Along its flattest face runs a single, crisp impression: not a thumb-whorl, for this power has no thumbs, but the delicate, unmistakable negative ridge of a Mason’s own thumb-pad, waiting to be met.

For twenty minutes, the Mason sits motionless in the upward-slanting light, its wide ceramic thumb pressed into that hollow. 

Through its wrist, up through its dry-pegged elbow and into its bran-stuffed chest, travels a vibration wholly unlike the groan of tight walls. It feels like room. Not emptiness—never cold, frightening emptiness—but *slack*. The generous, breathable slack of an unspooled cord; the quiet give of a mortise cut a hair’s breadth wider so the wood can swell in peace.

When the Mason withdraws its thumb, it knows what the tool is for. Craft recognizes craft.

Turning back to the buckled, over-crowded corner, the Mason takes its flint scraper and pares a tiny shaving of the strained air. As expected, the gap tries to snap shut immediately, choked by the six ceilings pressing in from every side. Before it can close, the Mason slips the pale, strange wedge into the hairline crack and taps it gently—*thump*, the sound arriving a breath early—with the heel of its stoneware hand.

The wedge does not force the bricks apart. Instead, it turns the crack *outward*.

Looking into the seam held open by the pale prism, the Mason’s swinging plumb-lines do not strike the back of an adjacent oak beam, nor do they drop into a crowded cistern. They swing smoothly forward, pointing into an unlaid vestibule the size of a threshing floor. 

There is nothing in this vestibule yet, and yet it is entirely native to the kiln-hall. The air inside it is already warm as a banked bread oven, smelling faintly of toasted chaff. The middle air already holds that clear, shadow-tilting afternoon brightness. There are no walls yet, only a soft, yielding margin of warm ochre silt waiting to be pressed into molds, and stacks of pale, unworked heartwood timber resting on dry lime, fragrant from an unseen adze, waiting for pegs. It is raw volume, pre-warmed and consecrated, folded seamlessly onto the edge of their crowded world without letting in a single drop of rain or breath of foreign wind.

More miraculous still: the pale wedge does not stay behind in the joint. Once the new vestibule has accepted the excess pressure of the bowed herringbone floor—the tight tiles sighing down flat and neat as they spill three courses of their length into the fresh space—the little prism loosens itself and drops softly back into the Mason’s waiting stoneware palm, completely unchipped, warm, and ready to be used again.

An hour later, a bristle-necked hare hops cautiously across the neat new threshold, its stiff ruff sweeping the virgin air with evident satisfaction. Behind it, a single copper-colored shoot extrudes from the last laid brick, leaning eagerly toward the open silt.

The old Mason rises, its bran-stuffed knees settling with a dry *shhhk*. It tucks the pale wedge carefully into its split-hazel pouch beside its flint scrapers and coils of sedge twine. Then it walks—taking six minutes to cross three paces of gourd-thicket—until it meets another Mason sighting along a sagging slate arch.

They pause. They raise their heavy stoneware hands and press their thumbs together, ridge locking into negative ridge. 

For a long time they stand joined in the warm, static quiet, while a parchment-winged moth steps off one felted cowl and lands high in the woven brick vault. Through their locked thumbs passes the news—not in words, which they have never needed, but in level, and plumb, and the sudden, glorious easing of ten thousand tiny joints throughout the hall. *There is room,* the vibration says through the clay and boxwood. *There will always be room.*

Dusting a little white lime from their sleeves, they part. One stays to braid a fresh grass cord for the gourds; the other turns back toward the new vestibule, wooden mallet in hand, unhurried, content, going to lay the first herringbone course of an endless afternoon.

And the wise power, having left behind nothing larger than a finger-joint and nothing louder than a well-fitted peg, withdraws its touch—staying only so far away as a builder stands when stepping back to admire a warm, plumb wall that will stand forever.
