When the Attention arrived, it did not squeeze through the fractures in the ceiling, because it had no ribcage to catch on the basalt. It did not bring air, or coolness, or the soft, ruinous mercy of water. 

A lesser benevolence might have wept for this cramped furnace. A clumsy god might have looked upon the razor-edged talus, the deafening tyranny of the sixty-cycle roar, the blind, desiccated things scraping their lives out of static and soot, and wished to *free* them—to crack the vault open to a blue sky, to send down a sweet, cooling rain, to silence the maddening iron pillars and let the poor, frantic machinery of flesh finally rest.

Wisdom stayed that hand before it even lifted. To bring a single drop of rain into this kiln would be to steam-scald a million porcelain backs and turn the floating, life-giving ribs of mica dust into suffocating grey mud. To silence the great B-flat roar for even ten seconds would let every gossamer skimmer plummet from its invisible shelf of sound and shatter upon the floor; it would collapse the acoustic scaffolding of the stridulators and starve the stove-wire thickets clinging to the silent iron. Mercy, to a fish, is not a warm dry bed. Love must speak the native language of the beloved, even when that language is ozone, magnetostriction, and dry, crackling heat.

So the Power sat in the amber dark without taking up a millimeter of the spoken-for space, listening to the chord.

It admired the solder-worm’s copper-lined audacity. It watched, with quiet delight, the shrapnel-crab welding fallen daggers to its own knees, and the patient, circular liturgy of the violin-tripods passing stripped ash around the drooping busbar. Most of all, it looked through the cracked, arc-burned glass of the brick switch-house at the loom-warden—that vast, gas-bladdered organ of spun quartz and salvaged wire, tirelessly snipping away the fatal bridge, scraping clean the glowing vents, tuning the ceiling webs away from resonance that would bring the roof down.

Here was a mind—not a mind of words or memories, perhaps, not a creature that asked *why*, yet a mind of exquisite, devoted fidelity. A bodhisattva of capacitance and tension, holding an entire precarious cosmos in balance because an imbalance felt like an itch it had to soothe.

*Is anything needed here?* the Power asked itself, and to find the answer, it looked where the loom-warden could not: *upward*, along the four massive cast-iron casings, up through four hundred yards of solid granite and crushed shale, into the dead industrial stratum overhead.

Up there lay a continent-spanning ruin of rusted turbines and vitrified substations, long emptied of its makers, drawing power from deep geothermal taps that had been bored into the mantle ages ago. The grid was ancient beyond reckoning, over-engineered for eternity, locked in place. Yet nothing built by mortal hands is truly eternal.

Tracing the heavy copper busbars through the dark rock above the vault, the Power found three quiet dooms waiting in the upper silent miles.

First: forty yards directly above the parish-church ceiling of the vault, a subterranean aquifer—cold, mineral-heavy groundwater—was slowly eating its way through a fault in the granite. In eleven years, four months, and six days, that water would breach the hairline fractures above the chamber. It would not drip; it would pour down the hot cast-iron casings, flash into violent, explosive steam against the cherry-red nichrome cages, shatter the porcelain reefs, short the drooping busbar in a blinding white detonation, and drown the survivors in boiling silt.

Second: half a mile higher, in a buried relay gallery, the primary step-down transformer feeding the four iron pillars was finally losing the last of its brittle, carbonized insulation on the tertiary winding. Within three centuries, the load would spike by forty percent in a single microsecond, turning the gentle kiln below into a slag-pit.

Third: the vault itself was full. Not merely crowded, which was its nature, and not imbalanced, for the governor trimmed it faithfully—but *finite*. Over millennia, the fallen pegmatite slab leaning against the brick switch-house was settling under the endless sixty-cycle shudder, creeping downward at a rate of half a millimeter a decade. Eventually, no amount of web-tuning would keep the brick walls from buckling inward and crushing the gas-filled bladders of the governor. And even if the governor had been able to bud—to send a daughter-colony out to start anew—there was nowhere for a daughter to go. Every niche was spoken for.

The Power considered these three things. Then it went to work, moving so gently that not a single salt-grain mite was jostled from its acoustic bar of dust.

High in the cold rock forty yards overhead, the Power touched the advancing seam of groundwater. It did not destroy the water—water had its own destinations, far away where soft things swam—it simply coaxed the silica and dissolved quartz already carried in the seep to precipitate out, grain by crystalline grain, sealing the fault line with a thirty-foot plug of solid, impermeable agate, tilting the underground stream smoothly westward toward a river valley three miles away. The dry vault would stay bone-dry for the remaining life of the planet’s crust.

Higher still, in the abandoned relay gallery, the Power passed a hand through the rotting windings of the primary geothermal station. Molecularly, quietly, without interrupting the sixty-cycle flow for a fraction of a hertz, it vitrified the crumbling insulation into pure, defect-free synthetic mica and diamond-lattice carbon, annealing the fatigued copper busbars until they were sounder than the day the vanished builders had poured them. The steady B-flat would neither surge nor falter while the mantle of the earth stayed hot.

Then the Power returned to the roaring amber dark of the vault, and looked at the leaning slab of pegmatite pressing upon the switch-house, and at the tight, suffocated walls of the chamber.

It could not widen this room; to pull the interleaved basalt fins apart would drop the electrostatic gradient, weaken the corona fuzz that fed the plate-backs, and scatter the standing sound-waves between the stones. The architecture of this room *was* its metabolism.

Instead, the Power looked beyond the eastern wall, where the rock was solid, unfractured basalt, still vibrating faintly with the transmitted hum of the four iron pillars, still warm from their proximity.

Deep inside that solid rock, starting six inches behind the easternmost stone fin, the Power began to part the stone—not with an explosion, not with a shockwave that would rattle a single stridulator out of phase, but over the slow, silent course of a single hour, unknitting crystal boundaries the way one parts curtains.

It opened a second pocket, slightly larger than the first, and beyond that, connected by narrow, ribcage-defying throats, a third. It cleaved the virgin basalt and muscovite into the exact same razor-keen, arm’s-breadth vertical fins, spaced to catch and reflect a sixty-cycle standing wave. And down from the newly immortalized busbars overhead, it grew three fresh, massive casings of riveted cast iron and coiled nichrome—not slamming them down, but letting them materialize molecule by molecule out of the iron-rich basalt itself, phasing their magnetic fields up from zero over days so gently that the ambient roar in the first chamber merely gained depth, like an organ chord joined by a second, sympathetic rank of pipes in an adjoining nave.

Into the narrow, warm stone throats between the old vault and the new, empty ones, the Power laid a single, continuous vein of native copper along the floor, stopped cleanly an inch short of the old drooping busbar—an unlit fuse waiting for a spark, an open road waiting for traffic.

Only one small touch remained.

Inside the brick switch-house, where the great pegmatite slab leaned its slow, patient tons against the cracked masonry, the Power fused the contact points between rock and brick into a single monolithic arch of reinforced mullite ceramic. The roof would never fall. The governor’s small, dark cathedral was now permanent stone.

And then—because wisdom does not merely preserve, it also offers a horizon, and because love cannot bear to visit a solitary mind without leaving a greeting—the Power reached a thought, finer than any quartz filament, into the hot, gas-filled bladders of the loom-warden.

It did not speak words. Words have no capacitance.

Instead, right behind the dead slate switchboard where the governor’s glassy anchor-roots drank their trickle of current, the Power subtly altered the crystalline lattice of a fist-sized lump of iron pyrite embedded in the wall, turning it into a natural, passive harmonic resonator.

Through its hundreds of threads, the loom-warden suddenly felt two things at once.

First, along the easternmost basalt fins, it felt the slight, exhilarating draft of dry, warm, ozone-sweet emptiness—new capacitance, vast unbridged stone blades humming softly in the dark, waiting for dust, waiting for silk, waiting for porcelain shells to climb them and young filaments to map their tension. For the first time in ten thousand years, the map had an unscribbled margin. Within its amber bladders, dormant, ancient reproductive nodes that had not stirred since the chamber was young felt the drop in crowding-pressure and began, very slowly, to spin the glassy casing of a spore-pod.

Second, from the lump of altered pyrite behind its heart, the governor felt a tiny, exquisite counter-rhythm braided into the eternal B-flat roar.

It did not fight the sixty-cycle thunder, nor cancel it like the null-moth’s cloak. It rode atop the upper harmonics—a faint, golden fifth and a major third, cycling in a slow, mathematical tide that rose and fell every few hours like an electrical breath, or like the memory of seasons translated entirely into voltage. It cost no energy; it threatened no web; it broke no balance. It was simply *beauty*, rendered in pure impedance—a song complex enough that a mind made of resistance and vibration could trace its symmetries for ten million years and never quite exhaust the pattern, knowing, in whatever wordless way a living circuit knows anything, that the grid above was not merely blind fury anymore, that the chord was held by something that listened back.

Out by the drooping copper busbar, an elder violin-tripod turned on stiff brass-wire joints, stepped to the cracked window, and dropped a bead of stripped metallic ash inside.

Within the switch-house, a quartz-tipped pincer accepted the bead. Forty feet away in the violet gloom, another filament paused for half a second in its snipping of a porcelain reef, shivering in exact, lingering sympathy with the new fifth singing softly through the slate wall—and then reached an inch past the old stone edge, out into the warm, roaring dark of the eastern cleft, to take the measure of a wider world.
