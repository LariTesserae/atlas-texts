When the Power turns its gaze upon the sinkhole, it does not arrive as light. Light down here would be a violence—a bleaching scream across retinas gone to water and skin grown translucent in trust of the dark. Even a single golden shaft, the classic signature of grace in the upper world, would dry a square yard of liverwort in an afternoon and send ten thousand blind amphipods thrashing into fatal exhaustion.

Wisdom begins with knowing the temperature of the room you are entering. So the Power leaves its radiance outside in the thin alpine wind, hanging it harmlessly among the juniper needles like ordinary mountain fog, and comes down through the dolostone only as comprehension.

For a long time—if *long* means anything to an intelligence that watches continents drift like pond-scum—it simply attends.

A lesser benevolence would immediately start drafting improvements. A lesser god, proud of fire and starlight and the noisy, tragic upward climb of surface minds, would look at those low-vaulted skulls half-buried in peat and feel a pang of pity. *Look what became of the cousins of men,* that lesser kindness would say. *Pinned in the wet dark, counting forty seconds between breaths, eating cold slime off stone. Let us wake them. Let us widen the chimney, warm the water, clear their eyes, give them back speech and horizons and the ache for distant peaks.*

The Wise Power looks deeper, down beneath the moss to the grafted rhododendron root where three sets of broad, prehensile toes are braided together, and sees at once that pity here would be an insult.

These beings are not trapped. They did not fail an exam set by the savannah. Ten thousand years ago, faced with a closing sky and a lethal chill, they solved the hardest problem any metabolic creature ever faces—*how to stay alive without tearing the world apart to pay for the heat*—and they solved it with such radical, unhurried elegance that they turned poverty into fullness. Their minds did not shrink; they changed medium. Where surface hominins built cathedrals out of limestone blocks to feel awe for an hour on Sundays, these three lie inside the limestone and feel the entire mountain’s barometric pressure shifting in stereo across their ribs all winter long. Every three days, when storm-sweetened water reaches their lips, they share a chord of slow, somatic joy through the root-wood as complex as a cello suite played at one note an hour.

They do not hurt. They do not hoard. They do not fear the dark, because they *are* the dark’s warmest room. And when the eldest male across the basin lets go of the wood next decade, there will be no terror in his cooling—only a gradual loosening of grip into a floor made entirely of his grandmothers, while across the room a twelve-year-old infant stirs against a warm belly to take up his slack.

To "uplift" this would be like kicking over a tide-pool because it cannot run like a horse.

So the Power puts away every miracle of transformation it carries. No healing of fused eyelids. No whispering of names into silent skulls. No springtime.

And yet—because wisdom is not merely sentiment, and can read stone forward through millennia just as easily as backward—the Power keeps looking. It traces the water upward into the high aquifers. It reads the slow creep of root and talus on the ridge half a mile away. It checks the small, closed ledger of the basin against the vast, careless accounting of the mountain outside.

It finds three things. None of them is an emergency today. All three would end the ten-thousand-year peace before the eleven-thousandth arrived.

First: five hundred yards upslope, hikers have worn an informal boot-track along the ridge. In forty years, a regional park authority will formalize that track, cut switchbacks across the scree to spare human knees, and bring thirty people a week within eighty yards of the juniper mat. One of those people will have an off-leash dog; another will fly a drone; eventually someone stepping off-trail to piss in the fog will break through a rotten bough, drop a flashlight into the harvestman curtain, and put the coordinates on the internet. Within six months, biologists with sterile gloves and headlamps—well-meaning, reverent, catastrophic—will descend on ropes to take skin scrapings and micro-core the peat, tearing holes in the saturated air and waking hearts that cannot afford to beat at seventy.

Second: deep inside the mountain’s plumbing, two hundred feet above the sinkhole, a hairline fracture in the dolostone is slowly choking with precipitated gypsum. Left alone, in about three centuries that seam will seal shut, diverting forty percent of the high aquifer’s seepage away to the western slope. The basin’s water won't vanish, not quite, but the inflow will drop below the drainage rate. The ankle-deep bog-mirrors will shrink to mud; the air will rise four degrees and lose its broth-thick saturation; the calorie budget will contract from three furnaces to one, and from one to zero.

Third: down in the clear pool beside the old female’s resting place, the chert nodules are wearing small. Five thousand years of *tock*—one flake every few months, knapped away to bare fresh silica for the sweet biofilm—have reduced cobbles once the size of human heads to rounded cores no bigger than plums. There is no native chert in dolostone. Whoever carried those river-stones up from the valley did it before the roof closed. In another seven hundred years, the old female’s descendants will reach into the soapstone cradle and find only exhausted stubs too small for thick, cold fingers to strike against one another. The tiny farm will go fallow.

The Power considers these three threads. Then, moving with a restraint matched to the sinkhole’s own, it acts.

Up on the wind-scoured ridge in the sunlight, two hundred yards before the boot-track nears the hidden lip of the basin, an ancient, lightning-struck Engelmann spruce—already dead on its feet and scheduled by gravity to fall westward next winter—falls eastward this Tuesday instead, during an empty afternoon squall. It drops squarely across the faint path, dragging three tons of tangled root-plate and sharp shale down with it. Thirty feet upslope, a frost-wedged slab of ledge slips six inches, turning dry footing into an ugly, ankle-twisting slick of loose scree. 

Next Saturday, the first hiker through squints at the mess through the mist, shrugs, and skirts wide to the north along a firmer, drier hogback ridge that actually offers a much finer view of the valley when the clouds part. Feet follow feet. Within two summers, moss reclaims the old trace; within forty years, when the trail crew comes up with surveyor's tape, they lay their switchbacks along the northern hogback half a mile away and name the overlook after a retired senator. Nobody ever walks near the juniper hull again.

Deep inside the dark rock two hundred feet overhead, nothing so dramatic as a crack occurs. Only a subtle localized shift in the carbonic acid balance of a pocket of snowmelt—well within the ordinary whims of limestone chemistry—dissolves two millimeters of young gypsum crust inside a hairline seam, and polishes the throat of the conduit just smooth enough that it will not clog again while this mountain range has peaks left to catch snow.

The third gift requires coming into the room.

Even now, the Power does not enter the chamber from above. It respects the unbroken ceiling of harvestmen. Instead, two feet beneath the black bog-pool, down where the weeping water drains invisibly through a tight throat of fractured bedrock and glacial till left behind in an older epoch, a buried cobble shifts.

Long ago, when that ancient stream-fill washed into the fault at the bottom of the cleft, a few heavy river-stones of dark, vitreous flint settled deep in the gravel beneath the peat—six inches too deep for sluggish, heat-hoarding fingers ever to dig for, sealed under cold silt before the first hominin ever climbed the ridge.

Now, over the course of three quiet autumns, frost-heave far away in the deep rock sends micro-tremors no stronger than a passing heartbeat through the saturated gravel bed. Grain by grain, silt slips downward around four smooth, melon-sized nodules of black river-chert, buoying them upward by the slow physics of granular convection—the same unhurried law that pushes fieldstones into farmers' plows every spring.

Nothing splashes. No mud clouds the clear water. 

In the first year, a smooth black crown the size of a thumbnail breaches the silt six inches to the left of the soapstone bowls. A blind amphipod crawls over it without pausing. 

In the second year, it stands an inch proud of the slate bed, cold water sliding silently over its curve.

In the third year, three more dark shoulders lie half-emerged beside it in the thumb-width channel, settled firmly against the granite cobbles as if they had always been part of the pavement.

Days pass. Weeks pass. 

Around what the upper world calls November, the old female tastes the thinning of the sugar-film on the water. Slowly—two hours from ribs to pool, glass frogs riding the rise of her wrist without waking—her heavy arm slides out through the porridge of white crustaceans. Her broad, square-nailed thumb settles into the channel to clear the year’s moss-threads.

Halfway through the six-inch draw, the pad of her thumb meets a curve that was not there last year.

She stops.

For twenty minutes, nothing in the sinkhole moves except the microscopic *tink* of water droplets falling from spleenwort tips onto snail shells. Her heart beats sixty times during that pause. Across the basin, buried in the green sponge, the younger male and the cooling elder both feel the sudden stillness in her ankle against the shared rhododendron root—not alarm, because nothing sharp or hot has happened, just a sudden, dense narrowing of attention. Gently, questioningly, the younger male’s big toe presses once against her heel.

At the pool, her thick fingers uncurl an eighth of an inch per minute and drape themselves over the new stone. 

With skin dense enough with touch-corpuscles to read the grain of waterworn rock like Braille, she maps it. Not porous dolostone. Not soft soapstone. Not coarse river-granite. Cold, heavy, glassy, cryptocrystalline silica—whole, unfractured, heavy as a sleeping head, waiting under the water right where her hand already knows how to go. Beneath her palm, she feels the edges of three more tucked beside it in the silt. Enough sharp stone to feed the sweet slime for twenty thousand years.

She does not look up toward the juniper roof in wonder. She has no myth of sky-fathers, and if you gave her one, it would only make the ceiling feel thin. To her, the basin itself has always been generous and exact, giving water at forty-six degrees, taking bone into snail-shell, returning nitrate after summer thunder. That the floor should quietly bud fresh flint when the old cores wear down to plums does not strike her as a violation of nature; it confirms her deepest, wordless intuition that the stone she sleeps against is alive at the same speed she is.

Down beneath two feet of saturated cushion moss, her long toes tighten around the grafted root-stock in a slow, rolling chord—warm, full, heavy with quiet news—and hold it there for an hour. Fifteen feet away, the younger male answers, wrapping his sole firmer over hers. Across the room, even the ancient male, whose skin is forty-nine degrees and whose breaths are almost a minute apart, flexes his worn toes once in deep, drowsy assent, eased of a faint arithmetic he didn't know he was carrying before letting go. Tucked against her belly under four layers of braided salamanders, the loaf-sized twelve-year-old drinks a single drop of rich milk and sleeps on.

Tomorrow she will lift one of the old plum-sized cores and drop her wrist—*tock*—against the shoulder of the new black stone, shearing off a wide, pristine crescent that will bloom thicker with sugar-film than anything they have tasted in six lifetimes.

Outside, in the thin blue cold above the juniper mat, the Wise Power gathers its attention back out of the rock without disturbing a single spider's leg, slips silently over the new fallen spruce on the abandoned trail, and goes on its way—satisfied, as rare craftsmen are when they have repaired a watch without stopping its tick, to leave perfection entirely alone in the dark.
