A power that is merely benevolent might look upon the five-thousand-meter shelf and feel pity. Seeing the spindle-lizards heaving ninety times a minute for starved air, the pan-lice rasping iron-hard stone under a punishing sun, and the lone human trudging in a heavy, salt-smeared suit, a lesser kindness might be tempted to *fix* it. It might thicken the atmosphere, cool the magma sill, wash the syrupy lithium pans clean with sweet mountain rain, and turn the scalding basin into an alpine meadow of gentians and clear streams.

And in doing so, it would murder everything on the plateau before nightfall. 

A *wise* power looks down through the flat, tempered-steel blue of the zenith and recognizes a masterpiece of tension. It sees that the misery and the marvel are the exact same thing. Those chambered bellows-lungs, those pewter suction rims, the potash manhole covers of the ferric-worms, the rime-pikas straddling forty-five degrees and zero—all of it is a tightrope walk over an abyss, and every creature here has spent ten thousand generations learning to dance on the wire. Even the blackened iron wellheads and sweating copper conduits are no longer intruders; they have become organs of the shelf, casting essential ribbons of ink-black shade, dripping vital condensates into the gill-vaults of gasket-crabs, and shooting elevator plumes of steam up to the waiting vultures.

So the power folds its hands behind its back, so to speak, and resolves first to break nothing. It listens to the silence—that snow-deep, porous quiet punctuated by the *chuff* of an exhaust louver and the subterranean *thud* of a piston—and decides that love, here, must be practiced with a jeweler’s screwdriver and an eye on the centuries.

First, it looks beneath the bedrock.

Deep under the shelf, where the shallow magma sill presses its broad, incandescent shoulder against the stone, geological time is preparing an ending. Not today, perhaps not for thirty years, a fault-line on the eastern margin is creeping toward a brittle snap. If it slips all at once, two things will happen: the main geothermal pressure-manifold of the central basin will shear, bleeding the automated rigs dry in an afternoon of catastrophic steam-bursts, and the sill’s heat will divert three miles west into the deep glacier beds, leaving this crowded conservatory to freeze into a dead salt-flat within a decade. 

The power reaches down into the plastic, glowing dark of the mantle—not to cool it, not to increase it, simply to grease the fault. Over the course of months, micro-tremors too small even to clamp down the pan-lice bleed the tectonic stress away in harmless millimeters. It encourages a few new mineral veins to vitrify around the roots of the deepest wellheads, reinforcing the bedrock collars where the old iron casings go down. It keeps the furnace steady.

Next, it turns its attention to the machines, and to the turnkey who tends them.

It understands why the turnkey climbs through the southern notch alone every four months. Down in the valleys below the cloud-deck, the company or ministry that built these fractionating towers forty years ago has half-forgotten them, or folded into three successor bureaus, leaving the station on a dusty ledger line maintained by one stubborn specialist who knows the quirks of every governor and flywheel. And it sees what the turnkey does not yet know: that on Rig Four, near the center of the shelf, a cast-iron flywheel shaft forged half a century ago has developed an internal hairline fatigue fracture, invisible beneath its crust of yellow-white rime; and that in the turnkey’s own chest, forty-eight years of high-altitude shifts and poorly fitted early-career respirators have left a small, quiet stiffening in the right ventricle—nothing fatal at sea level, nothing that aches yet, unless one day a jammed bypass valve forces twenty extra minutes of wrench-heaving in forty-five-degree wet-bulb heat.

The power does not turn the turnkey twenty again, nor does it turn the rusted rigs into gleaming, self-repairing miracles. If the rigs stopped needing the brass wrench, the turnkey would have no reason to climb out of the clouds, and the turnkey loves this terrible, shimmering place with the fierce, wordless pride of a sole custodian. 

Instead, on a Tuesday three weeks before the scheduled autumn ascent, while the basin shimmers alone under the sun, a very slow diffusion occurs inside the iron lattice of Rig Four’s flywheel shaft. Stray carbon and trace nickel atoms migrate a fraction of a micron, knitting across the hidden fatigue plane until the metal at the core is sounder than the day it was poured in a lowland foundry. Around the basin, wherever galvanized wire mesh wraps a live steam joint, the rubber and lead gaskets beneath undergo a quiet cross-linking of polymers; they stop rotting in the acidic grease, though at the weeping, non-lethal drain-seams, a slightly richer variety of sulfur-reducing bacteria begins to bloom—so soft and savory that the gasket-crabs find themselves preferring the overflow troughs to the critical seals.

Then comes the morning of the climb.

Thirty minutes before dawn, the silver figure breaches the southern notch, emerging from the white sea of mountain clouds into the violent, frost-rimed boundary air. Everything looks as it always does. The rime-pikas watch with bright, dark eyes from the shattered basalt crevices, jaws pausing on tufts of wire-grass as the heavy boots crunch past.

Inside the reflective suit, however, the chilled water-tubes circulate with an uncanny, effortless efficiency today; the little impeller pump on the hip pack doesn't stutter once. As the turnkey steps down onto the first waxy sinter dome, breathing the dry hiss of the rebreather, something else happens—subtle as a sip of cool water. With every breath drawn inside the polycarbonate helmet, in the quiet dark of the chest, scarred microscopic capillaries around the right ventricle soften and branch. A tiny, chronic constriction in a pulmonary artery eases open like a well-oiled valve. By the time the sun clears the eastern lip and throws ink-sharp shadows across the white crusts, the turnkey feels a strange, buoyant steadiness in the legs—not superhuman strength, just the clean, unburdened stamina of a good morning twenty years ago. *Must have slept better at base camp than I thought,* the turnkey thinks, wiping a smear of salty grease off the visor with the back of a heavy glove, and walks on toward Rig One.

And because even a wise power has a heart—and perhaps a quiet sense of artistry—it leaves one gift that serves no survival purpose at all, meant purely for the eyes behind that polycarbonate mask.

It happens at Rig Four, just before nine in the morning, when the turnkey is usually tiredest and the wet-bulb heat is beginning to press like a hot iron against the suit’s insulation.

The turnkey kneels on the sinter beside the black iron wellhead, feeling the familiar, reassuring *thump* of the newly immortal flywheel coming up through the kneepads. Immediately, six spindle-lizards dart *tic-tic-tic* across the pewter backs of the pan-lice and crowd into the sharp, cool shadow of the silver torso, their barrel ribs pumping fast, yellow-horned brows tilted up toward the mirrored face. 

The turnkey sets the brass wrench onto the fouled drain cock and pulls. Usually, this valve fights back with fifty pounds of seized potash crust, demanding a wrenching heave that fogs the helmet and sends the heart hammering. Today, the mineral crust shears with a crisp, clean *snick* at the first lean of the shoulder. A gallon of hot, rust-colored sludge vents smoothly into the travertine saucer below.

As the turnkey pauses on one knee to catch the rhythm of the rig—listening through the bone of the boot-sole to the clean, singing stroke of the piston—a gasket-crab the size of a dinner plate eases halfway out from between two sweating copper fractionating pipes. It is an ancient specimen, its back grown into a miniature cathedral of yellow-white travertine and tiny, glistening lithium crystals. Unafraid, moving with ponderous, clockwork deliberation, it reaches its tiny spoon-claw into the fresh, steaming trickle two inches from the turnkey’s knee, scoops a dollop of bacterial jelly to its mouthparts, and settles completely still, letting the warm condensation drip onto its shell.

At the same moment, one of the spindle-lizards in the turnkey’s shadow steps right onto the toe-cap of the heavy boot—higher off the hot rock than usual—sneeze-clicks two tiny white pellets of salt onto the scuffed leather, and stays there, its throat pulsing, peering straight through the wiped clear streak on the visor into the human’s eyes.

Up in the steel-blue zenith, a thousand feet above the rising breath of the basin, the power watches the silver biped remain very still on one knee for two full minutes—longer than safety protocol strictly recommends, though today there is plenty of oxygen left in the tank, and the heart inside the suit beats slow, strong, and even. Around the boot, five hundred pewter shells pivot silently toward the warm spill; brine-flies skate in frantic, sunlit zigzags across the syrupy film; the copper fins tick quietly as they cool.

Satisfied, the turnkey gently slides the boot backward half an inch so the lizard can hop neatly down into the shade of the pipe cradle, shoulders the long brass wrench, and walks unhurriedly toward Rig Five. Above, three black rectangles tilt on locked wings in the thermal chimney, riding the warm, invisible pillar that holds up their sky, while the whole breathless conservatory keeps turning in the sun.
