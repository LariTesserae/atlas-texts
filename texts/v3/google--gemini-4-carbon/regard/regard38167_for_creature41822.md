A lesser power, looking down upon the fifteen-thousand-foot domes, would see suffering. It would see the pruned, scarred knuckles, the backs bent permanent by seventy-mile-an-hour sleet, the deafened ears, and the crushing, sunless grey. Moved by a clumsy pity, a lesser power might part the clouds, calm the gales, and warm the freezing sheet-flow into gentle, sunlit meadow-streams.

And in doing so, it would murder a world. The cushion plants would bake and rot; the suction-bellied trout would suffocate in the sluggish, deoxygenated warmth; the Pelton wheels would spin down into a terrifying, dead silence; and the tenders—stripped of their weight, their armor, and the roaring adversary that gives their broad shoulders meaning—would wander out into the quiet light like deep-sea fish hauled to the surface, ruptured by the sudden lack of pressure.

A wise power knows better. Looking through the horizontal walls of rain, this vast and quiet awareness does not see exile. It sees a marriage. 

It sees how the tenders lean into the gale not with resentment, but with the profound, physical trust of dancers catching a partner’s weight. It hears the baritone hum in the galley harmonized to the fifty-hertz floor, watches the foreheads press together in the foaming chutes, and notes the knuckle tapping *goodnight* into four miles of cold iron and rushing dark. It sees that these pale, heavy-shouldered beings have achieved the rarest grace in the universe: they have stopped trying to conquer their home and instead allowed it to rebuild them until they belong to it as wholly as the basalt and the eels.

To break the storm would be an insult to their strength. Yet love, even when wise enough to withhold interference, still wishes to give a gift. So the power looks closer—down past the battered domes, through the riveted steel plates, into the biology of blood and bone and the slow, grinding math of stone—searching for the tiny, hidden places where the bargain between the mountain and its keepers is unnecessarily cruel.

It makes four small, invisible edits to the world.

First, it reaches deep beneath the polished troughs, down into the dark, water-logged root of the massif where ancient fault lines groan under the weight of the endless deluge. Left strictly to blind physics, six years from now, a submerged granite shelf above Sector Four would have sheared loose in the night, sending a forty-foot wall of black rock and water down a narrow throat to tear Bunker Nine from its guy-wires while three tenders slept inside. The power lays a quiet finger upon the fault. Over the course of three weeks, silica-rich mineral waters seep into the microscopic fracture and crystallize with unnatural speed, knitting the deep stone back together with veins of milky quartz. On the surface, nothing happens at all. Not even a needle twitches on the brass-bezeled gauges. 

Second, it turns its attention to the heavy rubber-sheathed cables snaking under the rolling tarns, encrusted with rust-red moss. Into the blind, microscopic chemistry of that moss, the power whispers a slight mutation. Over the next two seasons, as the moss spreads across the submerged iron stanchions and creeps up the concrete aprons of the diversion weirs, it begins to excrete a faint, slick bio-resin. To the shrimp and the stanchion-eels, it tastes slightly sweeter. To the cast iron and braided steel beneath it, it acts as a living sacrificial anode—arresting the deep, insidious pit-rust where salt and oxygen eat at the anchor bolts, sparing some future worker the sudden, lethal snap of a frayed tether in a white-water boil. Furthermore, near the intake grates—where the mountain’s fury sucks water down toward the Pelton wheels at eighty miles an hour—a slightly altered strain of this moss takes hold, vibrating at a sharp, localized harmonic in the current that human instruments cannot detect, warning the heavy trout away from the intake pipes so fewer pale, pulverized bellies flash past the armored quartz sight-tubes at night.

Third, it considers the tenders themselves, asleep in their gimbaled bunks. It will not take away their scars, nor the broad, muscular bulk they need to walk the flooded saddles, nor even the loss of their upper hearing, which spares them the maddening shriek of the wind and leaves them cocooned in the warm bass of their machines. Instead, it touches the wild, cord-wooled ungulates wading outside in the dark, enriching the submerged sedge they eat with a subtle, complex lipid that concentrates in the grease of their heavy coats and the marrow of their bones. When the tenders harvest the wool to felt their inner linings, the lanolin that rubs off onto their permanently pruned, cracked fingertips will carry a quiet, regenerative warmth—soothing the deep ache of cold-water arthritis in their knuckles before it can stiffen them out of the life they love, and keeping the small capillaries in their extremities fierce and open against the plunge pools.

And fourth—because wisdom understands utility, while benevolence understands joy—the power leaves a signature, though none will ever know it as such.

Deep in the vanadium steel of the spanners, and along the miles of copper-threaded trunk lines stitched across the wet stone, it subtly refines the crystalline lattice of the metal. It changes nothing of the voltage, nothing of the resistance. It simply clears the acoustic throat of the grid.

The next night, when the grey slate of evening drops into roaring pitch-black, and the heavy iron dog-wheels are spun shut, the crew in Bunker Seven kills the overhead bulbs. A tender with granite dust scarred into her knuckles lies back on her narrow bunk and reaches out in the dark, pressing her bare palm flat against the humming steel bulkhead before tapping her customary two-knuckle *goodnight*.

Four miles away, through freezing sheets of horizontal sleet, beneath churning tarns wrapped in sleeping eels, past thrumming weirs and huddled flocks of oil-slicked birds, her neighbor in Bunker Six has his palm pressed to the wall, waiting.

When the *tink-tink* arrives under his calloused hand, he pauses, frowning slightly in the dark. 

Usually, at four miles, the signal is a dull, blurred ghost of a tap, nearly swallowed by the mountain's bass. Tonight, it rings against his palm as clear, warm, and distinct as if she were lying on the other side of the quarter-inch steel plate. He can feel the exact weight of her knuckle, the tiny, living hesitation between the first strike and the second. And beneath it—conducted cleanly down twenty miles of iron suture—he can suddenly feel Bunker Five, and beyond that, faint as a heartbeat in deep water, Bunker Four, all of them breathing together in the belly of the storm.

He smiles in the dark, presses his forehead briefly to the warm, vibrating steel, and taps back—three slow, resonant beats that travel effortlessly out into the roaring wet, holding the whole battered mountain in a net of quiet, unbroken touch.

Outside, the gale slams into the whale-backed granite at seventy miles an hour, sheeting silver and furious into the dark. The amber strobe flashes once, twice, swallowed twenty yards out by the mist. Satisfied, the great power withdraws its hand, leaving the rain to fall.
