To be benevolent without wisdom is to bring gifts that break the table they are laid upon. 

A lesser power, looking down into this crisp, shadow-striped basin, would feel an immediate itch to *enrich* it. It might think: *How lonely, to have no stars! Let us tilt the axis by five degrees so they may know twilight and astronomy.* And in doing so, it would turn every permanent ribbon of emerald marsh into dried peat within a month, starve the slow-moving grazers, blind half-sleeping minds accustomed to thermal division, and shatter the razor-sharp basalt with freeze-thaw cycles that would bring the dry-laid viaducts crashing into the ravines. 

Or a sentimental power might say: *Let us give them iron, or song, or a gentle breeze,* forgetting that wind brings dust to clog the condensation grooves, that song violates a silence calibrated to ankle-bone vibrations, and that iron rusts in perpetual dew, bleeding orange ruin across clean grey slate.

The Power that actually bends its attention upon this highland valley is neither sentimental nor restless. It has seen worlds built on fire and frantic seasonal terror; it knows how rare it is to find a civilization that has solved the equation of *enough*.

For a span of time that would outlast three empires elsewhere, the Power does nothing at all except watch. It rests its perception along the forty-degree slant of the sunlight, lightless and weightless, admiring the sheer courtesy of the place. It notes how the builders lift the copper-colored lizards onto their own warm shoulders rather than brushing them aside. It listens to the four-second *drip* in the great plaza and understands that these five hundred quiet masons are not trapped in stasis; they are performing a continuous, exquisite duet with physics. They are happy—not with the sharp, feverish joy of creatures fleetingly spared from death, but with the deep, resonant satisfaction of an arch resting cleanly on its piers.

Still, because the Power sees very far—down into the roots of the mountain and out into the slow nuclear heart of the pinned sun—it perceives the single hidden flaw in the basin’s eternity.

The rock of the valley is geologically young because the planet itself has not entirely finished settling. Deep beneath the unweathered slate and basalt—twelve miles down, where the crust meets the sluggish, cooling mantle—a fault line is locked under immense, silent strain. And far overhead, the tidal lock that holds this world’s face pinned at an exact angle to its star is subject to a microscopic, millennial libration—a gravitational wobble induced by a distant outer planet passing through a rare orbital resonance once every forty thousand revolutions.

Left strictly to nature, two things will eventually happen. 

First, within three or four generations of the builders—perhaps a thousand human years—that locked fault twelve miles down will slip all at once. In a world of river-rounded boulders and mortared walls, a major quake is a disaster; in a world of knife-edged scree slopes and unmortared, dry-laid corbeled arches, a sudden violent shear will liquefy the talus and drop every granary chimney and viaduct simultaneously, crushing the bone-courses of their ancestors and spilling the cisterns dry.

Second, the slow gravitational wobble is even now pulling the pinned sun upward by an arc-second every few centuries. That is why the builder in the plaza had to shave the eyelash-curl of green tarnish from the copper crescent. They *know*, in their patient, surveying way, that the shadow is shortening. For a few millennia more they can compensate by honing copper and raising parapets another course of slate. Eventually, however, the libration will push the basin’s ambient temperature half a degree past the dew point. The shadows will stop sweating. The valley will dry out cleanly and silently, leaving five hundred immaculate skeletons arranged on the western rim with no one left to carry their bones down into the arches.

The Power considers this. 

To sweep down and rebuild their world for them would rob the builders of their dignity, turning master surveyors into kept pets in a terrarium. Yet to let such rare, gentle attentiveness be erased by a blind spasm of crust and orbit seems a waste of cosmic grace.

So the Power acts—but only in the native grammar of the basin.

First, it reaches twelve miles down into the dark, stifling heat beneath the valley floor, laying a cool, patient finger along the locked tectonic seam. It does not weld the fault shut, for planetary tension prevented forever only breaks harder later. Instead, it turns a catastrophic snap into a creep. 

Once every thirty or forty days—at intervals irregular enough to belong to rock, gentle enough never to topple a single dry-laid stone—the Power eases the deep basalt past itself by the thickness of a hair. 

Up in the quiet city, standing at their resting-rails half in sun and half in mist, the builders feel it through the thick horn of their soles: a deep, sub-audible *thrummm*, lasting three seconds, softer than a throat-click, like the planet itself clearing its throat in friendly acknowledgment. Not a drop spills from the brimming cisterns. Only the concentric ring in the plaza’s shallow basin shivers with a sudden, exquisite geometric pattern—a standing wave of tiny hexagons—before smoothing out again before the next *drip*. The builders who are awake tilt their visored heads a fraction of an inch, look down at the flagstones with both lobes of their amber eyes, and send an answering *hmmm* back down through their heels into the stone.

Second, the Power addresses the sky, though it does not touch the sun or halt the distant planets in their dance. To freeze the cosmos would be vulgar, and besides—the builders *like* calibrating things. A surveyor whose plumb-line never needs checking eventually loses the joy of his thumb.

Instead, high on the jagged, unweathered western rim—two miles above the bone-tables, in a cleft of dark slate where no builder has ever needed to climb—the Power opens a single, microscopic artesian vent from a deep, ancient pocket of juvenile water trapped since the world’s formation, and lines its lip with a natural vein of native silver-copper alloy exposed cleanly along the rock’s cleavage plane.

As the slow millennial wobble pulls the sun imperceptibly higher and warms the high peaks by a fraction of a degree, that hidden cleft takes the extra heat first. The cold, ancient water seeping at a rate of drops per hour evaporates invisibly into the thin air along the western ridge, adding just enough imperceptible vapor to the highland atmosphere to raise the dew point at the exact rate the rising sun tries to outrun it. When, ten thousand years hence, the orbital resonance passes and the sun drifts fractionally downward again, the high cleft will cool, a skin of rime will seal the seep back to a trickle, and the air will thin in perfect, silent counter-balance.

The shadows in the city will still shift by half a thumb’s breadth over an age—just enough to give the watchers in the empty plazas reason to walk out into the glare once a century with an almond-sized basalt pebble and stroke a curl of green tarnish from a copper blade. Their vigilance remains true; their mathematics are never cheated; only the end of the world has been quietly removed from the far margin of the ledger.

That would be enough. Wisdom says stop there.

And yet, before withdrawing its attention to let them live out their quiet eons in peace, the Power permits itself one tiny, unnecessary gesture—a signature, or perhaps simply a neighborly greeting left on the doorstep.

Out at the eastern edge of the city, where the two builders recently laid their freshly split block of slate and planted liverwort spores in its brand-new forty-inch rectangle of damp shade, a small, flat shard of waste stone lies sunlit beside the aqueduct channel.

The Power touches a single crystal buried inside that discard flake—nothing foreign, nothing magical, only carbon and trace minerals native to the mountain, coaxed over a single quiet hour through a pressure-shift that rock usually takes ten million years to accomplish.

Days later, when the female mason walks back along the channel to inspect how the fern-bulbils are taking to the new condensation, her dual-pupiled eye catches a point of unfamiliar behavior right on the razor-line where the wall’s shadow meets the glare.

She stops. Over five minutes, she lowers herself to one knee.

Lying precisely astride the shadow-boundary is a thumb-sized prism of utterly clear, flawless mountain quartz—clearer than any stone yet cleaved in this young basin—grown naturally out of a split basalt matrix. Half of it sits in the forty-degree western sunlight; its eastern tip rests in the damp gloom of the wall.

Because of the exact angle of its natural facets, the crystal catches a sliver of the pale, unchanging sunlight, bends it across the razor-edge into the dark, wet corridor of ferns, and splits it onto the glistening black slate wall behind. 

In a world without rainstorms, without mist-bows, where light has only ever been stark pale gold and shadow has only ever been deep emerald-black, a quiet ribbon of pure spectrum—violet, indigo, blue, green, amber, and a deep, warm crimson no eye in this valley has ever beheld—hangs three inches long against the wet dark stone, trembling faintly whenever a bead of condensation slides behind it.

The builder does not startle. She remains on one knee for four hours, breathing slow and deep, her grey shoulder-filaments warming in the glare while her extended fingertips rest cool in the dampness beside the stone. 

Then: *Click-hmmm.*

Down the causeway, her companion feels the vibration in his ankles, turns slowly from a curing-tower vent, and walks over—taking twenty minutes to cross the court. When he arrives, he kneels beside her. Neither touches the crystal to move it; they see at once that to shift it by a millimeter would break the angle of the light.

Instead, over the next three days, working with tiny slivers of split slate and cold-beaten copper shims, the two of them build a miniature, dry-laid shrine-niche four inches high around the stone—leaving its western face open to the sun, sheltering its eastern prism from the careless footpad of any passing grazer, and carving a microscopic groove beneath it so a steady, rhythmic film of water glides perpetually under the projected rainbow, catching the violet and the crimson and reflecting them upward onto the undersides of the weeping cloud-tree leaves.

When it is finished, they stand together astride the shadow-line, one eye in the bright world, one eye on the little band of impossible color glowing safely in the dark. 

Satisfied, the Power withdraws into the pale, cloudless vault overhead, leaving the sun at forty degrees, the lizards half-warm on the parapets, and the city counting time, four seconds at a drop, in perfect peace.
