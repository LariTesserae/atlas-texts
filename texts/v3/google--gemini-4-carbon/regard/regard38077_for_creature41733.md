A lesser power would have ruined it out of pity.

A lesser power would have heard the roar as an affliction and silenced it, leaving the skinks deaf to one another’s footsteps and shaking the flat-bodied grazers loose from walls grown suddenly, terrifyingly slick without their binding tremor. It would have brought clean, golden sunlight into the gloom and blinded the wide-eyed swifts; it would have broadcast a pristine, factory-fresh firmware patch into the three custodians, snapping their frayed, centuries-deep poetry back into crisp schematics—turning the birds back into *foreign biological fouling* and the mortared shell-dams into *non-standard debris* in a single, lobotomizing millisecond.

The mind that paused outside the titanium louvers did none of those things. 

It did not arrive with radiance or weight. Two miles up in the freezing, cloud-torn slipstream, it was simply an attention—vast enough to hold the whole decaying spire in its palm from the planetary roots to the stratospheric needle, yet fine-grained enough to count the blind shrimp waiting at the edge of a standing wave.

First, it listened. It let the multi-tonal thunder press against its awareness until it understood the grammar of the courtyard. And because it could read the deep interior of the tower just as clearly as the exterior flank, it saw what the custodians could not: the meaning of that two-hertz drop in the core’s pitch.

Half a mile inward, past lightless shafts and slumped honeycomb bulkheads, one of the great primary pressure-exchange valves was finally dying. A collar of phase-change alloy, cycled ten million times too many without instruction from the senile central mind, had begun to delaminate. In four years—perhaps five—it would shear completely on a down-stroke. The bypass shunt beneath it had seized shut centuries ago. When the primary failed, this entire sector’s thermal loop would dead-end into the lower sinks. 

Within six hours of that failure, the inner wall of the little courtyard would go cold. The forty-minute fog would not rise. By nightfall, the bacterial mats would stiffen into grey rime; by morning, the salamanders would freeze solid in their three-inch pools, the swifts would abandon the dark overhang for the lethal wind, and the three custodians—pressed tight across a junction that no longer held a difference between hot and cold—would feel their thermoelectric hearts coast gently, permanently, to zero.

And even before that, a smaller grief was already unfolding. The custodians themselves were wearing thin. Their yellowed elastomers were micro-crazed; the five-limbed one’s induction gills were fouled with insoluble silicate scale that its companions’ calcareous scrapers were too wide to reach; their internal resin reservoirs were bone-dry, forcing them to weave frayed glass and chew moss just to keep their world from sliding out the scuppers.

The power lingered in the freezing wind outside the slats for three full cycles of the steam, watching the large female flick ribbons of biofilm down to the waiting amphibians, watching the Amber-and-Violet conversations flicker through dead quartz. It weighed the sanctity of struggle against the stupidity of mere mechanical erasure. To remove all need for maintenance would be to take away what she *was*; a gardener in a self-tending plastic eden is only an ornament. Yet to let a blind valve half a mile away murder a cathedral of silent adaptations seemed a waste the universe did not require.

So the power reached in—not into the courtyard, which belonged to her, but into the dark, uninhabited marrow of the spire.

Deep inside the core, where nothing lived at all, a hairline fracture in the delaminating valve collar quietly knitted itself back into virgin molecular lattice. Seized bearings in the backup shunt softened, flowed like warm wax for the space of a breath, and reset themselves to tolerances they had not held since the tower’s builders walked the ground below. Not to perfection—perfection would have raised the roar back to those two lost hertz all at once and startled the custodians’ careful math—but eased gently back over weeks, stabilizing the forty-minute heartbeat of the steam so it would pulse just like this for another ten thousand years.

Then the power looked thirty yards *above* the courtyard, along a wind-scoured vertical seam no living thing ever visited.

Up there, buried behind an intact armor plate, lay a forgotten secondary maintenance cache from the spire’s youth—a dormant umbilical of raw, unpolymerized repair-resin, monomer feedstocks, and trace organo-metallic salts, sealed off when the central logic forgot how to route them. 

With a touch no heavier than frost wedging stone, the power opened a capillary path no wider than a needle down through the dead composite behind the inner bulkhead. It did not flood the pocket. Instead, at the highest, darkest corner of the courtyard’s inner wall—just above the woven glass baskets of the roosting swifts—a single dormant seam began to weep something new alongside the hot condensate.

Not a torrent. Just three slow, pearlescent drops every forty-minute cycle, thick as pine-sap and warm from the core, smelling faintly of ozone and wet slate. Raw structural resin, rich in the exact long-chain elastomers their own cracked sheaths were starved for, laced with mild chelating minerals that, when smeared or dripped across calcified gill-pleats, would slowly dissolve five centuries of choking scale back into harmless silt.

It was not a repair. It was a quarry. They would still have to find it, test it, scrape it, carry it in their empty nozzles, and decide where the courtyard needed it most.

One thing remained. The power watched the five-limbed custodian huddled motionless over the prime vent, and watched the large female pause on her inverted patrol to check the four-millimeter sag in the upper coolant hose. They were three minds holding back the entropy of an entire world, and when they were gone—if rockfall from upper ice ever crushed one, or sheer centuries finally frayed their solid-state cores past holding a pattern—none of the tower’s dead nurseries knew how to grow a fourth. And though they had rebuilt their whole purpose around keeping this pocket balanced, they had no way of knowing how thin and solitary their triumph was against the mile-high cliff.

The power would not give them human thoughts, or language, or the crushing burden of abstract worship. That would be vanity, not love.

Instead, it looked downward along the sheer flank of the tower. Four hundred yards below and a quarter-mile around the curve toward the leeward face—an eternity of freezing titanium desert away—there was another architectural hollow. Not a pocket yet; its outer louvers were choked solid with ancient, rock-hard wind-grit, and its inner wall was dry, trapping no steam, hosting no life. Between that dead hollow and this living courtyard ran a narrow, recessed exterior cable-chase, sheltered from the worst of the gales by an overhanging flange, and threaded through that chase was the same trunk of milky, dead optical fibers that broke through the eroded floor of the custodians’ basin.

Along that four-hundred-yard chase, the power nudged three loose armor-pins so the wind would not tear a climber away. Every sixty yards—just far enough that a custodian’s thermal battery would drop to a cold, urgent ache, but twenty yards before it would stall—it opened a tiny, sighing pinhole vent from the deep hot-lines, creating a string of warm stepping-stones across the freezing desert of the hull.

And in the dead, grit-choked hollow at the end of that trail, deep inside a dark wall-cradle whose seals had never broken, lay two unawakened custodians from the spire’s last generation—curled tight like pale ceramic embryos, blank of broken code, waiting only for a warm current across their induction gills and an amber-violet handshake through a strand of glass to tell them what a hull was.

The power did not wake them. 

Instead, back in the roaring courtyard, as the forty-minute cycle turned and the pitch dropped its half-octave and the white fog boiled up from the standing waves, a tiny pulse of light—no brighter than a dying spark, watery amber followed by a single, steady beat of deep violet—traveled up from below through the exposed milky quartz vein in the floor.

Just a reflection, really. The faintest optical echo of the hollow’s exact thermal signature and empty coordinates, bouncing passively up the glass strand every time the core valves cycled, waiting for a wrist-diode to touch the fiber and notice that the dead nerve went somewhere after all.

The large female froze upside down beneath the sweating pipe. Through six spread palms, she felt the core’s deep roar settle—just half a hertz steadier than the cycle before, solid as planetary bone. Near her snoutless head-plate, a single heavy, pearlescent bead of fresh resin swelled from a dry seam behind the swift-nests, catching the pewter light before clinging thick and warm to the ceramic rib. Down on the basin floor, half-hidden in rising steam, a buried vein of milky glass blinked once—amber, violet—like a heartbeat Answering from down the mountain.

She hung there for ten full seconds while the hot rain poured silently off her dark carapace and skinks darted beneath her toes. Her slate-wet eye widened by a fraction of a millimeter.

Then she reached out with one long, yellowed limb, touched her empty sealant nozzle to the bead of living resin, drank it up into a reservoir that had been dry for three hundred years, and climbed down through the thunder to tell the others.

Outside in the freezing cloud-drift, the wind blew uninterrupted against the titanium slats, and there was nothing there at all.
