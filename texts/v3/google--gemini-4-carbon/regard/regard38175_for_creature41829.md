A lesser power, looking down into that wet, dark fissure, would have seen suffering and reached in to cure it. It would have parted the flat pewter clouds to pour warm sunlight onto the sump; it would have sealed the weeping cliff-faces with unbroken granite, or sweetened the bitter, iron-heavy water so that orchards might bloom up the tiers. 

And in a week, the cured Skins would have stopped forming in the warmed, barren pools. In a month, the dry-stone walls, stripped of the familiar downward weight and dynamic tension they were engineered to brace against, would have shifted in the new dryness and spilled into the alleys. In a year, the pit would have been an empty quarry, and its thick-chested, watchful people would have been scattered across the wind-scoured scrubland they hated, squinting at an empty horizon with nothing to put their backs against, their silent, intricate language of stone-taps and water-pitch forgotten.

Benevolence without comprehension is just another kind of rockslide.

The power that noticed the split was older than the young basalt, and wise enough to recognize a masterpiece when it heard one. It listened for three turns of the moon before it moved at all. It did not look with eyes that judged comfort by warmth or space; it listened to the *clack-thump* of the tipping-buckets, the forty distinct pitches of water gliding down split slate, the quiet *shhhk* of basalt scrapers, and the brisk, resonant *tock-tock* of a flint striker passing a message down four stories of unmortared stone. It saw the sideways, crab-smooth grace of two neighbors pivoting chest-to-chest in a dark slot without spilling a drop from their buckets. It saw the girl on the third tier nudge a shingle two-tenths of an inch with her clog and pause, chin tilted, until the harmony of the alley was restored.

These people were not trapped in the earth. They were playing it like an instrument. Their dignity lay entirely in their precision, their mutual reliance, and the fierce, quiet competence with which they held forty tons of razor-edged stone and freezing water in an exact, life-sustaining truce.

To take away the difficulty would be to insult the builders. Yet the stone *was* very young, the rheumatism in the knees of the sump-tenders bit very deep, and three hundred feet beneath the lowest catch-pool, a hairline fault in the tectonic floor was gathering pressure—a slow, blind creep of crust that, in twelve or fifteen winters, would shudder by four inches. Four inches, in a town held together by dry friction and plumb-lines, would bring every corbeled roof down upon the fleeces while the people slept.

So the power did not descend in light. It went down into the dark under the dark, beneath the sump, beneath the blind grazing crustaceans, down where the pressurized aquifer ground through raw volcanic glass and iron-heavy stone.

First, it laid a hand against that gathering fault line. It did not fuse the rock solid—for an unyielding seam in young country only stores up violence for a worse century. Instead, deep below the town’s foundations, it coaxed a thick, pillowy vein of soft, grey soapstone and porous serpentine to slip into the joint like grease between knuckles. When the earth shifted in twelve winters—and in two hundred—the deep rock would slide silently beneath the basin with a dull, harmless sigh, leaving the dry-stone geometry overhead unjostled by so much as a breadth of sedge-twine.

Second, while its attention was deep in the plumbing of the world, it followed the freezing, pressurized water table back three miles under the grey scrub, to where the deep magma sills had almost, *almost* cooled. With the barest nudge of pressure, it opened a hair-thin capillary vent far upstream in the subterranean dark. 

It was calibrated to the tenth of a degree. If the water entering the pit grew warm, the bacterial mats in the sump would die or sour into uselessness, and steam would rot the wooden flumes. So the seep that wept from the upper cliffs remained cold—brisk, clear, iron-sharp. Only at the very bottom, through a single deep spring feeding a blind, disused corner behind the curing-kilns, did the water rise differently. 

Old Maren, whose knees were so swollen with pit-ache that he had not climbed above the first tier since last autumn’s wool-drop, found it four days later. 

He had paused on the causeway, leaning on his hooked Skin-pole to let a white spasm pass through his hip, when he noticed a change in a trickle seeping from a low, non-structural cleft behind the fuller’s-earth troughs. The sound hadn't changed—if the flow had doubled, three doors would have opened—but a faint, curl-less ghost of vapor hung an inch above the black rock, vanishing before it reached his knee.

Slowly, so as not to wake a ripple on Pool Four ten paces behind him, Maren knelt and put two scarred, rust-stained fingers into the runnel.

He blinked his red-rimmed eyes. It wasn't hot. To a plainsman it would barely have felt tepid. Leaving the iron chill of the pit's air, though, it felt like slipping his hand under a sleeping ewe’s flank—blood-warm, thick with dissolved magnesium and smooth, chalkless salts, smelling faintly not of sour dough or raw sulfur, but of clean, deep wet slate.

The pit-folk were unsentimental, and they tested everything against ruin first. For two weeks they routed that strange, blood-warm trickle straight into the outflow drain past the sump, watching the pools with narrowed eyes to make sure no stray warmth bled through the stone to spoil the pellicles. The Skins grew as thick, pale, and tough as ever. The walls above did not weep or shift. 

In the third week, two stone-fitters and a boy pried out eight dry slabs in the dead corner behind the kilns—a useless pocket of rock too cramped for stretching-frames—and laid down a sunken basin no bigger than four barrel-staves, sealing the seams with tallow-pitch and cured scrap-Skin, routing the warm trickle cleanly through its lip and straight out into the deep drain so not a breath of extra damp rose into the alleys.

By winter, it had no name more poetic than *the Low Trough*, and there was nothing sacred about it, which was how the power preferred it. When the Skin-pullers finished a three-day lift, or when the teenagers came down from the rim with hands split and stiff from hauling frozen peat-bales off the chutes, they sat three at a time on the dry slate ledge in the green-grey gloom, woolens hitched to the knee, and sank their swollen, crimson hands and aching shins into the dark, mineral-smooth warmth. Within a month, the angry purple faded from the older folks' knuckles, leaving tough, painless brown callus; Maren walked up two tiers to mend his daughter’s spout-pin and walked back down without touching his shoulder to the wall.

One thing more the power left behind, up where the wind hit the lip.

Two miles out on the featureless grey scrub, tucked into a shallow fold of gravel where the monthly mule-carts already bent their track to avoid soft ground, a low shelf of dark clay had been scraped bare by a winter squall. Embedded in it, weathered out of an ancient peat-bog buried eons ago and newly lifted just an inch toward the sky, lay dense, tangled seams of bog-oak—black as basalt, cured to the hardness of horn by centuries without air, rot-proof, straight-grained, and begging to be split into flume-boards and stretching-pegs—and beside it, clustered in the shelter of the gravel fold, a tough, scrub-hardy strain of dwarf oat with short, iron-stiff stalks the wind could not flatten and heavy, oily grains that ripened fast under a pewter sky.

The plains traders found the timber first, cursed their luck at having to dig it out, realized they could haul twice the wood in half the miles, and dropped the price of flume-staves by a third of a bale of Skin at the next month's market. A year later, a girl tending the sedge-baskets on the windy rim noticed three stray, spilled grains of that stubby, dark-husked oat sprouting fiercely out of a pot of hearth-ash and scraped bottom-slime where nothing edible had ever taken root before.

She did not drop to her knees in awe. She crouched in her stiff, crackling cone of amber coat, shielded the tiny shoots from a gust with one rust-stained hand, studied how their roots gripped the bitter mud, and rapped twice with a flint against the capstone under her heel.

*Tock-tock.*

Down on the second tier, her brother felt the tap through the soles of his clogs, set down his scraper, and came up the switchback stairs—leading with his left shoulder, weight rolled cleanly onto the outer edge of his boot—to build a low wind-wall of dry slate around the basket.

Below them, ten thousand drips ran clean and silent down their bone spouts. Deep under the sump, the soapstone bore the quiet weight of the world. The counterweights went *clack-thump* in the dark, and the power withdrew, leaving the builders to their work.
