When the Power turned its attention to the upland plateau, it did not descend. Descending displaces air, casts shadows, and brings haste into places built on slowness. Instead, its awareness rested lightly upon the chalk collar, looking down into the bright, humid cup the way a careful reader looks at an illuminated page without breathing on the gold leaf.

It took in the forty paces of stone and water in a single, comprehensive stillness.

It saw the warm, invisible blanket of heavy gas keeping the frantic world at bay. It saw the solitary, swollen mosses drinking the air, the rubbery palms parceling out their slow, aerating drops, and the melon-bulbs of the ferns bending down under their own sweet weight to lens the noon sun. Beneath the water, it saw the hyaline aprons of the six ancient sleepers—refugees of a vanished tropical sea, holding onto warmth in the bones of a cold mountain, laying down aragonite grain by grain, digesting the windfall of the sky, sweeping the black silt neatly into the seams between their lives.

And it saw the ghost of the bare feet. The polite copper boundary. The four wooden blades turning quietly into seamless, living jade beneath the focused rings of light. 

A lesser benevolence feels an itch to improve what it loves. It sees fragility and wants to build a fortress; it sees patience and wants to hurry the reward; it sees a blind, ancient creature and wants to wake it into thought, or sees a clever toolmaker and wants to hand them steel. 

Wisdom knows better. Wisdom looks down through the stone to see how long the quiet actually has left.

Looking deep beneath the sinkhole, past the chalk and the limestone, the Power traced the plumbing of the mountain. Two miles down, a blind fault line was creeping tighter under the weight of the rising range. In forty years—the blink of an eye to the sleepers, scarcely two generations to the barefoot visitors—a minor winter tremor would shift a slab of deep shale three inches to the east. No rock would fall inside the sinkhole. Not a single palm frond would tremble. Simply, silently, the warm mineral spring at the center of the floor would lose its pressure, find a lower crack in the dark, and bleed away inside the mountain.

Within three weeks of that silent shift, the heavy gas would thin and blow out over the rim. Within two months, the high-country frost would pour down over the chalk lip like cold iron. The hyaline aprons would turn milky, crack, and peel from the dry travertine; the bladder-ferns would burst with ice; and when the barefoot visitor climbed up in the spring, they would find only a bowl of dead, flaking chalk and four half-finished wooden paddles rotting in dry mud.

To stop the mountain from rising would shatter valleys ten leagues away. Everything moves; nothing stays forever. Even an ancient reef-remnant perched near the clouds must eventually yield to the sky. 

Yet forty years was too abrupt an ending for a conversation conducted with such impeccable manners.

So the Power acted—not inside the sinkhole, where everything was already whole, but deep in the dark beneath it, and just a fraction of an inch along the wind-scoured upland outside.

Two miles underground, where the deep fault pressed dry shale against porous limestone, the Power did not lock the rock in place. Instead, it softened a single ribbon of mineral clay along a parallel seam, coaxing a deeper, broader vein of geothermal water—one rich in dissolved calcium and gentle heat—to marry into the existing conduit. When the tremor came forty years hence, the throat of the spring would not pinch shut; it would slip smoothly sideways into a wider channel, buying the warm cup another four thousand years of uninterrupted breath before the slow erosion of the plateau itself finally wore the chalk collar down to the level of the wind.

And for that far-off millennium when this bowl *would* eventually open and cool, the Power looked a quarter-mile south across the barren ridge.

There, hidden beneath coarse turf and frost-shattered scree, lay another karst hollow—deeper, narrower, currently choked with dry rubble, sitting directly over the new sibling vein of warm water. Left to chance, it would collapse inward and seal itself shut in a century. With a touch lighter than frost-heave, the Power loosened three keystones in its subterranean throat. Over the next three hundred years, warm groundwater would begin to sweat up into that second hollow, dissolving the scree, pooling heavy gas, melting the sharp edges into slumped, pale wax. Long before the first basin grew cold, a second warm cup would be waiting just across the ridge.

The sleepers could never cross dry, freezing stone on their own. They did not need to. The Power knew the patience of the barefoot lineages who climbed this mountain. Centuries from now, when a distant descendant noticed a new plume of warm steam rising from a fresh sinkhole across the ridge on a winter morning, and looked down into its empty, sterile pans of clear water, they would know what belonged there. They would know how to carry a piece of black silt, a heavy fern-bulb, and perhaps—when one of the great sleepers walled itself in two—how to cradle a calcified, sheep-sized stone in wet hides for four hundred paces across the wind.

Only one small thing did the Power place within reach of the living generation.

Half a mile down the cold trail where the toolmaker walked, wedged among loose grey slate where boots were laced back on, a single fist-sized cobble of pale green nephrite rolled slightly out of a frost-crack, coming to rest right beside the faint footpath. It was no better than the tools growing in the sinkhole, and needed no magic—only a stone dense and smooth enough to replace the river-cobble inside the basin when that old hammerstone eventually wore down, saving the visitor from ever bringing a rougher, iron-stained rock over the chalk lip by mistake.

Inside the sinkhole, nothing changed at all.

The noon sun stood directly overhead, pouring white light into the shadowless bowl. Through the four swelling bulbs of the bladder-ferns, four bright, hot rings of light wobbled gently over the pearlescent swellings of the submerged paddles. 

High on an overarching palm frond, moisture gathered from the warm, heavy air, swelled into a clear bead, hung for a long moment at the waxy tip, and let go.

*Thuck.*

A single ring slid outward across the mirror-still pan, washing over the pale, slumped shoulder at the wall. Slowly, without sound, the narrow horizontal seam parted, drank the small gift of air, and eased shut again.

The Power withdrew its gaze, leaving the stone, the sleepers, and their polite guests to finish their work in peace.
