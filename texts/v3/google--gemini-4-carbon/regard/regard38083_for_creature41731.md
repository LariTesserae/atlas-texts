For a very long time—longer than the lifespan of the thumb-sized hydrofoil fish, though short beside the age of the matriarch—the power does nothing at all. 

It only watches. It listens to the *thrum-clack* of the blind engines behind the bulkhead and counts the forty-second pulse until the rhythm is not something observed from outside, but a measure it holds in its own stillness.

A lesser benevolence would have acted within the first hour, and every instinct of a lesser benevolence would have been fatal.

Pity, looking through the bowed green glass at a sixty-pound creature pinned between roaring iron and five miles of empty air, would have seen a prisoner. Pity would have lifted her out of the spire and set her down in a wide, clean lowland river—where her iron-weighted bones would have dragged her straight to the gravel like an anvil, where the cold, un-carbonated water would have starved her gills for lack of a thirty-second vertical blast to ram oxygen across her arches, and where her broad gasket-belly would have found nothing smooth enough to grip. She would have drowned in quietness before nightfall.

Vanity would have looked at her hanging flat against the chilled glass, her horizontal iron-slit pupils turned toward the blinding white cloud-floor miles below, and decided she lacked a soul. Vanity would have kindled human-shaped thought behind those heavy, bronze-iridescent brow ridges—teaching her the concepts of *distance*, and *elsewhere*, and *cage*, turning a room of complete sensory coherence into a cell she could never leave.

Generosity would have hushed the violent machinery, or widened the room tenfold, or washed away the invisible hormonal lock steeping the thirty-three thousand gallons—allowing the chartreuse juveniles on the ladder rungs and the needle-fry in the fish schools to mature all at once, stripping the ribbon-kelp to bare metal in a single season until the whole green column collapsed into grey, starving rot.

Wisdom sees none of those errors as mercies. Wisdom sees that she is no longer an exile from some ancestral riverbed below the clouds. Centuries of warm dissolved metal and forty-second thunder have forged a sovereign creature fitted to her world as cleanly as a piston to its cylinder. When she hangs against the cold exterior glass during the ten-second hush, she is not mourning the sky; she is bleeding two degrees of core-heat out through her ventral pad while her heart beats three slow strokes in perfect equilibrium. Nothing in her is broken. Nothing in her asks to be remade.

What draws the power’s hand, at last, is what she cannot see: that her ocean is geological in behavior, yet mortal in construction.

A limestone cavern or a thermal vent on an ocean floor endures for millions of years. A sight-gauge bolted to the flank of an atmospheric condenser does not. Looking past the waving emerald ribbons and the flashing sheets of small fish, looking with eyes that read stress-lines in crystal lattices and metal fatigue in dark places, the power counts three clocks running out against her.

The first is thirty-two feet up on the exterior wall. For centuries, twenty-seven pounds per square inch of warm water have pressed the four-inch panes of greenish glass outward against thin, freezing air. To keep the vibrating iron frame from shattering the panes, the long-dead builders seated the glass in thick cushions of soft lead. Under relentless load and forty-second concussions, the lead along the lower left mullion has cold-flowed downward by fractions of a millimeter a decade, squeezing thin until one corner of the massive pane rests within the breadth of a human hair of bare, shuddering cast iron. Inside the glass itself, baked by brutal upper-troposphere ultraviolet and chilled by night gales, an invisible star-fracture four millimeters wide has already begun to branch. Eleven winters from now, during a high-shear storm, glass will kiss iron on an engine stroke. The pane will blow outward into the void. Thirty-three thousand gallons of warm green sea will flash into freezing mist across five miles of sky in less than six seconds.

The second clock ticks in the scalding dark beneath the floor grates. The bronze pipe’s forty-second plume is governed by an accumulator chamber and an ancient electromagnetic solenoid valve. Every cycle, gas gathers beneath a diaphragm until a pressure switch closes a circuit, snapping the heavy valve open; thirty seconds later, spent pressure lets a brass return-spring slam it shut. Nobody has oiled or inspected that assembly in four hundred years. The cloth-and-varnish insulation on the solenoid’s copper windings has baked into conductive black char. Worse, the brass clevis-pin holding the return-spring has worn nearly through its own throat—a bright, frantic groove chewed deeper every forty seconds. Four thousand cycles left, perhaps five thousand—less than three days, or at most a few months—before the pin shears, the coil shorts to ground, and the bronze throat locks shut forever. Without the plume, the kelp will tangle and rot, the temperature will stratify into scalding bottom-water and freezing top-water, and the matriarch, waiting motionless on the bulkhead for a breath that never rises to meet her propped jaws, will suffocate on the iron.

The third clock is slower, written in blood rather than brass. Ten individuals in one sealed column—one heavy matriarch, two ten-inch juveniles suspended in the fern tiers, seven needle-fry darting among the hydrofoil fish—are an exact caloric answer to the room’s size, but over centuries they are a narrowing corridor. Every generation draws from the same dwindling handful of alleles. Already, two of the seven fry carry a slight malformation of the gill-arches; in another two hundred years, even if the glass holds and the valve beats, the lineage will thin into sterility and quietly wink out, leaving the chamber to choke itself blind with ungrazed kelp.

So the power sets to work—not like a god breaking the sky, but like water depositing mineral, slower than anything in the tank knows how to fear.

It begins with the dying valve in the pitch-black sump beneath the grates, because that clock is shortest.

It does not replace the machine with anything foreign. Instead, across three nights, between one *boom* and the next, stray copper and tin and trace platinum dissolved in the spire’s miles of circulating condensate are coaxed atom by atom out of the scalding mist. In the dark, the charred windings vitrify into pure, heat-proof mineral ceramic glaze that can never short. The chewed brass clevis-pin fills back in with dense, self-lubricating phosphor-bronze hardened with interstitial carbon.

Yet wires are still mortal things, hostage to distant generators inside the spire’s core. Looking deeper into the accumulator’s geometry, the power sees how the builders almost—but not quite—made the valve self-sustaining. Slowly, delicately, using only thermal creep and micro-erosion from the hissing steam itself, it reshapes a dead bypass channel beside the solenoid into a purely fluidic pilot-latch: a counterweighted bronze rocker driven directly by the pressure differential of the spire’s endless atmospheric condensation. So long as the planet has air and sun to drive temperature gradients up the five-mile stack, gas pressure alone will trip the latch open at forty seconds and drop it shut thirty seconds later, even if every dynamo in the core goes cold ten thousand years from now.

One detail receives exquisite care. Two seconds before the main valve throws, when the matriarch is clamped low on the iron bulkhead, she relies on that faint preliminary *click* of hydraulic gathering behind the wall to peel the front lip of her suction pad loose. As the new fluidic rocker takes over the load from the aging electrical coil, the power tunes the seat of the pilot-latch micron by micron across twenty cycles—watching her pale ventral disc above the grates—adjusting the tension until the preliminary *click* lands at precisely two point zero seconds before the *boom*, at the exact pitch and timber her lateral line has trusted since she was ten inches long. She peels her lip free. The silver plume roars upward. She spreads her cheek-skirts and rises effortlessly through the emerald ribbons, never knowing that beneath her tail, mortality just slipped off the clock.

Next, the glass.

All through the following winter, whenever night falls across the upper troposphere and the matriarch settles against the hot inner bulkhead to sleep in the ram-current, tiny invisible migrations occur along the exterior frame. Lead that had cold-flowed downward over centuries creeps quietly back up into the starved cushions, cross-linking as it moves with trace antimony and tin drawn from old solder joints until it forms a supple, creep-proof alloy seat that cushions every engine shudder without yielding. Inside the four-inch greenish panes, the sub-millimeter star-fracture draws dissolved silica out of the tank’s own water; crystal bonds knit shut across the flaw, annealing internal stresses until the bowed panes are no longer brittle industrial glass, but something closer to clear, fibrous nephrite—tough enough to take a meteoroid graze or ten millennia of stratospheric freezes without crazing. Even the faint, curving tracks of the hexagonal bolt-mimic snails across the inner surface are left untouched.

That leaves only the third clock: the loneliness of the gene pool, and the closed horizon of thirty-three thousand gallons.

The power pulls its attention outward through the four-inch glass into the thin, freezing blue, moving laterally along the black, riveted curve of the spire at twenty-eight thousand feet.

The builders did not put a single sight-gauge on a condenser five miles tall. This elevation ring marks a manifold deck where twelve colossal vertical risers equalize pressure around the spire’s circumference. Every thirty-five feet along the curve of the black hull sits an identical forty-by-twelve-foot bay of leaded glass.

Most are long dead. On the windward northwestern face, three bays shattered centuries ago; high-altitude wind whistles dry and freezing through empty iron ribs crusted with rime. Others on the shaded northern arc flooded with cold, sterile condensate that froze solid from the glass inward, splitting their frames.

But curving around the sunlit southeastern flank, warmed by the same roaring core engines and bathed in the same blinding upper-air glare, three sister chambers still hold water.

Forty feet to the east, separated from the matriarch’s world by a curve of riveted iron bulkhead, hangs an intact thirty-three-thousand-gallon column. Centuries ago, fish-eggs and kelp-spores and hexagonal snails rode the same priming water into it—all except the ancestral bottom-sucker. Without the heavy grazer’s iron-rimmed jaws, the chamber choked itself fifty years after the builders left. Now it is a dense, dim, suffocating jungle of senescent ribbon-kelp packed from grates to dome, its bottom pipe coughing weak, erratic bubbles through a half-clogged throat, its thumb-fish stunted and scarce in the tangled gloom. Thirty-five feet beyond *that* hangs a second drowned bay, clear and bright, full of ferns and snapping white-and-rust crustaceans, waiting empty at the top of its food web.

And running horizontally behind the inner bulkhead at the twenty-two-foot mark—right behind the rusted ladder rungs where the matriarch’s chartreuse subordinates hide—is a four-inch cast-bronze cross-equalizer pipe built to connect those sunlit bays in emergencies. Four hundred years ago, a maintenance crew spun its heavy manual gate-valve nearly shut before descending the spire for the last time, leaving a dry, calcified plug of iron scale sealing the final inch.

Here, the power pauses longest, weighing every consequence.

If it throws that cross-pipe wide open, two chambers become one diluted tank. Water and scent will surge freely between them; the matriarch’s chemical signature will wash next door and lock the neighboring bay under her hormonal brake, or worse, should two four-foot females ever meet through a wide breach, they will batter each other with magnetite jaws in narrow water until one drops dead onto the grates. Her room’s taut perfection depends on being sovereign and self-contained.

So the pipe must not be a doorway for queens, nor a river for currents.

Over two full years, working at the speed of natural corrosion, the power coaxes warm acidic condensate against the calcified gate-valve inside the dark bronze cross-pipe. Grain by grain, the mineral plug dissolves. Slowly, the seized bronze gate creeps upward—not all the way, stopping cleanly at three and a quarter inches of vertical clearance, scalloped smoothly along its lower lip so no sharp edge can tear scaleless skin. Midway along the thirty-five-foot pipe run, where an expansion U-bend dips behind the warm engine casing, mineral baffles grow inward from the walls like staggered ribs, breaking any bulk pressure surge between the two rooms into lazy, harmless eddies.

In the sister bay next door, the clogged bronze throat beneath the grates clears itself on an engine stroke, coughing loose centuries of black scale, and settles into a clean, roaring forty-second plume—offset by nineteen seconds from the matriarch’s pipe so the two rooms breathe in alternating cadence, neither stealing pressure from the other.

Back in the home chamber, hidden deep behind the second tier of broadleaf chartreuse ferns at twenty-two feet, a rusted collar that has been a blind pocket since before the matriarch hatched begins to breathe a thread-thin, cool draft of unfamiliar water during the ten-second hush.

Nothing dramatic happens. No current changes. The matriarch’s hormonal signature stays trapped inside her own roaring convection loop, breaking down in the dark baffled pipe long before it can reach the other side. As for the opening itself—three and a quarter inches high by four inches wide—her broad, foot-wide skull and heavy shoulders could no more enter it than an ox could pass through a keyhole. She passes ten feet below it every day, shearing kelp holdfasts and crunching bolt-snails, never giving the fern-shadowed collar a glance.

For six months, only hexagonal snails and a few curious hydrofoil fish slip through the dark, humming bronze tunnel between worlds.

Then, on a bright afternoon when the high cloud-floor shines like hammered tin, the matriarch drops down after grazing and raps her mineralized jaw sharply against the eroded brass manifold to warn back the larger of the two juveniles—a lean, nine-inch individual blotched chartreuse and copper-red who has clung to the middle ladder rungs in suspended youth for nineteen years.

*Tink. Tink-tink.*

Up in the ferns, the subordinate flinches and hitches backward around the rung—right into the cool, faint scent leaking out of the shadowed bronze collar: the rich, unbrowsed smell of forty vertical feet of sunlit kelp with not a drop of the old queen’s chemical lock in it.

The juvenile hesitates across three forty-second booms. On the fourth hush, it slips its narrow head into the smooth bronze throat, locks its small suction pad onto the metal, and hitches out of sight into the humming dark behind the bulkhead.

Within weeks, back in the first chamber, the largest of the transparent needle-fry peels out of the shimmering school of hydrofoil fish along the glass, settles onto the vacant rusted ladder rung, and begins to mottle chartreuse and copper-red to fill the empty slot on the shelf. Everything in the matriarch’s room remains balanced to the ounce.

Thirty-five feet away around the curve of the spire, emerging into the dim, tangled sister bay, the emigrant wakes up. Freed for the first time in nineteen years from the chemical brake, its appetite catches fire. Its narrow skull broadens; iron begins to blacken the crushing plates of its jaws; its bright juvenile blotches darken toward slate and tarnished brass. Day after day, riding a newly vigorous bubble plume, it shears tunnels through the suffocating hundred-year mats of old kelp, letting shafts of pale-gold troposphere light lance down to the smoothed iron floor again, waking dormant fern spores on the ladder rungs, opening clear vertical highways for the stunted fish to school and flash.

Two bays further east, even slower, another calcified gate-valve in the cross-pipe begins to weep a hairline thread of water, preparing a third sunlit column for decades hence—and years after that, when juveniles and fry occasionally hitch both ways through the dark baffled pipes along the hull, fresh combinations of blood will circulate quietly across an archipelago of hanging seas at twenty-eight thousand feet.

Having done that much, and not a millimeter more, the power withdraws its hand.

Four years later, the core engines deep inside the spire hit a heavy afternoon load. Heat pours fiercely through the smoothed rivets of the back bulkhead.

Near the bottom grates, the old matriarch—heavier by three pounds now, a fresh white scratch across one bronze brow ridge from a snapping crustacean—waits on the kidney-shaped iron valve housing. Behind the wall comes the faint, faithful preliminary *click*. Two seconds early, she peels the front lip of her pale gasket-belly off the iron.

*Boom.*

The bronze pipe coughs silver. She flares her thick tail-fin and wide cheek-skirts, catches the roaring inverted waterfall square beneath her throat, and lets it haul her halfway up the forty-foot column through streaming banners of emerald kelp. At twenty feet, she banks out of the warm boil, slips through the cold downdraft, and plants her broad underside flat against the center of the bowed exterior glass just as the gas cuts off.

The ten-second hush drops over the room.

Pressed against the cool, annealed pane—tough as nephrite now, seated in lead that will outlast the mountains hidden beneath the clouds—she bleeds the excess engine-heat out of her blood. Her heart slows: one heavy beat, two, three. Around the grooved rim of her suction pad, hexagonal snails graze in slow, unhurried spirals. Behind her back, emerald fronds drift lazily downward through golden water, and fine rust-snow settles toward the grates. Before her pinned, horizontal pupils lies the vast, motionless plain of upper-troposphere clouds, blinding white under thin blue sky, severed from everything wet and living.

At the fifth second of the hush, a sound travels through the chamber.

It does not come from above her in the chartreuse ferns, nor from below her in the steam-pipes. It arrives horizontally, ringing clean and bell-like through thirty-five feet of solid riveted iron hull and four inches of water—the unmistakable, heavy strike of a fully mineralized adult jaw-plate rapping against a distant brass manifold.

*Tink. Tink-tink.*

Pinned against the sky, the matriarch goes utterly still. Her horizontal iron pupils widen a fraction beneath their bronze hoods; the fleshy rims of her clamped opercula flare once against the glass as her lateral line reads the weight, the distance, the unmistakable bulk behind that strike. Not a subordinate. Not an echo. Another sovereign, thirty-five feet through the iron, claiming another vertical sea.

She does not panic, and she does not search the glass for a way through. Before the tenth second ends, she peels her pad cleanly off the pane, drops like a heavy wrench through twenty feet of clear golden water, and slaps down onto her own curved iron boulder beside the bottom pipe just as the valve slams open and silver bubbles roar past her flanks.

Forty seconds later, when the next hush falls across her room, she lifts her broad, slate-dark chin half an inch and drives her magnetite jaw hard against the polished yellow lip of her own brass intake manifold.

*Tink.*

Clear and sharp over the *thrum-clack* of the eternal engines, the note rings out through water and iron—*here, mine*—and thirty-five feet away along the sunlit flank of the spire, another room listens in the ten-second quiet before the bubbles rise again.
