First, the power looks for a long time without touching anything at all.

To a lesser benevolence, this trench would look like a wound in need of bandaging, or a prison begging to be cracked open to the sun. A lesser mercy would part the yard-thick roof of moss and pour golden afternoon down into the bruised twilight. It would leave neatly stacked billets of dry ash-wood beside the root-jars, kindle a smokeless coal in the dark bedrooms, lay knives of blue, unrusting steel along the greased-leather thoroughfares, and raise the air in the lanes by fifteen generous degrees so the verglas melted for good and the children could unclench their toes.

A wise power sees the murder inside every one of those gifts.

Raise the air in this sunken lung by fifteen degrees, and the roof-snow collapses in a drowning slush-flood; the panic-adapted willow-pines wake into a blind, violent frenzy of summer growth that crushes the hollow burls and chokes the alleys shut before four hundred pairs of hands can pinch the buds; the slow, sour ferment in the pitch-sealed jars turns overnight into putrefaction; lowland molds bloom in the lungs of a people bred for sterile cold. Give them steel blades, and clean slicing replaces the bruising pinch of horn-thumbs—the bark bleeds thin instead of thickening into load-bearing callus, and the arches weaken within a generation. Give them a vision of sunlit meadows—or even let them know they are watched from above—and you plant an incurable homesickness for a world that would kill them, teaching a people who are the bloodstream of their own house to despise the dark that keeps them alive.

This place is not a tragedy. It is a triumph of ferocious, patient intelligence, four centuries deep. To love a glacier, you do not bring it summer.

So the power folds its hands behind its back, lowers its gaze to the scale of root-hairs and crystal lattices, and asks a quieter question: *What here is hurting them for no reason? What blind accident of stone or chemistry is waiting to undo everything their thumbs have built?*

It finds three things.

---

The first lies two miles straight down beneath the root-vaults.

The ridge is geologically young—freshly shattered, sharp-edged, still settling from the violent uplift that split the trench open in the first place. Deep under the mountain’s shoulder, along the same fault plane that cradles the settlement, two massive slabs of granite are locked together on a jagged spur of quartz, storing the strain of the rising range like a drawn bowstring. 

To the mountain, what is coming in forty-odd winters is nothing: a minor adjustment, a half-second shrug. The hanging wall of the cleft will slip six inches inward and four inches down. 

On the surface, it will barely shake a cornice loose. Inside the trench, six inches of sudden shear will snap every horizontal street of fused trunks at the cliff-join, burst the pitch-lined ferment-jars, collapse the weeping ventilation shafts, and crush the sleeping burls like walnuts in a hinge. No amount of clever pruning can negotiate with six inches of stone moving at the speed of sound.

The power reaches down into the dark lithosphere, far below the deepest pale rootlet, where no eye will ever see. It does not weld the fault shut—locked rock only stores deadlier ruin for later. Instead, it coaxes a microscopic ribbon of deep, superheated mineral brine upward into the dry, grinding teeth of the quartz spur. Grain by grain, it alters brittle feldspar into slick, yielding leaves of talc and serpentine clay.

The bowstring never snaps. Beginning that night, the locked slabs start to slide—not six inches in half a second forty winters from now, but the width of a spider’s silk every month. Down on the pitch-black lowest tier, a pale-skinned sap-tender crouching in the icy trickle pauses with his granite flake against a base-trunk. Through the leathery soles of his feet, he feels a single, sub-audible sigh pass through the bedrock, softer than a sleeper turning over in a burl. Two fused pillars above him give a tiny, wet creak, drinking the infinitesimal shift into their living cambium, thickening their woody knuckles around the new angle just as they were bred to do. The sap-tender scrapes a curl of frozen amber into his mouth and thinks no more of it.

---

The second thing is not in the fault, but in the blood.

Nothing leaves the trench, and almost nothing enters it. The dead go down beneath the thorny plugs of alpine rose; their calcium, phosphorus, and nitrogen climb faithfully back up through the cartilage-fungi and the sweet inner bark into the bones of their grandchildren. It is a nearly closed circle—*nearly*. 

Because high-mountain snow has forgotten the sea, and young granite was born of fire rather than ocean beds, there is an invisible famine in the water weeping from the cliff seams. Every drop of meltwater that drains out the bottom of the fault steals trace molecules that snowmelt never puts back: salt, selenium, and above all, iodine.

You can see the slow tax of it if you look closely into the dark of the burls. For people who survive entirely by hoarding and trading metabolic heat, the tiny butterfly-shaped gland at the base of the throat is the bellows of the furnace. Deprived of iodine over centuries, the bellows labors. Some of the elders carry a soft, heavy swelling under their chin-fat; in deep midwinter, a subtle leadenness settles into young limbs; one child in five is born a little too quiet, their inner thermostat set half a degree too low to thrive, going back down to the root-vaults wrapped in sedge before their second winter. No thumb-skill can fix what simply isn't in the rock.

Four hundred yards inside the solid cliff wall, sealed away when the young granite intruded through older, vanished strata eons ago, sits a blind lens of ancient evaporite no bigger than a curled marmot—the compressed, crystalline ghost of a prehistoric lagoon, dark with sea-salts, trace iodides, and magnesium. Groundwater passes within two inches of it on its way to the trench and never touches it.

The power cools a single hair-thin boundary between two crystals of mica until the rock ticks open a capillary seam the width of a shrew’s whisker.

Cold water slips in, kisses the ancient salt, and slips back out into the main seep. The flow is calibrated to milligrams a week—enough that that single buried pocket will last twelve thousand winters without running dry, faint enough that the water bleeding into the sphagnum mortar does not even taste brackish, only strangely clean and round on the tongue, like melted frost off a warm lip.

Nothing miraculous happens in the morning. Only, by the following autumn, the violet cartilage-fungi down in the dark beds grow slightly denser and meatier. By the second winter, the sugar-sap bled from the lower trunks carries the forgotten memory of an ocean up into four hundred bodies. Over five years, the heavy throats among the elders slowly soften; the quiet dullness lifts from the deepest winter months; the infants tucked into the middle of the family heaps kick with hot, fierce, greedy little heels, burning their pine-mash bright and clean.

---

The third thing happens at noon, in the roaring white blindness above the world, and it is very small.

One of the surface climbers—a forty-year-old woman whose horn-thumbs are notched from decades of snapping buds, her face greased thick with tallow, her eyes shielded behind slit-carved slivers of gray slate—is two hundred yards out on the shattered ridge checking the farthest deadfall traps when the mountain drops a rogue downdraft off the upper peak.

Usually they can read a squall ten minutes before it strikes. Today the air simply shears. In four breaths, the sky falls onto the ridge in a horizontal shriek of ice-flour. 

Through the narrow slits of her slate goggles, white air and white granite fuse into one solid, roaring wall. Worse, the freak downdraft spins the wind ninety degrees, scouring the rising plume of warm, juniper-smelling air from the ventilation chimney straight away toward the drop-off instead of toward her flat, baffled nostrils. She has a dead winter hare stiff under her tunic, forty minutes of core heat left before the rime seals her breathing-passages, and no sky, no shadow, no scent. Miss the dinner-plate dimple of the heather-plug by six paces on this ridge, and you walk smoothly over a thousand-foot cornice into nothing.

She does not panic; panic wastes breath-heat. She drops instantly into her boulder-crouch on the swept stone, knees to chest, thick felt shedding the wind, leathery paddle-hands tucked into her armpits, listening through her soles, calculating angles against the treacherous shift of the gale. Her old granite pruning-flake, tucked in her belt pouch, has worn dull at the point; earlier she had paused five minutes too long trying to knap a clean replacement from a stubborn, crumbly ledge—those five minutes are why the squall caught her out.

The power watches this small, round, stubborn knot of warm blood crouching alone on the roof of a murderous world, refusing to waste a single calorie on fear.

It does not hush the storm. Storms belong to the mountain.

Instead, thirty yards away at the lip of the hidden shaft, a solitary alpine shrew—fat in its winter coat, hunting spiders inside the woven heather-plug—shifts to a warmer pocket in the moss, loosening a crust of rime around the vent’s edge so a slightly thicker breath of the town’s warm exhaust sighs upward into the gale. At the exact same moment, the power nudges a tumbling eddy off a jagged tooth of granite upwind, curling a narrow, fleeting back-draft uphill along the stone—just three seconds of rebel air swimming backward through the blizzard.

It hits the crouching woman squarely across the bridge of her nose: a sudden, unmistakable thread of wet wool, crushed juniper, sour root-ferment, and the warm tallow of nine cousins breathing two stories down.

Her milky-green eyes widen behind the slate slits. She shifts her weight to rise and walk straight down the spine of that scent—and as her bare, calloused fingers press the swept ridge for balance, her palm settles over a loose spall of stone popped free from a dark basaltic dike by the sudden drop in temperature.

Even through stiffened skin, her constantly reading hands know immediately what they have touched. Not crumbly, coarse-grained granite, which dulls in a week of cutting tough sedge, but dense, fine-grained, glassy hornfels—flinty, heavy, curved cleanly like a shell, with a natural bevel sharp enough to shave frozen bark and tough enough to outlast her grandchildren. 

She doesn't look up at the blinding sky to thank anyone. The mountain splits stones every winter; the wind curls strangely around boulders all the time. She slips the dark, lovely blade into her greasy pouch beside the stiffening hare, turns her broad nose into the vanishing ribbon of home-smell, counts forty-six deliberate, flat-footed paces through the screaming white, hooks her opposable big toe over the rim of the steaming heather-plug, and drops feet-first out of the gale.

---

Down in the bruised olive twilight, someone pulls the heather shut above her. Someone takes the frozen hare; someone else guides her chilled, wide hands beneath warm armpits while a teenager leans close in the gloom, gripping the rime on her eyelashes gently between flat, warm molars to melt it away. 

When her fingers thaw enough to move, she pulls out the dark, silky stone flake and passes it without a word into the palm of the elder beside her. It travels around the dim landing from hand to horn-thumbed hand—weighed, stroked along its keen edge, tapped softly with a thumbnail so it gives a tiny, clear *clink* before the wet moss swallows the sound. A good stone. A stone from a very deep seam. Four sets of shoulders shift an inch inward, a ripple of quiet satisfaction passing body to body in the cold dark.

Then the elder hands it back, turns down the greased-leather trunk of the street, and trails her thick thumb lightly along the living wall, snapping off a swelling bud without breaking stride.

Above them, the blizzard scours an empty white ridge. Deep beneath them, lubricated stone slides a breadth of silk without a tremor. Around them, water carrying a whisper of ancient sea ticks into clear ice on a lintel, melts again in the collective warmth of four hundred steady hearts, and drips down the sphagnum grooves to feed the roots. 

The power listens for one more breath to the quiet percussion of the lung, sees that nothing has been broken and nothing more is needed, and leaves them entirely to themselves.
