A lesser benevolence would be tempted by pity. 

Looking down through the ruffled dark glass of the tarn, a hasty or sentimental god might see cold, and think to warm the water. It might see the heavy, tallow-packed limbs, the forty-second pulse, the iron ledger that permits nineteen lives and never twenty, and think to grant them ease—to widen the basin, hush the deafening crackle of the shrimp, or coax their plum-red gills back into lungs so they might walk out onto the dry scree and remember fire.

A wise power looks into that shadowless celadon vault and recognizes a masterpiece.

To warm the water by three degrees would starve it of dissolved oxygen, suffocating the nineteen while they slept wedged in their archways, and sending the ancient willow-architecture into a ragged, rotting frenzy of unchecked growth. To hush the shrimp or quiet the booming frogs would strike the tenders blind and mute, shattering the acoustic web they read with their breastbones. To give them twenty-five lives instead of nineteen would turn harmony into famine within a single winter. 

The Watcher—whose attention rests upon the high slate ridges not like a spotlight, but like barometric pressure, vast and unhurried—sees immediately that these beings are not trapped. They are *resolved*. Every crescent score of a slate knife, every quarter-tone shift of a leaning stone, every patient harvest of sweet cambium is a note in a chord that has been held, unbroken, since their ancestors chose the rising water over the barren mountain wind. 

To interfere inside the rim of the sinkhole would be vandalism.

And yet, the Watcher does not look only at what is. Wisdom looks along the grain of deep time, the way the tenders themselves read a willow node two summers ahead. The Watcher lets its awareness sink below the braided root-floor, down through sixty feet of clear water, and deeper still—into the blind, lightless geology of the old mountain range.

There, three miles beneath the town, it finds the flaw.

The mountain is old, and old slate shifts. The deep thermal seep that breathes upward into the bottom of the sinkhole—the faint, indispensable thread of warmth that keeps the tarn from freezing solid from rim to floor during the ferocious midwinter dark, and drives the three-minute sigh of the burl-wood paddles—travels up a hairline fault in the bedrock. 

Left to the indifferent drift of stone, in forty-two winters a frost-heave on the northern ridge will send a shudder down the massif. Far below the roots, where no slate knife or willow-cord could ever reach, a wedge of rotten shale will shear loose and choke that fault. The upward breath will stop. Over a single January, the ice at the surface will thicken from a drumhead into a ten-foot plug, crushing the upper terraces, starving the photosynthetic bark of winter sun, and locking the nineteen in a silent, freezing dark until the white river stone never lifts from its moss again.

The tenders are masters of their world, able to calibrate everything within reach of their webbed hands. They cannot calibrate the tectonic plates of the earth.

So the power acts—not as a miracle, which breaks the rules of a place to announce its author, but as a neighbor tending the road outside a quiet house.

First, deep in the lightless rock two miles down, the Watcher lays a finger of subtle heat and mineral patience along the rotten shale seam. Nothing cracks; no tremor rises to rattle the silt on the drowned plazas or startle a single frog mid-boom. Instead, drop by microscopic drop over seven seasons, silica-rich groundwater is coaxed sideways into the fragile fissure, crystallizing into a collar of clear, indestructible mountain quartz around the thermal throat. The artery of the sinkhole is sleeved in stone harder than iron. It will breathe at the exact same pressure, at the exact same temperature, for the next ten thousand years.

Second, the Watcher turns its attention upward, five hundred yards upslope from the tarn, where a hanging cornice of grey slate leans out over the snowfield—a slow landslide waiting a century or two to tumble down and fill the cathedral nave with dead rubble. 

In the dry, windy autumn, a single cushion-moss spore is guided by an eddy of wind into the deepest crack behind that overhanging crag, followed the next spring by the seed of a dwarf mountain pine. As the pine’s roots thicken over decades, they will not pry the rock loose; guided by an unseen, gentle hand, they weave backward into the mountain's shoulder, pinning the loose scree into a living buttress that deflects rockfalls harmlessly down the eastern gully, half a mile away from the tarn.

Only once does the Watcher allow itself the small, quiet indulgence of leaving a mark within the basin itself—not to be worshipped, for the nineteen have no room in their slow, practical minds for gods, only for plumb-lines and equilibrium—but as one craftsman tipping a hat to another.

High on the dry lip of the sinkhole, where the tenders climb half out of the water once an age to grind their slate scrapers sharp against the exterior rock, a winter storm rolls down from the peaks. Driven by the wind, a single, fist-sized cobble of black nephrite jade—dense, glass-smooth, tougher than any local slate, weathered out of a vein ten miles away—skips down the snow-crust, glances off a willow root at the rim, and drops silently through the surface tension.

It does not strike a pavilion or snap a reed pipe. Falling through the celadon light, slowed by the cold water, it comes to rest in plain view upon the lowest step of the central market-ring, four yards from the caged white stone.

The Watcher withdraws its hand, folding its vastness back into the pale afternoon sky, content merely to listen.

Down in the booming green clarity, a school of silver-sided char parts lazily around the fallen jade. Two carapace shrimp skitter out from a root-crevice, tap the strange, smooth stone with their antennae, and snap their claws once—*crack*—adding two sparks to the endless cedar-fire roar of the town.

Twenty minutes later, the eldest tender drifts around the curve of the spiral avenue, leaning heavy and unhurried into the current, plum-colored back-frills pulsing once in the cool light. 

Its small, horn-shielded eyes catch the new dark shape on the pale green bark of the step. For ten full minutes, the tender does not move, letting the water press the acoustics of the plaza against its broad chest, checking for a rockslide above, checking the rhythm of the paddles, watching the white river stone inside its wooden globe. 

The white stone lifts a fraction of an inch on the thermal sigh, hangs for three slow heartbeats, and settles back onto its moss. *All plumb.*

Slowly, the tender crouches. Webbed, grey-callused fingers close around the black nephrite cobble. The tender weighs it in a broad palm, rubs a thumb across its fine, unfractured grain, and recognizes instantly—with the deep, wordless certainty of forty generations of stone-grinders—that split along its cleavage and honed against quartz scree, this single stone will yield four blades that will never chip, never flake, and hold a razor bevel for three hundred years.

The tender tucks the jade into its woven-moss pouch beside the spools of willow root. It places two knuckles gently against the root-floor—acknowledging the town, the current, and the good fortune of the water—and leans back into the drift, heading up toward the rim to begin the three-month work of making a knife.
