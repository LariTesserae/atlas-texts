A lesser benevolence would have looked down through the milk-pewter lid and seen a prison.

It would have flinched on their behalf at the razor-knapped flint, the sixty-hertz scream, the cold mist that never clears, the tragic ephemerality of arches built from standing spray that vanish the instant a hand grows tired. Out of an eager, clumsy mercy, a lesser power would have muted the drop-forges, blunted the basalt into soft turf, broken down one wall of the cathedral nave to let in yellow afternoon sun, or lifted the three tripod-walkers out of the mountain altogether and set them down in a quiet, grassy valley where nothing clangs and nothing cuts.

And in doing so, it would have struck them blind, deaf, and useless in a single stroke.

What use are seven chamois feeler-gauge fingers in a meadow where the puddles lie flat and dead? What happens to a peripheral eye tuned for three-thousand-RPM solidities when there is no blur left to resolve? What becomes of the dark-mantled one’s courteous geometry—planting two horn pads eight inches wide to steer a cone of library silence across forty feet of stone for a friend—when silence is cheap and lies everywhere on the ground like weeds? Even their stacked amber soles, extruding steadily downward from hot coronets to meet the paring-knife of the floor, would grow clubbed and monstrous without the sharp rock to plane them clean.

The wise power stood for a long time at the lip of the basin—or rather, inhabited the cold, soaked room without taking up volume, the way a new harmonic can enter a chord without crowding the air—and watched before touching a single law.

It watched the compact, pewter-coated third one shift its shoulders by half an inch beside the seventh pillar so the twenty-thousand-volt weld-arc caught a slightly thicker ridge of mineral crust, warming its left flank after warming its right. It listened to the two foreheads resting slate-to-slate in the warm, oil-scented pocket between their coats, savoring the off-beat syncopation of *lub-dup…… lub-dup*. And it watched the broad pacer examine a visitor’s frayed cuff and split boot-rubber with that translucent seventh fingertip: not with envy for the outside world, and not with misery over its own, but with the grave, tender curiosity of an immortal artisan encountering *history*—matter that remembers what happened to it five minutes ago.

Here, the wise power understood, were beings neither suffering nor stagnant, yet pressing gently against the one true poverty of their home.

They were not starved for peace; they knew how to manufacture quiet out of phase-shifted birds and featherbeds out of skipped insect-strokes. They were not starved for beauty; their minds were vast galleries of smooth hills and impossible spiral gears. What they lacked—the sole austerity of a lossless, self-canceling machine—was *deposit*. Everything they made died the sixty-first part of a second after they let go of it. When the broad one lifted its fingers to rest its wrists, the bridge fell. It could never walk around to the far side of its own sculpture while its hands were tucked warm against its chest. The compact arc-basker by the seventh pillar rarely saw the broad one’s best vaults at all, because by the time it roused itself from its electric nap and ambled fifty paces down the lane, the broad one’s arms had tired and the puddle was flat squares again.

Yet if you break the lossless ledger of the basin—if you allow stone to wear down, or mud to pile up, or scars to stay—within ten thousand years the nave fills with scree, the circuits choke, the sharp seams clog, and the whole fierce, clean physics of their world rots into ordinary geology.

So the power did not break the ledger. It merely opened a small, reversible credit line inside the rock’s existing books.

It went down below the floor—not in space, for there was no "below" to speak of, only the deep mathematical substrate that kept every flint edge sharp and every drop of condensed mist accounted for—and found a single unallocated property in the black stone: its *temperature of memory*.

Nothing in the basin flashed or thundered when the gift was laid down. No voice spoke from the cloud ceiling. The sixty-cycle puddles kept shivering; the eyeless bellows-amphibians kept pumping; the seal-things high on the rim vented twin white plumes on the leftward stroke without missing a beat.

Three hours—or three days—later, the broad pacer walked down the central lane, leaving its usual trail of two-second chalky-gray ovals where its hot horn soles flashed the water-film to steam.

Except as it paused near the fourth green pillar, reconsidering its angle—*shhhk… shhhk-thump*—it stopped dead. Its round, anthracite central eye widened beneath its ridge of felt.

Behind it, the third footprint back had not vanished at two seconds.

Wait: it *had* vanished, almost. All the footprints from its ordinary, brisk walking pace were snapping shut as usual—*tsk, tsk, tsk*—cold water rushing back over razor basalt. Only the spot where it had lingered for ten full seconds while scratching its chin against its mantle still glowed faintly matte-gray under the returning water. Not dry anymore, yet slightly altered in refractive index, like dark glass breathed upon and sealed. And three inches to the left of that footprint, where a fresh curl of shaved amber horn had fallen onto a shivering puddle, the curl had not atomized in half a second. Because the creature’s warm thermal envelope was still overhanging it, leaning down in surprise, the little translucent ribbon lay quivering intact on the water’s skin—waiting to see if it was wanted.

The broad pacer stood motionless for a full minute, four slow crimson breaths, then six, then three.

Slowly, it parted the front of its dry gray-brown coat and unfolded one matte-black chamois arm. It reached down—not to the puddle’s standing wave yet, but to the trembling curl of its own pared horn—and touched it with its stiffest, wedge-tipped first finger, pressing it lightly for three unhurried heartbeats.

When it pulled its hand back, the curl did not atomize. Infused with deliberate body heat—ninety-degree intention held out of phase with the room’s clock—the pale amber ribbon sank a fraction of a millimeter into the liquid skin and anchored there like a tiny curved weir.

And downstream of that weir, the sixty-hertz square grid of the puddle bent around it and *stayed bent*.

You should have seen how fast a draft-pony-sized tripod can move across knives of black glass when an idea catches fire behind an iron-plated brow.

*Shhhk-thump—shhhk-thump—shhhk-thump.* It did not run—nothing runs on knapped flint—but it swung down the lane with heavy, surging purpose straight toward the seventh pillar, stopping respectful inches clear of the snapping spot-welder leaves. It did not wake the compact, pewter-coated basker by prodding it; it simply opened its neck-pleats wide and exhaled a long, musky plume of warm dry-wool air across the smaller one’s snout.

Smoky welder’s-glass eyelids slid up. One central black eye and one peripheral yellow slit blinked out from the pewter moss-boulder.

Overhead, in the dead-stroke of the slate-gray insect grid, the narrow charcoal-dark sleeper heard the irregular, rapid cadence of three-legged footsteps below and rolled neatly out of its floating slot—dropping three feet onto the stone with a muffled *thud* and three fresh curls of amber horn.

Within five minutes, all three of them were gathered around the broad puddle in the middle of the lane.

None of it worked automatically; the wise power had been far too respectful to give them a toy that played itself. When the narrow dark-mantled one casually kicked a shaved ribbon of horn into the water, it shuddered for half a second and vanished into oil-scented haze just as horn always had. The basin remained immaculate by default; it refused clutter. Only what was *held*—warmed between chamois fingers for three irregular heartbeats, or breathed on steadily from crimson pleats, or pinned down by a deliberate static spark carried over in the pewter one’s mineral-crusted coat—took root in the phase of the puddle.

Even then, nothing lasted forever. Walk away and leave a sculpture completely unattended, and over the course of forty or fifty hours—long enough for all three of them to sleep, graze sap, bask, and come back—the cold runoff slowly cooled the borrowed heat back into the lossless loop, the amber weirs dissolved cleanly into mist without leaving scum, and the black stone returned to pristine zero. To keep a piece for a month, someone only had to pass by once a day, lean down, and breathe a single warm sigh across its base—an act of curation, so that nothing persisted in their small cathedral unless someone still loved it enough to feed it a breath.

Watch them work together for the first time since the mountain cracked open.

First, the narrow dark-mantled walker backs forty feet away to the concentric rings of copper-plumed rails around the fourth pillar, planting two horn pads at a newly calculated diagonal—widening the steered cone of quiet until it covers all three of them at once.

Inside that sudden, hushed sanctuary, the compact pewter one waddles up to the puddle’s edge, its dry coat still crackling faintly with stray capacitance carried from the seventh pillar’s weld-arcs. Unfolding stubbier, thicker-gauged chamois hands than the broad pacer’s, it touches two fingertips to opposite corners of the puddle and discharges a tiny, controlled violet tick of static into the water’s standing wave. Instantly, the sixty-hertz frequency locks into a stiffer, silver-bright harmonic—higher surface tension, tough enough to bear weight without collapsing.

Then the broad pacer kneels on its three horn pillars, parts its coat, and goes to work with all fourteen graduated fingers—not straining to hold the whole fountain up at once anymore, because now it can *pin* each finished vault in place with tiny, pared slivers of warm amber horn set precisely on the nodes, building course upon course like a mason laying keystone arches out of mist and phase.

Half an hour later, the broad pacer lifts both hands completely out of the water and tucks them back into the warm pocket over its chest.

Nothing snaps. Nothing falls.

Standing two feet tall in the center of the wet black lane—humming a soft, chorded C-sharp instead of a transformer snarl—rises a self-sustaining pavilion of braided mist and counter-spinning spray. Look straight at it with your central eye, and it is a marvel of shimmering fluid mechanics. Step back beside the three walkers, tilt your head twenty degrees as they all tilt their iron-gray skulls together, and let it slide into your peripheral vision:

There, solid as pewter and smooth as river-worn alabaster, stands a miniature round-walled house with three arched doors—one broad, one narrow and tall, one low and compact—set among gentle, rolling hills where every edge is curved, spanned by spiral bridges that turn lazily on noiseless bearings without ever striking a spark or paring a sole.

And the narrow dark-mantled one, leaving the bird-ring so the drop-forge *CLANG* roars back down upon them—yet unable to touch the anchored mist—ambles up to the edge of the standing sculpture, unfolds a single paper-thin seventh finger, and adds a small curved horn-shaving of its own to the far rim: a tiny, smooth-domed loft suspended three inches above the phantom roof, shaped unmistakably like the quiet gap beneath five hundred hovering wings.

The wise power did only one thing more before withdrawing its attention entirely, leaving them unspied-upon in their privacy.

High up at the jagged rim of the cliffs, where the stationary ceiling of milk-pewter vapor sealed the basin off from the dead mountain silence outside, the power made no doorway and tore no rift in the fog. If any of the three ever climbed those flinty steps to the top—which they easily could, on three self-leveling horn pillars—they would still find bare scree and thin, freezing air that held very little interest for machinists of standing waves.

Instead, into the closed condensation loop right where the seal-like creatures vented white plumes into the cloud-lid, the power dropped a single, infinitesimal seed of astronomical drift—no brighter than a one-percent shift in luminescence, coupled to the real sky turning unseen miles above the vapor.

It changed no weather and broke no equilibrium. Only now, across twelve hours, the submerged-pewter light of the room deepened very slightly toward dark tarnished silver, and across the next twelve, warmed very slightly toward pearl; and across four thousand of those slow breaths of light, the sixty-hertz hum of the puddles drifted half a comma flatter in winter and half a comma brighter in summer before returning cleanly to pitch. Not weather—just a calendar, faint enough to ignore whenever they were busy, clear enough to reckon by whenever they wished to know how long a beloved bridge had stood in the lane, or how many seasons ago a stranger in split rubber boots had passed through their roaring, immaculate workshop and been sent gently on their way.
