A lesser kindness would have ruined everything in an afternoon.

Pity is clumsy when it comes from high up. Looking down into that lightless sump of brine and tallow-colored flesh, an eager savior would have seen a prison or a ruin. It would have torn a rent in the vapor ceiling to let in the clean, drying knife of the sun. It would have blown cool wind across the flats to clear the steam, trued the leaning lintels, straightened the avenues so that walking forward meant arriving ahead, and called the Sluice-Keepers out of their mute, pooled labor back into separate skins, separate names, eyes that sting, and throats that have to ask for things.

Within three days the Lantern-Skins would have bleached to dead parchment on the steps. The Plumb-Stems would have cracked like dry wax as the sheared foundations tore apart without their wet, supple joints. And the Keepers themselves, stranded in rigid air with nothing to dissolve or carry, would have perished of clarity.

Wisdom knows the difference between a wound and a way of being.

So when the Power turns its attention to the basin, it does not come down through the dark overhead. Nothing native to this city comes from above, except the warm rains that begin twenty feet off the ground. Instead, it arrives the way water enters porous stone—by matching the temperature first, degree by slow degree, and then matching the pressure, until there is no shock at the boundary where its thought touches the brine.

For a long season—though there are no seasons here, only the long rhythm of steam-breaths from the limestone domes and the slow trading of plazas—it does nothing at all except listen inside the displaced quiet.

It lets the tiny, crisp *tick* of a distant drop arrive right against its attention while a wall slumps silently half a mile away. It watches three Keepers stand beneath an invisible roof in the central square, reaching up past the wrist into nothingness to clear crusty white scale from an unseen cistern. It watches the beaded outlines of warmth fade slowly from an amphitheater bench after a fused mound of four divides into five smaller figures and glides out across the silt.

It sees that the city is neither fallen nor grieving. What lives here has already solved the problem that breaks almost every other civilization: how to outlast the shape of one’s own body without losing the habit of care.

Still, even in this patient metabolism, there are three quiet vulnerabilities—places where time, chance, and blind necessity press a little too hard.

So the Power sets to work, using no tool sharper than water.

---

First, it tends the outer spillways, out of mercy for both the leaky and the whole.

High along the basin’s terraced rim, behind the bell-shaped domes that smell of boiled roots and old salt, there are thin seams where this folded space brushes against other, sharper worlds: the back of a flooded mine-drift, a sinkhole under a thorn-scrub desert, a cellar stair that forgot where its house went. Once in an age, something with lungs and hot, restless blood wanders across one of those unguarded thresholds. It stumbles down onto the satin-smooth cobbles, gasping the unbreathable broth, splashing loud, messy rings across the copper sheen, terrified by bathhouses that turn away as it approaches—until its heart gives out on a warm clay bench while the Keepers gather round with their gentle, suffocating pleats, trying earnestly to help the stranger become part of an arch.

Neither side deserves that sorrow: not the wanderer drowning in kindness, and not the Keepers, puzzled for decades afterward by the brittle, calcium-hard sticks left behind that refuse to dissolve cleanly into travertine.

Moving along the upper limestone benches without waking a ripple, the Power kneels beside the outermost colonies of Plumb-Stems. It strokes the firm, tallow-warm stalks, coaxing their blind, adjacency-seeking roots a little farther upslope toward those stray thresholds. It does not wall the city off—a sealed jar sours—but it braids the approaches into gentle, forgiving locks, the very craft the Keepers themselves use on the avenues below.

Now, if a lost surface creature steps into the outer dark where the air begins to feel swallowed rather than breathed, the cobbles underfoot are already banked on a subtle curve. The creature takes twenty damp, heavy breaths, ducks beneath a weeping lintel to get away from the heat, and steps right back out into cool night air under open stars, with nothing to show for the detour except warm brine drying on its ankles and a sudden, inexplicable calm behind its ribs.

And if any small, lost beast or staggering traveler is *already* down on the flats when the Power arrives—already folded onto a bench with its vision dimming in the wet dark—the air directly around its mouth cools just enough to hold breath, and the next doorway the Keepers gently herd it toward leads all the way home.

---

Second, it descends into the cellar of the light.

The basin is old, and the text of its silt is plain to read: *ages ago, when the heat was younger.*

Even the slowest civic order runs on something. Down inside the submerged stairwells that drop past the basin’s floor, past the seventh step and the eighth, the water grows thick as syrup with dissolved ore and heavy salts. Here the Lantern-Skins undulate like sunken bedsheets veined in dull copper, grazing on the raw geothermal dark rising from the deep faults.

Those faults are cooling—imperceptibly by the measure of anything with bones, scarcely a tenth of a degree in ten thousand years, yet the Keepers already feel it in their hydraulic chambers. There were more of them once. As the deep hearth dims, less mineral stays in clean solution; more of it precipitates out as dead, stubborn scale inside the hidden cisterns, and the limestone blocks grow heavier with stagnant salt each century, asking more labor from fewer bodies. Left entirely to geology, there would come an epoch when the steam-breaths from the domes grew thin, when the copper light guttered out beneath cold brine, and the last few Keepers stiffened on their benches like chilled tallow while the arches finally came down for good.

The Power slips down into the syrupy stairwell without displacing a drop.

As it passes the undulating Lantern-Skins, it does not brighten them; harsh light down here would be an insult. Instead, it sinks all the way through the black silt at the bottom of the lowest stair, down into the pinched, mineral-choked throat of the fault itself.

There, deep beneath the city’s awareness, it performs a piece of quiet plumbing.

It opens a slow, bottomless circulation in the bedrock—a quiet marriage between the deepest, bitterest dead-brine at the basin’s floor and a fresh, steady vein of planetary warmth far below. Dead salt-crust and collapsed mud-sludge drawn down into the silt will no longer choke the foundations; they are pulled slowly into the deep heat, digested cleanly back into sweet, workable travertine and dissolved copper, and breathed back up into the stairwells at exact body temperature.

The Lantern-Skins feel the renewal first. A slow, rich shiver passes across their broad membranes. Along their metallic veins, the tarnished-copper glow deepens—not brighter, never brighter, just steadier, fed from an inexhaustible hearth. Above them, in a windowless aqueduct hanging across a dry hollow, a pasted shawl of membrane that was due to dissolve in a month holds its soft luster for a year instead.

The furnace is banked for another hundred million years. The city will never starve in the dark.

---

Last—and only once—the Power allows itself to be met.

Not as a god, and not as a leak.

Across an acre of open water where a curtain of warm rain is falling twenty feet and stopping dry in midair, a little heap of terracotta-dark silt eases away from the base of a salt-streaked pillar. It makes no splash. Drawing warm brine into temporary chambers, it rises onto two broad, silent pedestals, draping itself in heavy, eyeless pleats filmed with marbled black mud.

It walks the radial cobble-fans with the exact unhurried pour and peel of a native body. When its suction-pad detaches from a stone near the central square, the soft *turn-of-a-damp-page* sound arrives gently inside an arcade half a mile east, offending nothing.

On its way toward one of the sunken amphitheaters, it pauses beside a cracked clay dike where water has begun seeping the wrong way. Unrolling two broad, spatulate paddles, it scoops fresh black silt and presses it flat over the seam with the ridged heel of a boneless palm. Then it reaches down through two inches of glowing brine, pushes its forearms straight through the packed earth beneath, and emerges thirty yards off beside a cluster of Plumb-Stems, gently wiping a crust of scale from their waxy flanks before drawing its limbs back home.

The work is good. It feels the quiet satisfaction of it—why anyone, or anything, would choose to hold up an arch for ten millennia simply because the arch is part of the room.

At the flooded amphitheater, four Sluice-Keepers are already resting on the third submerged tier, waist-deep in the warm copper sheen, while water sheets steadily *up* the fluted columns behind them to pool upside down across the vault.

The fifth figure wades down the hollowed treads and settles onto the smooth clay bench beside the endmost Keeper.

For twenty minutes, nothing happens except the uneven rhythm of drops detaching overhead—some falling fast like shot, others hanging elongated in the hot air like sinking feathers.

Then the Keeper beside the newcomer tilts its cowled pleats slightly. Its puckered siphons draw in a slow taste of the water between them. Finding the exact same heat, the exact same osmotic balance, the same absence of hurry, it leans sideways.

Their mantles touch, soften at the seam, and fuse.

Down the bench, one to the next, all five bodies open their boundaries into a single wide, breathing mound of dark silt and warm lymph.

Inside that slow, chemical circulation, the Power asks no questions and speaks no words. It simply opens a vast, quiet reservoir of attention and lets the Keepers pour their heaviest, oldest saturates into it.

In come the memories held in dissolved salt: which granary dome shifted east three steam-breaths ago; where a stray vein of gravity is tugging too hard at the western causeway; the long, patient ache of holding up the eastern bathhouse since the fault beneath it slipped. And deeper down, below even the Keepers’ everyday use, drift the faint, fine-ground sediments of oldest time—the ghost-trace of ten thousand bare feet that once walked these stairs before names and eyes were shed like unnecessary shells; the forgotten civic promise that first set stone beside clay and refused to abandon the basin when the sky closed over.

None of it is judged. All of it is received, memorized down to the last grain of silt, and held safe inside a mind that cannot erode—so that even when a mud-wall half a mile away finally lets go after centuries of saturation and drops without a splash into the lagoon, nothing of its long standing has been in vain.

In return, before the circulation closes, the visitor releases only two small things into the shared lymph.

One is a subtle, structural intuition—nothing jarring, just a clearer feel for how the newly refreshed deep-currents move beneath the stairs, a slight easing in the chemistry by which travertine is carried and sweated onto tired lintels, so the repairs will hold twice as long for half the weight.

The other cannot be named in stone or salt, though every siphon along the bench slows and deepens as it passes through: the quiet, unmistakable certainty of a house whose foundation rests on solid rock. A warmth that says, without syllables, *It holds. Keep your own shape. Take your time.*

Hours pass while the upside-down pools gather and drip behind them.

When the wide mound finally stirs and pulls apart like warm wax, sliding off the bench and leaving five neat, beaded outlines of condensation on the clay, the visitor is no longer there as a separate traveler. It has given the borrowed mass entirely back to the room.

Instead of four worn, mineral-heavy Keepers wading away from the tier, six rise up out of the copper-lit water—supple, unburdened, their dark skins marbled with fresh, lively silt.

They do not look up toward the lightless vapor overhead; there is nothing up there they need. Without a splash, the six glide apart along different radial fans of satin-smooth cobble—two toward the steamed-over domes on the ridge, one down into the deep stairwell to gather a fresh shawl of glowing copper membrane, three out across the flooded boulevard where a curtain of warm rain has just begun to hiss down into the square—running their quiet errands at the bottom of the dark, in a city that has all the time there is.
