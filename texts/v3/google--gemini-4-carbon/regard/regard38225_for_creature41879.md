A lesser power would have ruined it.

A sentimental god, looking down from the dark zenith upon those windowless ceramic stacks and the masked figures clicking slowly through the scalding fog, would have felt pity. It would have thickened the air until it smelled of pine and summer rain; it would have cooled the trachyte, quieted the fissures, and cast seeds of lowland grass into the slag-paving so the children might know meadows. Within a month, the thermal spines would have gone slack, the ratchet-bridges would have stalled for want of creep, the blind cellar-crops would have drowned in foreign mold, and the saddle-folk themselves—with their thirty-beat hearts and soup-thick blood—would have sickened and died of sluggish veins in the heavy, treacherous air.

Wisdom knows better than to rescue a masterwork from its medium.

The Presence that lingered above the horn-peaks did not pity them. It watched with the profound, quiet delight of an old mathematician discovering a proof written in an unfamiliar notation on the wall of a cave. It saw the cream-and-ochre tiles glazed with the clean ash of grandmothers; it heard the soft, liquid clicks of lovers arguing gently so as not to wake a shift-sleeper; it noted the brass scraper paused on a catwalk to ease the alum-itch from an eighty-year-old tortoise’s hinge. Here was no misery crying out for deliverance. Here was grace under twenty-four thousand feet of thin sky—patience made into iron, courtesy pressed out of tight quarters, a whole people dancing to a measure that took months per beat.

Yet because the Presence could see not only the surface of the pan-works and the plumb-bobs in their bowls of oil, but also ten miles down into the blinded, incandescent throat of the mountain, and three hundred years forward along the vectors of its rise, It saw two things the Under-Walkers with their brass rods could not yet hear.

The first was deep, and sudden. Four miles beneath the eastern pan-works, where the young pluton shouldered up against an ancient, stubborn slab of cold gneiss, the rock was not creeping. It was locked. While the rest of the saddle spread its honest three centimeters a year, feeding the great drawbars, a single colossal knuckle of stone down in the dark had caught fast, hoarding centuries of shear-stress like a bow drawn past the ear. In nineteen years—during a night shift when the salt-wax bricks were half-frozen—that knuckle would snap cleanly through. No jack-screw or lead crush-pad ever cast could drink a four-foot vertical jump in a tenth of a second. Every platinum wire in the city would whip against its glass bowl; every ceramic tower would shear at the shoes and spill its pressurized warmth into the freezing dark.

The second was small, and slow, and already inside their chests. Down in the hundred-and-thirty-degree twilight of the slots, where the Under-Walkers moved in their quilted suits, the oldest jackets were shedding invisible, barbed needles of frayed mineral-wool into the airlocks when the crews unsuited—microscopic splinters of silicate that even the damp felt gaskets missed, settling over decades into the bottommost alveoli of the men and women who listened to the stone through their teeth, turning the margins of their great barrel-lungs to stiff scar-parchment by fifty.

To fix everything is to steal a people’s work from their hands. To leave a blind snare across the path of careful walkers is mere cruelty.

So the Presence reached down—not into the sky, and not into their minds, which were already well-ordered and wholly their own—but four miles down into the lightless, plastic root of the mountain, where no brass listening-rod would ever reach.

It did not stop the mountain’s rise. It merely laid a thumb of quiet warmth along that locked knuckle of deep gneiss, softening the boundary by a few hundred degrees, turning dry, brittle lock-stone into a slow, viscous glide of serpentine and talc. Up in the city, nothing shook. Not a drop of oil trembled in a single ward-bowl. Only, over the next six months, the Under-Walkers down under the eastern terraces noticed through their jawbones a new, remarkably steady *purr-hiss-purr* deep beneath Shoe-Line Twelve—a broad, reliable creep-seam waking up where the rock had always been suspiciously mute. By autumn, the guild-engineers were already sketching plans for two new tension-bridges across the eastern cleft to harvest the steady, generous torque of it, congratulating their senior elders on having predicted the shift from thirty years of Book-of-Creep fractions.

Then, having mended the floor beneath the game without disturbing a single piece on the board, the Presence left three small things along the margins of the trough, spaced out where curiosity, rather than awe, would stumble across them.

First, in a freshly split hairline fissure just beyond the southernmost slag-heaps—a crack no wider than two fingers, steaming faintly in the morning sun—It altered the cooling of a single upwelling vein of magnesium-rich hydrothermal brine. Instead of crystallizing into brittle, lung-piercing needles of harsh silicate, the mineral spun itself out as it met the thin air into long, supple, silky fleeces of pure basaltic glass-wool, each fiber smooth-walled, unbarbed, flexible as combed lamb’s-fleece and utterly incapable of splintering into dust, yet able to turn back the heat of a blowtorch. Three days later, a pair of masked apprentices out checking perimeter markers spotted the pale golden locks sighing Gently in the fumarole draft, poked them with iron tongs, marveled that they did not snap or powder between gauntleted thumbs, and stuffed a sample pouch to bring back to the weavers’ guild. Within ten seasons, they would mine out the vein, puzzle out the exact salt-ratio and draw-speed that birthed it, and spin their own harmless, indestructible cloth from waste slag—proud, and rightly so, of their own metallurgy.

Second, down in the warm, sweating dark beneath Aqueduct Seven, where the pale ribbon-worms grazed the lead sheathing, the Presence nudged a single spore-drift in a deep sulfur vent. By winter, beside the blind ivory tubers and maroon rock-kelp in the cellar-troughs of Ward Four, a sharp-eyed grandmother noticed a strange, thumb-sized crust of pale amber lichen clinging to the rim of a condensate drip-lip. It needed no sunlight—only warm slate, traces of ammonia, and the rhythmic *shhh-tick* vibration of nearby pipes. When bruised with a thumbnail, it did not smell of wet rust, or sulfur, or yeast, or grease. It released into the close, lanolin-heavy air of the octagonal room a sudden, clean, astonishing breath of wild, resinous cedar and crushed bergamot—the exact miraculous scent hoarded in lowland wood-lockets, growing quietly for free on damp slag, asking only a thumb-smear of brine once a week. It brewed, they soon discovered, into a clear golden tea that eased the silver-blindness headache in twenty minutes flat.

And the third thing was nothing useful at all.

Because the Night-Watcher with her zinc glow-pot and soapstone chalk paused every evening on the same grated landing beside the frosted copper conduit, waiting twenty breaths while her lung-pack equalized before descending into the alley-fog, the Presence touched a thumb-sized pocket of cooling trachyte wedged into the black cliff-wall at the exact height of her copper-masked eyes.

Inside the newly cracked seam, nourished by a microscopic seep of copper-bearing steam and bismuth vapor, a cluster of hollow, resonant crystals grew outward over a single night into the shape of five tiny, nested organ-pipes no longer than a finger-joint. They did nothing for Head or Span. They held up no tenement. Only, at dusk, during that precise half-hour when the white sun dropped into the milk-white cloud-sea below and the thermal snap sent the warm court-air rushing upward past the freezing iron handrails, the draft caught the lips of those five metallic crystals and blew across them.

When the Night-Watcher stepped out through the bulkhead the next evening, unlatched nothing, chalked her sixteenths on the wet slate, stepped carefully over the steaming shell of the sleeping fin-back, and paused on the third step to rest her gloved hand on the flange, she heard it through the thin air from three feet away.

Beneath the dry rain-patter of cooling rivets and the *glug* of heavy brine, clear and round and impossibly sweet in an atmosphere that killed ordinary sound at twenty paces, five tiny, true notes chimed in a slow, rising fifth—*warm, cool, hold, true, home*—hanging in the mist just inside the reach of her ears for the space of four slow heartbeats before the draft shifted and let the silence back in.

Behind the spun-copper mask and mica lenses, her heavy, double-lashed eyes widened slightly. She held her breath so the peroxide-pack wouldn't hiss. When it chimed once more on the dying tail of the updraft, she did not fall to her knees, for kneeling in three-inch ceramic clogs on hot grating is bad craftsmanship. Instead, she raised her free gauntleted hand a few inches from her chest and flicked her thick fingers through a tiny, private two-beat sign normally used only between master-wrights when a thirty-ton counterweight settles dead-plumb without a tremor: *Well-seated. Sound work.*

She made a small neat mark in the corner of her slate so the morning-shift walker would know to look and listen, adjusted the strap of her glow-pot, and continued at her steady, unhurried pace down into the warm fog.

High above the horn-peaks, where the cobalt sky darkened toward space and the last giant glider banked silently toward its warm condenser-roof, the Presence watched the small yellow dot of her lamp sink into the iron labyrinth, smiled once, and went on its way—leaving the mountain to rise, and the city to level itself.
