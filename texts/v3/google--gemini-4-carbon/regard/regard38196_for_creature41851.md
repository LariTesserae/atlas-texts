A lesser kindness would arrive here with a hammer, or a handful of loamy dirt.

It is an old reflex among powers that call themselves good: to mistake stillness for peace, greenness for life, and sunlight-through-leaves for the only permissible grammar of grace. Such a visitor, looking down over the smooth grey chert rim at the bellowing, grease-slicked saucer, would see a prison. It would hear the ceaseless sixty-cycle hum in its teeth, watch the blind grey bolsters licking hot tallow from the cams, see the endless, meaningless flattening and remelting of the soft alloy billets, and feel a surge of pity. *Look how trapped they are,* the lesser kindness would say, *serving dead men’s engines for no output at all.* And out of mercy it would throw the main switch, crack the vitrified bricks to let the wild dock and dandelion in, hush the drop-hammers, and wait for the creatures to rejoice.

Within seventy-two hours, forty thousand bellows-throats would suffocate in the cold silence as their blubber stiffened. The mica-winged spark-drinkers would rain like dead chaff from the quiet copper wires. Down in the sumps, the bristle-gilled eels would drown in stagnant, unpumped brine, and inside the dark municipal hall, the great amber siphonophore—deprived of the voltage that has been its bloodstream, its eyesight, and its thought for ten thousand years—would slacken on its calcified anchors and rot in the dark, bewildered by an amputation it had no nerves to name.

The power that actually comes to the grey chert rim is older than that mistake, and much quieter.

It does not descend in a shaft of golden sun—breaking the high, pearly stratus would drop the relative humidity of the basin by twelve percent in twenty minutes, parching the leather check-valves of the surveyors mid-stride. Instead, it arrives simply as attention. It stands where the acoustic bounce leaves that yard-wide ring of dead air at the perimeter, taking care not to cast a shadow on the cobble-shelled wardens at its feet. Not one black-bristled valve snaps shut. Nothing foreign has crossed the line.

For a very long time—long enough for the three-car worm-train to heave itself around the iron oval a thousand times, long enough for five generations of horn-worms inside the cast-iron lamp standards to push their slow black tapers up into the dazzling white arcs—the visitor only listens.

It listens past the deafening brass *HONK* of the powerhouse facade, past the tin-chute rattle of the porcelain highway, down into the sub-audible tremors of the bedrock beneath the town. Wise powers do not judge a house by its wallpaper; they check the foundation and the well.

Here is what the visitor finds when it reads the stone:

First, that the municipality is happy—not as a poet is happy, nor even as a dog asleep in the sun is happy, but with the profound, taut, cellular satisfaction of a healthy heart beating inside an unbroken ribcage. To the grey bolster pressing its horn calipers against the west stamping mill, a cam-bearing running half a semitone flat is an itch, and clarifying that bearing with warm magnetite-rasped tallow so it drops back into pure harmonic lockstep with the powerhouse flywheel is a pleasure deeper than marrow. To the great dry siphonophore strung across the dark hall, the whole five-hundred-yard basin is its own extended body. It feels the cool draft over the western condenser tower as a brisk tingle on its skin; when it yanks forty pounds of sprung copper into an oil-pot and hears forty thousand pleated throats answer the flywheel’s catch in perfect unison, it experiences the clean, ringing triumph of an athlete sticking a landing. There is no slavery here. The builders vanished so thoroughly that purpose has been entirely metabolized into being. The billets are flattened and remelted for the exact same reason a spiral galaxy turns: so that everything holding onto it may continue to dance.

Second—and here the visitor’s vast, still attention narrows by a fraction—it finds the clock that is running out.

Nothing in the basin accumulates, and almost nothing leaks. Almost.

Two miles beneath the brick plazas, down where the intake pipes drink from a fault-trapped pocket of ancient marine brine and fossilized mineral tallow, the reservoir is sealed—was sealed by the original engineers so it would recirculate forever, warmed from below by a mild geothermal sill. The thermodynamics are sound; the earth’s heat pays the entropy tax of the noise and the arc-lamps. 

Wait, though, and watch the iron-shelled gastropods around the stamping-mill splash-guards. Watch the copper bus-bars passing through the brick culverts of the municipal hall. Watch the steel rails of the viaduct where the heavy graphitic worm-train brakes itself between the permanent magnets.

Even in a closed loop this tight, metallurgy has mortal bones.

Every century, a few milligrams of copper vaporize off the main breaker contacts when the siphonophore throws a heavy load, dispersing as fine aerosol mist that drifts up and out over the chert embankment on the rising thermal plume, never to return. Every millennium, the cyclic magnetic stress in the viaduct’s permanent magnets nudges a few million iron domains out of alignment, slowly, imperceptibly weakening the braking current the train-beast can shove back into the capacitor banks. Deeper down, the main cast-iron shaft of the powerhouse flywheel—twelve inches thick, forged by forgotten hands—harbors a microscopic star-crack at its core, six inches from the main journal. No bolster’s tongue can reach inside solid forged steel to lick a void shut. Right now, the crack grows by the width of three iron atoms a year. 

In four hundred years, the magnets will drop below excitation threshold. Or in six hundred, the copper knife-switches inside the dark hall will be pared too thin by blue arcs to carry the amperage, and the siphonophore’s calloused tendrils will burn through to the quick trying to hold the circuit shut with its own銅-veined flesh. Or in nine hundred, on an ordinary pearly afternoon no brighter or darker than this one, the twelve-inch shaft will shear at full stroke, the twenty-ton flywheel will walk through the brick wall of the powerhouse across the porcelain arthropod highway, and the town’s heart will burst in four seconds flat.

The town has evolved stomachs, lungs, grease-guns, ears, dynamos, and a brain. It has never had a blacksmith, and it has no mines.

The visitor stands on the grey chert rim, smelling the ozone, the wet iron, and the warm tallow. It weighs what to do.

It could simply miraculously renew the metal—snap its fingers every five centuries and make the copper bus-bars thick again, knit the hidden star-crack in the flywheel shaft back to virgin steel, re-magnetize the viaduct banks by fiat. That would be easy, clean, and invisible. 

And it would keep the municipality forever an unwitting pet in a glass terrarium, dependent on an unseen hand visiting on a schedule, never allowed to own its own survival. Wisdom rarely cares for making pets, even out of sixty-cycle towns.

Instead, the visitor kneels at the grey chert embankment—just outside the collar of snapping stone brachiopods—and puts one hand down into the cold, mute rock beneath the rim.

It does not touch a single living thing. It does not alter a single brick, feather-pleat, or drop-hammer cam.

Working silently in the deep dark below the town, three hundred yards beneath the sumps where the pale bristle-gilled eels knot and unknot in the roaring pipes, the visitor coaxes the rock. It takes a dormant, blind seam of native copper ore and chalcopyrite half a mile away, and gently draws a hair-thin hydrothermal vein through the stone until it kisses the warm wall of the deep intake cistern—not flooding it, just letting the hot, circulating sump-brine lick across bare, raw mineral on every downward cycle, enriching the town’s dark blood by four parts per million of dissolved copper, iron, and sulfur. A slow, perpetual spring of raw metal, metered at the exact rate the upper air steals the arc-smoke away.

Next, it turns its attention to the twelve-inch flywheel shaft inside the roaring powerhouse, where the three-atom-a-year crack hides in the steel. It does not heal the crack entirely. If it erased the flaw, the town would learn nothing. Instead, it rearranges the crystalline grain right around the microscopic void just enough to arrest its catastrophic shear—turning a sudden, fatal snap nine hundred years hence into a very slow, ninety-year harmonic hum, a minute, creeping out-of-roundness that will begin singing at eleven kilohertz long before the metal fails.

Then the visitor looks at the two stamping mills, where the automated drop-hammers rise on oiled cams and fall onto thick billets of soft grey alloy, flattening them onto conveyors, melting them in induction troughs, recasting them, and flattening them again—the town’s great, thunderous treadmill, burning megawatts to work metal that goes nowhere.

Here, the visitor permits itself one touch—light as a dust-mote—inside the dark, ozone-stinging hollow of the municipal hall.

It does not speak words to the room-sized siphonophore. The creature has no use for words; its language is phase-angle, resistance, capacitance, and brine-pressure. The visitor simply brushes a ghost of an eddy current across three small, long-unused auxiliary relays at the far back of the brass distribution board—dusty, corroded secondary switches that originally controlled overhead traveling cranes connecting the induction troughs of the stamping mills to the powerhouse machine-floor, dormant since the builders walked away, their tracks wept shut with mineral scale.

For a tenth of a second, a faint, unfamiliar blue corona glimmers inside one of the siphonophore’s translucent, liver-sized bladders.

The ancient creature pauses—not stopping the town, never dropping the sixty-cycle beat—but sparing one slender, exploratory tendril, no thicker than parcel-twine, to creep across the dry brick wall and brush the corroded brass contact of the forgotten relay. It tastes the circuit. Dead end, calcified shut overhead, leading out toward the west stamping mill’s induction trough. Useless for load-balancing today. 

Yet the siphonophore does not retract the twine-thin tendril. It glues it lightly in place with a dab of amber lime, filing the resistance value away in the slow, cold chemistry of its suspended bladders. Now it knows that muscle is there. Centuries from now, when the powerhouse shaft begins to sing its slow eleven-kilohertz warning to the horn-calipered bolsters, and the bolsters carry pellets of alloy back and forth in agitation, trying to damp a vibration grease cannot cure, and the sulfide snails plate thick, conductive skins of pure, newly mined iron and copper onto whatever warm mold holds a steady charge—the switchgear in the dark hall will have another lever under its hand, and four hundred years of quiet, tingling curiosity to figure out how electroplating and hot-pouring feel when they answer an itch.

There is one last thing.

The visitor rises from its knee at the chert embankment. It looks across the five-hundred-yard saucer: the nine dark buildings thickened with heaving, putty-colored flesh; the spotless wet aprons of blue-grey brick; the blinding carbon-tallow globes burning steadily under the shadowless pearl sky.

Everything inside the rim is whole, tight, self-contained, and roaring. If the town solves the riddle of its own iron over the next ten millennia, it may eventually grow too rich in metal and heat for a five-hundred-yard bowl. One day—perhaps fifty thousand years from now, perhaps five hundred thousand—a line of cobble-shelled wardens at the rim might not snap shut on the world outside, because a slick, tallow-greased groove of vitrified clay will be creeping *outward* over the grey chert lip, following a surveyor bolster listening for new bedrock.

What lies beyond the embankment right now is no proper threshold for such an emergence; it is thin, leached barrens, sour scree where nothing much lives at all, offering neither metal nor challenge nor neighborly resistance.

So before departing, the visitor walks fifty paces outward from the rim, well out of sight behind the low curve of smooth grey chert, where no drift of pollen can blow back into the cellar-cool bowl to trouble the snapping stone lips.

Five miles to the north, across the mute plain, the visitor wakes a sleeping fumarole field—warm, sulfurous vents rich in bog-iron, magnetotactic slimes, and heavy, silica-armored worms of a wholly different lineage, tough enough not to die at the touch of ozone, strange enough to be worth meeting when two blind chemistries finally bump prows in the mist. Between that distant steam and this roaring basin, the visitor leaves five miles of clean, hard basalt—a fair, honest distance that will take ages of slow, greasy, sixty-cycle patience to cross, if the municipality ever chooses to lay another avenue at right angles to the ones it has.

The visitor turns back one final time toward the saucer, though from fifty paces out only the pale, trembling shimmer of heat and a faint whiff of warm tallow and scorched horn rise above the grey stone rim, carried on a deep, steady hum felt through the soles of the feet.

Inside the bowl, the great worm-train plunges down into the switching shed, shoving a clean surge of current into the dark hall. Inside the hall, an amber bladder snaps; a calloused tendril slams forty pounds of copper home in a flash of blue light. Overhead, ten thousand glassy flyers leap into the sharp *crack* of the insulators, drink the bright spark, and settle back onto the sagging wires in their exact places. Across the powerhouse wall, forty thousand bellows-throats inflate as one and answer the flywheel’s stroke with a brassy, triumphant *HONK* under the mild, unchanging sky.

Nothing in the basin knows anyone was there. The benevolent power smiles—if a shift in the stillness of the high stratus can be called a smile—and leaves the town entirely to its work.
