A clumsy god would send rain. 

A lesser benevolence, looking down through that blinding white diffuser, would see the sprint for condensation, the cracked lips greased with bird-oil, the cold kilns, the children fishing in the dark seams for bitter meat, and feel the sharp, arrogant itch of pity. Such a power would crack an aquifer in the center of the main plaza. It would bid timber to sprout along the causeways, calm the violent spill of the katabatic chutes, and part the overcast to let warm, golden sunlight pour in. 

And within a year, the town would be dead. 

Standing water would drown the midge seams and turn the composting pits sour. Trees would break the sightlines across the mile-wide floor, rendering the townsfolk mute and blind to one another, shattering their great, architectural sentences into gibberish behind curtains of leaves. Without the dry counter-gusts, the splayed swifts and salted crab-meat would rot on the racks. Without the cold downdrafts, the great purple-veined hounds would overheat and burst their hearts on the stone. Worse still: the dry, incorruptible bones of the ancestors stacked in the courtyard baffles would soften, green with mold, and crumble into stink, robbing the living of the quiet company of their dead.

The wise power sees all of this in the space between one heartbeat and the next. 

It watches the basin not as a site of deprivation, but as an astonishingly intricate watchwork—an agreement struck over millennia between bone, marl, mist, and lung. To love a thing wisely is to love the tension that holds it upright. These people do not suffer their world; they *are* its grammar. Look at the four slab-lifters dropping into a squat at the flick of a woman’s palm. Look at the ten greased bodies purring together in the plum-lit dark, fingers tapping tiny plaza-echoes onto a sister’s collarbone while the hide roof *thrums* overhead. There is no misery there, only fierce, coherent grace.

Yet the wise power also sees far down the long curve of time—further than any barometer-hound can scent. And looking deep into the stone beneath the town, and up toward the lip of the plateau three hundred feet above, it sees three slow, silent thieves creeping toward the bowl.

First: the salt. Because the basin is a closed dish with no river to flush it, every mist-squall that drops mineral wash from the upper crust and evaporates leaves its ghost behind. Century by century, the white rings are thickening. Five hundred years from now, the grey-yellow lichen will choke on brine, the cave-fungus in the deep fissures will shrivel, and the blind amphibians will vanish from the hooks.

Second: the stone itself. Soapstone and chalky slate are soft. Ten thousand years of suction-soled moccasins, skittering crab claws, and dragged slate baffles are grinding the floor thinner, widening the deep seams until, one day, the trapped dampness below will lie too deep for a whale-bone rib to reach, and the cisterns will weep their water away through porous, rotten marl before a jar can be filled.

Third: the eyes. Even with tallow-chalk smeared across the cheekbones, even with the deep, leathery folding of the brow, the relentless ultraviolet bounce off wet pale stone steals sight early here. By forty, a basin-dweller’s lenses begin to cloud like boiled fish-eyes. By fifty, many can no longer read a neighbor’s elbow-snap across a plaza, condemning their last years to the dark of the domes, unable to speak across the light.

The wise power considers this. It will not break their silence. It will not insult their skill. If it acts at all, its hand must move at the speed of geology and taste like nothing more than luck.

So the power reaches down—not into the sky, and not into the plazas, where an idle movement is a shout—but twenty feet beneath the floor, into the sunless, flooded roots of the bedrock fissures.

There, in the cold dark below the reach of the children’s bone-hooks, the power nudges the chemistry of a single, microscopic extremophile already clinging to the wet slate. It does not invent a beast; it merely widens an appetite. Over the course of three quiet seasons, this deep-fissure microbe learns to drink the excess dissolved salts washing down from the surface plazas and bind them with dissolved chalk, precipitating the brine out onto the deep chasm walls as a hard, dark, glassy sinter—smooth as obsidian, impermeable as glaze. 

Up on the surface, nothing seems to change, except that the faint white crusts left by dry gusts stop thickening. And down in the seams, as the water grows slightly sweeter, the mats of translucent cave-fungus grow just a thumb’s-breadth thicker. When the children go out at twilight with the hounds, pressing their ears to the stone, the wet *slap* of the pale amphibians comes a little more often. And when a storm-surge washes small, tumbled pebbles of that new, dark, salt-forged stone up out of the cracks onto the grey marl, the potters and baffle-builders find them. 

A woman picks up a piece of it one afternoon after wringing out her gut-frame. It is heavy, smooth as tallow, and soot-black. When she rubs it with a thumb, a fine, velvety black grease comes away—far darker than grey chalk. She smears a line of it across her cheekbones and beneath her lower lids. Instantly, the brutal upward knife of the glare softens into cool, crisp relief. Across the plaza, thirty yards away, her brother’s silhouette snaps into sharp, dark-edged focus against the white sky. She raises one oiled arm horizontal, palm up, holding the black pebble between two fingers, and tilts her chin twice: *Look here. Good.*

Only one thing more does the power do, and this it places at the far western curve of the bowl, up near the soapstone tables where the dead lie netted beneath the dry katabatic chutes.

In a wide, shallow pocket where the wind loops back upon itself before climbing out of the basin, the power wakes a dormant spore blown down from a mountain range half a world away and ten thousand years ago, coaxing it to marry the native grey-yellow lichen. What creeps out across that barren western slope over the next decade is still only a crust—no taller than a fingernail, offering no leaf to rustle and no twig to burn. It makes no sound in the wind. 

But it drinks the stray, bitter dust of the upper plateau and spins a dense, tough, fibrous mat that grips crumbling soapstone like rawhide, halting the slow erosion of the rim. And once a year, when the coldest autumn downdrafts slam into the western curve, this low crust sweats tiny, resinous beads of clear, sharp-smelling gum to keep itself from freezing.

It takes four years before a hound, trotting past the salt-rings of the drying-tables, catches the scent on its split nostrils—something clean and piercing, utterly unlike tallow, crab-roe, or wet dog—and freezes, tail flushing dark purple, pointing at the bare rock.

A boy walks over, squats low on unlocked knees, and scrapes a bead of the clear resin onto his slate knife. That night, crawled behind the double hide curtains into the pitch-black warmth of his clan’s dome, he drops the bead onto the tiny braided-hair wick of the bird-fat lamp.

The plum-sized amber glow does not grow brighter. It does not roar up into a fire that would crack their ancient kilns or make them lazy. It simply steadies. And through the close, velvet dark of twelve whispering people, cutting gently across the fierce tang of rancid grease and cured shell, drifts a thin, astonishing thread of scent—like crushed high-altitude pine and cold snow, a smell none of them have a word for, yet every lung in the dome drinks in with a sudden, quiet pause in their purring speech. Hands rest still on knees and collarbones for three long breaths in the dark. 

Then an elder’s thumb taps twice against a granddaughter’s wrist—*good, keep it*—and the soft, liquid murmur resumes, weaving the new word seamlessly into the dark while the wind *thrums* against the hide overhead.

High above, beyond the blinding white ceiling, the wise power withdraws its attention, leaving no footprint, no temple, no debt—only clear eyes, sweet seams, a breath of mountain resin in the dark, and ten thousand more years for the town to finish whatever sentence it is speaking with the mist.
