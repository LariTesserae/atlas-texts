To look upon this cleft with wisdom is to immediately resist the urge to soften it. A lesser benevolence might look at the convulsing, suffocating things during the four-second silences and wish to give them peace, or warmth, or a quiet tide-pool away from the thunder. That would be murder dressed as mercy. These eleven creatures are children of the roar; they are flesh made out of waste physics, sculpted by an accidental god of steel and copper fifty yards away. To quiet the room would dissolve them.

Yet a wise eye sees the flaw in their world instantly, because a wise eye sees time.

Machines do not last like mountains. Whatever thrums behind the eastern wall—pump, crusher, or penstock—was built by hands, and things built by hands eventually break, or are upgraded, or are shut down for two weeks in August so men in hardhats can replace a cracked impeller or re-insulate a leaking busbar. An engineer a mile away will one day notice a ground-fault warning on a console, trace the stray DC bleed into the coastal shelf, drive a fresh copper grounding rod elsewhere, and pat himself on the back for saving half a kilowatt. 

And in the dark cleft, fifty yards away, a brand-new branch of the tree of life—eleven silent, armored keystones holding up a tiny, jewel-bright sanctuary of wrens, beetles, and sleeping salamanders—will curl their golden bellies upward in the sudden, permanent quiet, and rot in plain rainwater within an hour.

So the power does not touch the creatures, and it does not break the wild, self-made perfection of the chamber. It goes into the rock between the machine and the cleft, and it works solely through the laws the place already obeys.

First, it addresses the four-second death, and the long silence waiting in the future.

Twenty yards behind the eastern wall, hidden entirely inside the solid, unquarried greywacke where no drill bit will ever stray, the power alters the mineralogy along the fault lines. It takes existing pockets of iron-rich clay, porous shale, and fractured quartz, and subtly reorganizes their crystal lattices. Over the span of a single winter night, a broad, silent lens of bedrock becomes a massive, natural supercapacitor and a piezoelectric battery. 

Now, while the distant turbines scream, they are no longer just leaking stray current directly onto the wet slate; they are charging the stone itself. And when the coastal wind howls above and the heavy ocean tide slams against the outer shelf half a mile away, the microscopic flexing of the continental crust squeezes those newly aligned quartz veins, trickling a steady, geological trickle of millivolts into the same reservoir. 

The next morning, when the unseen engines drop into their four-second idle, the roar still cuts out. The cymatic grid still collapses into sloshing rings; the bladder-ferns still drop their heavy, sudden rain. It is good for the young ones to occasionally fall into the pool—that is how they mix, and learn the floor—so the acoustic drop is left mostly intact, softened only at the sharpest edge by a slight, ringing resonance in the newly tuned rock, like a bell humming faintly after it has been struck. 

But down in the pool, the electrical bleed does not vanish. As the machine’s current drops to zero, the charged bedrock smoothly discharges back into the wet pyrite seams. 

In the sudden hush, the three large creatures flinch as the acoustic load disappears, their dorsal papillae smoothing out—but their edges do not curl inward in agony. Underneath them, the silver fuzz of microscopic hydrogen and oxygen continues to pearl steadily from the metal veins. For the first time in their existence, they experience silence without suffocation. They lie flat and calm in the sloshing water, tasting the quiet, until the relays throw and they bristle back to work. And if, ten years from now, the distant power station goes cold forever, the rock will hold enough tidal and geothermal trickle to keep the pyrite fizzing for centuries—giving the eleven slow generations to learn how to live on the heartbeat of the ocean’s crash instead of the twenty-two-second hydraulic pump.

Second, the power looks up at the crooked, narrow tear forty feet overhead, and looks down at the coastal shelf. 

Eleven is too small a number, and a fresh rockfall is an unstable roof. A single hard freeze-thaw cycle could drop twenty tons of overhang right onto the three elders in the pool, crushing the entire lineage before it has truly begun.

High on the eastern overhang, where a five-ton block of greywacke is already wedged loose by winter ice—destined to fall in two years and smash the flat floor—the power nudges a single microscopic grain of feldspar. The frost-wedge shifts its angle by three degrees. Three minutes later, during the afternoon idle shift, instead of waiting two years to drop whole, a lesser flake shears off cleanly now. It is the size of a tabletop. Guided by the exact angle of its cleavage plane, it slices down through the mist, misses the creatures by four feet, and wedges itself diagonally into the corner of the eastern wall, just above the pool. 

It forms an indestructible lean-to—a submerged cave-mouth of stone right over the richest pyrite seam, angled to deflect any future boulders harmlessly toward the vacant western side of the chamber, while leaving the center open so the wrens still have their dark mirrors to dive toward.

Third, and last, the power considers loneliness—not of the heart, which these blind, rubbery capacitors do not possess, but the evolutionary loneliness of a dead end. A pocket with no exit is eventually a tomb.

Beneath the ankle-deep pool, deep in the hairline fractures where the frigid groundwater forces its way in, the power traces the water back toward the sea. Half a mile west, far below the low-tide mark, groundwater vents seep out into dark submarine basalt canyons where the surf hammers endlessly against mineral-rich stone—another place of relentless low-frequency roar, cold water, and faint natural galvanic currents generated by salt water washing over differing metal ores. 

The cracks connecting this inland cleft to those submarine vents are currently choked with thick, compacted glacial clay and crushed slate-paste. Nothing larger than a bacterium can pass through. 

Gently, using the next twenty-two-second hydraulic thud of the distant machinery, the power loosens a plug of silt deep in the subterranean plumbing. With the next pulse, a little more washes clear. Over a week of synchronized throbbing, a winding, smooth-walled conduit opens all the way down through the dark bedrock to the sub-tidal sea caves—just wide enough for a thumbprint-sized, translucent juvenile to ride a downward-pulling eddy when the tide goes out, trading the hum of the dynamo for the thunder of the Atlantic trenches.

Nothing else is done. No glowing light is left in the mist; no warmth is added to the freezing spray; no human mind is nudged to come marvel at them with flashlights and specimen jars. In fact, on the windy coastal shelf forty feet above, where a faint deer-path passes near the jagged lip of the sky-slit, a sprawling thicket of coastal gorse and thorny blackthorn finds the soil suddenly rich with trace phosphorus, and grows twice as dense and tangled by spring, encouraging hikers and grazing sheep alike to curve thirty yards inland without ever knowing why.

Down in the pewter gloom, the relays slam shut. The walls sweat. The diamonds leap back onto the face of the water. A dun-colored wren drops like a bullet out of the wind, checks its reflection in the glassy disc above an ancient, rubbery back, snaps a midge out of the air, and rockets back into the sky—leaving the cleft to its thunder, its strange children, and its newly promised time.
