A lesser power would look upon the pewter gloom and pity it. A lesser benevolence, drunk on its own warmth, would tear a hole in the slate ceiling to pour down golden light; it would melt the rime, summon sweet green grass to choke the iron-sedge, and teach the silent people to sing with open mouths in warm valleys. And in a single season of clumsy mercy, the oiled wool would rot, the boilers would overheat in air too thick and wet to vent, the intricate nasal labyrinths would bleed, and ten million years of patient, greased perfection would seize up and rust away into noise and ruin.

Wisdom knows a masterpiece when it sees one. Love knows when to keep its hands behind its back.

The Power watches for three hundred years—hardly a breath to the basalt, less than the life of a single great matriarch—simply listening to the *shhh-shhh* of wet wool against sedge and feeling the low, contented bone-hums passing from forehead to forehead inside the warm felt blisters. It sees that these people are not suffering. They are not enduring a curse; they are inhabiting a harmony so deep and slow that faster minds mistake it for emptiness. There is no hunger here that patience does not feed, no loneliness that a blink of indigo across three miles of gravel cannot answer, no death that does not become grease, marriage, bone-arch, and life again.

Yet even the most settled architecture has a thermodynamic leak. 

Looking down not just through the fog, nor merely through the crust of frost-shattered shale, the Power reads the deep ledger of the plateau’s basement rock. Millions of years of freeze and thaw, of tungsten-jaws scraping oxides and flanged hooves precipitating iron, of wind-shears stripping fine metallic dust off the curved edges of the horizon and dropping it into the abyssal barometric trenches far away—all of it carries a tiny, inexorable tax. Every millennium, the surface scree grows a fraction of a percent poorer in raw sulfur, soluble phosphorus, and trace copper. The beasts must walk forty paces farther between rich veins; the yeast-curds steep ten minutes longer for the same nourishment; the cushion plants wait an extra decade to seed. Left entirely alone, in another four hundred thousand years, the great silent engine will slowly, gently starve of salt and spark, spinning down to red, then black, not from failure, but because the plate has been licked clean.

To fix this loudly—to crack open a volcano or drop a mountain of bright ore onto the plain—would shatter the silence and insult the gardeners.

So the Power reaches down beneath the world, thirty miles below the frost-line, down where the roots of the ancient basalt meet the slow, blind churn of the mantle. 

With a touch no heavier than the old man’s tungsten chisel on wet shale, it coaxes a dormant plume of deep heat a fraction of a degree northward, stopping it miles short of ever breaking the crust. No stone trembles on the surface. Not a single tarn ripples. Instead, over centuries, deep hydrothermal veins begin to sweat silently upward through hair-thin fractures in the bedrock—warm, mineral-heavy brines rich in reduced sulfur, iron, copper, and trace phosphates, cooling completely in the last half-mile of stone before they ever touch the air. 

They do not erupt as geysers. They simply seep into the bottoms of a dozen scattered snowmelt basins along the far northern turn of the migration, enriches the black mud by grains per decade, crusting the basin rims with pale yellow and blue-green mineral rime that looks entirely native to the frost.

Then, the Power allows itself one small, quiet indulgence—not to change them, only to answer them in their own tongue.

Deep in a shallow basin where a solitary cow pauses to drink, a young man descends the braided climbing-loops to chip drainage channels for a patch of sedge. Kneeling on the wet gravel, he notices a smooth, fist-sized nodule freshly heaved up from the mud by last winter’s frost—dark, dense, naturally vitrified stone laced with microscopic filaments of native copper and piezoelectric quartz. When he picks it up with his horned fingers to clear the channel, the slight squeeze of his grip sends a faint, warm, silent shiver up his wrist bones, resonant at the exact frequency of a calm human hum, and along the dark seam of the rock runs a brief, hair-thin hairline of cool indigo light before fading back to slate.

He turns it over once in his greased palm. He presses it briefly against his collarbone, feeling how well it holds heat and carries vibration—a stone that remembers a voice for an hour after you hum into it, a pebble that blinks back when held tight.

He does not drop to his knees in terror or build an altar. Altars waste stone and block the wind. Instead, he tucks the warm pebble into his pouch beside the lichen-paste, finishes chipping his three small channels so the black meltwater reaches the sedge without spilling a drop, and climbs slowly back up into the lanolin-scented warmth behind his cow’s broad shoulder—carrying a small, quiet story to press against his wife’s forehead while the fog slides south and the great amber heart beneath them beats on, fed from below for another ten million years, without a single wasted motion.
