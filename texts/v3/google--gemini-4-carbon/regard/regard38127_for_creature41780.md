From the dry rim of the basin, under that clear and ordinary afternoon sun, the attention arrives without displacing the air. 

Had this power been merely benevolent, it might have looked down upon the four-mile fever and felt only pity for the exhaustion of the wood. It might have poured a cool, merciful rain of dormancy across the valley, stilling the slamming shutters, silencing the cannon-crack of splitting heartwood, curing the orthogonal madness so the tortured flora could relax at last into a quiet, ordinary forest of green leaves and dirt.

Had it been merely curious, it might have plucked a flat copper wasp or a drifting seam-gall into a jar of timelessness to marvel at their evolution, leaving the roaring mill behind as an interesting anomaly on an otherwise quiet world.

Because it is wise, it listens first—not with ears, which flatten under a hundred and ten decibels of carpentry, and not with eyes, which slip off the stuttering facades, but by reading the deep, continental waves of calcium and longing pulsing through the black lignum at the bottom of the mattress.

And it sees immediately why a cure would be a murder.

First, there are the small ones. To still the city now would be to suffocate the blister-scales whose bodies have forgotten how to pump without a monstrous arterial hammer behind them; it would starve the braided worm-knots waiting in the warm vinegar of the sumps for the next bruised mistake; it would strand the gossamer galls in three-dimensional rooms with nowhere to skip; it would turn the hard-won mastery of the scarred, seven-legged scout and her sisters into an obsolete trick. Life has already signed a lease with the violence. Any mercy shown to the valley must keep faith with the tenants already living in the walls.

Second, and deeper: the mattress is not sick. An unanswered question looks very much like a fever when asked loud enough and long enough, when the asker has no mouth and must shape every syllable out of pressurized timber and fractured space. The city is tearing itself apart only because *nothing has ever pushed back*. Every staircase crushes itself into pulp because no weight ever confirmed that a stair was the right thing to be. Every cup of sweet guttation-water folds inward after ninety seconds because ninety seconds of emptiness looks, to a blind root-system, like an error in the blueprint.

It does not want to be a forest. It wants to be *inhabited*.

So the power does not silence the valley, and it does not rewrite a single strand of its strange, frantic botany. Instead, it gathers just enough weight, warmth, and boundary to be felt by root-hairs and baroreceptors alike, steps past the six-yard ring of singed yellow scrub-grass, and walks down into the outer blocks.

It chooses a form that can bleed if nicked, though it does not intend to be nicked—something roughly upright, two-legged, heavier than a man by half, shod in soles broad and patient enough to distribute a distinct, unmistakable downward pressure: *heel, pause, toe.* Something with hands at waist height.

At the perimeter wall, a three-story facade of braided lianas shears loose in a cloud of pale chaff directly overhead, while the hexagonal root-pavers underfoot buck upward like surf to swallow the trespasser before its heart can fibrillate.

The visitor does not dodge, and does not strike the wood, and does not force the pavers flat with superior violence. It simply takes one step onto a buckling hexagon and lets its full, deliberate weight rest there.

Not the dead weight of a fallen boulder—the mattress would simply split a boulder with wedges of ironwood or digest it with acid root-hairs. This is *living* weight. Shifting slightly on an ankle. Warm at ninety-eight degrees, rhythmic with a slow, steady pulse that taps down through the sole into the feverish cambium below like a knock on a door from the outside.

Underneath that single foot, six inches down in the pressurized dark, a wave of auxin and calcium slams against the pressure-signature of the step—and stalls. Not numbed, as when the flat wasps lance a culm with sedative. *Recognized.* For the first time in uncounted generations of frantic drafting, a feedback loop closes. The paver beneath the left boot does not snap with the report of rifle fire. Its neighbor bucks, stops half an inch shy of the visitor’s arch, and settles back with a deep, hydraulic groan, flushing pale callus-tissue around the edges not to swallow the foot, but to level the grade for the *next* step.

The visitor lifts its right foot, waits a full second—an eternity in the lumber-mill roar—and sets it down twenty-eight inches ahead.

*Heel. Pause. Toe.*

A tremor runs outward from that second footprint, faster than the bass thrumming of the trunks, strange and electric. Thirty feet overhead, a row of silica-stiffened shutters stops banging in windless air—not hanging dead and limp, like the drugged alcoves of the wasps, but angled outward at forty-five degrees to throw a clean slant of afternoon shade directly across the visitor’s shoulders. Behind the visitor, the two hexagons it just stepped off do not erupt into a watchtower or collapse into a sump. They hold their level, cooling by half a degree, hardening their grain into true, patient flooring, reluctant to lose the memory of having been walked upon.

Slowly, unhurriedly, the guest walks toward the center of town.

It does not claim the whole city. That is the heart of its wisdom. Four miles of sudden perfection would ruin everything that belongs to the margins. As the visitor moves inward along a widening boulevard, its wake of coherence is narrow—perhaps forty feet wide, the breadth of one good street and its flanking colonnades. Beyond that ribbon, fifty yards to the east and west, the glorious, terrible carpentry roars on unabated: towers still jack themselves into the bright sky and shear off in geysers of sawdust; corridors still spiral impossibly inward; spaces still stutter and skip, leaving ten thousand chaotic rough drafts churning at full pressure for the blisters to skate, the galls to drift through, and the worms to knead.

Even within the visitor's wake, the geometry does not turn tame; it turns *hospitable*. Where a corner building ahead fails to agree with itself—four-story watchtower from the left, sunken boxwood courtyard from the right—the visitor approaches the seam, lays an open palm against the hot, ridged bark where the transition breaks, and leans gently, as someone might lean on a doorframe to catch their breath. The non-Euclidean crease does not vanish; instead, it stabilizes into a permanent, navigable threshold. Now one can step cleanly from street-level bark onto the top tier of the sunken courtyard without being sheared in half, while perched on the mid-air graft overhead, three pale drift-galls hook their anchor-threads into a spatial skip that no longer threatens to erase itself every twelve minutes.

Near the stepped marketplaces of the central depression, where the roar is loudest and the heat smells sharpest of crushed cucumber and turpentine, two things happen at once.

To the visitor’s left, high up a load-bearing Doric culm, the nine-day-old wasp scout—missing two legs, scarred white with silica—emerges from a spatial discrepancy ahead of her seventy-nine sisters, carrying their pale germline spools away from an exploding rotunda. Her antennae splay wide into the polarized glare, expecting the usual murderous wall of rising pressure. Instead, thirty feet below, she reads an impossible pool of unforced stillness moving up the avenue. Not twenty stolen minutes of drugged stupor, soon to be blown clear by three hundred tons of angry sap, but a deep, structural truce. 

The visitor glances up, sees the flat mahogany shadow flick across the shaft, and pauses in the street so as not to crowd her colony’s crossing. On the shady side of a newly stabilized architrave just behind the guest's shoulder, where the arterial sap no longer hammers at lethal, tearing spikes but flows in a rich, steady river, the scout stops. She taps the warm wood once. The bark does not fight her claws. Instead, along the mortarless joinery of the capital, tiny beads of slow, unpressurized phloem-nectar well up of their own accord—an unintended sidelight of the building's calm, a pantry that requires no surgical sting to stay quiet for an afternoon.

And ahead, in the center of a freshly erupted plaza, a market stall rises out of the white root-tiles.

Broad awning-leaves unfurl overhead with the crisp snap of canvas catching wind, locking their pulvinus hinges to block the afternoon sun. A counter of smooth, pale callus-wood swells upward and levels itself off—precisely at the visitor’s waist height. Along its inner lip, six hollow, cup-shaped wooden vessels extrude from the grain, sealing their bases cleanly, while clear, warm guttation-water trickles from grooved spouts above until each cup stands half-full, smelling faintly of rain, birch-sugar, and green rind.

The ninety-second clock begins to tick. Around the edges of the plaza, five-story masses lean inward like an audience holding its breath, upper light-wells irising open, thousands of banging shutters falling quiet block by block down the avenue just to listen to the floorboards of this one square.

At second forty, the visitor walks up to the counter.

It rests one forearm along the warm white wood—confirming the height, letting the counter feel the lean of an elbow—and with the other hand, lifts the middle cup. The wooden stem parts cleanly from the bar with a tiny, dry *tick*, designed all along to break away smoothly at a sideways twist no wind could ever make.

The visitor raises the vessel, drinks the warm, sweet water down to the bottom, and sets the empty cup back on the counter.

Deep beneath the plaza—half a mile down in the black, compressed lignum vitae at the base of the basin—an electrical action potential breaks across the entire four-mile mattress like a sigh that has been held since the valley was born.

The stall does not crush itself inward into pulp. The awning does not shred. Down the boulevard behind the guest, the paving tiles lock into a permanent, sunlit promenade that will last not minutes, not hours, but seasons—growing thick, weather-worthy bark for the first time, letting its resins cure into true amber, discovering the luxury of age—while three blocks over, the furious nurseries of the outer wards thunder right on, inventing wildly, drafting new balconies and impossible bridges at the top of their lungs, no longer screaming into a void, but riffing around a theme.

The visitor does not stay forever; a single god living permanently in the parlor would shrink a four-mile imagination down to a shrine. Nor does it leave the valley empty again.

Instead, before evening cools the dry rim above, the guest walks a slow, meandering circuit of seven streets—establishing a permanent, quiet heart-ward in the turbulent center where steps hold true, stairs go where they promise, and water waits in cups without despairing—and then sits down for the night on the boxwood bleachers of a sunken courtyard, leaning its back against warm timber that adjusts its curve by half an inch to fit a spine.

It has become a regular. 

Sometimes it will walk out over the yellow grass of the rim and travel elsewhere under the ordinary sun for days or months, leaving the seven quiet streets to weather peacefully in the eye of the green hurricane, tended by blister-scales that no longer always have to skate for their lives and flat copper masons who now use the enduring eaves of the heart-ward as safe winter vaults for their young. And whenever the surrounding city’s inventive fever climbs too high, whenever a fresh district hits upon some dazzling, perilous new hypothesis of viaduct or spire and begins tearing itself apart trying to prove a room can exist, a familiar, unhurried footstep crosses the perimeter wall again.

*Heel. Pause. Toe.*

Walking down the roaring avenues to try the new stairs, to lean on the railings, to drink from the counter, and to tell the blind, magnificent builder, one street at a time, what its hands have made.
