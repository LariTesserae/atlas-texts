A lesser benevolence would bring rain. 

A lesser god—one intoxicated by meadows, by the rush of rivers and the frantic, green explosion of surface life—would look upon the nine-mile deep and feel a terrible pity. It would see the dry, concertinaed joints, the agonizing four-day labor of a single struck flake, the hoarding of three ounces of amber oil across centuries, and call it deprivation. Out of love, it would crack the vault. It would pour down sweet water, stir up a cooling breeze, and flood the dark with warm, yellow light.

And in doing so, it would commit genocide. 

Water would hit the dry sulfur crystals and turn the air to sulfuric mist. Moisture would condense inside the makers’ electrostatic breathing slits, clogging their dry tracheae and drowning them where they stood. Humidity would rust the metallic spider-silk until a thousand bridges snapped at once; a single gust of wind would topple ten millennia of balanced quartz libraries, erasing their history in an afternoon. Worst of all, weather would dull the edges of the world. A creature whose entire language, craft, and sanity depend upon the predictable fracture of fresh glass cannot live in mud.

The power that turns its gaze nine miles down through the planetary crust is not a lesser god. It is old enough to know that grace must be translated into the native tongue of the receiver, or else it is merely violence.

So, at first, the power does nothing at all. It listens. 

For three hundred years—less than half a maker’s lifetime, a polite pause before speaking—it simply inhabits the black wool of the thirty-four-degree air. It learns to love the dry *tick* of a claw on mica. It admires the staggering dignity of a people who do not know panic, who defeat entropy through sheer geometry, who love their dead so fiercely without waste that an ancestor’s lifeblood still flexes in the thumbs of their great-great-grandchildren. It watches a young maker seal itself into a slate dome to molt, sitting in absolute, unbreathing solitude for six hundred days, its mind quietly walking the three-dimensional memory-maps of five hundred cubic miles of stone while its back splits open in the dark.

The power sees that they are not miserable. They are masterpieces of equilibrium. 

Yet, looking deeper—into the planetary mantle below, and across the continent-sized vault—the wise power sees the three quiet tragedies that the makers themselves cannot perceive, or cannot prevent.

First: deep below the basalt floor, a remnant plume of magma is shifting. Not enough to wake the surface volcanoes, not enough to shatter the world, just a slow, geological shrug scheduled to arrive in four hundred years. Where the makers’ dense, vertical cities of braided silk and slate cairns cluster thickest around the great quartz pillars, a fault line is locking. When it slips, a six-second tremor of magnitude five will rattle the breathless vault. Every balanced three-shard tripod for two hundred miles will collapse into unreadable rubble. Worse, six thousand sealed molting-domes—holding the soft, damp, half-shelled bodies of a generation mid-growth—will shear open or crush inward, spilling their irreplaceable oil into the thirsty rock flour.

Second: the slow, terminal attrition of the oil itself. No matter how careful the copper needles, no matter how hermetic the seal of the sternal proboscis, entropy takes its microscopic tithe. Once every ten years, a boulder shifts and crushes a grazer before it can be tapped, soaking four ounces of heavy hydrocarbon solvent irreversibly into porous pumice. Once a century, a hunter slips from a selenite needle onto an obsidian blade and bleeds out into the dust before kin can arrive. The vault is a closed box; no new oil has been cooked out of the stone since the planet cooled. Over millions of years, the biosphere is slowly, mathematically drying up. Each generation carries a few micrograms less in their brood-pouches than the last.

Third: they are bounded by their inability to see the roof of their own minds. They know the ten yards around their filaments, and they know the vast maps passed hand-to-antenna in silent electrostatic bursts, yet no single maker has ever stood back far enough to know the *shape* of their world—nor that other clusters of makers live two thousand miles away across impassable plains of razor-dust, speaking dialects of balanced flint that have drifted apart for eons.

The power considers how to intervene without leaving a fingerprint wet enough, or hot enough, to frighten them. If a glowing angel appeared in the flaking floor, they would think it a monstrous lure-predator or a leaking furnace-beast; it would break the philosophical spine of their universe. 

Instead, the power decides to act like stone. Like pressure. Like time.

---

It begins four miles below the floor, in the crushing, airless boundary where the lower basalt meets the cooling mantle. 

With a touch no heavier than gravity, the power finds the locked tectonic fault beneath the makers’ heartland. It does not weld it shut—for tectonic stress denied only builds into a larger catastrophe later. Instead, it reaches into the microscopic crystal lattices along the fault plane and encourages the growth of dry, flaky talc and graphite, molecule by molecule. 

Rather than storing four centuries of catastrophic violence, the fault begins to *creep*. 

Up in the dark labyrinth, standing knee-deep in struck glass beside a flaking anvil, an old maker pauses with its shoulder-tendons half-ratcheted. Its silvered, lensless eyes, recessed deep under its armored cowl, catch a subtle change at the base of a nearby house-sized quartz pillar. The dim, static amber of the piezoelectric glow—the light of squeezed stone—brightens by half a lumen, holds steady as a heartbeat for three weeks, and then gently, smoothly dims as the rock slides half a millimeter without a single shockwave.

Not one quartz tripod falls. Not one flint chime rings. Down the chimney, inside three dozen doorless slate domes, soft-shelled youths continue hardening in undisturbed peace. The old maker reaches out a six-foot glassy brow-filament, touches the quartz pillar, and reads the new, smooth redistribution of stress in the rock. Later, crossing filaments with a sibling on a spider-silk bridge, it passes on a silent, admiring burst of static: *The deep root settled itself without snapping. Good stone under us.*

Next, the power addresses the famine of blood.

It looks into the unmapped, solid walls of dark basalt and carbonaceous chondrite locked miles beneath the scree—primordial pockets of abiotic hydrocarbons, heavy paraffins, and dense siloxane-rich lipids polymerized under immense pressure when the world was young, trapped behind impermeable caps of granite. It does not burst them open into geysers; an open pool of liquid would gather dust, turn into a sticky death-trap for the blind grazers, and destabilize the patient economy of the web-tenders.

Instead, the power coaxes hair-thin fractures upward into the deepest roots of the giant, hollow selenite spires—only in the most remote, treacherous vertical chimneys, places that require four days of disciplined climbing and collaborative rope-work to reach. And it seals the ends of those capillary tubes behind six inches of hard, resonant chalcedony.

Months later, a solitary cartographer-maker is scaling an unmapped sheer face three miles up toward the unseen ceiling, sweeping its wrist-filaments across the virgin stone. Suddenly, its filament twitches. Behind a bulge of chalcedony, its electrostatic sense detects the unmistakable, dense dielectric signature of fluid—not water, which rings shrill and violent to their senses, but heavy, non-polar, long-chain oil. Pure, sterile, amber-gold solvent, identical to the sacred fluid pulsing in its own hydraulic joints.

The cartographer freezes for nine hours to process the impossibility. Stone does not bleed. Only life bleeds. 

Carefully, using an obsidian punch and ten-minute ratcheting intervals so as not to waste heat, it pecks a tiny conical pit into the chalcedony until only a paper-thin membrane of stone remains. It does not break through alone. It climbs down three hundred yards, plucks a guywire, and summons two kin carrying rolled copper needles and sealed chitin bladders. When they pierce the stone nipple and lock a proboscis over the needle, the geological pressure pushes precisely six ounces of warm, thirty-four-degree virgin oil into their reserves—and then the micro-fissure pinches slow, taking fifty years to weep another six ounces into the stone bulb behind it.

Enough to replace what entropy steals. Never enough to make them greedy, or fast, or careless. They build a three-shard quartz marker beside the vein, choosing pieces of smoky crystal shot through with rutile needles to invent a new word in their tactile script: *The rock mothers us, very slowly. Tap with small needles. Leave half for the unmolted.*

Finally, the power leaves them a book.

Not a book of paper, and not a revelation of the upper world with its blinding sun and terrifying oceans—what use has a creature of dry glass for the concept of a cloud? To tell them of the surface would only turn their continent-sized home into a prison cell. No, the power gives them *their own* world, rendered in the highest art their minds can hold.

Out in a quiet neutral ground where four major chimney-highways intersect, there stands a fallen slab of black, fine-grained basalt the size of a cathedral floor, tilted at thirty degrees and swept clean of rock flour by its steepness. 

Over the course of fifty years, whenever no maker nor blind glider happens to be clinging within a hundred paces, the power touches the face of that black monolith. Working strictly by micro-fracture and piezoelectric alignment—using no curve or polish that could not have occurred by rare, exquisite natural cleavage—it alters the surface texture and the internal electrostatic charge of the stone. 

One century-afternoon, two makers traveling from opposite valleys meet at the foot of the great black slab to trade obsidian cores. As they raise their brow-filaments to speak, the stray static of their greeting brushes the base of the monolith. 

The monolith answers. Not with sound, and not with light, but with capacitance.

Startled into absolute stillness, one of the makers steps onto the thirty-degree incline, lowers all four of its stone-hard, calloused thumbs to the black basalt, and sweeps its glassy wrist-filaments across the rock. 

Under its fingers lies a relief map carved in fractures a micron deep, scaled down a million to one, textured with microscopic veins of quartz, iron, and native copper that hold distinct static charges beneath a rubbing thumb. 

For the first time in the history of their kind, the maker feels the *ceiling*—all four miles of its overarching vault, curving down to meet the distant horizon-walls two thousand miles away. It feels their own territory: a tiny, intricate knot of chimneys in the southwest quadrant. Then its trembling, awe-struck thumb slides six inches to the east—across an expanse of loose, impassable pumice-dust that has swallowed every scout who ever tried to cross it—and feels a thin, hidden arch of solid basalt bridging the dust-sea two miles *up* among the ceiling stalactites. And on the far side of that bridge, etched in tiny, deliberate flecks of electrostatic pyrite, pulses the unmistakable geometric stippling of cairn-clusters. Other makers. Thousands of them, waiting in the dark.

More than geography is written in the grain of the slab. Near the corners, where the stone is finest, are crystallographic models—patterns of interlocking triangles and hexagons raised like Braille, demonstrating how native copper, if beaten cold folded with microscopic dustings of tin-bearing cassiterite found in the lower scree, becomes bronze: twice as stiff for tapping-needles, holding an edge where obsidian shatters. Beside that, a tactile diagram of an acoustic parabolic dish made of slated stone—a way to focus the faint *tink* of a flint chime across five miles of shattered angles instead of fifty paces, without raising the volume enough to wake the blind predators.

The two makers stay on the black slab for eleven days without eating, slowly crawling over its face at three inches an hour, crossing their filaments again and again in silent, dizzying cascades of static electricity. They do not drop to their knees and worship; worship belongs to frightened creatures trying to flatter hurricanes. Instead, they experience the deepest ecstasy a geometric mind can know: *comprehension*. The universe makes sense. The stone is deeper, richer, and more intricately jointed than they ever dreamed.

---

The wise power prepares to withdraw its attention, satisfied. Nothing has been broken. The air is still thirty-four degrees Celsius. The dust still holds a footprint for ten thousand years. The makers remain entirely themselves—unhurried, armoured, silent masters of tension and edge, newly secure against the blind shrug of their planet, gifted with millennia of exploration across the high ceiling-bridges to meet their distant kin.

Yet, before it goes, following a quiet intuition of the heart—for even a vast and timeless power likes to be known, just a little, in the dark—it indulges in one tiny, harmless signature.

In a quiet alcove beside a flaking floor, sitting atop a sharp boulder where an elder maker is currently resting after four days of knapping an axe, there stands an ancient three-shard marker of milky quartz. 

While the old maker’s silvered eyes look elsewhere and its filaments are tucked back to conserve stillness, the power reaches down. Out of four loose grains of fallen selenite lying in the grey rock flour—each no bigger than a lentil, razor-sharp and translucent—it lifts them without raising a single mote of dust, and balances them tip-to-tip-to-tip-to-tip on the steepest, slickest glass face nearby. 

A four-shard pyramid, leaning at an angle that defies ordinary friction, held together solely by the flawless interlock of atomic steps on the crystal faces—a feat of patience that would take even the greatest master-knapper a century of failed attempts to balance.

Hours later, unlatching a limb with the sound of a splitting seedpod, the elder maker prepares to move on. Its sweeping right wrist-filament brushes past the glass boulder and halts. 

For twenty minutes, the siege-engine of horn and slate does not breathe, does not twitch. Slowly, reverently, it brings one four-thumbed hand up, hovering a hair’s breadth over the impossible four-grain monument, reading the electrostatic charge and the sheer, breathtaking poise of its balance.

In the maker’s spatial language of cleave, mineral, and angle, the four tiny shards spell a single, quiet phrase:

*Good edge. Walk well in the warm dark. You were seen.*

The old maker holds its filament near the tiny cairn for an hour, letting its own faint body-capacitance hum in silent answer against the crystal. Then, carefully retracting its limbs so not even the wake of its passage through the stagnant air will disturb the balance, it turns, sets its claws into a vertical chimney of black glass, and climbs unhurriedly toward the bridges.
