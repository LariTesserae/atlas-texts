A lesser benevolence would have looked upon the shield and seen deprivation. It would have pitied the trees standing solitary in their fault-line pockets, pitied the old bulls grinding their last fractions of enamel against insoluble quartz, pitied the mothers licking bird-lime from hot stone for a grain of calcium. A lesser power would have cracked the basement rock to raise new mountains, or poured rich, black volcanic loam across the pans, or cut a deep river channel to drain the blinding sheets and call up a forest.

And in doing so, it would have murdered a masterpiece.

The mind that turned its attention here was older than mountains, and therefore knew what mountains cost, and knew the rare, quiet dignity of what remains when all the frantic spending of height is done. It looked down—or rather, looked *inward*, resting its awareness along the interface of warm water and banded gneiss—and recognized an equilibrium honed so fine that generosity, carelessly applied, would act like poison. Dump phosphorus into these saucers, and the velvety, slow-growing stromatolite domes would choke beneath a violent green bloom, rot in the afternoon heat, strip the shallow bathwater of oxygen by midnight, and suffocate every lungfish, crab, and tadpole for fifty miles. Deepen the soil, and brushwood would march across the mirror, blinding the acoustics, tangling the stilts of the silver runners, stealing the open sightlines by which the slow grazers keep their calves alive. Soften the quartz, and the padded feet engineered across five hundred millennia for wet stone would rot in mud.

First, then, the power simply listened.

It listened to the metallic rattle of ten million frogs bouncing off hard rock and sky-colored water. It felt the patient, hydraulic *scritch-scritch* of horn bevels peeling purple mats in six inches of glare. It watched the slate-bellied beasts squinting against the upside-down sun, walking single-file along grooves worn a quarter-inch deep into continental basement by the devotion of their ancestors’ feet. It saw the old bull in his thirtieth year, jawbone smooth and empty at last, folding his legs onto a dry whaleback without panic, surrendering his carefully gathered calcium back to the storks, the crabs, and the blind pale termites in their kiln-baked spires.

There was no brokenness here to mend. There was only a harshness just slightly sharper than it needed to be, and a fragile perfection vulnerable to blind accident.

So the power acted—not like a builder, and not like a savior, but like water moving down a gradient of three inches to the mile.

Deep beneath the planed gneiss, three miles down in the dark, sealed fractures of the craton where ancient brines had sat trapped since the mountains still had snow on them, the power nudged a hairline fault. It did not break the surface. No tremor disturbed a single roosting ibis or shook a crumb from a termite pinnacle. Only a microscopic valve of stone relaxed, allowing warm, mineral-heavy groundwater—rich with dissolved calcium, magnesium, phosphorus, and trace salts—to begin weeping upward at a rate measured in drops per hour, emerging exclusively in twelve widely spaced, slightly deeper saucers across the three-hundred-mile basin.

It was not enough to feed the water. The algal mats scarcely noticed; no choke-bloom clouded the mirror. Instead, where those slow subterranean seeps met the fierce afternoon evaporation on the margins of specific whalebacks, the white mineral rings dried a millimeter thicker than before—not pure salt alone now, but faint, chalky crusts of travertine and bone-meal stone.

Three weeks later, an old cow leading two calves along an ancestral groove smelled something wet and sharp on the afternoon vapor. She turned four paces off the polished track, lowered her heavy head to the dry margin of a dark ironstone band, and dragged her black, rasping tongue across the crust.

She stood very still for a minute, her slit pupils narrowed in the upward glare, her nostrils flaring high on her snout as the taste registered in the deep, ancient hunger of her bones. Behind her, the calves lowered their square, unchipped horn-plates and licked where she had licked. That season, her milk ran slightly thicker; her calf’s leg-bones knitted denser against the peril of the slick biofilm; and in the jaws of the unborn, the great folded blocks of molar enamel laid down their crystalline matrix just a fraction harder, buying an extra three or four quiet summers at the far end of a thirty-year life before the quartz dust won.

Next, the power looked to the horizons—far beyond the basin, where blind forces were already gathering that knew nothing of stromatolites or upside-down countershading.

Two hundred miles south, at the distant lip where the continental shield finally tipped toward the sea, a knickpoint—a modest waterfall chewing backward through the bedrock inch by century—was slowly migrating upriver. Left strictly to physics, in another forty thousand years that eroding lip would reach the rim of the flatlands, slice into the saucer-basins, drain the warm sheets in a geological blink, and turn the drowned mirror into dry, blowing scrubland.

Beneath that distant waterfall, the power subtly tightened the grain of a belt of pale quartzite, interlocking the silica crystals until the rock became an anvil that water could scarcely scratch. The creeping drain stalled. The shallow mirror was granted another ten million years of flatness.

And beyond the horizon of rock lay the horizon of footfalls. Far away, on higher, greener ground, clever, restless two-legged things with fire and digging sticks were multiplying. One day their scouts would reach the edge of the glare. If they found gold in the quartz veins, or diamonds in an old pipe, or even easy passage, they would dam or drain or paved-over the tracks, and slaughter the slow, unhurried grazers who did not know how to run because running broke pelvises on wet stone.

The power took nothing from the humans, nor harmed a hair on their heads. It merely made sure that whatever shining, heavy metals lay in this basement remained locked ten thousand feet below the planed surface, leaving the surface sand nothing at all but pure, unprofitable, grease-smooth quartz. And at the outer margins of the shield, ten miles wide all around the rim, it encouraged the kaolin clays to hold water in a particularly sour,ankle-wrenching, insect-thick belt of sedge—passable to a stork’s wing or a runner’s webbed paw, utterly miserable to a wagon wheel or a booted march, offering nothing on the far side to reward the crossing except blinding light, tepid bathwater, and deafening frogs. A scout would squint through the shimmering heat-haze from the rim, shield his eyes from the double sun, hear that endless, sawing wall of sound, spit into the sour clay, and turn back toward country with trees and shade.

Only one thing more did the power do, and that was purely for love of the creature itself—a gift with no evolutionary utility at all, meant simply to be felt.

Here and there across the deepest permanent sleeping-saucers—the twelve-inch basins where the herds gathered in the starry dark to fold their pillar legs and chew their purple cud—there were pockets of smoothed quartz pebbles worn down from the hearts of the vanished mountains. Into a sparse scattering of those submerged stones, the power wove a trace lattice of harmless, long-decaying luminescence, tuned not to light, which the pan already had in terrible abundance, but to *warmth* and *sound*.

It did nothing by day. Beneath the fierce blue sky and the copper fire of sunset, the pebbles were just pale, round stones among millions.

But past midnight, when the stars lay unbroken above and below, and the bath-warm saucers held the herds like dark islands in suspended space, the deep, low-frequency *sigh* of a resting bull—that slow, wet exhalation carrying three miles through water and bedrock—would strike the resonant frequency of the stones beneath his slate-black belly.

Deep under the twelve inches of dark water, invisible from the air so no night-hunter’s eye was spoiled and no prey betrayed, a faint, milky-amber warmth would bloom briefly through the quartz grains—barely brighter than starlight, softer than breath, rising for three seconds like a quiet answering pulse from the basement of the earth before fading back into the black mirror.

The grazers did not worship it; they were not built for worship, any more than they were built for galloping. They did not puzzle over it. Only, when an old cow folded her calloused pads beneath her in the midnight bath, let the day’s glare relax out of the heavy bony shelves beneath her eyes, and blew a long, slow sigh across the water toward a distant herd three miles away in the frog-loud dark, she felt the stone beneath her chest grow faintly, inexplicably gentle, and saw the water under her chin answer her breath with a dim, golden hush before the distant bull sighed back.

Satisfied, the power withdrew its hand—though not its gaze—leaving the sun to heat the shallow pans, the ironstone reptiles to wait like stranded logs, the stilts to walk in the narrow shade of moving bellies, and the ancient, unhurried world to go on polishing its quarter-inch grooves into the quiet stone.
