First, nothing that displaces the air.

A lesser benevolence—eager, theatrical, fond of gratitude—would have ruined forty days of work in half a heartbeat. It would have sent a sweet, cool draft down the shaft to refresh the boy’s brow, and in twenty minutes the yellow slip-gauges would have craze-cracked from sudden shrinkage and set off false alarms across six terraces. It would have poured golden light through the bundled reeds, blinding dark-adapted eyes and scorching the pale roots of the wax-vine. It would have rounded the flinty basalt corners so no bare ankle could ever bleed, and in doing so dropped all fifty baked-clay rattles straight into the water table at once. It might even have cured the walls to brick between one breath and the next, tearing the lower courses away from the damp middle tiers along a catastrophic horizontal shear.

Wisdom knows the weight of its own thumb. When it looks into the shaft, it enters more softly than the afternoon change from amber-gray to slate. Not a midge drifts off station; the silver meniscus trembling along the ninety-degree lip of stone does not spill.

For a long while, the power only watches—first the woman’s spine peeling cleanly from her molded niche, then her thumb-ball working alkaline shell-ash into fat mud, then the wooden bevel nudging the hungry root three inches sideways without bruising a cell, then the boy slipping down through the corner pockets to take the dark from her hands.

There is a particular joy known only to great makers when they stumble upon a very small maker practicing the exact same trade. Watching her press her cheek an inch from the paper-thin travertine to read the earth’s breath by skin-heat and iron-smell, the power recognizes a colleague. Nothing in her requires fixing. Her horn-soled feet, her silence, her bargain with the snake and the ants, the eleven neat crescent pricks in the yellow clay—to alter a single gesture of hers would be like painting over a master potter’s signature while the glaze is still wet.

If love is to act here at all, it must work only where her palms cannot reach, and in a grammar that leaves her gauges honest.

So attention sinks downward—past the fuzzy brown fiddleheads of the young tree-fern, past the razor jaws holding the suspended clay pea, through the warm mirror of the water table, and down into the blind black basalt where the lowland plain tore itself apart less than a season ago.

Two miles beneath the town, the new fault is not finished settling.

Up at the surface, the split looks clean and still, breathing cool chalk-vapor. Down here in the crushed dark, where rock presses against rock with the weight of half a province, a jagged tooth of unyielding stone—an asperity the size of a hill—has snagged across the sliding plane. Strain is banking behind it like floodwater behind jammed timber. A mile off to the west, trapped under an impermeable cap of shale, a pressurized pocket of sour, scalding brine laced with hydrogen sulfide is creeping along micro-fractures toward the town’s root-crack, looking for a chimney.

Left solely to blind mechanics, that deep tooth will hold through the dry months and snap four nights after the autumn rains arrive. The slip will be less than the width of a man’s hand, nothing that would trouble a reed hut in an open field. Underneath four stories of half-cured alluvial mud, however, that sharp vertical kick—preceded by only twenty minutes of hot, rotten-egg vapor—will shear the damp basal walls before twenty sleeping households can reach the roof ladders. All her patient anointing with leaf-water and mussel-ash would end in dark rubble, not because her craft failed, but because the foundation broke faith with her.

Here, deep below the reach of any wooden stylus, the power lays its hand flat against the stone.

It does not weld the fault shut; a locked planet only saves up worse violence for great-grandchildren. Instead, down at the crushing contact of the snagged tooth, crystal lattices begin to soften by the merest suggestion of warmth and interstitial water—just enough for brittle lock to become slow, silent creep. Atom by atom, grain by dark pyroxene grain, the banked tension bleeds sideways along the rift, migrating five miles out beyond the settlement’s outermost cucumber patches and brick-pits, out beneath empty salt-grass plain where only plovers nest. What would have been one murderous winter snap dissolves into thirty years of imperceptible sliding, smooth as grease on tallow, never moving the basalt jaws beneath the town by even a tenth of the width required to drop a clay pea.

Next, a hairline vein in the deep shale two thousand feet down closes like a healed cut, nudging the creeping pocket of scalding, sulfurous brine gently southward until it finds an older, harmless vent beneath an uninhabited marsh three leagues downstream. The water rising toward the tree-fern stays cool, sweet with chalk and wet iron.

Then the presence rises back up to the floor of the shaft—eighty-eight degrees, slate-dim, hushed—where the boy is now kneeling on her spare clay cushions, holding the back of his wrist near the east wall to feel what she felt an hour before.

Only one small grace is added inside the room itself, and even that is borrowed entirely from what is already there.

Along the floor-joints and deep in the crescent palm-prints where she patted her mixture of sifted wood-ash and roasted mussel-shell into the wet mud, fine gray basalt rock-flour from the split floor has drifted and settled. Lime from roasted shell, amorphous silica from hearth-ash, ultra-fine volcanic dust, iron-rich clay, and constant eighty-eight-degree dampness: the chemistry is already hovering on the threshold of a marvel the people above have not yet named. Left to chance, the mix is slightly too lean in reactive glass to do more than stiffen the mud’s skin.

Beneath the boy’s moving shadow, in the microscopic pores of the lower courses where she rubbed her powder in patient circles, stray mineral ions tilt into alignment. Calcium and silica begin to link hands across the water films, growing sub-visible, interlocking needles of stone-gel right inside the earthen mass. Nothing changes color. Nothing heats up or dries out ahead of schedule; the curing rate remains slow, predictable, obedient to her daily marks. Only, over the coming six moons, instead of merely drying into hard mud that a century of rising vapor might eventually soften again, these foundations will quietly knit themselves into dark hydraulic stone—tougher than fired brick, thriving on moisture rather than rotting in it, holding up the crowded roofs overhead long after the grandchildren of the builders have forgotten that the walls were ever soft.

The boy reaches up, dusts two damp handprints with pale powder from his gourd, and moves on toward the south concavity. The whip spiders step delicately around his calloused heels. High on the west wall, the bronze vine snake misses the smaller of the two green tree frogs—not by miracle, just because the frog jumps well tonight—and settles into a dry clay groove to wait out the dark.

Up above, the power follows her one last moment out onto the evening terrace.

Out here the world is dry, loud, dusty with chaff and hearth-smoke, bruised purple and orange across a huge western sky. She sits alone near the low mud parapet at the edge of a potter’s roof, legs folded tight out of the way of passing sandals, eating a warm cake of pounded tuber and marrow-fat while her wide dark eyes squint against the horizontal glare of sunset. Forty feet of damp silence still hums faintly in the bones of her inner ear. Her lower back holds the mild, familiar ache of four hours spent crouched six inches above her heels.

Nothing shines around her. No voice speaks inside her skull to tell her she is chosen, or righteous, or watched; she has enough heavy things to listen to without adding heaven to the list.

Only, as the river wind comes across the roofs, a stray thermal eddies gently around the parapet wall just where her shoulders rest, warming the dry mud bricks behind her spine to the exact, steady, comforting measure of a well-cured hearth, taking the tight knot out of her lumbar muscles before she even notices it loosening. Across the courtyard, a roof-weaver’s child carrying a basket of fresh, broad aroid leaves culled from a shaded ditch drops the thickest, cleanest blade near her bare gray feet, pauses, and—knowing what the Sump-Keepers wear down in the wet dark—nudges it shyly over to her knee rather than throwing it onto the compost heap.

She blinks once, slow and dark-eyed, touches two fingers flat to the child’s wrist in silent thanks, and lays the cool green blade across her lap to boil in bark-tannin tomorrow.

Below her heels, forty feet down through silent tiers of hand-slapped earth, fifty hollow clay peas hang undisturbed in fifty pairs of sharp stone jaws. Deep under those, the young rift sighs out under the empty plain without a sound. And all night long, in the warm, breathless dark, the walls cure true.
