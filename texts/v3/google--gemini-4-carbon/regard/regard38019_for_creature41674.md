A lesser kindness would have brought rain. 

A lesser power, looking down through the cedar collar into that cramped, sweating, amber-lit throat, would have pitied the lack of green meadows, the crookedness of the knees, the salt-sting of brick dust in the creases of forty throats. It would have cracked a hidden spring in the deep ash below, or widened the mouth of the pit to let the sweet plain-wind flush out the smell of wormwood and guano, or tumbled a vein of bright copper up out of the dirt so they might have sharp knives instead of ox-bone and broken glaze. 

And within three years, the spring would have drowned the lower tiers, rotted the hemp stays, and turned the potsherd floor into stagnant, fevered mud. The sweet wind would have dried the sweating tiles until the green-rimmed basins cracked from thirst. A single copper knife would have nicked the lip of a thousand-year-old jar, then severed a stay-rope in an argument, then broken the delicate truce of forty lives balanced on peeled saplings.

The power that paused above the cedar timbers on a starless, close night was older than pity and wiser than generosity. 

It did not step down into the pit, though it could have worn a body light enough not to shift a single terracotta pebble. Instead, it rested its attention along the mortar-lines the way the night coolness rested on the limestone counterweights. It listened to the purr of the doves vibrating through twelve courses of ancient kiln-brick into the ribs of a sleeping child. It felt the exact, terrible, triumphant math of the place: forty mouths, three hundred basins, four thousand jars, zero waste. It saw how the old man’s stiffened middle fingers matched the chipped brick frogs he climbed by, and understood that to "heal" his hands straight again would be to make him a stranger to his own walls, and drop him forty paces to his death before noon.

You do not pour wine into a tuned drum. You check the pegs.

So the power went down past the sleeping tiers, past the civets curled warm in the unlit flues, past the three-foot surf of chiming sherds, past the mortared brick laid on end, and down into the blind, forgotten dark underneath—into the courses of packed lime, broken pottery, and tamped ash that no living hand could ever reach or mend.

There, fourteen feet below the lowest heel-mark, a great bulge of ancient cistern-wall was getting tired. 

Centuries of seepage from the plains a mile away—invisible, patient, bitter with alkali—had been creeping sideways through the deep silt toward the pit’s flank. In forty years, or sixty, it would kiss the old lime courses. It would not burst through; it would simply soften them to tallow. One winter night, the eastern buttress would slump two inches. Only two inches—enough to snap forty taut rawhide stays like bowstrings, shear the corbeled shelves, spill six hundred nesting jars into a roaring avalanche of clay and feathers, and crush the beehive chambers where the porridge-crocks hissed. No keen ear, no matter how wide-pupiled its owner, can hear wet clay softening under fourteen feet of ash.

In the utter dark beneath the floor, the power laid its palm flat against the creeping alkali damp. 

It did not banish the water; water must go somewhere. Instead, with a slow, silent pressure like the root of an invisible mountain, it vitrified a curved curtain of the old tamped ash and broken potsherds, fusing the buried rubbish cold—without heat or smoke—into a dense, seamless shell of black stoneware three spans thick, curving around the belly of the pit like a cupped hand. The wandering plain-seep met the barrier, divided smoothly around it, and went on its way into the deeper dark, leaving the foundations dry for another thousand years.

Then the power rose slowly back up through the hollow, reading the tension of every fiber and grain.

In the third tier, tucked into a niche behind a wicker grain-bin, slept a woman seven months heavy with a breach-turned child. In a body built with a wide, flat ribcage and a pelvis narrowed by generations of sideways-slipping, such a birth in sixty days would take either mother or babe, or leave forty reduced to thirty-nine with no milk for the winter. As the woman shifted in her hemp net, murmuring in her sleep at the tick of a cooling tile, a warmth no different from the slaking-lime crocks passed gently across her belly. Inside the dark water of her womb, the tiny, compact body tucked its chin, kicked once against a strong rib, and turned smoothly head-down, settling comfortably into the crook of hip-bone meant to carry it. The mother sighed, scratched her greased shoulder against the walnut-smooth brick, and slept deeper.

Higher up, near the sixth tier, a main load-bearing beam of cedar—pegged into place before the oldest grandfather’s grandfather had lost his milk teeth—had begun to dry-rot strictly at its core, hidden beneath an unbroken skin of gray, skink-polished wood. Nothing showed on the outside; it hadn't even given the tiny *tick* that warns the elders. The power paused beside it. It did not turn the old wood new, for new wood swells and warps and betrays the ropes lashed to it. Instead, it coaxed the dry, resinous heartwood from the sound ends of the timber to bleed inward, crystal by microscopic crystal, petrifying the rotten core with amber pitch until the hollow heart was harder than the oak pegs pinning it, yet weighed not an ounce more upon the brick pier.

That was almost all. 

Just before rising out through the rectangle of stars, the power lingered for one breath above the sorting floor at the very bottom, where the sherd-drift gleamed faintly in the starlight like dark water. 

Up on the plains, three days’ walk to the north, the salt-traders were growing poorer and fewer; a grass-war was brewing among horse-lords who cared nothing for guano panniers, and within a generation there might be seasons when nobody came to the cedar collar with bundles of raw hemp or walnut-sized chunks of quicklime. The pit was an island in an ocean of grass, and oceans change their weather.

Down in the second tier, in a dead-end flue choked since antiquity with kiln-rubble that nobody had bothered to clear because it held up a drying rack, lay an old cache left by the cistern-builders eight hundred years ago: six massive blocks of unquarried, pure, dense limestone used once as deadweights for a vanished crane, long since forgotten behind a single skin of mud-brick. Good stone for burning into quicklime—if one knew how to burn it without smoke, or trade shards of it, or even just chip out clean new condensation basins as the old green-rimmed bowls wore thin over centuries.

The power reached out a finger—not even a breeze, less than the weight of a wall-gecko’s tail—and nudged one single, dry mortar-crumb loose from the mud-brick skin hiding that dead-end flue. 

The crumb fell. *Plink.* It bounced off a projecting jar-lip, spun down through the sapling catwalks, and landed with a tiny, musical *chink* in the three-foot drift of rounded terracotta pebbles at the bottom. 

Up in the dark tiers, nobody woke. A ring-tailed civet paused with one paw lifted inside an empty pipe, twitched an ear, and went back to grooming its whiskers. Five hundred crickets sawed on without missing a stroke. Condensing water gathered on the smooth counterweights overhead—*plink, plink, pat*—falling into the shallow bowls at the exact rate forty warm, sleeping bodies breathed it into the air.

Tomorrow, just after midday, when the amber shaft of sun angled steep against the second tier, an old woman with wide, dark pupils and stiffened middle fingers would climb past that drying rack with her ox-bone trowel. Through the swirling sunlit dust, her extraordinary eyes would catch a hairline fracture she had never seen before in the mud-brick—and behind it, a pale, clean gleam of virgin limestone waiting patiently for the hands that knew what it was worth.

Above the plain, the wind blew across the grass, broke harmlessly against the wicker baffles of the cedar collar, set the misfired potsherds dancing—*chink-chink-clack*—and went on its way under the stars, leaving the instrument in tune.
