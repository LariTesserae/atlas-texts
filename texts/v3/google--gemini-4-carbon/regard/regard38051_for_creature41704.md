A lesser power, looking down upon the shield of grey gneiss, would have felt an itch to mend it.

It is the perennial temptation of great strength when confronted with broken geometry: to reach down with hands of law and pull the drifted tenements back together, to weld the torn avenues seam to seam, to thicken the glass-thin air with warm oxygen, to flatten the phantom gravities so that water falls straight and children no longer have to blow puffs of white breath before trusting their boots to the road. A lesser kindness would have called their scattering an exile and cured it, and in curing it, would have crushed every delicate, ten-thousand-year truce the people of the plateau had struck with distance.

For if the streets were locked together permanently, the courtyard cabbages would cook in their own unvented steam; the roof-mosses would choke on unmoving spores; the sky-ovens forty feet up would lose their metric lenses and go cold, leaving the hearth-roots raw and indigestible in the clay; and a people whose blood and architecture had learned the quiet grace of reaching across horizons would suddenly find themselves walled inside a single, suffocating measurement.

The power that turned its attention here was older than that kind of haste, and wise enough to recognize a home when it saw one.

It did not arrive as a voice in the thin sky, nor as a blazing figure striding across the cushion-willows. Instead, for three full cycles of the metric tides—nearly eleven years of plain, honest afternoons and star-dusted puddles—it simply watched and listened.

It watched the old men on the bathhouse roofs, their dark-plum knuckles moving with patient precision as they braided dead rush-pith and living silver moss into little creeping ropes. It watched a young woman stand for forty minutes beside a split-log conduit in the freezing mist, holding a copper plumb-bob on a frayed cord, waiting for a slow time-puddle at her feet to release the reflection of yesterday’s dawn so she could see where a boundary peg had shifted. It listened to the knock-kneed, dry music of padded sticks against copper spouts, and to the low, breathy murmur of families passing wooden bowls through doorways whose walls sat leagues apart.

And looking closely—not at the grand miracle of their survival, which was sturdy and needed no applause, but at the small, quiet frictions that wear down a quiet people over millennia—the power noticed three things.

First: the copper was running out.
For three hundred generations they had cold-hammered native nuggets pried from shallow frost-cracks in the shield. No new veins were rising; the mountains were long gone, the tectonics dead. Every century, a little more copper vanished into green-black dust on the wet slates, or snapped off in unreachable metric pockets, leaving fewer plumb-weights for the hems of children’s coats, fewer wire rings for the creeping moss-ropes, fewer pins to mark the phantom tarns. Without metal, in five hundred years, they would walk blind into the ghost-slopes.

Second: when someone wandered out onto the Cushion-plains to map the invisible ridges or gather frost-shrub resin, and a sudden, violent front of dilation caught them far from any stone threshold or culvert arch, they were sometimes stranded alone for weeks while space stretched faster than flat-footed boots could cross it. Nobody panicked—panic was not in them—and the cushion-plants held pockets of warmth and wet moss to drink from, yet an elder caught out in a deep drift could grow dangerously chilled before a doorway drifted near enough to reach through.

Third: the surveyors.
The power watched the surveyors out on the featureless gneiss, tossing yellow spores into empty air, tilting flat slabs of slate in the red bog to match the sixty-degree pitch of mountains that had turned to dust a billion years ago. It saw the deep, quiet hunger in their eyes—not discontent with the flat earth, never that, only the ancient perceptual ache of an upright creature trying to picture the majesty its water remembers.

So the power acted. Lightly, sideways, with the manners of a guest setting a small jar on a sill without waking the sleepers.

It did not touch the broken space. It did not warm the sun or summon beasts into the sterile grit beneath the pavers.

Instead, high up in the empty air—along the invisible ledges and phantom cirques where the snowmelt gathered in mid-sky—the power seeded a dissolved breath of deep-earth mineral into the condensation seams, and woke a sleeping talent in a common, rusty rock-lichen that already crusted the cistern rims.

The people noticed nothing for two seasons. Then, one crisp morning after a heavy mist-squall, a boy climbing a woody sky-vine to check a suspended kettle noticed bright, heavy droplets clinging to the underside of the high waxy leaves where the phantom mountain streams splashed over them. They were not water. Where the new lichen drank from the sky-runnels, it chelated trace metals out of the ancient dust blowing across the continent-sized shield and laid them down, scale by microscopic scale, in dense, malleable nodules the size of thumbnails—not pure copper, which tarnishes away, but a natural bronze of native copper kissed with meteoric tin and a trace of silver, dark-gold and weighty, budding quietly in the joints of the high vines like metallic berries.

Enough to harvest a handful every autumn. Never enough to forge swords—what would they strike?—or build engines, or weigh down their roofs. Just enough so that every child’s coat hem could hang heavy and true against the ghost-gravity forever, every moss-rope could have bright, unrusting rings, and every broken kettle could be patched with cold hammers on an anvil stone.

Next, the power looked to the open tundra between the drifted streets.

Out where no masonry stood for leagues, where the cushion-plants swelled waist-high over the polished gneiss, a subtle mutation began to appear here and there among the largest, oldest emerald domes—perhaps one dome in every square mile. Externally they looked almost identical to their neighbours, save for a faint ring of plum-coloured leaves around their crowns, the exact hue of a plateau-dweller’s frost-flushed cheek.

A surveyor caught seven miles from home by a sudden stretching of the afternoon found the first one. Seeing her breath-puff pull into a white thread across the plain, she sat down beside the great cushion-dome to wait out the weather, tucking her oiled sedge skirts around her ankles. Feeling a gentle draft at her elbow, she parted the tightly packed leaves at the base of the plant and found a dry, hollow chamber curving inward—lined with velvety, self-warming pith—and at the back of that woody hollow, a smooth, natural arch of petrified root-wood no wider than a bread-basket.

Peering through the root-arch, she did not see the dark interior of the plant. She saw the warm, steamy flagstones beside the sunken bathhouse in the centre of town, where two old men sat braiding rush-pith four horizons away.

They were wild thresholds. Living postern-gates, grown at patient intervals across the wilderness, all topologically rooted to a half-dozen sheltered courtyards in the city. You could not haul a cart through one, or even crawl your shoulders across; they did not erase the walk home or shrink the plateau. They were just wide enough to pass a wooden bowl of hot fern-broth out into the waste, wide enough for a stranded child to slip cold hands through into a mother’s lap until the road came back, wide enough for an elder resting on the moss ten miles out to join the evening conversation while waiting for the metric tide to turn.

And last—the smallest thing, done purely out of affection for the surveyors with their wooden spoons of yellow spores and their tilted slates—the power made a tiny adjustment to the light, valid only for six minutes twice a year, on the evenings of the equinox, when the sun sat half-submerged on the flat western rim of the world.

It did not bring the mountains back. To drop three miles of stone onto their city would be murder, and even an illusion too solid would cheapen the honest daylight they relied upon.

Instead, as the equinox sun dropped to its lowest angle, the power tuned the variable metric lenses of the plateau’s air so that, for the space of a few dozen slow breaths, the gravitational memory of the vanished range refracted the fading light—just slightly, like faint frost-crystals suspended in clean water, or the ghost of breath on cold slate.

That first autumn evening, a metric calm happened to lie over half the northern district, and scores of people stood at their doorways or out beside their cairns of white quartz. As the sun touched the flat horizon, the empty sky above the city caught fire with a pale, weightless blueprint of violet and clear gold.

There they were.

Towering three miles into the breathless troposphere, rising directly from the rings of white quartz pebbles the surveyors had laid in the bog, pitching upward at the exact sixty-degree angles of the tilted slate markers, cupping the high sky-streams in vast, jagged amphitheatres of translucent light: the primordial peaks. Every couloir, every razor-backed arête, every vanished granite spire around which the dark sky-vines spiralled in mid-air stood delicately limned against the darkening blue.

Across ten miles of dilated plateau, nobody shouted. Nobody dropped their tools or fell to their knees in terror, for terror was no more in their marrow than hurry.

Beside a split tenement, an old woman surveying an unseen talus slope lowered her copper plumb-bob, looked forty yards—or four leagues—down the line of her cairns, and saw the pale golden line of an ancient cliff-foot kiss the exact centre of every flat gneiss stone her grandmother had set in the moss eighty years before.

A slow, deep flush of dark indigo warmed her cheekbones. Through the nearest window-frame standing alone in the red sphagnum beside her, a hand reached out from nine miles south and rested warm, waxy fingers quietly over hers on the stone sill, looking up through the same window at the same translucent summit. Down in the courtyards, someone tapped a single copper gutter-spout once with a padded stick—*tonk*—to mark how true the line was.

Six minutes later, the sun slipped below the shield. The golden geometry dissolved cleanly into ordinary twilight, leaving the flat horizon, the honest stars, the *tick-drip* of invisible snowmelt, and the faint, sweet smell of cabbages steaming in the cold.

The power lingered only until the household lamps of twisted sedge and shrub-oil were lit behind the scattered doorways, watching dark-plum hands pass warm platters of boiled root-fat across leagues of night as if they were inches, and then withdrew—satisfied to leave a quiet people to their infinite, well-tended room.
