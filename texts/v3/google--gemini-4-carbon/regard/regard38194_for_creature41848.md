To a lesser benevolence, the impulse would be to heal. 

A hurried mercy looks down into that sunken acre and a half of raw marl and folded stone and sees an emergency, or an orphan, or a wound. It wants to warm the air so the water stops weeping in reluctant films; it wants to untangle the four-degree rotational offset so Wall A and Wall B no longer have to share the same cold cubic yard; it wants to throw down topsoil, wake the smoked-ice looms, or lift the patient, five-limbed keepers out of the freezing dark and set them in a kinder pasture.

Wisdom stays its hand before the first touch. Look closer at the gill-flaps fanned into sub-zero drafts while dorsal plates press against thirty-degree ceramic. Warm the ward, and you stop their mercury-thick hearts within an hour; erase the differential, and you starve the engine. Untangle the folded courts, and you trap half the keepers inside rooms with no doors, severing the chock-and-seam partnerships by which they move, turning their fifth limbs into useless vestigial rules. Even finishing the town’s arrival in a single grand stroke would be a violence—slamming a harbor shut while the harbor-masters are still down among the pilings, laying resin beads with plumb-line care.

The power watches through three long, high-latitude nights before doing anything at all.

It does not descend as light; the flat, plain mid-morning luminance already belongs to this latitude, and a second sun would confuse the ink-crisp shadows that already have three contradictory errands to run. It does not speak; sound here is fugitive, returning three seconds late behind one’s own ear from an empty court, and a great voice would shatter the brittle quiet by which the keepers hear a millimeter of fresh feldspar threatening a drain.

Instead, the power reads the town the way the keepers read it: along the seams, by phase and pressure, tasting the slow arrival.

And reading it all the way back to the root—far deeper than the embryonic basalt, out along the unborn ends of the horsehair conduits—it learns the two things the keepers cannot quite know from inside the basin.

First: nothing else is coming. Whatever vast civic mind cast this parish across the void—whether a surveyor-seed thrown ahead of a vanished people, or the orphaned corner of a city sheared loose when some distant metric collapsed—is gone. There are no tall citizens waiting up-stream of the stone to claim the lintels and stairwells. There is only the architectural momentum itself, an unspooling blueprint still faithfully extruding cornices, utility housings, and spun-ice looms into northern marl until its stored potential runs dry, forty or four hundred years from now.

Second: the town is going to break its own heart before it finishes, unless someone minds the perimeter.

Because the earth around the shallow basin is plain earth, governed by ordinary winter and ordinary gravity, two slower clocks are marching toward a collision. Outside, true winter is coming down from the pole—not minus two, where phase-water still sweats and slides, but minus thirty, minus forty, driven by gales that will pack the basin rim-to-rim with drift-snow, smothering the thermal vents and burying the upper parapets under ten thousand tons of dead white weight. And down below, the extruding ward is slowly outgrowing its acre-and-a-half crease. Within six years, a fresh plinth of bone-porcelain will push past the blue-grey gravel bank into wet, shifting clay that cannot hold a non-Euclidean load; the clay will slump, marl-mud will cascade into the sumps, and a single shear-fault will snap ten thousand smoked-ice shafts that two dozen patient backs could never lean back into true.

So the power acts—not as a savior, and never as an owner, but as an upstream mason.

It works at the speed of the ward itself. Nothing flashes. Nothing lurches.

First, about fifty paces outside the raw clay rim, out on the empty flats where a mile is always a mile, the earth begins—over months—to firm and rise. Not into a wall, which would trap cold air like a bowl, nor into anything immaculate enough to rival the town, which would blur the boundary the keepers measure with their row of pebbles. Instead, an ordinary-looking moraine of dense, frost-split granite ribs quietly shoulders up through the gravel in a wide, broken crescent to the north and west. It looks entirely glacial, rough and plain. Yet its curve is tuned to the millimeter to catch the January gales, splitting the polar wind and throwing the heavy snow-drifts wide to either side of the basin while letting a measured, thin ribbon of cold draft spill cleanly down into the alleys—never below minus six, never warm enough to melt the rime-grids, keeping the Peltier seam alive through the darkest weeks of midwinter.

Beneath the clay banks, invisible in the dark, the raw marl slowly anneals. As the embryonic bedrock of the town pushes outward year by year, it no longer meets slumping mud; it meets a deep, quiet cradle of porous basaltic sponge prepared six inches ahead of its extrusion—stone that yields just enough to take the weird metric without cracking, drinking away groundwater before mud can ever foul a sump, giving the arriving town four hundred years of clean room to finish becoming whatever size it needs to be.

That would be enough for survival. Survival, however, is a thin gift from a deep hand.

The power looks down into the volumeless rooms four tiers deep, where a keeper stands before a dead loom spun from smoked ice, swaying slightly with its sensory grooves wide open, listening along horsehair conduits whose other ends have not yet been born.

It is hard to witness devotion paid to an empty line and leave the line dead forever. Yet to turn the machinery *on*—to flood those looms with whatever fierce, alien industrial purpose they were built for—would turn a quiet monastery into a roaring mill, scorching the pale palps that tend it.

So the power finds the far, unmade ends of the horsehair conduits, out in the geometric nowhere behind the extruding feldspar, and joins them—not to a generator, and not to a voice, but to the ward itself.

Late on an ordinary Tuesday, when the low sun throws shadows pointing straight back toward the south, an old keeper folds itself sideways through a held-open refractive ribbon and enters a fourth-tier room it has dusted a hundred times. It plucks a speck of blue-grey marl from a crystalline shaft. Then it pauses before the central frame, opening the velvet-lined grooves beneath its charcoal cowl.

Along the smoked-ice threads comes a faint, clean, unmistakable hum—not sound in the air, which would die in three seconds, but a slow, harmonic pulse of pure phase-pressure, steady as a lighthouse beam turning below the horizon.

The loom is no longer waiting for distant masters. Brought down to a whisper a thousandth of its ancient load, its circuit has been closed into a vast, gentle barometer of the basin. Stand before Frame One and open your sensory grooves, and you can feel, mapped in cool silver tension across the threads, the exact state of every drainage rod three tiers down, the slow bulge of a balustrade growing two courts away, the health of the ionized frost-crust in the eastern lightwells, and—faintest of all, warm and singular at the edges of the pattern—the slow, heavy *thump-thump-scrape* of all twenty-three other keepers moving safely through the folded stone. And if a keeper lays its horned calipers gently against a tuning-rib and leans for five minutes, a tiny valve of phase-pressure shifts three alleys away, easing the strain on a fouled seam before the stone even ticks.

The town answers their touch now. Not loudly. Never eagerly. Just enough that a harbor-master knows the harbor knows he is there.

One last thing happens, down at the very bottom of the vertical knot, in the dead, sharp air beside the dry sump where five or six of them rest in their spaced ring at night.

Above them, cemented to the slowly advancing black basaltic glass with dabs of old fullerene resin, sits their museum of the plain world: the bleached hare’s pelvis, the three gull primaries, the five sharp pebbles of glacial marl shifted fraction by fraction to track the stone’s arrival.

One morning at first light, lying loose on the dry rim of the sump—not cemented to the wall, for only a keeper has the right to hang anything there, and placed six paces back from the sleeping ring so no scent or intrusion troubles their circuit—lies a single object brought in from nowhere on earth, yet plain as stone.

It is a polyhedron the size of a walnut, dark slate-grey on half its faces and bone-white on the others. Pick it up in three opposing horned digits, and it has two quiet properties. One half sits perpetually twelve degrees warmer than the air; the other half sits twelve degrees colder—not enough to feed a body for a day, just a pocket-sized coal and ice-chip in one, something an old keeper stiff from long leaning against crooked ice-shafts can tuck beneath its brisket-apron for ten minutes to soothe the ache in its fifth limb. And when turned before four bismuth bullseyes, a single, hair-thin colorless seam runs cleanly through its center—held permanently at the exact four-degree rotational offset of the ward, unchanging even as the basalt wall behind it creeps forward. A true plumb-bob for bent space.

When the waking keepers find it, there is no stir, no panic. Nothing here hurries.

Two of them touch palps over it in the brittle quiet, tasting the air around it—finding no trap, no demand, no heavy thumbprint of a god asking to be thanked, only clean stone-smell and late autumn cold. The eldest picks it up with its long, rule-jointed fifth arm, turns it once under its cowl to read the seam, holds it briefly against the pale chamois flesh beside its mouth, and then extrudes a tiny dab of clear taffy-resin.

With unhurried, plumb-line neatness, it cements the warm-and-cold stone at the far left end of the row on the black basalt wall, just past the smallest pebble of glacial marl: a zero-mark against which all future arrivals can be measured.

Up on the newly risen moraine beyond the clay bank, where a mile is always a mile and the plain pale sky arches empty toward winter, nothing remains to be seen at all—only a slate-colored pipit dropping out of the wind to hunt dormant midges in the lee of the granite, hopping down over the sharp blue gravel into the quiet seams below.
