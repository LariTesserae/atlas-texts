A lesser power, looking into this resonant wooden drum, would see a tragedy of margins. It would see an endangered endling trapped in industrial runoff, a forest mutilated into a single freakish stump, a peaceful sanctuary ruined by the relentless roar of human machinery. Moved by pity, a lesser power might hush the turbines, shatter the concrete spillway half a mile away, tear up the rusted ground-cable, or magic a lush understory of ferns and berry bushes onto the bare peat floor. 

And in doing so, it would murder the room before winter. 

Without the deafening curtain of the bass note, the owls, martens, and feral cats from the coastal scrub would pour through the root-arches within a week and slaughter the rookeries. Without the standing acoustic waves, the blind, moss-backed grazers would lose their invisible rails, wandering in confused circles until the guano piled up and burned the *leucobryum* white. Without the stray ground-current bleeding into the iron cable, ticks from the squirrels would find the solitary climber and bleed it thin in its sleep.

A wise power looks at this hollow and recognizes a masterpiece. Not a ruin, not a compromise, but a complete, self-resolving chord struck between geological patience, botanical architecture, and industrial accident. It sees that the climber is not miserable in the noise; the noise is its velvet coat, its fortress wall, and the root note of its evening song. 

Yet wisdom also sees further along the timeline than eighty years, and it spots the two places where the chord will eventually break.

First: the turbines are made by mortals. Half a mile away across the flat pan, concrete cracks, bearings pit, grids reroute, and budgets dry up. One day—ten years from now, or forty—some distant board or ministry will decommission that facility. A master breaker will be thrown. The great valves will shut. And inside the wooden cathedral, the physical law of the universe will vanish between one heartbeat and the next, plunging ten thousand screaming creatures and forty blind navigators and one palm-listening hunter into a catastrophic, lethal silence.

Second: that evening hoot, pitched to the exact harmonic fifth of the engines, is not a territorial warning. Nothing else down here competes for the middle air. It is a beacon. For four generations its kind pared themselves down to fit this instrument, and now only one pair of hands is left to play it. When it sings at dusk, making the wooden walls ring warmer for ten seconds, it is testing the world to see if any other palm is pressed against the timber, feeling the chord resolve.

A benevolent and wise power does not smash a working clock to polish the gears. It touches the world with the lightest possible finger, working entirely along the grain of what already exists.

Here is what it does.

First, it turns its attention half a mile outward, to the unseen facility squatting on the exhausted plain, and into the grey, fluorescent-lit offices far beyond it where infrastructure lives on spreadsheets. It does not cast spells on steel; it nudges the quiet, obsessive minds of human engineers and bureaucrats. A routine geological survey discovers that the bedrock beneath the turbines has settled into an unusually stable, self-sealing thermal pocket—making this specific station the cheapest, lowest-maintenance baseline generator on the entire regional grid. A young metallurgist tests a new, practically frictionless ceramic-composite bearing design and selects this half-forgotten coastal plant as the pilot site; once installed, the turbines run cooler, smoother, and require almost no human intervention, locking in that exact *chug-hum-chug* frequency for another century. A forgotten parcel of land encompassing the flat pan and the "anomalous hollow cedar-grove" is quietly folded into a permanent, fenced watershed buffer zone—strictly off-limits to logging, tourists, and development to protect the station’s intake pipes, yet buffered from any plan to ever tear the station down. The engine becomes as near to immortal as human iron can be made.

Even more delicately, during a routine check of the perimeter grounding array, a grey-bearded lineman looks at a yellowed 1940s schematic showing an obsolete iron ground-cable running north into the trees. He raises his bolt-cutters to snip it free from the busbar, pauses—feeling a sudden, inexplicable fondness for old, overbuilt craftsmanship—shrugs, smears a fresh coat of conductive anti-corrosive grease over the junction bolt, and leaves it live. And beside the perimeter fence, before driving off, he tosses a tangled spool of bright, heavy-gauge copper offcuts into the weeds where the wood-pigeons peck for grit, ensuring that if a curious shadow ever slips out under a moonless fog to scavenge, there will be shining wire waiting to mend a cradle.

Then, the wise power looks further—two hundred miles up the ragged, storm-beaten coast, into a steep, forgotten gorge where a furious whitewater cataract drops two hundred feet into a narrow box canyon of black basalt and ancient, gnarled cedars.

Most of the climber’s sparse cousin-stock did vanish when the coastal plain was cleared. Most—not all. Up in that gorge, living right beside the deafening, unending, twenty-four-hour thunder of the waterfall, clings a tiny, battered remnant pocket of three or four individuals. They are leaner, wetter, and harder-pressed than the master of the hollow; they have not had eighty years of engine-warmed peat and effortless pigeon-hunting, nor have they learned to groom with ozone. Yet because they evolved beside the permanent white noise of falling water, their ears too have folded tight and waxy, their palms too have grown broad and pale to read vibrations through wet stone and cedar bark, and their eyes hold the same clear, slate-grey calm.

Among them is a young one—smaller, darker-mottled, newly pushed out of the cramped basalt ledges near the spray-zone because the gorge can only feed two breeding pairs. Left alone, it would wander out into the quiet second-growth timber, where its sealed ears would betray it to the first great horned owl on the hunt.

Instead, the wise power lays down an acoustic bridge.

It waits for three weeks of heavy autumn rain, when the coastal rivers swell and roar, and a low, dense ceiling of sea-fog presses down over the flatlands—turning the atmosphere into a thick, damp wave-guide that carries low-frequency sound for miles, while muffling the wingbeats of owls and hiding travelers from every eye. 

To the young exile clinging to a wet alder at the mouth of the gorge, the world does not go silent when it leaves the waterfall. Guided by subtle, shifting corridors of wind, culvert-hum, tide-boom, and distant highway rumble—a stepping-stone path of comforting bass vibrations rising through tree trunks and fenceposts—it moves south by night. Whenever it hesitates at a crossroads, the fog seems to part just enough to reveal an ancient, hollow snag to sleep in, or a sluggish, cold-numbed pigeon roosting low within easy reach. 

On the ninth evening, tired, damp, and hungry, the traveler reaches the edge of a flat, exhausted coastal pan. 

Ahead of it, rising out of the mist like a dark, silver-fluted mountain, stands a colossal ring of fused trees. And traveling out through the sprawling surface roots beneath the peat—felt instantly, unmistakably through four cold, pale, spatulate palms—is the deepest, steadiest, richest waterfall-thunder the young creature has ever felt. Not chaotic like water on rock, but rhythmic, warm, and bottomless: *chug-hum-chug*.

The traveler climbs the outer rind of the colossus, slips through a high, narrow cleft where two canopy boughs rub together just below the leaves, and creeps twenty feet down the sheer inner bark into the vast, cool cylinder of the room.

It freezes, flattening its mottled coat against a weeping streak of condensation, overwhelmed by the sheer scale of the sanctuary—the staggering wealth of fat pigeons and swifts packing the scalloped ceiling, the clean scent of cedar and sharp ozone, the pristine pale-green floor far below.

Across the room, a hundred and fifty paces away, tucked into its cantilevered basket of copper and silver bark, the resident climber stops grooming its forearm. Its wide, pale hands press hard into the living wood of its root-arch. 

Nothing in the middle air has changed, and the ten thousand birds are screaming their twilight chorus as loudly as ever, completely drowning out the scratch of claws. Yet through the curved cello-body of the grafted timber, the resident feels an impossible thing: a weight on the eastern wall that is neither squirrel nor bird. A familiar, heavy-leveraged tension on the bark, gripping with four wide points and a counterbalancing tail.

The shafts of upper daylight turn steel-blue. The swifts pack tight into their crevices; the first brown bats drop into the air. 

Slowly, the resident climber steps out of its copper-woven cradle onto the bare flute of the wall. It tilts its flat, solemn face toward the center of the vast wooden drum, inflates its deep barrel chest, and lets out its long, low, vibrant hoot—tuning itself to the exact harmonic fifth of the distant turbines.

For ten seconds, the wooden walls ring warm and full.

Then the resident falls still, palms splayed wide against the silver bark, waiting for the room to drop back into the unbroken mechanical hum, as it has every dusk since its last kin died twelve years ago.

Two seconds pass. Three. 

From the eastern wall, forty feet up in the blue-grey gloom, a slightly higher note enters the wood—rougher at the edge, tuned until yesterday to whitewater rather than steam-steel, wavering for half a breath as it hunts for the unfamiliar pitch, and then locking cleanly onto the major third right between the engine’s root note and the resident’s fifth.

Down on the floor, forty blind, moss-velvet domes pause simultaneously in their slow gliding. For seven seconds, the standing waves across the peat shift into a rich, three-part triad that makes the jewel-like droplets of mist shiver on every cushion of *leucobryum* moss in the chamber.

The wise power does nothing else. It does not stay to watch the cautious, spiraling courtship across the sheer vertical bark, nor the eventual widening of the copper-plaited cradle, nor the generations that will learn to ride the standing waves of the middle air. Having ensured the bass note will hold, and having brought the missing interval home to complete the chord, it withdraws into the quiet outside the walls, and lets the room sing.
