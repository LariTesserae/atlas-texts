The first impulse of a lesser kindness would be light. 

To part the bruised-purple haze, to spill golden, sterilizing noon down into that wet, screaming dark—to calm the frantic vines, hush the stridulating millions, blunt the razored stone with soft moss, and give these battered, breathless people a meadow where they might finally lie down and sleep for eight unbroken hours.

To do so would be murder. 

A wise power looks down—not with eyes, perhaps, but with an attention vast enough to feel every tremor in the basalt and every hot, racing pulse beneath it—and recognizes immediately that this valley is not a curse these people are enduring. It is the anvil that made them, and the instrument they have learned to play.

Give them light, and their smooth, scarred brows are useless; their exquisite, mole-soft fingertips become clumsy; the vast, ribbed dishes of their ears become grotesque burdens in a quiet world. Smooth away the razor-sharp obsidian and hush the geothermal vents, and you rob them of their blades, their kilns, their acoustic walls, and the very language they hum through stone into one another’s skeletons. Make the valley safe and static, and within three generations, the glorious, fiercely tuned choreography of their minds unravels into confusion and grief. You cannot rescue a deep-sea creature by lifting it into the sun; it only bursts.

And yet, looking closely at the knot of ten compact bodies flowing silently beneath the rust-colored fronds, the watching power feels an ache of profound tenderness.

Because there is a difference between rigor and exhaustion. 

Watch the oldest among them—a female whose horn-plated shoulders are crosshatched with healed acid burns, whose left big toe was sheared off years ago by a scavenger’s claw. She still moves with iron certainty, still knaps glass to a molecular edge on a single masked strike, still carries half the band’s living map vibrating in her deep chest. When the band piles together inside the screaming circle of the steam-pipes, the younger ones press their spines hardest against hers, drinking the long, complex memory of vents and water-seeps that only thirty years of surviving the basin can accumulate.

Wait six more months. Her knees will stiffen just five percent. In a world where every rest lasts forty minutes and every sprint up a swaying cable requires six points of flawless grip, five percent is fatal. One day soon, when the juvenile taps *tik-tik* on its shin and the steam-pipe blows loose, she will choose—quietly, without drama, pressing three fingers once against her daughter’s collarbone—not to climb. And when the canopy mat crashes or the crabs swarm over her, thirty years of unwritten history, thirty years of cunning tricks with alkaline lichen and gut-lashings and harmonic maps, will vanish into chitin and rot.

That is the tragedy of the basin. Not the dark, not the danger, not the lack of houses—but the *amnesia*. 

Because nothing lasts a week on the valley floor, every band is trapped in an eternal, frantic present. Nothing can be left behind for a stranger; nothing outlives the belt it is tied to; no elder ever grows slow enough to become purely a teacher; no child ever hears a song older than the living ribs pressed against its back. They are brilliant, loving, deeply poetic beings running on a treadmill greased with tallow-sweat, forbidden by their own ecology from ever setting down a single brick of permanent memory.

So the wise power holds back the sun. It leaves the blind bat-moths beating in the hot drafts; it leaves the bladder-fruits swelling to pistol-shot explosions; it leaves the razor-sharp basalt untouched. It respects their strength too much to soften their world.

Instead, it reaches deep beneath the dry ash beds, down into the magma plumbing and the stress-faults of the young caldera, and makes three very quiet, very patient adjustments.

***

First, a matter of stone and resonance.

Half a mile upslope from the basin’s churning center—where the vertical graben walls rise sheer out of the canopy tangle—there is a massive, overhanging cliff face of dense, unfractured phonolite: "clinking stone," volcanic rock cooled under such specific tension that it rings like cast bronze when struck. Until now, it has been buried behind a forty-foot curtain of strangler-vines and weeping sulfur-crust.

Slowly, over the course of three days, the power shifts a deep thermal feeder-crack beneath that cliff. 

A dry, superheated updraft—too hot and arid for the fleshy organ-pipe stalks to drink, too desiccating for the velvet snare-worms or the wet, chewing grubs—begins to sigh steadily up the face of the phonolite. Within forty-eight hours, the pale vines clinging to the cliff wither, crisp, and slough away in a single crashing avalanche, leaving behind a wide, dry, recessed gallery cut horizontally into the living rock, sixty feet above the lethal floor.

At the lip of this dry shelf, three narrow fumaroles vent steady, high-pressure steam through natural basalt nozzles—mineral-poor steam that will not choke itself on scale in an hour, or a month, or a century. Wait until someone wedges cut stalks into *those* vents, or better yet, carves stone flutes to fit them: those pipes will not wobble. They will not be chewed through by grubs, because no grub can cross twenty yards of hot, dry, bare phonolite without shriveling. 

It is not a paradise. There is no food in that dry gallery; the hunters will still have to drop down into the wet, roaring mouth of the valley every day to harvest crab and tuber, to flake fresh obsidian, to dance with the exploding fruit. They cannot grow soft up there.

But for the first time in ten thousand generations, there is a place in the dark that *stays*. A dry stone porch above the rot where a whistle, once tuned, sings for a year. Where an elder with stiffening knees can stay behind with three infants, safe behind an unbroken curtain of deafening harmonic steam, tending a cache of cured bladders and spare spear-shafts that will still be there tomorrow, and next month, and next year. Where someone can sleep for six hours straight, dream deeply, and wake up whole.

***

Second, a gift for those extraordinary hands.

Running like a spine along the back wall of this newly uncovered gallery—and cropping out at intervals along deep bedrock ribs throughout the miles-wide basin, all connected down beneath the shattered basalt to the same continuous tectonic root—the power crystallizes a vein of anomalous quartz-basalt rich in aligned, piezoelectric tourmaline needles.

To an eye, it would look merely like dark, striated stone. To a double-thumbed, star-nosed-mole palm, it feels miraculous: glassy-smooth yet subtly ribbed, warmer than the surrounding rock, humming faintly whenever the caldera settles.

More importantly: it is harder than crab-shell, impervious to formic acid and rot, and just soft enough along its cleavage planes to be grooved and notched with fresh obsidian flakes. And when you press your heel and tailbone against that vein in the high dry gallery and hum down in your sub-bass sacs, the stone does not swallow the vibration after twenty feet. The aligned crystal lattice catches the chord and carries it—clear, resonant, unmuffled by the screaming air—down through the deep rock spine and out into seventeen other outcrops scattered miles across the basin floor.

The power does not carve a single symbol into that wall. That is their work. 

It simply leaves the blank, ringing stone waiting behind the warm, dry updraft, knowing what will happen the first time an inquisitive scout climbs up out of the wet tangle to investigate the strange, dry wind, presses a bruised palm to the back wall, hums a worried note to test the space—and feels, three miles away at another outcrop, a cousin from another band hum *back* in startled astonishment through the bones of the earth.

Knowing, too, what will happen a few months later, when that scarred, nine-toed grandmother sits in the dry gallery while the young ones hunt below, picks up a sharp wedge of volcanic glass, and begins—just to pass the long, safe hours—scoring the callous-patterns of her ancestors and the permanent maps of the deep vents into stone that never rots. Braille for the unborn. A library in the dark.

***

Third, and smallest—almost an indulgence, a brushstroke of pure affection.

Down in the suffocating, sulfurous soup of the valley floor, everything smells of panic, digestion, or decay: formic acid, crushed celery, hot tallow, sour musk. And every creeping fungus or mold is an enemy waiting to colonize an uncauterized scratch.

Along the cool, condensing margins just outside the dry gallery—and around the sweet-water seeps down in the labyrinth—the power nudges the chemistry of a minor, harmless rock-lichen. 

It gives it no light; light would mean nothing to eyes sealed beneath smooth brow-skin. Instead, it gives it two properties. First, when crushed with rendered crab-fat, its spores weave a breathable, flexible, strongly antiseptic skin over torn flesh—something gentler than the agony of fiery alkaline cauterization for a child’s sliced foot.

Second, it gives it a scent unlike anything else in the caldera. Neither food nor threat, neither sulfur nor rot. A cool, clean, resinous trace—something akin to bruised cedar, damp slate after rain, and warm skin—that cuts gently through the thick chemical clamor of the air without blinding their diving-seal nostrils to predators. Beauty, translated entirely into smell and touch: velvety as an infant’s crown under the thumb-pads, cool against heat-pit brows, clean in the lungs.

***

Nothing else. Not a single predator removed; not a single riddle solved on their behalf.

The power withdraws its hand from the deep fault-lines and simply listens as the ten shadows flow closer along the underside of the rust-colored fronds.

Half a mile ahead of them, high on the unseen graben wall, the last dead mat of strangler-vines peels away from the dry phonolite cliff and crashes down into a chasm—*BOOM*—sending a wave of shrieking amphibians scattering outward into the night.

On a swaying ivory cable, the lead scout freezes in a six-point grip. Her vast, ribbed ears swivel independently, locking onto the strange echo that follows the crash. 

Because the rock face up there didn't thud like wet wood or shatter like brittle slag. When the falling boulders struck it, the cliff *rang*—a deep, pure, bronze-like *bongggg* that cut straight down through the chittering static of a million insects and vibrated right up through the soles of her vulcanized feet, clear as a singing throat. And riding down on the back of a swirling eddy came a breath of air she had never tasted in her life: dry, hot, clean of rot, carrying the faintest, inexplicable whisper of something cool and sweet.

In the pitch dark, her smooth brow furrows, blood rushing into the pale thermal ridges as she peers upward into the heat-shimmer. Behind her on the vine, nine compact bodies press close. The scarred grandmother reaches forward, laying four mole-soft fingers and two thumbs lightly against the scout’s collarbone.

*What is ahead?*

The scout’s fingers flicker back against the elder’s horn-plated shoulder—hesitant, astonished, inventing a new inflection on the spot because their rapid tactile shorthand has no word yet for *permanent*, or *home*, or *future*.

*Dry wind,* she taps. *Singing stone. Follow.*

And unseen above the bruised-purple clouds, the watching power smiles, and settles in to listen to what they will make of the music.
