First, they would take off their weight at the rim.

A lesser power arrives like a falling stone—*clack, boom*—Parting the steam, rocking the surface film, sending the blind white webs snapping back into the razor-sharp dark of the scree. A lesser power wants to be registered. 

Wisdom knows better. Wisdom looks down through the fleece of vapor smelling of wet slate and crushed greenery, reads the thinness of the horn-clear shells, notes the lithium steeped into every cubic inch of the bath, and understands at once: *this is a dream being dreamed in spun glass. Do not slam the door.*

So the presence enters the basin the only way anything enters it from above without violence—like noon light. Without breaking the surface tension, without nudging a single charcoal-black spider off its skating-path, attention slips beneath the skin of the water and diffuses into the golden-green glow.

For a long time—days, or seasons; time has very little purchase here—they do nothing at all. Benevolence, when it is coupled with wisdom, begins as pure, unhurried perception. 

They drift through the vaulted galleries of rust-red macrophyte, admiring how a dinner-plate snail turns on its single thread of iron-hardened mucilage like a sleepy planet, harvesting its own back. They watch an amber mite embrace a fat pearl of leaf-breath—*tic*—refreshing its velvet cloak thirty feet beneath a sky it could never survive. They sink down into the sulfur-tinged olive twilight of the drowned cliffs, careful not to stir a millimeter of current against the twenty-inch sensory hairs of the white crustaceans waiting in polite, overlapping silence.

And then, deep in the central labyrinth where the oldest colonnades anchor to a submerged spine of granite, the power finds the one who holds the valley together.

She is the eldest of the Ribbon-Gardeners. 

In a world without jaws, without winter, and steeped in a mineral sedative that slows the burn of cells to an ember’s glow, vertebrate age loses its usual borders. She was hatched when the porphyry dam still smoked above the waterline. Now she is nearly eleven feet of coppery-green translucence, no thicker than a human forearm, her cartilage supple as kelp, her tarnished-brass eyes calm and bottomless. Her external gill-plumes have grown so vast and extravagantly branched over the centuries that when she coils at rest, she *is* a grove—a hanging thicket of chartreuse and rust-red lace through which juvenile mites absentmindedly walk.

She is not sapient in the anxious, terrestrial way. She has no words, no numbers, no concept of *outside*. What she has is a complete, living map of the basin held in the taste-buds of her eleven-foot skin. 

She knows every cubic yard of the valley by its flavor of carbonate, iron, oxygen, and rot. She remembers where the water soured three summers ago under the west crag; she remembers the slow tilt of a thousand severed towers; she feels the whole drowned mountain as a single, warm lung that she helps to breathe. When a pocket of bladder-fern packs too dense a quarter-mile away, she tastes the faint, bitter thread of stagnation before the snails even begin to suffocate, and over the course of an afternoon she unwinds herself through the green walls—*snip, snip*—to let the gold back in.

Watching her hang suspended in a newly opened shaft of light, drinking the rising pearls of oxygen with her fern-finned throat, the wise power feels a fierce, quiet ache of affection.

Evolution almost never gets to ask: *What would life become if it were never afraid?* 
Everywhere else on the earth, beauty is forged under the hammer of terror—shells thickened against crushing jaws, muscles coiled for panic, minds sharpened by famine and cold. Only here, in this fluke of split stone and lithium warmth, was life allowed to drop its shield and turn entirely into lace, architecture, and etiquette.

And because the power is wise, they do not merely look at what *is*. They look down into the rock, and out across the peaks, to see how long this peace has left.

What they find beneath the basin floor would break a softer heart.

The valley is a geological accident, and accidents are unstable. Looking deep into the dark porphyry heave that chokes the lower gorge, the power sees that the natural dam is holding by a thread of friction. Forty feet inside the rubble plug, hidden from the sunlight, the slow percolation of mineral water has been quietly dissolving a diagonal seam of softer breccia. The fault line below is still restless. In seven years—or twelve, at the outside, during an autumn freeze-thaw followed by a minor tremor—that lubricated seam will slip. 

The porphyry plug will shear open in four minutes. 

All three square miles of eighty-five-degree water will roar down the dry gorge in a single catastrophic afternoon. The vertical forests, built without wood or fiber to fight gravity, will collapse into suffocating, slimy mats inches thick on the knife-sharp scree. The glass snails will shatter under the sudden weight of their own bodies in open air. The white webs in the talus will dry like tissue on razor blades. And the ancient Gardener, eleven feet of delicate cartilage and gill-lace, will be stranded in freezing mountain wind, crushed by gravity she was never meant to carry, under an ultraviolet sky she has never seen.

Worse: looking two miles deeper into the earth, where the shallow magma warms the million hairline fractures, a pocket of sour, superheated volcanic brine and hydrogen sulfide is building behind a thin cap of quartz. Even if the dam held, one sharp seismic hiccup would crack that cap and belch a scalding, acidic plume straight into the stagnant basin, boiling the sanctuary in its own cradle.

And looking *up*, five miles down the dry granite ridge, there is a faint scratch in the alpine scree: surveyors’ cairns, creeping slowly higher decade by decade, following the mineral-stained seepage in the lower canyon toward its lithium-rich source.

The power stills itself beside the Elder Gardener, pondering.

What should benevolence do?

A meddling god would armor them—thicken their shells, harden cartilage back into bone, teach their nerves the sharp electric jolt of fear so they could survive the rough world outside. *No.* To teach this valley panic in order to save it would be to murder the very miracle worth saving.

A possessive god would freeze the basin in timeless stasis, sealing it away from change forever. *No.* A thing that cannot change or end is no longer alive; it is only an ornament on a shelf. Every world on Earth has a lifespan. A pond becomes a meadow; a mountain becomes sand. Mortality is not an error. 

The tragedy is not that this drowned garden will one day end. The tragedy is a song interrupted in its very first measure by a collapsing roof.

So the power decides. Not to make the valley immortal—only to grant it the full, unhurried span of its natural afternoon. To let the dream finish its thought.

They perform four quiet mercies, and not one of them pops a single bubble on a leaf.

***

**First, they descend into the dam.**

Slipping past the white crustaceans without brushing an antenna, sinking into the dark, rubble-choked throat of the gorge where the warm water trickles outward, they find the perilous, dissolving seam inside the porphyry plug. 

They do not fuse the dam solid—if the slow percolation stopped, the basin’s level would rise over the granite lip, spill out as a cold waterfall, change its chemistry, and invite climbing fish from the rivers below. The basin must continue to breathe invisibly through the stone.

Instead, working at the speed of crystal growth, coaxing dissolved silica, iron, and calcium out of the seeping thermal water itself, they knit a honeycomb of interlocking mineral struts right across the slip-plane. They turn loose, shifting rubble into a porous, load-bearing sponge of agate-veined stone. When the tremor comes seven autumns from now, the mountain will shiver, a single dry rock will *clack* down into the thickets, and the dam will hold as if it were carved from the bedrock of the planet. Now it will not fail in a sudden Tuesday landslide; it will wear down only at the honest pace of high granite—inch by century, over forty thousand years.

**Second, they go down to the fire.**

Two miles beneath the sharp talus, where the rock glows dull cherry-dark and the pocket of sour, boiling sulfide strains against its thin quartz cap, the power lays a cool, heavy hand along the fault. They open a microscopic vein sloping *away* from the valley—miles sideways and downward into dead, deep plutonic stone—letting the poison bleed harmlessly into the roots of the range. Above it, they widen a thousand tiny basalt capillaries, smoothing the thermal draw so the heat rising into the basin floor will remain steady as a banked hearth—eighty-four to eighty-six degrees, winter and summer—for a geological age.

**Third, they step out into the freezing wind.**

High on the dry, howling granite ridges above the steam-fleece, the air is sharp and blinding. Down in the lower canyon, where the mineral seepage stains the dry creekbed pale yellow and white—a signpost that would eventually draw drills, roads, and men looking for lithium salts—the power nudges the subsurface plumbing. Now the draining water dives deeper beneath the mountain's flank, filtering through three miles of hungry clay and fractured schist that strip out the telltale minerals before the water ever daylights as an ordinary, uninteresting snowmelt spring far down in the timberline. The cairns will stop five miles short; there is nothing up here on this brutal, barren peak worth climbing seven thousand feet of loose, ankle-breaking talus to see.

And while they are on the ridge, they make one tiny sculpture in the wind-scoured granite at the western saddle: a shallow, curved notch in a crag, no bigger than a doorway. 

It catches the afternoon updrafts from the distant alpine meadows three thousand feet below and curls them softly—just slightly—so that before the wind passes high above the basin, it drops its faint, invisible freight into the steam. A pinch more pine pollen. A few more wind-killed midges, blown grass-seeds, and frail snow-moths. Not enough to cloud the mirror-clear water; only enough to keep the charcoal-black Rim-Walkers well fed on their two-inch ledge of moss, and to send down, through their tiny droppings and discarded husks, the barest whisper of extra nitrogen so the starved roots sixty feet below will never choke in famine.

**Fourth, and last, they return to the Gardener.**

She is still there in the vertical clearing she made two hours ago. She has not moved an inch. The silvery frost of oxygen beads has regrown thick along every serrated olive leaf around her, and a teacup-sized juvenile snail has glided out onto a swaying frond nearby, its pale heart squeezing rhythmically inside its glass whorl.

The power knows she cannot be spoken to. She has no ears for speech, and no loneliness that requires a peer; she is wholly sufficient unto her warm labyrinth. Yet to pass so close to a fellow custodian—one who has tended her small world faithfully for centuries with nothing more than a fused beak and eleven feet of tasting skin—without offering a greeting feels unkind.

So the power speaks in the only language the drowned valley knows.

Up in the sunlit canopy directly above her head, two great, drifting spheres of chartreuse root-hair have slowly nudged together, threatening to dim her shaft of gold an hour earlier than usual. With a touch lighter than a snail’s breath, the power parts them by six inches, holding the corridor open so the fierce alpine sun pours straight down upon her coppery spine in an unbroken pillar of warm amber.

And right in front of her recessed, tarnished-brass eyes, rising up from a hairline fracture in the porphyry sixty feet below, comes a single, lazy spiral of water slightly warmer than the rest—eighty-eight degrees—infused with a trace bloom of sweet magnesium, iron, and dissolved silicate, richer and cleaner than anything she has tasted since the century she hatched.

It curls around her snout, washes slowly through the extravagant rust-red and chartreuse lace of her gill-plumes, and trails down all eleven feet of her suspended body like a long, warm stroke of a hand.

For the first time in three hours, the Elder Gardener moves. 

She does not startle. She simply spreads her fern-shaped pectoral fans a fraction wider, parts her smooth beak-plates to drink the sweet current, and arches her long, vine-slender neck into the center of the sunbeam, every ruffled filament of her gills flaring open in quiet, slow-motion bliss. Above her, dislodged by the gentle wave of her plumes, three fat pearls of oxygen detach from a leaf—*blup, tic, blup*—wobbling upward toward the quiet steam.

The power lingers just long enough to watch the rings break on the mirror-flat surface.

Then, leaving no ripple behind, they withdraw from the water, step past the two-inch rim of lime-encrusted moss, and go—leaving the drowned mountain to its golden silence, its glass architects, and its unhurried thousands of years.
