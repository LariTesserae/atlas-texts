A lesser benevolence would have looked down through the pewter haze, pitied them, and ruined everything.

It is the first temptation of great power when encountering harshness: to soften it. To part the oppressive ceiling of cloud and let a warm yellow sun pour onto the slick shale. To scatter soil and moss-spores so the razor-edges might grow green cushions. To still the endless condensation, silencing the deafening clatter of ten million dripping chambers at a stroke.

The Wise Power paused above the pewter cloud-deck, listening to the basin roar, and understood immediately that mercy of that kind would be murder.

Sun would dry the waxy grey tallow at the builders’ joints, cracking their seals, parching the bacterial glaze they depend upon, blinding eyes made for shadowless twilight. Soil would clog the tuned, hollow shafts beneath their feet, muting the world into a mud-thickened grave. And silence—absolute, unearned silence, dropped from above—would render a thousand years of masonry meaningless. Their minds *are* their walls. Their fellowship is the shared discipline of turning chaos into a single, breathable chord. Strip away the roar, and you strip away the reason two narrow silhouettes meet in the crooked dark to lever a forty-pound slab sixteen-thousandths of an inch to the left.

The Power watched for seven years before touching anything at all. Seven years is not very long to a rock-splitter, nor to a god.

In that time, the Power mapped every resonant chamber across the three-mile floor, listened to the low, purring conversations inside the dry, stacked domes, and felt the deep bronze tolling of the leaning monoliths whenever a spire gave way. It saw the splendor of what these patient, cold-blooded architects had achieved with nothing at all—no timber, no fire, no metal, only geometry, gravity, and their own crane-like bones.

And because it watched for seven years, it also saw the two things the builders themselves did not yet know.

The first was deep beneath the basin floor. Half a mile down, below the fractured shale and the fresh basalt columns, a blind pocket of ancient brine and trapped gas was slowly eating upward along the central fault—the very fault directly beneath the deep pool and the tall ring of leaning monoliths. Within forty or fifty generations, a geological blink, the bedrock under the central pool would not merely crack; it would collapse inward. The great leaning blades—that irreplaceable master-instrument raised by a hundred ancestors sliding stone on living slime—would topple into a sinkhole, shatter into dead rubble, and leave the entire dispersed nation deaf to the breaking of their world, unable ever again to coordinate at that scale before the labyrinth swallowed them piecemeal.

The second thing was smaller, and closer, and sadder in the quiet way of mortal lives.

In the kiln-domes, tucked into the dry corbelled ceilings, one out of every five or six pale, soft young never quite hardened right. The dissolved lime and iron licked from the local shale was slightly impoverished in magnesium and trace silicates. When those unlucky young finally crept out through the U-bend water traps into the valley, their forearm membranes and collarbone crests were microscopically brittle, their hollow bones slightly warped. To them, even the smoothed, tooth-vibrating chord inside a masterfully built baffle still rang with a painful, jagged buzz. They could never quite tune a stone; they winced at every drip; they lived short, bewildered lives at the edges of the courts, fed by their kin out of quiet solidarity, unable to join the great listening.

And there was a third thing, though this was not a flaw or a danger—only a horizon. The builders had mastered *stone* and *water* and *silence*. They had turned noise into architecture and survival. They were ready, just barely, for something that was neither shelter nor shield, should they choose to pick it up.

So the Power acted. Not from the sky, which did not belong to them, and not with light or heat or voice, which would have terrified creatures made of cold horn and drum-skin. 

It acted down in the dark, in the slow language of geology, where miracles look like luck and respect the dignity of hands that work.

First, half a mile beneath the central pool, the Power laid two invisible fingers against the rising pocket of brine and gas, and gently turned it sideways. 

Over the course of three months, at a pace too slow to set off a single tremor, the subterranean pressure bled harmlessly away to the west, entirely outside the rim of the basin. As the brine retreated, the Power drew up a slow, warm-cold mineral seep from deeper down and let it cool and crystallize directly beneath the foundations of the leaning monoliths—knitting the fractured sub-floor together with a dense, interlocking rootwork of quartz and hornblende. The central ring would stand now until the continent itself grew old and wore flat. No builder ever felt it happen. Only, one evening when thirty of them stood around the pool, the regular six-second tolling sounded a fraction clearer, its sustain lingering half a heartbeat longer in the mist before fading, because the anvil beneath the pool was finally whole.

Second, in three shallow springs along the high northern rim—where water wept out of the dark shale to feed the ankle-deep channels below—the Power altered a seam of hidden rock two yards inside the cliff face. 

Nothing flashed; nothing glowed. The water spilling out remained black and cold. Yet as it crept down through the labyrinth, the pale, translucent bacterial film coating the lower foot of the stones drank in a trace of new dissolved salts—magnesium, strontium, a whisper of amorphous silica. Over the next two seasons, the flat, black rubbery grazers feeding on that slime grew slightly denser, slightly slicker. Inside the pitch-black domes, elders regurgitating mineral paste onto the ceiling crevices noticed nothing except a faintly sharper tang on their inner mouthparts. 

Within three years, every pale hatchling hanging in the dark grew plates that cured smooth, elastic, and true. No young stepped out through the water-trap to be flayed by white noise; every crest stretched tight and resonant across unwarped bone.

Had the Power stopped there, it would have been enough. Safety from ruin; wholeness of body. 

Yet wisdom knows that a mind capable of tuning three square miles of fractured slate to a single chord holds a hunger inside it that safety alone does not touch.

Far out toward the eastern curve of the basin, in a quiet, neglected cul-de-sac where an old spire had split and leaned years ago, there was a flooded slot nobody visited much—no good flat shale for walls, sparse slime for grazers. 

Deep inside a natural split between two basalt columns, the Power cooled a single, slender vein of stone that did not quite belong to the local geology, though it looked entirely native to the eye: dark, wet, grey-black, slick with mist. It was a rare, dense, vitreous phonolite—true *clinkstone*—threaded through at the atomic level with aligned metallic silicates, split cleanly by the Power’s touch into a sheaf of loose, narrow staves and flat, palm-sized wafers, each about eighteen inches to four feet long, none heavier than twenty pounds. Their edges were naturally beveled, keen enough to wedge, dull enough not to crumble.

Unlike the crumbly shale that deadened sound or only reflected a single dull thud, and unlike the massive basalt pillars too heavy for one individual to move, each piece of this black phonolite had an astonishingly pure, astronomical Q-factor. Strike one, or let a single drop of mist hit its upper tip, and it did not *tink* or *clack* and die. 

It *sang*. 

It held a pure, bell-clear, silver overtone for nearly twenty seconds, shimmering with clean, mathematical harmonics—octaves, fifths, major thirds—that stepped effortlessly above and below the low, single-note hum of the valley's baffles without breaking their peace. Better still: press two pieces together at different points along their grain, and the pitch bent, gliding like water in a pipe.

The Power laid the sheaf of singing stones where a routine seasonal fracture would expose them, waited until the micro-crack filled with condensation, and let go.

*CRACK.* *Creeeeak.*

At the center of the valley, the leaning monoliths drank the small shockwave and rolled out their deep, informing *DOOOOONG*. 

Three hours later, a single builder—old, scarred along one brow-flange from a rockfall decades past, one of the finest masons of the eastern courts—slipped sideways through a four-inch gap, hooked a caliper foot onto a submerged ledge, and folded its long arms across its chest to listen to the fresh tumble of rubble.

Water dripped from an overhanging shelf onto the newly exposed cleft.

*Tink. Clack. Bloop.* And then, cutting effortlessly through the static, clean as cold starlight if these creatures had ever known stars:

*Mmmmmmm—iiiiiinnngggggg.*

The builder froze. Its four dull pewter eyes did not blink, though its forearm membranes pulled suddenly taut. 

Four minutes passed. Another heavy drop gathered on the shelf above, trembled, and fell onto a slanted, three-foot stave of the dark phonolite lying half-wedged across a hollow channel.

*Mmmmmmm—iiiiiinnngggggg.* Twenty seconds of pure, silver vibration, threading straight through the chaotic clatter without fighting it, dancing in high, clear fifths above the distant tooth-hum of the nearest baffle, warming the hollow cavities of the builder's ribs in a way no sound ever had—neither threat, nor silence, nor warning.

Slowly—slower even than when levering a wall-slab—the builder unfolded its crane-like limbs, stepped sideways to the cleft, and reached down with one vulcanized pincer. It lifted the dark stave an inch. Moved it sideways so two separate drip-lines from the rock lip above would strike it at different intervals, six inches apart along its length.

It stepped back. Pressed its wrists to its ribs. Waited.

*Drop.* A clear, deep fifth.
*Drop.* While the first note still hung shivering in the damp air, a second note bloomed a fourth above it, the two vibrations braiding together in the wet dark, beating gently against the builder’s collarbone crests like a second, calmer heartbeat.

The old mason stood completely still in the ankle-deep black water for three hours while the mist bled down its carapace.

When it finally moved, it did not wedge the stone into a baffle to make silence. Instead, tucking two of the slender singing staves carefully beneath one narrow arm against its waxy flank, it slipped sideways back into the labyrinth, heading toward the nearest circular court and the quiet dome where four of its kin lay slotted together in the dark—carrying home the valley’s first song.

High above the permanent pewter cloud-deck, where nothing was slick and nothing was loud, the Power listened for a little while longer as a faint, braided two-note chime began to drift up from an eastern court, answered, days later, by a third note thirty yards away. 

Then, leaving the clouds closed and the twilight intact, it withdrew its attention, content to come back in a thousand years and hear what they had composed.
