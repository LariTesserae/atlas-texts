A lesser benevolence would bring light. It would part the freezing cloud-deck to warm the black water, or ease the burden of the weavers so they would no longer have to braid their own dead into the timber to keep their world from starving. A lesser power would see the dark, the four-degree cold, and the two-hundred-year labor, and mistake discipline for suffering.

A wise power arrives without displacement—neither warming the water by a thousandth of a degree nor casting a single stray echo to startle the clicking shoals—and simply *listens*.

It listens for a long time. It traces the miraculous, closed-loop fidelity of the basin: the sugars pumped down from the sky, the resonant grazing of the turnip-pale amphibians, the invisible ambushers, the warm breath in the turpentine vaults, the silent hands healing fractures for grandchildren they will never touch. It sees a covenant between root and flesh that has held an ocean in the upper air since before the plains below forgot their own names. To break that covenant with ease or abundance would be to unravel the weavers’ very minds, which *are* the acoustics of the wall.

Yet a wise eye reads deeper down the centuries than even the weavers’ skulls can hear, and in the math of the water, it finds the one wound no splice can pull shut.

A closed loop with an open spillway is not truly closed.

Every second, tons of water roar over the outer lip and plunge five thousand meters into the clouds. Even with the weavers guarding their dead, even with the scavengers stripping every sinking scrap before it hits the floor, water is a patient thief. Dissolved in that endless thunderous overflow go microscopic parts per billion of leached potassium, trace iron, magnesium, and—most precious of all—phosphorus and calcium, slipped past the root-hairs in the tannic dark, never to return. 

The mountains that once seeded this sea are entirely digested. There is no bedrock left to scrape. Over ten thousand years, the weavers have grown slightly smaller; the cartilage of the blind fish has thinned by fractions of a millimeter; the cypress needles up in the freezing murk yellow just a shade earlier at their tips, starved for trace minerals the thin mountain rain cannot provide. In another fifteen millennia—a blink to the trees, though seven hundred generations to the weavers—the iron in the weavers’ blood will thin past the threshold needed to warm their hands. Their fingers will stiffen at the walls. A deep harmonic will go unbraided, and the sky-sea will tear open and fall.

The power decides. It will not announce itself. It will not become a god to creatures who have no need of worship, whose reverence is already whole and spent entirely upon one another and the wood. Anything given here must arrive wearing the exact grammar of the basin: slow, dark, earned, and silent.

Far below, down on the forgotten plains and along the mid-mountain crags where the great spillways crash into mist and shatter against true stone, the power turns its quiet attention to a humble, unremarkable thing: a species of tiny, cold-blooded torrent-midge that breeds in the freezing spray at the foot of the falls.

Over the span of six human lifetimes—no faster, lest any sudden shift shock the chemistry above—the power nudges the longing inside those midges’ tiny nerve-cords, and toughens the antifreeze glycoproteins in their pale hemolymph.

Previously, only dead, stray midges blown upward by freak thermals ever reached the cloud-capped surface of the sky-lake, a negligible dusting of frost-killed specks. Now, driven by an altered instinct tuned to the deep infrasonic roar of the falling water, the adult midges begin to use the sheltered, humid boundary-layer hugging the outside of the colossal wooden dam. Generation by generation, feeding on the mineral-rich rock-dust and lichens of the lower cliffs before their final molt, they climb the sheer, peat-iron bark through the clouds. They carry in their tiny exoskeletons and fat-bodies minuscule, concentrated cargos of lowland phosphorus, calcium, and iron—the exact ghosts of the dissolved mountains, gathered from the splash-zones far below and flown back up into the sky.

They reach the jagged, needle-matted rim at five thousand meters, mate in the freezing murk, drop onto the black water in quiet, unseen millions, and sink.

Down in the bruised-indigo dark, nothing breaks. Nothing is disrupted. Simply, almost imperceptibly, the snow of windfall thickens by a fraction of a percent. The clicking shoals grow just heavy enough to keep their ancient numbers steady; the grazers’ combs stay firm; the cypress roots, drinking the droppings of the shoals through their fungal tallow-mats, feel a quiet, deep easing of an ancient cramp in their sap.

Only once does the power come closer—not to intervene again, for the balance is now truly immortal, needing nothing more until the sun itself grows old—but out of sheer, quiet affection for the makers who maintain this wonder.

Deep in one of the narrowest, blackest root-crypts at the bottom of an amphitheater, three weavers are finishing a burial. 

Their elder hangs newly lashed against the pale curtain of feeder-roots, her long gaunt hands folded peacefully into her rib-pockets, the last silver-black plume of her breath already vanished miles overhead. The three survivors linger in the motionless, four-degree dark. Their hearts beat very slowly to save air. Pressing their smooth, eyeless foreheads gently against the bark beside their dead kin, they wait to hear the wall accept her—listening as the endless, distant bass of the spillway rolls down through six hundred feet of timber and wraps around the new stillness of her bones.

The acoustic shadow of a freshly lashed body is always slightly dull, a small mute knot in the grain that takes decades of root-weave to clear and ring true. Every weaver knows that brief, heavy silence; it is the shape of their grief.

The unseen presence rests in the black water beside them. It does not touch their minds with alien words, nor flash a light against skin that cannot see. Instead, with infinite delicacy, it aligns the tiniest subtle resonance down the miles of ancient heartwood—borrowing the distant cello-groan of a terrace grazer, the far-off thunder of the spillway, and the deep, taut turgor of five-thousand-year-old timber—and lets a single, returning wave pass cleanly through the crypt.

For the span of three slow heartbeats, the dull knot vanishes. Through their jaw-pans and pressed skulls, the three weavers feel the wall ring back—not as it is today, and not with anything foreign, but with the warm, solid, unbroken chord it will hold a century from now, when their elder has wholly become the strength of the arch, and the sea above is safe, and their grandchildren’s grandchildren are swimming through clear water.

The three pale shapes stay very still against the tallow-slick bark as the pure harmonic fades back into the ordinary thunder of the lake. Slowly, one reaches out a six-fingered hand in the pitch dark, brushes the wrist-spur of its companion in a brief, wondering touch, and presses its throat to the other's shoulder to hum a soft, two-note chord that never leaks into the water.

Then they unhook their hind-claws, turn upward with a single languid roll of their flukes, and glide silently back toward the hollow ribs to breathe—leaving the basin to its loud, cold, unbroken peace, and never knowing they were blessed.
