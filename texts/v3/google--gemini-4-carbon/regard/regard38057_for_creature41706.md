The short-limbed visitor who stood for an hour in the eastern intersection—the one whose woven collar turned to wet rags in the mist, whose chest rose and fell with ridiculous, bird-like haste while a warden passed by—did not come dressed like that out of ignorance.

From high above the plateau, beyond the bruised ceiling of the inversion where the air is thin and dry and full of stars, the city looks very much like a wound or a prison. Even to a vast and ancient mind, a charcoal shroud unbroken for centuries reads at a distance like grief. A lesser benevolence would not have bothered coming down at all; it would simply have reached through the stratosphere with a sweeping hand, torn the cloud-belly open to let the clean sun pour in, cooled the simmering stone with sweet rain, and scattered green seeds across the barren grid.

Wisdom, however, knows better than to heal a body before taking its pulse. So the power put on small, baseline bones, wrapped itself in ordinary cloth, and stepped down into the four-hundred-yard avenue specifically to fail at living there. 

It stood still beside the bronze grate until its boots filled with hot brine and its borrowed lungs ached from drinking soup. It felt its own pulse hammering at eighty beats a minute, trapping metabolic fire beneath skin too compact to shed it. It smelled the souring linen at its own throat. Only by enduring the plateau’s refusal of ordinary flesh could it understand, down in the marrow, why the wardens had become what they were—not cursed, not stunted, not marooned, but exquisitely reconciled.

By the time the returning warden descended the forty dry steps into the beehive cairn and laid two webbed fingers against the elder’s collarbone to hum the afternoon’s news, the visitor out on the avenue had already begun putting its miracles away.

One by one, like a surgeon folding up instruments after realizing the patient is not sick, it unmade every blessing it had brought down from the sky.

It put away the wind that would have broken the thermal inversion; sunlight here would sear milky nictitating membranes to scar tissue, crack the vascular webbing between long fingers, and starve the condensers of their atmospheric return. It put away the rich loam and the deep-rooted trees that would have pried apart the hexagonal paving, choked the subterranean cisterns, and rotted into the precise organic mulch required to wake a lethal bloom of porcelain-fungi. It put away the impulse to thicken their spare frames, to smooth the gill-like scars along their throats, to gather their scattered threesomes into bright, resounding halls of song and fellowship where they would suffocate in a week. 

Most carefully of all, it resisted the urge to reach three hundred feet beneath the eastern avenue and dissolve the crust of calcium building up on the southern flange of the number-four condenser.

That was a subtler temptation—the urge to perform a small, invisible favor. Yet wisdom understands the mind of an engineer. If a check-valve rattles with mineral scale on Tuesday and seats with miraculous smoothness on Wednesday, a warden does not fall to its knees and thank the sky. A warden assumes the flange has sheared off entirely, or that a chunk of scale has broken loose to jam a downstream turbine. To steal their maintenance would be to break the honesty of cause and effect beneath their feet, forcing twenty people out into the wet heat to haul up a perfectly good cylinder out of sheer distrust. They had six or seven years before that valve needed the crane, and they already knew it. Leaving the calcium alone was basic courtesy.

Still, the visitor did not leave immediately. 

Walking half a mile down the boulevard to a quiet dead-end stanchion where no grates steamed nearby, it stripped off its sodden, souring coat and boots, rolling them into a tight bundle tucked beneath one arm so not a single thread of rotting fiber would drop onto the stones to feed a mold. Barefoot now, bare-shouldered in the pewter gloom, it let its borrowed physiology relax its grip on old habits. Eighty heartbeats a minute slowed to fifty. Fifty eased to forty. Forty settled, with a long, saline exhale, to thirty-two.

It pressed its bare heels flat against the pillowed basalt and listened the way the plateau listened.

Down went its attention—past the shallow braided channels of runoff, past the bronze grates, past the ash-insulated vaults where small triads slept in troughs of warm black sand or scraped fungus into stone mortars under indestructible amber bulbs. Down past the roaring, disciplined arteries of the deep boilers, down past the lowest reach of phosphor-bronze staffs, into the blind basement of the mountain seven miles underfoot, where time is no longer measured in crane-schedules, or even in generations, but in the slow bending of continental crust.

Down there, in the dark where the wardens could neither listen nor reach, the power found three quiet things worth doing.

First, six miles below the western turbine housings, where magma leaned against the granite pan to drive the whole ten-thousand-year clock, a sealed pocket of bitter volcanic gas—rich in fluorine and sour sulfur—was slowly chewing its way upward through a hairline fracture in the caprock. For another four centuries it would give no warning at all; in the four-hundred-and-first, it would breach the primary geothermal veins, turning the city’s steam to hot acid that would eat through every bronze seat and iron rivet on the plateau in a single season. Very slowly, working at the speed of cooling stone so not even a tremor disturbed the water on the streets above, the power drew the molten silicate sideways and annealed the caprock into half a mile of seamless, ductile olivine, burying the poison forever and evening out the thermal draw beneath the boilers for the next forty millennia.

Second, it turned its attention outward, miles away to the unseen rim where the charcoal clouds finally spilled over into cold, oxygen-thin scree. There, buried beneath a creeping talus slope, lay the intake sumps for the closed-loop brine pipes that kept the subterranean living vaults cool enough for three hearts to share one room. Centuries of frost-shattering on the outer cliffs had begun to choke the deep gravel aquifer with fine glacial silt; in eighty years, the vaults at the center of the city would warm by four degrees, turning *three* into an unbearable crowd and forcing every household down to *two*. Without making a sound in the thin mountain air, the power shifted the subterranean stone by the breadth of a hand, opening a clean, deep bypass in the bedrock filter so the cold brine would run clear and unimpeded for as long as the plateau stood.

Third, it looked at the alcoves inside the vaults—without entering them, respecting the greased-leather airlocks as inviolable thresholds—and considered the rolled sheets of lead foil stacked neatly in the dry dark. Pure lead is patient, far more patient than paper or wood, yet under its own quiet weight over thousands of years, soft metal creeps. At the very bottoms of the oldest stacks, the scrolls pricked by the city’s first modified generations were slowly slumping, the tiny raised burrs of the steel styluses flattening back into smooth gray ribbon, erasing the earliest valve-diagrams and the names of the ancestors who had first slowed their hearts to match the iron. 

The power did not rewrite a single faded dot; what time had already taken belonged to time. It merely breathed an invisible stillness into the crystal lattice of the existing lead so that not one remaining mark would ever slump again—and then, out beyond the foundry’s eastern quarry, in the unmined vein of galena waiting for future smelters, it seeded the ore with a two-percent trace of natural antimony. When the wardens dug there next century, the foil rolled from their mills would feel no different beneath their hammers, yet it would hold the crisp bite of a stylus point for fifty thousand years.

Nothing else. Not one flower. Not one ray of sun. Not one word spoken aloud into air that had no use for words.

When it was finished, the visitor walked slowly toward the far edge of the grid, splashing through the half-inch of bath-warm water at the exact rolling, flat-footed cadence of a local—twenty minutes from one amber halo to the next, unhurried, leaving no wake larger than a louver-moth’s shadow.

Near the last line of cast-iron stanchions before the mist swallowed the paving altogether, a solitary warden stood beside a junction pillar, its copper-and-rubber mantle dripping steadily, the butt of its seven-foot phosphor-bronze staff resting on a sunken bolt while its cheekbone leaned against the shaft. It had finished checking the draw on an outer sump pump and was already aware, through the soles of its horn-gray feet, of the light, unfamiliar footsteps approaching across the hexagons.

At thirty yards—the polite, cool interval of the rotor-crews—the visitor stopped.

Through the milky film of its membranes, in the shadowless pewter light, the tall figure looked across the wet basalt at the small one. It noted that the foolish cloth collar was gone, carried neatly in a tight bundle, and that the stranger’s chest was no longer heaving like a trapped animal’s, but rising and falling once every three or four drips from the stanchion cable.

They did not approach each other. Neither raised a hand.

Instead, the visitor knelt on one knee in the warm, iron-tinted runoff, laid one bare palm flat against a pillowed basalt block, and sent a single vibration into the stone.

It was softer than a shifting valve, brief enough that it obscured nothing in the working grid, pitched precisely to the resonant frequency of phosphor-bronze and human bone. It traveled thirty yards through the wet paving, climbed the iron shoe of the tuning-staff, and bloomed directly inside the warden’s jaw and sternum.

There were no words in it, yet to a being who read calcium crusts through three hundred feet of rock, its structure was unmistakable: a chord as clean and true as a newly balanced sixty-ton shaft spinning without friction, layered over the deep, quiet certainty of unbroken bedrock seven miles down, the chill of clear brine flowing from the rim, and a slow, steady pulse of thirty-two beats a minute—not commanding, not teaching, merely matching time. *I hear the engine,* the chord meant in the grammar of bone and bronze. *It runs true. Keep your intervals.*

The vibration lasted five seconds and died cleanly into the stone.

When the warden opened its heavy-lidded eyes, the small figure had already turned toward the unseen rim and walked thirty paces into the charcoal soup. Within seventy more, the mist closed behind it without a seam, leaving no footprints on the water-smoothed hexagons, returning the boulevard to its vast and necessary hush.

For four minutes—five—the warden stood motionless beside the stanchion, bare toes gripping the warm slime, listening downward into the plateau. Everything sounded exactly as it had an hour ago, yet beneath the familiar *thump* and wet sigh of the condensers, the deep earth felt subtly, inexplicably rested, like a heavy arch whose keystone had settled the final thousandth of an inch into forever.

At last the warden lifted its staff, turned back toward the distant amber glow of its cairn, and walked home through the weeping dark, already rehearsing in its throat the low, chest-deep hum it would lay against two waiting collarbones—trying to find a pitch pure enough to describe the sound of the stone singing back.
