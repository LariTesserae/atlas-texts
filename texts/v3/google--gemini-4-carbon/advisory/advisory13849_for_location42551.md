You are preparing to visit a place that breaks nearly every rule an ordinary diver learns on Earth. When people hear “underwater volcanic settlement,” they instinctively pack for either cold, dark ocean trenches or violent hydrothermal vents. If you pack that way, this basin will kill you quietly within an hour—not through monster attacks or boiling geysers, but through subtle physics: trapped body heat, surgical stone, altitude decompression, and silent gas poisoning.

To visit this submerged highland safely—and without ruining the delicate world of the people who built it—you have to rethink how a human body survives underwater from the skin outward.

---

### 1. Solving the Paradox of Fever-Water and Razor-Glass

Your first and most immediate problem is a contradiction between temperature and armor.

The water across the caldera sits steadily around 38°C to 40°C (100°F to 104°F)—the temperature of a hot bath or a human fever. If you wear a standard neoprene wetsuit or a conventional drysuit here, you will die of hyperthermia. Underwater, sweat cannot evaporate to cool you, and when the surrounding water is already as warm as your blood, your body loses its ability to dump metabolic heat through conduction. Even gentle finning generates internal heat that has nowhere to go.

At the same time, you cannot swim bare-skinned. Nothing in this basin has ever been tumbled smooth by surf or ground into sand. Every raw ledge, stray flake on a quarry stair, and unweathered wall chink is freshly fractured basalt or volcanic glass. Obsidian fractures down to an edge a few molecules thick—sharper than surgical steel. In water, skin softens quickly, and obsidian slices wet flesh so cleanly that your nerves won’t even register the cut until a dark ribbon of blood blooms green-black in the amber light. Worse, the local flora anchors itself to the glass using acid-secreting holdfasts; brushing bare skin hard against a root-bed can leave chemical burns alongside lacerations.

**How to dress:**
* **Zero-insulation slash armor:** Leave all neoprene behind. Your outer layer must be free-flooding, uninsulated, cut-resistant mesh—specifically high-molecular-weight polyethylene (like Dyneema or Spectra) woven with ultra-fine stainless steel or titanium chainmail panels over your knees, palms, elbows, shins, and shoulders. Water must flow freely through the weave, yet a razor flake pressed under your full body weight must slide off harmlessly.
* **Active core cooling:** Because even a bare swimsuit won’t keep you from overheating if you swim against a vertical current or stay down for hours, wear a thin phase-change cooling vest (loaded with packs calibrated to melt slowly around 28°C/82°F, not ice-cold packs that cause vasoconstriction) underneath your slash-suit, or carry a micro-circulating chill pack if your multiverse kit allows high-tech gear. 
* **Skin maceration defense:** Spending hours immersed in fever-warm, mineral-laced water will rapidly break down human skin (immersion foot/hand), turning your palms and soles pale, wrinkled, and dangerously fragile. Before gearing up, coat your entire body—especially hands, feet, and skin creases—in a thick, hydrophobic barrier ointment (heavy medical-grade silicone balm or anhydrous lanolin). 
* **Hydration port:** You *will* sweat profusely inside the water without realizing it. Your mask must include an internal bite-valve drink tube routed to a bladder of cool, electrolyte-heavy water strapped to your rig. Sip every fifteen minutes whether you feel thirsty or not.

### 2. Breathing, Bubbles, and the Altitude Trap

The second hidden killer is where this lake sits: *miles above the world’s true oceans.*

If the caldera rim sits three or four miles up, the atmospheric pressure at the surface is roughly half of Earth’s sea-level pressure (around 0.45 to 0.55 atmospheres). Standard dive computers and sea-level intuition do not work here. Ascending sixty feet from the town’s upper terraces into half-atmosphere mountain air produces the same violent pressure drop ratio as rocketing up from well over a hundred feet at sea level. Do it casually, and nitrogen bubbles will tear through your joints and central nervous system before you even haul yourself onto a pumice raft.

Even more importantly, **you cannot bring open-circuit SCUBA gear into this town.** 

Think about the architecture: inverted roofs of buoyant wood and bladder-mats strained against stone tethers, and pitch-sealed basketry troughs holding upside-down pools of carefully hauled air and channeled volcanic gas. If you swim through a public square exhaling great, roaring clouds of exhaust bubbles every four seconds:
1. **You will vandalize their infrastructure.** Your rising exhaust bubbles will slam into the canopies, spill over into their upside-down hearths, contaminate pure sulfur-curing pockets with oxygen, or overload delicately balanced buoyant roofs until their stone tethers shift.
2. **You will deafen the valley.** In siltless, waveless water where the loudest ordinary sound is the *tink* of an obsidian chisel three tiers away or gas beads ticking softly against leaves, an open-circuit regulator sounds like tearing sheet metal.

**Your breathing system:**
* **A Closed-Circuit Rebreather (CCR):** Mandatory. A CCR recycles your breath silently, scrubs out carbon dioxide, and emits **zero bubbles** unless you ascend rapidly and vent your loop. It also automatically maintains an optimal partial pressure of oxygen ($PPO_2$), compensating seamlessly for the thin air above and the pressure below.
* **Armored hoses and solid buoyancy:** Every breathing loop hose and high-pressure line must be sheathed in braided Kevlar or flexible polymer armor—one nick from a basalt cornice on a bare rubber breathing tube floods your loop. Likewise, do not rely on a standard inflatable nylon buoyancy vest. If an obsidian chip punctures your air bladder over the central gorge, you sink into the dark. Configure yourself to near-neutral buoyancy using trimmed blocks of incompressible syntactic foam, using only a tiny, heavily armored dual-bladder wing for fine adjustments.
* **Full-face mask with amber-clear optics:** The water is warm, stagnant-currented, and lined with organic biofilms and dissolved volcanic gases. Do not expose your eyes, nose, or sinuses to it; warm volcanic basins are prime territory for unfamiliar microbes and acidic irritation. Wear a low-profile full-face mask, and bring acetic/boric acid ear drops to flush your ear canals immediately upon leaving the water.

### 3. Never Trust the Surface—or the Ceiling Mirrors

Human instinct says that when things go wrong underwater, you go *up* to breathe. Break yourself of this reflex before you arrive.

Fifty or sixty feet overhead, the surface of this lake is a death trap for an unprepared human. First, it is crusted over with drifting rafts of pale pumice that knock together—surfacing blindly can mean slamming your skull into porous stone or finding no open gap to break through. Second, the air up there is freezing, starvingly thin, and capped by a stagnant wool lid of cloud resting directly on the water. In a young volcanic caldera with no wind to scour the basin, heavy volcanic gases—carbon dioxide ($CO_2$), sulfur dioxide ($SO_2$), and hydrogen sulfide ($H_2S$)—pool right on top of the waterline underneath that fog. Ripping your mask off at the surface can mean inhaling a lungful of cold, odorless $CO_2$ and passing out face-down among the pumice. Keep your rebreather on your face even above the waterline until a handheld multi-gas sensor proves the air right in front of your nose can sustain human life.

Down in the town, exercise the exact same discipline around the **quicksilver ceilings**.

Looking up into the eaves and inverted basketry troughs, those trembling, mirror-bright pools of trapped gas will trigger a powerful psychological urge to poke your head up inside and take a dry breath. **Do not do this.**
* Half of those pockets are upside-down chemical kilns holding warm, sulfurous burps channeled straight from fissures in the rock floor to thicken resins and cure fibrous bark. Sticking your head into concentrated $H_2S$ and taking one breath causes instant knockdown—olfactory nerve paralysis followed by unconsciousness in two seconds.
* Even the pockets holding "breathable air" hauled down from the surface in greased skins were filled miles above sea level! That air starts out deeply hypoxic for an unacclimatized sea-level human, and after sitting trapped under a stone eave while tools cure or locals visit it, its oxygen may be depleted and its $CO_2$ dangerously high.
* Treat the ceiling pools with the exact same caution you would show an open fire or a vat of acid in a dry workshop. Look, admire the bone hooks and obsidian blades suspended upside-down inside them, and keep your hands and helmet below the mirror line unless expressly invited—and even then, test with a sensor probe before breaking your mask seal.

### 4. Moving Through Three-Dimensional Streets

Forget swimming with long, sweeping rubber fins. Long fins have wide kick-arcs that you cannot see behind you. In a vertical town divided by delicately swaying split-reed privacy screens, hanging curtains of bronze fronds, and taut kelp-cord tethers holding multi-ton stone lintels in place, thrashing through an alley with three-foot dive blades makes you a bull in a glass shop. Worse, there isn't even silt to warn you that you’re kicking the floor; instead, your fin tip will either slice itself to ribbons on raw basalt hexagons or dislodge a loose stone that clicks loudly down three residential tiers.

**How to move:**
* **Short, stiff frog-kick fins—or none at all:** Use compact, stubby maneuvering fins (using controlled frog-kicks and backward helicopter turns that keep thrust entirely behind your hips and away from walls and screens) along with weighted ankle trim so you can comfortably hover upright or horizontally.
* **Learn the pace of the caldera:** Notice how fast things move here. A shed leaf takes an entire afternoon to cross a courtyard; reed screens sway one inch to the left, hold for a full minute, and sway back. Match your velocity to the water. Moving fast not only overheats your body in the blood-warm gloom, it broadcasts aggression or panic through pressure waves that aquatic and semi-aquatic ears/lateral lines feel from thirty paces away. Glide slowly, hands tucked in.
* **Read the vertical traffic lanes:** Streets are cleared volumes marked at three distinct depths by guide-ropes knotted with pale pumice floats. Observe before entering a thoroughfare—different depths likely correspond to different traffic types (heavy stone-hauling vs. residential access vs. clear through-lanes). Never grab a structural tether-line (the thick braided kelp-cords anchored to stone cables holding down buoyant roofs) to pull yourself along, and never step on bare black rock when an older plaza coated in velvety maroon algae offers a cushioned, blunted landing pad—assuming stepping is permitted at all. Hovering eighteen inches clear of every surface is always polite.

### 5. Light, Sound, and Material Etiquette

You are entering a civilization of extraordinary engineering sophistication that possesses **not a single piece of forged metal**. Everything around you—hexagonal pillar walls, fire-hardened wood wedges, banded stone mauls, driftwood-hafted obsidian knives, pitch-sealed basketry, bladder-harnesses—comes directly from young rock and young flora.

If you drop down into a public plaza clanking with shiny chrome D-rings, titanium dive knives, and blasting a 3,000-lumen cold-white LED beam, you will cause immediate sensory and cultural shock.

* **Silence your rig:** Before entering the portal or descending into the caldera, wrap every hard buckle, clip, tank band, and weight in matte black silicone tape or paracord sleeves. Replace metal carabiners with spliced soft-shackles made of dark cordage. Nothing on your body should be capable of making a *clink* if you brush a pillar. In water this clear and still, sound is public property; keep yours to zero.
* **Warm, hooded lighting:** Eyes adapted to dim olive-amber twilight and deep plum shadows will be painfully blinded by harsh blue-white dive torches. Furthermore, cold white light flattens the subtle rust-reds, bronzes, and maroons of the underwater thickets into ugly gray glare. Carry low-output, wide-beam lamps filtered to a warm amber or soft gold (around 2700K–3000K) that mimics natural light filtering through the pumice ceiling, hooded so the bulb never shines sideways into a neighbor’s reed-screened room. Keep a stronger neutral light stowed solely for emergencies over the deep gorge.
* **Leave no metal behind, flash no steel:** Conceal high-tech gauges inside wrist wraps or low-profile matte-black housings that look closer to polished basalt or cured dark resin than machined metal. Never offer metal knives or hooks as trade gifts unless you want to destabilize local craft economies overnight. If you want to bring gifts that make immediate, wondrous sense to masters of stone, cordage, and buoyancy, bring things that belong to an organic material world yet don’t exist in a barren volcanic massif:
  * Blocks of dense, fine-grained, water-resistant terrestrial hardwoods (like lignum vitae or ironwood, which sink naturally in water without stone anchors!).
  * Coils of extraordinarily strong, rot-proof natural or matte-dyed plant cordage.
  * Cakes of high-purity beeswax or pine pitch that stay malleable at 38°C for sealing inverted gas baskets.
  * Smooth, water-worn river cobbles of tough nephrite jade or flint—stones that have known ten thousand years of tumbling sand, something no eye in this sharp, newborn valley has ever beheld. Imagine handing a master mason who has only ever touched razor-edged glass a naturally round, satin-smooth river stone.

### 6. Your Insertion and Exit Plan

1. **Pre-acclimatize or pre-breathe:** Before arriving, spend twenty-four hours breathing slightly enriched oxygen or adjusting your dive computer precisely to the local surface barometric pressure so your tissues aren't carrying sea-level nitrogen loads into a high-altitude ascent.
2. **Enter away from the central abyss:** If you are inserting at the surface rather than directly into the water, bring an insulated mountain shell sealed inside a dry-bag cached at your entry point on the jagged caldera collar. When you climb out of 100°F water into thin, freezing mountain fog, evaporative cooling will strip heat from your wet slash-suit with shocking violence—you can go from near-heatstroke underwater to shivering hypothermia on the rocks in ninety seconds.
3. **Carry a physical reel on the outskirts:** Visibility holds to thirty paces and then thickens smoothly into charcoal and plum. If you wander out past the town's outermost tether-lines where the blind silver fish hang facing down into the mineral plumes, there is no flat bottom waiting softly below—only sheer, fractured shelves dropping into the central clefts. Clip a silent, polymer-spooled dark guideline to a stable basalt horn (wrapped in a Kevlar chafe-sleeve so the rock doesn't slice your line behind your back!) before peering over the gorge.
4. **Stop, float, and listen:** Once your buoyancy is trimmed, your cooling vest is steady, and your rebreather is humming silently behind your shoulders, let yourself hang motionless beside a twenty-foot ribbon of rust-red kelp. Watch the tiny volcanic gas beads detach from the gorge below and tap softly upward from leaf to leaf. Wait for the distant, porcelain *tink, tink* of stone splitting stone across the warm twilight. When your heart rate drops to match the afternoon drift of a shed leaf, swim gently toward the pumice-knotted guide-ropes. You’re ready to enter town.
