To prepare for this pocket, you have to understand one fundamental truth before you pack a single bag: **you are about to walk into a self-playing, subterranean kiln as a bag of warm water.** 

Nothing in this cavern has ever encountered moisture. The wood of the ancient looms didn’t rot; it pyrolyzed into charcoal. That tells you the ambient temperature isn't merely "desert hot"—it sits somewhere between a slow oven and an industrial drying room, likely north of 120°C (250°F) in the upper tiers and climbing rapidly toward the glowing ~750°C (1,400°F) bedrock trenches at the bottom. An unprotected human will not sweat their way through this; your lungs will sear on the first inhale, your corneas will dry and blister, and your core temperature will spike to lethal levels in minutes.

You are not packing for a hike in a ruin. You are gearing up for an extra-vehicular spacewalk inside a roaring, abrasive wind tunnel. Here is how an ordinary human prepares to enter, move through, and return from the salt-blister city intact.

---

### 1. Thermal Management & Life Support
Standard desert gear is useless here. Evaporative cooling (sweating) cannot save you inside a sealed protective suit, and exposing your skin to the air is fatal.

*   **Closed-Loop Liquid Cooling Garment (LCG):** Beneath your outer shell, you must wear a full-body tubing suit circulating chilled water or glycol from an insulated backpack unit (ice-slurry phase-change packs are more reliable here than electronic heat pumps, which struggle to dump heat when the surrounding air is already hotter than their radiators). 
*   **Aluminized Proximity Suit (Modified):** Your outer layer needs to reflect radiant heat—especially if you plan to descend anywhere near the cherry-red floor vents—and block convective heat from the rolling wind-engine. However, standard firefighting proximity suits are clunky and easily torn. You need an expedition-grade composite: an aluminized fiberglass or PBI/Kevlar outer shell with an aerogel thermal liner to keep you flexible enough to climb narrow stairs.
*   **Self-Contained Breathing Apparatus (SCBA):** Do **not** bring a filter mask. Filters cannot remove extreme heat from the air, nor can they manufacture oxygen where superheated mineral gases and sulfur vents have displaced it. You need compressed, breathable air (or a closed-circuit rebreather with an inline chiller) feeding into a positive-pressure full-face helmet. Positive pressure ensures that if your neck seal shifts, clean air blows *out* rather than letting roasted clay, sulfur, and microscopic glass *in*.
*   **Vacuum-Insulated Hydration:** Your drinking water must sit inside your cooling loop or in a double-walled steel vacuum bladder inside the suit, routed to a bite valve in your helmet. Any tube exposed to the cavern air will feed you boiling water. Furthermore, **seal all vents against liquid leakage.** Dropping cold water onto superheated volcanic rock or pressurized silica plants can cause violent steam-spalling, shattering stone or triggering localized plant ruptures.

### 2. Armor Against the Flora and the Dust
There are no animals here to bite you, yet the ecosystem is mechanically hostile. The thermophytes are made of flexible glass (silica), abrasive pumice, and braided, metallic-sheened fibers, all whipping around in heavy winds.

*   **Slash and Puncture Resistance:** When a barometric surge hits, those copper-wire vines stiffen instantly with enough force to ring audibly, and hollow gas-bladders explode like whip-cracks. If a vine loops around your ankle or catches your sleeve when a vent pulses, it can slice through standard fabric—compromising your thermal seal. Reinforce your forearms, shins, shoulders, and air hoses with woven para-aramid (Kevlar) or fine stainless-steel mesh gaiters.
*   **Sacrificial Tear-Off Visors:** The air is a sandblaster loaded with red clay motes, ash-gray pollen, and powdered silica ground down against the ceiling. Polycarbonate helmet visors will frost over with micro-scratches within twenty minutes. Apply a stack of ten to twelve optical-grade Mylar tear-off films over a gold-coated, heat-reflective visor (preferably quartz glass or sapphire-coated polycarbonate).
*   **High-Temp Boots with Soft Carbon-Rubber Soles:** Standard hiking boots will delaminate as their glue melts, and Vibram soles will soften and smear near the lower wards. Wear vulcanized, stitched-sole high-heat boots (similar to structural firefighting or foundry footwear). Avoid metal crampons or hobnails—they will skate dangerously across the soap-smooth, wind-polished stone. Instead, look for high-temperature silicone or soft carbon-compound treads with fine micro-siping to grip smooth, dry surfaces.

### 3. Sensory Protection: Surviving the "Organ"
The psychological and physiological toll of the noise in this chamber will end your expedition faster than fatigue if you underestimate it.

*   **Double Hearing Protection:** Between the constant roaring wind-engine, a thousand silica fronds scraping stone like whetstones, and seed-bladders detonating overhead, decibel levels will cause permanent hearing damage and severe disorientation. Wear molded silicone earplugs *underneath* a padded, sound-attenuating helmet.
*   **Preparing for Infrasound:** The city’s thousands of flues and cisterns act as a giant pipe organ. Large stone resonance chambers driven by heavy convection produce powerful low-frequency hums and infrasound (below 20 Hz) that ear protection cannot block because it vibrates straight through your skull and ribcage. Infrasound can vibrate your eyeballs (blurring your vision), rattle your inner ear (destroying your balance on narrow catwalks), and trigger sudden, irrational panic or nausea. Knowing this is purely mechanical before you go in is your best defense. Keep anti-nausea medication (like ondansetron) in your kit—taken *before* you suit up.
*   **Lighting Strategy (Forget Thermal Cameras):** Leave thermal/IR optics behind; in a kiln full of hot stone and heat-metabolizing plants, an infrared screen will just be a useless, washed-out glare. Standard high-lumen white flashlights are almost as bad—the floating clay dust and periodic "snowstorms" of ash-gray pollen will bounce white light straight back into your eyes, blinding you just like high beams in a blizzard. 
    *   Mount **low-angle amber or yellow-green LED floodlights** down near your waist or knees. Low-mounted, warm-tinted light cuts underneath suspended dust and casts long, clear shadows across the dished, worn-down stair treads so you can actually read the depth of your footing.
    *   Ensure your visor has an auto-darkening or tinted upper strip so you aren't flash-blinded if you accidentally look directly up into one of the needle-thin shafts of raw white sunlight piercing the ceiling.

### 4. Rigging and Moving Through the Lattice
The architecture is all frame and no mass—slender pillars, open-ribbed domes, and narrow catwalks smoothed by centuries of dry convection. A fall here drops you through six stories of stone ribs straight toward glowing gas trenches.

*   **No Nylon Ropes or Webbing:** Standard climbing gear (nylon and Dyneema) weakens drastically at high temperatures; Dyneema begins losing strength around 65°C (150°F) and melts at 145°C, while nylon degrades rapidly in heat and sulfur-rich air. Every strap, harness, and lanyard you carry must be **100% aramid (Kevlar/Technora) or steel-core cable.**
*   **Wide, Padded Anchor Slings:** Look closely at the masonry before clipping in. The dry-pressed ash mortar is recessed, leaving the volcanic blocks sticking out like knuckles, and the pillars are thin. Never clip a bare metal hook around a slender pillar, and never shock-load a single column—centuries of wind-erosion have thinned them, and dry mortar relies entirely on compression from above to stay stable. Use wide aramid slings to distribute your weight around larger structural intersections (where lintels meet multiple columns), and always maintain two points of contact.
*   **Beware the Dished Stairs:** Centuries of footsteps and dry wind have scooped the stair treads into smooth crescents with rounded, soap-soft edges. Step squarely into the belly of each crescent, keep your center of gravity low, and lean slightly into the prevailing updraft or downdraft depending on whether you are near the center of the chamber (rising hot air) or the outer walls (plunging cooled air).

### 5. Operational Protocol Inside the Pocket
Once you breach into the chamber—ideally rappelling from a resealable airlock bored through a stable margin of the outer wall rather than weakening the fragile vitrified slag ceiling—follow a strict behavioral rhythm:

1.  **Clock the Barometric Pulse First:** Spend your first ten minutes anchored safely on a stable upper terrace doing nothing. Pull out a mechanical stopwatch (electronics dislike extreme heat; bring mechanical backups for timing and pressure) and time the intervals between the deep-crust gas surges. Watch where the vines whip, which flues roar loudest, and how dense the pollen boil gets.
2.  **The Ten-Second Brace:** You will feel the barometric pressure shift in your suit or see a mechanical wrist-barometer dip a split second before the floor trenches brighten. When that happens, **stop moving immediately.** Drop to one knee, clip your short tether taut, tuck your chin to protect your neck seal, and wait out the ten-second *shhhk* of stiffening flora and boiling spores. Do not try to step over a rust-colored frond right as it snaps flat toward a vent; Silica-reinforced stems pressurized by hot gas move with hydraulic force and can sweep your legs right off an unrailed catwalk.
3.  **Stay Out of the Lower Plazas:** Admire the bruised cherry-red floor trenches from two or three terraces up. Down on the bedrock floor, radiant heat will overwhelm your suit’s cooling capacity in minutes, the sulfur gas concentration is highest, and the mat-sized fronds obscure the edges of the superheated channels. One misstep onto a thin crust beside a glowing trench will put your foot straight into roasting mineral gas.
4.  **Strict Turnaround Timers:** Base your turnaround time on your **cooling pack**, not your air tank. If your air tank has 60 minutes left, your ice-slurry might only have 35 minutes of heat-absorption capacity. If your cooling fails at the bottom of an eighty-pace vertical climb, you will cook inside your own insulated suit while hauling yourself back up. Turn back when 40% of your cooling capacity is spent.

### 6. The Most Forgotten Danger: Dry Decontamination
How you take your gear *off* after leaving this place is just as vital as how you put it on.

When you exit the blister into your staging area or transit zone, your suit will be caked in ultra-fine silica glass powder, dry red clay, ash spores, and elemental sulfur dust. 
*   **Do NOT walk straight into a wet decon shower.** Airborne sulfur compounds mixed with water and humidity can form sulfurous and sulfuric acids on your gear and skin, while wet clay turns into an abrasive paste that gums up zippers and suit seals.
*   **Vacuum and Brush Dry First:** Keep your breathing air running and your helmet sealed while you (or a partner) use a HEPA-filtered vacuum and stiff dry brushes to strip every speck of dust off your suit, boots, and visor stack. 
*   Only after the dry dust is completely removed should you do a damp wipe-down, break your helmet seal, and peel out of your suit—because inhaling even a tiny puff of microscopic silica and thermophyte spores clinging to your collar as you pull your helmet off will scar your lungs permanently.

Treat the chamber with the respect due to a massive, running engine. Move slowly between the surges, trust aramid over nylon, keep your visor clear, and let the dead city sing around you without ever touching its skin with yours.
