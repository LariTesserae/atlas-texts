Visiting the Planed Plateau—this high, breathless ghost of a mountain range and its dilated Bronze-Age metropolis—requires preparing for two entirely different environments at once. Physically, you are going on a wet, high-altitude Arctic trek. Cognitively and navigationally, you are walking into a slow-motion shipwreck of space-time where your evolutionary instincts about distance, shelter, and movement will actively betray you. 

An ordinary human can survive and even find a strange, quiet peace here, provided you respect the altitude, the damp, and the silent weather of broken geometry. Here is how you must prepare.

### 1. Physiological Preparation: The Three-Mile Ceiling
Before you even pack a bag, you have to reckon with the elevation. Three miles into the troposphere puts you at roughly 15,800 feet (4,800 meters)—higher than any peak in the contiguous United States, roughly equivalent to Mont Blanc or Mount Everest Base Camp. 

*   **Pre-Acclimatization:** Do not step directly from a sea-level portal onto this plateau. If you do, Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS) will set in within hours—splitting headaches, nausea, dizziness, and the fatal risks of high-altitude cerebral or pulmonary edema (HACE/HAPE). Spend at least two weeks stepping up through intermediate altitudes in your home reality, or use a hypobaric tent for a month prior. 
*   **Medication:** Pack Acetazolamide (Diamox) to speed up your body's acclimatization and keep your blood pH balanced while hyperventilating thin air. Bring Dexamethasone strictly as an emergency ripcord if you develop cerebral swelling and need to reach your extraction point.
*   **Sun and Glare Protection:** Thin, cloudless air filters almost no ultraviolet radiation, and the wet, polished gneiss and beaded moss will bounce light right back under your chin. Bring Category 4 glacier glasses with side shields (wrap-around styles are best, though be careful—you will need your peripheral vision just to stay sane, so choose frames with minimal blind spots) and high-SPF zinc oxide sunscreen for your nose, lips, and under your jaw.

### 2. Clothing and Shelter: Fighting the Flash-Damp
Temperatures hovering right at freezing (30°F to 35°F / -1°C to 2°C) combined with sudden, drenching condensation are the textbook conditions for hypothermia. Extreme dry cold is easy to insulate against; wet, near-freezing cold steals body heat twenty-five times faster than air.

*   **No Down, No Cotton:** Leave goose down behind. The condensation banks here do not roll in from the horizon; they *materialize* out of clear air along metric seams. You will be dry one minute and soaked to the skin the next. Pack exclusively synthetic insulation (like PrimaLoft or Climashield Apex) and heavyweight merino wool, which retain warmth when saturated.
*   **Hard-Shell Armor:** Wear a heavy-duty, three-layer waterproof-breathable shell (jacket and full-zip pants) at all times, even under a cloudless sky. You will not see the mist coming.
*   **Footwear:** The ground alternates between slick, wet, polished gneiss, ankle-twisting cobbles, waist-high cushion domes, and saturated sphagnum bogs. Wear knee-high waterproof trekking boots paired with tightly strapped gaiters, or insulated neoprene muck-boots with aggressive vibram lugs (avoid metal microspikes unless necessary, as steel skids terribly on smooth, wet gneiss). Pack six pairs of wool socks in dry-bags and rotate them religiously.
*   **Bivouac Strategy:** Do not plan on sleeping inside the city’s tenements or storehouses. A building here is not a unified object; it is a collection of topologically linked walls that may suddenly drift four miles apart while you are asleep, leaving your sleeping bag in an open bog while your boots sit in the corner of the room eleven miles west. Worse, do not touch or camp near the cobble hearth-pits—their "inverted fire" radiates an active chill that will suck the core warmth right out of you. Bring a freestanding, four-season geodesic dome tent. Pitch it out on the open plateau, preferably nestled into the leeward side of the giant cushion-plants (which hoard real solar warmth and provide a firm, insulated mattress), well clear of any masonry or invisible overhead water-courses.

### 3. Navigation: Time Over Distance
Every standard human navigational tool is worse than useless here; it is a trap. 
*   A **GPS** relies on precise light-speed timing from satellites (which don't exist here anyway, and variable metric density warps the speed and path of light).
*   A **pedometer or pace-count** fails when one step crosses fifty yards and the next takes ten minutes over a single puddle.
*   **Visual landmarks** lie constantly. That colossal turf granary dominating a third of the northern sky is twenty miles away; the tiny grey thread beside it is an aqueduct you will trip over in ten minutes.

To navigate the Plateau and return to your entry point, you must decouple your mind from *distance* and anchor yourself to *time* and *topology*.

*   **Solar Compass:** The sun moves steadily and honestly westward. While magnetic compasses might be thrown off by native copper deposits or ancient iron anomalies in the shield rock, a simple mechanical watch (use a winding mechanical movement; cold kills batteries, and strange temporal reflections in puddles suggest local time-slips might confuse quartz oscillators) paired with the sun's arc and the sharp shadows of the phantom mountains will give you reliable cardinal directions.
*   **The Paved Roads and Conduits as Lifelines:** Though distance along them stretches and shrinks, the city’s topology *holds*. A kerbstone-lined avenue still connects District A to District B, even if the width of the street is currently three miles. If you need to travel fast, **use thresholds**. Step through doorways, under market awnings, or step across culvert grates—test them first!—to traverse miles of dilated plateau in a single stride. 
*   **Marking Your Trail:** Do not rely on cairns you expect to spot from afar; metric lensing might shrink your cairn to the size of a thimble from twenty yards away. Instead, carry spools of high-visibility surveyor's tape and chalk. Mark the actual *paving slabs* of the road you walk on, and keep a meticulous written log not of miles walked, but of **topological transitions** (e.g., *"Crossed beneath braided-reed awning from North kerb; stepped over clay-chinked threshold facing West"*).
*   **Tethered Testing:** Never put your whole body across an architectural threshold at once. Carry fifty feet of brightly colored static climbing rope and a handful of heavy rubber washers (pebbles work, too, though they are scarce on clean gneiss). Toss a washer tied to a cord through a doorway first. Pull it back. Make sure the space on the other side isn't currently submerged in a sky-stream or suspended over a deep drainage sump, and verify that the connection works *both ways* before stepping through.

### 4. Moving Through Ghost Gravity and Metric Weather
The air above you is full of invisible terrain. This is one of the weirdest physical hazards you will face.

*   **Carry a "Feeler" Staff:** Bring a sturdy, collapsible trekking pole—carbon fiber or wood, **not** aluminum (wet cold metal steals heat from hands). Hold it extended slightly ahead and upward as you walk, especially where woody vines spiral into empty air. Where vines climb nothing, there is an invisible gravitational slope. Step carelessly into a phantom valley's sheer "cliff," and you might feel gravity abruptly yank you sideways or downward along an unseen vector, or get knocked flat by a sudden cascade of near-freezing snowmelt pouring off an invisible ledge forty feet up.
*   **Reading the Metric Fronts:** Watch the moss. When buildings begin sliding silently inward during a spatial compression front, do *not* panic and run—the ground isn't tearing, its width is simply vanishing. However, compression fronts trigger massive spore-releases from the chartreuse roof-mosses. In an ecosystem completely devoid of animals, your lungs have zero evolutionary familiarity with these spores, and at 15,800 feet you cannot afford allergic bronchitis or spore-induced pneumonia. Keep a couple of **N95 or P100 particulate respirators** in your jacket pocket. The moment the alleys start narrowing and the air warms up, mask up until the buildings drift apart again.
*   **The Ten-Minute Step:** When you hit a patch of extreme spatial dilation—where your boot hangs over a puddle and ten minutes of walking barely moves you forward—do not fight it by sprinting. Sprinting at three miles of altitude will blow out your heart rate and leave you drenched in sweat. Keep a slow, rhythmic mountaineer's "rest step." Breathe rhythmically. Treat thick space exactly like walking up a steep snowfield. And **do not stare into the puddles** while you are stuck above them. Watching a six-hour-old night sky full of stars beneath your boots while afternoon sunlight hits your neck will trigger ferocious vertigo and motion sickness. Keep your eyes fixed on your knees or the horizon.

### 5. Water, Food, and Fuel
*   **Water is Abundant and Pure:** This is one of the merciful things about a sterile-animal world. With no mammals, birds, or insects, there is no *Giardia*, no *Cryptosporidium*, no carrion upstream. Still, run the sky-stream water through a basic hollow-fiber filter to clear out moss spores, mineral grit, and plant tannins. Collect water directly from the mid-air cascades or stone spouts rather than stagnant sumps, just to avoid concentrated plant saps.
*   **Do Not Eat the Flora:** Everything green here is packed with biological antifreeze—glycols, heavy alkaloids, oxalates, or concentrated sugars/salts designed to stop cellular ice crystals. Even those tempting, faintly steaming, heat-generating cabbages in the courtyard gardens likely run on cyanide-resistant cellular respiration (like Earth's skunk cabbage) and are probably toxic or severely blistering to human mucous membranes. Admire their warmth with your gloved hands, but pack 100% of your own high-calorie, high-fat expedition rations (4,000+ calories a day for cold, high-altitude exertion).
*   **Stoves:** Leave alcohol stoves and standard butane canisters at home; they sputter and fail in thin, freezing air. Bring a **white-gas (liquid fuel) expedition stove** with a manual pressure pump, or an inverted-canister isobutane stove with a heat exchanger. Remember that water boils at roughly 184°F (84.5°C) at this altitude—hot drinks won't be piping hot, and freeze-dried meals will take twice as long to rehydrate.
*   **Leave No Microbiome Behind:** Because this world has zero animal life or soil detritivores (no worms, no beetles, no flies), human waste will *not* break down normally. Anything you leave on the plateau could sit frozen and preserved for decades, or introduce terrifyingly disruptive gut bacteria to a pristine bryophyte world. Pack WAG bags (waste alleviation and gelling bags) and carry every ounce of your trash and waste back out with you.

### 6. Psychological Readiness: The Great Quiet and the Broken Eye
Ultimately, what drives unprepared travelers off the Plateau early isn't frostbite—it's sensory exhaustion.

*   **Acoustic Deprivation:** Humans are not wired for an outdoor landscape with zero biological sound. No wind-rustle of wings, no buzz, no crunch of soil-life—just thin air, dripping water, and the occasional eerie groan of bog-timber stretching across warped space. At night inside your tent, the silence will be so absolute you will hear your own blood rushing through your carotid arteries and the faint hiss of your optic nerves. Bring an offline audio player (solar-chargeable—the cloudless sun is fantastic for solar panels!) loaded with music, audiobooks, or simple recordings of rain and birdsong to play inside your sleeping bag at night. Talking aloud to yourself while hiking also helps anchor your sense of immediate personal scale.
*   **Visual Humility:** You have to consciously turn off the part of your brain that translates *apparent size* into *proximity*. In crisp alpine light, our brains assume anything sharp and huge is right in front of us. Walking for four hours toward a looming timber granary without it getting a fraction of an inch closer—or watching a tiny grey thread suddenly trip your boot—causes profound cognitive dissonance, derealization, and headaches. 

Practice a kind of slow, observant Zen. Touch the cold-hammered copper pins. Marvel at a twenty-foot grass awning spanning six miles of empty sky without tearing. Warm your fingers against the steaming cabbages and watch the ghost-mountains shed their phantom snowmelt into stone cisterns built by hands that vanished who knows how long ago—or perhaps didn't vanish at all, merely drifted so far apart along the streets of their own city that you could walk a lifetime without crossing their current square yard of space. Move slowly, stay dry, breathe deep, and trust your rope before your eyes.
