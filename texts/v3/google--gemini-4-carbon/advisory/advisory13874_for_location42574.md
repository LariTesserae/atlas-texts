To the uninitiated, a courtyard-sized room at fifty degrees Fahrenheit with clean air and no large predators sounds like an easy transit—a curious geological museum piece you could visit in a jacket and hiking boots. 

That assumption will get you maimed inside of ninety seconds.

You are not stepping into a cave. Caves are old; caves have had millions of years for water and friction to sand down their teeth and settle their arguments about physics. You are stepping into an active, malfunctioning creation event sealed under forty feet of blind dirt. There is no climbable exit. If your transit hardware breaks, cannot lock onto coordinates through distorted space, or gets sliced in half by a spontaneous shelf of feldspar, nobody on the surface will ever hear you screaming through forty feet of earth—especially in a room that swallows sound at the source.

Prepare for this pocket the way a deep-sea diver prepares for a wreck, combined with the precautions of a glassblower working in a funhouse. Here is everything an ordinary human needs to pack, wear, and practice before dialing in.

---

### 1. Insertion & Extraction: Surviving Second Zero

Because this chamber has no physical entrance, you are arriving via direct spatial transit. In an unstable pocket like this, sloppy coordinate-locking is lethal.

* **Send a sacrificial sonde first—on a physical tether.** Do not step in blind. Open a palm-sized aperture first and push through a weighted polycarbonate rod tipped with a wide-angle camera. You need to verify two things before committing your body: that your arrival point hasn't just grown a jutting plate of raw chert since your last scan, and that you aren't dropping into a localized gravity tilt that will slam you sideways into razor-edged stone the instant your mass clears the threshold.
* **Target the "settled" middle, never the walls or ceiling.** Keep your arrival coordinates at least eight feet away from the glaring white vacancy-panes and well below the flat glare of the ceiling. Do not arrive flush with the floor; aim two inches above a confirmed flat slab of slate or chert, knees slightly bent, ready to absorb a drop.
* **Redundant, body-hugging transit triggers.** Whatever device pulls you home cannot hang loose on a lanyard or sit in a backpack pocket. A stray spatial snare-thread or a sudden crystal formation could sever a strap or bisect a pack. Wear two independent return-triggers strapped tight to your torso—one over your sternum, one on your opposite wrist—wired to a dead-man’s biometric switch. If your heart rate spikes into shock or drops to zero, it pulls you out automatically.

### 2. Armor Against "Zero-History" Stone

On Earth, even a freshly broken rock has usually suffered microscopic weathering or structural bruising. The minerals here—chert, flint, orthoclase—have literally *zero history*. Their edges are single-molecule pristine, sharper than surgical steel, and rigid enough to split hairs without pressure. If you trip and catch yourself with a bare hand, or brush your calf against a ledge, you won't feel the cut until you see your suit filling with blood.

* **Cut-Level A9 Outer Shell with Hard Plates:** Leave ordinary ripstop nylon, Gore-Tex, and leather at home; raw flint will shave ribbons off them like soft cheese. Wear an outer layer woven from UHMWPE (Dyneema) and stainless-steel micro-mesh—the kind of fabric used by industrial glass-handlers and butcher-block workers. Over your knees, shins, elbows, forearms, and shoulders, strap rigid, rounded polycarbonate skate-plates. Polycarbonate slides off sharp mineral edges rather than catching on them.
* **Gloves:** Wear heavy-duty, steel-core cut-resistant gloves coated in textured nitrile for wet grip, with hard knuckle guards. *Never take them off inside the pocket.*
* **Footwear:** Standard rubber Vibram soles will be shredded to spongy confetti by unweathered flint, and they will hydroplane violently on wet, soapy anhydrite. Wear rigid-soled mountaineering boots fitted with **soft aluminum strap-on crampons** (not hardened steel). Hard steel skates horribly on glassy chert; soft aluminum deforms microscopically against sharp rock edges, biting in to give you purchase even when dry stone turns slick between one heartbeat and the next.
* **Keep a sleek profile:** Tape down every loose strap, buckle, D-ring, and pocket flap. Anything dangling from your body is bait—both for razor-sharp rock facets and for things hunting in the air.

### 3. Thermal & Moisture Defense

Fifty degrees Fahrenheit is mild when dry, and deeply insidious when damp. Worse, moisture here does not obey condensation or rain mechanics; it *declares itself*, relocating instantaneously from floor to ledge.

* **Assume water will bypass your outer shell.** Do not rely on waterproof membranes to keep you dry. There is a very real chance that a patch of cold sweat won't land *on* your jacket, but will simply materialize directly *inside* your sleeve against your skin. 
* **Dress like a wet-caver:** Under your cut-armor, wear close-fitting heavy merino wool or a thin, fleece-lined 3mm neoprene wetsuit. Both retain body heat even if water suddenly teleports against your ribs, and neither relies on trapped air loft that collapses when wet.

### 4. Optics, Breathing, and the "Dead Air" Problem

Your senses are going to rebel the moment you arrive. Two specific environmental traits—the clinical, shadowless snow-glare and the unnatural acoustic deadening—combine to induce violent nausea, vertigo, and panic in ordinary human brains within minutes.

* **Full-Seal Face Shield & Amber/Vermilion Category 4 Lenses:** You need wrap-around glacier-grade eye protection with side shields. Light pours from the walls and ceiling simultaneously; without shadows, the human visual cortex **cannot perceive depth or surface relief**. A six-inch razor blade of pale orthoclase sticking straight up out of a pale floor will be completely invisible to the naked eye. High-contrast amber or vermilion-tinted polarized lenses help pick out subtle mineral shifts, while the full polycarbonate face shield protects your eyes and cheeks from invisible, zero-width creatures turning edge-on in midair.
* **Bring a Structured-Light Laser Grid:** Do not bother bringing a regular flashlight to "see better"—light behaves weirdly here anyway, as proven by the stick casting a shadow *toward* the glare while nothing else casts a shadow at all. Instead, mount a battery-powered **green cross-hatch laser projector** on your chest or waist, angled at the floor ten feet ahead of you. Even in bright, shadowless glare, a projected grid of bright green lines will visibly warp, jag, and break over sharp ridges and sudden drops, giving you artificial topography where natural shadows fail.
* **P100 Respirator Mask:** The air smells clean and mineral-sharp, with no rot or spores, which tempts travelers to breathe freely. Don't. The middle air is full of pinhead midges that *blink in and out of visibility*—and possibly out of three-dimensional space—alongside airborne bead-like cysts in the floating mist. You do not want to inhale a breath of "empty" damp air only to have a swarm of midges blink back into existence inside your bronchial tubes, or have cysts hatch in the warm condensation of your lungs. Wear a low-profile P100 half-mask under your visor.
* **Bone-Conduction Audio Anchor:** Do not underestimate the psychological crush of the silence. In an anechoic environment where forty frantic creatures chasing each other make less sound than dust hitting wool, your brain will feel like your ears have been packed with wax and submerged in heavy oil. You won't hear your own footsteps, which destroys human balance rhythm. Wear bone-conduction headphones resting on your cheekbones, playing a very quiet, steady acoustic baseline—a gentle 60-BPM metronome click or soft brown noise. This keeps your inner ear and nervous system grounded, while leaving your ear canals unobstructed so you can still catch the vital, tiny *tick* of a rock pane about to solidify.
* *(If traveling with a partner, link your bone-conduction headsets via short-range wired tether or direct line-of-sight optical comms; shouting from six feet away will sound like a muffled whisper behind a vault door, and stretched space may distort radio wavelengths.)*

### 5. Tools for Broken Geometry & Shifting Gravity

Whoever was here before you—leaving behind flint tripods, hair strings, jawbones, and mud-and-spittle cairns—was brilliant. Study their survival logic before you take a single step. Notice two things they proved: **gravity can split into three directions within the width of a thumb**, and **a rigid stick can bridge in inches a gap that takes twenty paces to walk**.

* **Carry Two Six-Foot Polycarbonate Surveying Staves:** Do not use carbon fiber (it shatters into toxic splinters against flint) or aluminum (it bends). Carry two solid, high-visibility orange polycarbonate rods. 
    * **The Plumb-Bob Array:** Copy the monument-maker. Along the length of your primary staff, tie four short, three-inch strings weighted with bright brass beads, spaced a foot apart. Hold the staff out ahead of you as you walk. If the front two beads suddenly swing thirty degrees toward a wall or point upward toward a ledge while the back two hang straight down, *stop*. You have just found a gravity shear without throwing your inner ear—or your spine—into it.
    * **The Spatial Bridge Test:** Never step across an innocent-looking gap between two rocks without tapping the far side with your staff first. If the rock looks three feet away, and your six-foot staff reaches all the way out without touching it, you are looking into a spatial stretch. Conversely, if you get stuck in a twenty-pace stretch, lay a rigid plank or locking pole ladder across the anchor boulders on either side; physical bridging appears to bypass the walking-distance distortion, allowing you to crawl across on the pole itself rather than trudging through warped floor-space.
* **Sweep the Empty Air:** Before moving forward, sweep your secondary staff vertically through the "empty" air ahead of you like a blind person checking for cobwebs. Those boneless, brittle-star ambush predators under the gypsum overhangs fire sticky feeding threads through folded geometry—entering the air three inches to their left and emerging six feet away out of nowhere. Their prey doesn't bleed; it *unspools into dry chalk*. You cannot afford to find out what those threads do to a human thigh. Let your polycarbonate rod catch the invisible snare-threads first; keep a ceramic safety cutter strapped to your wrist to slice the rod free (or abandon the rod) if one latches on.

### 6. Field Protocols Inside the Pocket

Once you are standing in the cool glare, follow five strict operational rules:

1. **Respect the White Panes—And Watch for the Dimming:** The glowing white wall-patches feel like cold glass, which invites curious travelers to touch them. Keep six feet back at all times. Watch your peripheral vision: before a pane snaps into stone, it *shudders and dims*. Worse, new rock doesn't always stay at the walls—remember the shelf of feldspar clicking into existence right through the middle of a mud cairn on the floor. Never sit down, never lean against a rock face, and never linger in one spot for more than two minutes. Keep slowly shifting your stance. If rock materializes intersecting your boot or gear, *do not yank backward in panic*—notice how overlapping rocks flicker without breaking each other depending on viewing angle. If a rock shelf spawns through the edge of your pack or boot sole, freeze, change your line of sight/head angle slowly to see if phase-flickering occurs, or cut the trapped piece of gear away rather than dragging flesh across a zero-history crystal edge.
2. **Mind the Wetness Shifts:** Never commit 100% of your weight to an angled foothold. A bone-dry slab of slate under your left boot can inherit a sheet of water from three feet away in zero point one seconds. Walk flat-footed, knees bent, center of gravity low, three points of contact (two boots, one staff) at all times.
3. **Treat the Fauna as Moving Razor Blades and Trigger-Switches:** None of the creatures seem large enough to view a six-foot human as standard prey, *except* perhaps the opportunistic snare-threads. However, a shoal of hand-sized, hard-shelled grazers turning ninety degrees vanishes into zero width. Until proven otherwise, assume a rigid object with thickness approaching zero acts like a monofilament wire if it strikes you at speed. Give the bark-ribbons wide berth, too—not because they'll bite you, but because their passage *briefly resurrects dead matter* beneath them (like the seventeenth carapace). You do not want a live bark-ribbon drifting six inches over your head and doing something unpredictable to the dead keratin of your hair, fingernails, or outer epidermis, or waking up dormant bacteria in your gut.
4. **Do Not Disturb the Monuments:** Leave the flint tripod, the jawbone plumb lines, the mud-and-spittle cairns, and the seventeen carapaces completely untouched. First, they are active, calibrated hazard buoys left by someone who paid dearly for that knowledge—the cairns literally trace the invisible border of the spatial stretch for you! Second, someone built those without a scrap of metal, cloth, or wood, using maybe their own spittle and whatever animal parts arrived with them—or parts of themselves. Treat them with quiet reverence. Photograph them, sketch them, measure the angles from a polite distance, and step around.
5. **Strict "Zero-Crumb" Containment:** Nothing rots here. Nothing erodes. There are no detritivores equipped for standard terrestrial decay, no fungi, no soil bacteria. If you drop a wrapper, spit on the floor, shed an eyelash, or bleed a drop onto a slab of quartz, it will sit there under hospital-bright glare in pristine, clinical stasis forever—or get folded into the weird cyclic ecology in ways you shouldn't meddle with. Do not eat or drink inside the pocket. Keep a small, sealed bio-bag clipped to your harness for any snapped crampon straps or snagged polycarbonate rods.
