To step into this place unprepared is to step into a dry-stone sandblaster that happens to be lit like an operating theater and loud as a jet engine. As an ordinary human, your body is mostly soft water, fragile membranes, and psychological habits that rely on open space and changing stimuli. This room is hostile to all of those things—not out of malice, or extreme heat, or alien venom, but through sheer, relentless physical intensity. 

Preparing for this visit requires treating the twelve-foot chamber less like an archaeological site and more like the interior of an active, stone-built wind tunnel. Here is how you must prepare your body, your gear, and your mind before you cross over.

### 1. Sight and the Shadowless Glare
Your first instinct upon arriving without protection will be to slam your eyes shut, and even through your eyelids, the world will glow a violent, bright pink. Because the chalk-white limestone reflects light from every angle—including straight up from the vertical floor-slabs—ordinary sunglasses are useless; glare will simply bounce up behind the frames and sear your corneas. Within an hour, unprotected eyes will suffer photokeratitis (snow blindness), made infinitely worse by microscopic alkali dust.

*   **Mountaineering Goggles:** Do not wear glasses. Wear fully sealed, foam-gasketed glacier goggles (Category 4 tint, allowing only 3% to 8% of visible light through). If the foam vents are open to prevent fogging, make sure the foam is dense enough to block fine mineral particulates. Wraparound side-shields are non-negotiable.
*   **Depth Perception Calibration:** Because there are almost no shadows here—only subtle shifts from bone-white to silver-grey—your brain will lose its primary cue for depth perception. A corbelled arch two inches from your forehead will look the exact same brightness as the wall three feet behind it. Before you move, accept that you will be navigating as much by touch as by sight, even in blinding light.

### 2. Hearing and Bone-Deep Vibration
The sound in this cistern is not background noise; it is a physical wall of pressure. A blast-furnace roar combined with a high, crystalline whistle that *never fluctuates* will cause permanent hearing damage quickly, and profound disorientation almost immediately.

*   **Dual Hearing Protection:** Standard foam earplugs are not enough, and over-ear muffs alone are too bulky for squeezing sideways between the stone piers (if a stone rib catches your earmuff and knocks it askew, the sudden blast of sound will stun you). Wear deeply seated, high-decibel-rated silicone or foam earplugs, and cover them with a tight neoprene or heavy fleece headband, or a low-profile bump helmet with slim integrated ear cups. 
*   **Securing the Earplugs:** Ensure your earplugs are corded and clipped to your collar. If one slips out of your fingers here, the wind and vertical floor-chinks will claim it instantly.
*   **Vibrational Fatigue:** Even with your ears sealed, you will *feel* the chord of the room in your teeth, your sternum, and the soles of your feet. The stone itself hums. Wear thick, shock-absorbing elastomer insoles in your boots to dampen the constant high-frequency buzz coming up through the vertical flagstones.

### 3. Respiration and Rapid Desiccation
The prompt notes that the roaring dryness would desiccate soft tissue in hours. You are essentially stepping into a cold food dehydrator laced with blown gypsum and alkali salt-pan dust. Alkali dust is caustic when it hits wet lung tissue or damp skin.

*   **Particulate & Moisture Masking:** Wear a tightly fitted elastomeric half-mask respirator with P100 (or FFP3) particulate filters. A simple cloth scarf will let microscopic gypsum right through, though tying a heavy silk or tightly woven cotton bandana *over* your respirator valves can help keep the dry blast from chapping your face and clogging the filter pleats too quickly.
*   **Heat-and-Moisture Exchanger (HME) or Humidification:** Breathing this air straight will crack your lips in twenty minutes, trigger nosebleeds in forty, and leave you with a raw, hacking throat for days afterward. Coat the inside of your nostrils thickly with petroleum jelly or saline gel before putting on your mask. Better yet, use a cold-weather breathing mask designed for polar runners, which traps the moisture from your exhaled breath and uses it to humidify your next inhale.
*   **Barrier Creams and Total Coverage:** Leave zero skin exposed. Any bare patch of wrist or neck will be wind-burned and alkali-chapped by the constant abrasion. Coat your face, hands, and neck in a heavy, wax-based barrier balm (not water-based lotion, which will evaporate almost instantly and freeze-dry your skin). 
*   **Internal Hydration:** Drink a liter of electrolyte-heavy water in the hour *before* you arrive. Carry water on your person—not in a rigid bottle strapped to a pack, which won't fit through the piers, but in a flat hydration bladder worn *under* your outer jacket, with the bite-valve tucked inside your collar so the mouthpiece doesn't get crusted with alkali dust.

### 4. Clothing and Clearance (Dressing for the Squeeze)
This world is twelve feet across and choked with stone pillars. To cross it, your chest will scrape one wall while your shoulder blades catch on the next. Furthermore, while the limestone is worn smooth like soap, the calcified scrub wedged in the seams has leaves like sharkskin, and the pewter-colored stems stretched across the gaps are rigid as iron wire.

*   **Ditch the Backpack:** You cannot wear a backpack here. You will get wedged between two dry-built buttresses like a cork in a bottle. Everything you bring—multiverse transit anchor, water, emergency light, rations—must be flat and distributed across your torso in low-profile chest pockets or sleek thigh pockets, leaving your back and sides completely slick.
*   **Zero Snag Hazards:** No loose straps, no dangling carabiners, no belt loops sticking out, no billowing hoods, no knit sweaters. If a loop of nylon catches on a rigid wire-stem or wedges into a tight compression-joint between two cherts while you are sideways in a ten-inch gap, freeing yourself without being able to turn around will be terrifying. Tape down every zipper pull and strap end with heavy cloth tape.
*   **Abrasion-Resistant Shell, Warm Core:** The air is sharp, cool, and strip-mines body heat via relentless windchill. Wear a close-fitting base layer of merino wool or heavy synthetic thermal fabric, a thin windproof membrane, and an outer layer modeled on caving oversuits—smooth, heavy-duty ballistic nylon or Cordura that slides easily against polished rock without tearing on sharkskin leaves. 
*   **Head and Hands:** Wear a sleek, ventless caving helmet or skate-style bump cap. At nine feet high, the ceiling has low, overlapping corbelled arches stepping inward; in the shadowless whiteout, you *will* misjudge a low overhang and crack your skull against solid limestone without one. On your hands, wear tight-fitting, high-dexterity leather work gloves (like goatskin riggers' gloves). You will constantly be bracing your palms against vibrating, cold stone and brushing past calcified twigs.
*   **Footwear:** The floor is made of upright flagstone edges packed like books. While smoothed by time, it is rigid, unforgiving, and deeply grooved. Wear boots with stiff shanks (so the edges don't bruise your arches) and narrow toes, with laces tucked completely inside the boot collar and taped over so they cannot snag on low, wire-tight plant stems.

### 5. Transit Safety and Spatial Discipline
Before you trigger your jump into this universe, verify how your multiverse transit method interacts with dense matter. 

*   **Arrival Coordinates:** The habitable pocket is tiny and packed with masonry. If your arrival coordinates drift by eighteen inches in almost any direction, you risk materializing *inside* a solid limestone pier or wedged chest-deep in a corbelled cantilever. If you have a scout-probe or periscope capability, use it first to map an exact standing pocket—even then, arrive in a narrow, upright posture with your arms crossed tightly over your chest, knees slightly bent, feet together.
*   **Tether Every Item:** Look at the floor description again: upright flagstones packed like books, *extending downward to an unknowable depth*, with clean, wind-scoured chinks between them. If you drop your transit key, a lens cap, or a stylus, and it slips edgewise into a vertical gap between floor-slabs, it is gone forever. You are not prying apart stones locked under ten thousand years of ferocious tectonic compression. Every single critical item must be dummy-corded to your body with thin, high-tensile cord tucked inside your sleeves or pockets.
*   **Respect the Flora (and Yourself):** Do not grab the pewter-colored stems for balance, and do not try to snap off a piece of the grey-green rosettes as a souvenir. Those plants took centuries of trace-vapor harvesting in a howling desert box to grow; snapping one is vandalizing a miracle of endurance. Practically speaking, they are heavily calcified and stiff as wire—grabbing one bare-handed or falling against one could easily puncture a glove or slice fabric.

### 6. Psychological Preparation
Do not underestimate how strange this room will feel after the first five minutes of awe wear off. 

Human brains rely on *change* to measure time and maintain calm: the flicker of a shadow, the lull in a breeze, the scurry of a bug, an echo of your own footsteps. Here, there is none of that. The light is locked in an eternal, shadowless afternoon. The roar never rises or falls by half a decibel. Your own voice disappears two inches from your mouth—you cannot even hum to yourself and hear it normally, though humming *will* create weird, competing beat-frequencies inside your skull against the stone’s vibration. Meanwhile, walls press against your chest and back wherever you stand.

*   **Claustrophobia Management:** If you struggle with tight spaces, do not go. Even if you don't, practice slow box-breathing before you arrive. When squeezing between two smooth-bellied piers with rock touching both your sternum and your spine, remind yourself of the room's greatest mercy: *utter mechanical peace*. Nothing here is going to shift. Nothing is going to collapse on you. This masonry finished settling before human history began. The walls are safer than steel.
*   **Set a Hard Timer (With Haptic Feedback):** Because the light never dims and the wind never shifts, time blindness will set in fast. Ten minutes can feel like two hours, or you might freeze up in sensory overload and lose track entirely. An audible watch alarm will be swallowed by the roar; wear a wristwatch with a strong *vibrating* alarm strapped tightly against your wrist bone (noticeably slower and more rhythmic than the high-frequency hum of the rock) to tell you when twenty or thirty minutes have passed. That is plenty of time for a human visit.

### How to Experience It Once You Are Inside
Once you arrive—sealed in slick nylon, eyes behind dark glacier glass, breathing moistened air through filters that carry just a faint, sharp ghost of crushed juniper and flint—stop moving. 

Wedge your hips securely into one of those smooth, parabolic pockets worn into a pier over five thousand years. Brace your gloved palms flat against the pale, soap-smooth limestone on either side of you. Let your eyes adjust to the silver-white geometry of the corbelled arches kissing overhead. Feel the colossal, steady chord of the salt-flat wind vibrating through the stone into your bones. Look closely at a tarnished-pewter stem bridging two rocks a hand’s breadth from your goggles, holding its millimeter of wind-shadow without trembling a fraction of an inch. 

You are standing inside an ancient, accidental stone organ pipe that has achieved perfection. Take it in, don't drop anything, keep your elbows tucked, and leave before the cold air drinks you dry.
