Before you step through into this world, understand what you are actually visiting: this is almost certainly not a bath meant for lounging, nor a natural pool, nor a ruin. Every clue—the upstream screens, the empty central sump, the slotted slate outflow, the overhanging cornice, and the sheer, balustrade-less utility stair—points to a newly commissioned **hydraulic settling tank** (*castellum* or header basin). Someone with sophisticated pre-industrial masonry skills just finished tapping a geothermal spring and built this chamber to calm the water, drop out any stray grit, and feed clean, hot flow downline to a bathhouse, villa, or temple complex. 

Visiting it feels less like lounging at a spa and more like standing over the open cylinder of a massive, sun-drenched stone engine. Because there is no soil, no shade, no sound-absorbing foliage, and not a millimeter of weathered softness anywhere, the environment will hit your nervous system hard in the first three seconds.

Here is how an ordinary traveler should prepare to visit safely—whether you only plan to peer over the edge, or you intend to descend those submerged eastern stairs.

---

### 1. Spatial Calibration (Don’t Step Into the Sky-Hole)
Because the coping stones are set completely flush with the surrounding ground and there is no parapet, wall, or railing, the basin looks from a distance like a bright white square painted onto the earth—right up until you are standing on the lip of a twenty-foot vertical drop. 
* **Pad your arrival coordinates:** If your travel method allows precision targeting, aim at least ten to fifteen feet back from the edge, ideally on the east or south side so you aren't immediately blasted by splash and steam from the northern conduit. 
* **Wear grippy, wet-stone footwear:** Even if you plan to stay dry, the coping stones vibrate constantly from the acoustic thunder of the spout, and windblown spray from the narrow air gap below can slick the smooth limestone. Wear approach shoes or deck shoes with soft, siped rubber soles—not leather soles, stiff hiking lugs, or flip-flops.

### 2. Shielding Your Eyes and Ears
The combination of noon sun, freshly quarried white limestone, pale mortar, and churning clear water turns this twenty-foot cube into a parabolic light trap. Looking straight into it with naked eyes is like staring into a welder’s arc reflected off snow.
* **Dark, polarized wrap-around sunglasses (Category 3 or 4):** Polarization is non-negotiable here. Without it, you will only see a violent sheet of surface glare and flashing white caustics; *with* it, the surface scatter cuts away, allowing you to actually read the mason’s scratches fifteen feet down, see the emerald ribbons of algae, and judge the narrow tread of the submerged stairs. Use a retaining strap so they cannot slip off your nose into the tank if you lean over the edge.
* **Full-coverage sun protection:** There is zero shade at noon, and UV light will bounce straight back up from the white stone and water to burn the underside of your chin, nostrils, and ears. Wear a wide-brimmed hat with a chin cord (the rising hot draft from the shaft will try to lift a loose hat) and a lightweight, breathable long-sleeved linen or UPF shirt.
* **High-attenuation earplugs:** Do not underestimate the acoustics of five flat stone walls and an overhanging cornice trapping a heavy waterfall. The low-frequency boom vibrates through your skeleton while the high-frequency hiss of bursting bubbles bounces endlessly in that two-handspan air gap. Within ten minutes, raw exposure will cause auditory fatigue, raise your heart rate, and make it hard to think clearly. Bring foam or silicone musician’s earplugs so you can observe the chamber in calm focus.

### 3. Testing the Water Before You Touch It
Looking at clear, sparkling turquoise-white water under a blazing sun triggers a deep human instinct to dive right in. **Do not jump in, and do not touch the water until you test two things.**

* **Bring an instant-read digital thermometer (on a string):** The description notes the water enters *well above skin temperature* straight from a thermal spring, supporting thermophilic algae. To the human eye, soothing 102°F (39°C) water, dangerous 110°F (43°C) water, and scalding 125°F (52°C) water look identical—all of them steam lightly and shimmer clearly. Lower a tethered thermometer down the eastern wall before putting a toe in. 
  * *Up to 104°F (40°C):* Safe for brief, careful immersion.
  * *105°F–108°F (40.5°C–42°C):* Extreme caution; wading or brief dips only, rapid heat-stroke risk.
  * *Above 109°F (43°C):* Look, don't enter. Pain and thermal injury begin very quickly here.
* **Pack pH test strips and smell the air:** Two chemical realities overlap in this pit. First, it is fed by raw geothermal water, which can carry dissolved hydrogen sulfide, carbon dioxide, or heavy minerals—take a cautious sniff up-wind at the rim to verify the steam is clean and breathable, not sharp with sulfur or pooling heavy gas under the cornice. Second, the entire cube was just pointed with **fresh lime-and-sand mortar**. Uncured or newly submerged lime leaches calcium hydroxide into hot water, raising the pH and making the water subtly caustic—enough to sting your eyes, strip the oils from your skin, and leave a slippery, soapy feel on your fingers. A quick dip of a pH strip tells you whether the incessant torrent has flushed the basin neutral (pH 7–8.5) or if the boundary layer near the walls is still alkaline.

### 4. Gear and Rules for Descending the Eastern Stair
If your thermometer and pH strip give you the green light and you decide to experience the underwater architecture firsthand, you have to dress and move for **unweathered geometry** and **heavy hydrodynamics**.

#### Protect Your Skin from "Green" Stone
Natural river rocks are tumbled smooth; ancient Roman baths are worn silky by centuries of feet. This tank has *neither*. Every block bears crisp iron tooth-chisel grooves, and the stair risers have raw, ninety-degree stone edges. Furthermore, soaking in hot water softens human skin within minutes, making calluses and knuckles tear like wet paper at the slightest brush.
* **Hard-soled neoprene dive booties or sturdy water shoes:** Never walk down those steep, narrow risers barefoot. One slip on a microscopic film of green protonema or slick lime will slice your heel or shin open.
* **Thin abrasion gloves and a full-length rashguard (not heavy neoprene):** Wear a thin Lycra/spandex dive skin—zero thermal insulation so you don't overheat, paired with full-body coverage so scraping an elbow or knee against the eastern wall while descending doesn't draw blood. Light sailing or dive gloves let you brace confidently against the rough chisel-grooves on the wall since there is no handrail.
* **Tight-sealing swim goggles (tinted or mirrored):** Even neutral thermal water under blinding underwater refraction is painful to open bare eyes in. Mirrored goggles let you sink below the surface glare and clearly inspect the crisp masonry joints, the empty central sump, and the oak-pegged slate grate in surreal, sunlit clarity.

#### Respect the Physics of the Tank
Once you step below the waterline, remember that ninety percent of this room is submerged and capped by an overhanging cornice:
1. **Stay completely out of the northern froth plume:** Beneath the terracotta conduit, millions of air bubbles are driven ten feet down. Aerated, frothy water has drastically reduced density—**humans cannot float in it**. Even a strong swimmer swept under that spout will lose buoyancy and be driven downward by the weight of the falling column. Admire the whipping emerald algae from the quiet eastern or western margins.
2. **Never swim close to the southern slate grate:** The outflow moves silently, and the undertow is described as slow, which is reassuring—until you remember how hydraulic head works in a twenty-foot column of water. Slotted grates at the bottom of deep tanks create localized pressure differentials if blocked by a body or loose clothing. Do not wear baggy shorts, dangling straps, or untied long hair, and stay at least six feet clear of the southern bottom corner.
3. **Mind the overhanging cornice:** Because the water rises to within a handspan or two of an overhanging stone lip, **you cannot climb out over the sides**, and if you let the current drift you away from the eastern stair into a turbulent corner, choppy waves slapping upward against the cornice can leave you gasping in hot, spray-choked pockets of echoing air. Keep one hand or foot in range of the eastern stair unless you are wearing a waist-tether anchored to a heavy weight back on the surface.

#### Guard Against the "Hot-Tank Blackout"
This is the quietest and most lethal danger for a solo traveler here. Immersing yourself neck-deep in nineteen feet of hot water under a blazing noon sun causes rapid vasodilation—your blood vessels open wide and your blood pressure drops. While you are underwater, hydrostatic pressure squeezes your legs and keeps blood flowing to your brain, so you feel completely fine, just deeply warm and heavy. 

The moment you climb up those steep, rail-less eastern stairs and emerge into the baking noon air, hydrostatic pressure vanishes, blood pools in your legs, and **orthostatic dizziness hits instantly**. Fainting near the top of a narrow, sharp-edged stone staircase suspended over nineteen feet of water is fatal.
* **Keep a strict 10-minute timer.** Do not linger until you feel woozy; get out while you still feel energetic.
* **Stage two liters of ice-cold, electrolyte-rich drinking water right near the top of the eastern stair** (tucked safely back so it can't fall in).
* **Ascend in stages:** Climb halfway up so your chest is out of the water, pause for sixty seconds bracing both gloved hands flat against the eastern wall, let your blood pressure equalize, and crawl—don't walk upright—over the top coping stone onto flat ground before standing up.

### 5. Etiquette, Contamination, and the Absent Builders
Finally, remember the human and ecological context of this strange, shining room:

* **Keep an eye on the horizon:** "The builders left recently." The mortar is fresh, the oak pegs in the slate are newly swollen, and the tools were just packed away. Masons, hydraulic engineers, slave crews, or custodial priests from the local civilization could walk up to inspect their handiwork at any moment. Keep your modern gear packed tightly in a single muted bag near your feet so you can leave cleanly without turning their engineering marvel into an incident.
* **Do not foul the water:** Because this is an active header tank with a spotless settling sump—likely feeding bathhouses, fountains, or drinking troughs downstream—treat it as a clean-room reservoir. Do not wear greasy sunscreen, bug spray, or deodorant into the water (which is another reason to wear a clean, pre-rinsed UV dive skin instead of lotions). Rinse the dirt off your water shoes using a canteen *away* from the rim before descending the stair so you don't track the very first mud into their immaculate central sump.
* **Spare the tiny pioneers:** On the shaded south wall, two palm-sized maidenhair ferns are clinging to an unmortared weep-hole, and a delicate dust of moss protonema is just beginning to tint the splash zone. Do not grab that weep-hole for handholds or kick heavy wakes against it. In a blinding, roaring cube of sterile white stone, those tiny trembling greens are the very first sparks of life figuring out how to survive here—leave them undisturbed to claim the walls over the centuries to come.
