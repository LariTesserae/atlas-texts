To visit this high-altitude, roaring inland sea as an ordinary human traveler, you must abandon every instinct and piece of equipment designed for terrestrial oceans. You are not going for a dive; you are stepping into a cryogenic, hyper-acoustic wind tunnel filled with dense liquid glass, perched at the altitude of a mountain peak, floored with billions of surgical scalpels. 

If you translate directly into this water in standard scuba gear, you will die in less than two minutes—not from one single hazard, but from four or five competing physics problems tearing at your biology simultaneously. 

Here is how you must prepare your gear, your body, and your mind to survive a transit through the Singing Caldera.

### 1. The Vessel vs. The Suit
First, make a foundational choice: **do not enter this water in a soft suit if you have the option of a rigid, single-person atmospheric submersible.** 

A twelve-knot current is roughly twenty kilometers per hour (nearly twenty-one feet per second). Olympic swimmers peak at roughly four knots for a few seconds. Twelve knots of hypersaline water—which is vastly denser and heavier than regular seawater—is an unstoppable, solid wall of kinetic force. In a flexible suit, you cannot swim against it, you cannot turn perpendicular to it without having your limbs wrenched, and if you try to grab onto a stationary object to stop yourself, the water will hammer you against it like a ragdoll in a hurricane.

However, if your transit constraints or weight limits force you to go in *personally*—in an exosuit or heavy diving rig—your preparation must be absolute.

### 2. Thermal and Chemical Armor
Because the water refuses to freeze despite sub-zero temperatures, you are looking at a brine chilled well below 0°C (likely between -5°C and -15°C, depending on the exact salt composition). At this temperature, exposed human skin freezes almost instantly, and normal neoprene or rubber turns brittle and shatters like ceramic.

*   **Active-Loop Hardsuit:** You need a rigid or semi-rigid atmospheric diving suit (ADS) constructed from composites and titanium alloys impervious to hypersaline corrosion (though low oxygen limits rust, salt crystallization in joints will seize standard mechanisms). 
*   **Redundant Heating:** Passive insulation will fail quickly in fast-moving cold water due to convective heat stripping—the twelve-knot current will wick heat away from your hull furiously. You need dual-redundant, closed-loop liquid heating garments worn underneath the suit, powered by high-density solid-state batteries. Pack chemical heat packs against your core as a last-ditch backup if your electronics fail.
*   **Cut-Proofing the Exterior:** The seafloor is fresh, unweathered obsidian and basalt. Volcanic glass fractures down to a molecular edge—literally sharper than surgical steel. If you drop down into the lower boundary layer where the current slows, one brush against a ridge in a soft drysuit will slice your suit cleanly open, flooding it with sub-zero brine. Any flexible joint on your suit must be armored in articulated titanium scales or ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene (UHMWPE) weave. 
*   **Ballast Calculations:** Because the water is hypersaline and frigid, its buoyancy is extreme. You will float like a cork unless heavily over-weighted. Do not guess your ballast; bring a variable-buoyancy system capable of pumping dense hydraulic fluid into external bladders, and carry emergency drop-weights made of solid lead or tungsten.

### 3. Surviving the Sound
The description notes a "bone-shaking roar... a solid wall of acoustic pressure." This is the deadliest hidden killer in the basin. Sound travels over four times faster in water than in air, and much faster still in dense, cold brine. Worse, because the human body is mostly water, underwater sound waves do not bounce off your skin—they pass right through your tissues, vibrating your organs, lungs, and skull directly.

*   **Internal Organ Shielding:** Prolonged exposure to extreme low-frequency underwater acoustics causes pulmonary hemorrhage (your lungs tear from vibrating against the air pocket inside them), gastrointestinal bleeding, and severe vestibular concussion (vertigo so violent you vomit inside your helmet and drown). You cannot wear simple earplugs; the sound enters through your ribcage and jawbone.
*   **Acoustic Metamaterial Cladding:** Your suit or pod must be wrapped in an outer decoupling layer—an acoustic metamaterial or syntactic foam baffled with vacuum-microspheres designed specifically to reflect and scatter low-to-mid-frequency pressure waves before they reach the rigid hull. 
*   **Helmet Isolation:** Inside the suit, your head should not touch the outer shell. Wear a fluid-damped, suspended inner helmet, and keep a thick silicone bite-block clamped between your molars. The prompt explicitly mentions the sound "vibrates in the teeth"—without a bite-block, micro-vibrations over several hours can crack tooth enamel, rattle fillings loose, and induce blinding migraines.
*   **Active Noise Cancellation (ANC):** Once you are physically decoupled from the water, use bone-conduction ANC tuned specifically to a constant, unchanging drone. Because the roar has "no rhythm, no gusts, no lulls," phase-inverting electronics can actually cancel a massive percentage of the residual hum inside your helmet, giving you back the ability to think clearly.

### 4. Navigating a Twelve-Knot Sheet Flow
You will not fight the current. You must plan a **drift dive**. 

*   **Insertion and Extraction:** Set your multiverse entry coordinates dozens of miles *upstream* (east) of whatever you want to inspect, and plan your extraction coordinates downstream (west). Accept from second one that you are a projectile fired down a barrel.
*   **Hydrodynamic Trim:** Strip everything snag-prone off your exterior. No dangling gauges, no loose hoses, no boxy camera housings. You want to look like one of the native white swimmers: a smooth, teardrop fuselage. 
*   **Tethering to the Relics (Extreme Caution):** If you intend to stop at one of the iron turbine-anchors, *do not swim up and grab it*. Hitting a stationary iron post at twelve knots is equivalent to sprinting full-speed into a brick wall. Furthermore, do not touch the braided steel cables—they are shedding cavitation bubbles. Cavitation collapse generates microscopic shockwaves and localized temperatures of thousands of degrees; touching a vibrating, cavitating cable will shred your gloves and pit your suit's metal. 
*   **Harpoon Braking:** To stop at a station, approach from directly upstream, using hydrodynamic fins to steer carefully into its acoustic/kinetic wake (be mindful that the twenty-foot predators also camp in these wakes). Deploy a long, elasticized dynamic-rope tether tipped with a magnetic clamp or hook to snag an iron rung ahead of you, letting a mechanical spool pay out line with progressive friction brakes to bleed off your twelve-knot momentum gently over fifty yards before reeling yourself into the leeward eddy behind the concrete silo.

### 5. Managing the Void and the Optics
Psychologically, this environment will trigger acute spatial disorientation and agoraphobia. In normal water, murk gives you a sense of scale—things fade out twenty or a hundred feet away, enclosing you in a cozy visual bubble. Here, there is no scatter.

*   **Laser Rangefinders & HUD:** Without particulate matter in the water, human binocular vision fails completely at judging distance beyond thirty feet. A basalt spire might look like a tiny jagged rock ten yards below you when it is actually a thousand-foot mountain two miles down. A twenty-foot predator might look like a finger-sized minnow until it is right on top of you. You *must* have a Heads-Up Display fed by a blue-green lidar/laser rangefinder (do not use standard sonar—see below) constantly printing hard numbers onto your visor: *Depth to Surface, Altitude above Floor, Distance to Target, Drift Velocity.*
*   **Polarized Visors:** The light falling from the high-altitude sky is clinical, harsh, and un-scattered. Looking up at the flat, moving ceiling of the water from below will create blinding total internal reflection—like staring at a sheet of polished mercury. Bring adjustable polarized tinting on your faceplate.
*   **Psychological Anchoring:** "Hollowing out thought" is a real phenomenon in sensory-overload/visual-deprivation environments (known to polar explorers and deep-sea divers). Between the relentless white-noise roar and miles of sterile geometric emptiness, dissociation sets in fast. Bring a familiar, rhythmic audio track—music with a changing beat, or spoken-word recordings—playing softly inside your helmet to give your brain a sense of passing time. Set a haptic watch to tap your wrist every ten minutes so you don't lose track of reality.

### 6. Ecological Etiquette (Do Not Go Blindly Loud)
The native fauna tell you everything you need to know about how not to get eaten or crushed.

*   **Zero Active Sonar:** The twenty-foot white swimmers have no eyes; they hunt entirely by reading *acoustic shadows*—places where objects block or warp the ambient roar of the basalt ridges. If you turn on an active sonar pinger or noisy thruster, you will light yourself up like a flare in a dark room, or worse, mimic the high-frequency chatter of spawning shell-plates. Rely strictly on passive optical cameras and low-wattage laser rangefinding. Since the water is perfectly clear and sunlit for hundreds of feet, optics outperform acoustics anyway.
*   **Mind the Acoustic Shadows:** Realize that the moment you drift between a vibrating basalt spire and one of those twenty-foot fuselages, *you cast a shadow across its sensory organs*. They know your exact volume, density, and shape instantly by how your suit muffles the background roar behind you. Fortunately, they are filter/ambush feeders waiting for drifting clouds of brittle, piezoelectric shell-plates; a solitary, six-foot, rounded lump of silent composite material drifting past won't register as a spawning cloud, *provided you do not hang in front of their open mouths*. Keep your eyes peeled upstream and downstream; you can see them long before you enter their immediate strike zone. Use small control surfaces (fins, not noisy propellers) to glide wide around the cables where they congregate.
*   **Deflecting the Spawning Clouds:** If a miles-long cloud of the dinner-plate-sized shell creatures washes through your lane, curl inward and protect your visor and seals. At twelve knots, if you are anchored to a turbine station and a cloud of corrugated, hard-shelled plates hits you, it is like standing in front of a pitching machine throwing heavy ceramic dishes at twenty miles per hour. Plus, where the cloud goes, the twenty-foot mouths open blindly. If you see that glittering cloud of "broken windshields" upstream, unhook from whatever station you are holding onto immediately and drift *with* them—when you match the current's speed, your relative velocity to the plates drops to zero, and they will simply float gently alongside you like weightless glass leaves.

### 7. Surface and Post-Dive Survival
Do not forget the very first detail of this world: **the surface is perched two miles above the planetary baseline, and the water is oxygen-poor.**

*   **Hypoxia at the Surface:** Two miles up is roughly 10,500 feet (3,200 meters) of altitude—comparable to Cusco or high alpine passes, assuming a 1-bar planetary baseline. Worse, because the water below is explicitly "oxygen-poor" and devoid of photosynthetic life (no algae, no kelp, no phytoplankton), the boundary layer of air skimming right above this forty-mile-wide sheet of frigid brine may be severely depleted of oxygen, or heavy with volcanic gases (sulfur dioxide, carbon dioxide) bleeding subtly from the geologically recent caldera. **Never crack your helmet upon breaching the surface** until an external chemical sniffer verifies partial pressures of $O_2$, $CO_2$, and $H_2S$. Plan to breathe your own closed-circuit rebreather gas from entry to exit.
*   **Decompression:** Diving in a high-altitude lake changes decompression tables drastically because the atmospheric pressure at the surface is much lower than at sea level; dissolved nitrogen comes out of your blood much faster upon surfacing, causing rapid, violent decompression sickness ("the bends"). This is one more overwhelming reason to wear a 1-atmosphere rigid suit rather than breathing pressurized gas. If you *do* breathe ambient-pressure gas, you must use custom high-altitude heliox tables and carry hours of decompression gas while drifting dozens of miles downstream at shallow depth.
*   **Getting Out:** There is no beach. The caldera walls are "too far away to ever be seen," and the surface moves at twelve knots without waves—meaning it looks deceivingly calm, like moving glass, until something breaks the tension. You cannot climb out onto a turbine tower unless its maintenance cap breaches the surface half a mile up (unlikely, as they are bolted to the deep ridges). Ensure your dimensional recall beacon, portal tether, or extraction craft can lock onto a target moving horizontally at twenty kilometers per hour, and trigger your jump while suspended cleanly in mid-water, away from cables, rocks, and drifting wildlife.

### Summary Checklist for the Traveler
1.  **1-Atmosphere Hardsuit or Micro-Sub** (composite/titanium, obsidian-proofed joints, smooth hydrodynamic shell).
2.  **Acoustic Metamaterial Baffling + Bone-Conduction ANC + Silicone Bite-Block.**
3.  **Dual-Redundant Active Liquid Heating** (rated for -15°C convective brine stripping).
4.  **Lidar/Optical HUD** (no sonar; distance-readouts to defeat crystal-clear depth illusions).
5.  **Progressive-Brake Magnetic Tether** (only if planning to dock in the lee of an iron silo; stay clear of cavitating cables).
6.  **Self-Contained Rebreather Supply** (ignore local air; assume high-altitude hypoxia and volcanic trace gases).
7.  **Moving-Target Extraction Lock.**

Prepared properly, the terror of the place gives way to something monumental. Safely cocooned behind acoustic foam, warm water looping across your chest, a quiet piano concerto playing softly through your skull to defeat the madness of the drone, you will drift weightlessly through sunlit liquid space. Far below, the pristine black glass of a newborn world will slide past like dark starlight; amber maintenance lamps built by vanished engineers will glow softly in the cold daylight; and giant, blind, ivory leviathans will hang suspended around humming steel threads as you glide silently past them on an endless river of salt.
