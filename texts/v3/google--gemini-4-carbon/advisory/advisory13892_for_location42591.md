If you are going down there as an ordinary human—no augmented lungs, no sub-dermal heating, just baseline flesh and bone—you need to treat this less like a tropical reef dive and more like a spacewalk on a dark, cold moon. Seventy meters of water at four degrees Celsius does not care about curiosity. It will pull the heat out of your marrow in minutes, crush the air in your lungs to eight times surface density, and swallow your sense of direction within thirty seconds of leaving your arrival point.

Here is how you prepare to step onto those drowned cobblestones, and how you make sure you come back.

### 1. Managing the Portal and the Crush (Barotrauma & Breathing)
First, understand your insertion method. If your multiverse transit drops you directly onto the flagstones at seventy meters, **do not arrive at standard surface pressure.** Going instantaneously from 1 atmosphere (surface) to 8 atmospheres (70m depth) will violently rupture your eardrums, crush your sinuses, and collapse your lungs before you can draw a breath from a regulator. 

*   **Option A (Surface Insertion):** Open your transit point seventy meters up, in the grey winter chop. Bring an inflatable tender or heavy surface buoy to anchor your exit portal, drop a weighted descent line with strobe beacons, and descend slowly, equalizing your ears every meter. Note that if you dive from the surface, a single hour on the bottom will require *hours* of freezing, motionless decompression stops in dark, empty water on the way back up.
*   **Option B (Pressurized Saturation Insertion - Recommended):** Bring a portable hyperbaric staging lock into your home base before you jump. Spend a day slowly compressing yourself to 8 atmospheres in dry warmth, breathing a Heliox mixture. Then step directly through the portal onto the seabed. Your body is already equalized to the immense weight of that inland sea. When you are ready to leave, step right back into your pressurized lock at home and decompress safely in a warm bed with a book.

**Your Breathing Gas:** Do not breathe standard compressed air at seventy meters. At 8 atmospheres, the nitrogen in regular air becomes violently narcotic—you will feel drunk, paranoid, or foolishly euphoric in a place that demands quiet focus. Worse, the oxygen in regular air becomes toxic at this depth, risking fatal underwater convulsions without warning. 
*   You must use **Trimix** (Oxygen, Helium, Nitrogen) or **Heliox** (Helium and Oxygen), with the oxygen dialed down to around 16–18%.
*   **Use a Closed-Circuit Rebreather (CCR), not open-circuit tanks.** This is crucial for two reasons. First, helium strips heat from your lungs with every breath; a rebreather recycles your gas through a chemical scrubber, which generates warmth via an exothermic reaction—meaning you breathe warm, humidified gas instead of freezing dry gas. Second, open-circuit scuba blasts noisy, violent clouds of exhaust bubbles every time you exhale. In a world defined by geological hush and near-subaudible hums, roaring exhaust bubbles will ruin the entire experience, scatter decades of undisturbed peat-silt from overhead lintels, and shatter the quiet you came to witness.

### 2. Defeating the Four-Degree Drain (Thermal Protection)
Water at 4°C (39°F) is at its absolute maximum physical density. It feels noticeably heavier and thicker than tropical water, and it conducts heat away from the human body twenty-five times faster than air. Without active heating, loss of finger dexterity happens in ten minutes; shivering and cognitive fog follow shortly after; hypothermia kills you soon after that.

*   **The Suit:** Wear a heavy-duty **crushed-neoprene or trilaminate drysuit**. Do *not* inflate your drysuit with your breathing gas if it contains helium—helium is a terrible insulator and will freeze you inside your suit. Carry a small, dedicated pony bottle of **Argon gas** clipped to your waist solely to inflate your drysuit. Argon is dense and holds body heat wonderfully.
*   **Active Heating:** Underneath the drysuit, wear a thick thermal base layer wired with an **electric heating vest, heated insoles, and heated glove liners**, powered by an external canister battery clipped to your harness. 
*   **Face and Hands:** Use dry-gloves locked onto wrist rings—wet neoprene gloves will leave your hands uselessly numb within twenty minutes. Wear a **full-face mask** or an integrated dive helmet. Having 4°C water pressed against your bare forehead and lips causes an intense "ice-cream headache" pain that fades into a dangerous facial numbness, making it easy to accidentally drop a standard mouthpiece without realizing it.

### 3. Navigating a Continent Without Landmarks
Read the terrain description carefully: *hundreds of kilometers of low-relief stonework, no spires, no canyons, a uniform grid muffled in a thirty-meter kelp jungle, in twenty hours of absolute black.* 

This is an entanglement and disorientation nightmare. Every courtyard looks like the courtyard four blocks east. Every sagged granite lintel and bulging retaining wall looks identical in a flashlight beam. And every four hours, the entire forest leans ten degrees in a new direction, subtly altering the geometry of the alleys.

*   **Zero Snag Profile:** Kelp stipes as thick as human wrists and ten-meter leathery ribbons hanging through doorways will catch on anything sticking off your body. Route all hoses tightly under elastic bands along your torso. Do not wear dangling gauges, loose clips, or long fins. Use short, stiff, wide technical fins (like rubber jet-fins) meant for frog-kicking. 
*   **Cutting Tools:** You are not cutting fishing nets here; you are dealing with cartilaginous laminarians tough as cured leather. A tiny line-cutter won't do if you get wrapped up in a dense thicket while a slow current shifts. Carry heavy-duty **titanium trauma shears** strapped to your forearm and a stout, serrated dive knife on your chest harness—though your goal should be to part the fronds gently with your hands and never cut anything unless trapped.
*   **Finding Your Way Back:** Magnetic compasses may be useless here—this isn't Earth, there may be no magnetic pole, or the granite blocks beneath you might be rich in magnetite and spin your needle. 
    *   Drop an **acoustic homing transponder** right at your arrival portal/descent line, paired with a wrist-mounted receiver that gives you exact distance and bearing through the stone walls and kelp.
    *   Carry a **primary cave-diving reel** with at least 300 meters of braided white nylon line, plus two backup safety spools. Tie off physically at your entry point and lay continuous line as you walk or glide down the avenues. Wrap it gently around a wrist-thick holdfast or hook it over a rounded window jamb whenever you turn a corner. Your physical line is your absolute truth in the dark.
    *   Attach a low-intensity green tritium glow-marker or a slow-pulsing LED strobe high up in the thirty-meter kelp canopy above your portal, tethered like a balloon so you can spot it if you swim *over* the forest rather than through the streets.

### 4. Lighting the Gloom
For twenty hours a day, it is pitch black. For four hours around midday, it is the color of a faint grey bruise. 

*   **Bring two kinds of light.** First, a tight-beam primary canister light (around 2,000–4,000 lumens) for cutting through the murky water column to check the canopy overhead or signal down long causeways. Second—and much more pleasant for actually experiencing this place—mount **two wide-angle, warm-tinted video floodlights** on low power to your helmet or wrists. 
*   **Mind the Color Spectrum:** At seventy meters, even at noon, natural red and orange light do not exist. To the naked eye in the dim ambient gloom, the burgundy sea-oak and deep-red dulse will look pitch black or muddy slate-grey. When you switch on a warm-spectrum floodlight (around 3500K–4500K), the dead dark stone will suddenly burst into startling, quiet color: deep arterial reds in the thickets, muted chalky pinks and lavenders of the coralline crusts cementing the cobbles, and rich amber-brown in the towering kelp blades. 
*   Keep a dimmer switch handy. Turn your lights *completely off* at midday, let your eyes adjust for fifteen minutes in the stillness, and watch the thirty-meter forest sway beneath that bruised, distant ceiling of light.

### 5. Movement and Etiquette in the Silt
Look at what covers the floors of the roofless rooms: *decades of translucent, decaying kelp blades and a fine, velvet layer of dark peat-silt.* Because there are no crabs or burrowing worms to churn the bottom, this silt has settled undisturbed for centuries. It has zero weight.

*   **Do NOT walk on the bottom.** It is deeply tempting, given the cobbled streets, worn staircases, and thresholds, to weight yourself heavily and walk through the town like a deep-sea diver in brass boots. Don't do it. One heavy footstep in a courtyard will kick up a billowing black cloud of bacterial peat-silt that will hang motionless in the weak current for hours, blinding you completely and coating the delicate thumb-sized anemones under the stone benches.
*   **Master Neutral Buoyancy and the Frog-Kick:** Trim your weights so you hover motionless fifty centimeters above the ankle-deep moss and black wrack. Keep your knees bent ninety degrees so your fins stay angled *up* toward the water column, never pointing down at the streets. Use slow, deliberate frog-kicks, or better yet, pull yourself gently along with two fingers on bare, rounded granite lintels (checking first for pale anemones or slow-rasping limpets).
*   **Move at the Speed of the Forest:** The prompt gives you the golden rule: *easy to move through if one moves slowly.* If you thrash or swim hard, the heavy water and dense curtains of rubbery kelp will fight you, exhaust you, build up dangerous carbon dioxide in your bloodstream, and tangle your gear. Swim as if you are moving in slow motion. Part the wet curtains across an ancient doorway with open palms, glide through, pause in the center of the sunken room, and wait.

### 6. Psychological Preparation: The Weight of the Quiet
Do not underestimate what this environment will do to an ordinary human mind. 

We are evolved for worlds with motion and animal company. Even in Earth's deepest caves or barren deserts, our brains expect *something*—a spider, a darting minnow, the snap of a shrimp. Going down into an environment built entirely by human-like hands—seeing recognizable paddocks, granaries, worn doorsteps, chisel marks in granite—while simultaneously realizing **nothing with blood or eyes lives here anymore**, creates a profound, eerie cognitive dissonance. 

Combine that ghost-town intimacy with seventy meters of black water overhead, cold pressing in on your ribs, and a silence broken only by the deep continental hum in your chest cavity and the occasional *shhh-thump* of rubbery weeds sliding over rounded stone... and panic can creep in very quietly. It won't feel like adrenaline at first; it will feel like an overwhelming heaviness, an sudden urge to bolt upward toward the grey sky.

*   **Do not go alone if you can help it.** Bring one dive partner, tethered to you with a three-meter buddy line in thick kelp, equipped with wireless ultrasonic comms built into your full-face masks. Hearing another human voice—even just a quiet breathing check every ten minutes—anchors your sanity.
*   **If you must go solo, bring an internal anchor.** Load bone-conduction headphones inside your dry-hood—not to blast music over the sea, which defeats the purpose, but perhaps with a quiet, recorded voice note, or a subtle, warm chime every fifteen minutes to mark the passage of human time against geological time.
*   **Respect the Slate:** Though the walls have settled into permanent equilibrium, the prompt notes that every great once in a while, a flat slate shingle slips from an eaves-course with a dull *clack*. Wear a light bump-helmet (standard for cave divers) over your neoprene hood to mount your lights and protect your skull if your rebreather hose brushes an overhanging lintel and dislodges a tile that has been waiting a thousand years to fall.
