Go as a mountaineer, move as a field naturalist, and never mistake the water for architecture.

For an ordinary human, the most plausible injuries are mundane: altitude illness, a cut from volcanic glass, a slip on a wet slab, or severe chilling after returning to the wind. The anomaly’s main danger is subtler—it encourages you to trust surfaces, routes, and enclosures that are not physically there.

## Before the approach

Make it a **daylight visit in stable weather**, preferably with at least one companion and with someone elsewhere who knows your route and expected return. Use navigation and emergency communications that actually work in that world; do not assume familiar satellites, phones, or rescue services will.

Acclimatize to the altitude before attempting the scree. The warm, dense air inside does **not** mean the altitude has gone away. Brief ear tightness at the boundary is not evidence that the oxygen or physiological strain has changed. Headache, nausea, unusual breathlessness, confusion, or unsteady walking are reasons to turn around, not reasons to rest beside a hot fissure and wait for improvement.

Do not schedule the visit around seeing a complete condensation cycle. It follows cloud and moisture, not an appointment book. Clear weather may show only a vertical pool edge or a few climbing drops. Cloud may reveal more, but you should never remain on an exposed volcanic shoulder through deteriorating visibility, wind, lightning, or cold merely to see the installation become complete.

Leave enough time that you will never be tempted to hurry back across wet glass or scree. There is no shelter, settlement, food supply, or reliable campsite in the basin.

## What to carry

Dress for two climates and carry the means to survive the transition between them.

- **Footwear:** stiff, supportive boots with soles you trust on scree and wet rock. No sandals, smooth soles, or barefoot pool exploration. Volcanic glass can cut through casual clothing, and clear water conceals both footing and depth.
- **Clothing:** non-cotton, quick-drying layers; lightweight long trousers are useful protection against scrapes. For the approach, carry windproof outerwear, insulation, gloves, and a warm hat as conditions require.
- **For the exit:** a dry base layer, dry socks, and a warm hat sealed in a waterproof bag, plus a small towel. This is important. You may enter from cold wind, become warm and wet inside, and then step back into conditions capable of chilling you rapidly.
- **Sun protection:** sunglasses, sunscreen, and a hat. The basin has no real canopy, and the description’s “ordinary daylight” can still be hard, high-altitude daylight.
- **Water and food:** carry all drinking water for the approach, the wait, and the return. None of the basin’s water should be considered potable, including clear condensation and water climbing through empty air.
- **Pack protection:** a pack liner or dry bags, waterproof protection for electronics, and a lens cloth. Cold glasses and camera equipment may fog when they enter the warm humidity; keep a camera bag closed while its contents adjust if practical.
- **Mountain essentials:** a headlamp, emergency shelter appropriate to the approach, basic navigation, and first aid emphasizing cuts, bleeding, sprains, and blister care. A pressure dressing is more relevant here than exotic antidotes. Trekking poles may help on the scree.
- **Approach-specific protection:** if the route passes beneath loose faces or requires technical climbing, use qualified guidance and appropriate equipment. A helmet does not make lingering beneath unstable rock acceptable.

Arrive with clean boots and equipment so you do not seed this very young ecosystem with organisms from another mountain—or another world.

## Crossing the boundary

The boundary may be impossible to see, so fix your entry location using external landmarks and your route notes rather than expecting to find a visible line later.

When the wind stops and the heat closes in, **stop walking for a moment**. Let your ears adjust gently, allow fogged glasses to clear, and check your footing before proceeding. The smells of warm minerals and wet leaves, the sudden silence, and the pressure sensation can make the transition more disorienting than its lack of visual drama suggests.

Remove excess insulation before you sweat through it, but keep your dry exit clothing protected. If anyone develops more than brief, mild ear discomfort—especially faintness, chest symptoms, or breathing difficulty—go back out and reassess rather than treating the boundary as something to push through.

Keep the party together. Do not let one person wander ahead while others are still adjusting to the change in light, sound, and temperature.

## Moving inside

The basin’s small scale is deceptive. It may be only a few hundred paces across, but nearly every surface is young, wet, unstable, sharp, or ecologically fragile.

Stay well back from the open rim. There is no parapet, and clouds far below do not provide a useful sense of where the edge becomes unsafe. A viewpoint is not worth approaching across loose blocks or wet, tilted stone. Do not anchor ropes to the bench, cable, artifacts, or vegetation.

Walk slowly and avoid jumping across pools or channels. Do not put your hands into cracks to steady yourself. Avoid lingering beneath broken faces, and do not climb the fault-steps merely because they look like stairs. Rock ticking may simply be thermal expansion, but fresh falling debris or shifting blocks are reasons to withdraw.

Where possible, use stable, bare rock without trampling the green islands. The mosses, ferns, rosettes, mud, and thin sediment fans are not decorative ground cover; they are part of what the basin is actively becoming. A few careless footsteps can destroy a root pocket or redirect a rill that has only just begun to form.

Treat the artifacts as evidence, not equipment:

- The bench is not trail furniture.
- The steps and threshold are not a route to somewhere.
- The cable is not a handle or anchor.
- The ceramic squares are not known controls.

Do not pry them up, pull the cable, clear away sediment, or rearrange them to see whether the system responds. Moving them reportedly changes nothing, and digging for machinery would only damage the site and destabilize the ground.

## How to treat the held water

The essential rule is simple: **the water is real; the implied structure is not.**

A vertical pool edge is not a wall. A suspended runnel is not a handrail. An overhead lattice of droplets is not a roof. The absent installation governs water behavior, but nothing described suggests that it will support your weight, stop a fall, shelter you, or provide a floor where the ghost of one appears.

You do not need to touch it to verify this. Avoid putting your hand through a rising ribbon, drinking from a vertical stream, blocking a channel with your boot, or placing equipment in its path. Clear water may be hot, mineral-laden, biologically active, or simply deeper than it appears. Near fissures, assume heat until you have good reason to think otherwise; never test a pool with bare skin.

Do not swim, wash, use soap, or rinse muddy gear in the basin. Avoid groping through warm sediment, both to protect the pools and because at least one unobtrusive hunter lives there. The translucent grazers’ apparent survival of the water cycle is not a safety demonstration for humans.

Standing within the ghost installation’s outline is less concerning than behaving as though you are enclosed. Choose stable ground, remain aware of the real drop and fissures, and expect to get wet.

## When the cycle completes

Tell everyone about the click beforehand. It may sound close to the ear regardless of where you stand, and a startled step is undesirable beside glass, loose blocks, or the rim.

When it comes, **stand still rather than running for shelter**. The release is described as a dense, brief rain, not a flood. Protect vulnerable optics, keep your footing, and let the suspended surfaces vanish. Afterward, expect darker and slicker rock, softened sediment, bowed vegetation, and slightly extended rills. Do not immediately hurry toward the exit or lean over a pool to see where the water went.

The profound quiet can make the process seem inactive when it is not. It can also make your own footsteps and voices feel disproportionately loud. There is no need to fill that silence.

## Leaving

Before facing the exterior wind, dry yourself as practical and change into protected dry clothing if you are wet or sweat-soaked. Have your wind layer ready at the boundary. Pack wet items separately, check that everyone is together, and leave enough daylight for the scree.

Take out everything you brought, including food scraps and other organic waste. Carry no pool water, plants, animals, artifacts, or interesting pieces of glass as souvenirs. Follow whatever cleaning, drying, or quarantine procedure applies before taking muddy equipment into another catchment or universe.

Finally, prepare for the possibility that the visit will seem visually uneventful. You may see little more than wet black rock, a few ferns, and one bead swelling where no corner exists. That is not a failed visit. The Held Rain does not need to be activated, repaired, or persuaded to perform.

The best preparation is therefore not a weapon, ritual, or ingenious device. It is mountain judgment, dry clothing for the cold exit, enough patience to watch water carefully, and enough restraint to let the place continue building itself without your help.
