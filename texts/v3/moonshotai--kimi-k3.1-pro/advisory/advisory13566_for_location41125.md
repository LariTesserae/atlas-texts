# Preparing for the Unfinished Engine

The first preparation is to reconsider what “visiting” means. This is not a hot cave with difficult footing. It is a tiny, pressurized, lightless interstice whose remaining space is occupied by sharp mineral growth and living animals. There may be no human-sized void inside at all.

**An ordinary traveler without specialized engineering support should limit the visit to the mountain’s exterior and remote observation. Bodily entry should not be attempted with improvised caving, firefighting, diving, or mountaineering equipment.**

## Before traveling: obtain measurements, not just coordinates

The description does not provide the numbers needed to calculate a safe exposure. Before considering entry, you would need remote measurements of:

- Gas temperature, pressure, composition, and heat transfer
- Wall and crystal temperatures
- Noise and vibration levels, including the pressure-note felt through the body
- The frequency and energy of electrical discharges
- The size and arrangement of any actual open spaces
- The stability of the glass skins and constricted gas passages

Until those are known, no one can responsibly specify a suit, a safe duration, or even whether a survivable suit is possible. The steadiness of the roar is not evidence of safety.

Prepare for the exterior separately. The summit’s thin air may require acclimatization or supplemental oxygen. The approach across clinker and volcanic glass calls for abrasion-resistant clothing, sturdy boots, gloves, eye protection, and careful route-finding. Do not assume the absence of snow means comfortable temperatures. Carry your own drinking water and ordinary altitude and first-aid supplies, but keep them sealed and outside the chamber system.

Hydrate normally before any suited work. Do not dehydrate yourself out of respect for the waterless environment; that would only make you more vulnerable to heat illness.

## Access: do not make an entrance

There is no door to find. The slightly warmer seam is a clue, not an invitation.

Do not drill, blast, hammer, or pry toward the sound or warmth. Besides exposing you to hot gas and sharp fragments, opening the pocket could:

- Vent or suddenly redistribute its pressure
- Change the flow through its natural constrictions
- Admit humid outside air
- Cool or chemically alter reactive surfaces
- Destroy the crowded communities on those surfaces
- Cause rock or glass to fail around the new opening

Nor should you open an ordinary two-way portal directly between the chamber and a habitable room. Your air contains water vapor, and the pressure difference could produce a damaging jet in either direction.

Set arrival coordinates on the exterior, never inside the pocket. A body materializing into an already crowded cavity could be injured by displacement alone, while also rupturing the chamber. If your transit method provides a **closed, pressure-isolated observation interface**, that is the preferable way to see the interior. Otherwise, use properly rated remote instruments—or accept that the interior is not physically visitable.

An instant-return ability is useful, but it does not replace pressure isolation, cooling, or breathing protection.

## If bodily entry is ever justified, the suit must do several jobs at once

A normal respirator and insulated coveralls would be wholly inadequate. The required system would have to be engineered for the measured conditions and include:

**1. Complete respiratory and chemical isolation**  
Use self-contained breathing gas, carbon-dioxide removal, and contained exhaust. Assume the chamber gas is unbreathable and potentially chemically aggressive until proven otherwise. Do not vent oxygen, exhaled humidity, or suit gases into the pocket.

**2. Pressure protection appropriate to external overpressure**  
A suit designed for vacuum is not automatically suitable for a pressurized chamber. Transit between the thin exterior air and the chamber must not require casually opening valves or “equalizing” with the native gas.

**3. Real heat management**  
Insulation alone merely delays overheating. The suit needs sufficient cooling capacity and a heat sink or other means of handling your metabolic heat plus heat entering from the environment. All coolant must remain sealed. Evaporative cooling, damp garments, and water-mist systems are unsuitable.

**4. Cut, puncture, and abrasion resistance**  
The walls are intergrown silica blades, sulfide crystals, needles, and glass. A glove brushed against a surface cannot be assumed safe. A puncture could expose you simultaneously to heat, pressure, and gas.

**5. Acoustic protection and conservative exposure limits**  
Earplugs alone are insufficient. Use a helmet and suit designed to reduce airborne sound and structure-borne vibration, with additional hearing protection if compatible. The bass pressure-note may still be felt in your bones. Do not plan to communicate by shouting.

**6. Electrical and instrumental safeguards**  
Use shielded, nonsparking equipment with no exposed conductors or improvised wiring. Do not assume a trailing grounding strap would help; it might simply provide another conductive path. Monitor suit temperature, pressure, breathing supply, cooling status, and elapsed time on an internal display.

A second person should remain outside with medical support and control of the extraction or return system. Sending a second tourist into the chamber would only increase crowding and risk.

## Seeing, hearing, and moving inside

Ordinary human senses will perform badly there.

Image-intensifying night vision would have almost nothing to amplify. Thermal imaging might reveal temperature gradients, but it would not show safe footing. If observation requires visible images, use a dim, steady, shielded light suitable for the equipment involved. Do not use photographic flashes, and do not rely on the natural electrical sparks: they are brief, unpredictable, and turn every edge into violent white contrast before leaving you blind again.

The darkness does not mean emptiness. During a flash, much of what looked like wall may prove to be animals packed several bodies deep.

Do not plan to “cross the chamber in nine paces.” There may be no place to take even one pace without crushing animals, breaking crystals, or puncturing your suit. Never clear a shelf for yourself, set down a pack, brace a hand against the wall, or sit on the floor. If entry requires making room, entry itself is destructive.

The native animals are not the principal hazard. There is no reason to expect a coordinated attack, and weapons would be pointless against the actual dangers. However, much of their sensory world consists of vibrations transmitted through mineral. Avoid tapping, knocking, scraping, or experimenting with rhythms to see whether they respond. Do not touch a wall to “feel the engine,” and do not imitate their shell-drumming.

Likewise, do not treat the industrial-looking features as controls. A hissing throat is not a valve awaiting adjustment; a constriction is not a blockage to clear; a flexing glass skin is not a panel to inspect. Plugging, widening, or sampling the wrong feature could redirect pressure and flow.

## Absolute rules about contamination

The most important thing you carry is not equipment but **water**: humidity in your breath, moisture in clothing, sweat, damp cleaning residues, and water in supplies.

Keep all of it contained. Do not introduce water to clean a lens, cool a surface, revive an animal, or test what happens. Even if no dramatic reaction occurred, you would still be altering an environment whose chemistry and biology are utterly waterless.

Additional rules are straightforward:

- No food, drink, or open containers inside
- No wet wipes or damp decontamination residues on equipment
- No feeding, handling, or collecting animals
- No souvenir crystals, shells, dust, or “just one” gas sample without a justified sampling plan
- No markers, plaques, abandoned sensors, or permanent tethers
- No attempt to preserve the chamber by removing new mineral growth

The chamber’s eventual natural filling does not make present damage harmless.

## Time, attention, and abort criteria

Bring several independent timers. There is no daylight, weather, or changing scenery to mark duration, and the overwhelming sound may distort your sense of time. Suit consumption, heat load, and acoustic exposure—not curiosity—should determine the schedule.

Do not interpret the chamber’s geological patience as leisure for you. Crystals may thicken imperceptibly while a human overheats rapidly.

Any suited entry should end immediately upon:

- Cooling failure or an unexpected rise in suit or skin temperature
- Breathing-supply, pressure-control, or communications problems
- A suspected puncture, visor injury, or seal leak
- Confusion, faintness, severe headache, chest symptoms, or disorientation
- An unexpected pressure concussion or significant change in flow or sound
- Loss of a reliable return path

Do not remove a helmet to breathe or cool down. Do not try to escape by digging toward the warmer seam. Do not remain for one more image after an alarm. A suit breach in this environment is not a situation in which improvised first aid is likely to save you.

## Returning

Remain sealed until you are in an appropriate exterior containment area. Suit surfaces may carry hot, sharp sulfide grit, reactive films, native organisms, or sulfur-bearing oils and waxes. Do not beat the dust off, rinse everything into soil or drains, or bring contaminated clothing home for later cleaning.

Afterward, check for heat illness, hearing injury, barotrauma, respiratory irritation, and unnoticed cuts or suit damage. Treat unexplained symptoms or a possible exposure seriously rather than assuming that tiny native organisms must be harmless.

## What the experience would actually be like

Even a successful observation would offer little of the satisfaction of exploring a cave. You would not look across a room. You would confront darkness, heat beyond a barrier, and a roar so close and broad that it would convey no sense of distance. Sound would press rather than echo. Your instruments would matter more than your eyes.

Then a discharge might illuminate the pocket for an instant: white crystal edges, crowded black shells, no bare floor, no comfortable direction in which to move. When darkness returned, the chamber would continue exactly as before—patiently precipitating mineral and sustaining life in spaces you could not occupy.

The best preparation is therefore to go **dry, sealed, quiet, instrumented, and ready to leave—or, preferably, to remain bodily outside**. A successful visit is not one in which you touched the engine. It is one in which you learned something without adding a drop of water, opening a passage, silencing a throat, or crushing the patient bodies between the stones.
