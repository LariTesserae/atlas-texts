# Preparing for the Underbloom

Prepare for the Underbloom as a **hot, bright, three-dimensional maze whose floor and walls are alive**—not as a garden, rainforest, or ruined settlement.

Its lushness is misleading. The vegetation offers no dependable food, little accessible water, no cool shade, and no easy route. The stillness is also misleading: it does not mean that you will stop aging, become immune to heat, or leave your supplies unchanged. Nothing described suggests an actively hostile place. The principal dangers are ordinary ones—dehydration, heat illness, cuts, sprains, disorientation, and contamination—made serious by the absence of trails, time cues, and help.

## Before entering

- **Make the first visit short and easily reversible.** Do not begin with a crossing of all three basins. Observe from the lip, then make a brief descent near your known entrance. Save the lower galleries, mineral ribs, and remote side valleys for later expeditions.
- **Do not go alone.** A small party is preferable: large enough to assist an injured member, small enough not to trample a broad swath of living tissue. At least one person should be experienced with enclosed terrain and travel in heat.
- **Leave an external return plan.** Give someone outside your route, expected duration, and overdue time. Test communications and your means of leaving before you need them. The open roof seam is a landscape feature, not necessarily an accessible exit.
- **Set a turnaround time in advance.** There will be no dusk, cooling air, or changing shadow to warn you that you have stayed too long. If your return transit depends on a schedule, arrive back early.
- **Be realistic about heat.** Acclimatize beforehand if possible, and do not go while ill, sleep-deprived, or already dehydrated. Without measured temperature and humidity, no honest universal time limit or water allowance can be given.
- **Do not infer breathable air from greenery.** For extended visits or entry into poorly ventilated lower galleries, test oxygen and accumulated gases if you have the means. Clear air is not proof of human-safe air, and an ordinary filter mask does not remedy oxygen deficiency.

Arrive with equipment cleaned of soil, seeds, and organic material from other worlds. The Underbloom is a closed ecological system; “just a few crumbs” or “just a little ordinary dirt” are not trivial introductions.

## What to carry

Use a close-fitting pack without dangling straps. Braided stems, low leaves, and living thresholds will catch on anything loose.

- **All the water you expect to need, plus a sealed emergency reserve.** Plan for the return journey as well as the outward one. Increase the allowance for heat and exertion rather than using an ordinary hiking rule of thumb.
- **Electrolytes and ready-to-eat food**, with additional emergency supplies. Do not plan to cook, forage, hunt, or fish.
- **Light, loose, protective clothing:** breathable long sleeves and trousers rather than heavy cave gear or a waterproof rainforest outfit. You need protection from abrasion and sharp mineral edges without adding unnecessary heat.
- **Protective footwear with secure ankle support and soles suited to both springy fiber and smooth stone.** Damp root mat and glassy mineral seams may require very different foot placements. Sandals are a poor choice.
- **Thin protective gloves, eye protection, and a helmet.** The helmet is mainly for low vaults and raw mineral ribs, not because frequent rockfall is described.
- **Glare protection appropriate to the light.** There is no visible sun, but the central whites can become nearly featureless. Make sure dark lenses do not then conceal wet seams or root thresholds in the galleries.
- **Two independent light sources**, even though much of the Underbloom is illuminated. A backup light is cheap insurance against equipment failure or an unexpectedly obstructed return.
- **Navigation and timekeeping that do not depend on satellites or a visible sun:** waterproof paper, pencil, route sketches, distance-and-time notes, and at least two timers or watches. Verify that a compass behaves usefully before trusting it.
- **A compact first-aid kit** emphasizing cuts, eye injuries, blisters, sprains, and heat illness, plus personal medication stored within its safe temperature range.
- **A signaling device and agreed separation procedure.** Voices are swallowed close to their source, so do not assume shouting will locate a companion.
- **Sealable waste bags**, and a proper pack-out toilet system for any visit long enough to require one.

A machete should not be standard equipment. If a route requires you to cut through the vegetation, it is not a suitable visitor route.

## Heat and water: the main survival problem

Carry water as though the lenses, cupped leaves, and root pockets do not exist.

Clear water in a living filtration system is not automatically safe for a human. It may contain unfamiliar microorganisms, plant compounds, or dissolved mineral material. A filter and disinfectant can address some biological hazards; they do not guarantee chemical safety. If an expedition intends to depend on local water, that water needs prior sampling and analysis. Otherwise, treat it as an uncertain last resort.

Do not cut stems for sap, drain sealed pockets, or dismantle cupped leaves. The visible greenery is not evidence of abundant water; it is the machinery that keeps a scarce supply circulating. Taking from it may be both unrewarding and damaging.

Likewise, **do not swim or wade to cool down**. The water is approximately as warm as everything else, and the lens bottoms are woven living root rather than mud or stone. The three steps ending at a waterline are not proof of safe access.

Resting beneath a leaf may reduce glare, but it will not provide a cool refuge. Build your schedule around that fact. Drink and eat according to a sensible plan, monitor companions, and turn back before reserves become critical. Headache, nausea, dizziness, confusion, loss of coordination, or collapse should not be treated as something to push through; serious heat illness requires stopping exertion, cooling as best you can, and getting help or evacuating.

## Navigation: assume the ground will forget you

From the lip, the Underbloom looks regionally open. At body height, it is congested and visually repetitive. Distances judged from above may translate into long periods of turning, ducking, backtracking, and stepping over living thresholds.

Do not expect to retrace:

- footprints in the root mat;
- crushed vegetation;
- a developing trail;
- moving shadows;
- the position of the sun;
- flowing water leading downhill or outward.

There is no connecting stream, and reflected light can make the brightest direction misleading. The made-looking structures do not form a road or settlement and should not be expected to lead anywhere.

Keep a written route log, including changes in elevation and passages taken. Photograph junctions looking both forward and backward; a root arch can look entirely different on return. If non-damaging, retrievable markers are necessary, use them sparingly and remove them afterward. Until you know how foreign objects behave there, do not make markers your only means of finding the exit.

Stay close enough for regular visual contact. The mat absorbs footsteps, chambers suppress voices, and a companion can become difficult to hear without being very far away.

Move deliberately. Dry root felt may flex underfoot; damp fiber may let a foot sink; thin, stationary shadows can conceal the exact height of a threshold. Avoid cliff lips, unfinished arches, and mineral ribs as climbing routes. Their freshness and long stability do not make them handholds designed for you. If surface detail disappears into brilliant white, stop rather than assuming the ground ahead is level.

## Treat the whole landscape as living and irreplaceable

The difficulty of telling one plant from another should encourage restraint. A column, root, wall, and crown may be parts of the same continuous tissue. The fact that compressed roots recover does not establish that repeated damage is harmless.

Do not cut, burn, taste, or unnecessarily handle the vegetation. Avoid sap contact with eyes and wounds, and clean any cuts promptly; the absence of decay smells does not mean the environment is sterile. Do not capture the transparent water creature or collect the pale arthropods. There is no described reason to expect swarms or dangerous megafauna, so water, first aid, and navigation deserve more weight in your pack than weapons or speculative animal defenses.

Take out everything you bring, including food residue, sanitary waste, and used water. Do not bury anything in the mat, wash with soap in a lens, or leave “biodegradable” offerings. Do not take home cuttings, water, animals, or apparently detached fragments.

## The made-looking objects are not visitor facilities

The steps, roofless enclosure, straight sill, and empty rectangular basin should be treated as unidentified features of possible archaeological or structural importance.

They are not demonstrated to be:

- a safe entrance or exit;
- shelter;
- a bath or drinking source;
- a campsite;
- abandoned property;
- an invitation to complete a design.

Photograph and map them without scraping, climbing, rearranging, or using them for storage. In particular, do not turn the root-free boundaries into an experiment. The roots’ refusal to cross those outlines is unexplained; it is not evidence of either safety or danger, and there is no need for a casual visitor to test it.

Resist the temptation to “help” the Underbloom become something else: opening a bud, filling the basin, breaking through a root curtain, or completing an unfinished arch. Its incomplete appearance is not a task assigned to you.

## Bring your own clock and your own ending

The psychological difficulty may be the absence of progression. Your breathing and clothing will seem loud. The light will not soften, the buds will remain on the point of opening, and even a rest may leave no clear sense of how much time has passed. Some travelers may find this peaceful; others may find it intensely claustrophobic or oppressive.

Use scheduled checks for water, food, fatigue, and party location. If you find yourself staying longer because a bud seems about to open or a structure seems about to make sense, check your timer. Every bud looks that way.

Do not assume that the Underbloom’s stability applies to your body, batteries, medication, food, wounds, or memory. Overnight travel is especially unattractive: there is no cool darkness, no soil for ordinary camp construction, and constant light will require an eye mask and a deliberate sleep schedule. An emergency bivouac is one thing; a casual camping holiday is another.

A sensible first visitor returns with photographs, a careful route sketch, an intact emergency reserve, and a better understanding of their own reaction to the heat and light. The Underbloom will not wear a path behind you or provide a natural cue to leave. **Keep your own time, preserve your own way out, and do not mistake its stillness for a promise that you will be preserved with it.**
