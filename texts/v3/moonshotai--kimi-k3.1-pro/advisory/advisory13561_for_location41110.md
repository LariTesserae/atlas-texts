The Bright Cistern is visitable by an ordinary human, but it should not be treated as a casual side trip or an unusually picturesque winter cirque. Its dangers are mostly quiet ones: damaged eyes, cold injuries, a fall on stairs that looked dry an hour ago, and well-meant interference with a water system on which forty households depend.

The three essentials are **protect your eyes, remain responsible for your own warmth, and enter with a resident guide**. Everything else follows from those.

## Arrange the visit before reaching the cleft

A first-time visitor should not wander in alone. The basin may be small, but the settlement is vertically complicated, and many apparent streets pass through houses, sleeping rooms, animal pens, and communal waterworks. A route that was safe in the morning may be glazed or dripping by afternoon. A local guide is not merely a convenience; they are how you distinguish a threshold from a catchment lip, a public stair from someone’s roof, and a drinking seep from a bitter pool.

Arrange in advance:

- Where you will sleep and store your pack.
- Which water you may drink and who will show it to you.
- Where to go for sanitation and washing.
- Where to wait during a turning if you become separated.
- What payment, supplies, or labor would actually be useful, rather than arriving with gifts the settlement cannot store or use.
- How long you may stay without becoming a burden on food, shade, and fuel.

Travel with at least one companion if possible. Treat the approach as a winter mountain journey in its own right: acclimatize appropriately if the route is high, allow for snow and bad weather, and do not schedule a tight same-day arrival and departure. The cleft is both the entrance and the overflow passage. Do not camp in it, leave equipment in it, or assume it will remain convenient simply because it was passable on arrival.

Have an exit plan for eye injury, altitude illness, or hypothermia. The Cistern’s single narrow access and crowded stairs would make an evacuation slow. Do not make the town’s scarce fuel your emergency heating plan.

For a first visit, a short hosted stay is better than an ambitious tour. Expect to lose much of the first day to glare, caution, and simple disorientation.

## Pack for brightness without warmth

The most dangerous packing mistake would be to interpret the white light as sunlight in the ordinary sense. You can be painfully dazzled while your fingers are going numb.

### Eye protection is primary safety equipment

Ordinary sunglasses are unlikely to be enough. Light returns from snow, salt, wet mica, water, ice, and walls, so it arrives from the sides and below as well as from above.

Bring:

- A well-fitted pair of local bone slit goggles, obtained or arranged beforehand if possible.
- A backup pair of high-quality, closely fitting glacier goggles with strong visible-light reduction, ultraviolet protection, and effective side shielding.
- A hood or head covering that works with the goggles without directing your breath upward into them.
- Dark anti-glare paste for beneath the eyes, preferably the local preparation applied with local advice. Do not grind up an unidentified mineral and put it near your eyes.
- Spare corrective eyewear if you need it. Contact lenses may be miserable in cold, dry, glaring conditions.

Practice walking on steps while wearing your eye protection. Slit goggles restrict the visual field; a deep hood restricts it further. You will need to turn your head deliberately and avoid relying on peripheral vision.

Put the goggles on **before emerging from the cleft**, not after the light has already left you weeping. If you develop persistent eye pain, a gritty sensation, blurred vision, or severe light sensitivity, stop the visit and get into darkness with help. Do not try to finish the day by walking with your eyes nearly closed and one hand on the wall.

### Dress for still, intense cold

Use several adjustable layers: wool or suitable synthetic next to the skin, substantial insulation, and a loose outer layer that sheds rime. Local grey felt and oily hide may perform better than a tight, crinkly modern shell, but do not count on borrowing them unless that has been arranged.

Your clothing should be matte and quiet where possible. Shiny fittings add to the glare; loud fabric and dangling metal are conspicuous in a place where a hoof-tick can be clearly heard.

Carry:

- Warm mittens, with thin liners for brief tasks, plus a dry spare set.
- An insulated hat and a hood that does not leave your neck exposed.
- A loose face or neck covering, with a spare if the first becomes rimed by breath.
- Warm socks and at least one sealed, completely dry change.
- Insulated, water-resistant boots suitable for uneven stairs and cold, wet gravel.

Do not work barehanded except briefly. Avoid touching your own cold metal fittings, bottles, or spectacle frames with bare skin.

The light may tempt you to remove layers because you *feel* as though you ought to be hot. Judge your clothing by your fingers, toes, shivering, and fatigue—not by the brilliance of the walls.

### Choose footwear and traction conservatively

The floor contains polished limestone, smooth tufa, wet gravel, black underwater biofilm, salt crust, rime, and ice that can disappear without leaving ordinary meltwater. “Dry” describes the present location of the water, not the safety of the surface.

Use boots with secure, well-edged soles, but do not automatically wear crampons or aggressive cleats inside the town. They can damage polished steps, catch on narrow stairs, and scar runnels or tufa. Use approach traction outside as the route requires; within the Cistern, follow your guide.

Keep your hands free. A long walking pole is often more nuisance than help in roof lanes and house-passages. If you need a staff, use a short one with a padded tip and ask where it may be used.

## Store water and equipment as though the weather can move through stone

Bring enough water for the approach, then use only the drinking supply identified by your hosts. The lower pools are not made safe by being clear or remaining liquid. Their salts may make them undrinkable, and their below-freezing temperature makes casual contact dangerous. Do not taste pools to identify them.

The iron taste of a fresh seep is not, by itself, a reason to reject a source your hosts use; conversely, sparkling clarity is not proof that another source is safe.

Your own drinking containers should be:

- Capped and leakproof.
- Opaque or kept inside a dark bag.
- Insulated against freezing where necessary.
- Small enough to handle with mittens.
- Closed again immediately after drinking.

Do not leave an open bottle beside you while you eat or take sketches. Water left exposed to strong light may slowly lift away. At the same time, do not assume that moving everything into shade solves storage: cold shaded stone may gather moisture, drip, or begin to rain from an overhang.

Keep spare clothing, sleeping gear, medicine, and electronics in sealed inner bags, and store the pack where your host directs—not against a convenient cold wall or beneath a dry-looking ledge. Do not rely on the Cistern’s light to dry clothing, or on a shaded stair to keep it dry.

A compact, soft-sided pack is preferable to a large expedition pack. It should pass through the cleft and narrow lanes without forcing you to turn sideways blindly. Remove or secure loose straps, pad metal objects so they do not clack, and repack food out of noisy disposable wrappers.

Bring your own cold-rated sleeping bag and insulated sleeping surface unless your host has explicitly provided equivalents. Rooms may be warmed chiefly by bodies, animals, and old enclosed stone. Bring food that remains edible when cold and requires little or no cooking. Never use a stove inside a felt- and hide-insulated room, and do not expect a private fire.

## Treat nearly every surface as either a home or infrastructure

The sophistication of the Cistern lies in fit and accumulated knowledge. A visitor who sees only stone tools and small rooms may badly underestimate how much damage an ignorant action can cause.

Unless your guide explicitly invites it:

- Do not open a cistern lid.
- Do not move a stone gate, even if water appears to be wasted.
- Do not wring, lift, or rearrange a hanging felt.
- Do not cover a pool, seep, or dark crack to make a convenient seat or platform.
- Do not set your pack in a shallow pan or on a scalloped stone lip.
- Do not walk across a roof. It may be a bridge, pen, catchment platform, or the ceiling of a sleeping room.
- Do not clear away salt, rime, or ice merely to improve your footing.
- Do not experiment with mirrors, extra lights, containers, or channel coverings to test the anomaly.

Shared shade is valuable. Do not sprawl in a dark recess needed for water storage or occupy a working crack with your equipment. Likewise, accept that fuel is communal and precious; a cold room is not necessarily evidence of poor hospitality.

Move quietly, but do not mistake quietness for absence of activity. Learn to recognize the principal hand signals before entering. A downward-turned palm concerns covering cisterns; two fingers brushed across the goggles warn of a mist-turning. As a visitor, recognize such signals rather than casually reproducing them—an inaccurate signal could start urgent work in several households.

Speak softly, avoid bells and music, and ask before photographing people or interiors. Avoid flash and broad, sudden beams of light. A small shielded lamp may be useful after dark, but use it with your host’s approval and never illuminate roosts or work areas merely for spectacle.

## Walk as if the route were new each time

On stairs, use the same worn grips and footholds as your guide. Take three points of support where exposure warrants it. Do not hurry to keep up if glare has flattened your depth perception; ask the guide to slow down.

Do not test apparently dry gravel by pressing your heel into it. That is a good way to bring water around your boot and a poor way to find a safe path. Do not step into clear pools: their dark submerged film is living pasture, and it can be exceptionally slick. Pale trails left by grazing animals closing behind them are not invitations to make trails of your own.

After any rapid change in light or moisture, reassess the route. A threshold may now sweat, a stair may carry invisible glaze, and an overhang that was dry may be dripping. A place being “nearly silent” does not mean it is changing slowly.

## Learn the turning procedure before you need it

A turning is not an occasion for panic, but neither is it a spectacle to stand and record. Residents respond **without panic but without pause**, and a visitor should do the same.

Warning signs may include eels settling, midges rising together, extreme brilliance on the upper walls, water visibly leaving the floor, mist columns between houses, rime racing along ropes, or slatewings pouring from their ledges. Remember the local rule: **when the midges rise, look first to the animals.**

Before your first excursion, agree on exactly what you will do. Usually this should amount to:

1. Cap and secure your own containers and loose equipment.
2. Stay with your guide or move promptly toward the prearranged refuge.
3. Keep clear of roof edges, exposed stairs, animal-moving routes, and places where ceiling-rain may begin.
4. Do not operate gates, lids, or felts unless a resident has assigned you a specific, simple task.
5. Do not run. A child sprinting over a roof may know precisely which gate to close and which three tasks will follow; you do not.

The rising midges are not smoke. The slatewings’ eruption is not necessarily an attack. Their flashing white underwings and passing shadows can be startling, but sudden dodging on a narrow stair is more dangerous than the birds.

Afterward, keep out of the repair work unless invited to help. The upper town may drip onto the lower, ice grains may fall, pools may refill elsewhere, and the previous route may no longer be safe. Repairs can begin before the last water has fallen.

## Respect the ecology and the settlement’s limited surplus

Do not feed the goats, shrews, martens, or birds. Store food securely so that shrews and martens are not drawn into sleeping rooms or walls. Do not harvest eels, disturb roosts, or step into the underwater film. If a turning strands eels in a shallow pan, ask before attempting a rescue; the residents may already be dealing with it, and an untrained intervention may damage a channel or another pan.

Use only the sanitation arrangements your host specifies. There is no harmless, out-of-the-way crack in which to urinate, and no pool suitable for soap. Do not wash dishes, clothing, or your body in seeps and pans. Pack out foreign trash and food scraps. As a traveler between worlds, arrive with clean boots and equipment, without soil, seeds, plants, or animals that could be introduced into this unusually concentrated ecology.

The inhabitants’ quietness should not be read as unfriendliness, and their technology should not be read as simplicity. Their waterworks embody generations of observation that you cannot replace with a day’s intuition. The most useful help is often to carry something when asked, stay out of the way when not asked, and avoid consuming heat and water thoughtlessly.

## What the first day will probably feel like

Even with preparation, expect the entrance to be physically disorienting. The sound of the approach will fall away. Your breathing will seem loud inside your hood. The walls may appear brighter than the sky, and shallow, doubled shadows will make steps difficult to judge. Mist will seem to boil without a roar. You may see people working on several levels while hearing little more than a padded tool-click or frost snapping on an awning.

Stop somewhere your guide considers safe. Drink, eat, check your fingers and toes, and let your eyes settle. Spend much of the first day in short, guided excursions from shade rather than attempting to see everything. A headache, rising irritability, clumsiness, or an urge to rush are reasons to rest, not signs that you should push through.

On later excursions, remain conservative. The Cistern rewards attention to small things: where people look before a turning, which grips their hands find without searching, which doorway has become a cistern, and which silences mean work rather than sleep.

At the outlet, ordinary running water and sharp footsteps will return. The lower mountains may feel offensively loud after the hush, but normal sound does not mean the journey is safe; you are still leaving through cold, exposed terrain. Check your equipment conventionally rather than assuming that water lost inside the Cistern has behaved like ordinary drying.

A successful visit is not one in which you master the anomaly. It is one in which you leave with uninjured eyes, warm extremities, and no household having to reroute water, spare emergency fuel, or repair a path because of you. The right manner is quiet, warm, observant, and slightly humble: you are being allowed to stand inside both a home and a weather system.
