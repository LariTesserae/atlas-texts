# Preparing for Vael

Treat Vael less as a city than as an **archipelago of inhabited, machine-stabilized districts** scattered across an unfinished mountain. An ordinary human can visit, but the sensible visit is hosted, slow, and geographically modest. Choose one or two districts rather than trying to “see the whole city.”

The dangers are mostly quiet. The rock will probably feel perfectly solid. Space will probably not shimmer or announce that it has changed. You are more likely to get into trouble through altitude, wet footing, an empty district with no one to ask, a stale map, or an overconfident return journey than through anything spectacular.

## Before arrival

### Arrange a host, not merely accommodation

Have someone meet you at a named pressure door, depot, or engine house. Confirm in advance:

- where you will sleep;
- how washing and drinking-water allocations work;
- which interdistrict routes are currently in use;
- whether any recent geological report has left bridges, stairs, or cable bearings awaiting inspection;
- when the next common engine rhythm is expected;
- whom to contact if you fail to arrive on schedule.

Do not expect to land in an arcade and find a crowd, a taxi, or an available passerby. An empty street in Vael is not necessarily abandoned, but it may be very far from help.

For lodging, a room near a long-running district engine is generally a virtue, not a drawback. Bring ear protection for sleeping, and never suggest shutting down an “idle” engine for quiet. Its power output may be the least important thing it does.

### Be conservative about altitude

Vael’s air is thin, its height cannot be cleanly stated, and breath remains visible outdoors. Ask your hosts what newly arrived humans normally require: staged ascent, rest days, medical screening, or supplemental oxygen. Do not infer a safe acclimatization plan from a map’s elevation.

For the first day or two, avoid hard exertion, alcohol, and unnecessary stair-climbing. Headache, nausea, unusual breathlessness, clumsiness, confusion, or difficulty walking straight should initially be treated as possible altitude illness—not as evidence that space is misbehaving. Seek local medical help promptly. In particular, do not respond to confusion by simply “heading downhill” on an unfamiliar staircase.

If your multiversal transit method requires coordinates, do not key arrival or departure to “the summit,” an absolute altitude, or one survey pillar. Use a currently occupied municipal site, and confirm your return arrangements while you are still safely inside it. If your only way home depends on retracing an unmaintained route, you are not adequately prepared.

## What to carry

**For cold, damp, and poor light**

- Layered wool or synthetic clothing, an insulating layer, and a windproof, water-shedding outer shell.
- Gloves, a warm hat, and several pairs of dry socks. Expect handrails to stain unprotected palms with rust.
- Boots with ankle support and soles chosen for **wet iron as well as wet rock**. Smooth city footwear is unsuitable.
- Two independent lights, at least one head-mounted, with spare batteries. You may need a lamp at noon.
- Clear, fog-resistant eye protection. The dimness does not eliminate the need to ask about high-altitude sun exposure on open gantries, where light may also reflect upward from the cloud sea.

**For an industrial mountain**

- A properly fitted helmet for quarries, roofed streets, work areas, and interdistrict excursions.
- Hearing protection for ore stamps, compressors, and the common engine rhythm.
- A small first-aid kit emphasizing cuts, abrasions, blisters, and personal medications.
- Respiratory protection only with competent local advice. A dust mask does not make sulfur-bearing foundry air safe, and no filter protects against oxygen deficiency.

**For navigation and delay**

- A waterproof paper map showing the year in which it last agreed with the ground, plus district and survey-epoch information.
- A notebook and pencil for door numbers, landings, route changes, and check-in times.
- A watch, so you need not infer the hour from Vael’s permanently dusk-like sky.
- Emergency food, an appropriate water supply, and additional warmth or shelter on any interdistrict journey.
- A written itinerary left with your host, including an overdue time.

Electronic positioning, altimeters, rangefinders, and downloaded maps may remain useful, but none should be your sole authority. Cable length, travel distance, and apparent elevation are not interchangeable in Vael.

## Learn the local disciplines immediately

The children’s rules are an excellent visitor’s foundation:

**Close the pressure door.** Never prop one open as a convenient breadcrumb for your return. Record its number instead.

**Do not waste cistern water.** Vael looks soaked and is nevertheless water-poor.

**Do not descend a staircase that has more landings than its building.** If you cannot establish that a stair is correct, use another route. Curiosity is not a reason to continue downward.

Beyond the maintained network, the rule is **a chain and a companion**. For an ordinary visitor, the best implementation is usually not to go there. If travel beyond the last anchor is essential, hire people who know the local chain procedure and have actually walked the route. Do not assume that carrying a climbing rope, a GPS unit, or a metronome is equivalent. Even Vael’s residents do not know whether vibration, measurement, or collective expectation does the important work.

Treat survey markers, rails, pipelines, articulated masts, and engine foundations as parts of one safety system. Do not move markers, scavenge “disused” hardware, paint over old numbers, or cut across machinery for a shortcut.

## Moving between districts

A line connecting two districts on a map is not necessarily a pleasant urban walk. It may represent two kilometres of exposed rock, a stairway, or a cable disappearing into dim air. Prefer staffed, authorized transport and maintained routes. An unattended cable station is not automatically a help point, and an ore installation is not a passenger service.

Budget by reserves, not by the posted distance alone. A route can lengthen while its endpoints remain fixed, and the return journey need not cost what the outward journey cost. Turn around while you still have enough water, warmth, and time for a route **longer than the one you expected**.

Keep your companion close. Thin air, wind, and industrial noise can make voices useless at surprisingly short range. Agree on visual signals and rendezvous points before you need them.

Animal trails deserve respect as recent information, but not blind obedience. If a trail disagrees with the map, the trail may indeed be the newer document. It may also have been made by a split-hooved grazer capable of using a ledge that would kill you. Treat it as evidence to consult a local, not as an invitation to follow.

## If the route begins to disagree with itself

Possible warnings include an implausible number of landings, a journey that keeps lengthening, incompatible distance signs, a repeated cliff, a loop returning at another elevation, or a shadow remaining after its object is gone.

Do not split up to test the phenomenon. Do not send one person ahead, run for the nearest door, or assume that retracing your steps will automatically reverse the discrepancy.

Stop adding unnecessary distance. Move only as required to reach a safe, maintained position, keeping your companion and any continuous reference with you. Check your last confirmed door, anchor, and survey epoch. Use the local emergency or chain procedure you established beforehand, and wait for assistance if waiting is safer than experimenting. Report the discrepancy afterward; do not “correct” signs or markers yourself.

Most of these changes accumulate slowly. A persistent shadow is not necessarily an immediate attack, and a repeated cliff is not something you need to fight. They are reasons to stop trusting improvisation.

## Rock, weather, and machinery

Vael’s stillness can be misleading. The mountain is not necessarily shaking every day, but its stone is young, sharp, and unfinished. Scree has not rounded; fractures can end blindly far below; courtesy “valleys” may contain neither a route out nor water.

Stay off open scree and uninspected construction. Do not enter a cleft looking for a stream. Rain often vanishes directly into fissures, and there is no summit river to follow home.

When cloud passes through a district, expect wet, slow dimness followed by slippery rails, sweating pipes, and dripping condenser fins. Wait in designated shelter rather than navigating exposed ground from memory. Roofed streets exist for a reason.

Learn which industrial sounds are normal and which require action. Noise may travel through rock farther than a voice travels through air, so loudness is not a reliable measure of distance. Vael can sound like an enormous factory immediately out of sight when its sources are widely separated.

After a deep subterranean report or other significant movement, assume that stairs, foundations, bridges, and cable bearings may require inspection. Do not cross merely because they still look intact.

## Water, gardens, and animals

The visual abundance of water is one of Vael’s easiest traps. Condensation on a pipe is not necessarily clean, available, or yours. Still black pools and covered cisterns are habitat and infrastructure, not public fountains. A purifier may address biological contamination without making industrial or mineral contamination safe.

Arrange legitimate refills, carry your allocation carefully, and expect washing to be procedural and rationed rather than hotel-like. Do not put soap into pools, clean equipment at intakes, or strip moss and growth from condenser surfaces.

Likewise, green does not mean soft, stable, or edible. Moss can conceal sharp, slick rock; hanging gardens may surround hot exhaust ducts; roots can occupy fractures. Do not forage without expert advice.

Most animals are part of the city’s ordinary occupancy rather than hazards to be confronted. Even so:

- Do not reach into cisterns or disturb pale salamanders and pipe intakes.
- Keep clear of condenser arrays when gliders are preparing to lift off at shift changes.
- Yield calmly to grazers on stairs and ledges rather than startling them toward an edge.
- Secure food and waste, and give the cat-sized predators distance without assuming either aggression or tameness.
- Do not open generator houses merely to look at the moths.

At dusk, clicking feet, harsh rookery calls, and brief eyeshine near cable anchors may simply mean that you are finally noticing the district’s majority population.

## What visiting will feel like

At noon, you may still be walking by lamplight. Cliffs can appear illuminated from underneath while the street remains dark. A warm wall may be lush with ferns a few steps from bare, blue-black fracture. You may cross long arcades with no crowd, then realize that nests, cropped vegetation, and moving water below a cistern surface show intense occupation.

The emptiness, detached machinery noise, and permanent dusk can distort your sense of time and scale. Keep regular meals and sleep, use your written schedule, and do not respond to loneliness by wandering toward an interesting sound.

Be safely inside your district before the common civic rhythm begins. It is an excellent time to reconcile your map, confirm your position, and review your departure plan—but not a guarantee that an outer quarry has become safe. The surveyors’ claim that every map is then correct is the best available civic assurance, not something anyone has managed to verify completely.

Observe from an authorized place, protect your hearing, and stay clear of working machinery. District after district will answer across the empty kilometres. Gliders will settle; grazers will lift their heads; the beat will come through your boot soles as much as through the air.

The visitor who does well in Vael borrows some of the city’s own discipline: close the same doors, keep the same check-ins, record the route, respect the engines, conserve the water, and turn back early. Then the strange parts can be enjoyed without needing to conquer them—the underlit cliffs, the green heat-veins, the animal traffic at dusk, and the brief sensation that a scattered city has gathered itself around a single measure.
