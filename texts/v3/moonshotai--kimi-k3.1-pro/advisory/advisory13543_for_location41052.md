# Preparing for a Visit to the Root-Ward

Treat the Root-Ward less as a cave than as occupied, living infrastructure: a cold, wet settlement whose walls are growing, whose drainage is alive, and whose residents know considerably more about surviving there than you do. An ordinary human can reasonably make a short visit, but not as an independent exploration. The essential preparations are warmth, restraint, reliable guidance, and the ability to remain calm in darkness among things that crawl.

## Arrange the visit before approaching the bog

Do not try to find the entrance from the description alone. Above ground there is only a rain-swollen black tussock, water in the hollows, and perhaps vapor at the plug. Probing tussocks, cutting stems, or searching for a hollow with a pole could damage the entrance or the root itself. The surrounding bog also offers no reason to assume firm footing.

Arrive with someone who knows the safe surface approach, and enter only with a resident escort. Keep the visiting party very small—ideally one traveler, or two at most—so that everyone can remain within reach of the guide and no one has to wait unsupervised in a side passage.

Before entry, settle several practical matters:

- How long the visit will last, and who decides when it must end.
- Whether artificial light is permitted, and where it may be used.
- Which signals mean stop, become silent, move, and prepare to leave. They must work in darkness; hand signals alone are insufficient.
- What you should do during a pressure pulse.
- Where you may put your feet, your hands, and any small pack you carry.
- Whether you may use the common cistern or midden-lock. Do not assume either is available to visitors.
- What happens if you are delayed, injured, or separated from the guide.

A first visit should be short and should not include an overnight stay. Fatigue, sleep, and cold make it much harder to notice instructions or changes in the water. There is also no practical way for a visitor to get comfortably dry beside a fire.

Arrange a surface return plan as well. Somewhere away from the tussock and its drainage, have dry clothes, warm socks and gloves, shelter, a warm drink, and someone who knows when to expect you back. That person should not try to open the plug or search the bog if you are late; they should alert the residents through the agreed method.

Because you are crossing worlds, take biological contamination seriously in both directions. Arrive without mud, seeds, insects, or decaying organic matter on your equipment. Postpone the visit if you are contagiously ill. On leaving, do not carry away root fragments, animals, eggs, or “interesting” water. A multiverse traveler can be an invasion route as easily as a guest. Complete whatever atmospheric and physiological compatibility checks your circumstances require; the presence of residents does not automatically prove that every human visitor can safely breathe or tolerate the local biology.

## Dress for cold that cannot be undone

The temperature is only a little above freezing, but the real problem is the combination of cold, condensation, springy wet footing, and inactivity. There may be no wind, which makes the danger easy to underestimate. You will not warm yourself by walking briskly, because brisk walking is precisely what you should not do there.

Wear non-cotton insulation beneath suitable bog-weather clothing for the approach. For the Ward itself, use clean, close-fitting, quiet layers as directed by your guide:

- A warm cap and dry insulating layers.
- Thin gloves, with a dry spare pair kept warm against your body.
- Clothing with quietly closed cuffs and collar, so that water and small animals do not readily get inside.
- Flexible, warm footwear with clean soles, or whatever foot coverings the residents consider appropriate. Do not bring bog mud inside on heavy boots.
- Nothing with loose straps, wide hoods, trailing cords, or crackly waterproof fabric that announces every movement.

Avoid clothing so bulky that you brush both sides of a passage. You need to be able to crouch, step carefully over thresholds, and balance on a yielding vascular rib without automatically grabbing a wall. Sharp step lips and splintered abandoned doorways are additional reasons not to shuffle or feel your way hurriedly.

Be honest about your tolerance for confinement, darkness, and arthropods. You need not enjoy them, but you must be able to remain still and follow instructions when something runs across your glove or a cloud of nearly silent wings hatches beside you. If panic would make you run, shout, or tear at the surroundings, arrange only a threshold visit during an outer-plug inspection.

Your turnaround signs should be conservative: soaked footwear or gloves, persistent numbness, clumsiness, uncontrollable shivering, increasing apathy, lamp problems, or any concern expressed by your guide. Do not wait until you feel severely hypothermic. There is no fire below with which to recover.

## Carry very little

If the residents permit a pack, make it small, sealed, close-fitting, and easy to keep off the floor. A reasonable visitor’s kit might contain:

- Personal medication and a compact first-aid kit, especially wound dressings.
- A small quantity of drinking water, if the visit is long enough to require it.
- A sealed, dimmable electric lamp and a separate backup light, **only if artificial light is acceptable**. Keep spare batteries warm, and disable flashes, strobes, alarms, and vibration.
- Any specifically requested safety item, and nothing merely carried out of habit.

Your eyes will not necessarily become useful through dark adaptation. If the residents prefer darkness and cannot provide a safe way to guide a sight-dependent visitor, do not pretend that courage will solve the problem. Limit yourself to the gray light of an inspection or do not go below.

Leave behind open flames of every kind: candles, torches, stoves, fuel heaters, and smoking materials. Smoke would persist, and flame would injure the same tissue that shelters the town. Also leave behind trekking poles, general-purpose knives, digging tools, drones, adhesive markers, chalk, insecticides, soaps, and loose rope. A knife is not a universal emergency tool in the Ward; used on a blocked passage, it may simply create a new wound in your shelter.

Do not plan to lay a cord trail. Cord pressed against the root can become structure, and survey cords already serve purposes you may not understand. Nothing should be left behind, including “biodegradable” scraps. In the Ward, biodegradable material is precisely what the animals and root can process—often in places where you do not want them processing it.

Do not arrive with technological improvements: pumps, lamps for every house, metal tools, or a stove. The absence of these things is not evidence that the residents have failed to imagine them. Their knotted maps, timed cuts, sluices, and pressure work are technical knowledge. Ask what compensation or gift, if any, is actually wanted; otherwise bring none.

## How to behave below the plug

If you enter during a brief gray inspection, you may see ivory steps, straw-colored tissue, green-black seams, and beads of clear sap. Use that light to orient yourself, not to memorize an escape route. Once the plug is closed, your experience of the town will become much narrower.

Keep your voice low enough for conversation at arm’s length. Step where your guide steps. Do not stamp, scrape your feet, or let equipment knock against the walls. If you cannot hear the gutter yourself, make sure the guide has a tactile way to tell you when it has gone still.

Keep your hands to yourself:

- Do not use survey cords, house cords, veins, or rootlets as handrails.
- Do not clear an apparent blockage, scrape away growth, or helpfully open a narrowing passage.
- Do not touch the cistern rim, dip a bottle into seep-water, or taste sap.
- Do not set your pack against living tissue or leave it unattended.
- Do not enter dwellings, abandoned doorways, watch niches, or side pores without explicit direction.

The empty central oval is not a village square, and the long unbuilt intervals are not vacant land. Do not gather, picnic, or take a group photograph in the oval merely because it is open. Its emptiness is part of the settlement’s caution about shifting lanes.

Likewise, do not treat the residents’ work as a demonstration in which you can participate. During maintenance, your most useful contribution will usually be to stand where you are not obstructing a bridge, gate, or survey cord.

The animals are not an infestation surrounding the “real” town. They are much of its ordinary movement. Most should not be interpreted as attacking you. Do not swat indiscriminately, poke into ceiling pores, or test the statement that a hand laid on a wall soon acquires a living outline. Keep out of the gutter. If something gets inside your clothing or you are bitten or stung, tell the guide quietly and deal with it where they direct, rather than thrashing against the root.

Bring no crumbs into the settlement, and carry out every wrapper, dressing, and dropped object. The midden-lock is infrastructure, not an automatic solution to visitor waste. Human blood and tissue also belong in your first-aid planning: cover existing wounds before entry, and if you begin bleeding, contain it and tell your guide.

## Rehearse the two important events

### When the gutter suddenly becomes still

This is not a picturesque local superstition. Still water means the blind hunting eels may be listening for vibration.

Stop what you are doing as safely as possible. Do not splash, speak, adjust equipment, or hurry toward an exit. Wait for the residents’ signal. This is why watches and devices with vibrating alarms should be silenced before entry, and why you should not rely on noticing the change in sound by yourself.

### When a pressure pulse passes through

A pulse is not automatically an emergency. It is a recurrent event around which the settlement organizes work.

The guide may recognize the far seal tightening before you notice anything. The walls will cool and draw inward; the gutter will rise and may briefly reverse. Animals will emerge in layers, and the residents will begin lifting, closing, opening, and retying things.

Move only as instructed, keep your hands out of gates and bridge joints, and do not imitate the residents’ work. The burst of activity may be almost silent. Afterward, do not navigate by your memory of where a cord, bridge end, or threshold used to be. Nothing has quite returned to its former place.

If your light fails or you lose contact with the guide, stop rather than wandering, unless immediate danger requires the prearranged movement. Use the agreed signal. Do not shout routinely, feel your way into side passages, or cut your way toward what you think is the exit. If a route is blocked, the residents’ knowledge of growth and drainage is more valuable than improvisation.

## What the visit will feel like

You probably will not see the Root-Ward as a town in a single comprehensible view. You will know it in fragments: the guide’s sleeve near your hand, cold coming through your soles, a floor that gives slightly under each step, a drop finding the back of your neck, and the mixed smell of crushed stems, wet shell, water, and faint sweetness.

Your hearing may become almost too attentive. A cistern drop will seem enormous. A beetle turning over will sound like a small mechanism. At some point the roof may release its accumulated condensation and briefly stipple the lane with rain. In the darkness, it will be difficult to distinguish distance, size, and movement.

During a pulse, you may feel the settlement become intensely busy without becoming loud. That, more than any danger, is likely to be the revelation: nineteen households continually coordinating with a structure that does not consult them, maintaining streets that cannot be expected to remain streets.

When you return above ground, ordinary bog wind and birdsong may seem harsh. Looking back, you will see only wet vegetation around a black tussock. Resist the urge to make the location easier for others to find. Do not publish identifying coordinates, and ask permission before recording or sharing images of the entrance or dwellings.

A successful visit is not measured by how deep you went or how much you saw. It is one after which the residents have no additional repairs, no contaminated water, no injured animals, no lost visitor’s cord incorporated into a wall, and no reason to regret opening the plug. Prepare to be warm, quiet, patient, and willing to leave early. The Ward does not need you to be useful; it is enough if you can briefly avoid being harmful.
