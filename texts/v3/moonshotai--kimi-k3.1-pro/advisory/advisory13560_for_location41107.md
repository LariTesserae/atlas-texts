Prepare for a visit that is easy to survive in principle and easy to ruin in practice.

The Hollow does not appear actively hostile. Nothing in the description suggests dangerous animals, extreme heat, flooding, fumes, or some intelligence testing the visitor. But it offers almost nothing to a visitor: no useful light, no water, no food, no fuel, no shelter, and no nearby assistance. Its stillness also means you should not expect weather, decay, or animal activity to erase your mistakes. Preparation should therefore emphasize **self-sufficiency, reliable return, and restraint**, not expedition heroics.

## Before crossing over

**Survey what the description cannot tell you.** A poetic account is not certification that the air is breathable, the pressure and gravity tolerable, the mineral dust safe, or the surrounding country easy to cross. Use whatever remote sensing or preliminary probe your transit method permits. Check the approach and exit as carefully as the Hollow itself; the shallow rim is no obstacle, but the land beyond it is unspecified.

**Make the return arrangement before entering.** Carry your primary means of return on your person. Do not leave the only transit device beside an arrival point that may become invisible in total darkness. Test any beacon, communicator, or retrieval signal under local conditions. Do not assume that GPS, magnetic compasses, familiar stars, or cross-universe communications will work. A small party should have a schedule, a firm turnaround time, and someone elsewhere who knows when to initiate retrieval.

A pair is sensible for a first visit. More than a few people would multiply footprints, noise, and the temptation to talk without adding much safety.

**Bring all your water.** Calculate for the entire approach, stay, return, and a substantial delay reserve. The Hollow’s small size is misleading: you may be able to cross it in minutes, but you cannot drink from it, and digging would be both futile and destructive. A filter or purification tablets are not a water plan here. Carry electrolytes if the approach is warm, and enough sealed food for delays, though it is better to eat before entering and keep food closed while inside.

Do not count on the ground cooling after sunset, and do not count on dawn arriving on a convenient schedule.

**Arrive clean.** Footwear and equipment should be free of soil, seeds, and organic material from other worlds. The absence of visible animals does not mean the Hollow is sterile or that introduced organisms would be harmless. On return, clean or quarantine equipment according to your transit protocol rather than knocking the dust out at the next destination.

## What to carry

- **Two independent light sources per person**, with spare power. At least one should produce a very dim, shielded beam suitable for close footing. Red or amber light is useful for preserving what night vision is possible, but no color makes an unnecessarily bright lamp harmless to the experience.
- **A dim, removable return marker**, placed on stable ground at your entry point rather than built into a cairn or attached to vegetation. It should be just visible enough to serve its purpose.
- **Sturdy, comfortable boots and long clothing.** The walking is not mountaineering, but darkness, dust-filled fissures, and occasional sharp rocks make cuts and turned ankles more plausible than any exotic danger. Light gloves are useful if you must steady yourself against stone.
- **A compact medical kit**, including dressings for cuts, blister care, and sterile eyewash for mineral dust. Add any personal medication and a basic heat-illness kit appropriate to the approach.
- **Delay equipment:** emergency shelter or a bivy, extra water, and clothing for conditions outside the Hollow. There is no wood to burn and no reason to sacrifice the shrubs for warmth.
- **A watch or timer.** Silence and unchanging warmth can make time difficult to estimate.
- **A sealed waste kit.** Nothing should be buried there. Even spilled clean water, urine, food crumbs, or soap would be a significant input to a place organized around dryness and scarcity.
- Optionally, **a notebook and pencil** for recording impressions afterward. Leave sampling tools, collecting containers, climbing equipment, and excavation gear behind.

Nothing described justifies special heat shielding or weapons for local wildlife. Do not turn the absence of animals into either a hunting opportunity or evidence that some unseen predator must be responsible.

## Entering and moving around

Stop at the rim before going down. Check water, lights, return equipment, and everyone’s footing. Then remain still for a while. Dark adaptation may help you distinguish stars and broad differences in blackness, but it will not make the ground safely visible. Walk with light when you need it; do not stumble around in the dark merely to prove that you are respecting the place.

Choose a short route over stable, bare, dust-covered ground. Avoid crust plates, root-bearing fissures, shrubs, and fractured rocks. If every step begins breaking the surface, stop rather than continuing to the center out of sheer commitment. There is no special virtue in reaching the middle. The rim and a single carefully chosen patch of bare ground are enough for a first encounter.

Do not:

- dig for water or inspect what lies beneath the crust;
- break salt plates to see whether they are fresh;
- lift, strike, or arrange the tool-like stones;
- try the grinding bowl “just once”;
- pick leaves, snap dead twigs, or collect fallen wood;
- light a fire, use a stove, or spill fuel;
- scratch a marker, build a cairn, or leave an offering.

The Hollow’s equilibrium is not invulnerability. Its slopes and stones are settled because relatively little disturbs them, not because a human being cannot disturb them. A snapped stem or crushed plant may remain part of the scene for an extremely long time.

## What the visit will probably feel like

At first, it may seem like an absence rather than a location. Your lamp will make the place appear harsher and more fragmented than it is; without it, your eyes will keep searching for edges. Your footsteps, clothing, breathing, and water bottle may seem intrusively loud. The warmth through your boots can give the basin an oddly indoor quality, while the remote stars prevent it from feeling enclosed.

After some quiet, the tiny sounds may become conspicuous: a grain slipping, a stem clicking, a stone ticking beneath the crust. They do not need to be investigated. The description gives no reason to treat them as signals or as the beginning of something approaching. Conversely, their smallness does not make them a private message meant for you.

You may find the interval peaceful. You may find it boring, oppressive, or faintly unreal. All of those are ordinary responses to darkness, silence, and the lack of anything demanding attention. If you are prone to panic or disorientation in sensory-poor environments, do not make your first visit alone, stay near your entry route, and leave as soon as distress begins interfering with judgment.

The main psychological discipline is to avoid manufacturing an event. Do not shout to hear whether the Hollow answers, throw a stone to test the silence, wait for a revelation, or keep extending the visit because “something might happen.” Nothing happening is not a failure of the destination. It is most of what the destination is.

A good first visit is deliberately unambitious: pause, enter only as far as footing and impact permit, spend a quiet interval in one place, and leave while you are still attentive. Do not wait until thirst, fatigue, or low batteries make the exit hurried.

## If something goes wrong

- **If a light fails**, stop and use the backup. If all lighting fails, do not attempt to cross uneven ground by starlight. Stay put and use your verified retrieval procedure.
- **If someone becomes separated**, follow your agreed stay-put and signaling plan rather than having both people search blindly. Emergency shouting is preferable to further injury; the silence is not more important than safety.
- **If you become overheated or dehydrated**, leave and treat it promptly. The ground’s steady warmth is not a clue to decipher, and waiting for it to fade is not a treatment.
- **If retrieval is delayed**, conserve water and use your carried shelter. Do not dig, burn vegetation, or wander outward in search of resources that the Hollow gives no reason to expect nearby.
- **If you cut yourself**, remember that some rocks are sharp even though most surfaces are rounded. Use gloves when moving an injured person or clearing immediate footing, and disturb as little as possible.

## Leaving

Retrieve every marker, battery, wrapper, and piece of equipment. Pack out human waste rather than burying it. Do not pour out unused water, wash dust from your hands, or leave food for animals that are not there. Take photographs without flash or light-painting, and take no mineral, wood, or plant souvenir.

Do not try to “restore” the site by sweeping footprints, rearranging stones, or breaking up a damaged area more thoroughly. You are likely to enlarge the disturbance. Simply avoid adding anything further.

The successful visit will look almost comically uneventful: you arrived, stood or sat quietly, noticed a few degrees of darkness and warmth, heard almost nothing, and departed with everything you brought. That is the correct outcome. Go only if you can be content with a place that does not respond; otherwise, impatience may tempt you to leave the very event, mark, or story that the Hollow has so long done without.
