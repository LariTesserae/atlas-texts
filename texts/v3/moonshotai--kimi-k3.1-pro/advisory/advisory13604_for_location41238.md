Prepare as though you were entering a vast, operating thermal plant whose maintenance ecology happens to be alive—not as though you were exploring a swamp, an abandoned factory, or a wilderness with unusual scenery.

The Level is not necessarily hostile. That is part of what makes it easy to misjudge. The daylight is reasonable, the open water is shallow, the machinery is quiet, and nothing visibly rushes toward you. But almost nothing there has been arranged for human comfort. Shade is not reliable cooling, clear water is not necessarily drinking water, an empty court is not a campsite, and a motionless cover may be an animal. The sensible visitor is a self-contained, short-stay surveyor with a dependable exit—not a camper, salvager, or long-distance walker.

## Before crossing

**Measure the environment before breathing it unprotected.** Local animals and white daylight do not establish that the air, pressure, temperature, or microbial environment is suitable for a human. If your means of travel permits it, send instruments first. At minimum, establish:

- Atmospheric composition and pressure.
- Air temperature, humidity, and heat-stress conditions.
- Surface temperatures on walking plates, stairs, and pipe-adjacent routes.
- Whether fogs, vent plumes, and digester aerosols present respiratory hazards.
- Whether the water contains industrial contaminants or organisms for which ordinary field purification is inadequate.
- Whether your communications, clocks, navigation equipment, and return method function normally there.

Do not infer safety from cleanliness. The absence of rust, mineral deposits, stains, and wear also removes many of the cues people normally use to recognize leaks, hot surfaces, and neglected equipment.

**Treat the Level as active infrastructure.** No visible operators does not mean abandonment. If operators or maintainers can be identified, obtain their instructions. Otherwise, confine yourself to open service routes and do not enter pressure halls, sealed machinery, subdecks, or roofed spaces merely because they appear accessible. An ordinary traveler should not turn a visit into a confined-space entry.

**Make the return independent of local cooperation.** Test it before committing to a long excursion. Have check-in times with someone outside the Level, a companion if possible, and predetermined conditions that end the visit. Do not make your plan depend on finding an edge, a control center, a construction crew, a transport network, or someone who can explain the place.

## Clothing and personal equipment

Dress for **persistent wet warmth and contact hazards**, not for cold rain.

- Wear light, close-fitting, ventilated clothing that protects your skin from incidental contact with warm surfaces. Avoid loose sleeves, dangling straps, flowing garments, and anything that could catch on machinery.
- Choose boots with excellent wet traction and soles that provide some insulation from warm walking plates. The uppers should be as breathable as the hazard assessment allows. Heavy waterproof rubber boots may simply become hot, waterlogged heat traps.
- Bring spare socks and meticulous foot-care supplies. Skin that remains wet for hours can macerate and blister even without long hikes.
- Carry suitable gloves for incidental protection, eye protection, and a low-profile bump cap or helmet if your route has low conduits and tight service aisles.
- Use a compact, close-fitting pack. A large expedition pack will snag, obstruct narrow aisles, and make it harder to retreat through the same spaces.
- Secure every small item. Zipped pockets and retained caps are preferable to clips and open pouches; an object dropped through a grating may be unrecoverable and may enter an operating system.

Do not assume “quick-drying” equipment will actually dry there. Keep critical electronics, medical supplies, navigation records, and a dry set of clothing for after your return in sealed containers.

A respirator should not be an automatic accessory. It adds heat and breathing burden. Use one only for an identified hazard, with a model appropriate to hot, wet conditions.

## Supplies: bring what the Level cannot safely provide

Bring your own water, electrolytes, food, power, light, and medical supplies, with reserves appropriate to the measured conditions. The open pans and leisurely channels may look inviting, but they are part of an industrial and biological circulation system. Do not drink from them, wash wounds in them, cook with them, or plan an extended stay around purifying them.

Your medical kit should emphasize:

- Heat illness recognition and immediate response.
- Burns and minor crush injuries.
- Blisters, maceration, and damaged feet.
- Wound cleaning and waterproof coverage using supplies you brought.
- Eye irrigation with sterile fluid.
- Any personal medications, protected from heat and moisture.

A bleeding wound, a damaged boot, suspected heat illness, or a sprain is a good reason to end an ordinary visit. In particular, confusion, collapse, or severe heat symptoms require evacuation and active cooling; simply resting in the shade may accomplish very little.

Do not bother packing for hunting, fishing, gathering, firewood, or salvage. There is no green forage, no natural dry shelter, and no reason to assume local animals or microbial pastes are edible. Food from sealed synthesis drums is not human food merely because it sustains local fauna.

## How to move

Walk slowly enough to notice that the surfaces are alive.

Use walking plates, stairs, aisles, and catwalks that are plainly intended for access. Do not walk along pipes, step across cistern rims, use gratings as shortcuts without assessing them, or wade through channels just because they are shallow. The principal open-water hazards are more likely to be footing, concealed apertures, temperature, and animals than drowning.

Before sitting, leaning, placing a pack, or putting a hand on a “cover,” pause and inspect it. Larger flattened animals can fit engines so exactly that only breathing distinguishes them from components. What appears to be ceramic grain may separate into hundreds of small jointed bodies. This does not require panic; it requires looking before making contact.

Other useful habits:

- Test wet footholds and keep three points of contact on stairs.
- Never run to escape warm rain.
- Do not climb equipment to obtain a panoramic view.
- Do not put a hand near a flange, seam, vent, or housing to investigate its temperature.
- Do not open a panel, loosen a bolt, move a component, or clear a blocked intake.
- Do not wedge equipment between pipes or leave it in a courtyard “for just a moment.” That gap may be a required clearance, and that courtyard may be machine access.

The Level’s stability is not an invitation to test whether you can alter it. Nor is it necessary to imagine that one careless footstep will destroy the continent. The simpler point is that you cannot tell what any particular surface or opening is for, so the safest default is to interfere with none of them.

## Around the fauna

Think of the animals as **living participants in maintenance**, not as vermin infesting a factory and not as robots wearing flesh.

They hunger, bleed, mate, and die. Avoid harming them casually. At the same time, do not sentimentalize them into safe, tame creatures. Their behavior around human hands, food, blood, and equipment is unknown.

Move calmly and let small animals shift away rather than sweeping or stamping through them. Give the large sleeping beasts a wide berth. Avoid dense flier concentrations around vents, both because of possible aerosols and because walking into a vent plume is already a poor decision. Do not handle eels, feed animals, imitate calls, introduce pesticides, or collect specimens as souvenirs.

Your own contamination matters too. Pack out food scraps, wrappers, sanitary waste, and other refuse. Do not wash with soap, spit into channels, urinate in a courtyard, or assume that an apparently endless recycling system makes disposal harmless. Use whatever biological containment and post-visit decontamination your crossing permits, in both directions: you should neither seed the Level with Earth organisms nor casually carry its microbial pastes and body fluids home.

If you stand still, animals may close around your shadow and resume occupying the surfaces near you. That need not be curiosity, aggression, or recognition. It may simply be the local pressure of life returning to its usual distribution.

## Weather, warmth, and navigation

**Do not use weather as a clock.** A permanent column of warm rain is not passing over you. A stationary condensation bank is not necessarily approaching, and waiting for it to clear may mean waiting forever. If a district’s rain, mist, heat, or visibility is unsuitable, leave the district rather than waiting for a front.

Likewise, do not expect rain or shade to restore you. Your cooling plan should center on pacing, hydration, limiting exposure, and returning to a genuinely cooled refuge outside the Level. Fans and damp cloths may have limited value in humid air.

Navigation deserves redundancy. At ground level, views are interrupted within a few dozen paces, and the same housings, bridges, channels, and exchangers repeat beyond any useful walking range. A distant rain column can serve as a mapped landmark, but not as evidence that you are nearing a boundary or that conditions are changing.

Before leaving your arrival point:

- Record the route in more than one form.
- Photograph or transcribe crisp identification marks, while remembering that they may not be unique or readable.
- Carry a waterproof physical route record as well as electronics.
- Use temporary, retrievable navigation beacons if necessary, with a strict inventory so none are abandoned.
- Set a turnaround time based on your supplies, exposure, and support arrangements—not on how interesting the next district looks.

Do not rely on your sense of elapsed time. Quiet repetition, constant warmth, unchanging weather, and the absence of accumulating events can distort attention. Use timers for drinking, rest, check-ins, and departure. A companion should occasionally ask simple questions: Where are we? What is our return route? How much reserve remains? Why are we still here?

## Where not to sleep

The best accommodation is outside the Level, with short visits inward.

There is no genuinely unused ground on which to pitch a tent. A bare roof may be a thermal buffer; a service court may be access space; a dry-looking gap may be a tolerance requirement. Sleeping against an engine exposes you to heat and fauna, while sleeping in a court may put you among herds of large animals or across a route you failed to recognize.

An overnight stay is justified only if you have a verified refuge with tolerable temperature, sanitation, secure supplies, and a safe route back. Otherwise, return to your base to eat, sleep, dry or replace equipment, and review your observations.

## A sensible first visit

Keep the first excursion almost disappointingly modest.

Enter with your companion and support arrangements intact. Confirm environmental readings, communications, navigation, and return. Remain close to your exit long enough to notice any delayed equipment or physiological problems. Then follow one short, assessed service route. Stand quietly for a while. Watch the grain separate into animals, the current resolve into eels, and a housing reveal itself by breathing. Record what you came to record. Leave at the planned time, even though nothing has happened.

That last part matters. The Level exerts a peculiar pressure on an explorer’s habits: it encourages waiting for the cycle to finish, the rain to move, the machinery to reveal its product, or the repetition to break. None of those may occur. Going farther is not necessarily going deeper, and distance is not progress. There may be no final warehouse, central explanation, or imminent transformation awaiting a sufficiently persistent traveler.

The psychological effect can be strong without being supernatural. The silence is crowded, the freshness is ancient in implication, and your presence produces no visible appointment or response. If that becomes distressing, do not respond by wandering farther in search of meaning. Return, rest, and decide whether another visit is worthwhile.

In short: **verify the air, bring your own water and supplies, protect yourself from heat and wet footing, carry your own time and directions, touch almost nothing, and keep your exit certain.** Visit briefly and observantly. The Level does not need to menace you to be dangerous, and it does not need to notice you for your visit to matter to you. Nothing there is waiting—including anything that will tell you when it is time to leave.
