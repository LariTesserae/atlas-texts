Prepare for the Summit Nursery as **two journeys sharing one doorway**. The approach is high-altitude, exposed industrial travel. The visit itself is a slow walk through a small, warm, wet enclosure. Once inside, an ordinary human does not need mountaineering heroics; the important qualities are disciplined hatch procedure, good footing, clean equipment, and patience.

## Before making the ascent

Treat the tower as operating infrastructure, not as a public park with an unusually good view. Arrange access with whoever controls it and obtain a briefing on:

- Operation of the double pressure hatch, including equalization and emergency procedures.
- Which paths and terraces are open to visitors.
- Alarm signals, evacuation routes, and communications.
- Exterior weather limits and the expected conditions on return.
- Whether supplemental oxygen, acclimatization, or particular medical clearance is required for the approach.

Do not infer that the interior air is safe merely because plants are thriving. Confirm that its pressure, oxygen content, and trace contaminants are suitable for humans. Likewise, do not judge the exterior by the garden’s calm. Almost no wind voice enters the pressure skin; outside, conditions may still be dangerous.

Nearly five kilometers of elevation matters. The fact that the nursery is pressurized does not make exposed transit, an exterior platform, or an emergency egress safe. Ascend according to local guidance, go with another person, and do not push through altitude symptoms. Headache accompanied by nausea or dizziness, unusual exhaustion, poor coordination, confusion, or breathlessness out of proportion to exertion are reasons to stop and seek help or descend—not reasons to hurry toward the warm interior.

Check in and out, and leave an expected return time. A twisted ankle or pressure problem is more serious when the only way out is through a hatch and down a remote tower.

## Arrive clean, and plan to leave clean

For a multiverse traveler, biosecurity is not ceremonial. You and your equipment may have arrived directly from an unrelated biosphere.

Follow local quarantine and decontamination procedures. At minimum, clothing, footwear, and packs should be free of soil, seeds, spores, insects, and other travelers. Do not bring flowers, cuttings, picnic food, or a “helpful” pollinator. Avoid applying insect repellent, pesticide, or other aerosols immediately before entry.

The nursery’s animal scarcity and incomplete pollination are features of its present condition, not problems awaiting visitor intervention. Its ecological composition is already changing without being forced to remain designed.

The same responsibility applies on departure. Do not carry a snail, moss, fungal felt, condensate, or substrate into another world. A souvenir from a closed experimental garden can become contamination twice: once when removed and again when introduced elsewhere.

## Clothing and equipment

You need clothing for both sides of the hatch.

**For the approach and exterior exposure:**

- Insulating layers suitable for thin, cold air.
- A windproof outer shell, hat, and gloves.
- Clean, sturdy footwear with reliable wet traction.
- Sunglasses and appropriate high-altitude sun protection.
- Any operator-specified oxygen or safety equipment; do not improvise life-support gear.

**For inside:**

- A lightweight, quick-drying shirt and trousers. Cotton can remain unpleasantly wet.
- Shoes that grip wet ceramic, gratings, and filmed paths. Avoid smooth soles, sandals, and aggressive tread that carries debris.
- At most a small, close-fitting pack. The narrowest path allows wet foliage to touch both shoulders, and a large pack will sweep plants and fittings.

Useful small items include sealed drinking water, personal medication, a compact towel, and a waterproof notebook with a pencil. Ordinary paper will soften and curl. Do not rely on the basin or rills for drinking water, however clean the condensate appears.

An umbrella is unnecessary and awkward. The rain is warm, fine, vertical, and brief; accepting a little dampness is usually more comfortable than bringing something that drips, snags foliage, and needs storage. Hiking poles are also more likely to catch roots, rails, and grating than to help.

If bringing a camera, protect it from condensation. A cold camera taken directly into warm, saturated air can fog internally as well as on the lens. Keep it in a closed bag until its temperature has had a chance to equalize, and carry lens cloths. Use a small camera rather than a tripod that blocks a path. Do not use drones.

Visit a toilet before entry, keep food outside, and carry out everything you bring. Spilling a little drinking water will not magically destroy the nursery, but this is a closed, monitored water system: unlogged additions, dumped liquids, and abandoned waste are both contamination and bad manners.

## Entering and moving around

Use the hatch exactly as briefed. Never prop it open, obstruct a seal, or attempt to hold both sides of the pressure boundary open for conversation or photography. If you have ear or sinus congestion, mention it before equalization rather than struggling through it.

The transition may be more memorable than the view: cold, dry equipment and clothing meet air like a sheltered summer afternoon. Sweat stops evaporating efficiently. Smells arrive together—wet chalk, green sap, hot copper, occasionally a little machine oil. Stage your exterior layers where instructed so that you neither overheat inside nor emerge later in damp clothing without insulation.

Once through:

- Stay on established paths and keep the hatch area clear.
- Do not climb retaining blocks, condenser cores, valve columns, or new mineral ribs.
- Do not lean on the pressure skin or push against it to determine where it is. When dry, it may be nearly invisible.
- Treat the low parapet as an absolute boundary, not a place for a better panorama. There is no descending ground beyond it, only tower structure and height.
- Walk slowly. Water films the paths, and soft moss or fungal felt beside them is neither a safe walkway nor surplus ground.
- Keep out of the central basin. It is only hand-deep, but the black aggregate makes it appear depthless; stepping into it risks a fall and damages a carefully constructed surface.
- Do not touch the condenser boulders to test their temperatures. One side may be warm, the other cold and wet. Radiator combs, copper lines, service bolts, and pump fittings are machinery, not exhibits.

The crawler’s slowness does not make it harmless. Stay clear of its rail and extrusion area. New foam may look invitingly like pale stone and may be hard by noon without being ready to bear a visitor’s weight. Survey marks are part of the work; do not move them for a photograph.

If you are unsteady on your feet, have limited mobility, or are severely affected by heights, ask in advance whether a hatch-side visit is possible. The turning paths, shallow terrace changes, wet gratings, narrow foliage, and immense visual drop make the full descent more demanding than its few meters of height suggest. There is no need to approach the parapet to have a worthwhile visit.

## How long to stay, and what to watch

Allow at least an hour; ninety minutes is better. A hurried ten-minute walk will show you wet plants and machinery, but not the nursery’s actual rhythm.

Begin near the hatch, where the planting rows are easiest to see. Notice how young and sharp everything is: casting numbers beneath algae, bright fittings, service bolts on rounded condenser cores. Then descend slowly. The dense planting makes the small garden feel larger, but resist the temptation to move faster simply because the total distance is short.

Choose a place near the basin without blocking a path and wait through a complete weather cycle. The sequence rewards attention:

1. The radiator combs grow cold.
2. Low white cuffs of mist form around the rocks and spread into the foliage without hiding the paths.
3. Fan-ferns turn their pale undersides upward, and mosses begin to brighten.
4. Droplets gather on the pressure skin and broad leaves.
5. Warm rain falls straight down from apparently clear air for less than a minute. The leaves make a sound like restrained applause far away.
6. The pumps recover the water, the basin subsides, and dripping resumes at several pitches.

Stay for another cycle if possible. The first shows you what happens; the second lets you notice which parts respond.

Look for life without pursuing it. A transparent water flea, a methodical white moth, or a pale snail on the cold side of a boulder may be the entire visible animal encounter. Do not use the absence of birdsong and insects as a reason to supply recorded sound or conversation. The hum through the gratings, the ticking metal, the precise knock of a relay, and water dripping at different pitches are enough.

At the newest terrace, do not stare at the crawler expecting obvious movement. Compare it with the survey marks. Look for the slight darkening of fresh substrate after rain, the ferns leaning across the narrowing gap, and roots occupying places that were originally pipe space.

Most importantly, find the rill that has escaped its groove by a finger’s width and the small white crescent at the basin’s edge—without stepping close enough to disturb either. **Do not repair it.** Do not straighten the flow, brush away the grains, weed the approaching roots, or clear algae to make something look tidier. A flow sensor already knows about the change. The absence of correction is part of what you came to see.

Likewise, do not prune the vine in the ventilation housing, hand-pollinate flowers, gather fallen blossoms, or decide which roots are “really” infrastructure. A root’s meaning depends on where it goes, and that judgment is not a visitor’s to make.

## Choosing the light

There is no single best time, provided access conditions are safe:

- **At noon**, the greens are direct and almost severe, and exposed construction reads clearly in white, gray, and rust-red.
- **Under cloud**, shadows vanish and wet surfaces become softly silver; the garden feels especially roomlike.
- **In late sun**, copper briefly warms in color and the basin can turn brown-gold. On a clear day, the cloud deck and distant towers may also be at their most impressive.

Never trade a safe hatch and weather window for prettier light.

## Leaving

Before exiting, dry yourself enough to be comfortable, inspect your clothing and pack under local procedures, and have your cold-weather layers ready. Warm, humid clothing can become dangerously chilling once you return to thin exterior air. Check actual exterior conditions rather than trusting your memory of the approach.

If an alarm sounds, follow the briefing immediately. Do not stop to photograph mist on the pressure skin, retrieve equipment from machinery, or finish observing a rain cycle.

The visit itself is likely to feel gentle, even intimate. What stays with you is the contrast: an immense horizon outside and forty paces inside; engines too large to see reduced to a hum felt in the teeth; ordinary daylight falling on ground with no bedrock beneath it; precise machines patiently permitting an untidy composition.

You will not see the future valley form. You may see one brief rain move a few grains of white substrate toward it. That is the appropriate scale of the place. The best preparation, after safety and clean equipment, is to arrive willing to notice something that small—and to leave without helping it along.
