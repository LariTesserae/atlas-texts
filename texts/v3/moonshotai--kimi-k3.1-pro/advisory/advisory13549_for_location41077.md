# Preparing for a Visit to Serein

The main mistake would be to prepare for Serein as merely a picturesque mountain city. It requires three separate kinds of readiness: **high-altitude self-sufficiency, exceptional restraint around water, and protection against your own expectation that time will announce itself through change.**

Serein does not sound dangerous in the sense of being hostile or poised on the edge of disaster. The greater risk is that a visitor becomes tired, dehydrated, sleepless, or careless while waiting for the place to behave more like an ordinary world.

Go alone or in a very small party. Its open spaces are not an invitation to bring an expedition. A dozen visitors camping around a cistern, filling the courts with conversation, and requesting food and lodging could alter the experience of a ward simply by arriving.

## Prepare the body for the basin, not for its stillness

The exposed rock, cool air, stairs, and sharp visibility can make Serein look easier to cross than it is. A ward that appears close enough to inspect in detail may still require a substantial walk and several climbs. The whole settlement being visible at once does not make it compact in effort.

Because the actual elevation is unspecified, do not guess about oxygen equipment or altitude medication. If the approach permits, acclimatize gradually and keep your first day undemanding. Do not attribute headache, nausea, dizziness, confusion, or unusual exhaustion to the basin’s peculiar atmosphere. Treat them as possible altitude illness; severe breathlessness at rest, confusion, or difficulty walking require help and descent, not further observation.

Practical equipment should include:

- Broken-in boots with good grip on both dry stone and wet, mossy slabs.
- Moderate insulation and a protective outer layer. There is no reason to pack polar expedition clothing, but stone buildings and shaded clefts may feel cold after exertion.
- A hat, sunglasses, sunscreen, and covering clothing. The daylight is described as ordinary, but altitude and unbroken exposure make sun protection sensible.
- Personal water containers, food for the intended stay plus a reserve, and necessary medicines. Prefer food that does not require local fuel or elaborate cooking.
- First aid for cuts, blisters, and ordinary falls. Young, freshly fractured rock can be sharp; still cliffs are not necessarily safe cliffs.
- A reliable timepiece with an alarm, a notebook, and a light-blocking sleep mask. Earplugs may also be useful: in profound quiet, a single dripping jar can become surprisingly difficult to ignore.
- Binoculars, which are particularly well suited to Serein. They let you watch goats, birds, and distant wards without approaching ledges or disturbing water gardens.
- A pack-out plan for rubbish and sanitation. Do not assume that Serein’s lack of accumulation will dispose of anything you leave behind.

Keep the load light enough that carrying it up civic stairs does not itself become an altitude problem.

## Bring your own day with you

An ordinary human is poorly adapted to a working day with no external ending. Serein’s shadows do not lengthen, the cloud does not advance, and there is no dusk or seasonal change to tell you when to eat, sleep, or leave.

Before arrival, establish a schedule based on elapsed time: meals, medication, rest, sleep, check-ins, and a firm departure point. Use an eye mask and, if possible, a darkened sleeping place. Do not simply rest when you happen to feel tired; continuous daylight can allow mild exhaustion and dehydration to accumulate while the scene around you remains apparently unchanged.

Keep a private continuity log. Record when you arrived, when you slept, how much you have eaten and drunk, and any significant symptoms. This is useful even if everything behaves normally, and especially valuable if your own fatigue becomes the clearest evidence that time is passing.

Until you have strong reason to believe otherwise, assume that **you and your belongings are not included in Serein’s equilibrium**. Assume that you can be injured, that food can spoil, that batteries can drain, that medicines still need to be taken on schedule, and that a bad decision will not quietly reset itself. Water flowing and animals breathing demonstrate that processes occur; they do not demonstrate that visitors are protected from consequences.

Your return arrangements should likewise not depend on a local sunset, a change in weather, the movement of the cloud sea, or a seasonal alignment. If your method of multiverse travel permits it, use an external or elapsed-time trigger and verify your exit arrangements early rather than at the end of an open-ended stay.

Do not rely on the sun for navigation. Use marked paths, local directions, and a map or sketch. If you need evidence that your visit is progressing, put it in your notebook—not in a cairn, a moved stone, or a mark on a wall.

## Treat visible water as infrastructure, not scenery

Carry enough water for the approach and an appropriate reserve. Serein has no river, and its abundant-looking green pockets do not indicate general abundance. The city’s supply is gathered drop by drop and distributed through carefully compared heights. Clear water, cold stone, and healthy reservoir animals also do not prove that a source is safe for an off-world human to drink without guidance.

If possible, arrange water, lodging, and—if translation is not otherwise provided—interpretation before a prolonged visit. Water customs are too precise to negotiate entirely by guesswork.

The central principles are simple:

- **Public does not mean unrestricted.** A public cistern is shared and ordered, not a free tap.
- **Visible does not mean available.** A dark fracture, a pool in a hollow, or moisture beneath an overhang may be essential to a wider system.
- **Restoration is not permission.** Do not interpret a reservoir returning to its incised mark as proof that visitors can draw endlessly.

Ask where and how visitors may draw water, and use the vessel or measure you are shown. Do not dip dirty hands, cups, or equipment into a cistern. Keep covered reservoirs covered; do not open them merely to inspect the translucent shrimp. Do not wash yourself, clothing, cooking equipment, or dust from your boots in channels, pools, or overflow gardens. Soap and food waste should never enter the water system.

The condenser nets, split-wood frames, stone fins, grooves, gutters, and ceramic pipes are not rustic ornaments. They are the city’s essential technology. Do not touch, adjust, clean, or “improve” them without explicit supervision. Even a well-meant alteration could be equivalent to casually rewiring a water supply elsewhere.

Do not pitch camp beside a seep for convenience. That risks trampling its vegetation, monopolizing a resource that cannot be privately enclosed, and turning someone else’s waterwork into your personal campground.

Sanitation needs equal forethought. The basin has little deep soil, and its drains and clefts are connected to cherished water and ecological systems. Use designated arrangements or carry out waste; do not improvise a latrine beside the nearest dry-looking patch of rock.

## Move through Serein as though every empty space is intentional

Serein is sparse, but it is not abandoned. A quiet storehouse is not necessarily disused. An empty meeting ground is not a ruin. A house with no one visible outside it is not open for inspection, and a long civic stair without pedestrians is not evidence that nobody cares where you go.

Arrive by marked routes and make initial contact in public spaces rather than wandering through residential courts or waterworks in search of someone. Do not expect a noisy market, readily purchasable provisions, or a commercial district in which money can solve every preparation error.

Moderate your noise. Sound carries clearly, but there is little continuous sound to absorb it. A rattling pack, recorded music, a drone, or an ordinary group conversation may dominate a courtyard. Speak quietly, avoid shouting across open rock, and let silence remain the default rather than treating it as something to fill.

Stay on paving and established paths. The boundary between habitats can be only a pace wide, and the green places are deep, complex pockets rather than decorative strips of lawn. Do not walk on moss for its softness, push through ferns for a view, pick aromatic plants, or turn over stones to find salamanders. If a goat is drinking at a cistern, wait rather than driving it away. Untroubled animals are not necessarily tame animals.

At the cliffs and saddles, do not let the absence of fresh scree persuade you that the rock is safe to climb. The clean fractures and split pinnacles are reasons for caution, not invitations. Beyond the eastern saddle, the cloud below the rim is neither solid ground nor a usable descent route. It is a view across an inaccessible boundary.

## Do not try to improve, rescue, or experiment on the city

Serein’s technical sophistication lies in exact measurement, patient fitting, and repeated sufficiency. A visitor accustomed to equating progress with enlargement may find this difficult to recognize. Resist the urge to suggest a deeper channel, a higher wall, a larger cistern, a more generous roof, or a machine that could make the keepers’ work unnecessary.

Even gifts can be disruptive. Timber, copper, tools, seeds, livestock, or construction materials are not automatically benevolent in a place that deliberately uses materials sparingly and does not want the rest of the city to change around each new object. Offer nothing substantial without asking whether it is wanted.

If invited to help, expect the desired result to be **restoration rather than optimization**. Replacing a slab with one of the same dimensions may be the entire achievement. Cleaning a gutter without altering its fall may be more skillful than redesigning it.

Do not test the equilibrium by draining a pool, damming a channel, chipping a block, shifting a fern, or leaving an object to see whether it disappears. Even if the waterline returns or a keeper repairs the damage, you will merely have converted your curiosity into someone else’s disturbance. Nor should you conduct experiments on yourself to discover whether injuries, hunger, or aging are suspended.

Most importantly, do not assume the inhabitants require liberation from their condition. Nothing in the description suggests that their quiet is mournful, that their healthy gardens are about to fail, or that a catastrophe is being held back. A traveler who arrives determined to discover what Serein is “really” waiting for may miss what is directly in front of them.

## What the visit is likely to feel like

At first, Serein may simply feel exceptionally legible: grey wards descending through bare rock, vivid green held in precise pockets, distant birds individually visible, and no haze softening the edges of anything. Then its soundscape will become apparent. A hoof scrape, two mallet blows uphill, wings, a low conversation behind a wall—each sound arrives separately rather than blending into bustle.

Only later may the fixed daylight and unchanging details begin to press on you. You may repeatedly check a shadow, a cloud edge, or a reservoir mark. Your own hunger and stiffness may seem almost inappropriate, as though you have brought private weather into a place without any. If that produces restlessness, do not answer it by making a mark on the city. Walk, sleep according to your schedule, or shorten the visit.

Serein also offers something gentler: relief from having to anticipate the next change. There is no approaching storm against which work must be finished and no sunset that must be reached before the walk becomes dangerous. But that freedom only remains safe if you supply your own limits.

Leave at the time you chose, not when the light tells you to. Carry out everything you brought, take no stone, plant, waterwork fragment, or animal as a souvenir, and do not wait for a final transformation to give the visit its meaning.

A successful visit to Serein is one in which you remain healthy, keep your own time, use only what has openly been allotted to you, and learn to distinguish maintenance from improvement, emptiness from abandonment, and stillness from lifelessness. The city does not need to become different in order to prove that you were there.
