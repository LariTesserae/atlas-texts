# Preparing to Visit the Unbuilt Works

**The safest preparation is to arrange not to enter in person.** Use remote observation if your purpose is scientific or aesthetic. If your purpose is to cross the Works, choose another route. If it is to bring back a component, abandon it: a removed stone is wet gravel, a removed swimmer is dirty water, and there is no finished product waiting to be collected.

If physical entry is unavoidable, do not think of it as a cold-weather expedition. Think of it as a brief insertion into a regional electrochemical ecology that has no empty ground, no dependable geography, and no reason to preserve your boundary.

An ordinary human arrives as a warm, wet, salty, electrically active object. The Works uses all of those properties. Nothing needs to hate you, or even notice you in an intentional sense, for your visit to become dangerous.

## The non-negotiable requirement: a way out

Your primary piece of life-support equipment is not your suit. It is your extraction method.

**Do not enter if getting home requires retracing your steps to a fixed local landmark.** Footprints may be claimed almost immediately; fog destroys visual ranging; herds and compression fronts alter the terrain’s usefulness; and crowding can insert additional inhabited lowland between you and where you were. Walking harder does not necessarily solve a distance that the Works can manufacture.

Your return should therefore be:

- independent of local landmarks and, ideally, local distance;
- anchored or supported from outside the Works;
- operable while you are injured, disoriented, or unable to hear;
- redundant, with both deliberate and automatic activation;
- tested under conditions resembling spatial insertion and disordered event sequence, not merely in an empty staging area.

A rescue party is not an adequate backup. A party entering after you brings more heat, noise, surfaces, and crowding. It may simply create a second emergency.

If you cannot satisfy these conditions, ordinary winter clothing, determination, and a handheld multiverse beacon do not make the trip acceptable. Stay outside.

## What being there would feel like

At ground level, you would see no factory and no clear mechanism—only exhausted mud, low animal backs, black pools, and zinc-colored fog. Yet the ground would not feel like scenery. Events would arrive through your boots before reaching your ears. What looked like gravel might shift because it was a packed generation of animals. A swell would not necessarily offer high ground, and a dry-looking route might still be a river expressed only as moving wetness.

You would instinctively search for somewhere bare to stand, somewhere quiet to rest, or something solid to hold. Those instincts are all unreliable. Bareness is immediately occupied. Quiet may be a predator. Solidity does not imply electrical or physical safety. Even a fresh-looking surface may appear ancient, so visual age gives little help in judging stability.

The cold would be serious, but your warmth would be the more distinctive problem. You would not merely be losing heat into the environment. You would be advertising a rare resource.

## Wear a self-contained sortie system, not camping equipment

### Closed breathing and complete skin protection

Until rigorous testing proves otherwise, treat the atmosphere as unsuitable even if its oxygen content appears adequate. A simple filter mask does not address oxygen balance, pressure behavior, metal-rich aerosols, concentrated brine, sulfide, or airborne motes.

Use a sealed environmental suit with closed-loop breathing and no warm exhaust plume. It should have:

- integrated boots and gloves, with no exposed fabric, absorbent seams, or open closures;
- protection against cold brine, chemical exposure, puncture, and crushing at least sufficient to reach extraction;
- a visor protected against fogging, impact, and sudden electrical glare;
- all eating, drinking, sanitation, and medical needs handled internally for the duration of the sortie.

Do not open the visor “just to smell the air” or wipe a lens with a bare hand. Water here condenses, creeps upward, and appears where ordinary drainage intuition says it should not. A smooth, sealed exterior matters more than conventional hiking convenience.

### Thermal containment, not simply heating

A heated suit can keep you alive while making you more conspicuous. An unheated suit eventually leaves you cold, clumsy, and unable to extract yourself. You need insulation against the Works **and** a way to absorb your own metabolic heat without continuously dumping it outside.

Every watt you produce must go somewhere. Your safe duration ends when you can no longer manage both your internal temperature and your external heat leakage.

Do not use open flames, external chemical warmers, heated camps, or waste-heat vents. If small fauna begin collecting around you in layers, the situation has already progressed beyond “minor wildlife interest.” Larger animals follow such gatherings.

There is no perfect camouflage. A cold outer shell may still collect condensation and cold-triggered motes, while a warmer shell risks attracting heat seekers. This is one reason to keep the visit short rather than searching for an imaginary suit setting that makes you ecologically neutral.

### Electrical and material precautions

Use sealed, isolated power supplies and as little external conductive material as possible. Avoid trailing wires, metal trekking poles, long tethers, exposed batteries, and improvised grounding arrangements. A conductor is not just a shock risk; it may become something for other processes or organisms to align with.

The exterior should be electrically insulating where practical, but do not treat dielectric boots as immunity. The voltages, current paths, and scale are unknown, and the entire environment is saturated.

Test seals, lubricants, batteries, and suit materials under local conditions. Ordinary cold-weather ratings assume that freezing is a comparatively well-behaved phenomenon. Here, ice is not a dependable thermometer, and a frozen-looking surface is never a promised platform.

### Sensory protection without sensory blindness

The roar can injure hearing, but complete deafness would conceal one of your most important warnings.

Use impulse-limiting hearing protection that still preserves some ambient awareness. Do not use aggressive active noise cancellation that could make a hush difficult to distinguish from normal operation. Supplement hearing with substrate-vibration sensing and visual alarms inside the suit.

Keep communications and alarms quiet externally. Fog herds feed on sound, and shouting, sonic beacons, or loud machinery are not neutral actions. A silent suit alarm should be visual or haptic.

Do not depend on a bright headlamp. Fog will degrade it, local blue-white flares may be more informative than your own illumination, and solar charging is not a dependable option.

## Plan around processes, not a map or a daily schedule

Observe remotely through several local pressure, condensation, charge, and migration sequences before entering. This will not reveal a safe season; there is no day-night rhythm, and the cycles never synchronize for long. The purpose is to recognize what a comparatively quiet local interval looks like and to identify behavior that precedes trouble.

Then plan **one short task**, not an itinerary. A good sortie requires no sleep, camp, battery recharge, hunting, water collection, or multi-day walking.

Additional rules:

- Keep support personnel outside and send the smallest practical entry party.
- Do not establish a base camp or set down a warm equipment pile.
- Do not wait for a compression stroke merely because it would be spectacular to witness.
- Rehearse aborting when a warning has no visible cause. You cannot afford to stop and debate whether an anomaly is “real.”
- Set a conservative time limit before entry. Wonder, poor visibility, and incomplete observations are not reasons to extend it.

Remember that the anomalies are not necessarily random lawlessness. Many are patterned, banked, and used successfully by native fauna. Your disadvantage is that you do not know those patterns and cannot learn them safely during one visit. Copying animal behavior may provide warning, but it does not make you adapted; native animals also die when warnings are wrong or belong elsewhere.

## Conduct once inside

**Do not wade or swim.** Pools contain currents, wire-eel alignments, predators, and phase behavior you cannot read from the surface. Do not cut through an eel chain or disturb a darkening pool. Predators may be attending to interruptions in conductivity rather than to individual animals.

**Do not treat herds as terrain or transportation.** Rillback flanks grind together, their backs carry other fauna, and their migrations are part of the Works’ circulation. Do not stand between crowded animals, ride one, or follow a migration route because it appears well traveled.

**Do not head toward silence.** If visible footfalls, vent activity, and herd motion continue while sound vanishes over a moving region, assume a hush. Initiate extraction. Do not approach it to confirm the effect, and do not fire a weapon or shout into it.

**Do not head toward apparent emptiness.** Newly inserted lowland arrives already inhabited, complete with scars and histories. It is not a sterile escape corridor.

**Watch and feel for collective behavior.** A herd turning before a visible strike, swimmers scattering from an apparently empty pool, or a vibration arriving underfoot without an accompanying airborne sound all deserve immediate attention. Do not wait for the event to become visually intelligible.

**Keep sealed during strange weather.** Reverse rain means a clear sky is not assurance against a sudden release of water. Fog expulsion, brine sheets, and cold-triggered motes are additional reasons not to open equipment outdoors.

**Leave no deliberate offerings.** Do not feed animals, discard a battery, release wash water, or leave markers to see what the Works does with them. Everything you carry should return with you, including waste and damaged equipment.

## Immediate abort conditions

Any of these should end the visit rather than begin an investigation:

| Event | Response |
|---|---|
| Animals begin layering around your heat leakage | Extract before the gathering attracts larger fauna. Do not wait to identify the small ones. |
| Ambient sound abruptly disappears or visible motion becomes soundless | Assume a hush or a critical sensor failure. Either is sufficient reason to leave. |
| The ground tightens, deep vibration arrives underfoot, or vents begin anomalous activity | Treat compression as imminent or underway. Do not wait for a visible mud vent. |
| Pools flash, eel alignments change violently, or a pool suddenly darkens | Break off the observation. Do not touch the water or attempt to repair an interruption. |
| Fauna flee or turn before any perceptible cause | Treat their behavior as an alarm, but do not blindly follow their route into a herd or compression front. |
| The horizon shifts, additional lowland appears, or your return landmark seems farther away | Stop local navigation and use nonlocal extraction. Do not try to “walk off” the new distance. |
| Your suit breaches, your visor becomes unusable, or heat containment fails | Extract immediately. Field repairs are only for keeping you sealed long enough to get out. |
| You receive a warning before its apparent cause, especially one concerning you | Treat it conservatively. Do not approach a premature carcass, answer a future distress call for curiosity, or try to complete a causal loop. |

A false alarm is preferable to cleverness. And there is no honest wilderness-survival fallback for failed extraction: you cannot safely drink the brine, live off unstable fauna, build a fire, or walk confidently toward an edge.

## Samples, contamination, and return

The appropriate scientific posture is passive observation. Removing a piece does not give you a miniature Works; it destroys the relationships that made it meaningful. Even photography should be secondary to avoiding interference.

On return, quarantine the suit and equipment before opening or cleaning them. Treat adhering mud, droplets, condensation, and unfolded motes as potentially active material rather than ordinary dirt. Do not brush everything off in the arrival chamber or rinse it into a drain. Check for cold injury, hearing and pressure injury, chemical exposure, and electrical effects, and preserve your records without forcing them into a tidy chronological sequence if they do not support one.

A successful visit may look disappointingly brief from outside. You arrive sealed, record a few vibrations and blue currents, notice the ground is more occupied than your eyes first suggested, and leave when the herd turns before you know why. You bring back no souvenir and have no story about surviving a full compression.

That is success. You observed the Works without allowing yourself to become one of its temporary production arrangements.
