Prepare for a **short, cold, carefully coordinated visit**, not a ruin crawl. There is no indication that you need weapons, climbing equipment, or artificial light. The main risks to you are cold, poor footing, losing track of time, becoming separated in absolute silence, and uncertainty about the entrance. The main risk to the hollow is you: a large, warm visitor carrying grit, moisture, skin oils, crumbs, and organisms from elsewhere.

The hollow’s stillness should not be mistaken for either lifelessness or invulnerability.

## Plan the visit before you find the seam

Choose a settled period of weather and give yourself ample daylight for the journey across the plain. The hollow has no sunset to hurry you along, but the plain may have darkness, wind, rain, and exposure. Carry enough water and ordinary emergency supplies for the surface journey; do not plan to obtain anything below.

Navigation deserves attention. The green seam is easy to miss, the surrounding gravel offers few landmarks, and an electronic map or positioning device from another world may not be dependable. Use whatever local navigation works, backed by bearings, distances, or a sketched approach route. Do not build a cairn beside the hollow or clear vegetation to make it easier to find. When approaching the likely location, do not walk across the mossy seam or probe it with poles: vegetation, roofs, and retaining work may lie immediately beneath.

A small party is preferable. Ideally, someone remains above with the surface equipment, watching the time, the weather, and the entrance, while the smallest practical number of people go below. A solo traveler should be very conservative—perhaps limiting a first visit to what can be seen from the entrance. A fall below would produce neither a cry nor a audible clatter.

Settle permission beforehand if there is any local custodian or community to ask. Once inside, you cannot call out to explain yourself or request consent.

Most importantly, do not treat egress as automatic. The reference to the entrance closing behind a visitor is sufficient reason for caution. Before committing yourself below the three steps, establish that you can turn around and leave. If the gap is known to close, and there is no reliable, non-damaging way to reopen or observe it, an ordinary traveler should not improvise a full exploration. A rope is not a simple solution: it can snag in the compressed passages, damage masonry, interfere with the entrance, and create false confidence.

## Dress for damp stillness, not for the apparent brightness

The illumination may make the hollow look temperate. It is not. Dress for air just above freezing, cold stone, and long periods of standing nearly motionless.

- Wear a wool or synthetic base layer, an insulating layer, and suitable wind and rain protection for the plain. Avoid arriving sweaty; damp clothing will make the visit much colder.
- Bring a hat, warm socks, and gloves that still allow careful movement. Pay particular attention to the gap between glove and sleeve, since the damp cold is noticed early at the wrists.
- Use clean, close-fitting footwear with secure, slip-resistant soles. The worn steps are rounded and may be damp, while aggressive tread can carry gravel into the hollow and grind it against the stone.
- Keep spare dry gloves, socks, and insulation above, along with water, food, first-aid supplies, and any shelter needed on the plain.

Enter dry if possible. Leave your large pack, walking poles, tripod, and other projecting equipment outside. Secure loose straps, scarves, cameras, and glasses so that nothing brushes a wall or drops into a basin.

Your equipment below should be minimal: a reliable visual watch or timer, personal medication, and perhaps a small notebook and pencil for communication. Keep food and drink sealed at the surface. Use the toilet before entering.

Clean clothing and footwear of soil, seeds, and insects before arrival, especially if you have come directly from another world. Do not wash or decontaminate equipment beside the seam.

You should not need a lamp below. There is no true darkness, and adding a hot lantern, open flame, stove, or unnecessary bright light would be intrusive. A surface lamp may still be useful for the return journey across the plain. A whistle is likewise useful above and useless within the hollow.

## Rehearse communication for a place without sound

Do not rely on voices, radios, whistles, audible alarms, or the sound of footsteps. Agree on a few unambiguous visual signals before descending:

- stop;
- all well;
- return to the entrance;
- I am too cold or unwell;
- help is needed.

Keep companions in sight. A person who rounds one corner may as well have vanished until you see them again. Check visually at regular intervals, and do not split up to inspect separate chambers. If conversation is necessary, face one another and use the notebook rather than repeatedly raising your voice.

The silence can also affect movement. Without the scrape of a sole or the sound of cloth, you may receive less feedback about your own footing. The flat white light and shallow gray-green shadows may make worn step edges harder to judge. Move slowly, look at each placement, and never hurry to reassure someone who cannot hear you anyway.

Psychological reactions vary. Some people will find the silence restful; others will experience it as pressure, deafness, or sudden isolation. Neither response, by itself, proves that anything supernatural is happening to you. Pause at the threshold and see how you tolerate it. If you become panicky, disoriented, or compulsively focused on testing the silence, leave calmly. Do not clap, strike masonry, or shout repeatedly to see whether the condition can be broken.

The surface party should understand that an unanswered shout is normal. It is not, by itself, evidence of an emergency. Failure to return at the agreed time is much more significant.

## Enter less than you are physically able to

Descend the three steps one at a time. Pause when the air changes, notice the cold and damp, then turn and confirm the entrance and your return route before proceeding.

Once below, stay on bare stone wherever possible. The clear, polished bands and unplanted stair treads are better routes than apparently softer moss. Do not step onto fern joints, basin rims, roof cushions, leaf axils, or the dark pockets where fallen material disappears.

The town’s compression is not an invitation to squeeze through it. If your shoulders or clothing would brush both sides of a stair or passage, stop. Do not remove a layer, force your pack through, or brace yourself against pipes and vegetation. The place has fitted itself together; it has not necessarily left room for a visitor.

For a first visit, the principal lane and open court are enough. Avoid climbing onto roofs or retaining walls for a better view. Antiquity and settled masonry do not certify that a terrace will carry your weight, and the roof vegetation has almost no soil in which to recover from a footprint. Do not crawl into drains, cistern spaces, granary niches, or other confined infrastructure.

Treat wall recesses as storage and habitat, not displays. Baskets, awls, grinding stones, lamps, and clay pipes should remain untouched. Do not clear moss to examine a join, pour water to trace a drain, straighten an inclined wall, or “repair” a root’s path. Nothing there has asked to be restored to an earlier condition.

If you crouch to watch the animals, do so only where there is bare, stable stone and where you can rise without pushing against a wall. Remember that cold stone will draw heat from you quickly. Give the gray-furred animals and other small creatures the right of way. Do not feed, handle, corner, or lift leaves to expose them.

Photography, if permitted, should use the existing light without flash. Keep the camera close to your body and do not set it on a basin, wall, or standing stone.

## Add no water, food, warmth, or offerings

Bring all the drinking water you need, but leave it outside. The cistern’s clarity says nothing about potability, and its black water, fish, and crustaceans constitute habitat rather than a traveler’s supply. Do not open it, touch it, stir it, fill a bottle, wash your hands, or drop anything into it. The same applies to the hollowed stones, thumb-sized cups, pipe bends, and water held in leaves.

Given how small and finely distributed the visible reserves are, even a minor withdrawal or spill could matter. Do not pour out unwanted drinking water to see how the drains respond.

Likewise, bring no snacks below and leave no crumbs, spit, urine, soap, hand cream, insect repellent, or other chemical residue. A morsel that seems negligible to you may be a substantial nutrient input to an ecology that ordinarily loses almost nothing. Do not leave coins in the cistern, food for the animals, or a well-meant offering in a wall cup. There is no basis for thinking the hollow needs anything you own.

## Do not mistake equilibrium for protection

The recovery of displaced moss and the disappearance of stains are not permission to experiment. Do not press a footprint into vegetation to watch it vanish, cut a sample to see whether it regrows, or take away a pebble on the theory that the town will replace it. Apparent restoration may have limits, costs, or timescales you cannot perceive. A place that does not readily accumulate marks may still accumulate harm.

Nor should you assume that the condition extends to your body. Fixed light does not prove that time has stopped, and an equilibrated town does not necessarily keep a visitor warm, hydrated, uninjured, or safe. Continue to treat cold, fatigue, injury, and thirst as ordinary physiological problems. Do not sleep there, use it as a refuge from surface weather, or remain simply because nothing appears to change.

Set a deliberately short first visit—long enough to acclimate and observe, not an open-ended afternoon. Use a visual timer rather than an audible phone alarm. Brightness will not tell you how long you have been inside, and fascination can make minutes difficult to estimate. Standing still to watch a beetle or fish will also chill you faster than walking.

Leave at the agreed time, or earlier if you develop numb fingers, clumsiness, persistent shivering, dizziness, unusual fatigue, anxiety, or any doubt about the entrance. An ordinary visitor should also withdraw rather than investigate if conditions depart unexpectedly from those for which the party prepared.

## Return to the plain carefully

Exit without rushing. When sound returns, the crunch of gravel and ordinary voices may feel startlingly loud. It helps if the person above does not greet you with a shout or drop equipment beside you. Stand still for a moment, regain your bearings, and then move away from the gap.

Above, check the time and weather, warm and dry yourself, and account for every person and possession. Inspect cuffs, footwear, and equipment before traveling onward so that you do not carry soil, seeds, or small animals into another world. Do not make an immediate second descent merely to check whether your visit left a trace.

A good first visit is almost uneventful: arrive clean and dry, pause on the steps, follow only the easiest bare-stone route, touch nothing, stand quietly long enough to notice one small movement, and leave while you are still warm and oriented. The hollow’s fascination lies in how completely it has already been fitted together. The appropriate preparation is therefore not the equipment to alter it, but the discipline to visit without asking it to accommodate you.
