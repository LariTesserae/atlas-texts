# Preparing for the Upper Manifold

Prepare as though you were visiting a cold, high-altitude industrial roof with no guardrails, no reliable water, and no rescue service. Then add one further assumption: **the route behind you may not remain the route behind you.**

An ordinary person can make a careful short visit. The mistake would be to treat it as a hike through peculiar scenery.

Your first impressions will be physically contradictory. The basin will look immense and open while your body is continually turning, ducking, and squeezing between fins and ducts. The light will suggest heat, but cold will creep through your boots and gloves. You may find yourself speaking quietly without deciding to. After you stand still for a while, the silence will become populated: a relay inside a wall, a panel ticking as it contracts, wings disturbing lamplight, a vibration in your teeth. None of this means the works are abandoned or safely switched off.

## Before committing to the trip

**Establish the altitude before you go.** “The height of mountains” is not an acclimatization plan. Arrive already acclimatized if possible, and know the signs of altitude illness: persistent headache, nausea, unusual exhaustion, clumsiness, confusion, or breathlessness at rest. Severe symptoms require immediate evacuation, not a rest stop and another attempt at the map. Descending through the decks is not a dependable substitute; there may be no ground beneath them, only further works.

**Do not go alone.** A small party is preferable, with everyone carrying their own water, warm layer, light, first-aid basics, and route notes. Agree beforehand on check-ins, hand signals, a turnaround time, and a lost-person procedure. Never split up to investigate two apparently identical landmarks.

**Arrange support outside the Manifold.** Leave someone your intended landmark sequence and pickup windows. Prefer an exit whose operation does not depend on a particular hatch, door, duct, or other component remaining where you left it. If your only exit is itself part of the Manifold, keep the first visit extremely short and close to arrival.

**Obtain the right kind of map.** An architectural plan is of secondary value. You want the latest route ledger: where condensation repeatedly stains a flange, which recess contains an old nest, which threshold snails have polished, which tray supports the same grass, and what pump or seasonal conditions accompanied those observations. Treat even that map as something to verify, not as a guarantee.

For a first visit, avoid the Assembly Margin, deep unverified galleries, and overnight travel. Plan by thresholds and decisions, not kilometers. A destination visible across the basin may be hours away, a few steps away, or inaccessible by the route you intended.

## Clothing and equipment

### For cold, glare, and sharp surfaces

- Wear layered, non-cotton clothing, with an insulated jacket and a windproof, water-resistant shell. A rain shower can occupy one court while everything above it remains dry, so visible weather is not a forecast.
- Bring a warm hat, sun protection, and two kinds of handwear: warm gloves and close-fitting, cut-resistant gloves for handling rails and edges. Panels and stair noses are still keen.
- Use sturdy boots with excellent traction on wet metal and grating. Condensate films and ceramic dust can make apparently dry surfaces slick.
- Wear a low-profile helmet. Keep pack straps short and secured; a large expedition pack will continually snag in maintenance clearances.
- Carry serious UV eye protection with side coverage, plus clear protective glasses for shade and close inspection. Glacier-grade glare and dark galleries require different solutions.
- Sunscreen should include easily forgotten reflective exposure: beneath the chin, around the nose, and at the ears. The district can appear illuminated from several directions.
- Long sleeves and eye protection help against ceramic flour, zinc scale, and glass fiber. Avoid brushing or disturbing outer-ledge dust; use a well-fitting particulate respirator if dust conditions require one and you can breathe comfortably in it at altitude.

### For falls and exposure

A climbing or industrial fall-arrest harness is appropriate on exposed routes, but only with anchors that have actually been assessed. Do not tie off to a convenient pipe, valve stem, cable tray, fin, or newly installed railing. **A geographically stable landmark is not necessarily a load-bearing one.**

Do not use a rope as an Ariadne’s thread through unexplored hatches or ducts. It may tell you where your hands have been without telling you where its far end now is.

Carry an emergency bivouac and an insulated pad even on a day visit. Bare metal will drain heat from a seated or injured person long after the air seems tolerable. Overnight shelter should not be part of the ordinary plan.

### For self-sufficiency

Bring all your water and food, including an untouched emergency reserve. For most travelers this means several liters, not a single bottle. Cold and altitude can suppress thirst while exertion continues.

Do not count on:

- collection trays, which may hold only a fingerprint’s depth;
- condensate, which may take hours to form;
- sealed reservoirs, which should not be opened;
- filtration alone, which will not reliably remove dissolved metals, lubricants, or industrial residues.

Pack food that can be eaten neatly with gloves. Take out all crumbs and waste.

Each person should have a headlamp and spare batteries kept warm. A small low-angle inspection light is useful for revealing snail trails, films, and stains that overhead glare conceals. Radios, whistles, and visual signals are worthwhile, but none should be your only means of keeping the party together.

Your medical kit should emphasize bleeding from sharp edges, sprains, eye irritation, hypothermia, and the difficulties of moving an injured person through narrow clearances. Prevention matters more than the size of the kit.

## Navigation: treat history as infrastructure

The central rule is simple:

**Clean sameness is uncertain. Accumulated difference is comparatively dependable.**

Follow routes supported by several independent signs of history—a repeated damp stain, an established nest, animal polishing, salt patterns, longstanding vegetation—not merely by a single mark on a single panel. These routes are safer in the geographic sense, though not automatically easier or structurally sound.

Avoid pristine galleries and brilliant ranks of new fins, especially under hard glare. Do not enter a clean fin field to test whether its intervals are stable. If touching one edge produces the impression that you are touching the whole field, stop experimenting with your body and withdraw toward the last verified, history-rich landmark.

Keep a route log based on **individual identity**, not architectural labels. A useful entry might read:

> Passed beneath the flange with the fan-shaped dark stain; old nest in the second recess above; grass tray visible below on the right. Crossed the snail-polished threshold and regrouped beside the split-view overlook.

“Third blue tank” is nearly useless. A manufacturing mark identifies an interchangeable component, not necessarily a unique place. Photograph both the close detail and its surrounding context, and have more than one person keep notes.

You may add non-damaging temporary markers if your expedition protocol permits them, but do not trust them absolutely. A tag might appear on more than one corresponding instance, and a scratch does not instantly give a component years of separate history. **A mark proves that you marked something; it does not prove that the thing remained where you left it.**

Additional rules:

- Do not take an unknown hatch or duct merely because it appears to shorten the journey.
- Regroup after every surveyed threshold. No one should walk ahead while the rest of the party is still emerging.
- Do not assume that retracing a threshold is perfectly symmetrical.
- Do not navigate toward a sound. A single drip or relay may be audible in relation to several districts.
- Do not treat line of sight as distance. The rim looking near enough to touch is not a reason to continue past your turnaround time.
- Use compasses, GPS, altimeters, rangefinders, and photographs as supplementary evidence. None can prove route continuity.
- Prefer good, diffuse visibility. If traveling under clear sun, be particularly conservative around pristine identical arrays. Do not let concern about glare push you into traveling after dark.

Above all, move slowly enough to notice when your confidence is coming from recognition rather than evidence.

## Around the machinery, water, and animals

Treat every part of the works as active. Slowness is not inertness.

Do not open inspection covers, enter pressure hulls, operate valves, clear vents, or “repair” an apparently failed component. Closing something in one district may have consequences somewhere else. Avoid suspended loads, crane paths, assembly cradles, empty sockets, and uninstalled components. A shadow without a visible object at the Assembly Margin is not an invitation to stand in it and wait.

Do not camp beneath ducts, in drains or collection trays, or beside machinery merely because it has remained motionless during your visit. Never use an engine’s position as a clock.

The animals are not the principal danger. There is no need to approach them armed, but do not handle them, feed them, or follow them. A furred animal’s aperture may be an excellent route for that animal and an impossible one for you. Use animal signs as evidence of established history, not the animals themselves as guides. Leave moth-filled lamp housings closed, both for the electrical hazard and because opening them destroys shelter.

Likewise, do not step into the Pan’s trays or collect “just a little” water from a rare plant’s condensation site.

## If repetition or disorientation begins

If you pass the same flawed tank twice, or a companion insists that a landmark has moved, **stop before taking another threshold**. Do not keep walking in the hope that the geography will explain itself.

1. Put on warmth if needed, drink, and check everyone for altitude symptoms or panic.
2. Compare notebooks and photographs. Look for accumulated differences rather than arguing about which instance is “real.” Both may be valid views of something that is not separate in the way you expect.
3. If it is safe, retrace only the last verified chain of unique landmarks. Do not substitute a cleaner, straighter gallery because it looks easier.
4. Do not split the party, send someone ahead through a door, or descend toward supposed ground.
5. If you cannot reconstruct the route, use your agreed signals and outside-support procedure from the most history-rich, sheltered location you can safely reach. Avoid waiting in a newly finished chamber.
6. Keep essential equipment with you. Do not cache your only water, exit device, or warm clothing in a chamber whose continued location you cannot verify.

A serious injury or altitude emergency overrides ordinary exploration priorities: evacuate by the fastest safe means available rather than waiting for a clearer pattern to emerge.

## A sensible first visit

Make the first trip a few hours, not a traverse.

Arrive at a previously surveyed site with several strong signs of accumulated history and a verified retreat. Spend time adjusting to the light, breathing, and silence before moving. Walk one short, easily audited route to an overlook, preferably where you can see the Pan or the Fin Fields without entering their cleanest reaches. Observe more than you touch. Let your eyes adjust to snail trails and the almost invisible films on panels.

Turn around while everyone is still warm, alert, and well supplied. Turn around even if the next landmark looks close. Turn around especially if the party begins debating whether a place is familiar.

Afterward, compare accounts independently and update the ledger with stains, nests, condensation conditions, thresholds, and discrepancies. That record may be more valuable than anything you photographed from the rim.

Finally, do not try to improve the Manifold by tidying it. Do not scrub stains, remove nests, clear residue traps, introduce soil, scatter seeds, or take a component as a souvenir. Those small irregularities are not dirt obscuring the true geography; they are how geography is coming into existence.

The successful visitor is patient, somewhat suspicious of beauty, and willing to treat a snail-polished threshold as more trustworthy than a straight white corridor. The unsuccessful one keeps walking because the destination looks close and the way back looked obvious.
