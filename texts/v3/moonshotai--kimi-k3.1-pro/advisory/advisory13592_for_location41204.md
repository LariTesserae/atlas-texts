# Preparing for the Low Cistern

The first preparation is to abandon the idea that this is a swim, a shoreline expedition, or an ordinary recreational dive.

An unmodified human should assume, until measurements prove otherwise, that arrival offers **no reachable breathable surface**, that the floor lies at a pressure no human body can tolerate unprotected, and that “swim upward” is not an emergency plan. The safest first visit is by instrument. If physical presence matters, go inside a dry, pressure-rated vehicle or equivalent protective enclosure, with a tested way to return directly to your point of origin.

Nothing in the Cistern hunts you. There will be no storm, no predator, and probably no dramatic event at all. Its danger lies in offering an ordinary human almost nothing unaided: no air, no warmth, no dependable light, no food, no easy landmarks, and no reason to expect timely rescue.

## Make arrival and return an engineering problem

Do not make your own body the first probe.

Before a personal visit, send through instruments that can measure pressure at the arrival point and at the intended observation depth, as well as temperature, basic water chemistry, and suspended sediment. Test communications and the return mechanism under those conditions. If your route involves a portal, remember that a portal is not automatically an airlock: an opening held between a dry world and deep water may transmit water, pressure, or contamination in both directions.

Whether your equipment is technological, magical, or both, it should provide independently:

- Pressure protection and a breathable internal atmosphere.
- Heat, not merely insulation.
- Power and propulsion sufficient for a delayed return.
- Navigation that does not depend on seeing the entrance.
- Lighting and life support on separate reserves.
- A return method that does not require surfacing, finding a landmark, speaking clearly, or performing delicate gestures with cold hands.
- A rescue or secondary-return option with the same pressure capability as the primary expedition.

A breathing charm, oxygen tank, or drysuit solves only one problem. A vehicle with a single power source, single navigation system, and single portal key is not much better.

Do not go solo. For an ordinary traveler, the sensible arrangement is to accompany people qualified to operate the life-support and return systems, with another party monitoring from the dry side. If that support is unavailable, take the remote visit. A patient camera can show you a great deal without requiring anyone to survive the experience.

## Carry your own light, orientation, time, and supplies

Powerful lamps will not reveal the Cistern’s extent. They will only make a nearer volume of water visible; beyond it, distance will still become darkness. Near the floor, suspended leaf-dust may turn an intense forward beam into a pale, obscuring wall.

Use adjustable, comparatively restrained lighting, preferably offset from the camera or viewport. Bring sensitive close-focus imaging equipment rather than relying on a searchlight. The appropriate subjects are a paving joint, the mouth of a conduit, the edge of a mat, or tool marks in a protected recess—not an imaginary panorama waiting to be illuminated.

Navigation deserves more attention than lighting. The construction is repetitive, the relief is low, and there is no central monument or dependable peripheral landmark. Clear water may provide no useful sense of depth or motion. Do not descend merely to “find the bottom” and give yourself a reference. Use pressure or depth readings, elapsed time, a recorded track, and at least two independent means of determining position. If beacons or tethers are used, they must be planned so that they neither snag in the growth nor require attachment to ancient masonry.

Bring clocks and use them. The faint seasonal directionality in the upper water is not a substitute for daylight, and waiting for the brightest part of the cycle will not make a floor expedition visibly bright. Inside the vehicle, maintain a deliberate schedule of observation, checks, food, rest, and return deadlines.

Your personal supplies can otherwise be simple:

- Warm, layered clothing for the vehicle’s interior.
- All drinking water and food you will need.
- Necessary medication and ordinary emergency supplies.
- Waste containment.
- A voice recorder, plus paper and pencil as a backup.
- Any personal return token or control, secured so that it cannot be dropped or separated from you—but not relied upon as the sole means of escape.

Do not plan to drink the water because it looks clear, eat the vegetation because it is abundant, or supplement supplies by harvesting anything. There are no animals to catch, and no animals does **not** mean sterile. Microbes, spores, dissolved minerals, and unfamiliar biological material remain possible hazards.

## Visit without touching the Cistern

For a first personal expedition, remaining inside the vehicle is the prudent rule. Even where pressure and breathing could somehow be managed, an excursion on foot or by swimming would add entrapment, chilling, disorientation, and ecological damage without greatly improving the view.

Approach only a previously surveyed area. Hover rather than land.

A level pavement may be the roof of a buried void. A low dome may be a collapsed chamber bound together by roots. A smooth ceramic surface may be deformed under load. Rounded edges do not imply stability, and the absence of natural bedrock means there is no obvious place to anchor safely. Do not rest equipment on flags, tile roofs, blisters, or root-bound deposits. Do not tie lines to timber braces, stems, or gratings. Do not enter ducts or chambers to see where they lead.

Keep propulsion wash away from the floor. A careless burst could lift fine sediment, erase visibility, tear a mat, or move fragments into another conduit. Because circulation and decay are so slow, the disturbance may remain legible long after your departure.

Plan a short route with a conservative turn-around point. Do not spend your range searching for a more impressive district, a center, or an edge. There is no promised centerpiece. A good first visit might consist only of holding position in open water, approaching one exposed patch of pavement, examining the transition between swept tile and old growth, and returning with reserves intact.

The weak currents still matter, especially to navigation and longer deployments. You may feel perfectly stationary while drifting. A water front may announce itself only as a veil of dust, a slight change in the fronds, or a small instrumental change in temperature or dissolved content. This is not weather you can reliably read by eye. Record it rather than trying to dramatize it.

Likewise, prepare everyone for the sound. Most of what you hear will probably be your own vehicle: fans, pumps, cooling equipment, the hull responding to temperature or pressure. If a distant slab settles, the note may arrive with enormous presence and little sense of direction. It does not necessarily mean something is approaching. Do not investigate it, and do not respond with a panicked ascent. Withdraw only according to local conditions and your existing procedures.

## Treat every contact as a long-term intervention

The Cistern’s stillness makes it easy to mistake for an abandoned ruin that can be handled without consequence. In fact, it is a functioning combination of waterworks, sediment system, and extremely slow ecology.

Do not scrape growth away to expose a tool mark. Do not break off a tile because it appears loose. Do not clear a clogged pipe, straighten a grating, open a passage, or attempt a small act of “restoration.” The present blockage may be part of how water is stored, filtered, and diverted through the district. Repair and damage are not easily distinguished here.

Avoid releasing dyes, nutrients, lubricants, disinfectants, wastewater, or other tracers into the basin. Keep external lighting and waste heat to what the visit actually requires. Take everything back that you brought.

Equipment should also be cleaned under a controlled, closed protocol before arrival and after return, without discharging cleaning agents into the Cistern. Plants spread by spores and fragments, so material carried on a vehicle could transfer life between districts—or between worlds. Any local water, sediment, or biological sample brought back should remain sealed and quarantined. For an ordinary visitor, the best sample collection is none.

The governing assumption should be that you cannot tell the age of what you disturb. A mat that looks like a thin stain may represent an accumulation far older than your entire journey. A footprint is not necessarily a temporary footprint.

## Prepare for the psychological experience

The first sensory impression may be less beautiful than alarming.

Outside the vehicle there may be no visible floor, ceiling, or direction of travel. Clear water can make the darkness feel strangely empty, as though you are suspended in space or falling without moving. Your lights will create a small, temporary room of visibility, beyond which nothing answers. Inside, machinery may seem indecently loud.

When the floor finally appears, travelers expecting a drowned city may initially be disappointed. There are no commanding walls and few picturesque silhouettes. The architecture emerges through close attention: a joint remaining straight beyond expectation, an obtuse turn, a buried conduit becoming a shallow depression, an apparent boulder resolving into masonry. The immensity is inferred from continuation rather than seen in one view.

The vegetation may intensify the loneliness. It is dense and living, but almost nothing in it performs life on your timescale. No fish cross the beam. Nothing grazes, nests, or startles. The garden can feel like an archive more than a forest.

This combination—confinement inside the vehicle, boundlessness outside it, silence, darkness, and repetitive detail—can produce fascination, drowsiness, grief, panic, or a strong urge to make something happen. Discuss that possibility before departure. Use scheduled voice checks, structured observation tasks, and explicit abort criteria. If you need music or conversation, keep it inside the cabin rather than broadcasting sound into the water.

Give yourself permission to observe one thing for longer than feels necessary. Watch how a mat lifts slightly where water passes beneath it. Compare the growth inside a pipe mouth with that on the surrounding stone. Look, without touching, for adze facets or the crescent left by a stone maul. The Cistern is too large to meet you at human scale, but its components can. A finger groove in mortar may affect you more than the entire invisible province above and around it.

For a longer stay, the requirements become those of a closed research habitat: resupply, waste containment, continuous power, ecological monitoring, protection against gradual fouling, and a permanent evacuation capability. The habitat should be associated with a purpose-built arrival structure, not casually planted on the ancient floor. Continuous external light, heat, and noise would all become alterations of the place, not neutral conveniences.

## The compact rule

**Go dry. Go measured. Go with redundancy. Carry your own air, warmth, light, time, and way home. Stay off the bottom. Touch nothing. Do not wait for an event.**

A successful visit may end with no samples, no dramatic story, and no image that conveys the whole place. You may return with only a short transect, a few close views, instrument logs, and the memory of one distant note passing through the water.

That is enough. You do not go to the Low Cistern to see everything. You go to learn how to see one small part of it—and then to leave its roots, fractures, currents, and patient accumulations continuing without you.
