# Preparing for the Low Choir

Prepare as though you were entering three hazards at once: an unlit cave with uncertain air, an airborne biological swarm, and an extreme low-frequency noise environment. The greatest danger is that ordinary human instincts will repeatedly mislead you.

Open space is not empty. Dry ground is not lifeless. A draft is not necessarily fresh air. Rain is not merely rain. Silence is not safety. A small light near the ground may not be another traveler.

An ordinary person should not attempt a crossing, camp there, or explore alone. A first visit should be a short, carefully surveyed intrusion at the margin of a basin, close to the exit, with a trained partner or small team and support outside. If reaching your objective requires passing through a basin at full activity, choose another objective.

## Before entering

Survey the specific route, not merely the region. Conditions can differ between neighboring depressions, and one reading at the entrance says little about air in a cold basin farther on.

At minimum, establish:

- Whether the air along the route is breathable by humans. Animal activity proves nothing about human safety. Measure oxygen and carbon dioxide, and test for sulfur gases and any other hazards indicated by the local chemistry.
- The expected sound and vibration levels. If your protective equipment cannot provide a conservative exposure margin, do not enter at full chorus.
- Whether the basin is approaching a wet reactivation. A first visit is better made during a stable, lower-activity interval, though dormancy must not be mistaken for safety or emptiness.
- A route that avoids deep sinks, pool margins, active seeps, major grazer paths, heavily colonized overhangs, and the densest shell flats.

There is no dawn and no dependable daily lull. Do not plan to “wait until things quiet down.” The most extensive quiet may mean a predator is present. Bring independent timepieces, set a short trip limit, and rest outside rather than trying to establish a camp.

Practice with your equipment beforehand. You should be able to exit with your main light failed, speech impossible, and your partner communicating only through predetermined signals. Practice mask and filter checks, suit repairs, and turning back while disoriented. Also practice the psychologically difficult part: not tearing off your face protection when your skin crawls, not running when the sound becomes unbearable, and not speaking aloud when the basin suddenly hushes.

## Breathing and body protection

A simple dust mask is inadequate. The lower air contains crowded winged animals, organic dust, spores, shell debris, and whatever your footsteps disturb. Animals may coat your equipment and obstruct an exposed intake.

Use a sealed, abrasion-resistant outer suit with protected closures at the wrists, ankles, and neck, together with:

- A compatible full-face breathing system, selected on the basis of atmospheric testing.
- Appropriate particulate protection and protected, easily checked intakes.
- An independent air supply where the atmosphere cannot reliably be made safe by filtration.
- A personal gas monitor carried where it will measure the air you are actually entering.
- A helmet, puncture-resistant gloves, eye and face protection, and boots with wet traction, ankle support, and resistance to sharp shell fragments.
- Knee and shin protection, but only for emergencies; routinely kneeling or putting a hand down will injure both you and whatever is living there.

Remember that a respirator does not supply oxygen, and a filter cartridge is not a universal remedy for unknown gases. If the monitor alarms, leave. Do not descend into a depression to investigate, and do not follow a cool current of air in the hope that it leads outside.

Wear layers suitable for both chilling and overheating. The ground and air are cool, but walking carefully in protective equipment while under sensory stress can be strenuous. Eat and hydrate before a short visit so that you do not need to open the suit inside. Carry no loose food, and produce no waste there if you can possibly avoid it.

## Hearing is not enough

Hearing protection must be selected for the measured sound spectrum. Simply making yourself as deaf as possible is not the ideal solution: you still need a monitored way to detect equipment alarms, your partner, and changes in the chorus.

Use properly fitted protection, conservative sound-dose limits, and a communication system whose output is capped at a safe level. A separate vibration indicator can help warn of changes that damaged or overwhelmed microphones may miss. Do not rely on active noise cancellation alone, and do not assume any ear protection makes infrasound harmless. The great grazers’ notes and the bass held in deep sinks act on the whole body.

Leave if you develop nausea, disorientation, chest discomfort, headache, visual unsteadiness, or sudden anxiety that seems disproportionate to your thoughts. These may be ordinary panic, but they may also be warnings of noise, vibration, gas, or breathing-equipment problems. The Low Choir is a poor place to distinguish among them by experimentation.

Do not shout, sing, tap rhythms on the stone, or play recordings to see how the basin responds. A human voice will not conduct the whole province, but deliberately adding signals to a medium you do not understand is reckless. Avoid active sonic navigation for the same reason.

## Light and navigation

Carry redundant lights, but use them modestly. You need enough illumination to place your feet without crushing animals or stepping into a concealed pool; you do not need to sweep a powerful beam across walls and colonies.

A lamp will not give you a landscape. It will give you a small, crowded bubble: armored backs underfoot, wet limestone, wings striking the beam, perhaps a brief view of something withdrawing. Beyond it, the basin will remain as extensive and invisible as before. After switching the light off, the darkness may feel even more complete. A red setting may preserve your own dark adaptation, but do not assume it is invisible to local animals.

Most importantly:

**Never follow an unexplained cold point of light near the mud.** If it is not your partner’s agreed signal or a marker you placed yourself, do not approach it. At least one hunter uses light as bait.

Human ears will not quickly learn the inhabitants’ navigation by pressure, scent, vibration, and returning sound. Do not imagine that a few hours of attention will make the echoes legible. Use a surveyed out-and-back route with redundant, preferably nonacoustic navigation and a reliable means of retracing it. Any physical guide system must be installed so that it cannot entangle animals or damage colonies, and removed afterward.

Do not rely on speech for routine communication. At full activity your partner may be unable to hear you from arm’s length. Agree on unambiguous haptic or equipment signals for stop, turn back, check your air, and remain still.

## How to move once inside

Move slowly and keep your hands off the landscape.

The pipes on an overhang are breathing organs. A solid-looking patch of wall may be a brood surface capable of suddenly seething. Shell flakes may conceal flattened hunters. Wet seams may contain animals you cannot see. Animals may also drop from walls without warning. None of these surfaces is a convenient handhold.

The floor is worse. In favorable country, there may be no bare stone on which to step. You are walking on food films, living grazers, shells, droppings, and accumulated dead. Footing can shift, shell layers can break, and pale mud can conceal irregularities. Wet films are slick; dry crusts and dust are not sterile or inert.

There is no genuinely impact-free hike through such ground. Stay on the shortest surveyed margin route, and accept that if your conscience or your mission requires leaving the dense basin untouched, you should not enter it.

Other rules follow naturally:

- Do not touch or scrape the iridescent microbial films. They are the base of the local food web, not cave discoloration.
- Do not drink from any pool or temporary stream. Clear water may still be chemically unsuitable, crowded with animals, or thick with larvae.
- Do not step into a pool whose surface is crowded with openings, and do not shelter beneath an active colony.
- Do not swat at winged animals. Swatting only adds fragments and bodies to the air. Keep your seals closed and let your partner check your faceplate and intakes.
- If a deep note becomes localized and ground vibration increases, yield to the great grazer early. Slow does not mean harmless, and its body carries an entire community of smaller animals.
- Never climb onto a grazer, shelter beneath one, or treat the cleaned stone behind it as a convenient road.

## Chorus-rain: leave before it begins

Condensation-rain is not a pleasant ecological spectacle for a visitor to wait through. It is a starting gun.

The warning may be a change in rhythm before it is a change in water: several colonies entering the same deep phase, droplets beginning to jump, isolated falls becoming a patchwork of ticking. Once a wider ceiling surface begins releasing moisture, you should already be withdrawing.

Within minutes, sealed raspers may open, buried young may wake, temporary streams may become milky with larvae, hunters may converge, and the winged population may rise into the air. The noise can double while the rain is still beginning.

Do not remain on a basin floor to watch. Do not assume a low divide is a safe grandstand; from there you may simply be exposed to several choruses at once. Follow your prepared route out, avoiding sinks and newly flowing channels. Never open your mask because water or animals are covering the outside of it.

## The hush: do not mistake it for rescue

A spreading silence is the most important event to recognize.

If you are still near the margin and the hush is approaching, withdraw promptly along a known route. Do not go toward its center to investigate, and do not try to outrun it across unfamiliar ground.

If the hush reaches you before you can leave, stop. Extinguish unnecessary light. Signal your partner without speech. Avoid running, knocking shells, or filling the silence with nervous conversation. The predator may be feeding elsewhere, but you cannot locate it reliably by looking, and your own noises will feel—and may be—exceptionally exposed.

When calling resumes at the edges and begins closing inward, use the returning chorus as cover for a controlled withdrawal. This is a conservative response based on the behavior of the basin, not a guaranteed defense. There is no weapon, light, or noisemaker that an ordinary traveler should trust as a substitute for avoiding the situation.

In the roar, you may want to run. In the silence, you may want to call out. Do neither blindly.

## Turn back early

End the visit immediately for any of the following:

- A gas alarm, breathing difficulty, rising filter resistance, or a compromised suit seal.
- Loss of reliable communication or route finding.
- Failure of essential hearing protection or monitoring.
- Sound or vibration exposure approaching your predetermined limit.
- Disorientation, panic, nausea, or unusual fatigue.
- Signs of impending chorus-rain.
- An approaching hush.
- An injury that makes slow, precise movement difficult.
- Any realization that you are continuing merely because you have already come so far.

A rescue party would face the same air, noise, crowding, and navigation problems, and your shouts would not guide it. Self-rescue and early withdrawal matter more than heroic equipment.

On exit, keep your breathing protection on until you are clear of the swarm and contaminated dust. Use contained, two-way decontamination: do not carry films, spores, larvae, or shell fauna onward into another world, and do not introduce surface microbes into the Low Choir on a return visit. Check equipment seams and treads for stowaways. Treat persistent ringing ears, cough, headache, dizziness, or chest symptoms as possible exposure injuries, not inconveniences to ignore.

## What the visit will feel like

You will not stand at a viewpoint and comprehend the province. You will stand inside a limited circle of lamplight while an enormous country presses against your other senses. The floor will hiss and click beneath you. Bass will arrive through your boots before you recognize it as sound. Your partner’s faceplate may become obscured by wings. Your own breathing will seem unnaturally loud inside the mask, while your voice is lost outside it.

The most disturbing moment may not be the full chorus, but the instant it opens a hole around you and you hear one wet claw, one drop, and your own sleeve moving.

A successful human visit may therefore seem very small: a few careful minutes at a surveyed margin, no samples, no crossing, no attempt to join the choir, and an uneventful return. That is enough. The Low Choir does not need your light, your voice, or your footprints, and it offers no special reward to the visitor who stays until fear, curiosity, or exhaustion begins making decisions.
