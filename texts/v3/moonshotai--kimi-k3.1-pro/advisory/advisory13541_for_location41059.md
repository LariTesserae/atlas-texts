Prepare as though you were visiting an operating instrument at the edge of a fatal drop—not a conservatory, not a ruined garden, and certainly not a swimming pool.

The cistern’s attractions are also its traps. Clear water invites swimming. Apparently motionless engines invite you to assume they are switched off. The impossible water at the open sides invites touch. Cloud invites you to forget the height. Good preparation consists largely of refusing those invitations.

## Arrange the visit before you arrange your equipment

For an ordinary human, the best visit is **guided, mostly dry, and unhurried**. If the permitted experience is simply to stand in the entrance lock and look, that is not an inferior visit. The whole chamber can be understood without immersing yourself in it.

Before ascending:

- **Have someone qualified confirm that Header Nine is in normal operation.** In particular, the containment system should be known to be healthy, not merely quiet. You cannot assess the level engines by looking at their flywheels, listening at the door, or laying a hand on a rail.
- **Know the authorized route in and out**, including what to do if cloud arrives, if the tower must be evacuated, or if your guide gives an instruction you do not understand.
- **Do not go alone.** A companion is useful for altitude illness, vertigo, heat illness, and simple disorientation—but a companion is not a substitute for an industrial rescue plan.
- **Set your own schedule and turnaround time.** The room provides almost no useful sense of passing time. Do not wait for “something to happen” before leaving.
- Keep your return arrangements independent of the cistern’s unusual physics. The containment is a specific, continuously renewed property of the reservoir, not a general suspension of gravity and not a promise that the tower will protect you.

If nobody can confirm the operating state or provide safe access, do not improvise a visit around the containment system.

## Prepare for altitude, exposure, and two kinds of confinement

The plain being more than two kilometres below does not, by itself, establish the tower’s elevation above sea level. Find out the actual atmospheric conditions rather than guessing from the view. Until then, treat it as a potentially demanding high-altitude visit.

Acclimatize if necessary, carry personal medication, and know your own symptoms of altitude trouble: headache, nausea, unusual breathlessness, clumsiness, dizziness, or impaired judgment. The plants oxygenate the water; that is not a guarantee about the air your lungs will encounter. If you become unwell, descend rather than trying to recover in the warm chamber.

Bring an outer layer for the approach and return, even though the cistern itself is warm and humid. Wear secure, non-slip footwear wherever footwear is permitted. Cloud, wind, and exposed structure should be treated as ordinary industrial mountain hazards, notwithstanding the extraordinary water indoors.

Also consider your psychology. The High Cistern can produce **vertigo and claustrophobia in the same visit**. Its church-sized boundaries are visible, yet its interior is a crowded maze. Its sides are open to immense height, yet a swimmer would be hemmed in by leaves, roots, ceramic, and pipework. If either sensation begins to impair you, leave. There is no achievement in enduring it.

## What to bring—and what to leave outside

Useful possessions are simple:

- a watch or timer;
- any required communication or check-in device;
- drinking water for the approach, consumed outside the chamber;
- warm and protective clothing for the exposed portions of the visit;
- a notebook or camera, if permitted, used from a fixed safe position;
- clean, secure bags that prevent small objects from escaping.

Warm humidity can fog optics brought in from cooler air. Let camera equipment acclimatize rather than wiping it frantically or opening every seal immediately. A camera should be secured against being dropped, but not trailing straps into machinery or vegetation. Do not take it swimming merely because the water is photogenic.

Leave behind anything that could become chemistry or litter:

- food, crumbs, drinks, and sweets;
- sunscreen, lotions, soaps, and insect repellent—there are no insects to repel;
- loose jewelry, coins, offerings, and “harmless” organic souvenirs;
- pets, plants, seeds, or water from another world;
- unnecessary ropes, hooks, or tools.

A complete water exchange takes decades. A substance or object you casually introduce may therefore become a long-term constituent of Header Nine, not a brief visitor’s mess. Follow any cleaning or biological-quarantine procedure exactly. “No animals” does not mean “sterile,” and it does not mean the system cannot be altered by what you carry.

## At the threshold

Give yourself several minutes before doing anything.

The air will be humid and warm. The smell should be clean: tile, wet metal, vegetation, without the sweet or rotten notes you might associate with an abandoned greenhouse. The silence may at first seem like an absence of operation. It is not. Nor is it evidence that something has gone wrong. This is what successful operation sounds like.

Look before moving. From the entrance, the room’s smallness can make you overconfident: every outer boundary is visible, so it feels as though the whole place ought to be comprehensible. It is not. Forty paces across is still ample space in which to conceal a route, lose an exit behind vegetation, or mistake a maintenance clearance for an open passage.

If permitted, you may feel the engines as a faint pressure conducted through a fixed handrail. Do not investigate them further. Do not put chalk on a flywheel, test a piston, or touch a valve to see whether the system is “real.”

Above all, **do not touch the vertical water at the open sides merely to satisfy your curiosity**. There is no hidden pane of glass to discover. The description of the boundary is not an invitation to perform an experiment:

- It is not a safety wall.
- It is not a net.
- It is not evidence that a human body would be caught or supported.
- It holds the reservoir while the containment measurement holds; it makes no separate promise about you.
- Never push a tool, camera, rope, or other object through it.
- Never use an open side as an entrance, exit, or shortcut.

The fact that a withdrawn hand leaves only a temporary depression may make the act seem harmless. It is still an unnecessary disturbance of the machine keeping several metres of warm water beside an open fall.

When cloud rises, remain at a known safe position or follow the established exit route. Do not move along exposed edges in milk-white visibility. Cloud can make the chamber feel supported and intimate, but it has changed the view, not the drop. When it sinks, the altitude may return suddenly enough to cause vertigo even in someone who was comfortable moments before.

## Do not plan to swim

For most visitors, entering the water would be the single worst way to appreciate it.

Blood-warm, exceptionally clear water sounds forgiving. Here it is not. Open water exists mainly as maintenance clearance. A person may have to turn sideways between obstructions; roots and leaves can obscure the route; ceramic thresholds, crisp machinery, sumps, and tank walls produce an underwater topography with no soft natural margins. You cannot assume that every submerged position has a clear vertical path to breathable air.

The warmth also removes useful warnings. It will not brace you like cold water, and exertion in warm water and humid air can lead to overheating before you feel dramatically ill. Clear visibility may show you a gauge several metres away while telling you almost nothing about how to reach it safely.

If immersion is explicitly part of an authorized visit, treat it as **confined industrial water entry**, not recreation:

- use a site-specific guide, attendant, route, and rescue procedure;
- enter only through the approved point;
- use only the clean, close-fitting equipment specified for that system;
- do not improvise fins, loose scooters, dangling equipment, or your own entangling tether;
- do not force a new passage through vegetation or push roots aside to make one;
- do not grip valves, root shoes, pipes, or trellises for propulsion;
- do not dive alone or remain longer because you feel pleasantly weightless;
- leave at the first sign of overheating, dizziness, breathlessness, disorientation, or route uncertainty.

Do not drink the water. Its clarity and its status as a polishing reservoir do not make it a beverage, and the absence of animals does not make it sterile.

## Behave as though everything is new, because it is

The cistern may look overgrown from a distance, but it is not a ruin being reclaimed. Do not prune it into a more comfortable garden, clear leaves for a better photograph, climb the “hill” of a pump housing, or treat a pipe rack as an old jungle gym. The sharp ceramic, crisp bolts, readable valve numbers, and unchipped enamel are signs of youth, not inactivity.

Do not pick an ivory flower. Do not assist the brass pollination carriage. Do not feed anything, introduce an animal for company, or try to make the ecology more “natural.” Nothing has fled, and there is no absence for you to repair.

Likewise, do not expect the carriage, flywheels, buds, or drifting leaves to perform on a human timescale. A mechanism can be working perfectly while appearing parked. A visit that contains no visible event may be a completely successful visit.

## If something changes

You are not qualified to diagnose the containment system from its appearance. Do not wait for dramatic confirmation.

If the attendant orders an evacuation, if the installation’s operating status becomes uncertain, or if there is an unexpected alarm, structural disturbance, or water movement, leave promptly by the prescribed route. Do not approach an open side to look, do not return for equipment, and do not remain to record the failure. Nobody outside the system’s engineering knowledge can responsibly predict how quickly “gravity is willing to wait” might become an immediate fact.

## What the visit will actually be like

It will probably be quieter and less spectacular than you expect—and more affecting because of that.

There will be no blue abyss, luminous growth, wildlife, or gothic decay. Daylight will remain ordinary. White will be white on gauge faces, brass will be yellow, submerged stems will be dark red. On an overcast day, industrial lamps will simply switch on. The garden’s density will gradually stop looking like neglect and start looking like extraordinary precision: root bed, service number, brace, trellis, filter, and habitat occupying the same decisions.

You may hear your own breathing and become uncomfortably aware of your footsteps. A swimmer would be the loudest event in the room; even a dry visitor may feel like one. Speak only when needed. Do not whistle for an echo or tap the structure to make it respond.

Watch instead for small evidence of scale and duration: the mineral line drawn straight across the upper surface; a stencilled number seen through clear water; a root patiently fitted around a bolt; condensation swelling beside a glazing bar; the brass carriage apparently doing nothing. Do not expect a roof droplet to fall for you.

The strongest memory may be the contrast: a minute, crowded, blood-warm world held in a surveyed rectangle, with almost nothing beyond its handrails but height. You will be the quickest, loudest, most impatient process in the chamber.

So bring a watch, clean hands, an exit plan, and no expectation of entertainment. Leave with everything you brought, including the leaves and flowers you might have wanted to keep. Header Nine does not need to remember you. The best visit is one that does not interrupt its long agreement to continue.
