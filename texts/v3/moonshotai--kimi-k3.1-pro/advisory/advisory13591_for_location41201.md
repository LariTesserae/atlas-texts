# Preparing for a Visit to Vey

Approach Vey as a high-altitude pressure expedition into an unlit industrial reef, not as an ordinary city visit with unusually bad weather. Most of the place will not try to harm you. It simply will not organize itself around human eyesight, human hearing, human balance, or a human schedule.

An ordinary human can visit, but a first visit should be hosted, carefully equipped, and generous with rest. Do not make your initial descent a solo trek from the rim.

## Arrange the visit before choosing equipment

Secure a local host or guide who has actually accommodated unmodified humans. A “pressure habitat” is not necessarily human-compatible merely because it holds pressure. Confirm, in advance:

- A reception habitat with a suitable pressure, gas mixture, temperature, and humidity.
- Sleeping quarters with meaningful acoustic protection, while still delivering evacuation and structural alarms.
- Human-safe food, drinking water, sanitation, and medical support.
- Suit servicing, brine decontamination, and a place to clear fouled filters and faceplates.
- Which routes are currently usable, and which are closed by construction, deformation, reservoir operations, or animals.
- How you will summon help if your guide, communications, or navigation system fails.

If your arrival method permits, come directly to a staffed reception facility rather than materializing or landing on the exposed plateau. The rim offers little visual reward and considerable opportunity to begin your visit exhausted, lost, cold, and damaged.

The five-kilometer elevation matters, but acclimatization alone is not a preparation plan. Obtain medical advice about altitude, pressure transitions, cold exposure, and exertion in a heavy suit. Pre-acclimatization may help with transfers; it does not make an unsuitable atmosphere breathable or replace staged pressurization.

Also assess the less obvious stresses. Vey combines darkness, confinement, continuous noise, vibration, and occasional uncertainty about which way is upright. Travelers with significant vestibular disorders, sound sensitivity, severe claustrophobia, or a strong dependence on visual orientation should consider limiting themselves to hosted interiors. Do not expect to solve these problems through determination once the chords begin calling.

Keep the itinerary flexible. If a street has been closed by scutes, it may remain closed for decades. If a reservoir route is unavailable because of a chord, that is not a delay you can reasonably wait out. Have alternative routes and enough supplies for an extended stay, but do not confuse an extended stay with waiting for local conditions to become convenient.

## Use locally approved equipment

An excellent suit from another world may still be entirely wrong for Vey. Rent or have your equipment inspected locally whenever possible.

### Pressure and exposure protection

Treat every uncertified exterior space as pressure-suit territory. Your suit should provide:

- Pressure integrity and an emergency bailout supply appropriate to the distance from refuge.
- Heating and power reserves for delays, not merely for the planned excursion.
- Resistance to cuts from basalt, volcanic glass, and exposed salt edges.
- Seals and outer materials compatible with prolonged exposure to concentrated local brine.
- Protection against both external cold and internal condensation.
- Gloves and boots that allow careful movement without encouraging you to grip or climb arbitrary surfaces.

Use local traction equipment rather than assuming mountaineering crampons are appropriate. They may perform badly on inhabited ceramic, damage construction surfaces, or injure the animals coating them.

Carry a pressure patch kit and know how to use it with gloves on. Practice until a torn outer layer or damaged seal does not immediately become a panic problem.

### Faceplate and filter management

The sleet will be one of your most persistent practical problems. It is alive, but it will still obscure your faceplate, cling to seams, and foul equipment.

You need an approved way to clear the faceplate without opening the suit, along with suitable intake protection and spare filter elements where applicable. Practice clearing your view while stationary, then while following a guide’s instructions. If you cannot see and are unsure of your footing, stop before attempting to walk.

Do not improvise with solvents, deliberate overheating, biocidal coatings, or dumping accumulated animals into a drain. Use the local clearing and disposal procedure.

### Hearing, communication, and alarms

Ordinary earplugs are inadequate. They may reduce some damaging sound while also removing warning tones, and they do little for the components of a chord call felt through your chest and skeleton.

Use an integrated system that provides:

- Suitable hearing protection.
- Helmet or bone-contact communication with your guide.
- Access to low warning tones and other signals you might otherwise filter out.
- Visual displays inside the helmet and tactile alarms for situations in which audio is unreliable.
- A way to distinguish routine district sound from an alarm without simply turning everything down.

Do not rely on consumer noise cancellation as your only protection. A system that makes Vey seem quieter by deleting information you need is not making you safer.

Your sleeping refuge matters as much as your helmet. A chord call may last for days, and its physical effects cannot necessarily be slept through. Do not try to create your own quiet room by hanging blankets or acoustic foam over walls. In Vey, making a wall absorb too much sound may turn part of the city’s locating system into a hazard.

### Navigation and light

Carry locally compatible field imaging, a registered locating beacon or transponder, and at least two independent ways to receive your route and alarms. You should be able to identify your guide’s signal, your refuge, and the general evacuation instruction without improvising.

Do not wander off intending to learn the district echoes as you go. That is a citizen’s skill, not a weekend hobby.

Likewise, do not assume that a powerful headlamp is either welcome or useful. Vey’s darkness is not evidence of abandonment, and its sparse lighting is not a request for visitors to illuminate it. Use enclosed displays and permitted, shielded task lighting. Ask before using anything that casts visible light into public space. Avoid unapproved active sonar, drones, loud signaling devices, and recorded playback; they may interfere with navigation, loom diagnostics, or animals as well as etiquette.

## Learn to move slowly in a place that is already slow

Your first excursions should be short. The fatigue may surprise you. It will come not only from cold or altitude, but from continuously interpreting a place through mediated senses.

The visual scene may be nearly empty while your suit reports a crowded street. Rain will tick against you like weather and then accumulate like living fouling. A step or handrail may shift slightly because it is coated in scutes. A sound may seem to come from the whole basin rather than from a direction. You will be tempted to stop interpreting and simply push forward. That is usually when mistakes happen.

Take regular refuge breaks. Eat, drink, check your suit, and leave enough reserve to return while tired. Maintain a deliberate sleep and meal schedule; there will be no dawn to reset one for you.

If you become nauseated, disoriented, unable to judge your balance, or unusually irritable and careless, treat that as an exposure problem. Do not wait for a chord to finish or for the vibration to become pleasant. Move to a suitable refuge under guidance.

Never shout to make yourself locatable. Use your beacon and communications. If a loom’s note or the surrounding structural resonance changes in a way your guide treats as significant, leave or report it as instructed. Do not approach to investigate the discord.

## Treat terrain and water as unresolved systems

Outside the maintained city routes, expect raw edges, glazed plates, unsupported-looking surfaces, and cracks whose continuation is unknown. There are no mature river valleys to provide intuitive routes.

In particular:

- Do not descend by following water. A provisional stream may sheet across a plate, vanish into a fault angle, or enter a route no person can follow.
- Do not trust a pool because its surface is black, skinned over, or apparently solid.
- Do not assume city brine is safe merely because it is liquid. It is not drinking water, and immersion may involve cold, chemistry, depth, machinery, and pressure hazards simultaneously.
- Keep clear of intakes, pressure shafts, locks, and unauthorized canal crossings. An intake’s continuous song is not an invitation to approach.
- Be cautious at the heat boundary. A route may move repeatedly between liquid brine, glaze, and ice accumulation, including on your own equipment.
- Do not use a dull heat-exchanger seam as a hand warmer or comforting landmark. It may indicate a failure.

For most visitors, an exposed rim excursion is unnecessary. From above there may be little more than wet black rock, cloud, and an occasional shielded ember. The basin’s grandeur is primarily physical and acoustic, not panoramic.

## Do not explore the unfinished city

“Unfinished” does not mean abandoned, harmless, or available for climbing.

A staircase ending in dark air is still an open edge, even if its warning tone has been sounding for generations. An arch that has remained a finger’s breadth from its footing for two centuries is not a harmless curiosity you can duck beneath. A slender structure may be carrying load in ways that are not visually intuitive, especially where a loom field remains involved.

Enter active construction volumes only under explicit authorization, and normally observe from outside them. Do not cross deposition boundaries, touch feedstock, handle vertically suspended moisture, attach lines to structural elements, or add equipment to a surface whose living and structural loads you do not understand.

Patient structural failure is still failure. A district that has been deforming for a generation is not therefore safe for a brief visit.

## Remember that the animals are also infrastructure

Vey’s wildlife should not be treated as a collection of pests, monsters, or tame attractions.

The **sleet** will probably cause you more inconvenience than danger, but the contact is continuous. Prepare psychologically for being struck and coated by tiny animals. Clear them according to local procedure rather than crushing, heating, or chemically repelling them.

The **scutes** may make apparently solid architecture shift under your hand. Stay on routes your guide identifies. Do not pry them apart to see what lives underneath, scrape them from steps to make a path, or remove them from an arch to “lighten” it. Their weight is already an engineering concern; their activities also help keep surfaces and drains functional. Neither their removal nor their redistribution is a visitor’s job.

The **chords** are immense, slow, and acoustically overwhelming. Do not enter a cistern, lock, or reservoir route to approach one. Their slowness does not make water displacement, confined spaces, or resonance harmless. Never play back recordings of their calls as an experiment.

Avoid pump intakes, carcasses, and exposed ceramic borings. A carcass too large to remove quickly may contain an entire transient community, not merely an interesting specimen.

More generally, do not feed anything, discard food outdoors, take biological samples, or operate water gates and valves without authorization. Eating outside can release organic dust into a system whose animals and intakes already respond to it. A small action may have consequences out of proportion to your size.

Because Vey has no soil, vegetation, or rich competing ecology to buffer casual additions, arrive with equipment cleaned of foreign soil and organic matter. Use the city’s biological-control procedures on arrival and departure. Do not improvise sterilization by pouring biocide into local water, and do not carry sleet, brine, or microbial film home in your suit seams.

## What is worth doing there

Do not spend the whole visit trying to make Vey resemble a conventional sightseeing destination.

From approved shelters, the outer dams may offer the closest thing to a visual spectacle: clear ice stacked like curtains on one side, dark liquid water crawling downward on the other. A loom observed from a safe distance can give some sense of construction as deposition rather than assembly. Municipal maps are also revealing: a watercourse promoted from *provisional* to *incipient* tells you more about Vey’s timescale than a monument would.

A guided acoustic interpretation, with suitable protection, may be the city’s deepest experience. You are not simply hearing a large animal. You are hearing open water, stacked dwellings, machinery, and stone become parts of one temporary response.

Plan short outings with substantial recovery between them. Keep some private, enclosed illumination if you need it for human comfort, but do not expect public avenues to brighten for you. Do not wait for clear cloud, visible stars, a dawn, an end to a condensation cycle, or a convenient pause in the city’s sound.

## If something goes wrong

Register your excursions and check in as your host requires. Carry reserve air, power, heat, water, and communications appropriate to the route.

If your faceplate becomes obscured, stop before stepping blindly. If your suit loses pressure or thermal control, that takes priority over every appointment and observation. If separated from your guide, use your beacon and established emergency procedure; do not navigate toward the loudest sound, downhill along water, or toward a convenient heat seam. If your present position is safe, remaining at a known, mapped location may be better than improvising through streets, scutes, and unfinished edges.

Above all, do not run merely because your senses are overwhelmed. Vey itself is rarely in a hurry. You will usually be safer if you avoid becoming the fastest and least informed object in the vicinity.

Five rules summarize the visit:

1. **Dark does not mean deserted.**
2. **Slow does not mean safe.**
3. **Unfinished does not mean abandoned.**
4. **Water is infrastructure, not scenery.**
5. **Animals are inhabitants and maintenance systems, not weather or debris.**

You may leave Vey with very few conventional photographs. You are more likely to remember the pavement shifting beneath your glove, the need to clear living weather from your faceplate, and a voice too low and too large to locate making the city answer around you. That is not a failed sightseeing trip. It is what Vey offers.

A successful visit is one in which you return with your hearing, pressure integrity, and sense of balance intact—and in which the water, looms, and living load have no reason to notice that you were ever there.
