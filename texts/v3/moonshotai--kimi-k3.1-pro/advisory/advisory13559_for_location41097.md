# Preparing for the Drowned Nave

Prepare as though you are visiting an active geological process, a cliff made of glass, and an unreliable map simultaneously—not as though you are going to a warm mountain lake.

For an ordinary, unmodified human, the prudent visit is a **short rim reconnaissance with a predecided retreat**, using remote instruments for anything below the water. If your purpose requires swimming to the bottom, change the purpose. There may be no consistent bottom to reach, and pressure can become abyssal before the descent looks sufficiently serious to alarm you.

## 1. Decide what kind of visit you are willing to have

Do not make your first objective a complete circuit, a water crossing, an exploration of the “ruin,” or a search for the source of the sound. Choose something small and explicit:

- Reach the lowest-risk accessible rim viewpoint.
- Observe the standing meniscus and the enclosure.
- Make a brief instrumental record, if that is your work.
- Return by the approach route without entering the basin.

The basin’s small footprint is misleading. A place that can be walked around in an hour under ideal geography may still be impossible to cross safely once a route, wall, or sequence of events stops agreeing with itself.

Treat the mountain approach as a separate expedition. Until local knowledge proves otherwise, prepare for a high, wet, cold ascent through cloud, followed by abrupt heat and humidity near the basin. Acclimatize if the altitude warrants it. Camp and sleep below the elevation where the roar is felt in the body, not merely below where it becomes uncomfortable to hear.

Leave an itinerary and a hard overdue time with someone outside the expedition. A companion at the Nave is valuable for noticing confusion, injury, or withdrawal, but **a companion is not a rescue system**. No one should proceed on the assumption that a swimmer, climber, or vehicle can safely be sent in after them.

If your multiverse transit provides emergency extraction, test its limitations elsewhere. Do not assume that a transit aperture, beacon, or return coordinate will remain meaningful during a failure of near and far, before and after, or depth and descent.

## 2. Prepare for the sound before you prepare for the water

The sound will dominate the visit more completely than the view.

Use properly fitted earplugs under compatible, high-quality hearing protectors, and put them on before reaching the zone where the roar becomes bodily. A helmet, eye protection, and hearing protection must fit together without gaps or pressure points. Do not use music, noise-cancelling consumer headphones, or shouted radio traffic as substitutes.

Understand the limit of this equipment: it may reduce injury to your ears, but it cannot stop vibration arriving through rock, water, teeth, sternum, and skull. It does not create a safe exposure duration. Headache, nausea, vertigo, chest discomfort, visual disturbance, disorientation, muffled hearing afterward, or a sense that your thoughts are coming apart are all reasons to end the visit—not obstacles to push through.

Because speech will be irrelevant:

- Rehearse a very small set of hand and light signals: **stop, look at me, retreat now,** and **I cannot continue**.
- Practice them while wearing gloves, eye protection, and hearing protection.
- Use a vibrating timer or watch alarm; an audible alarm may be useless.
- Make all consequential decisions before reaching the rim. There will be no quiet interval in which to debate them.
- Keep one member of the party watching the retreat and the other people, rather than allowing everyone to become absorbed in photography or instruments.

Rehearse simple tasks at camp. If opening a pack, checking a companion, or turning back is confusing there, it will not become easier when your bones are being used as sounding boards.

Anyone with significant cardiac, pulmonary, ear or sinus, balance, heat-intolerance, or altitude-related vulnerabilities should regard remote observation as the default. Every member of a rim party should be capable of walking back out; an unconscious or non-ambulatory casualty inside the basin presents a problem for which ordinary preparation offers no good answer.

## 3. Dress for cold approach, hot rim, and cutting stone

Bring ordinary mountain equipment for the ascent: insulation kept dry, weather protection, navigation appropriate to the approach, emergency shelter, food, clean drinking water, and a first-aid kit that can manage severe cuts, burns, and eye injuries.

At the rim, add or emphasize:

- A secured helmet.
- Clear eye protection; the light is too poor for casually tinted lenses.
- Stiff, supportive boots with soles suitable for broken volcanic rock.
- Tough gloves and abrasion-resistant clothing that will not become intolerable in humid heat.
- Two protected lamps, even for a noon visit.
- Secure, streamlined carrying: no loose straps, dangling instruments, or objects you would be tempted to chase if dropped.

Do not sit, kneel, or brace a hand without inspecting the surface. The absence of rounded cobbles and sediment means there are few forgiving places to rest. Fresh faces and scree can cut through an incautious hand or knee, and white salt or ochre sulphur deposits may shear away precisely where someone would naturally seek a hold.

The rim’s warmth does not make the water potable or the air automatically benign. Carry your own water and salts, and manage layers before sweat leaves you chilled on the descent. Because this is a hot, sulphurous, fractured environment, volcanic gas is an uncertainty worth screening with appropriate instruments if you have the expertise. Smell is not a gas monitor, and a respirator does not remedy oxygen deficiency.

## 4. Do not let the water’s warmth invite you in

The open basin may be pleasantly warm while water immediately above a fracture can scald. Temperature cannot be inferred from the general comfort of the surface, and a hand is not an acceptable thermometer.

Keep back from the exposed meniscus. The water already stands above the rim; dry stone is not proof that you are above every possible water surface. Sheets can peel away and collapse, cavities can close with enormous force, and water can return against the undersides of ledges. Do not shelter beneath an overhang to avoid spray.

A line of bubbles is not necessarily a harmless vent or an interesting photographic subject. It may be the first visible stage of a glass fin appearing where there was open water a breath earlier. Do not approach to inspect it.

Most importantly, **do not treat swimming ability or recreational scuba as preparation**:

- There are no beaches or easy exits.
- The surface itself can cease to behave like a surface.
- Bubbles cannot always be trusted to show up.
- A depth gauge may be reporting pressure, not a dependable geometric depth.
- A short descent can produce an ear injury or crushing pressure long before an ordinary diver’s instincts say “abyss.”
- A tether can be cut, and even an intact tether may not constitute a sensible route during a spatial failure.

For underwater observation, use a sacrificial remote probe where possible. Log absolute pressure and temperature independently rather than converting everything into a reassuring depth profile. Expect to lose equipment, and do not put a human body at the other end of a taut retrieval line merely to avoid losing it.

If a crewed descent is genuinely indispensable, it belongs to a specialist engineering expedition involving pressure protection far beyond scuba, heat and impact protection, and navigation that does not depend on local geometry. Even then, the Nave offers no basis for calling the attempt safe.

## 5. Give the false shelter no authority over you

The horseshoe of basalt, its steps, the squared block, and the threshold-like stones will exert a strong psychological pull. They look like evidence that someone once understood the place, and therefore like a promise that it can be understood.

Do not use them as shelter, anchors, stairs, seats, or proof of a stable route. The lee is only where a buttress **most often** forms. The masonry may become straight fractures in bedrock. The wall does nothing against the roar, and the stones have no demonstrated past as human construction.

Likewise, do not clear a campsite, build cairns, leave offerings, or rearrange the stones to improve the resemblance to architecture. The Nave has not necessarily issued an invitation merely because it has produced the outline of a door.

## 6. Conduct at the rim

A disciplined first visit should look almost disappointingly brief:

1. **Arrive already protected.** Do not wait until the sound or fragments become intolerable before putting on equipment.
2. **Stop short of the most dramatic viewpoint.** Identify the least exposed available ground and the shortest practical way back toward your approach. Avoid fresh fracture lines, hot seams, overhangs, standing excess water, and the false shelter.
3. **Keep the visit single-purpose.** Take the observation or reading you came for. Do not let a camera viewfinder consume your peripheral awareness.
4. **Do not extend the stay because the scene becomes visually quiet.** A few black ribs and empty water can make the Nave look like an abandoned room while the mountain continues to roar underneath it.
5. **Leave after the first significant anomaly, nearby structural failure, equipment failure, or bodily symptom.** Do not wait for a second event to establish a pattern.

Aborting does not always mean instantly running. Running across glass scree while distance or verticality is uncertain can be more dangerous than pausing. The decision to leave should be immediate; the physical retreat should be as controlled as the immediate rock, heat, and water hazards permit.

## 7. If a relationship fails

There is no universal maneuver that makes the Nave coherent again. Your aim is to avoid adding a human commitment to the failure.

### Stop making new commitments

Do not jump, dive, squeeze through a new opening, step toward an apparently closer wall, or reach out to test whether it can really be touched. Do not follow descending bubbles, an apparent bottom, a repeated mineral seam, or a remembered number of steps.

### Check the ordinary dangers first

Warmth, pressure, salt, hard stone, and your own vulnerability remain normal. Check footing, nearby fractures, hot spray, airway, companion condition, and pain or pressure in your ears and chest. A local failure of geography does not make a burn less hot or a fall less injurious.

### Do not resolve contradictions by choosing the most comforting interpretation

If vision, bodily sensation, and instruments disagree, do not decide that the instrument must be broken simply because the visible route looks easy. The outer boundary is the best general reference the basin offers, but it is not an absolute guarantee. Keep movements small and avoid interior landmarks as proof of location.

### Retreat when a physically coherent route permits

Do not wait for the original arrangement to be restored exactly. The returning relationship may leave a fin displaced, a step added, or the bottom once more beyond reach. “Back to normal” may not mean “back to where we were.”

### Do not build a forecasting system from one strange event

A pressure in the chest preceding a cliff split may be an echo arriving early. It is not a dependable countdown. The transparent, eyeless swimmer may move just before major concussions, but it appears to use a pressure-hollow and an order of events that your body cannot assume it shares. Never chase it or use it as a guide.

Above all, do not panic-search for the one law that has failed while ignoring falling stone or heat. The Nave’s selective normality is part of the danger: **you remain an ordinary body having ordinary injuries while the relationships around you become unreliable.**

## 8. Record honestly and leave the sparse life alone

If you are conducting research, preserve raw observations rather than forcing them into a smooth map. Keep instrument logs and human recollections distinct. Write personal impressions afterward at camp, and label uncertainties in sequence and location instead of silently correcting them. A photograph may be useful evidence of a surface while being useless evidence of distance.

Because you are traveling between worlds, use clean equipment and observe whatever biological isolation your transit allows. Do not wash gear, yourself, or samples in the basin. The olive films, wine-dark threads, and chalk-white snails are so sparse that a seemingly insignificant patch could matter greatly to the local ecology. Do not graze a boot across a film, collect the one visible snail, or assume that a similar clump appearing on a new ledge makes life here abundant.

Take no casual souvenirs. Pack out everything you bring, but do not enter the water to retrieve something dropped. At some point, equipment must be treated as lost rather than pursued.

After leaving, check everyone for cuts, burns, heat illness, hearing changes, balance problems, headache, and confusion before resuming a difficult descent. Persistent tinnitus or muffled hearing, ear pain, vertigo, respiratory symptoms, or neurological complaints warrant medical assessment. Rest and compare accounts below the roar; do not make an immediate second visit merely to confirm that the first one really happened.

## The essential preparation

The best-prepared traveler will appear overequipped and underambitious. They will bring mountain clothing to a blood-warm place, hearing protection that cannot make it quiet, lamps for noon, and a retreat plan for a basin small enough to see all at once. They will not bring a swimsuit, trust the shelter, follow the bubbles, or wait for silence.

Go prepared to leave while your curiosity is still arguing for another minute. The Nave’s most seductive intervals are those in which the eyes briefly mistake it for an empty, austere room. Nothing has settled. Nothing has agreed to remain a shore, a bottom, or a wall. Your safety lies not in learning to feel comfortable there, but in retaining a simple purpose and the willingness to depart before the place requires you to understand it.
