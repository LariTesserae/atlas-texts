# Preparing for the High Cistern

Think of the High Cistern as a vast, inhabited waterworks at altitude, not as a mountain resort. A healthy human should not need climbing equipment or routine supplemental oxygen, but this is also not a destination for an improvised warm-weather holiday. Your main difficulties are likely to be thin air on long stairs, persistent damp, glare, noise, unfamiliar food, complicated vertical routes, and delays that nobody else regards as extraordinary.

The most useful comforts you can arrange are simple: **a dry upper room, a reliable source of potable water, good footwear, maintained window screens, and enough time to wait.**

## Before you travel

### Make an altitude plan

The lowest streets are nearly three kilometres above the lowlands, and some destinations lie higher still. If your route permits, acclimatize at an intermediate elevation rather than transferring directly from sea level. If you must arrive directly, keep the first day or two deliberately light: no cross-ward hikes, industrial tours, heavy luggage hauling, or celebratory exertion.

Warm weather can make the altitude easy to forget. You may feel comfortably warm while becoming dehydrated, sunburned, or unusually breathless on stairs.

If you have heart or lung disease, or have previously suffered serious altitude illness, obtain medical advice beforehand. Carry your usual prescriptions and any altitude medication only as advised by your own clinician. Know how you would descend or evacuate; a tightly scheduled return transfer is not an emergency plan.

A mild headache or unusual fatigue deserves rest and attention. Worsening symptoms, vomiting, confusion, inability to walk steadily, or breathlessness while resting are not something to endure for the sake of the itinerary. Get help and descend or evacuate.

### Plan by district, not by the whole city

The city takes weeks to cross. For a first visit, choose one principal ward and perhaps one excursion to a reservoir district, factory range, or escarpment. Do not schedule appointments on opposite sides of the city as though they were across an ordinary town.

Arrange a local host, guide, or reliable interpreter if you need one. Ask about:

- planned sluice work, reservoir stages, and major machinery overhauls;
- herd routes and whether a crossing is expected;
- which streets, stairs, and tram connections are currently usable;
- which public taps are actually potable;
- local alarms and the signals for leaving low arcades;
- the weather at your destination, not merely outside your window.

Clear sunlight in one ward tells you very little about another. Reservoir fog may remain for the day, and a herd crossing can stop transport for longer than your entire planned visit.

Addresses and directions may need a vertical component. A two-dimensional map can put you directly above or below your destination with no obvious connection between them. Write routes down in local terms—ward, level, stair, arcade, bridge, and landmark—and do not rely exclusively on your home positioning or communication systems.

### Choose accommodation carefully

Wealth in the High Cistern is expressed in dry upper rooms, clear water, good tile, and well-fitted metalwork. These are also excellent practical criteria for lodging.

Ask for a room that is:

- above the sacrificial or sluiced ground-floor level;
- ventilated but properly screened;
- not immediately beside a crowded roost, compressor house, or major pump if you are sensitive to ammonia or noise;
- served by maintained overflows and drains;
- accessible without repeatedly crossing an active herd route or industrial loading court.

A cheap ground-floor room may be cheap because water and animals are expected to pass through its surroundings.

Bring several days’ reserve of essential medication and some food, not because the city lacks supplies, but because your particular route may pause. Keep your lodging reservation and exit arrangements flexible.

## What to pack

For your body, favour **drainage, ventilation, and drying**. For documents and electronics, favour thorough waterproofing.

- **Clothing:** lightweight, quick-drying garments you already know do not chafe you; spare socks; a breathable hooded rain layer; and one light insulating layer for exposed escarpments, wet evenings, or overly vigorous air-conditioning in a dry room. Do not expect to buy familiar textiles locally. Heavy rubber clothing will probably make you wetter from sweat.
- **Footwear:** closed-toe shoes or boots with excellent wet-stone grip, plus a second pair so one can dry. Soles should tolerate dished stair treads, slimy microbial films, snail-covered channel margins, and wet iron. Do not plan to wade or walk barefoot.
- **Skin and foot care:** sunscreen, lip protection, chafing treatment, foot powder or an equivalent drying aid, and supplies for treating small blisters early. Inspect your feet daily. Skin that never quite dries can turn a minor rub into a major nuisance.
- **Sun and glare protection:** a hat and high-quality UV sunglasses. At noon, wet rails and white limestone can be genuinely painful to look at.
- **Waterproof storage:** a pack liner, dry bags, corrosion-resistant cases, and separate protection for medicines and documents. Include a way to isolate wet clothing from dry clothing.
- **Health supplies:** your normal first-aid kit, oral rehydration salts, personal medications, and a water-treatment method you understand. Treatment is a backup, not permission to drink from industrial channels; a filter suitable for microbes may do nothing about chemical contamination.
- **Food:** enough familiar, stable food for arrival and delays. For a long stay, plan deliberately for dietary fibre, micronutrients, and any staples you require. There is no reason to assume fresh plant foods will be readily available.
- **Sleep equipment:** earplugs, and perhaps an eye mask. Use earplugs only after arranging how you will be alerted to an alarm or called by your host.
- **Lighting and power:** a small waterproof headlamp, spare power, and no assumption that local outlets or voltages will match your equipment.
- **Industrial visits:** your own well-fitting hearing protection is worthwhile. Use any additional eye, head, or respiratory protection required by an authorized guide. A particle mask may help with some dust or soot; it does **not** make a gas-filled sump or oxygen-poor culvert safe.

You do not need an expedition’s worth of rope, cold-weather equipment, or mountaineering hardware. You do need equipment that still functions after being damp for a week.

## Your first day in the city

After arriving, do little beyond learning your immediate surroundings.

Locate the potable water point, the nearest upper-level refuge from a water release, the maintained stairs, and the place where local notices or instructions are given. Ask your host which sounds are ordinary and which require action. The city’s soundscape is too rich for guesswork: pumps, pipes, wet insulators, animals, trams, and bell-throats can all produce noises that initially seem significant.

Take a short walk. Let your breathing and feet teach you the place. Notice how rounded the thresholds are, how the centres of steps have hollowed, and how streets can become shallow troughs. White or ochre tufa is not necessarily a secure handhold. A dark, slick film may be the local ecological equivalent of pasture, but it is still treacherous underfoot.

Do not “improve” your room by covering an overflow, stuffing a vent, or laying luggage over a drain. The local rule—**do not seal; drain**—is hard-won building knowledge. If water is going somewhere unacceptable, tell maintenance rather than diverting it into a wall or a neighbouring room.

## Moving around safely

### Treat water releases as more important than rain

The rain is often warm and gentle. The sudden movement of impounded water may not be.

When sluices are being worked or a release is expected, leave low arcades, culvert margins, and street troughs. Do not remain to watch the animals pour through. An interesting surge of eels, crustaceans, and debris is also a good reason not to be standing at ankle level.

Never enter a culvert, sump, basement cutting, or unused industrial channel as a shortcut. Beyond water and animals, such places may present confined-space, electrical, gas, or structural hazards. If you would not enter an unfamiliar sewer or pumping station at home, do not enter a more biologically active one here.

### Give the bell-throats absolute priority

An avenue descending directly into water may be deliberate. An unusually wide gate or high arch is not wasted space. These are signs that the street plan has already conceded something to the herds.

When a herd crosses:

- stop and follow local instructions;
- do not try to slip between animals to save time;
- do not approach calves or juveniles in canals;
- do not stand between an animal and its apparent route to water;
- do not block a submerged avenue for photography;
- expect the interruption to last, possibly for days.

At reservoir and quarry margins, keep well back unless on an authorized route. A slate-dark back shedding water can initially resemble part of the bank. The animals’ deliberate movement does not make their mass harmless.

Their night calls are normally part of the city, not an alarm. Treat a crossing as weather or a road closure, not as an invasion requiring your assistance.

### Do not mistake administered space for wilderness

Reservoirs, catchment slopes, animal shallows, quarries, and flooded workings are not parks merely because they are open. They have gauges, service roads, access stairs, and maintenance offices because they are part of the city’s operation. Do not swim, picnic, climb quarry faces, tamper with gauges, or use service roads as scenic shortcuts without permission.

Likewise, an abandoned ward is not an empty, quiet museum. It is more likely to be a sump, a roost, unstable masonry, and an increasing concentration of animals. Urban exploration is a poor reason to enter one.

## Water, food, rooms, and animals

Good water exists, and clear running water is prized. Use designated potable supplies and ask whether their status changes with maintenance or reservoir conditions. Do not drink from a fountain simply because it looks clean.

Above all, remember that **fertile is not the same as wholesome**. An outfall rich in wastewater and animal refuse may support extraordinary quantities of life. That does not make its eels, snails, films, or water suitable for an informal meal. Do not forage from channels or outfalls.

Eat initially at establishments accustomed to feeding people, favour properly cooked food, and introduce unfamiliar proteins gradually if you are sensitive. Make allergies and ingredient requirements explicit. Eel, snail, herd meat, rendered oil, microbial curds, and vat-protein cakes can provide a substantial diet, but you should not assume they automatically satisfy your long-term nutritional needs or dietary restrictions.

In your room:

- keep screens closed and report damage;
- keep food in closed containers and dispose of scraps properly;
- keep luggage closed and off the floor where practical;
- do not leave an unused doorway or vent propped open;
- do not attempt to evict roosting animals yourself.

An unused space is available habitat. Your open suitcase, dry cupboard, or abandoned storage alcove may be considered in that light.

Use designated waste systems. Do not wash soap into channels, pour oil or chemicals into drains, or feed animals with travel rations. Small additions can have disproportionate effects where the food web is already responding eagerly to nutrient-rich water.

## Around industry and maintenance

Visit factories and pumping stations only with authorization. Outside, a great pump may appear contemplative: a pause, a compression, a stroke, and a long answer in the main. That apparent slowness conceals enormous stored force. Never cross a guard, touch a flywheel, step into a machine’s apparent “resting” position, or assume a paused mechanism is inactive.

Inside, use hearing protection and follow instructions exactly. Keep clear of wet electrical installations, vapour, unguarded drops, and loading routes. If an industrial visit requires equipment or training you do not have, admire the machinery from outside.

Do not scrape tufa for a souvenir, remove shells from infrastructure, clean away microbial films, or adjust a troublesome tap. Maintenance crews are not picturesque remnants of an older economy; their endless scraping, dredging, patching, and culling are what keep the city usable. Their work has priority over your photograph and your schedule.

## Prepare yourself psychologically

The absence of green may affect you more than expected. There are no shaded tree-lined streets, no grassy verge on which to sit, and no abandoned district quietly becoming a forest. Stone, brick, iron, animal bodies, ochre films, and mineral deposits dominate the eye. After rain, the smells of lime, oil, iron, musk, and ammonia can layer together. Your skin may remain damp, and true silence may never come.

Bring a few familiar, nonperishable comforts. Schedule time in a dry room. Do not try to compensate for initial discomfort by seeing more each day.

Also leave behind any expectation that efficiency means speed. A bridge described as “new” may be generations old. A herd may take days to pass. A repair may restore the city to almost exactly the form it had before. This is not stagnation or passivity; it is continual labour directed toward keeping a settled form available.

Finally, practise multiversal biosecurity. Unless explicitly cleared, do not bring seeds, soil, live food, or other ecological introductions. Do not take home live snails, films, or ajar of seep water as a curiosity. Clean and dry footwear, cuffs, and containers before departure in both directions.

The visitor’s version of the surveyors’ rule might be:

**Safe water and footing first; waterworks and herds second; your itinerary third.**

Travel supplied enough to wait, lightly enough to move when instructed, and humbly enough not to interfere. Once you have adjusted, the reward is not merely the view from the western escarpment. It is understanding that the pump notes rising through the evening air, and the bell-throats answering from unseen reservoirs, are not background atmosphere. They are the sound of the city continuing to work.
