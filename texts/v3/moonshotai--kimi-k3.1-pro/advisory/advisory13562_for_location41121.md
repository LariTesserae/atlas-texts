## Preparing for the Ninth Cold Basin

Treat the basin as **an operating industrial plant with wilderness-scale distances**, not as a cavern containing machinery. An ordinary human can visit, but the safe unit of travel is not the hike; it is the escorted movement between known, occupied refuges.

The visit will probably feel deceptively peaceful at first. You will walk through long darkness with cold coming through your boots, hearing water and machinery whose locations you cannot determine. Serial numbers, layout paint, and sharp casting edges will make the place look newly completed and therefore controlled. Then a pump loads, fog obscures the ridges, or a Flush reveals channels across floor you thought you understood. Preparation should address that changeability, not merely the cold.

### 1. Arrange local support before arrival

For a first visit, do not plan a solo exploration, an overnight camp, the full twelve-kilometre traverse, or a trip to the construction face. Arrange entry through a staffed pressure lock and ask for an operations escort who knows the sectors you will cross.

Before entering, obtain:

- **A current route plan**, including pressure zones, designated refuges, warming points, ladders, and at least one alternative exit. Ask what has changed since the last survey. The drawings may describe the structure accurately while still missing water connections.
- **The operating-phase forecast**, without treating it as a guarantee. Know which parts of your route become unusable during Draw, Weep, or Flush.
- **Alarm and communications training.** Learn the Flush horn, the all-clear, visual warning signals, radio procedure, and the hand signals to use when noise or hearing protection prevents speech.
- **The exact local response to sudden silence.** Do not settle for the general explanation that a pump has stopped. Ask where pressure and water may go in your sector, which refuge to use, and which doors must not be operated.
- **Atmospheric entry requirements.** Confirm that the air has been tested and whether a site-calibrated personal monitor is required. A generic filter mask is not universal protection against an industrial release.
- **A check-in, check-out, and overdue-rescue procedure.** Your conventional exit should not depend on your multiverse transport returning punctually or your personal communicator penetrating the pressure hull.

Also verify practical matters the landscape description does not settle: actual temperature range, pressure-equalization procedure, required clothing standards, and whether your batteries, electronics, and medical supplies tolerate the conditions.

If local escort, atmospheric clearance, and a verified exit cannot be arranged, postpone the visit. Personal equipment does not compensate for those omissions.

### 2. Dress for water above, cold below, and sharp edges everywhere

There is no single basin temperature to dress for. You may encounter chilled air driven across exposed floor, damp oily warmth near motors, ice forming a few steps away, and sustained cold conducted through metal decking.

Use site-approved industrial equipment, including:

- **Layered clothing:** wool or synthetic base layers, insulation that still works when damp, and a waterproof, wind-resistant outer shell. Avoid cotton. Keep loose fabric and dangling straps controlled around machinery.
- **Insulated waterproof safety boots**, with puncture-resistant soles and traction approved for wet metal plate and ice. Ordinary hiking boots are likely to leave you with cold feet, inadequate edge protection, or both.
- **Hand protection that combines warmth, liquid resistance, and cut resistance.** Carry dry spares. New castings can cut a glove, and a wet glove quickly becomes a cold injury risk.
- **Hard hat, eye protection, and high-visibility outer clothing**, as required locally. Anti-fog eye protection is useful during Weep and when entering warmer pump houses.
- **Site-compatible hearing protection and communications.** The continuous drone causes fatigue even when it is not immediately painful. Do not use consumer noise-cancelling earbuds as a substitute; you still need to receive horns, voices, and visual or radio warnings.

Wear the complete system before entry. Make sure you can operate zips, lights, radios, and door hardware with your gloves on.

### 3. Pack for disorientation and delay

A sensible day pack should include:

- **Two independent lights**, with spare power kept warm. A headlamp alone can flatten wet decking, ice, and shallow water into similar reflections. Keep the reserve light protected rather than using both continuously.
- **A waterproof local map and written section references.** Do not rely on satellite positioning, a compass, or an off-world mapping device without testing them. Record your position by section number and pressure zone, not by descriptions such as “near the large pump.”
- **Your required communications and atmospheric-monitoring equipment**, with alarms understood before entry.
- **Drinking water and food for the planned visit plus delays.** Insulate the water so it does not freeze. Never drink condensate, melt basin ice, or sample a seemingly clean channel. Clear ice does not mean fresh water.
- **Personal medication, basic first aid, bleeding-control supplies, and waterproof dressings.** Cover existing cuts before entering; brine, oil, metal scale, and industrial dust make even small wounds worth avoiding.
- **Dry gloves and socks, an insulated sitting pad, and compact emergency thermal protection.** These are for surviving a delay at an appropriate refuge, not permission to bed down on an open pan.
- **A sanitation plan.** Use facilities before entry and follow local waste procedures. The basin is a water-recovery system as well as a workplace.

Secure small objects inside the pack. Anything dropped may travel during Flush, foul a screen, strike equipment, or become impossible to recover safely.

### 4. Learn the phases as route-changing events

**Draw:** Falling sumps do not make the exposed floor trustworthy. Newly exposed membranes, panels, ice, and intake approaches may be slick or unstable. Chilled airflow increases heat loss. Stay clear of intakes and do not use low water as an opportunity to take a shortcut.

**Weep:** Expect cold water from above, frost grains, rapidly deteriorating visibility, and different footing as surfaces wet or rime over. A ridge can disappear behind falling water in minutes. If visibility collapses, do not continue toward a lamp or sound merely because it seems close; follow the escort’s procedure from a known safe position.

**Flush:** Be off the pans and at the designated safe location **before** the first horn. Do not plan to outrun the water afterward. Even shallow sheets can hide panel backs, cuts, oil, and changes in level while carrying you toward screens or channels. Pools may rise against walls with no visible inlet. After the all-clear, reassess the route rather than assuming it has returned to its previous condition.

**Sudden silence:** Treat it as an operational emergency, not a welcome reduction in noise. Account for your party, contact control if possible, and use the prearranged response for your exact location. Do not open pressure doors, cross low floor, or approach pumps and valves to investigate.

Schedules are useful, but the horns, local instructions, and current conditions take precedence.

### 5. Move according to industrial discipline, not landscape intuition

Several normally useful instincts fail here:

- **Do not navigate from lamp to lamp.** Lamps mark valves, doors, and ladders, not necessarily a route. A chain of lights may lead to a dead end or across an operating area.
- **Do not navigate by sound.** Echoes and the open pans can make a distant tool or pump seem nearby. Welding flashes and red construction lamps are not navigational beacons.
- **Do not treat shallow water, clear ice, or dark brine as readable surfaces.** Submerged panels are sharp, membranes are not paths, and brine can remain mobile at temperatures that would freeze fresh water.
- **Do not rest somewhere merely because it is warm and dry.** A warm patch may indicate a failed thermal break. Motor casings may be oily, loud, and close to moving equipment. Use designated warming spaces.
- **Never prop, force, or improvise with pressure doors.** Confirm that your escape route remains usable under operational faults, not just during normal entry.
- **Give scrubber-crawlers room.** Slow does not mean harmless. Ask whether they detect stationary people and who has right of way. Do not ride them, block them, shelter behind them, or follow their tracks as though they were safe trails.
- **Stop in a safe place before looking around or taking photographs.** Do not back up for a better view of a fin wall, pump house, or water pattern.

The eastern construction face warrants a separate specialist visit, if permission is granted at all. Stairs may end in open air, floors may be incomplete, and red lamps may mark grades that do not yet exist. Stay out of lifting and welding exclusion zones, never look around welding screens, and do not step beyond the last authorized solid surface.

### 6. Watch your body and your judgment

Cold, vibration, noise, and darkness erode attention before they produce an obvious emergency. Eat and drink on schedule, warm your hands and feet before they become painful, and tell your companion early about clumsiness, persistent shivering, numbness, unusual fatigue, or confusion. Do not wait until you cannot operate your own equipment.

Use elapsed-time alarms and a firm turnaround point. There is no daylight to mark a late afternoon, and the three phases are weather, not a reliable clock.

The scale can also distort decisions. An opposite pump house may be only a lamp and a vibration; the ceiling may be absent from view; long unoccupied reaches may make the basin feel abandoned. It is not abandoned, and emptiness does not reduce the consequences of an injury. If you find yourself becoming careless—skipping section checks, walking toward sounds, or treating the phase schedule as a guarantee—that is reason to shorten the visit.

### 7. Know the few immediate emergency rules

- **If you smell ammonia or an atmospheric alarm sounds, do not investigate.** Leave by the prebriefed route, notify operations, and use escape equipment only as trained. Do not use your nose to decide when conditions are safe again.
- **If caught by a Flush horn or sudden pump silence, follow the sector-specific emergency plan.** Do not invent a shortcut to apparently higher or drier ground.
- **If separated or lost, stop wandering.** If you are in a safe refuge, remain there and transmit your last confirmed section, pressure zone, and direction of travel. If the location is unsafe, move only along a known emergency route. Wandering toward isolated lights makes rescue harder.
- **If injured, give rescuers a section number and access information**, not just a description of the scenery. Control bleeding and protect against cold without entering water or climbing onto unverified structures.

### 8. Avoid becoming part of the basin’s accidental ecology

The living basin exists in small, easily damaged islands: moss around lamps, films at sheltered waterlines, and tiny animals beneath occasional tiles. Do not walk through moss beds to reach a light, lift tiles for a better look, handle biofilms, or collect water, ice, salt, moss, or animals as souvenirs. The capillary recovery surfaces and their climbing silver lines are equipment, not curiosities to touch.

Eat only where permitted, pack out all waste, and clean or decontaminate footwear and equipment as local crews require. A multiverse traveler should be especially careful not to carry organisms or chemical residues between pans—or between worlds. Scrubber-crawlers already redistribute disturbance; there is no need to add another uncontrolled vector.

## A suitable first visit

A good first itinerary might be simply a staffed lock, an occupied pump house, and a marked gantry or walkway, returning by a route with a confirmed refuge and exit. If operations permit, watch part of a Weep or Flush from an approved protected position. Skip the construction face and leave the long traverse for a later visit with sector-specific experience.

That is enough to understand the place: cold accumulating through the boots, lamplight catching serial numbers beneath rime, water ticking inside beams, and an apparently empty floor suddenly revealing its connections in sound and motion.

You do not need exceptional courage or expedition athleticism. You need to remain warm, visible, locatable, and willing to turn around while everything still looks calm. The basin rewards careful observation, not mileage. It is entirely manufactured, but it will not reliably behave as though someone has anticipated your presence.
