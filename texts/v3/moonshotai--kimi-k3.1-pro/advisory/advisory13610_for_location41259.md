# Preparing for the Feltworks

The essential thing to understand is that the Feltworks is neither wilderness nor ruin. It is a small, inhabited industrial settlement whose ground, crop, building material, and maintenance problem are all the same living substance.

An ordinary human should need no special physiological protection. The daylight is ordinary, the air is cool, the distances are short, and the vegetation is slow. You will not have to fight your way through the felt, and standing still for an afternoon will not cause it to swallow you. A dry pair of socks, advance permission, and some restraint will be more useful than a machete or an elaborate survival kit.

## Arrange the visit before arriving

Go alone or in a very small party, and arrange a local contact through whoever administers the works or controls access. This is not a place to arrive with a large tour group and expect to find a ticket office.

Confirm in advance:

- Where visitors may enter and walk.
- Whether the mill, condensary, or powerhouse may be visited, and only with what supervision.
- Whether the canteen serves outsiders.
- Where toilets and washing facilities are available to you.
- Whether potable water can be provided or refilled.
- Where you may sit, eat, and leave a pack.
- How you will depart, and at what time.

Do not treat the freight timetable as a passenger timetable. The electric trolley is industrial equipment, and its walking pace should not fool you into assuming that it is available for luggage, rides, or an improvised return journey. Likewise, do not assume there is an inn, shop, public charging point, or guest room merely because the settlement has the apparatus of a town. Unless accommodation has been explicitly arranged, plan a day visit.

The basin may be compact, but that does not make it publicly accessible or easy to leave after your arranged transport has gone.

## Pack for damp, elastic ground rather than severe weather

A light day pack is sufficient. Use a waterproof liner even if rain is not forecast: on a condensation-heavy morning, cold metal, window corners, and anything left against them can become wet without a drop falling from the sky.

Bring:

- **Closed shoes with flexible, grippy soles.** Maintained lanes should be comparatively firm, but the courts are springy and uneven, and wet leaves, worn brick, iron, and shallow runnels may be slick. Heavy mud cleats or spiked footwear are unnecessary and may damage the mat.
- **A hooded waterproof or waxed outer layer and a light warm layer.** Follow the functional example of the workers’ waxed canvas rather than dressing for either a hot jungle or a severe storm.
- **Long trousers and spare socks.** Damp cuffs and feet are likely to be more troublesome than cold air alone.
- **Drinking water and a simple packed meal**, unless your host has confirmed both. Carry enough for your stay plus a modest reserve without expecting to draw heavily on the condensary.
- **Personal medication, a small first-aid kit, hand sanitizer, and a small towel.** Washing water should not be presumed abundant.
- **A small lamp with spare power** if there is any chance of being out near dusk. A few streetlamps do not constitute continuous lighting between buildings.
- **A charged power bank and offline copies** of your contact details, permission, and return instructions. Do not assume your usual communications or charging arrangements will work.
- **A notebook and pencil**, or a well-protected camera, if you want to record the visit.

If you are unsteady on your feet, discuss the route beforehand. The boundary stair, incline, dished steps, soft hollows, and elastic footing may be more consequential than the short distances suggest. A broad rubber-tipped aid is preferable to sharp trekking spikes.

Leave behind camping equipment, stoves, pets, live plants, collecting jars, and anything intended for digging or cutting vegetation. There is no suitable empty ground on which to pitch a tent, and no reason to make peg holes in a living industrial floor. Avoid drones as well: they would be intrusive, noisy, and hazardous around wires, roofs, and machinery.

## Treat the felt as working ground, not as a lawn

Stay on established lanes unless your host directs you otherwise. The pale, shortened lanes are maintained by exactly the sort of repeated use you are joining; a new shortcut across a court is not automatically helpful.

The courts are not vacant lots. Their different heights and textures record harvest rotations. The lush seams and rounded mounds around older structures are not necessarily ruins awaiting exploration. Even obsolete foundations may still be serving as curbs, boundaries, or places to sit.

Do not:

- Cut a leaf or stem as a souvenir.
- Dig into the mat to see how deep it goes.
- Peel growth away from a wall or axle.
- Pick the small cream-colored flowers after rain.
- Water, prune, flush, or clear anything “to help.”
- Set up a long-term campsite or abandon equipment on the ground.

At the same time, do not become absurdly fearful of touching it. Sitting on the canteen wall, pausing on a lane, or setting your pack on an approved step for lunch is fine. The felt responds to stillness over seasons, not minutes. Its discipline of opening unused doors and running quiet-day machinery belongs to the settlement’s maintenance calendar, not to a visitor’s immediate survival.

One local detail is especially worth remembering: **an open door is not necessarily an invitation.** It may have been opened precisely because nobody otherwise needed to pass through it. Workers’ houses remain private, and apparently unused sheds are not necessarily abandoned.

## Be careful around water and machinery

The settlement’s water system is deliberate and limited. Do not drink from the black cooling basin, the stone sump, open runnels, gutters, or an unidentified pipe. Do not wash yourself, your clothes, or your dishes in them. Use only water your host identifies as potable, and ask before taking a refill.

Do not pour soap, wash water, food waste, or other liquids into the courts or drains. A substance that seems harmless elsewhere may alter a small, closed water-and-growth system here. Pack out litter and food scraps, and use only designated sanitation facilities.

The mill, condensary, powerhouse, pumps, and trolley line should be treated as an active industrial site:

- Enter operating areas only when invited and escorted.
- Keep clear of the press, rollers, engines, generators, switching boxes, cables, and trolley.
- Do not touch condensary fins, cold pipes, or wet machinery out of curiosity.
- Watch for wet floors, oil, partly hidden channels, low foundations, and slick edges around open water.
- Secure loose straps and clothing before entering any workspace.
- Use hearing or eye protection if your escort specifies it.
- Do not smoke or use open flame near fiber bales, drying sheds, engines, or oil.

Do not try to assist by flushing a drain, moving a rooted axle, clearing a doorway, or operating the trolley. In the Feltworks, apparently simple maintenance may be part of a carefully timed rotation.

## Prepare to be conspicuous and quiet

The absence of crowds and animal noise will make you more audible than you expect. A zipper, a camera shutter, or an ordinary conversation may carry across a court. Put your phone on silent, avoid speakers, and do not feel obliged to fill the silence with commentary.

The quiet is not evidence that the settlement is empty, hostile, or unsafe. Nor does a sparse population mean that everyone is available to act as a guide. At shift change, give workers room. A person crossing an empty court may be maintaining a route rather than taking a leisurely walk. Ask before photographing residents, houses, or industrial processes, and do not use the canteen merely because it looks like the obvious visitor facility.

It would also be polite to avoid describing the place to residents as either a town conquering nature or a ruin being reclaimed by it. Both interpretations miss the arrangement they work to maintain. The felt is not simply weeds, and the pipes and presses are not intrusions awaiting purification.

## Choose your timing for sound and light, not spectacle

If your host permits, early morning is rewarding: water beads on cold fittings, and for a while the settlement ticks with dripping. A bright morning gives you flashing metal roofs and nearly silver felt. A brief rain is not necessarily a reason to cancel; under shelter, you may hear it arriving roof by roof, followed by the runnels, gutters, and changes in the pumps.

Do not expect abundant wildlife or a sequence of conventional attractions. A moth in a lamp housing may genuinely be one of the day’s notable encounters. The interest lies in smaller distinctions: the change underfoot between two harvest rotations, a polished handrail, the press sighing behind wet windows, or the smell of crushed stems beneath wet iron.

Give yourself more time than the map seems to require. The pleasure of the Feltworks is in pausing without interfering. But keep a firm margin for departure, and leave before dark unless overnight arrangements and lighting have been confirmed.

## Practice biosecurity in both directions

For a multiverse traveler, this may be the most important preparation after permission.

Arrive with footwear, cuffs, pack seams, and equipment free of foreign mud, seeds, and invertebrates. Do not bring a pet or introduce a supposedly useful plant or animal. The local ecology has few obvious animals and depends partly on people, clothing, runoff, and machinery to move seeds; an outsider could alter that balance without noticing.

Before leaving, inspect your shoe treads, trouser cuffs, pack, and any fiber goods for seeds or small hitchhikers, and clean them only in the manner and place your host recommends. Do not carry away living felt, raw mat, or an untreated cutting. If a finished, dry fiber product is offered for sale, ask whether it is processed and permitted for export, and declare it on return if required. Something harmless in its basin could be a serious ecological problem in another world.

The best-prepared visitor therefore arrives lightly loaded, provisioned, dry, and willing to be a little unobtrusive. The Feltworks does not ask a traveler to endure spectacular danger. It asks you to notice how much deliberate work can be contained in a place that appears, from the rim, to be doing almost nothing.
