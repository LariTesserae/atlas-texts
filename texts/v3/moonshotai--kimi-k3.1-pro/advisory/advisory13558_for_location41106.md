Anvess should not be approached as a desert trek with three towns attached. Treat it as a warm, extremely loud, still-changing inhabited region whose safe places are precise and whose richest habitats may exist for only a few minutes.

An ordinary human can visit comfortably—but mostly by staying within the systems that make Anvess habitable. Plan from refuge to refuge and according to time, not according to apparent distance. Thirty kilometres may be effortless inside the gate network and effectively impossible outside it.

## Arrange the visit before you pack

**Make Senn your base.** For a first visit, there is little reason to attempt an independent crossing of the plain or an open-air camping trip. An ordinary tent provides neither verified acoustic protection nor a dependable water supply.

Arrange in advance:

- **A local host or guide**, particularly for the slab field, condenser lines, reservoir installations, and vegetated seams.
- **Sleeping accommodation inside a maintained silence boundary.** Do not accept “thick-walled” or “set back from the plain” as equivalent. In Anvess, an open doorway can be silent and a substantial-looking structure need not be.
- **Gate access, full addresses, and return arrangements.** Know which arches are pedestrian routes, which are freight routes, and whether your destination is sheltered or exposed when you emerge.
- **Current local guidance on engine noise and ground-making activity.** Ask what warnings, exposure limits, and evacuation procedures visitors are expected to follow.
- **Human-compatible essentials.** As with any multiverse journey, confirm that the air, local food, and treated water are suitable for your physiology. Carry personal medication and, if compatibility is uncertain, a modest reserve of familiar food. A densely populated outpost may still have no medical supplies appropriate for an unmodified human.
- **Your own dependable departure plan.** Senn’s civic gates should not casually be assumed to serve as your route back to your own world.

If possible, arrive during a quieter part of the daily acoustic cycle and go directly indoors. Do not schedule your first plain excursion immediately after arrival.

## The engines are the principal travel constraint

The sound is not merely an unpleasant background. It determines where you can safely be, how you communicate, and how much margin every excursion needs.

For open-ground travel, use whatever protection local experts recommend. This will likely include **properly fitted earplugs together with independent, well-fitted earmuffs**, with spares. Make sure the muffs, eye protection, and any headlamp or helmet fit together without breaking their seals. Consumer noise-cancelling headphones are not a substitute.

Most importantly:

**Hearing protection is equipment for reaching shelter, not permission to remain outside at the daily maximum.** The roar also arrives through the feet and body. Absence of pain does not prove that exposure is harmless.

Put your protection on **before** crossing a silence boundary. Once outside, do not lift a muff or remove a plug in order to converse. Establish visual signals in advance for at least:

- stop;
- return immediately;
- put on or check protection;
- leave the vegetation;
- proceed to shelter;
- I need help or quiet.

Keep companions within sight. A whistle, shouted warning, or voice-operated device may be useless when it matters most. Use written messages or compatible visual displays, and do not rely exclusively on vibrating alarms—the ground itself may already be vibrating.

Plan exposed excursions for quieter periods and return with a generous margin before the usual afternoon maximum. Do not treat that cycle as an infallible timetable. If a surge catches you outside, follow the predetermined route to shelter. Do not run across fractured rock, do not go searching for the sound’s source, and do not assume the nearest fern bed is a safe hiding place.

After each excursion, rest inside a verified silence boundary. Persistent ringing, muffled hearing, headache, disorientation, or unusual exhaustion should end further exposure until you have obtained local advice. If you are especially sensitive to sound or vibration, consider an interior-based visit with short, closely supervised threshold excursions rather than trying to “acclimate” through repeated exposure.

And do not make the founding engines themselves an expedition objective. No exposed engine has been found, the sound offers no dependable direction, and “the engines” are not a destination toward which you can simply walk.

## Pack for sharp stone, dim heat, and abrupt microclimates

The orbital shade changes the light, not the need for heat preparation. Noon resembles a long, dim afternoon; white surfaces look pewter-coloured and shadows give poor depth cues. Meanwhile, the ground remains warm, even after sunset. Do not let the light persuade you that it is cool, and do not use the sun’s apparent height as your only clock.

A practical field kit should include:

- **Locally suitable boots with stiff, thick soles and cut-resistant uppers.** Ordinary light hiking footwear is a poor choice for the eastern slab field. Conchoidal edges can cut boots, and good boots still do not make careless stepping safe.
- **Long, breathable, quick-drying clothing.** It should tolerate parched open air, sudden warm rain, and the steamy humidity of a green seam without requiring a complete change of kit.
- **Eye protection and a suitable dust mask or respirator**, chosen with local advice. Dust can rise in motionless air, and unfamiliar mineral dust should not be assumed benign.
- **A headlamp with spare power.** Dim, uncertain shadows can conceal tilted planes and fracture edges even during the day.
- **Rain protection mainly for your equipment**: dry bags, a pack cover, and protection for medication, documents, and electronics. A heavy personal waterproof may simply make you hotter.
- **Water for the entire excursion plus a delay reserve**, sized with your guide. Add appropriate salts or food if recommended for your exertion and heat exposure.
- **Cut and blister care**, a small general first-aid kit, personal medication, and lip and skin protection for the dry open air.
- **A timepiece, written route card, non-voice communication backup, and visual emergency signalling equipment.** Do not depend on a network connection or assume that a rescue vehicle can drive to you.

The research station may require additional protective equipment for the slab field. Follow its instructions rather than improvising with climbing gear. Do not walk beneath fault walls or among tilted slabs during vibration, and do not put a hand down blindly to steady yourself on fresh rock.

## Treat gates, addresses, and map dates as safety equipment

Senn’s apparent modesty is misleading. It is a city in section: courts, shells, freight levels, cisterns, terraces, and inhabited interiors. Learn the **complete address** of your lodging and destinations, including the relevant level or interior route. A mark on an exterior map may be nearly useless.

For every gate excursion, write down:

- the exact departure and destination addresses;
- the correct pedestrian arch;
- the relevant schedule;
- whether hearing protection must be worn before emergence;
- the nearest verified shelter at the destination;
- the return procedure and an alternative if you miss your intended connection;
- your check-in time and whom your host should contact if you fail to return.

Do not step through an unmarked arch to see where it goes, and do not use freight infrastructure as a shortcut.

A missed or delayed gate connection is not necessarily a minor inconvenience. There may be no vehicle route and no reasonable walking fallback. Follow the host’s agreed procedure rather than deciding to cross the intervening plain.

Likewise, do not navigate from condenser vane to condenser vane. They can be hours apart, may have no public access point, and are not reliable signs of nearby habitation. A survey marker is not automatically a shelter, water point, or safe-zone marker. Animal tracks are not evidence of a route to water; something may simply have crossed without stopping.

**Check the date and revision of every survey map immediately before departure.** After a nearby ground-making boom, check again. A beautiful map from last month may be a historical document.

If a level patch begins vibrating, the air tightens, or a sound seems to pass through your body, leave the area promptly by the known safe route. Do not remain to record the event. Do not touch the cool, unfamiliar stone afterward or assume that the absence of volcanic heat means the site is stable. Report what happened and wait for local assessment.

## Respect water without trying to live off the landscape

Carry water, but do not romanticize scarcity. Senn’s water system is sophisticated, not inexhaustible. Use authorized supplies, observe local allocation and conservation rules, and ask before filling containers or washing equipment.

A remote condenser vane is **not** an emergency tap. Do not cut pipes, open housings, or interfere with the clear threads of water along its edges. The accessible ceramic structure is only part of a device whose working volume is described as “adjacent”; that is an excellent reason for a visitor not to experiment with it.

Warm rain is not automatically potable. Neither are opaque reservoir pools, moisture beneath moss, droplets in fern cups, or temporary pools in fractures. Do not chase a moving rain cell in hopes of cooling off or replenishing your bottles. The rain is warm, may last only minutes, and can leave exposed stone dry again within half an hour.

Its immediate travel hazards are more prosaic: reduced visibility, another layer of noise, slick condensation or water on fresh stone, and the temptation to step into depressions whose edges you cannot clearly see.

## Assume every seam is alive and every garden has a job

The bare ground between seams can be almost lifeless while a black crust, a thread of gel, or a shallow depression is crowded with dormant organisms.

Stay on established paths unless your guide explicitly directs otherwise. In particular:

- Do not scrape or peel dark crusts; they may be bladder moss waiting for water.
- Do not poke gel-like threads in empty depressions; they may be dormant rain eels.
- Do not wade in a hand-deep temporary pool, throw stones into it, collect its animals, or “help” by pouring in extra water.
- Do not cut veinwood roots, harvest fern fronds, or take mirrorbacks, moths, moss, or stone samples as souvenirs.
- Keep soap, food waste, wastewater, and other contaminants out of seams, root mats, fractures, and cisterns.
- Follow any boot-cleaning or decontamination instructions, and do not carry mud, seeds, water, or organisms between seams—or between worlds.

In Senn, the gardens are living reservoirs and construction systems, not decorative planting. Exact pruning and roots routed around conduits indicate active municipal management. Freshly planted, partly enclosed construction is not necessarily abandoned, and the city should not be explored as though it were a picturesque ruin.

Be especially cautious around the **outer fern beds at humid dusk and during engine surges**. Long-legged listeners are fast wild predators that use the engines as hunting cover. Nothing in the description establishes that they routinely prey on humans, but an ordinary visitor should not test their behaviour, approach one for a photograph, or assume that a motionless animal is harmless. Follow local encounter advice and leave when your guide says to leave. Generic acoustic animal deterrents are unlikely to be useful in a place where many animals barely rely on airborne hearing.

## A sensible first visit

A good first itinerary is deliberately small:

1. **Spend the arrival period inside Senn.** Learn the address system, confirm water and gate procedures, fit your protective equipment, practise visual signals, and cross a silence threshold with someone experienced. Pause out of the flow of pedestrian and freight traffic, and give your body time to accept the abrupt change. Do not repeatedly step in and out for novelty.
2. **Take one guided gate excursion in a quieter period**, perhaps to an authorized part of the reservoir or condenser system. Return well before you need to, rather than well before you hope to.
3. **Visit the slab field only as a separate, current-survey excursion**, with station approval and the recommended protective equipment.
4. **If conditions permit, watch one rain-cell event from a managed path or sheltered terrace.** You may see ferns opening, moss turning translucent, mirrorbacks gathering, and eels rising into a pool that will soon vanish. If no cell arrives, or the acoustic conditions make the excursion unwise, accept that. The rain is not an appointment.
5. **Keep unscheduled margin.** Maps change, gates have schedules, and sensory exposure is tiring even when the actual walking distance is short.

The landscape is provisional; Senn’s administration is not. Its addresses, water rules, pruning lines, and schedules are not bureaucratic decorations to be evaded by an adventurous traveller. They are part of how people endure in a basin whose geography is still being revised.

The reward for preparing well is not that Anvess becomes tame. It is that you can safely experience its contrasts: the pressure of the plain giving way to the ticking of a cup inside a doorway; enormous empty distances collapsing into a single step; dry bronze dust on one side of a seam and warm silver rain on the other; an entire habitat becoming active in eight minutes.

Sleep in verified silence. Carry current information. Never treat protection as invulnerability. And when the ground begins to change, be already leaving.
