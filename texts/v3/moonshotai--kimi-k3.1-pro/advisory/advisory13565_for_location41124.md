Prepare for Senn as you would for a brief, accompanied visit to an inhabited resonance chamber—not as a stay in an underground garden. The greenery is inviting, but it does not make the place cool, quiet, wet, or benign. For an unadapted human, the principal dangers are sensory overload, heat, dehydration, disorientation, damaged hearing, and the temptation to interfere with systems whose purpose is not immediately visible.

## First, resolve access and permission

The absence of a doorway in the dome’s upper surface is not a puzzle to solve with tools. Do not drill, dig, tap test the shell, or search for a “weak spot.” The shell’s friction against the surrounding salt is part of what drives the resonance, and its buried structure should be treated as both sensitive and potentially dangerous.

Arrange entry through someone who already knows the route and, ideally, through a resident host. Confirm that an ordinary human can safely breathe the interior air and tolerate at least a short visit. Do not assume that the inhabitants’ tolerance of light, sound, food, or mineral dust matches yours.

Senn is also a poor place for solitary exploration. You may cross several tiers without meeting anyone, speech will not summon help, and there is no described visitor infrastructure. Before entering:

- Leave your itinerary and expected return time with someone outside.
- Establish a return route that does not depend on electronic navigation or audible signals.
- Keep a reserve of water, food, and shelter outside the shell, at a guide-approved location off the dome.
- Carry enough on your person to survive an delayed exit.
- Do not plan to “find somewhere to sleep” after arrival.

A first visit should have a firm turnaround time chosen while you are still outside and thinking clearly.

## Protect yourself from the sound—but do not trust protection too far

Well-fitted hearing protection is essential. Use the best passive protection available, preferably properly inserted earplugs combined with rated earmuffs, with spares. Active noise cancellation alone is not adequate, particularly against unpredictable low-frequency pressure and vibration.

More importantly, understand what hearing protection cannot do. Senn’s foundational tone is felt in the teeth and chest. It is conducted through the structure and, potentially, through your body. Sitting on the floor, lying against a wall, or pressing your back to a parapet is not a way to escape it. A resident laying her cheek against a wall is performing a skilled diagnostic examination; a visitor copying her is simply increasing exposure.

Do not regard a quieter-seeming tier as proof that you have adapted. Adaptation to injurious sound is not a reliable safety strategy. Plan to leave before headache, irritability, dizziness, nausea, panic, ear pain, or difficulty concentrating becomes severe. Because the noise itself degrades judgment, use a visual or tactile timer rather than deciding only when you feel you have had enough.

The deep, old lanes are not refuges. Sound accumulates there, and their seclusion may make them worse than the open tiers. Nor should the central void be treated as a quiet courtyard: it is where the resonance assembles. Genuine relief means leaving the shell, not finding a more comfortable corner inside it.

Anyone with significant hearing sensitivity, vestibular problems, sound-triggered migraine, or a health condition that would make heat, respiratory protection, or rapid evacuation dangerous should reconsider entering. Senn is also unsuitable for pets and for anyone unable to understand nonverbal instructions or move reliably on steep, worn stairs.

## Prepare for continuous glare and continuous warmth

Ordinary sunglasses are not a sufficient plan for the white hours. Obtain eye protection appropriate to the shell’s actual intensity and spectrum, preferably based on the experience of previous unadapted visitors. If that cannot be established, guessing with increasingly dark lenses is unsafe: lenses dark enough for the luminous roof may make stairs and passages invisible.

Carry backup eye protection and, if conditions require it, separate clear protection against dust for darker passages. Goggles, hearing protection, and any respirator should be tested together beforehand. A combination that fogs badly, pinches painfully, or prevents you from seeing your guide’s hands can turn protective equipment into a fall hazard.

Do not stare into the central brightness or directly at the illuminated roof. A brimmed helmet or close-fitting head covering can help with glare and falling grit, provided it does not obstruct your guide’s signals.

There is no cool night to look forward to. Dress for persistent warmth with light, close-fitting clothing that protects skin from grit and snagging vegetation. Avoid loose straps, flowing garments, and wide packs. The lanes are narrow, and a projecting pack corner can strike bricks, foliage, or another person.

Carry all the drinking water you will need, in sealed, non-glass containers, with additional reserve outside. Senn has no well, cistern, or drinking culture on which a human traveler can rely. Your need for water is an anomaly there. Do not:

- Pour water into garden beds.
- Wet down dust.
- Wash mortar, tools, or walls.
- Offer water as an obvious universal gift.
- Empty containers or wastewater inside the shell.

Bring your own food as well. The fact that seed oil feeds the inhabitants does not establish that it is safe for human digestion. The plants’ non-aqueous circulation and varnish-forming leaf oil are strong reasons not to taste seeds, leaves, oils, or prepared foods without reliable human-specific knowledge.

A well-fitted particulate respirator is prudent where salt grains, shell grit, or fresh wall dust are present, but it must be balanced against heat stress and breathing effort. Use smooth, high-traction footwear suitable for polished, cupped stair treads and dusty ledges. Keep your pack small enough that you can pass gardeners carrying baskets without turning sideways into a wall or balcony.

## Establish a nonverbal operating plan

Do not expect to improvise communication after entering. Shouting is useless, and audio alarms may be equally useless.

Beforehand, learn whatever local manual vocabulary your host considers necessary. At minimum, you and your guide need unambiguous signals for:

- Stop.
- Exit now.
- Stay where you are.
- I need help.
- I am dizzy or ill.
- I do not understand.
- Yes and no.

Practice receiving signals as well as giving them. Make sure they remain visible while you are wearing gloves, goggles, and hearing protection.

The inhabitants’ tapped codes are not simply an alternative form of casual knocking. Do not drum on railings, tap patterns into floors, or experiment with vibrations to see who responds. You may send a meaningless instruction, imitate a maintenance signal, or distract someone whose work depends on touch.

Stay with your guide and use a buddy if possible. Agree on what to do if separated: normally either remain visible at a designated checkpoint or return by a known safe route, according to the host’s instructions. Do not wander downward looking for quiet or shout your companion’s name while moving through unfamiliar tiers.

Use no open flame, and avoid unnecessary powered equipment. Senn’s dry resins and oils, hand-maintained structure, and acoustic balance are poor contexts for improvisation. Leave behind drones, speakers, musical instruments, power tools, glass containers, and anything with loose parts that could fall into the void. A small, cool, non-strobing backup light may be useful for passages, but the interior generally does not need additional illumination.

## Treat the city as a working balance, not a collection of curiosities

### The hollow center

The central emptiness is not unused real estate, a garden opportunity, or a theatrical atrium. It is functional infrastructure.

Observe it only from places your guide approves. Do not lean over parapets, trail ropes across it, suspend instruments above it, fly anything into it, or throw objects to disturb the hanging dust patterns. The motionless discs and vertical curtains are not platforms, veils, or solid surfaces. They are visible evidence of forces you should not try to handle.

Also remember that the void makes distances deceptive. A destination visible directly across the opening may require a long route around the perimeter and several changes of level. Allow for that exertion rather than hurrying on narrow stairs.

### Flues, baffles, ovens, and resonance rooms

Never adjust anything to improve your own comfort. Do not open a flue to encourage a plant, close one to quiet a lane, move a baffle, or use an oven without explicit permission. Ovens may also be damping chambers; ventilation and plant health are coupled; excessive growth can powder the brick beneath it.

The rooms in which a dropped husk hops for minutes are not party tricks. Do not test them with seeds, coins, dust, or equipment. Avoid striking walls to “hear” their condition. You will not learn what a gardener learns by touch, and you may damage something or expose yourself unnecessarily.

### The gardens

The orchards are ecological and structural participants in Senn, not decorative landscaping. Stay on the permitted route. Do not cut a leaf to see its oil, collect seed capsules, climb vines, move roots aside, or clear foliage from a stair without instruction.

Do not try to rescue a plant from the noise. The gardens depend on vibration. Conversely, do not assume that lusher growth is always healthier: it may indicate that the architecture is being consumed.

At transitions when seed capsules open, expect sudden activity: black seeds falling through foliage, gardeners moving along ledges, and fresh dust descending from above. This is a work pulse, not merely a spectacle. Keep your eye protection on, yield the stairs, and remain at the agreed observation point rather than following gardeners into their route.

### Buildings, belongings, and apparent debris

Few inhabitants does not mean abandonment. Do not enter dwellings, granaries, storage niches, or work areas merely because no one is visible. Do not lean heavily on balconies or parapets, and do not “help” lift a sagging beam or replace a loose brick.

Senn continually recycles its material. What looks like refuse may be future soil, mortar, cordage, lamp fuel, or a moth nursery. Take no souvenirs—not a brick chip, a polished grain, a discarded capsule, a dry vine, or a pinch of central dust. Pack out everything you bring, including ordinary travel waste.

If you are fortunate enough to see one of the colorless moths, do not capture it, chase it with a light, or disturb seed capsules in search of others. Its appearance is an event for the residents, not a collecting opportunity.

## Choose the visit’s timing conservatively

There is no obviously safe or peaceful hour:

- External noon brings the fiercest interior light and a rising shell note.
- External darkness does not bring interior darkness or silence.
- The amber hours involve deeper contraction resonance.
- Transitions can bring mass seed release and increased maintenance activity.

Let your host choose the timing according to current conditions. Do not make your first entry at noon simply to witness the maximum spectacle, and do not extend a visit because the exterior basin has become dark.

Unless a resident-approved, genuinely suitable resting place has been established, sleep outside the shell. An eye mask alone does not solve continuous resonance, heat, and cognitive fatigue. On a longer stay, sleep deprivation and impaired judgment may become more dangerous than any single dramatic feature of Senn.

After exiting, rest before undertaking demanding navigation or travel. Check yourself and your companion for persistent dizziness, headache, hearing changes, heat illness, or respiratory irritation. Do not use the fact that you tolerated one short visit as evidence that an overnight stay will be tolerable.

## What to expect emotionally

Even with good preparation, Senn may be difficult to enjoy in the ordinary sense. The pressure may reach you before you consciously identify the sound. You may find yourself unable to formulate a complicated thought, then feel embarrassed by it. The empty center can induce vertigo precisely because it is so large and visually bright, while the crowded perimeter can feel abruptly constricting. The gardens’ hardness and dryness may be strangely disappointing after their lush appearance.

The reward is not comfort. It is the experience of seeing a settlement that has survived by refusing both conquest and stillness: repairing without freezing its form, cultivating without irrigating, and preserving an enormous emptiness because filling it would be fatal.

The best visitor therefore arrives briefly, follows a knowledgeable host, touches almost nothing, and leaves before endurance becomes bravado. If access, guidance, suitable protection, water, and a reliable exit cannot all be arranged, do not enter. Senn’s gardens and its hollow are both load-bearing; a visitor’s safety lies in changing neither.
