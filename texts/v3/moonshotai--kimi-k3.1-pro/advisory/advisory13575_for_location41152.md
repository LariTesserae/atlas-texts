# Preparing for the Old Green Height

For an ordinary human, the safest way to visit the Old Green Height is to make the trip much smaller than the landscape.

Do not begin with plans for a traverse, a summit, or a journey to the plateau’s edge. There is no summit view to earn, no dependable river to follow, and no clean edge at which to orient yourself. Plan instead for a short, self-supported stay at one carefully chosen place, with a few cautious excursions and a return route that never depends on finding your way out of the forest.

The Height’s apparent gentleness can mislead you. It is warm, quiet, and almost without steep terrain, but an unmodified human can still get into serious trouble there through altitude, damaged skin, a turned ankle, contaminated water, wet equipment, or simple disorientation. The most likely miseries are not dramatic: socks that never dry, a sleeping liner slowly becoming damp, fogged optics, a small cut that will not close cleanly, and the growing realization that neither sunlight nor flowing water can tell you where to go.

## 1. Settle the questions of air, altitude, and biology before packing

Warmth at elevation does not tell you whether a human can breathe comfortably there.

Before crossing, obtain actual pressure and oxygen information, or a trustworthy equivalent assessment. If the effective altitude is high enough to require acclimatization, arrange it beforehand. A crossing that delivers you directly from a lowland environment may give you no opportunity to acclimatize gradually after arrival. Do not assume that the absence of ice means the absence of altitude illness.

Likewise, an Earthlike smell of wet stone and stems is not evidence of biological compatibility. Spores, waterborne organisms, plant tissues, and animal proteins may be harmless, dangerous, or simply unlike anything your medical preparations anticipate. Ideally, the first visit should follow some form of atmospheric and biological survey. If that is impossible, recognize that a respirator, water filter, or course of antibiotics does not turn an unexamined biosphere into a safe one.

Preparation must also run in the other direction. Arrive with clean equipment, free of soil, seeds, insects, and old organic matter from other worlds. Establish a return procedure for inspecting and containing contaminated clothing, water containers, and muddy equipment. Do not bring the Height home accidentally in the seams of a pack.

Finally, assume there is no local rescue, food supply, medical assistance, or communications infrastructure. Do not go alone. Keep the party small enough to avoid trampling the mat, but large enough to manage an injury; for a self-supported visit, three people is often a more sensible minimum than a large expedition.

## 2. Make the return journey independent of local navigation

Whatever mechanism brings you to the Height, treat its return leg as essential safety equipment.

Your exit should have:

- A fixed, unambiguous location.
- Redundant power or activation means, where applicable.
- A written schedule and a firm latest-return time.
- A way for one failure—a dead battery, a lost pack, a fogged screen—not to disable the whole plan.
- No dependence on satellite reception, celestial sightings, radio contact through the canopy, or reaching a distant landmark.

Keep critical crossing equipment distributed so that one person falling or one pack becoming wet does not strand everyone. Leave responsible parties elsewhere with your schedule and overdue procedures, but do not use the possibility of rescue as an excuse to travel beyond your own ability to return.

A cloud margin is not an emergency exit. The margins are immense, rounded, forested, and difficult to distinguish from the weather around them. Walking toward what you hope is “down” could simply take you farther from your return point without ever producing a recognizable boundary.

## 3. Pack for permanent damp, not for mountaineering

Unless separate approach conditions demand otherwise, leave the heavy alpine clothing behind. Bring a light insulating layer for injury, immobility, or unexpected exposure, but expect your principal clothing problem to be heat and moisture management.

### Clothing

Use lightweight, quick-drying, non-cotton clothing with long sleeves and full leg coverage. Physical coverage is preferable to relying heavily on repellents or other chemicals in an ecosystem where every surface is occupied.

Carry:

- A well-ventilated rain layer, rather than a completely sealed suit you will sweat through.
- A head net or similar fine physical barrier if small flying animals prove troublesome.
- One complete set of sleeping clothes sealed in a reliable dry bag and opened nowhere else.
- More spare socks than an ordinary warm-weather trip would require.
- Personal medications in duplicate, separately waterproofed.

There is no perfect footwear for the Height. Sealed boots may eventually become wet inside; draining footwear accepts that your feet will remain wet. Prioritize a secure fit, protection for the toes and soles, and excellent grip on slick, rounded stone. Heavy mountaineering boots are unnecessary, but open sandals are an invitation to cuts and unseen contacts with the mat.

Foot care matters more than the hope of dry feet. Stop early for hot spots. Inspect your skin at least morning and evening. Change socks before damage develops, and keep your sleeping feet as dry as your shelter permits.

### Keeping essential things dry

Use several independent dry bags or waterproof cases, not merely a rain cover over one pack. Separate critical equipment into categories: sleep, medical, navigation, power, and return-crossing equipment. A single leak should not wet all of them.

Bring absorbent cloths, small repair materials, and desiccant for sealed electronics, while recognizing that desiccant cannot dry an entire camp. Leather, untreated metal, down insulation, and anything that requires prolonged airing will be difficult to manage.

Assume everything left outside your shelter will acquire a film of water.

### Shelter

A good shelter is:

- Freestanding, because there is almost no honest soil for stakes.
- Highly ventilated, because condensation may be as troublesome as rain.
- Equipped with full fine mesh and a waterproof floor.
- Small enough to fit on a durable site without clearing vegetation.

Do not plan to use a hammock. A column that looks like a tree trunk may be a bundle of stems around a hollow, wet, inhabited center. Roots may also be supporting the stone to which you intend to attach a line.

Camp only on a durable, low-sensitivity stone site identified without clearing growth, above and away from basins and outside root-vaults or hollow columns. If no such site can be found near your return point, make the first visit a day trip. Do not manufacture a campsite by cutting roots or stripping moss.

### Light, power, and records

Each person should have a primary light and a small independent backup, with spare power sealed against moisture. Do not rely on solar charging beneath the canopy.

Use the lowest practical light and keep it directed downward. Avoid sweeping beams through hollows or using bright light to examine every animal that moves. At night, the light is for camp safety, not for walking farther.

Bring at least two reliable timepieces per party. The vague cloud-light and absence of a visible solar disc make subjective timekeeping unreliable. A waterproof notebook and pencil will outlast many electronic devices; use them for route notes, times, observations, and equipment checks.

For looking at the Height itself, a hand lens is likely to be more rewarding than binoculars.

## 4. Carry your own food, and be conservative about water

Bring all your food. Do not taste leaves, drink from a leaf-cistern, collect swimmers from a pool, or assume that an animal’s food is safe for you. A place can be full of life and contain nothing an ordinary human should eat.

For a short visit, ready-to-eat food is preferable to elaborate cooking. It reduces fuel, odors, contaminated wash water, and time spent working around the mat. Keep food sealed, eat at your established site, and pack out every wrapper and crumb.

The water is not automatically poisonous because it is dark, nor safe because it is clear. Its color is not the issue; its participation in an immense living system is. For a brief first visit, carrying your full drinking supply, with a reasonable reserve, is the simplest option when weight permits.

For anything longer, water collection and treatment should be based on prior assessment, not improvised beside a convenient pool. Organic material can clog filters, and no single treatment method addresses every possible biological or chemical hazard. If local water has not been characterized, do not pretend that a filter straw is a complete answer.

Do not swim, wash with soap, clean cookware, or dispose of hygiene products in basins, hollow columns, springs, or root channels. Carry electrolytes if you need them, but do not let flavored salts encourage you to drink untreated water.

## 5. Make navigation trivial before you enter

Most familiar landscape shortcuts fail here.

- **Do not follow water home.** A rill may reveal its direction only after an hour, and the wider root system can move water independently of visible slope.
- **Do not descend merely to escape mist.** The margins may remain forested and indistinct for enormous distances.
- **Do not wait for the sky to help.** A rare gap shows only a pale region of cloud.
- **Do not treat an altimeter as a map.** The broad swells are too gentle to provide useful route finding by themselves.
- **Do not assume a compass, satellite device, or inertial instrument works merely because it works elsewhere.** Test it, and use it only as a supplement.

A compass may tell you where north is without telling you where you are.

The conservative solution is to make the route so short that sophisticated navigation is unnecessary. Establish your return point and camp first. From there, make brief out-and-back excursions during the best cloud-light, keeping companions within sight rather than merely within shouting distance. The vegetation absorbs sound efficiently.

Do not leave flagging tape, paint, cairns, cut stems, or permanent markers. If a properly planned visit requires temporary physical markers, they must be harmless, logged, and completely recovered—but the need for an extensive marker system is usually a sign that the itinerary is too ambitious.

If you become uncertain of your position, stop before taking speculative steps. Consult your time and route notes, use your agreed close-range signals, and retrace only ground you can positively identify. Never follow a pool outlet, an animal route, a wall, or a flight of steps simply because it appears to go somewhere.

## 6. Walk as though the ground were both structure and habitat

The root mat is not necessarily quicksand, and there is no reason to imagine it swallowing a traveler without warning. The more credible dangers are concealed voids, uncertain load-bearing surfaces, slick stone, hidden water, and the damage caused by your own weight.

Move slowly. Test questionable ground gently with a blunt staff rather than driving a sharp pole into living tissue. Do not jump between roots or stones. Avoid root spans, floating vegetation, and fallen-wood causeways unless there is a compelling reason and you have assessed them; the easiest choice is usually not to cross.

Remember:

- Hollow columns may contain water or vertical gardens.
- Rounded stone can be continuously wet.
- Roots entering stone may be holding it together.
- A ridge supported by roots is not a place to pull “loose” vegetation away for a better grip.
- A broad floating leaf is not a human raft, however substantial it looks.
- Five old steps entering water are not evidence of a safe ford.

Stay out of the pools. Their depth is difficult to judge, submerged roots complicate footing, and some hunters are nearly indistinguishable from the roots among which they lie. There is no need to exaggerate the danger: uncertain water, poor footing, and difficult evacuation are already sufficient reasons not to wade.

Stop traveling well before the cloud-light fails. The lower forest at night is not an environment in which to improvise a route by headlamp.

## 7. Respect silence as a form of communication

The scarcity of sound is not evidence that the forest is empty. It is partly the result of lives conducted through pressure, vibration, scent, and extremely restrained movement.

Do not drum on roots, strike hollow columns, imitate calls, play recordings, or release scented markers into water. You could be making a very loud statement in a language you do not understand. Avoid shouting except in a genuine emergency, and do not rely on shouts to maintain party spacing.

If you encounter a moss-backed browser, do not approach it or treat the garden on its back as a botanical exhibit arranged for you. Its soft feet may allow it to appear at close range without much warning. Give it room and avoid obstructing its route, but do not back blindly toward a basin or untested root arch. Step aside only onto ground you already know.

Do not feed animals, catch them for closer inspection, or move them “out of the way.” Many small creatures live in water films, leaf tissues, bark, and other places a visitor might casually touch. A shelter’s mesh protects you, but it also prevents you from rolling onto or crushing inhabitants during the night.

No fires. No cutting a trail. No collecting plants, stones, water samples, insects, or souvenirs. A machete is especially easy to misunderstand here: roots are not inconvenient ground cover but part of the plateau’s surface, structural support, and plumbing.

## 8. Treat the ruins as interruptions, not facilities

The deliberate angles are among the few things that might tempt a traveler into careless behavior. Resist that temptation.

A roofless cell is not an available shelter. Its floor supports a pale plant found nowhere else; entering, sweeping it out, or setting down a pack could damage something irreplaceable. A low wall ending in a basin is not a route marker for your purposes. Steps disappearing into water do not oblige you to discover where they once led.

Observe without clearing roots, moving stones, rubbing surfaces, or dismantling joints. The roots may now be preserving the arrangement rather than destroying it. Photograph and record what you can from outside, then leave.

The absence of tools and debris does not make the remains meaningless, and their apparent abandonment does not make them yours.

## 9. Manage your body and your expectations on a human timescale

The Height’s slow processes do not make human injury slow. Heat stress, inadequate sleep, damaged skin, and altitude symptoms still develop on the scale of hours or days.

Build regular checks into the schedule rather than waiting for discomfort:

- Drink and eat according to plan.
- Inspect feet and other areas of constantly damp skin.
- Clean and protect small wounds promptly.
- Check that the dry sleep set is still dry.
- Confirm time, power reserves, and return arrangements.
- Turn back before anyone becomes tired enough to walk carelessly.

Warm, saturated air can make exertion surprisingly unpleasant because sweat provides little cooling. Strong sun is not required for heat illness. Conversely, being continuously wet during a long period of immobility or an injury can still be debilitating even without alpine cold.

Severe headache, repeated vomiting, confusion, unstable walking, or breathlessness at rest in a person who may be altitude-affected are reasons to use the evacuation plan, not reasons to wait for morning.

Psychologically, prepare for enclosure. Some travelers will find the silence deeply restful. Others will experience the lack of horizon, weak shadows, and unchanging warmth as disorienting or oppressive. Use fixed times for meals, sleep, excursions, and departure. Do not respond to the feeling of “getting nowhere” by walking faster.

The Height does not offer a scenic payoff for pushing onward. Its interest is likely to appear in one leaf-cistern, one root arch, one line of drowned growth, or the slow recognition that a motionless pool is inhabited. Sit quietly where you can do so without crushing anything, and let close observation replace mileage.

## 10. A sensible first visit

A good first itinerary is almost offensively unambitious:

1. **Arrive with ample cloud-light**, already acclimatized and with the return system checked.
2. **Establish the fixed base** on suitable stone, or abandon the overnight plan if no low-impact site exists.
3. **Rest, eat, inspect your equipment, and observe the immediate area.** Do not celebrate arrival by taking an exploratory walk into the mist.
4. **Make one short excursion during the best light**, avoiding water crossings and returning with a substantial margin.
5. **Remain in camp after dark.** Listen if you wish, but do not investigate every drop, exhalation, or tremor of water.
6. **Leave while your reserves are still comfortable**, not after the last dry clothing and easy patience have been consumed.

One or two nights are enough to learn whether your skin, sleep, equipment, and navigation tolerate the place. They are not enough to justify crossing a province or trying to interpret the watershed.

When departing, account for every person, marker, battery, wrapper, and piece of equipment. Pack out human waste in an appropriate containment system; do not bury it in the mat or release it into the watershed. Do not try to erase your footprints by tearing up additional growth. Inspect the site, but leave its living arrangements alone.

After return, follow your contamination and health-monitoring procedures. Keep equipment contained until it has been dealt with, and take unusual respiratory symptoms, persistent wounds, or other illness seriously.

The essential preparation is mostly subtraction: no traverse, no trail cutting, no swimming, no foraging, no nighttime wandering, and no expectation that water or weather will explain themselves to you.

Bring a dry place to sleep, an early departure, and enough humility to be interested in a very small area. Do not mistake warmth for safety, stillness for shallowness, silence for emptiness, or age for indestructibility. The Old Green Height is already complete; a successful visit adds almost nothing to it.
