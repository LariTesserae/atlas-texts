# Preparing for the High Vacancy

The High Vacancy should be approached less like a ruined city than like an unsurveyed extravehicular landscape. Its architecture is not infrastructure. A stair is not necessarily a route, a room is not necessarily shelter, and an avenue is not a promise that its two ends can be reached with comparable effort.

If your plan consists of warm clothing, an oxygen bottle, and a camera, do not go.

Nothing there needs to be malicious for a visitor to die. The principal dangers are unbreathable air, exposure, falls, separation, exhausted supplies, and an unjustified faith in the return journey.

## 1. Bring a complete human environment

Assume until direct measurement proves otherwise that the atmospheric pressure is incompatible with human survival. An oxygen mask alone is not adequate protection against insufficient pressure, and this is not a place to attempt acclimatization.

Use a pressure suit or equivalent full-body protection, with:

- Independent breathing supply and carbon-dioxide removal.
- Thermal regulation and insulation from cold stone.
- Protected seals and connectors that can tolerate adhering, electrostatically charged dust.
- Puncture-resistant gloves and outer layers, especially if working near exposed black glass.
- Emergency breathing reserve, suit-repair materials, and a backup light that does not depend on the suit’s main power system.
- Enough water and food for the entire visit. There is no frost to melt, no seep to find, and no useful distinction between searching shallowly and searching deeply.

A pressurized, insulated shelter should be established for anything beyond a very short excursion. It should be freestanding or ballasted rather than fixed into cracks, which may contain animals. Include a dust lock, independent power, sleeping provision, and enough capacity to support the whole party if an exterior return route becomes temporarily unusable.

Do not count on solar power. The small sun provides weak light and almost no warmth. Carry stored energy, keep batteries within their operating temperature range, and budget for lighting as well as life support.

Even a momentarily tolerable pressure reading should not be treated as a regional guarantee. Weather here includes pressure fronts. Keep protective equipment immediately available, and do not remove a helmet merely because the stillness makes the landscape seem benign.

Most importantly, carry supplies for delay rather than for distance. A return leg cannot safely be provisioned by multiplying the apparent length of the outgoing route. Maintain an untouched reserve and turn back according to elapsed time and consumables, not according to whether a landmark looks close.

## 2. Secure your departure before making an itinerary

Your multiverse egress should, if possible, be independent of local geography. It is unsafe to rely on:

- Retracing every step exactly.
- Reaching a particular natural arch.
- Passing through an opening that previously led to the arrival terrace.
- Descending to a lower plain.
- Recognizing the campsite solely by its apparent distance from another landmark.

If your transit method requires a fixed local coordinate, determine whether that coordinate survives a regional settling. If it requires a specific doorway, choose another destination unless you have a thoroughly tested alternative.

Make an immediate departure-and-return test if your method permits one. Everyone in the party should know how to operate the emergency exit; do not leave that knowledge with a single person.

Do not travel alone. A sensible small expedition has at least three people: no one explores solo, and someone can remain with the shelter and egress during short surveys. Rescue should be planned as self-rescue. There is no local assistance, no occupied lower valley, and no reason to expect that calling toward the lights below will accomplish anything.

A first visit should be short, during the strongest available twilight, and should not require crossing between mesas. Overnight stays and unsupported-door exploration belong to later expeditions, if they belong anywhere.

## 3. Choose camp as if the city were a cliff landscape

Establish camp on broad, continuous, opaque bedrock, well back from:

- Cliff rims and arcades ending in open air.
- Deep shafts and elevated dust channels.
- Thin or translucent mineral walls.
- Natural bridges and their approaches.
- Unsupported openings.
- Talus, even where it appears ancient and carefully sorted.
- The outer saltfield and its intermittent shadow district.

An enclosed room may feel psychologically preferable, but an open court with a clear view of the shelter and egress is often easier to leave safely. Do not camp in a threshold. Do not assume a shaded recess offers pressure, warmth, or protection merely because it resembles human shelter.

Use removable lights to identify camp, but remember that a beacon identifies an object, not necessarily the route by which you reached it. Avoid drilling, carving, painting, or leaving permanent survey marks. Apart from damaging the site, a mark on stone may outlast your ability to interpret its position.

## 4. Navigate by verified connections, not by scale

The High Vacancy will continually invite you to estimate distance with your eyes. Resist the invitation.

Enormous perspectives may enclose very small distances. An old court may be trivial to cross along one side and expensive along another. Architectural scale, apparent elevation, and travel time do not form a dependable system.

Keep a survey record containing:

- The order in which places connect.
- Photographs looking both forward and backward along every important leg.
- Actual elapsed travel time.
- Consumables used.
- The local illumination phase.
- The date or cycle on which the observation was made.
- Any peculiarities of shadow, dust, or duplicated views.

A map here is a dated set of observations, not a permanent chart.

When surveying a court, return by the same verified edge rather than improvising a diagonal shortcut. Do not assume that a route followed by pale dust is a trail. Dust records pressure, charge, and erosion; it does not certify human passage.

Check in by scheduled radio calls and visual signals where possible. Radio contact proves that someone can hear you, not that they are physically near. Do not use sound alone to find a missing companion.

Near edges, use ordinary fall protection in addition to all the unusual precautions: helmet, short tether, sound anchors, and slow movement. Never rely on a parapet. Never lean on a translucent membrane. Do not descend toward the darkness beneath the cliffs in hopes of finding a base level, and do not treat the lower lights as destinations.

The dim, upward illumination will make depth perception worse. Arch soffits may glow while their upper surfaces disappear; tower roots can be brighter than their summits. Carry several lights, including a movable source that can cast side illumination across steps and fractures. Move carefully enough to notice vertigo before it becomes a fall. The horizon sometimes appears to lie beneath you, and neither the lights above nor those below should be used as a reliable horizontal reference.

## 5. Treat openings as unexplained transitions

An unsupported doorway is not an architectural curiosity. It is an unclassified transition between elevations or routes.

Do not test one with a hand, head, or foot. If examination is necessary, use a tethered probe first, inspect both sides visually, and determine whether a return passage exists before any person crosses. Even then:

- Never make an unverified opening part of your essential return route.
- Never assume repeated successful use guarantees the next use.
- Do not tether people together across one unless the behavior of the tether itself has been tested.
- Do not follow immediately after someone who crosses and falls silent.
- Do not camp, store supplies, or leave your only egress equipment inside one.

A person may emerge slightly above the terrace they just left, or on a bridge over a gulf that had been behind them. The transition may be gentle. That does not make it convenient or reversible.

Most changes in the High Vacancy are slow. Towers do not ordinarily swap places without warning, and arches do not usually vanish between one glance and the next. Nevertheless, if two landmarks begin admitting the same view, or their shadows become difficult to distinguish, stop treating them as independent navigation references. A blank wall developing a seam is not an invitation to wait for the rooms behind it. Newly discovered rooms are already ancient; their novelty is yours alone.

## 6. Know what to do during weather and settling

Pressure fronts may have no visible approach. Keep away from shafts when black grains begin rising, and do not stand beneath elevated channels when pale dust starts pouring outward in level banners. Those banners are not surfaces, wind socks, or safe indications of direction.

Avoid unnecessary climbing and cliff work during weight tides. Do not jump to test your weight.

A regional settling is rare, but its warning signs should be memorized:

- Dust lifting simultaneously from many streets in straight, motionless filaments.
- Slightly uncertain weight.
- Pebbles leaning toward cliff edges without rolling.
- Distant terraces drawing closer together or separating.

At the first signs, stop nonessential movement and account for everyone. Secure loose equipment. If you are already on broad, safe ground, stay low and remain there. If you are close to an edge, move inward only by a short, direct route that requires no bridge, opening, or complicated descent.

Do not run for camp if doing so means committing to an uncertain passage. Keep your suit sealed and wait. The single dry tick sometimes associated with the event is not, by itself, an all-clear. After the dust has descended and the skyline has remained stable, resurvey before traveling. Check the egress first. Previous travel times and intermediate landmarks should be considered provisional until observed again.

## 7. Leave the shadow district alone

The additional city cast upon the saltfield is best observed from solid ground.

Its shadows affect dust, but that does not establish how they will affect a human body, a pressure suit, a rope, or a transit aperture. A shadow-wall may deflect grains without supporting your weight. A shadow-door may admit a stream of dust without being a passage you should enter.

Do not shelter inside it, use it as a shortcut, or schedule essential navigation around its outline. It does not shift quite in sympathy with the ordinary shadows and may linger after they disappear. It is an exceptionally poor landmark and an unnecessary place to conduct bodily experiments.

Likewise, do not chase the sequence of interruptions that occasionally passes among the lower lights at night. Nothing visible rises, and there is no reliable body to observe or avoid. There is no evidence that it hunts people—but also no evidence that human confidence would interest it. Watch, if you watch at all, from established shelter rather than approaching a rim for a better view.

## 8. Walk as though the sterile surfaces are inhabited

The charged dust lattices and crack-animals are easy to damage precisely because they barely announce themselves.

Do not brush angular patches from walls, discharge equipment against them, or collect “ordinary” dust without examination. Avoid sitting on shaded stairs or placing hands and poles into fractures. Seen edge-on, an animal may effectively disappear; seen from above, it may resemble nothing more than a crack changing direction.

There is no food to gather and no sensible reason to handle these organisms. Keep electrical experiments away from the lattices, and do not interpret a terrace full of motionless cracks as proof of sterility.

The silence requires similar discipline. Footsteps may arrive after their maker has passed. An empty room may return one breath or one grain striking stone. These phenomena are not automatically messages, pursuit, or evidence of another person—but neither should every sound be dismissed without checking.

Use agreed check-ins. If footsteps seem to follow you, stop, establish visual or radio contact with your companions, and check your instruments. Do not remove your helmet to listen more carefully. Do not leave a safe route to investigate a sound.

## 9. Prepare for the psychological experience

A well-managed visit may be almost entirely uneventful. That is not a wasted visit.

You will spend much of your time conscious of your own breathing, suit machinery, and the small noises of equipment. Outside, there may be hours between audible ticks. You may cross a court large enough for a settlement and find only a slab, a few grains, and a rectangle of cold shade. The absence of furnishings and inscriptions can produce a strange pressure to invent occupants, purposes, or a catastrophe. Resist that pressure as well.

The High Vacancy is not necessarily haunted, and it is not abandoned in the human sense. Its sounds need not be voices. Its gates need not have been intended to admit anyone. Its rooms need not be waiting for use. The reward of going is not discovering who built it, but remaining long enough to understand that no builder is required.

Bring company, regular rest periods, and a familiar schedule inside the shelter. Do not let the local sun determine your sense of morning or bedtime. Quiet awe, agoraphobia, irritability, and an urge to “do something” are all more plausible immediate problems than a dramatic geological transformation. The urge to open one final door or cross one final bridge should be treated as a reason to stop, not as a special insight.

For a first visit, a good expedition is deliberately modest: arrive in the strongest twilight, verify egress, establish shelter, survey one short route without openings or bridges, observe the upward light and the dust from solid ground, and leave before the dimmest part of the cycle. Pack out waste and markers. Keep exterior equipment contained until it can be checked for clinging dust or unnoticed organisms, and do not release local material into another world.

The place’s stillness is not reassurance, but neither is it a threat. Most of its processes have more patience than any visitor. Bring everything you need, disturb almost nothing, and never assume that getting back will cost what getting there did.
