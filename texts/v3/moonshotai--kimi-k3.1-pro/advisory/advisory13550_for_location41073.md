# Preparing for the Unweathered Town

Prepare as though you were combining an extreme-depth expedition, an active-volcanic worksite, and a visit to a quiet, inhabited garden. Do **not** prepare for a scenic swim through picturesque ruins.

Three facts should govern every decision:

- **The surface is not an emergency exit.**
- **Warm, clear water is not necessarily safe water.**
- **A town with few visible bodies and many roofless chambers is not abandoned.**

Without purpose-built protection or thoroughly verified physiological adaptation, an ordinary human cannot simply enter this environment.

## 1. Decide how you will stay alive before deciding what to pack

The description does not provide an actual depth, so no honest traveler should arrive with a preselected suit rating, breathing mixture, or decompression schedule. Have the destination surveyed first. You need pressure and temperature profiles along the entire route, chemical measurements from both ordinary water and active channels, current information, and an assessment of volcanic activity.

For most human travelers, the safest first visit would be inside a small, quiet, one-atmosphere vessel, stationed only where the inhabitants permit it. This avoids exposing your body to ambient pressure, but it does not make the trip casual: if the vessel fails, there may be no survivable swim to another refuge. It needs independent reserves, recovery support, and a rescue plan that works **at depth**.

An ambient-pressure suit or diving system might allow more mobility, but only as part of a specialist expedition. It must address breathing gas, carbon-dioxide control, decompression, thermal regulation, chemical exposure, and emergency refuge. Conventional recreational scuba is not a reasonable fallback. Nor is a breath-hold, an improvised helmet, or a charm that merely claims to let you breathe water.

If your multiverse equipment offers gills, pressure adaptation, or a temporary local body, treat these as separate problems:

- Water breathing does not necessarily solve crushing pressure.
- Pressure tolerance does not necessarily protect you from brine, dissolved minerals, or osmotic injury.
- Thermal tolerance must include both the warm terraces and the colder outer seams.
- Navigation may require senses you do not possess; human eyesight alone is poorly suited to routes remembered through warmth and slope.
- Returning to your original body may reintroduce decompression or contamination problems.

Test any transformation and its reversal under controlled conditions, not in a public garden.

### If arriving by portal

Do not open an uncomplicated doorway from a low-pressure room directly into deep water. The pressure differential could injure you, flood your point of origin, and damage the town. Use a pressure-isolated receiving system, or arrive within a vessel whose safety does not depend on the portal remaining open.

Likewise, **a portal home is not automatically a decompression chamber**. Do not assume that instantaneous transportation removes dissolved gas, reverses pressure adaptation, or satisfies biological quarantine.

## 2. Obtain guidance, permission, and a human-compatible refuge

Arrange for a local guide and, if necessary, an interpreter. Ask which courts and routes may be visited, how visitors are expected to announce themselves, what light and propulsion are acceptable, and what to do when the mountain sounds.

A guide is especially important because apparent stillness is not inactivity. A motionless gardener may be working. Masons easing a block into place may be in the middle of a delicate operation. Silence is not necessarily consent, and an unanswered request to enter is not permission.

Do not count on local buildings for shelter. A chamber that usefully holds warmth or darkness for an inhabitant may offer nothing to a human who needs breathable gas, sleeping space, sanitation, or medical support. Unless a human-compatible refuge has explicitly been provided, your accommodation must remain within your own vessel or life-support system.

The town’s compactness is deceptive. You may be able to see only one or two courts safely, and that should be enough for a first visit.

## 3. Pack for pressure, chemistry, and silence

Everything should be selected for the measured environment, not merely for ordinary seawater. Warm mineral-rich water, dense brine, suspended flakes, and volcanic glass may affect seals, viewports, sensors, joints, and intakes in unfamiliar ways.

A sensible equipment philosophy is **compact, contained, quiet, and difficult to drop**:

- Carry redundant life support, power, and communications appropriate to your chosen method of visit.
- Protect yourself against cuts and punctures without assuming that gloves make it acceptable to touch things.
- Secure loose buckles and metal fittings so they do not clink against stone.
- Keep tools and instruments inside closed containers or otherwise secured. Do not chase a dropped object over a terrace edge.
- Use the least intrusive propulsion possible. Thruster wash, finning, and exhaust bubbles can disturb fronds, sediment traps, and carefully separated flows.
- Bring sealed drinking water, food, necessary medication, and waste containment. Do not drink the clear water or forage from the gardens.
- Protect equipment from suspended mineral material without discharging concentrated waste into the town.

Lighting deserves particular restraint. Bright lamps will not recreate a surface afternoon. They may simply illuminate a blizzard of suspended flakes, produce backscatter, and erase the faint root-velvet markers from your perception. Use dim, adjustable, indirect light; passive low-light optics may be better where permitted. No flash photography. Do not use active sonar, ranging pings, external speakers, or other loud conveniences without explicit approval.

Avoid anchors, grapples, guide lines attached to vegetation, sample jars, harvesting tools, and “just in case” objects you do not actually need. Do not bring seeds, food scraps, soaps, lubricants, or other materials that could enter the water. Equipment should be cleaned and biologically contained before arrival and after departure, using methods that leave no chemical residue at the site.

## 4. Prepare your body and your expectations

Even inside excellent equipment, this could be an exhausting place to visit.

The warm water may overheat a sealed or insulated traveler, while colder seams can produce rapid chilling or equipment stress. Do not treat either as a comfort problem alone. Hydration, fatigue, and temperature control need deliberate management.

Psychologically, the town combines opposite forms of exposure. Its lanes may be too narrow for your full equipment, while the water beyond the ridges offers no nearby bottom, no shelter, and no visible horizon. Practice operating in confined spaces and in featureless darkness. Be able to remain still without grabbing a wall, and to control panic when your own breathing or pumps seem louder than the world outside.

Do not visit solo. Do not use alcohol or sedatives to make confinement easier. Make sure your medical support understands pressure-related injury and that evacuation does not depend on an immediate ascent.

Also prepare for very little apparent spectacle. At local noon, the water may only thin to bottle-glass green and the highest leaves acquire a bronze edge. Night changes that display comparatively little. Your excursion should be limited by life support, fatigue, and the hosts’ wishes—not by a hope that the town will become more entertaining if you wait.

## 5. Make the approach slowly

Local noon is the sensible time for a first approach, provided your hosts agree. It offers the best natural visibility without requiring you to force the scene into brightness.

Let instruments detect the changing warmth and mineral chemistry. Do not taste the water, open a sampling port with your hand, or interpret clarity as purity. As shapes emerge from darkness, resist the temptation to approach the closest wall immediately. Fresh basalt and volcanic glass can be exceptionally sharp, and a sheltering ledge may also be an unstable or active work area.

Stop at the agreed outer point or marker stones. Announce yourself only by the prearranged method, then wait. Do not tap on masonry, knock on a hull, or swim into a court to look for someone.

Beyond the last markers, do not improvise navigation. Following warmth by itself may take you toward a seep rather than toward your destination. Nor should you treat the saddle to the outlying vent gardens as an afternoon excursion. The open water between peaks is exposed, uninhabited, and difficult to read. Travel there requires a separate plan, separate reserves, and its own emergency refuge.

## 6. Move through the town as if every surface has a purpose

Almost everything you see is a crop, a tool, a structure, part of the waterworks, or a structure gradually becoming mountain. When in doubt, it is not a seat.

Specific cautions matter here:

- **Roofless does not mean ruined.** Do not enter chambers merely because they are open above.
- **A woven screen is a door.** Closed screens should be treated as signs of privacy or restricted entry, not as evidence that nobody lives there.
- **Doorways at several heights may all be in use.** Do not hover in one simply because it is above your idea of head height.
- **Keep stairs and landings clear.** They are freight routes for stone and other heavy loads.
- **Do not pull yourself along by fronds or root mats.** Roots help bind masonry and mineral crust together; damaging them may harm both garden and structure.
- **Do not squeeze into the oldest passages.** If you cannot pass with all essential safety equipment and without contact, that route is not part of your visit. Never abandon bailout equipment to fit.
- **Do not scrape mineral rind from thresholds or grip marks.** Age here appears as thickening and narrowing, not necessarily as decay.
- **Treat the upper quarry as a workplace.** Pecked dots are instructions for possible future rooms, not decorative patterns or an invitation to help.
- **Do not clear sediment, open a drain gap, straighten a basket, or gather a faded leaf.** An intact dying leaf may be someone’s future fiber harvest, not litter.

Almost nothing in the gardens is torn or grazed. Damage that would seem trivial elsewhere—a broken frond, a dragged fin, a chipped grip mark—may remain conspicuous for a very long time. A dropped object may lie exactly where it falls for decades.

## 7. Leave the waterworks entirely alone

The channels, gates, gutters, chimneys, and inverted flumes are among the most sophisticated and sensitive parts of the town. They are not decorative fountains.

Do not touch a hand-cut gate. A movement that seems minor to you could send a different temperature or chemistry through an entire terrace.

Do not put a hand or vehicle intake into a brine gutter, warm chimney, rising thread, or ascending drop. Density interfaces and plumes may alter buoyancy, trim, visibility, and sensor readings over very short distances. Their chemistry should be considered hazardous until measured otherwise. The “drops” rising through the fountains are not supplies of air, and any gas pocket encountered elsewhere should never be presumed breathable.

Do not wash equipment, release exhaust, empty wastewater, or discard scrubber material into a convenient channel. The town’s plumbing connects exactly the places you would least want to contaminate.

At junctions, leave the hollow stones and their cultivated luminous root-velvet undisturbed. They are not spare lamps, and they are not wild growth available for collection.

## 8. Match the town’s quiet without mistaking it for a rule against communication

Water carries sound farther than you expect. Secure your equipment so that nothing knocks, avoid dropped tools, and keep external narration and alarms to the minimum compatible with safety. Arrange visual or otherwise nonintrusive signals with your guide beforehand. If conversation is appropriate, use the place and manner your hosts prefer; the work courts may be more suitable than a garden lane.

Do not expect rapid responses, crowds, or demonstrations. The town’s urban order lies in its gates, drains, courts, storage, and routes, not in visible busyness. A mason’s long interval between blows may include listening. Your arrival is not a reason for that interval to be shortened.

If the mountain gives its low internal note and work stops, stop too. Follow the local procedure rather than inventing one. Do not automatically flee into exposed water, shelter beneath a fresh ledge, or resume simply because the sound has ended.

The rare animals require the same restraint. Do not feed the transparent grazers, disturb sediment traps, or pursue a large shape beyond the ridge. If something passes as a temporary absence of light, do not turn a powerful beam toward it.

Finally, do not describe the settlement to its inhabitants as drowned, post-catastrophic, or waiting to become an island. Those are your narratives, not necessarily theirs. Its handwork is exact, its infrastructure deliberate, and its slow construction is not evidence of failure. Do not arrive with power tools, machines, or other unsolicited “improvements.”

## 9. Make emergencies plans that do not depend on the surface

Before entry, everyone should know the lost-visitor procedure, refuge locations, communication failures, reserve limits, and response to volcanic signs.

If separated, do not improvise an ascent, descend in search of a bottom, cross open water toward another peak, or blindly follow a warm gradient. Conserve life support and follow the prearranged procedure. Do not tether yourself to vegetation or disturb a channel in an attempt to make a signal.

Help may not be immediately visible, so your expedition must be capable of sustaining itself. A surface ship, a distant portal operator, or a rescue team that first has to travel down from above is not sufficient by itself.

If equipment or life support cannot be kept fully functional without touching gardens, blocking lanes, or making dangerous noise, the correct response is to end the excursion—not to make the town absorb the cost of your visit.

## 10. Keep the first visit small

A good first itinerary might consist of:

1. Remote surveying and biological screening.
2. Permission, a guide, and agreed signals.
3. Arrival near local noon in a pressure-safe, low-disturbance vessel.
4. A patient wait at the designated outer point.
5. Observation from one agreed public area, with entry into a court only if your full equipment can move there without contact.
6. No visit to the oldest narrow passages, no quarry climbing, and no crossing to outlying peaks.
7. Departure with substantial reserves, before fatigue makes you careless.

You may spend long minutes seeing almost nothing: black-green water, nearly motionless mineral flakes, the faint interruption of a root-velvet marker. Then regularity will emerge—a straightened fracture, evenly spaced ledges, the edge of a retaining wall. Perhaps a gardener will tie back one frond. Perhaps you will hear a single padded masonry blow, followed by a long interval in which the loudest sound is your own equipment.

That is not a failed visit. It is probably the visit the place can most safely offer.

## 11. Leave as carefully as you arrived

Account for every object. Remove all food residue, wastewater, filters, cartridges, packaging, and other waste. Complete any required decompression or medical observation; do not let a convenient return route rush it. Quarantine and decontaminate equipment under containment, and do not carry home bacterial felt, mineral samples, leaves, or “unclaimed” tools.

Record only what your hosts permit. Do not publish sensitive routes or instructions that would allow less careful travelers to follow you.

Bring more patience than luggage, and more restraint than light. The Unweathered Town records its history through additions: mineral upon stone, root upon wall, room upon quarry. The best visit is one that leaves no unintended addition behind—no scar, chip, chemical trace, severed frond, or dropped tool remaining where it fell for the next several generations.
