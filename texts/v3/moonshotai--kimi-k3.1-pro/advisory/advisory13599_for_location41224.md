# Preparing for the Serein Catchment

Treat the catchment as three places at once: **a high-altitude dry landscape, an operating industrial installation, and an extremely young forest with almost no soil to spare**. Its greenery is not a promise of drinking water, its silence is not abandonment, and its apparent distances are not usable distances.

A good first visit should be much smaller than the view from the southern rim invites you to attempt.

## Before crossing over

Confirm the basics needed by an unmodified human: atmospheric composition and pressure, actual elevation, temperature range, and the length of the local day. “Thin and cool” is not enough information to plan around. If your method of travel takes you directly from low elevation to the catchment, remember that your body receives no acclimatization merely because the journey was instantaneous. Acclimatize elsewhere if the altitude warrants it, keep the first exposure short, and retain an immediate way to leave.

If there is an operator, scientific authority, or maintenance organization, obtain permission and a briefing. Ask specifically:

- Which paths, if any, are safe for people rather than merely for maintenance mechanisms?
- Whether visitors may approach the condenser masts or condensary.
- Whether any shelters are genuinely available in emergencies.
- How to summon help, and whether anyone is likely to receive the summons.
- Whether any hollows or prepared channels present an overflow risk.

The absence of residents, smoke, or visible staff proves nothing. The annual trimming of a door seam and the continuing operation of buried valves suggest a system that may be attended rarely but is still very much in use. If you cannot establish that a route is open and safe, content yourself with observation from the rim rather than treating the whole basin as accessible wilderness.

Travel with at least one companion, but keep the party small—ideally two to four people. A larger group would have difficulty remaining together in the forest without trampling its margins or communicating noisily. Leave a route, expected return time, and overdue-response plan with someone outside the catchment. Do not assume that local radio, satellite navigation, or your usual multiverse return method will work simply because it worked elsewhere.

Clean your boots, clothing, pack, and equipment before arrival, and decontaminate them again before departing. A visitor carrying mud, seeds, fungal fragments, or water between worlds could become an extraordinarily efficient dispersal mechanism. Take no biological souvenirs home.

## Plan for deceptive scale and no support

The opposite slope is not a reasonable destination merely because a mast on it looks close. Folds of ground can turn an apparently short walk into half a day, and the return may be harder than the approach. Use surveyed distances, bearings, elevation changes, and realistic travel times—not the size of objects in the clear air.

Service paths are not a trail network. They are narrow, discontinuous, and may lead only to a valve, a door, or a dead end. Do not navigate by hopping from mast to mast, following buried pipes, or assuming that concrete steps and rails connect to a settlement. Similarly, “lower ground” is not necessarily the way out: the linked hollows descend toward a fault wall, not necessarily toward safety.

For a first visit, a sensible plan is:

1. Arrive at a known, stable point on or near the southern rim.
2. Observe the basin with binoculars before walking anywhere.
3. Follow one authorized route to the forest edge or one nearby installation.
4. Return by the same route with substantial reserves of daylight, water, and energy.

Do not try to cross all three hollows, reach the condensary, and explore the fault wall in the same excursion. Unless there are designated visitor facilities, do not plan to camp. The maintenance shelters are not cabins, and the empty, green-sided channels are particularly poor campsites: they were prepared for water even if their beds have never carried more than a stain.

## What to carry

### Water and food

Carry all the water you expect to need, plus a reserve for a substantially delayed return. Depending on exertion and conditions, a full day may require several liters per person. Divide it among multiple containers so that one failed cap or punctured bottle does not become an emergency. Drink regularly; dry lips on the rim and damp clothing beneath the canopy can both conceal how much water your body is losing.

Water treatment is a prudent backup, but it does not make the catchment’s water a dependable supply. Do not plan around:

- Ceramic cups beneath condenser masts.
- Water dripping briefly onto stone.
- Black pools beneath slabs.
- Damp clay hollows.
- Rare rainfall.
- Wet leaves or root chambers.

That water may be ecologically important, industrially allocated, inaccessible, contaminated, or simply absent when you need it. Never take condensate casually just because it looks clean.

Bring all your food. There is nothing to forage or hunt, and local fungi, leaves, and pool creatures should not be treated as provisions. For a short visit, ready-to-eat food is preferable to cooking. Avoid open fires entirely.

### Light, cold, and damp

The sunlight is more threatening than the air temperature initially suggests. Bring high-quality UV-blocking eye protection, preferably with side coverage; a brimmed hat; sunscreen; and lip protection. Do not rely on the early hour to spare you the glare. Mica and waxy foliage can make looking around genuinely tiring.

At the same time, carry an insulating layer and light gloves. Exposed stone can be painfully bright yet cold to the touch, and a long, motionless observation in sharp shade can chill you after walking. Wear breathable long trousers and sleeves, sturdy broken-in boots with protective soles and good traction, and gaiters if you will cross scree or wet vegetation. Keep a dry base layer and socks sealed in your pack. Cuffs and knees may become wet beneath the canopy even when no rain has fallen.

A rain shell is still necessary. Clouds can remain beyond the western rim for days before rain arrives abruptly, and a path that appears merely speckled may conceal vegetation loaded with water.

### Rock, navigation, and emergencies

The scree has no kindly rounded surfaces. Expect sharp edges, unstable blocks, cuts, and ankle injuries. Do not scramble near the rim, overhanging slabs, or fault wall without appropriate competence; a helmet is advisable anywhere rockfall is a credible hazard, though avoiding those areas is better. Trekking poles can help on open broken ground, but use them carefully and fit non-damaging tips where appropriate. Never probe among pipes or root-covered machinery with them.

Carry redundant navigation: a paper route description, map if available, compass, altimeter, and any electronic navigation known to function locally. Screens can be difficult to read in the hard light, so do not make them your only copy. Bring a headlamp and spare power even for a day visit, but use lights carefully around light-sensitive pool life.

Your emergency kit should address bleeding, punctures, sprains, blisters, cold exposure, and your personal medical needs. Add an emergency bivouac, a whistle, a visual signaling device, and a compatible communicator if one exists. The whistle is for emergencies, not routine contact in the forest.

Bring a pack-out waste kit. There is essentially no soil in which to bury anything, and human waste, soap, or food residue could persist or contaminate an isolated reservoir.

Finally, bring binoculars, a notebook, and patience. They will reveal more than an attempt to reach every visible structure.

## Moving through the landscape

On open rock, assume every step may be slower and less stable than it looks. Keep people from walking directly above one another on loose slopes. Do not dislodge blocks for amusement or to test the silence.

At the forest edge, remember that green ground is not necessarily soft ground. The root and fungal felt may lie directly over stone, pipes, valves, or uneven fractures. Stay on established durable routes. Do not push into apparently short vegetation simply because the trees are not tall: the forest’s depth, not its height, is the obstacle. A person can vanish from sight within a few steps, and the foliage may close behind without leaving a useful trail to follow.

Keep visual contact with your companions. If doing so requires leaving the path or repeatedly calling out, the route is unsuitable for your party.

Do not cut branches, clear roots, or take samples. A crowded thicket may be one ancient clonal organism, and a small wound may affect something far older and larger than the stem in front of you. Dead leaves are not useless litter; they may remain intact for years as part of the catchment’s extremely slow soil-making process.

## Around the machines: be quieter than seems necessary

The quiet is functional, not merely atmospheric. Set phones and cameras to silent, pad loose metal objects in your pack, and avoid running, tapping, clapping, or testing echoes. Do not use a drone for an overview. Do not shake foliage to make it drip, strike a pipe to see whether it sounds full, or touch cold tubing out of curiosity.

Stand well back from condenser masts. Your hands can contaminate surfaces, your body is a source of heat, and your movement can disturb the very process you came to see. Never use a mast as a handhold, backrest, drying rack, or trellis for your own convenience.

Likewise, leave numbered valves, insulators, ceramic cups, gauges, recessed doors, and root-enclosed machinery alone. Do not “help” by freeing a valve from wood, cleaning lichen from a marker, moving a collector along, or opening a door to see whether anyone is inside. A gauge that appears motionless may be working on a timescale of months.

The condensary should be treated as an operating plant, not a ruin. No chimney and no smoke do not mean no energy, pressure, refrigeration, or electrical hazard. Observe it from an authorized distance unless explicitly permitted to approach.

In a genuine emergency, of course, make whatever noise and use whatever shelter or equipment are necessary to preserve life. Concern for a little condensate must never delay a distress signal.

## Weather, water, and turning back

Do not use familiar river-valley instincts here. There are no dependable streambeds to follow, no gravel bars on which to rest, and no sequence of pools that necessarily leads to a larger source. Rain may vanish so completely that you doubt it occurred. A lush patch may indicate a buried distribution pipe rather than a natural spring.

Set firm turnaround conditions before entering:

- A latest time based on safe remaining daylight.
- A minimum water reserve that will not be used except for the return or an emergency.
- A limit for deteriorating footing, weather, or visibility.
- Immediate action for injury or symptoms of altitude illness.

Unusual headache, nausea, dizziness, or exhaustion at altitude should stop the visit. Confusion, loss of coordination, or breathlessness at rest requires urgent descent or evacuation by the safest known route—not simply continuing downhill into another hollow.

If separated in the forest, follow the protocol agreed beforehand. Generally, stop rather than crashing onward through vegetation in the hope of encountering someone. Retrace your steps only if the route is unambiguous and safe; otherwise remain where you can be found and use your emergency signal. Assume no caretaker will happen along in time to rescue you.

## What the visit will actually feel like

The first mismatch will be between eye and body. The basin looks wet and compact; you experience it as dry and large. Your lips may tighten on the rim while, later, your cuffs slowly darken beneath the canopy. Bright stone can dazzle your eyes and chill your fingers in the same minute.

The second mismatch will be auditory. You may keep listening for birds, running water, wind, or distant machinery and hear none of them. Your own breathing and clothing may begin to seem intrusively loud. Then a pipe ticks once, or a drop falls inside a cistern, and the silence becomes spatial rather than empty.

At a condenser, you may initially conclude that nothing is happening. To see the collector move, choose a fixed branch behind it and watch patiently. Even then, the reward may be only a change in alignment. Do not try to make the installation perform for you.

The hardest thing for a visitor may be accepting that a day is not the catchment’s natural unit of time. Its meaningful events include a root sheath thickening over decades, a fracture remaining damp for a few more centuries, and a prepared channel waiting for water that has not yet arrived.

So bring **more water than the greenery suggests, more warmth than the sunlight suggests, more time than the distances suggest, and a much quieter itinerary than the emptiness seems to permit**. A successful visit is not one in which you reach every structure. It is one in which you leave with the next condensation bead, the next root tip, and the next cold stone undisturbed.
