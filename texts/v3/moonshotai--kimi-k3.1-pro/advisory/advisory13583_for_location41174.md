## Prepare for the heat first, and the impossible second

Treat the Unfinished Orchard as a remote, high-altitude industrial hot room containing an extraordinary ecosystem—not as a garden visit. Nothing described suggests that it is predatory, actively hostile, or likely to produce a sudden temporal catastrophe. The dangers most likely to hurt an ordinary human are much more conventional: the mountain approach, thin air, dehydration, burns, heat illness, sharp mineral surfaces, electrical contact, and difficulty getting back through the cleft while unwell.

The temporal strangeness is the reason to visit. It should not distract you from the reasons to leave.

### Before making the approach

**Arrange a boring, reliable way home.** Your departure should not depend on noon, a seam pulsing, the hum stopping, the fourth grove becoming tangible, or the orchard reaching some imagined completion. Those processes may operate over years, centuries, or longer.

**Acclimatize to the altitude before exerting yourself.** Outside the cavity, the air is thin and sharp. Heat acclimatization is also useful, but it does not make kiln-like conditions safe. Anyone with significant cardiac, respiratory, or heat-tolerance problems should be especially cautious; combining altitude, exertion, and extreme heat is a poor way to discover one’s limits.

**Treat the mountain as a serious route-finding problem.** There may be no softened trail, convenient gravel, or familiar weathered landmarks to guide you. Fresh crystalline edges can be sharp, and an exact-looking ledge is not automatically a trustworthy handhold. Use the climbing equipment the approach actually demands, including a helmet where falling or dislodged material is a possibility. Do not assume that the absence of scree means the route is safe.

The cleft is easy to miss from only a few paces away. Carry precise approach notes and more than one means of navigation. If necessary, use a temporary, removable marker outside and retrieve it afterward; do not paint or carve the mountain. Leave enough daylight for the descent, and set your turnaround time before entering.

**Do not go alone if you can avoid it.** For a first visit, ideally one person remains at or just outside the entrance while the other makes a short reconnaissance. Assume that no local rescue service, animal, inhabitant, or attendant will come to help.

### What to carry

- **All your water, plus a reserve**, calculated for the entire mountain journey rather than merely the time inside. Include electrolytes if appropriate. Use leakproof, preferably insulated containers.
- **Cold-weather protection for the exterior approach**, including a dry layer for after you emerge. You may leave the cavity overheated and sweaty and then become chilled rapidly outside.
- **Light protective clothing for the cavity**, sturdy boots with heat-resistant soles, suitable gloves, and eye protection. Avoid bare skin against stone or vegetation, but do not improvise a heavy, airtight suit that makes the heat burden worse.
- **A slim, soft, low-profile pack**, or several small bags. A bulky framed pack may make the sideways entrance needlessly hazardous. Leave approach ropes, large metal hardware, and trekking poles outside unless they are essential for safe exit.
- **A watch, notebook, and ordinary camera.** A thermometer and atmosphere monitor rated for the environment are more valuable than specialized occult equipment. At minimum, consider oxygen and carbon dioxide monitoring if available.
- **Normal mountain emergency and first-aid supplies**, with particular attention to cuts, burns, dehydration, and heat illness.

Do not assume the green vegetation means the air is breathable or enriched with oxygen. Its biology is not yours. Likewise, the resinous smell and absence of smoke are not proof that the atmosphere is safe. A cloth dust mask is not life support. If you cannot establish that both the heat and atmosphere are within human tolerance, confine yourself to observation from the threshold or use properly rated remote equipment.

### Entering the cavity

Hydrate and cool yourself before entry. Keep the exit clear, remove anything that might snag, and do not force your body through if your chest or equipment binds. A person becoming wedged in the cleft would turn a strange but manageable visit into an emergency very quickly.

Make the first entry **short—minutes, not an intended afternoon**. Set the limit while you are still outside and thinking clearly. A thermometer reading is useful, but your planned exit should not depend on reaching a particular symptom. “I still feel fine” is not a sufficient reason to extend the visit.

Hydration is not heat protection. Water can help replace losses; it cannot make an excessively hot enclosure safe. Do not rely on a wet towel, repeated drinking, or sheer determination.

Once inside:

- Stay in the broad bare intervals wherever possible.
- Do not sit or kneel casually on the floor; salt, glass, metal, and stone may be hot, sharp, or conductive.
- Avoid touching the walls to steady yourself, especially while standing among roots or metallic seams.
- Keep water sealed unless you actually need it. A major spill could contaminate a uniquely water-free environment, alter its chemistry, and create an unwanted conductive path.
- Do not eat, smoke, light a flame, camp, or leave waste inside.

Leave immediately for dizziness, headache, nausea, confusion, unusual weakness, breathing difficulty, equipment alarms, or simply feeling abruptly unwell. Do not wait to see whether the sensation is “the orchard” or merely your body. Treat it as your body first.

### Moving around the groves

The orchard’s electrical system may be gentle, but “natural” is not a safety rating. Its scale and voltage are not established merely because there are no machines or cables.

Stay clear of fertile plumes, root-beds, exposed metallic deposits, and places where seams meet the walls. Do not touch a plume to test its charge, put an ear against a humming wall, or bridge two surfaces with a metal tool or your body. Remove dangling jewelry and avoid trailing conductive equipment. Anyone with implanted medical electronics should treat the uncharacterized electrical environment as an additional reason for caution.

Do not collect specimens. In particular:

- **Do not break a stem to obtain the clear fluid.** It is not drinking water, and it may be chemically or electrically active as well as essential to the plant.
- **Do not take pale, delaminating foliage.** It is not necessarily rubbish; it is material being reclaimed over centuries.
- **Do not pocket loose metallic crystals or bands.** They may be part of the wall, the circuit, or the manufacturing process.
- **Do not assume a tiny green grain is inert sand.** It may be a young plant whose first leaf is still decades away.
- **Do not “prune,” water, feed, or transplant anything.** There is no gardener’s role for you to fill.

There are no animals to avoid feeding and nothing known to hunt or startle you. Weapons and sampling tools are less useful than restraint.

### How to handle the fourth grove

No special temporal ritual is indicated. There is no basis for expecting rapid aging, memory loss, sudden disappearance, or the fourth grove springing into existence around a visitor. The revisions described are slower than growth.

The sensible precautions are therefore simple:

**Do not confuse a shadow with an object or an apparent age with a date.** Three groves may be physically present while a fourth is botanically precise on the rear wall. A groove that looks ancient may have appeared only shortly before the roots that seem to have made it. If you keep notes, number your observations in your own order rather than trying to infer sequence from how weathered something looks.

**Do not try to assist causality.** Do not scratch the blank wall where the hum is strongest, insert metal into a promising seam, extend a root-channel, lay down a wire, or place an object where a future trunk “ought” to stand. You would not be completing a puzzle; you would be contaminating a process you do not understand.

**Do not wait on the bare glass for something to happen.** The corresponding grove may require more than a human lifetime. There is no scheduled manifestation and no indication that standing there makes you part of it.

A stronger hum is not necessarily a countdown, and its cessation is not necessarily an all-clear. Similarly, an isolated crystal tick is not a reason to panic. Leave because of your predetermined time limit, your physical condition, or a concrete environmental problem—not because you have interpreted an ambiguous sound as a personal warning.

If you and your companion disagree about what you saw, record both accounts and leave calmly if the disagreement makes either of you disoriented or distressed. Do not conduct prolonged metaphysical debates in extreme heat.

### What the visit will probably feel like

The heat may register before the beauty. After the constriction of the cleft, you will be in a small room rather than a vast hidden valley. The air will be almost motionless. Your breathing, clothing, and footsteps may seem louder than the orchard itself. The hum may sit at the edge of hearing or seem to belong to the entire room; occasional ticks will not necessarily form a rhythm.

The groves will be lavish but profoundly still. No insects will move through the plumes. No leaves will rustle for your benefit. You could watch for hours without seeing a leaf turn, a root extend, or a mineral layer form.

Meanwhile, ordinary sunlight will make the impossible shadow look completely factual. You may repeatedly look from the rear wall to the bare floor, searching for some obstruction or hidden object that explains it. The absence of theatrical effects can make this more unsettling, not less. There is no darkness to blame and no transition into a dream.

It is possible to feel both disappointed and overwhelmed: disappointed because nothing visibly happens, overwhelmed because your body is under stress and your eyes insist that something absent is casting a shadow. That combination is normal. Plan to leave while curiosity is still telling you to remain a little longer.

Once outside, deal with heat exposure first, then protect yourself from cooling too quickly in damp clothing. Descend before darkness, record your observations while they are fresh, and do not reinterpret ordinary exhaustion as a message from the orchard.

A successful visit should be operationally uneventful. You enter briefly, touch nothing, spill nothing, take no souvenir, and leave without waiting for completion. The mountain does not need to rise noticeably, the plants do not need to move, and the fourth grove does not need to arrive. The orchard is already doing the thing you came to see.
