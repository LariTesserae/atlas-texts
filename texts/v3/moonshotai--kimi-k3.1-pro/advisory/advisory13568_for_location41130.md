Treat the Unfinished Cirque as **an active alpine construction site, an unvalidated machine, and an exceptionally fragile ecosystem**—not as a ruin, a shelter, or a small scenic bowl. An ordinary human could probably make a brief visit, but the visit should remain optional, short, and easy to abandon.

The cirque does not need to be hostile to be dangerous. It only needs to continue.

Several ordinary instincts will mislead you there. Warm air does not mean the altitude is harmless. Transparent water does not mean drinkable water. Solid-looking stone does not mean permanent ground. An echo does not necessarily locate a wall. And apparently empty wet rock may be both densely alive and part of the construction process.

## Before approaching

**Learn the actual elevation and prepare for the approach separately.** The warmth appears local to the cirque; the route up the mountain may still involve cold, wind, poor visibility, or technical climbing. Acclimatize appropriately, ascend slowly, and carry a real descent plan. Headache with nausea, unusual clumsiness, confusion, or breathlessness at rest are reasons to descend, not conditions to monitor while waiting for the next interesting event.

Do not go alone. A companion is important even though the cirque is only a few hundred paces across: a sprained ankle, a hidden cliff boundary, or a changed return route could make a short walk impossible. Ideally, leave another person or a remote observer at a surveyed base outside the active area, with agreed check-in and overdue times.

That base should not be on the open rim, beneath an extruding cliff, or beside any of the structures. If your multiverse-travel method uses a fixed anchor, put it somewhere outside the cirque’s construction zone and make sure you can reach it without depending on local machinery. **Never assume the black ring is a transportation device, and never make it part of your exit plan.** Keep essential return equipment with you, protected from water and shock, rather than in a pack left on the ground.

Before entering, use a period of comparatively good visibility to identify:

- the access and exit route;
- the open side above the clouds;
- a generous buffer from that side;
- the positions of the six structures;
- any visibly active seams, suspended grains, or ruled shimmer.

If you cannot establish where the open side is without walking toward it through mist, do not enter. There is no safe technique for “finding the edge” by pacing, throwing stones, or following echoes.

Set a turnaround time on an independent watch. Do not plan to camp, sleep, or wait for completion. There may be no completed state and no reliable construction schedule.

## What to carry

Dress for **warm wetness inside the cirque and colder mountain conditions outside it**:

- Boots with excellent grip on smooth wet rock, plus long, quick-drying trousers and sleeves.
- A ventilated rain layer rather than clothing that will make you overheat while exerting yourself in thin air.
- A sealed dry base layer, insulation, and spare socks for the descent or an emergency outside.
- Gloves suitable for occasional contact with sharp rock, clear eye protection, and a head net for midge columns.
- Well-fitted hearing protection, with additional muffs available. The construction cracks are already described as painful; do not wait for the first crack to think about your ears.
- A headlamp with a diffuse setting and spare power. Flat light and wet highlights will make ledges and pool margins difficult to judge, while a narrow beam may simply turn mist into a white wall.
- A watch, notebook and pencil, basic first-aid supplies, blister and sprain care, and normal mountain emergency equipment.
- Enough food and drinking water for the visit and a delayed return.

Do not rely on the local pools. Their clarity says nothing about dissolved minerals, unfamiliar microbes, or machinery in the water. Boiling or ordinary filtration would not prove them safe. Do not bathe in them, however inviting the warm water looks.

A trekking pole may help with balance, but it is not a tool for probing fractures, active seams, pools, or structures. Rope is not a universal answer either: never anchor yourself to a pylon, the ring, fresh stone, or a cliff whose growth and strength are unknown. If the approach requires technical rope work, that requires proper mountaineering competence, not improvisation.

An ordinary dust mask should not be treated as proof that the mist’s microscopic machinery is safe to breathe. Keeping exposure brief and avoiding active shimmer are more important than using a mask to justify lingering.

## How to move inside

Walk slowly and keep your party together. A useful arrangement is for one person to observe while the other remains responsible for watching the exit and the mist; swap roles only while stopped. The person taking photographs should not be the person navigating.

Stop before looking up at a kite-wing or stepping back for a better view. A silent diving predator may be less dangerous than the startled step it provokes toward an unseen edge.

Favor bare footing where possible, but do not scrape away wet films to improve traction. The stone has no mud or rounded gravel to soften a fall, and its fresh fractures and crystals may be sharp. Keep fingers and boots out of cracks, pinholes, and seams. Do not put a hand on a ledge ending in suspended grains.

**Do not assume that watching a ledge prevents it from growing.** The grains appear motionless while watched; that is not a guarantee that observation makes the process safe. Never place a body part or piece of equipment within an active construction volume to test the idea.

Look behind you whenever you stop. Your exit is not a line on a map; it is something that must be repeatedly verified. A new pool, thickened patch of floor, or altered wall behind you means the route is already out of date. Do not simply retrace your footprints.

Keep well away from the cloud boundary and avoid unnecessary time beneath cliffs. The absence of scree is not evidence that the cliffs are stable; it may only mean they are too young to have produced the usual warning debris.

## Do not experiment with the structures

None of the six structures should be treated as furniture or infrastructure.

- **The pylons:** Do not touch, climb, strike, or use them as anchors. Material that feels like a firmly held vibration is not a reassuring handrail.
- **The black ring:** Do not pass through it, reach through it, throw anything through it, or use it to frame a photograph. Keep the whole party out of the area where distances begin to disagree with the eye. If a short walk takes an implausible number of steps, stop rather than trying to “average out” the discrepancy.
- **The roofless shelter:** Its dry dome demonstrates that water is being diverted. It does not demonstrate protection from noise, vapor, falling, or new stone. It is not your emergency refuge.
- **The four descending steps:** They are not necessarily a route downward. Do not stand on them to see whether the solid stone will yield.

There are no accessible controls, so do not search for an off switch during an emergency. Distance and departure are your available safety mechanisms.

Also avoid sustained or repetitive sound. Do not sing, hum at the resonators, imitate the bell-newts, play recordings back into the cirque, or test the echoes with claps and whistles. The relationship among choruses, resonator pitch, and seam opening is not understood. What feels like eliciting a delightful response could instead be requesting a growth pulse at close range.

## Treat every wet surface as inhabited

The visible plants are sparse, but the ecological baseline is the film on the rock. A boot across a violet pool margin or copper-green seam can destroy grazers and films in one step. Because later growth often follows grazed trails, your passage might do more than damage the ecology: it might leave a pattern to which the construction responds.

Stay out of pools, avoid the bright grazing trails, and do not handle the newts, crabs, plants, or crystals. Do not feed anything. Carry out food scraps and all other waste; there is no soil in which to bury anything, and the cirque is not a place for human waste or washing.

Clean footwear and equipment before arrival so you do not seed the young ecosystem with organisms from another world. Afterward, keep wet gear contained until it can be cleaned and dried under whatever cross-world biosecurity procedure you use. Do not simply rinse local films or water into the next watershed you visit.

When the rain slackens, the apparent emptiness can become a swarm very quickly. Avoid walking through midge columns, do not swat at insects near an edge, and give emerging sieve-crabs room. The bell-newts are especially useful as **warnings, not guides**. Their gathering where water has yet to appear, sudden movement to higher ledges, or convergence toward a common chorus should all end a casual visit. Do not remain among them to watch what happens.

## If a growth pulse begins

The likely warnings include the resonator tone deepening until it is felt in the teeth, concentric rings forming on pools, and loose droplets lifting above the rock. Newt behavior may provide earlier notice, but there is no guaranteed sequence or countdown.

At the first credible warning:

1. **Abort the visit.** Do not wait to identify which seam will open.
2. Move deliberately toward the verified exit or, if there is not time, away from active seams, cliff bases, the open side, and the structures. Do not run blindly across wet, unfamiliar stone.
3. Put on hearing protection if it is not already in place, secure loose equipment, and keep visual contact with your companion.
4. If caught exposed, stay low on the broadest verified footing available, protect your head and neck, and keep your face out of any visible burst of vapor. The dry dome is not a proven alternative.
5. Wait for the detonations and settling groan to stop and for visibility to return before moving again.

Afterward, assume that **both the map and the acoustics are obsolete**. Do not follow a shout or echo toward a companion or supposed wall. Regroup visually where possible. Inspect the exit rather than assuming it remains open, and do not walk onto newly arrived stone merely because it looks solid. Do not assume there will be only one pulse.

If a new pool is shining behind you and three newts are already calling at its edge, that is not the moment to approach for a better look. It is confirmation that the ground over which you passed has ceased to be a reliable description of the ground on which you must return.

## Leave early, and leave nothing behind

Other sufficient reasons to end the visit include mist erasing your route, unexpected distance discrepancies, eye or respiratory irritation, dizziness, altitude symptoms, separation from your companion, or any active shimmer appearing near your exit. Do not wait to determine whether disorientation comes from thin air, sound, or geometry.

Once outside, check for cuts and clean them with water you brought; assess hearing and breathing; and do not carry away stones, crystals, water, animals, or “inactive” material from a structure. A sample taken from a process that persuades wet air to become rock is not an ordinary souvenir.

A successful first visit may be pleasantly uneventful: warm rain on your sleeves, careful breathing, a distant newt, a cup-plant left untouched, the low tone felt more than heard, and an exit that remains exactly where you verified it. You do not need to inspect all six structures or witness a growth pulse to have visited.

**Leave while you still want to stay for one more chorus.**
