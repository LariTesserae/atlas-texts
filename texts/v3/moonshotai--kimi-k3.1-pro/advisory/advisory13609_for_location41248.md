# Preparing for the High Remainder

The High Remainder should be approached as a **cold, uninhabited, high-altitude expedition with a navigation problem**, not as a climb and not primarily as an encounter with the supernatural. Much of the walking will be easy. That is precisely what makes it treacherous: the landscape invites you to wander far from shelter on the strength of a gentle slope and a clear view, then charges an unpredictable price for returning.

The most dangerous assumption you can carry is: **“We can always go back the way we came.”**

## 1. Settle breathing and extraction before anything else

Do not infer safe air from ordinary sunlight, liquid water, or healthy vegetation. Establish atmospheric pressure and oxygen conditions before making an unprotected arrival, if your means of travel permits it. If the air is human-breathable but thin, use a proper acclimatization plan and treat altitude illness as a serious possibility.

The plateau’s low relief creates a particular problem: **there may be no conveniently lower ground to which you can descend.** “Go downhill if someone becomes sick” may be neither topographically nor geometrically possible. Supplemental oxygen, if needed, should be treated as support for treatment and extraction, not as permission to keep walking.

Your exit is even more important than your map.

- If leaving requires retracing your steps to a fixed arrival point, that point can become effectively unreachable even when it appears nearby.
- If you can choose where to arrive, choose an intact old town, preferably one with continuous pavement, sound walls, and a usable cistern.
- If your exit can be anchored within stable architecture, do so.
- Test any multiverse communication or extraction method under local conditions before you need it. Do not assume that a device which worked elsewhere will work here.
- If you have no dependable exit except a long walk, keep the visit extremely close to that walk’s starting point.

No survey, compass, satellite receiver, or perfectly straight line of travel substitutes for an exit. A compass may be entirely correct while the country between you and home changes its mind.

Do not travel alone. Go with enough people that no one needs to scout alone, be left alone while injured, or be sent back by themselves for help. There is no local rescue service, and a search party can become as lost as the person it seeks.

## 2. Make the first visit a stay, not a traverse

A sensible first expedition is based in one town, with short excursions. It should not attempt to cross the plateau or even to reach the next town.

Towns occur at enormous intervals. Between them may lie days of bare stone, with no dependable water, shelter, or food. A route that looks easy on the outward journey may become three days long on return, lead into country already crossed, or cease to connect at all. There is no demonstrated upper bound that would let you make such a route safe merely by packing “twice as much” or “three times as much.”

Choose the brighter part of the year if other considerations are equal, but do not mistake that for warmth or reliable dryness. Shaded ice survives then, and one watershed can remain under seasonal rain while another stays dry. There is no meaningful plateau-wide forecast on which to base a tight schedule.

Carry reserves for delay, not merely for the planned itinerary: extra food, fuel, medication, batteries, and warm sleeping capacity. Then recognize that reserves mitigate exposure; they do not make an open-country crossing geometrically reliable.

## 3. Pack for cold, wet stone and total self-sufficiency

### Clothing and shelter

Plan for freezing conditions on most clear nights, cold rain, cloud immersion, and long periods of wind on shelterless bedrock.

Bring:

- A complete insulating layering system, including spare dry base layers.
- A genuinely waterproof shell, trousers, gloves or mittens, and head protection.
- More hand and foot insulation than you expect to need; cold, wet stone is hard on both.
- Footwear with good wet-rock traction, plus trekking poles for balance and for probing frost crust, moss, and concealed pavement.
- Eye protection and sunscreen. Being above cloud decks can mean severe ultraviolet exposure even when the air feels cold.
- A sleeping system rated comfortably below the expected minimum, with a dry backup for critical insulation.
- Substantial insulation beneath you. A cold stone floor will drain heat through an inadequate sleeping pad. Two pads, or an equivalent redundant system, are sensible.
- A freestanding or minimally anchored shelter. There may be no soil in which to place stakes.
- Waterproof pack liners and separate dry storage for sleeping equipment, medication, and records.

A roofed town chamber may offer valuable spatial stability, but judge it first as an old structure. Slate roofs fall, dry-laid walls collapse, and stone slab doors can jam or crush. Often the best compromise is your own shelter pitched on dry, continuous pavement inside a sound court, rather than beneath a dubious overhang.

Do not plan to burn local wood. The sheltered trees are scarce, slow-growing components of fragile pockets of soil and vegetation. Carry a cold-weather stove, ample fuel, and redundant ignition. Use it with ventilation; an ancient stone room can kill you by ordinary carbon monoxide poisoning just as efficiently as a modern one.

### Food

Bring essentially all of it.

There is nothing to hunt or fish, and no reason to assume that any moss, fernlike plant, fungus-like growth, or garden remnant is edible. The deep-soil gardens are not evidence of surviving food crops. Choose food that remains palatable when you are cold, tired, and at altitude, and include food that can be eaten without cooking if the stove fails.

There is little reason to devote weight to weapons or hunting equipment. Put that weight into food, insulation, water capacity, and medical supplies.

### Water

The Remainder can be wet without being conveniently watered. Condensation, cloud combing, brief rain, and a few hours of meltwater do not amount to a reliable supply network.

- Carry insulated bottles inside the pack, not exposed skins or external hydration hoses that will freeze.
- Have enough capacity to take advantage of water when it appears, even if your plan says another source is close.
- Fill during rain collection or melt windows, but do not stand in channels or enter reservoirs to do so.
- Treat water from tarns, gutters, ice, and cisterns. “No animals” does not mean “sterile,” and plant decay, sediment, spores, and your own contamination remain possible.
- Protect water filters from freezing, and remember that chemical treatment may work slowly in very cold water.
- Carry a treatment backup and enough fuel to melt ice if necessary.

A cistern’s persistence is reassuring about its identity, not about its potability or fullness. Likewise, a dry channel may have been dry for generations and still fill in separate sections when rain comes. Never camp in a channel, conduit, empty reservoir, or tarn hollow merely because it looks long disused.

### Medical and repair equipment

Prepare especially for hypothermia, frostbite, dehydration, altitude illness, infected wounds, blisters, and sprains. A twisted ankle is much more serious when the afternoon return can become a three-day problem.

Carry extra personal medication, foot-care supplies, wound care, cold-weather repair materials, and a means of keeping essential medication from freezing. Altitude medication and oxygen planning should be discussed with a qualified clinician before travel.

Do not explain every symptom away as local weirdness. If someone becomes confused, uncoordinated, severely short of breath, or unusually drowsy, treat it as a possible medical emergency. A changed skyline may be geography; a companion who cannot walk straight may be hypothermic or altitude-sick.

## 4. Use navigation methods appropriate to places, not to a single map

Paper, pencil, mechanical watches, sketches, and written observations will be more useful than an impressive global map.

For each local route, record separately:

- The starting and ending places.
- The outward travel time.
- The return travel time.
- Weather and cloud cover.
- The condition of any causeway, wall, or pavement.
- Available shelter and water along the way.
- What the reverse view looked like.

A table of recent traversals is more honest than a line drawn through empty country. Photograph skylines in both directions. Do not identify a basin only by a distinctive plant colony; apparently identical growth and frost damage can occur in separated hollows.

Compasses, pace counts, radios, and electronic instruments may still be useful locally. None should be promoted to a guarantee. Triangulation can produce precise numbers describing relationships that no longer hold. Breadcrumb markers and cairns may help with immediate orientation, but they do not establish a permanent connection to camp.

Most importantly, **do not navigate downhill**. Water always runs downhill while watched, yet its next section may occupy another basin. Following a stream is not a way out. Neither is picking a distant green hollow and walking toward its color.

## 5. Travel conservatively

Prefer intact causeways to open country. They make outward and return journeys approximately reciprocal, which is better than the wilderness offers, but still not a contract. Stay on the causeway rather than taking a tempting shortcut across bare stone. Treat a badly breached or discontinuous section as a warning, not merely an obstacle to step over.

Travel in daylight and clear weather whenever possible. Keep the next refuge in view when you can. Avoid long loop routes, which assume that country will close neatly behind you.

Every person leaving base should carry personal emergency warmth, shelter, food, water treatment, light, and essential medication—even for a short excursion. A day walk can become an unplanned bivouac without any dramatic event announcing the transition.

Cloud cover deserves particular caution. When horizons begin disappearing:

- Stop nonessential movement before visibility is gone.
- If you are in a stable town, remain there.
- If you are exposed, prioritize physical shelter and warmth rather than trying to outrun the cloud.
- When the view returns, establish what is still true locally before deciding where you are. Do not immediately walk toward the most familiar-looking feature.

If a road bends differently, a swell appears from the wrong side, or a return takes longer than expected, stop treating the anomaly as a puzzle to solve by walking farther. Shelter, record the observation, check everyone’s physical condition, and reassess. A changed route should be handled more like a road closure than an invitation to improvise.

Never split the party to test two possibilities.

## 6. Use the towns, but do not romanticize them

The towns are the best demonstrated safety infrastructure in the Remainder. Favor old, continuous, well-founded work: intact pavement, substantial straight walls, sound courts, and cisterns that have remained themselves over repeated observations.

They require no ritual. There is no need to “activate” them, leave offerings, or perform hourly maintenance. Equally, do not assume that a tent circle, a stretched cord, or a row of visitor-built cairns reproduces their effect. The town agreements appear to reside in weight, repetition, continuity, and construction—not in symbolism alone.

Do not damage that continuity. Avoid removing slate, prying out copper fasteners, widening doorways, dismantling walls for a campfire ring, or clearing a route by breaking masonry. A wall breached in too many places may become just another outcrop.

At the same time, intact geometry does not eliminate ordinary hazards. Vegetation can conceal open cisterns, culverts, deep joints, slick pavement, and drops into reservoirs. Reclaimed streets may be dark and wet beneath low canopies. Probe uncertain ground, use lights, and do not assume that a courtyard visible through greenery is empty.

If you leave a cache, place it in the most stable shelter available, sealed against water and cold. Do not make that cache your only supply of something essential, and never store supplies in a water cistern.

## 7. Respect the silence and the living cover

The absence of animals removes many familiar expedition concerns, but it also removes familiar cues. There will be no birds to startle, no insects around water, and no distant animal noise to give scale to the country. Wind may make little sound until it meets a wall. Shouts and whistles may not carry as far as you expect. Keep companions within immediate visual or close signaling range rather than relying on sound to maintain contact.

The silence can be restful, but during a long wait it may also become oppressive. Maintain ordinary routines: regular meals, written logs, sleep schedules, equipment checks, and shared conversation. Do not spend an exposed halt arguing about whose memory of the skyline is correct. Write down the present one and get warm.

Protect the vegetation as you would protect an extremely slow-growing archive. Soil in a sheltered basin may represent an immense accumulation of plant growth and decay. Stay on bare stone or pavement where practical, avoid trampling cushion plants and frost crust, and do not dig sanitation pits in rare deep soil. Pack out waste where feasible, and keep it away from channels and reservoirs.

After rain, avoid deliberately disturbing plants that are releasing spores. There is no evidence that the spores are hostile, but unknown respiratory exposure is an unnecessary complication at altitude. Do not taste specimens or transport soil and plant material between basins. Clean contaminated gear before arrival and before leaving; an ecosystem with no animals and almost no loose soil is not one to casually seed with outside life.

## A prudent first visit

A good first visit would look deliberately uneventful:

1. Arrive in or immediately beside an intact town, with breathing conditions and extraction already addressed.
2. Establish shelter on dry pavement, inspect any roof before trusting it, and secure water and reserve supplies.
3. Spend time learning the immediate courts, walls, cisterns, and causeways.
4. Make only short excursions in clear weather, with full emergency equipment, and record outbound and return travel separately.
5. Suspend travel during cloud or disorientation, and leave while substantial reserves remain.

Much of the visit should consist of drying clothing, protecting bottles from ice, listening to rain tick into cisterns, examining moss and quartz at close range, and waiting patiently beneath an ordinary pale sky. If the most dramatic event is that a familiar swell appeared from the wrong side and you declined to walk toward it, you have probably traveled well.

The High Remainder does not appear to require courage, conquest, or appeasement. It requires **margins**—of warmth, food, water, time, and attention—and the discipline not to spend all of them on the outward journey.
