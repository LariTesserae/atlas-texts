# Preparing for the Unfinished Hollow

Prepare as though you are visiting a very young ecosystem inside a room that can briefly stop being a room.

Do **not** prepare as you would for ordinary caving. There are no passages to explore, no deep chambers to reach, and no darkness to conquer. The animals are tiny; the light is already provided; the principal dangers are your own scale, contamination, unreliable sequence, and the interval in which the chamber becomes solid marl.

The qualities most useful here are not courage or endurance. They are clean habits, slow attention, and a willingness to leave without having seen everything.

## First decide whether you need to enter at all

For an ordinary human, the best visit will usually be an **observation visit**, through an existing opening or a nonintrusive viewing arrangement maintained by someone familiar with the Hollow.

The root-thin seam is not an invitation to excavate. Widening it, drilling a camera port, or breaking away “just a little” marl could alter the seep, destroy microbial pasture, bury animals, or interfere with whatever process is opening and closing the chamber. Having crossed universes to reach the place does not make enlarging its doorway a small intervention.

Full bodily entry should be considered only if all of the following are true:

- There is already a human-scale means of access that does not require you to damage the Hollow.
- Someone knowledgeable about its behavior has agreed to supervise the visit.
- You can withdraw immediately, without turning around, crawling deeper, moving stones, or waiting for a seam to reopen.
- The atmosphere has been checked rather than merely smelled. Mineral freshness is not proof of breathable air.
- You have an exit plan that does not depend entirely on the seam remaining where it was. If your multiverse transit method is the exit, confirm that it works from inside the Hollow and under local changes—not merely from the surface.
- Another person remains outside, and your rescue plan is something more realistic than “they can dig me out.”

That last point matters. The Hollow is shallow beneath the soil, but conventional shallowness offers little reassurance if the space around a visitor has become solid stone. A shovel is not a rescue plan for that.

Most importantly, **do not infer human safety from the animals’ safety**. Springtails and salamanders may remain visible and uncrushed during the solid interval. That is fascinating, but it is not evidence about what would happen to a human body, human lungs, or human consciousness.

If those conditions cannot be met, observe from outside. That is not a lesser form of visit.

## Choosing when to go

There is no reliably safe season or hour. The solid-marl interval occurs irregularly, and neither rain nor dryness is known to prevent it. Be suspicious of any guide who claims to have found a guaranteed safe window merely because previous visits were uneventful.

Rain above may make the Hollow more rewarding to watch: the seep spreads, microbial skins thicken, springtails emerge, and the rest of the little food web follows. During drier periods, activity contracts around the remaining moisture. Those are different viewing conditions, not different safety conditions.

Several short observations are preferable to one long vigil. A human visitor is not a neutral pair of eyes. Your breath adds warmth and moisture; your clothing carries spores and grit; your footsteps and shadow dominate a place whose significant events may be a mite turning or a droplet falling.

Prepare yourself for bright, cool stillness. The light will not warm you, so bring a light insulating layer. Avoid very dark glasses inside: you need to be able to notice changes in the brightness, and tinted lenses will also make the transparent animals harder to see. If the light is painful or if enclosed spaces make you panic, choose remote observation rather than trying to endure it.

## What to bring

Keep the kit small and clean.

**Suitable:**

- Freshly cleaned, close-fitting, non-shedding clothing, ideally not previously worn in another world’s soil or cave system.
- Clean, soft-soled footwear and simple protection against scrapes if entry is authorized.
- A way to secure long hair, loose sleeves, and dangling equipment.
- Your own drinking water and any necessary food or medication, kept outside the Hollow.
- A notebook, also kept outside, for use immediately afterward.
- Optionally, a camera with no flash, no laser focusing aid, and no loose tripod legs inside the chamber.
- Appropriate atmosphere-sampling equipment if human entry is being considered.
- A small ordinary light for the surface approach, if needed—not a headlamp to blaze around inside.

**Leave behind:**

- Digging tools, sample hammers, collection vials, and “just one small container” for a souvenir.
- Chalk, marker flags, adhesive tape, paint, measuring stakes, or anything used to annotate the chamber.
- Food, open drinks, scented products, and cave gear carrying mud or microbes from elsewhere.
- Flash photography, bright auxiliary lamps, and experiments involving added water.
- Offerings, coins, tokens, or objects intended to see whether the Hollow “incorporates” them.

A rope or tether should not be treated as sufficient protection. It may lead toward an opening that shifts, become pinned, or simply give you confidence you have not earned.

Follow whatever arrival and departure decontamination procedures your guide uses. This is especially important for a multiverse traveler: an invisible organism harmless in your last world could become the Hollow’s first catastrophic invasion.

## How to behave there

Approach quietly and avoid putting heavy equipment or unnecessary weight directly above the shallow lens. Do not stamp, probe, or test the marl with your fingers. Its fresh fractures may be sharp, but they are also crumbly and easily changed.

At the viewing point, let your eyes adjust. At first the chamber may appear disappointingly empty. Resist the urge to make something happen.

Do not lean on the walls, kneel in the central dish, or cross the floor for a better photograph. The vacant center is part of the Hollow, not unused space set aside for visitors. Keep clear of the pool and the seep. Never wash your hands in the water, drink it, or add some of your own.

The three architectural features deserve particular restraint:

- Do not add a flake to the little crescent.
- Do not straighten, sit upon, or step across the threshold slab to see what it does.
- Do not extend the three incisions, place a measuring object beside them, or try to reconcile their geometry with the chamber’s changing dimensions.

Their freshness does not make them unfinished work waiting for your assistance. They may be the most consequential things in the room.

Likewise, do not lift either pseudoscorpion’s stone, pursue the salamander beneath its ledge, or pick up a transparent snail to examine its body. If the salamander remains unseen, accept that. A visit is not improved by turning a hunter’s shelter over.

When the Hollow is dry, be especially careful around the remaining water: much of its living community may be concentrated there. When it is active after rain, remember that the clear skins are pasture, not mineral discoloration.

## Dealing with the anomalies

Some strangeness is normal here and does not, by itself, require panic. A shadow may lag. An echo may precede its sound. Reflections may show animals that have not yet arrived. The correct response is usually to remain still and continue observing—not to investigate.

A few rules will keep wonder from becoming recklessness:

**Do not navigate by shadows, reflections, tracks, or anticipated sounds.** Use the directly visible chamber and your prearranged route out. A track ending at a wall is not evidence that you should touch the wall. A claw ticking inside solid-looking marl is not a request to dig.

**Do not treat a preview as an instruction.** If a reflection or shadow appears to show you doing something, you are not obliged to perform the action in order to keep events consistent. Nor should you conduct experiments to discover whether you can contradict it. An image of apparent safety is not reassurance; an image of danger is not a reason to lean closer.

**Do not use your own body to test the Hollow.** Do not put a finger beneath a reversing drop, place your hand across a shifted pool, or wait to see whether your shadow can act independently. The place has not promised that every anomaly is harmless to visitors.

If delayed shadows and shallow depth cues make you nauseated or uncertain of your footing, stop moving and withdraw calmly if you can. Do not continue deeper simply because the chamber is too small to become conventionally lost in.

Let the person outside keep the visiting schedule. Constant light is a poor measure of elapsed time, and this is not the place to stand around comparing watches. Agree beforehand that either supervisor or visitor can end the visit without debate.

## The solidification warning

One sequence requires immediate action:

- The brightness becomes denser.
- The walls turn luminous.
- The floor loses its grittiness.

**Leave at once.**

Do not wait for a rumble, cracking sound, falling dust, or darkness. The described change is silent. Do not pause for a final photograph, retrieve equipment, or watch the animals to see what happens to them. Do not assume that lying still, flattening yourself against a wall, or holding your breath will help.

If the original seam has moved or vanished, use the independent egress arranged before entry. This is why that arrangement is nonnegotiable. Do not improvise by digging blindly into fresh marl or following sounds into a wall.

There is no honest, generally applicable advice for surviving the solid interval as an ordinary human. The correct preparation is to avoid using yourself as the test case.

## Leaving

Leave when planned, not when something interesting begins. “One more minute” is an easy sentence to say in a place where small events feel like weather and sequences cannot safely be taken for granted.

Check that you have everything you brought. If something falls into the Hollow, do not automatically reenter or delay an emergency withdrawal to retrieve it; tell the guide. A lost button, lens cap, or coin is contamination, but adding a second problem through panic is worse.

Clean yourself and your equipment according to local instructions before traveling onward. Take no marl, water, husks, crystals, “loose” flakes, or living specimens. Here, even remains cannot safely be assumed to be mere debris.

Write your account promptly, separating what you observed from what you concluded. If your sequence does not agree with your companion’s, do not force the notes into a tidier story. Persistent disorientation, breathing difficulty, unexplained injury, or a serious disagreement about elapsed time should be reported before any return visit.

## What a good visit is like

A successful visit may appear almost uneventful.

You remain at the permitted viewpoint, cool despite the white-gold brightness. For a while there is only raw marl, shallow silver shadow, and the vacant dish of the floor. Then a drop ticks. A glassy springtail crosses a damp skin. Something curled moves inside a snail’s transparent shell. The pool reflects a visitor that has not yet come.

You do not discover who made the crescent. You do not see the salamander. You do not learn whether the Hollow is becoming a cave, a room, or something for which those words are premature.

And then you leave.

That restraint is the heart of the preparation. To the Hollow’s inhabitants, your breath is weather and your boot is a landslide. Make yourself brief, clean, quiet, and undemanding. Above all, do not try to help the place finish itself.
