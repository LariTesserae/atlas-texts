# Preparing for a Visit to the Sinter Basin

Treat the Sinter as an operating watershed, factory, residence, and ecosystem occupying the same surfaces. Do not treat it as a picturesque ruin with canals.

For an ordinary human, the principal dangers would not be a single monster or catastrophic event. They would be accumulated heat, damp, noise, contaminated or mechanically active water, poor footing, rapidly expiring route information, and the temptation to assume that something solid-looking will remain solid.

Three assumptions would be especially dangerous:

- **“I can dry out later.”** Perhaps you can reduce visible moisture, but do not count on becoming thoroughly dry.
- **“I can find my way back by sight.”** Frontages, bridges, rooms, and even pavement may cease to exist along your route.
- **“I can just go indoors, climb higher, or wade through shallow water.”** Interiors may be scheduled for removal, apparent height differences are hard to judge, and shallow drainage can reverse violently.

A short, hosted visit to one district is a reasonable first undertaking. An independent crossing of the basin is not.

## What being there would feel like

The first hours would probably be spent fighting habits rather than danger.

You would repeatedly want to wipe your hands, find a quiet corner, and memorize the appearance of streets. None of these strategies would work for long. Water would return around seals and through pavement. Pump vibration would bypass ordinary hearing protection and arrive through floors, boots, and bone. A memorable façade might become exposed ribs or a stack of sealed dwellings before your return journey.

The warmth of the rain would be particularly deceptive. It would not offer the clean shock or cooling associated with rain elsewhere. It would feel like more of the same warm moisture, suddenly falling with force. Even after it stopped, steam, pump noise, and activity would make it difficult to tell whether conditions had actually become safe.

The absence of plants might prove more unsettling than expected. Every distant suggestion of green would promise moss or some softening of the landscape and then resolve into copper staining. There would be no verge on which to sit, no patch of waste ground beyond the city’s attention, and no countryside to which you could retreat if overwhelmed. “Outside” would often mean only a larger exposed piece of the same industrial interior.

At the same time, the Sinter would not feel dead or abandoned. Quite the opposite: wet walls would feed, grates would move with reservoir life, arches would be crowded with roosts, and repair would begin before damage had finished being conspicuous. You would be a guest inside plumbing, structure, and a food web simultaneously.

## Arrange these things before arrival

### 1. A local guide who can read operations, not merely streets

You need someone who understands construction fronts, drainage phases, storm refuges, load nodes, and local alarm signals. A person who only knows addresses and tourist landmarks is insufficient.

Your guide or host should be able to answer:

- How long is the current route information valid?
- Which construction and pressure phases are expected during your stay?
- Where is the designated storm refuge, and what route reaches it?
- How are storm warnings, evacuation orders, and all-calls transmitted when speech is impossible?
- Which water and synthesis foods have been verified as safe for an off-world human?
- Which parts of your lodging are expected to remain physically present throughout your stay?
- What should you do if a load-field crack, drainage reversal, or construction front occurs while you are inside?

That last question about lodging is not eccentric in the Sinter. Ask it plainly.

### 2. Lodging chosen by drainage and structure, not by view

Do not select accommodation because it appears elevated. On this plain, a difference too small to notice may determine whether a district floods. Ask about its behavior during drainage reversal and major storms.

Your lodging should have:

- A known storm refuge or direct access to one.
- At least one recognized evacuation procedure that does not depend on a single bridge, outer room, or retractable connection.
- A reliable way to receive visual or tactile alarms.
- Verified water and food arrangements.
- Protection from construction haze and direct exposure to active outer material.
- Somewhere to sleep where fatigue from vibration and noise can be managed.

The great pump stations and principal channels are useful persistent landmarks. They are **not automatically good shelters**. They may be extremely loud, industrially hazardous, or surrounded by outfalls and flood flows.

### 3. A departure plan independent of temporary architecture

Keep any personal return device, key, beacon, or equivalent on your body in a sealed, shock-resistant case. Do not leave it in an outer room that might depart as a freight block.

If departure requires a fixed site, have that site checked shortly before use and establish an alternate procedure. Never make your only way home a doorway whose continued existence depends on a favorable construction schedule.

Also accept that “I will simply leave the city if things go wrong” is not a useful emergency plan. There may be no walkable outside within any meaningful distance.

### 4. Medical and physical preparation

Before going, assess your tolerance for heat, humidity, respirator use, broken sleep, and prolonged vibration. Heat acclimatization and reasonable walking fitness would help, but they do not make you immune to heat illness.

Plan for:

- Heat exhaustion and dehydration.
- Skin maceration and foot damage.
- Eye irritation from glare, droplets, dust, and construction haze.
- Respiratory irritation from exhaust and fine mineral particles.
- Hearing fatigue and stress from low-frequency vibration.
- Infection or chemical exposure through cuts and abrasions.
- Delayed departure, requiring extra medication and supplies.

A first visit should be short enough that sleep deprivation does not become your main impairment. Build several days of reserve into essential medication and supplies rather than planning to the exact hour.

## Clothing and protective equipment

Where possible, rent or obtain the principal protective equipment locally. Equipment designed for the Sinter is more likely to integrate hearing protection, alarms, communications, and rain protection correctly.

### Head, hearing, eyes, and lungs

Use:

- **A local-pattern protective helmet**, preferably with appropriate hearing protection and an induction or other non-speech communication system.
- **Rated hearing protection**, not ordinary music earbuds or consumer noise cancellation. Consumer devices will not solve vibration transmitted through your body and may prevent you receiving alarms.
- **Anti-fog eye protection**, with additional glare protection for exposed daylight travel. Wet ceramic at noon can be fiercely reflective.
- **Fit-tested respiratory protection appropriate to local hazards.** A particulate filter may help with dust but does not automatically address exhaust vapors, biological material, or active constructor haze. Follow local assessment rather than improvising cartridges.

Keep spare filters and sensitive components sealed until needed. Humidity can degrade equipment that appears perfectly serviceable in its packaging.

Respirators and helmets add to heat strain. Their use must be combined with rest and access to a suitable refuge, not simply worn harder and longer.

### Clothing

Dress for warm, wet industrial conditions, not for a cold rainforest:

- Ventilated, quick-drying clothing with close enough cuffs and hems to avoid snagging.
- A protective storm outer layer that can be donned quickly, but not an impermeable full-body suit intended for continuous wear in heat.
- Gloves for designated work or contact surfaces, while remembering that wet walls may be grazing colonies rather than bare masonry.
- No loose cords, flowing garments, or dangling straps around conduits, shafts, and machinery.

Do not expect clothing hung in a room labelled “dry” to dry completely. Locally, that word may only mean that no water is presently visible.

### Footwear

Footwear is critical. Use protective, puncture-resistant boots or shoes with soles selected for wet, polished ceramic and mineral films. They should either drain well or be part of a locally recommended water-management system.

Avoid open footwear. Sagging steps, rounded canal margins, shell fragments, warm seepage, and hidden colonies make bare or lightly covered feet a poor choice.

Inspect your feet at least daily, and more often on long walks. Treat rubbing and small cracks before they become open wounds. Once skin is broken, keep it away from local water and surfaces until properly assessed.

## What to pack

Keep a small emergency bag on your person whenever you leave lodging. It should contain:

- Your return device and identification.
- A local locator or distress beacon.
- A visual and vibrating alarm receiver, if these are separate from your helmet.
- A rugged light, with a backup.
- A small verified water supply and appropriate electrolytes.
- A compact ration.
- Essential medication.
- Basic wound and foot care.
- A sealed offline reference showing your persistent landmarks, refuge, host contact, and separation procedure.
- A power reserve in a corrosion-resistant, waterproof case.

Main baggage should include spare protective equipment, sealed filter replacements, several changes of suitable clothing, additional food and medication, and moisture-resistant storage for electronics. Use desiccants inside genuinely sealed cases where appropriate, but do not imagine they will dry the room around you.

For water and food:

- Carry enough for immediate needs and delays, but arrange verified resupply for anything beyond a brief visit.
- Bring complete, human-compatible rations if compatibility has not been established.
- Do not assume that ordinary camping filters, ultraviolet treatment, chemical purification, or boiling make local water safe. At best, a treatment might address microbes while leaving minerals, chemical exposure, or constructors unresolved.
- Never drink from channels, cisterns, seepage, or storm water merely because it looks clear.
- Do not forage eels, grazers, waders, or reservoir animals as an emergency adventure. Their chemical accumulation and effects on human digestion are unknown.

Leave behind plants, seeds, pets, and casual biological souvenirs. The Sinter’s ecology is enclosed and highly utilized; introducing outside organisms would be irresponsible.

## Navigation: use persistent geography, not appearances

A street map alone is nearly a promise to get lost.

Record destinations using whatever persistent references the locals use: pump-station identity, drainage divide, principal channel, load node, pressure phase, and current access surface. An address may outlive its building, so the address alone does not tell you what you will find when you arrive.

Good habits include:

- Revalidate your route each morning, before every major leg, and after every storm or nearby construction front.
- Record when the information expires.
- Use local passenger transport rather than improvising rides in freight conduits or counterweighted shafts.
- Never take arches, reservoir covers, bridge rooms, or maintenance passages as shortcuts.
- Confirm that a bridge is currently available before committing to a route with no alternative.
- Arrange meetings at persistent, recognized safe locations, not “the third arch” or “the white frontage.”
- Agree in advance on what to do if separated.

If separated during changing conditions, do not simply chase the last known appearance of your companions. Use the agreed beacon and refuge procedure. A moving search through unfamiliar drainage or construction zones can make rescue substantially harder.

A waterproof notebook or grease pencil is useful as a backup, but even paper cannot rescue obsolete information. Write dates and phases on everything.

## Everyday conduct

Move as though every surface has another user.

Do not set down baggage in an open patch merely because nobody is standing there. It may be a drainage path, a future construction surface, a freight clearance, or space required by a pressure operation. Do not camp.

Other useful rules:

- Do not open seals, hatches, cisterns, or wall panels.
- Do not block even small drains with rubbish or equipment.
- Do not lean casually on wet walls; what appears to be staining may be a dense animal colony.
- Keep hands out of crevices, seepage channels, and outfalls.
- Avoid pump outfalls and street grates, particularly before storms.
- Do not feed animals or introduce synthesis waste into their habitat.
- Treat arches and vertical shafts as occupied industrial spaces, not picturesque voids.
- Use local sanitation and waste-disposal procedures. Organic leakage already feeds the basin’s films; that does not mean additional dumping is harmless.

Learn the local touch or induction conventions for **stop, storm, construction hazard, return, remain here, help**, and **route closed**. Practice them before you need them. During heavy rain, a complicated spoken explanation may be impossible even helmet-to-helmet.

Schedule rest deliberately. Warm rain is not a cooling break. Headache, dizziness, nausea, cramps, unusual irritability, or deteriorating coordination should stop your itinerary. Confusion, collapse, or serious breathing difficulty is an emergency.

## If you encounter a construction front

Warning signs may include granular hissing, grey pellets, a haze of constructor activity, exposed structural ribs, withdrawing surfaces, or the cracking of field footings accepting load.

Do not cross the haze to save time. Do not touch softened material, collect pellets, or stand at the edge to photograph exposed structure.

Instead:

1. Follow the guide or local crew immediately.
2. Withdraw by a confirmed route rather than ducking into an unfamiliar room.
3. Keep loose equipment secured and use respiratory and eye protection as directed.
4. If your lodging is being reworked, take your personal emergency bag and follow the occupants’ procedure. Do not return for ordinary luggage.
5. Treat every footing crack as an instruction to pay attention. It may be normal load transfer rather than collapse, but an untrained visitor cannot use that distinction as permission to remain in place.

A building can remain occupied while its outer material leaves. Your presence inside it is not evidence that every room around you is intended to remain.

## Major storm procedure

The distant signs may be beautiful: a grey lid over remote districts, columns of mist above heat plants, and an advancing line along which wet surfaces suddenly shine. Do not wait for the spectacle to reach you.

At the first local warning:

- Stop nonessential travel.
- Go directly to the preselected refuge.
- Secure loose gear and keep your emergency bag with you.
- Establish visual or tactile communication before the roar makes speech useless.
- Confirm that everyone in your party knows the separation procedure.

During the storm, avoid:

- Canal margins and low passages.
- Pump outfalls and street grates.
- Bridge decks and connections that may withdraw.
- Active construction fronts.
- Outer façades and rooms likely to shed material.
- Arches, shafts, reservoir covers, and improvised rooftop shelter.

**Do not wade through moving storm water merely because it appears shallow.** Drainage reversal, sudden channel formation, submerged openings, structural debris, and violent local currents matter more than depth. If an evacuation becomes necessary, follow rescuers or local crews rather than choosing your own water route.

The noise of emergency load fields may sound like structural failure. The sight of blocks shedding material may look like general collapse. Neither appearance should cause you to bolt blindly into the drainage system; conversely, neither should reassure you. Stay with the refuge procedure.

Do not use the end of rainfall as the all-clear. Wait until local authorities or your host confirm that water has returned to manageable channels, refuge egress is sound, and your route has been revalidated. Animals recolonizing wet surfaces and construction resuming are signs of the Sinter’s normal cycle, not proof that it is safe for you.

## Psychological preparation

Expect sensory fatigue before you expect fear.

Constant vibration can produce irritability, poor judgment, nausea, and profound tiredness even when you believe you have grown accustomed to it. Lack of plants, uncomplicated darkness, silence, and dry clothing can create a peculiar homesickness. The endless built plain may also make distances difficult to estimate.

Bring a small, familiar, non-contaminating personal object. Schedule rest in the best available quiet refuge, but do not expect absolute silence; local quiet may mean only that you can distinguish one pump from another. Use sleep protection only where it is safe to do so and where visual or tactile alarms will still reach you.

Do not mask the pumps continuously with entertainment audio. That usually adds stimulation while reducing awareness.

A good first visit should be deliberately unambitious. Repeated travel between a few persistent landmarks is not a failure to explore. It is how you learn the difference between the Sinter’s durable form and its replaceable substance.

## Before departure

Use local decontamination and inspection procedures rather than simply washing everything when you get home.

- Do not carry away water, grey pellets, fresh ceramic, shells, animal bodies, or “inactive” construction material as souvenirs.
- Check equipment seams and containers for biological hitchhikers.
- Keep potentially contaminated gear contained until cleared.
- Monitor wounds, respiratory symptoms, hearing problems, and persistent effects of heat or sleep disruption.
- Allow recovery time; vibration-related exhaustion may outlast the visit.

## When not to go

Postpone or cancel if:

- You have no guide who can interpret current operations.
- Your only navigation aid is a static street map.
- You have no verified storm refuge or no way to receive its alarms.
- Safe food and water for your physiology have not been arranged.
- Your return depends on one temporary structure.
- You cannot tolerate the necessary protective equipment in the local heat.
- Your emergency plan amounts to walking out of the city or waiting for conditions to become dry and quiet.

The Sinter is weathered, but it is not a ruin; hyperactive, but not chaotic in the way a visitor first assumes. Its old channels and pump geography persist while walls, rooms, and routes pass through them like weather.

Visit it as you would visit a vast industrial operation during maintenance: briefly, attentively, with current information, and without touching anything merely because it looks inactive. You will probably not leave dry, and you may remember the pump stroke in your bones longer than the white glare or the pottery-colored evenings. With proper preparation, however, you can come away having seen something rare: a city whose antiquity lies not in untouched material, but in the patterns that survive continual replacement.
