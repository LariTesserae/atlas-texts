Prepare for the Rootworks as you would for a half-day visit to a flooded, densely overgrown industrial site—not as a quick walk across a hundred paces, but not as an expedition into an overtly hostile wilderness either. Nothing described suggests that it attacks visitors, closes passages within hours, or is actively spreading. Its immediate dangers are more ordinary: exhaustion, concealed footing, stored mechanical energy, disorientation, and contamination in either direction.

Its small size is the main thing likely to make you underprepare.

## Before entering

**Give the visit much more time than the map suggests.** A person can circle the rim in ten minutes, but an interior crossing may take an hour. Add time for reconnaissance, rest, careful observation, and returning by a route you already know. A first visit that includes the pump house should have most of a day available, with a firm turnaround time and ample daylight in reserve. Do not schedule it tightly against a departure or multiversal transit window; you do not want to be hurrying over an elastic root mat because a portal is about to close.

**Avoid a storm-recovery period.** Check not only whether rain is forecast today, but whether the previous week or two has been unusually wet. After prolonged storms, the pools widen and the root mat becomes faintly buoyant; the pumps may need weeks to restore the usual water level. A bright day following such weather is not necessarily the best day to enter.

**Do not make a solo interior crossing.** Use a small party—large groups would trample the place—and leave an off-site contact with your location, intended route, and overdue time. For an extended visit, having someone remain at a base outside the rim is worthwhile. If you must travel alone, confine yourself to approach and rim observation unless you have an unusually reliable rescue arrangement.

Set that base on firm ground outside the hollow. Keep dry clothing, spare water, medical supplies, and contained cleaning equipment there. Do not assume the surrounding featureless flats will make the site easy to find again; carry coordinates or a surveyed approach route if available, and verify that your navigation tools work locally.

## Clothing and equipment

Dress for warm, close air and complete wetness, even after several rainless days.

- **Secure, ankle-supporting boots** with good grip on wet wood and rounded concrete. They should fit well enough that suction or tangled roots cannot easily pull them off. Expect water inside them. Sandals, low trail shoes, and loose rubber boots are poor choices.
- **Light, quick-drying long trousers and sleeves.** These protect against triangular reed stems, rootlets, and slick foliage without becoming as oppressive as heavy waterproof clothing. Carry a rain shell, but do not necessarily walk in it; overheating is a greater immediate concern than staying perfectly dry.
- **Gloves and clear eye protection.** Much of your movement will involve putting a hand against vegetation or parting growth near your face.
- **A small, close-fitting waterproof pack.** Avoid dangling straps, wide loads, and anything likely to snag. Include a headlamp despite the ordinary daylight, a watch, a whistle, a compass, a waterproof notebook and pencil, personal medication, blister and wound supplies, clean irrigation water, and high-energy food.
- **Enough drinking water for the visit plus a reserve.** The clear pools and cistern are not supplies for you. Purification equipment can be an emergency backup, but should not be treated as a guarantee against unfamiliar spores, exudates, or other biological hazards.
- **A blunt-tipped staff or pole**, used gently to test footing. It is for probing, not for repeatedly spearing the root mat.

Full waders are usually a poor default: hot, cumbersome in dense growth, and potentially dangerous if you fall. Likewise, a machete is the wrong standard tool. Cutting your way through would damage structural roots, living belts, buried services, or the chambered canes powering the engines. If a route requires cutting, it is not a visitor route.

Keep a dry set of clothes sealed at base. Inside the hollow, there may be nowhere comfortable or dry to sit, change, or lay out equipment.

## Crossing the rim and moving inside

Walk the rim first. Listen to the pumps from outside, identify the old concrete arcs, and note where ordinary rushes and firm mud resume. Do not assume the rounded concrete forms a safe walkway; it may be wet, stained, and slick.

At your chosen entry, take photographs looking both inward and back toward the rim. A temporary marker can be placed on existing concrete if necessary, but remove it afterward. Do not paint stems, blaze roots, or drive stakes into the floor—the mat may conceal both living tissue and pipes.

Once inside:

- Take short steps and let your weight settle gradually. The root mat’s elasticity can make a hurried stride bounce or slide unexpectedly.
- Do not treat apparent stones as stepping stones. They may be rounded root-knobs.
- Avoid the pools whenever possible. Their clear surfaces conceal dense white stems that divide the water into narrow cells. Do not test their depth with your leg, and do not jump between apparent hummocks.
- Keep your pack and loose clothing close so cable-thin climbers cannot catch them.
- Rest before you are tired. Heat, humidity, and constant resistance from vegetation can make a short distance disproportionately draining.
- Maintain regular visual contact with your companion. Pump strokes, valve cracks, and water hammer can mask voices or make it difficult to judge where a sound came from.

Do not use pipes as handrails, rails as a promised route out, or the ladder as a viewing platform. The rail is only a fragment, the pipes belong to a recirculating system, and the ladder leads into leaves rather than to any known safe destination. Similarly, **do not follow flowing water expecting it to lead outside**: the Rootworks lifts and returns its own water.

The vegetation will not meaningfully grow over your route during an afternoon. The realistic navigation problem is visual repetition and fatigue, not being rapidly entombed. If you become uncertain, stop rather than forcing onward toward the loudest machinery. Retrace a known route if possible; firm mud, separate roots, and ordinary rushes are better indications of the boundary than sound alone.

## Around the pump house

Treat every engine as pressurized machinery, even when it appears motionless.

The absence of a boiler or fuel supply does not mean an absence of stored energy. Water-swollen canes can load the mechanism for hours before a trip valve releases it. A flywheel that seems stationary throughout a conversation may still advance, a counterweight may settle, and a belt may tighten without the sort of continuous motion that normally warns people away.

Stay outside the swept paths of flywheels, piston rods, yokes, belts, and counterweights. Never put a hand through a spoke to test whether it is moving, step across a belt, or brace yourself against a pipe. Do not stand over a cane valve to inspect it. If a valve cracks or a piston advances, do not startle and jump backward into unseen footing; remain still if you are already clear, then move deliberately.

Do not attempt to help. Do not oil a bearing, replace a belt section, clear rust, open the cistern grille, unblock a gutter, or cut roots away from an engine bed. What looks like neglect to a human visitor may be the site’s normal maintenance: woody tissue preserving an iron pipe’s shape, root-gum sealing a joint, or living material flexing as part of a belt.

The old brick, lintel, ladder, and cistern should not be assumed to support a person merely because they have remained stable for decades.

For observation, patience is safer than proximity. A fixed photograph of a flywheel spoke and a stationary reference, repeated later, can reveal motion without requiring you to lean into the mechanism. A notebook record of pump strokes, valve ticks, and water movement will tell you more than poking at individual components.

## Water, health, and weather

Carry all the water you expect to need and drink before you feel severely thirsty. The hollow is warm, and its stillness can make you underestimate exertion. Dizziness, new clumsiness, headache, or unusual irritability are reasons to turn around, not conditions to push through for the sake of reaching the center.

Avoid getting pool water, sap, amber gum, or the green oil-cup exudate into your eyes, mouth, or broken skin. Cover existing cuts, wash your hands before eating, and do not touch your face after handling wet growth. The scarcity of decay odor does not indicate sterile conditions; dead tissue is simply overgrown quickly. Nor do the colorless snails and lone white eel make the water wholesome.

Insects may be scarce, but that is no reason to assume an unfamiliar wetland presents no allergic or infectious risk. If you develop eye irritation, respiratory symptoms, or an escalating skin reaction, leave and rinse the affected area with clean carried water. Any later medical assessment should include the fact that the exposure was to an unfamiliar biology.

Rain will be experientially confusing. It begins as muffled drumming overhead, becomes separate streams, and may be difficult to distinguish from the machinery. Water levels change slowly, so this is not primarily a flash-flood scenario, but footing and visibility can still deteriorate. Any abrupt departure from the site’s patient rhythm—violent vibration, a major new leak, repeated uncharacteristic releases—is a reason to increase your distance, not investigate.

## Conduct and biological containment

The safest ethical assumption is that the Rootworks is one continuous, irreplaceable living system, regardless of whether it is technically one organism or thousands. It is not obviously spreading, conquering the plain, or asking to be repaired. Its apparent product is its own continuation.

Accordingly:

- Take no cutting, root nodule, snail, eel, gum sample, or “already dead” fragment as a souvenir.
- Do not release dye into gutters, tap the cistern, block a pipe to measure pressure, or introduce animals to make the ecology seem more normal.
- Pack out all food waste and other refuse. Do not wash with soap in the pools or use the hollow as a toilet.
- Arrive with gear already cleaned of seeds and mud from other worlds.
- On departure, contain muddy clothing and equipment rather than rinsing them into the surrounding marsh. Inspect cuffs, pack seams, boot treads, and drainage openings for fragments.
- Clean and dry everything later under whatever contained decontamination procedure your crossing protocols require.

The fact that the Rootworks maintains a tiny boundary at home does **not** prove that a water-carried spore or root fragment would remain harmless in another world’s wetland. Its own restraint is not a substitute for yours.

## What the visit will feel like

Expect brightness, not enchanted gloom. At noon there will be wet green wood, waxed leaves, iron, and visible beads of water under an ordinary sky. Expect sap, wet rope, and ferruginous water rather than a heavy stench of rot. The center will be loud without roaring: a pump stroke roughly every half minute, belt creaks, ticking valves, droplets at different pitches, and buried water hammer that can sound convincingly like footsteps below you.

The near-absence of animals may be more unsettling than the noise. Prepare for that psychologically, especially if traveling with only one companion. Do not let a hollow wooden note twenty paces away, or the impression of someone walking beneath the floor, provoke a hurried retreat through bad footing.

If you expect spectacle, the Rootworks may initially disappoint you. Its revelations are a spoke that has changed position while you were looking elsewhere, iron polished by decades of slow contact, and water passing leaf by leaf through a system with no human operator. A good visit is therefore closer to inspecting a living waterworks than exploring a ruin.

Move as though the floor is alive, the machinery is pressurized, the water recirculates rather than leads anywhere, and every organism on your boots matters. Leave with photographs, notes, and dry clothes waiting outside—not with a piece of the Rootworks.
