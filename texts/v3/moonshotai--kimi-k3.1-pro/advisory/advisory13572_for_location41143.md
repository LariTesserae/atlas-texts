# Preparing to Visit the New Cut

Approach the New Cut less as a cave than as **a running industrial installation, a dust-filled confined space, and a closed living system occupying the same ground**. An ordinary human’s first visit should be short, instrumented, and mostly observational. If remote optics can obtain what you need from the threshold, use them. A full circuit of the chamber is not automatically worth the exposure.

The most important principle is simple:

**Permanently new does not mean safe for visitors.** The chamber may conserve its own walls, pellets, dust, and animal population. Nothing says that it will conserve your hearing, blood, body water, equipment, or route home. Do not test its equilibrium with an injury, a discarded object, or your life.

## What being inside will probably feel like

The first sensation may not be sound in the ordinary sense, but pressure: vibration arriving through your boots, teeth, ribs, and the contents of your chest. Speech will be nearly useless. Even with good hearing protection, your body will remain inside the machine’s acoustic field. Protection can reduce injury to your ears; it cannot make the chamber quiet.

The warmth may initially seem mild, even pleasant. It is not a place to cool down. The air is approximately body-warm, still, and evenly heated, so there is no promise of a cooler refuge beside the far wall. Once you add respiratory protection, sealed clothing, and exertion, heat can accumulate quickly.

Visually, expect poor depth perception. Amber lamps and the engine’s red slot leave much of the upper chamber unresolved. Dust glitters, mica vanishes edge-on, shadows appear fixed, and the ceiling occasionally seems to breathe as the kites flex together. The dark overhead is not empty space: it contains rock clefts, bell-throats, wings, and projecting wedges.

The crowding may be more psychologically difficult than the dimensions suggest. The floor moves with scalebacks, the walls are occupied, and the air is layered with animals. Nothing looks frantic, but your human-sized body will not easily fit into the gaps. A chamber that could be circled in minutes when empty may offer no ethical or safe route across it at all.

Finally, the unchanging chord, unflickering lamps, and perpetual freshness may distort your sense of duration. **The chamber is not progressing, but your exposure is.** Your air supply, heat load, fatigue, and hearing risk continue whether or not anything around you appears to change.

## Before entering

### Establish access and rescue first

Sixty metres of rock overhead turns a minor injury into a serious evacuation problem. Do not assume that reaching the black clinker plain means you have found a usable entrance, and do not blast or cut your own way into a chamber whose walls and machinery already interact in difficult-to-understand ways.

Keep your return route independent of the New Cut’s fixtures. Do not anchor to the rails, insulator bracket, lamp cages, engine, or service recess. Do not make your return depend on a handwheel, a blank stud, or an assumption that the stencil **II** explains anything.

Use:

- An attendant or support party outside the chamber.
- A tested means of egress and multiversal return.
- Scheduled visual or hardwired check-ins.
- An independent elapsed-time device, not estimates based on machinery or animal behaviour.
- A predetermined lost-communications procedure, normally immediate withdrawal.

Do not go alone. At the same time, keep the entering party as small as safety permits; every additional body adds heat, moisture, obstruction, and ecological disturbance.

### Assess the environment before breathing or touching it

The animals do not prove that the air supports human life. This is a mineral-and-fibre ecology with no green vegetation and no evident human-style respiration. A smell of hot porcelain or graphite is not an atmospheric analysis.

Assess, as remotely as practical:

- Oxygen and carbon dioxide.
- Toxic products associated with electrical discharge.
- Suspended mineral grains, fibres, and conductive dust.
- Sound pressure and low-frequency vibration.
- Heat stress under the protective equipment you intend to wear.
- Electrical fields around the engine, studs, insulators, and wiregrass.

Until the atmosphere is understood, supplied breathing air with an adequate exit reserve is the prudent choice. Full-face protection is preferable to a mouth-only respirator because the dust and fibres also threaten the eyes. If the equipment needed for safe breathing makes heat stress unmanageable, that is a reason not to enter, not a reason to remove protection.

Measure the noise before deciding that ear protection is sufficient. If the sound or vibration exceeds what your equipment and exposure plan can handle, observe remotely.

## Personal equipment

### Hearing and communication

Use properly fitted passive earplugs together with well-sealed earmuffs or an equivalent integrated protective system. Consumer noise-cancelling headphones alone are not adequate.

Do not plan to shout instructions. Carry a communication method that does not depend on hearing speech:

- Practised hand and light signals.
- A protected hardwired intercom, if it can be routed without snagging.
- Visual check-in signals and timers.

Never route cables or safety lines near belts, flywheels, pistons, or the pellet-handling routes.

Leave immediately for dizziness, nausea, balance problems, blurred or vibrating vision, chest discomfort, severe headache, or any abrupt change in hearing. Do not interpret growing tolerance of the noise as adaptation; it may be injury or simple sensory overload.

### Eyes, lungs, skin, and head

Wear a low-profile helmet with a secure chinstrap. The ceiling is low, the fractures are fresh, and the apparent permanence of projecting stone is not something on which to stake your skull.

Clothing should be clean, close-fitting, abrasion-resistant, and free of loose straps, cords, scarves, or trailing equipment. Protect your hands and feet, but remember that gloves and boots do not make it acceptable to handle animals or walk across a living floor.

Avoid loose hair and exposed skin. Apart from the industrial hazards, you are carrying moisture, oils, microbes, shed fibres, and salts into a place whose dryness is original and whose ecology is closed.

Bring at least two reliable, dimmable light sources. Begin with diffuse, moderate illumination rather than a glaring beam. Avoid flash photography, strobes, and unnecessary lasers. Do not assume your lights will resolve the strangely fixed shadows or every gap in the floor.

### Heat and supplies

Arrive rested and hydrated, with drinking water, salts, cooling, and first-aid supplies waiting outside. Keep exertion low and the visit short. Do not bring a fan: moving air would disturb dust and kite stations, and there is no cool corner to which you can retreat.

Do not eat, drink, spit, or dispose of waste inside. A spilled water bottle is not merely untidy here; it introduces a substance the chamber has never required and may alter dry chemistry or roots. Nothing in the New Cut should be treated as human food or water.

Retain every item securely and inventory your equipment before and after the visit. A dropped screw, lens cap, battery, or notebook page is not an acceptable offering to the equilibrium.

## How to move and behave

### Stay at the threshold unless there is a genuinely clear route

Do not walk across scalebacks simply because their armour resembles stone. Do not block their movement between the press outlet and intake. If there is no verified bare route, do not make one by sweeping animals aside.

Keep your arms down and your profile compact. A raised hand, camera pole, or helmet can intersect overlapping kite wings or bell-throats clinging above. Avoid kneeling or sitting; besides bringing you closer to the crowded floor, the service stool is not necessarily furniture in any meaningful operational sense.

Do not lean on walls. Fresh fractures can be sharp, wall animals are fragile, and belts visibly pass through boundaries that look like solid rock. A wall that is mechanically permeable to the engine should not automatically be treated as a dependable barrier or anchor.

### Give the engine an industrial safety distance

Treat every wheel, piston, belt, hammer, and perforated casing as unguarded moving machinery. Dry graphite bearings do not make it clean or harmless.

Do not:

- Peer into the red slot.
- Touch the intake, outlet, or pellet piles.
- Put tools near the places where belts enter the walls.
- Attempt to stop, brake, lubricate, clean, or repair anything.
- Use the engine as a power source, clock, or navigation reference.

The absence of fuel input and useful output does not mean there is little energy present. It means you do not understand the energy budget.

The other built objects deserve the same restraint. **Discontinued is not the same as safe.** A handwheel that moves nothing in sight may move something elsewhere. Blank studs are blank only to you. An insulator without a visible wire is not proof of de-energization. Rails ending in rock are not an invitation to test the boundary.

Do not sit on the stool, turn the wheel, press studs, add wiring, clean the stencil, or try to “complete” an arrangement merely because it looks unfinished.

### Treat sound and vibration as habitat

The noise is not merely an inconvenience imposed on the ecology. Wiregrass uses vibration; kites station themselves within it; bell-throats occupy its gaps; scalebacks respond to the press.

Therefore, do not clap to test the acoustics, tap walls, strike the casing, play tones to the bell-throats, or attempt to introduce counter-sound. Do not bring a drone whose rotors add wind, vibration, and a moving hazard to already occupied air.

If your presence causes kites to abandon their stations, scalebacks to reroute persistently, or bell-throats to change their breathing, regard that as evidence that you have become a disturbance. Withdraw rather than seeing how much accommodation the system will make.

### Handle nothing living

Do not pick up, swat, feed, or deliberately injure any animal. An injured scaleback coming unwoven without blood is not necessarily sterile or harmless; it may simply put more unknown fibre and dust into your breathing space.

The wiregrass deserves particular care. There are only eleven known tufts, so damaging one is not a trivial sampling decision. Do not touch it to test its warmth or conductivity, break its brittle spines, harvest red filaments, water its roots, or stand in the charged dust released by grazing.

Take no souvenirs. A pellet is not a manufactured product waiting to be collected; it is material circulating through the chamber. The same caution applies to dust, fibres, dead remains, stone flakes, and paint.

## When to abort

Leave at the planned time, not when you feel you have seen enough to justify the journey. Leave earlier for any problem with air, communications, lighting, timing, heat, hearing, balance, or your exit route.

Also pay attention to the chamber’s baseline. Its ordinary condition is constancy. If your arrival is followed by a lamp flicker, a change in the engine’s chord, dust beginning to settle, a new heat gradient, animals leaving their usual stations, or any other departure from the steady state, **do not stay to investigate**. In a place defined by equilibrium, unexpected change is an abort signal.

Do not run. A panicked human is more likely to crush animals, strike the ceiling, or become entangled than to escape efficiently.

## Afterward

Decontaminate outside the chamber in a contained area. Do not beat dust from clothing or use compressed air, and do not wash local material back into the New Cut. Treat clinging grains and fibres as potentially sharp, conductive, toxic, chemically reactive, and ecologically contaminating until shown otherwise. This is also a matter of responsible multiversal travel: do not carry its dust into another world, and do not leave another world’s biology behind in it.

Check your hearing and respiratory condition after the visit. Persistent ringing, muffled hearing, coughing, eye pain, headache, or heat-illness symptoms deserve attention rather than dismissal as part of the experience.

## What a successful visit looks like

A successful visit is not one in which you discover what the engine manufactures. It manufactures no product for you. It is not one in which you turn the handwheel, explain **II**, take a pellet, or wait for the chamber to complete itself.

With luck, you will remember the floor pressing sound into your bones, the red grass appearing briefly in your own light, the scalebacks flowing around their necessary routes, and the layered ceiling seeming to inhale. Then you will leave with your supplies, your companions, and the chamber’s loops intact.

The New Cut does not need to be brought to an ending. **Your visit does.**
