# Preparing for the Level Crown

Prepare as though you are visiting three environments at once: an unbreathable high-altitude desert, a navigational void, and the exposed surface of a continental machine whose maintenance can rearrange the ground beneath you.

The greatest danger is that none of this will look as alarming as it is. The sunlight is ordinary. Shadows behave. The stone appears solid. There may be days of profound calm. A visitor who waits for the Crown to feel dangerous before taking precautions has already misunderstood it.

For an ordinary human, an unsupported solo crossing is not a reasonable first visit. Go with people experienced in Crown survey work, with pressurized transport, independent personal life support, and someone off-route monitoring your progress. If that cannot be arranged, choose remote or overhead observation rather than improvising a landing and a walk.

## Carry a complete human environment

Do not plan your equipment around the word *altitude*. Plan around measured pressure, atmospheric composition, temperature, and the absence of rescue.

Unless local measurements clearly justify something less protective, assume you need a full pressure suit rather than merely warm clothing and an oxygen mask. It should provide:

- Primary and independent emergency breathing gas.
- Carbon-dioxide removal for both systems.
- Active heating and cooling, not simply heavy insulation.
- A protected visor suitable for the local star’s spectrum.
- Reliable pressure, oxygen, and battery readings visible to the wearer.
- Gloves and boots that remain dexterous and warm after prolonged contact with cold conditions.
- A repair kit you can actually use while wearing the gloves.
- Enough water, power, and consumables for substantial delay, not just the scheduled excursion.

Keep emergency life support on your body. A pressurized rover is excellent until a fold, powder flow, or navigation error separates you from it.

Thermal preparation is less intuitive than “bring a warm coat.” Noon sunlight may heat dark outer fabric while exposed stone remains cool and shade carries a penetrating chill. You can be overheating at the shoulders and becoming dangerously cold at the boots. Nights are colder still, and the absence of frost tells you nothing reassuring about either temperature or the availability of water.

Bring all your water. There is no useful survival technique involving digging, scraping frost, following animals, or searching hollows. There are no damp hollows, buried ice deposits, or pore-sealed brines to find. Likewise, bring food and power. Seamgrass is not provisions, and a station is not an electrical outlet.

For travel between stations, use suitable long-range pressurized transport rather than assuming that flat ground makes walking practical. Stations may be hundreds of kilometres apart. The vehicle should be sealed against fine dry powder and serviceable without local water, but no vehicle should be described as “storm-proof” unless Crown surveyors have demonstrated exactly what that means.

Your sleeping shelter must not depend on the plate remaining a passive foundation. Avoid designs that require deep anchors, trenches, or rigid attachment to the surface. Even then, regard shelter as protection from exposure, not guaranteed protection from an active front.

## Navigate by coordinates, and distrust almost everything else

Before arrival, obtain the most current survey information available:

- The local coordinate datum and its units.
- Verified station coordinates.
- Recent front activity, if such records exist.
- Current procedures for approaching or using station outer rings.
- The location of the Crown’s convex shoulders and major descent systems.
- Any limitations on communications, rescue, or departure during storm activity.

Carry independent navigation methods. Use the local absolute positioning system if one exists, backed by inertial navigation and whatever radio or celestial references local surveyors trust. Do not assume that a compass, satellite receiver, map projection, or transit device from another world will work merely because it worked at your previous destination.

A barometric altimeter is of limited conceptual use here. There is no sea from which to derive an intuitive elevation, and the old survey zero is an archival convention. Your body cares about measured pressure and oxygen, not an impressive altitude number.

Record camps, vehicles, samples, and route changes by coordinate and time. Do not navigate by “the shallow dome,” “the three sockets,” “the foundation outline,” or “the straight groove.” Any of these may be transient. Even a genuinely ancient ruin may be unusable because it is too small, too similar to another mark, or invisible when you need it.

A good Crown map will look almost empty. Do not mistake that emptiness for missing survey effort.

Physical breadcrumbs are also unreliable. Tracks can vanish, markers can be buried or displaced, and material can travel great distances while the province restores its original mean level. If you cache equipment, assume that recovering it requires more than returning to a visually familiar place.

### Stay away from the edges on a first visit

The outer shoulders are particularly deceptive. You can walk toward the lower country for days with little apparent descent, only to encounter slopes and escarpments on a scale that makes ordinary mountains look like seams below you.

Never treat “walk downhill toward thicker air” as an emergency plan. Never approach an edge casually to improve the view. A descent toward the basins is a separate expedition, not an afternoon excursion or a suit-failure contingency.

## Learn the difference between atmospheric weather and solid weather

A Crown front is not a dust storm with unusually strong geological side effects. Most of it is organized movement in the ground itself.

It may first appear as a bevel or second edge on the plain. There may be no wind. Dust can rise in sheets that look almost motionless. The front can advance across enormous distances without becoming louder. When it arrives, ridges and walls may grow as continuous solid forms rather than breaking noisily out of the ground. The vibration transmitted through your body may be astonishingly slight.

Therefore:

- Do not wait for wind, thunder, pressure changes, or a roar.
- Do not use the absence of ground vibration as an all-clear; by the time you feel the event underfoot, it may already be upon you.
- Do not assume that days of local calm indicate a safe season.
- Do not treat reports of storms elsewhere on the Crown as either an immediate local warning or a reason to relax.
- Do not assume that an empty centre within a vast storm system is a refuge.

Use current survey observations if available, maintain a visual horizon watch, and learn the appearance of seamgrass before and during contraction. Optical instruments and time-lapse observation may help reveal movement that the eye interprets as a stationary flaw in the horizon. Instruments supplement local knowledge; they do not replace it.

The essential rules should be rehearsed until they require no debate:

1. **Coordinates before landmarks.**
2. **Old level ground is not thereby stable ground.**
3. **When seamgrass folds, the interval has ended.**
4. **A station’s outer ring may remain passable; that does not make it safe.**

Seamgrass is useful but incomplete warning. It is sparse, and its contracted grey nodules can be indistinguishable from gravel. No vegetation in sight means no biological warning in sight—not an absence of danger.

### When warning comes

Stop sampling, photographing, repairing nonessential equipment, or “just checking” the station. Execute the response plan you prepared before leaving shelter.

If your plan uses a surveyed station outer ring, follow that plan only while the designated route and timing remain viable. Do not improvise a race against a front whose distance and speed you have estimated by eye. Do not assume the station interior is safer than its exterior.

Avoid active troughs and flowing mineral powder. Do not shelter in a hollow because it appears sheltered, climb a new ridge for visibility, or put your back against a wall or tower because it looks solid. Solid is not the same as persistent. A formation can be coherent enough to cast an ordinary shadow and still cease to exist within the hour.

Keep your suit sealed, your personal life support available, and your communications and position beacon active. Follow the guide’s storm drill rather than generic earthquake instincts: there may be no falling debris, no loud shock, and no obvious moment at which the event has finished.

There is no universally reliable advice for surviving a major front while caught unprepared on open ground. That is precisely why route planning, turnaround margins, and local support matter more than courage or a heavier vehicle.

Afterward, do not resume travel merely because the plain looks restored. Obtain a new position fix, inspect personnel and equipment, check for another front, and wait for the appropriate local all-clear if one is available. Reopening seamgrass is encouraging evidence of a stabilized interval, not a guarantee about the next one.

The Crown restores its flatness. It does not promise to restore your road, tracks, cache, vehicle, or companions.

## Treat stations as addresses in unknown machinery

A station may look like an abandoned house, but it is not an abandoned house.

Its lack of seams, fasteners, humming machinery, or visible controls does not establish that it is inert. Its slight warmth does not make it a heated shelter. An open doorway is not an invitation, and an empty room is not evidence that nothing is happening.

Unless entry is part of a knowledgeable survey plan:

- Observe from outside.
- Do not drill, hammer, cut, ground, or attach equipment to it.
- Do not use it as an anchor for shelters, antennae, or vehicles.
- Do not connect your power system to charged films or attempt to draw energy from the plate.
- Do not remove an object left by a previous visitor.
- Do not leave an offering or instrument merely to see whether the station responds.

The same restraint applies to the surrounding ground. The station is not the whole machine; the mineral province itself is the machine. A harmless-looking test may be an intervention in a process no visitor adequately understands.

## Be careful around life, without expecting it to help you

Learn to recognize seamgrass without trampling it. Its isolated black-green felts are easy to overlook and easy to destroy. Avoid camping or parking on them, and do not collect them as food or souvenirs. Their electrical physiology is not evidence that you can safely use them to power or ground your equipment.

The plate-skate is not described as aggressive, but unfamiliarity is not domestication. Do not touch or turn over a dormant specimen. Its mineral-grey back and covering of dust can make it look like part of the surface. Several faint, evenly spaced dotted tracks that simply end may indicate that the animal has settled at the endpoint, not that it vanished.

Check before kneeling, placing equipment, or making camp. Avoid disturbing the charged films near stations, both for your own safety and because skates use them.

Keep waste, spare water, and biological contamination contained. The Crown’s dryness does not make it an appropriate place to discard moisture, organic material, or microorganisms from another world.

## Prepare for the psychological effects

Outside the suit, sound will scarcely help you. Inside it, you may hear little except breath, fabric, valves, and your own movements. Companions a short distance away may as well be beyond shouting range. Use radios, in-helmet alarms, agreed light signals, and scheduled check-ins. Do not rely on external speakers or on hearing a vehicle in trouble.

Practise long suit operations before arrival, including emergency changeovers and navigation tasks. Acclimatization to ordinary mountains is not a substitute for this training, and it does not make unbreathable air breathable.

The visual field requires preparation too. Exceptional visibility and almost no scale references can distort judgement. A fist-sized stone can acquire the emotional importance of a settlement; a black felt of vegetation can become an irrational destination. You may begin to distrust your eyes when the province’s curvature affects the horizon.

Use scheduled position fixes rather than navigating only when you feel uncertain. Have companions cross-check decisions. Do not chase a visually compelling object because it seems close, meaningful, or familiar. Keep a simple time-and-coordinate log; it will help both practically and psychologically when the landscape appears to have forgotten an event you just witnessed.

The storm itself may produce a dangerous sense of unreality. Because there is no roar and the sunlight remains commonplace, part of you may insist that the rising country is an optical effect. Rehearse the correct response beforehand so that you do not waste the warning interval arguing with your senses.

## A sensible first visit

A good first visit is short and almost disappointingly uneventful:

- Arrive with verified pressure, thermal, navigation, and departure arrangements.
- Check every suit and backup system before leaving pressurized support.
- Make a brief accompanied traverse with a current front watch and a clear turnaround time.
- Observe a station exterior without treating it as a service building.
- Examine seamgrass from a respectful distance and learn its contracted state.
- Return with reserves largely intact.
- Leave before curiosity about seeing a front becomes the reason for staying.

Do not plan your departure around a landmark that must survive until you return. If your multiverse transit method requires a station, a coordinate, or your own conscious operation, build in margins for delay and equipment failure. An assumed instantaneous escape is not a substitute for life support.

On a successful visit, you may remember only grey stone, a small dark structure, a patch of black-green felt, and the sound of your own valve. Evening may make the plain look exactly as it did before some distant country was raised and erased. That apparent anticlimax is not wasted travel. It is what the Crown is like when its work has temporarily left nothing to see.

Go quietly, remain self-sufficient, and never confuse restored flatness with forgiveness. The Crown’s fidelity is to its own final shape, not to yours.
