# Preparing for the Black Catchment

**Do not begin by buying a brighter lamp. Begin by learning to be blind in a loud place.**

The Black Catchment combines the difficulties of a lightless cave, a high-altitude tropical marsh, a flood-control network, and an operating industrial site. It offers none of their usual safeguards. There are no dependable settlements, no stations that can be treated as hotels, no dawn by which to reset yourself, and no reason to assume rescue will come.

An ordinary human can visit, but the first visit should be short, guided, and close to a reliable way home. Do not make it a station-to-station crossing. The stations are days apart, separated by terrain that can turn a minor injury into a fatal delay, and reaching one gives you neither supplies nor safety.

## 1. Make your return independent of the Catchment

The most important piece of equipment is not footwear, navigation, or shelter. It is an extraction method that does not depend on local machinery, local schedules, or your ability to retrace several days of travel.

Before leaving the arrival point:

- Confirm that the return link, beacon, gate, or tender is functioning.
- Ensure every member of the party can operate the emergency return procedure **by touch**, while tired and wearing gloves.
- Establish automatic check-ins with someone off-world if your transit method permits them.
- Give the extraction system its own protected power supply and keep its reserve separate from expedition batteries.
- Place the arrival and return point on previously assessed, stable ground, well away from escarpments, sinks, outfalls, pylons, and basin floors.

If the return system cannot be verified, go back. Do not continue merely because the ground underfoot feels solid.

Travel with a small, capable party rather than alone. Ideally it should include someone with previous Catchment experience, a wilderness medic, and at least two people competent at route finding without sight. Everyone should be able to perform everyone else’s essential tasks.

## 2. Prepare for both thin air and wet heat

The Catchment’s warmth is deceptive. You may feel enclosed and heavy while still suffering the effects of altitude. Exertion will produce breathlessness sooner than expected, while the saturated air prevents sweat from cooling you efficiently. Warm rain does not necessarily relieve heat stress.

Before traveling:

- Obtain a medical assessment if you have any significant heart, lung, or altitude-related risk.
- Acclimatize to reduced oxygen and separately to humid heat, under appropriate guidance.
- Train for slow movement with a load, repeated probing of uncertain ground, and long periods without sitting comfortably. Speed is much less useful than endurance and balance.
- Learn the symptoms of altitude illness, heat exhaustion, and heat stroke. Headache, nausea, dizziness, and unusual weakness are not things to “walk off” here; they may also indicate contaminated air or simple exhaustion.
- Carry emergency oxygen only as a supplement to evacuation, if advised and properly trained. It is not permission to continue pushing onward. A filter mask does nothing for thin or oxygen-deficient air.

Constant wetness will become a medical problem before it becomes a comfort problem. Expect chafing, split skin, macerated feet, infected small wounds, and fungal growth. Foot inspection must be done by touch on a fixed schedule, not postponed until something hurts badly.

Sleep is another physiological hazard. Without dawn, and with pumps, rain, thunder, insects, and animals continuing around you, it is easy to become badly sleep-deprived without noticing. Keep a strict expedition clock and rest schedule. Do not rely on station load changes as morning, and do not use sedatives casually in thin air.

## 3. Train for total darkness before arrival

Night vision will have no ambient light to amplify. A lamp may work if you bring one, but nothing about the location guarantees that artificial illumination will make the country usefully visible. Even a functioning lamp may show only rain, fog, wet leaves, and a small patch of ground while concealing the larger shape of the terrain. Thermal equipment may be equally confusing in a landscape of warm stone, warm water, and warm outflows.

Your itinerary must remain possible if every visual aid fails.

In a controlled, safe environment, practice blindfolded until you can:

- Dress, eat, use the toilet, and pack your shelter.
- Find and replace batteries without mixing spent and fresh cells.
- Inspect a boot, clean a foot, and apply basic dressings.
- Operate communications, navigation, medical equipment, and the return mechanism.
- Recognize each companion’s voice and agreed tactile signals.
- Remain still when startled instead of immediately stepping backward or running.

That last skill matters. In the Catchment, panic is likely to be more immediately dangerous than the animal or machine that caused it.

Practice listening as well, but do not train your hearing by exposing yourself to damaging noise. Learn to attend to layers: close rain on equipment, intermediate water and vegetation, distant low-frequency movement, and regular mechanical beats. Acoustic fatigue is real; after hours of overwhelming sound, people stop noticing changes. Rotate the responsibility for listening and navigating.

If you require silence to sleep, a visible horizon to remain calm, or frequent visual reassurance to control panic, this is not a suitable first destination.

## 4. Pack for touch, corrosion, and delay

Everything should be operable without looking at a screen. Alarms should vibrate as well as sound. Controls should be distinguishable by shape. Critical information should exist in tactile or audio form, not solely in a visual display.

### Clothing and shelter

You will not remain perfectly dry. Dress to function while wet rather than to seal yourself inside a sweat-filled waterproof suit.

Bring:

- Quick-draining, full-coverage clothing that protects against insects, roots, and abrasive surfaces without causing severe overheating.
- Boots with excellent wet traction, puncture protection, and drainage. Merely waterproof boots can fill with water and remain full.
- Several changes of socks and a carefully packed foot-care kit.
- Wet-grip gloves, face and eye protection where insects are dense, and insect mesh for rest.
- Light insulation. The country is warm, but wet exhaustion and ridge wind can still leave you chilled.
- A corrosion-resistant, insect-proof shelter that does not depend on tying lines to roots, pipes, cables, or other convenient-looking structures.

Do not expect anything to dry completely between uses.

### Navigation and communication

Do not assume satellite navigation, radio infrastructure, or celestial fixes exist. Use locally validated methods and redundancy: tactile route logs, elapsed-time records, inertial tracking if available, and physical confirmation of terrain.

A cable hum, pump beat, or tuned survey wire can be a useful route cue, but **no single sound should be allowed to command the route**. If your navigation fails when one pump stops, it was not navigation.

Use radios with vibrating alerts and tactile controls, backed up by simple companion signals. Avoid unnecessary horns, call playback, or active sonar. The Catchment’s animals have divided the soundscape among themselves; introducing loud artificial signals may erase the very cues on which you depend and disturb hunting or communication.

### Air, water, and power

Carry a calibrated atmosphere monitor suitable for oxygen deficiency and likely industrial or geothermal gases, with alarms that do not rely on hearing. Sulfur odor is not a dependable measuring instrument, particularly after your nose has adapted. Test the air before entering gorges, enclosed hollows, spring areas, or the vicinity of stations. An escape respirator may help against a specifically anticipated contaminant, but it does not make an oxygen-poor space safe.

Carry all food from home. The local food web incorporates sulfur, dissolved metals, oils, brine, and discarded power sources; neither edibility nor biochemical compatibility should be assumed.

Bring enough food, purification capacity, and power for a substantial delay, not merely the planned journey. Keep spent batteries, medical waste, food waste, and damaged equipment contained and pack them out. A discarded power cell is not harmless rubbish here; it may become part of the ecology.

## 5. On arrival, do less than you think you should

Remain near the arrival point for an initial rest period. Check breathing, hydration, equipment seals, atmospheric readings, and communications. Do not immediately march toward the nearest vibration or large-water sound.

Then listen before exploring.

An experienced guide should help establish a local baseline:

- What rain sounds like on stone, fern, pipe, pool, and open marsh.
- Which mechanical rhythms are normal at this location.
- Where the herd calls, frog bands, insect rhythms, and high sonar voices usually sit.
- Which sounds indicate water close underfoot and which carry from far away.
- What the party should do if any of these patterns changes.

Make short passive recordings if useful, with timestamps from your own clock. Do not answer animal calls or play recordings back into the marsh.

A sensible first excursion is a brief walk over known ground followed by a return to the arrival site. It is not an attempt to reach a station, identify a product in a main, or find the plateau’s edge.

## 6. Move as though the ground were a hypothesis

The surface may be stone, interlocked stems, root mat, mud, peat, or a crust over empty space. Rain can make all of them sound more substantial than they are.

Develop a deliberate sequence: **probe, load gradually, confirm, then move**. Never jump across an uncertain interval. Do not use flooded roots as handholds, and do not reach blindly into root tangles, pipes, or spring cavities. Avoid descending ravines as shortcuts; a sequence of shelves and plunge pools can become difficult to reclimb and dangerous when water changes.

Plan travel in time and effort rather than distance. A route that looks short on a survey may be impossible after several watersheds of weather have passed through it.

Use sound, but corroborate it:

- The familiar rattle of a gravel crossing is evidence, not a permanent guarantee. Storms can alter depth, current, and the bed itself.
- A cable route through reeds may be useful to a guide, but do not approach or touch electrical infrastructure, and do not let its hum override what the ground tells you.
- If an embankment gradually becomes vibrating metal, a hollow support, or a narrowing beam, stop and backtrack. Do not continue to discover whether it becomes “nothing” in the same way the old routes do.
- Never enter a dry pipe to shorten a journey. Dry describes its present condition, not its next operating cycle.
- Keep well back from sinks, fissures, gorge lips, and escarpments. The sound of enormous water is not a reliable measure of distance.

If something startles you, stand still on ground already bearing your weight. Do not run toward silence, loud water, or an engine in the hope that it represents safety.

## 7. Treat machines and animals as parts of the same warning system

The network is not a collection of ruins. It is a distributed, functioning system whose local condition may affect a basin far away.

A human-height handle means only that a humanlike hand can operate it. It does not mean that you should. Fresh grease and recently replaced metal are not invitations to explore; they are evidence of active maintenance.

Therefore:

- Do not open valves, doors, housings, mains, or inspection plates.
- Do not shelter inside a station merely because it has walls and a roof.
- Do not block an outflow, clear a channel, move a number plate, or take components as souvenirs.
- Do not assume a station’s noise protects you. Soft-footed hunters use its sound shadows.
- Keep away from discharged mineral liquids, warm brine, open channels, and anything whose temperature or composition you have not measured.

If hair lifts, you develop a metallic taste, or nearby insects become agitated around electrical infrastructure, withdraw along the known safe route. Do not approach to diagnose the cause.

Most importantly, learn the difference between ordinary acoustic complexity and a change in pattern:

- **A hole developing in the soundscape:** stop. Silence may be a hunting condition, an acoustic shadow, or a warning. Do not walk into it simply because it offers relief from the noise.
- **Mossbacks leaving a basin, fliers gathering abnormally around a warm pipe, frogs losing their usual order, or animals hesitating and changing pitch:** treat these as possible early warnings. Leave low ground if you have a preplanned safe route.
- **An engine stopping:** do not investigate. The absence of pump noise does not mean the machinery has become safe. Flooding may be delayed, and the first useful warning may be animal movement rather than rising water.
- **A new vibration in the ground or pressure in the teeth:** you may be approaching a station before any structure is within reach. Slow down and reassess rather than following the sensation.

The correct response to many of these signs is not dramatic flight. It is an early, orderly withdrawal before water or panic removes your options.

## 8. Be conservative with water, weather, and camp

There is no single Catchment forecast. Conditions must be assessed by watershed. Thunder wandering between basins does not tell you that a storm is departing, and storms may circle the high country for days.

If rain seems to stop but boulders, cables, and hillsides continue to weep, you are experiencing condensation, not necessarily improving weather. Nothing becoming dry is not a reliable safety indicator.

Treat all local water as both an industrial and biological question. Clear water is meaningless even where you can see it, and warmth is not purification.

- Avoid separator brine, machine outflows, spring felt, water held in vegetation, and stagnant warm pools.
- Filters and ordinary disinfection may address particles and microbes without removing dissolved metals, salts, or industrial contaminants.
- Directly caught rain may be useful in some locations, but only under local guidance and away from questionable atmospheric discharges.
- If your equipment cannot make a source safe, that source is not drinking water.

Camp only on previously assessed, stable ground above basin flooding, with more than one possible exit. Avoid gorge floors, sink margins, outflows, game paths, warm pipes that attract animals, and exposed ridge crests. Do not assume “higher” automatically means safer if it also means more exposed to storms.

Maintain your own waking and resting periods. Use hearing protection only when off watch or otherwise safely relieved of the need to monitor the environment. Never walk through hazardous terrain with both ears deliberately blocked.

## 9. Decide your abort conditions before you arrive

Turn back or extract immediately for such things as:

- Failure or uncertainty in the return system.
- Loss of the experienced guide or inability of the party to navigate without sight.
- An atmospheric alarm that cannot be resolved by immediate withdrawal.
- Confusion, fainting, inability to walk straight, severe breathlessness, or other serious altitude or heat symptoms.
- Unplanned water rise or a pattern of animal movement suggesting impending failure.
- Loss of safe water, critical medical supplies, or essential communications.
- Panic, exhaustion, or sleep deprivation severe enough to make slow movement unsafe.
- A significant change in machinery along your intended route.

If lost, do not automatically walk toward the loudest engine or largest water. An engine is not rescue, and large water may mean a gorge, sink, or fall. Stay on confirmed stable ground if conditions permit and follow the agreed separation or extraction protocol.

A successful visit may involve turning back very early.

## What visiting will actually be like

At first, you may feel buried despite standing in open country. There will be no sky to give you distance and no visual horizon to steady your balance. Warm rain will touch your face; water will find its way into your clothing; iron, sulfur, crushed vegetation, and wet hide will seem unusually strong because you have so little else to interpret. The ground will transmit movement through your boots. During exertion, your lungs will remind you that warmth is not the same as dense air.

The sound may initially seem intolerable. Then, if you are patient and not too tired, it will begin to separate. A fern will acquire a different outline from a pipe. A pool will sound close-mouthed beside the broader hiss of marsh. Pump strokes will become intervals rather than noise. You may notice animals using those intervals. You may realize that a change in rhythm tells you more than another hour of staring would have done.

This does not mean you have become safe. It means you have begun to understand how much you were missing.

The Catchment is not unchanging because it has no dawn. It changes constantly: rain shifts between watersheds, roots and silt move, herds open paths, pumps alter temperatures, and animals redistribute themselves through the sound. What it lacks is not time, but your preferred way of marking time.

Go quietly. Interfere with nothing. Leave before curiosity persuades you to open, cross, taste, or follow something merely because it is there. If you return with damp equipment, sore feet, careful recordings, and the memory of the whole country changing its note beneath the rain, you will have seen about as much of the Black Catchment as an ordinary human visitor should.
