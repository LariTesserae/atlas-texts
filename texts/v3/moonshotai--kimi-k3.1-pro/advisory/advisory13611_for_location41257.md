# Preparing for the Weeping Crown

Prepare for two different journeys without confusing them.

Inside the anchors’ influence, you are visiting a warm, saturated, remote forest province: difficult underfoot, loud, visually enclosed, and surrounded by immense drops. Beyond that influence, you are at eleven kilometres in conditions for which an ordinary human body is not equipped. The practical art of visiting the Crown is to enjoy the first place without ever blundering into the second.

**Safety there is not simply a matter of elevation. It is a matter of where you are in relation to machinery you cannot see.**

## Make the first visit deliberately unambitious

Do not begin with a traverse, a solo expedition, or an attempt to find an unmapped anchor. Choose a short, currently verified route with a known camp or refuge and an out-and-back return. Travel with someone who can read the old markers as they exist now, not merely someone who owns an old map. Weathered cairns, faint carvings, and paths made by animals are easy for an outsider to misinterpret.

Arrange both arrival and departure carefully:

- **Enter well inside the moderated region**, not at its suspected edge. If you are using your own multiversal transport rather than an established approach, have the actual destination checked before exposing yourself. Dense air alone does not prove that its composition is suitable for you.
- **Make the emergency extraction point safe as well.** A device that can remove you to an arbitrary exterior coordinate is not necessarily offering rescue; it may merely be offering a faster arrival at lethal altitude.
- **Do not plan to climb or descend improvise­dly from the dry basins.** Acclimatization, fitness, or a small oxygen supply does not make an arbitrary journey outside the anchors’ influence a reasonable undertaking.
- **Assume no rescue, road access, supplies, or medical care.** The shelters and field building do not constitute a settlement. Carry reserves for a missed departure window, an injury, or several days of slow going.
- Use communications and navigation equipment only if it has been demonstrated to work locally. Neither satellites nor a familiar GPS coordinate system should be assumed. An altimeter cannot tell you where the anchors are or which direction leads toward safety.

If no current route knowledge is available, join a properly equipped survey party or postpone the visit.

## Prepare your body for wet heat, not for a mountaineering record

The ordinary work of the Crown will be walking slowly on slick, uneven ground, stepping over roots, using your hands, and carrying a pack while warm rain or vegetation keeps you wet. Train for that. Practice hiking and modest scrambling with your actual load, and learn the difference between discomfort and the beginning of heat illness.

The close humidity may make exertion more taxing than the gentle weather suggests. Warm rain is not necessarily cooling rain. Plan short days, rest before you are exhausted, and do not try to maintain the pace you would use on a dry trail.

Before going:

- Obtain any medical advice appropriate to your heart, lungs, heat tolerance, mobility, and personal medications.
- Learn basic wilderness first aid, especially blister care, wound care, sprains, heat illness, hypothermia, and recognition of hypoxia.
- Practice foot care. Persistent wetness can turn maceration, chafing, or a small blister into the factor that ends the trip.
- Break in your footwear and practice keeping your sleep equipment dry while everything around it is wet.
- Take the first day easily. Even where the air is known to be comfortable, you will need time to learn the heat, footing, noise, and scale.

Everyone in the party should know the warning signs of approaching insufficient air: disproportionate or persistent breathlessness, headache, nausea, dizziness, unusual fatigue, confusion, or clumsiness. Fitness does not protect against these conditions.

Most importantly, **do not respond by automatically going downhill**. Descending may take you farther from the effective anchor network. In an emergency, the correct direction may be back along the marked route toward the last confirmed safe area, not toward lower geometric elevation.

## Pack for permanent wetness and one reliable dry refuge

You will probably not remain dry. Waterproof clothing eventually wets through, water runs off foliage, and sweat accumulates beneath a rain shell. Design your equipment around **functioning while wet**, while preserving one sealed, uncompromised dry kit for sleep and emergencies.

### Clothing and footwear

- Quick-drying, non-cotton clothing, including long trousers and sleeves where vegetation or insects make them useful.
- A light, serviceable rain shell rather than an elaborate expedition suit.
- Well-broken-in footwear with excellent grip on wet rock and roots. No sole makes mossy basalt or sinter trustworthy.
- Several changes of socks and a small kit for managing chafing and skin damage.
- Sun protection for open saddles and plateaus; the familiar-looking light does not justify guessing about exposure.
- A separate insulating layer, warm hat, gloves, and emergency bivouac equipment in case you are delayed or accidentally approach a colder margin. These are emergency equipment, not permission to explore the margins.

Do not pack solely for a tropical forest, but do not burden yourself with an entire high-altitude snow-climbing kit either.

### Shelter and sleep

Bring your own shelter even if dry ceramic shelters lie along the route. They are sparse, may be unsuitable or occupied, and should not form a single point of failure.

Use a shelter appropriate to saturated ground and warm nights, with ventilation and insect mesh if needed. Carry a sleeping pad and insulation chosen from reliable local night temperatures, not from the number “eleven kilometres.”

Protect the following with redundant waterproofing:

- One set of clothes reserved exclusively for sleep.
- Dry socks.
- Sleeping insulation.
- Personal medication and essential documents.
- A headlamp and its backup.

Assume that nothing left out overnight will dry by morning.

### Other essentials

- Current route information, waterproof written notes, and a marker key; a compass if it has been checked locally.
- A headlamp, backup light, spare power, and waterproof means of taking notes.
- Communications that work in the region, plus a check-in and overdue-party plan.
- First aid, blister and chafe supplies, personal medicines, and any emergency equipment your guide requires.
- Water-treatment equipment, food, cooking fuel if needed, and a reserve beyond the planned itinerary. Do not count on making a fire from the wet, ecologically important wood around you.
- Earplugs or other hearing protection **for rest and sleep**, if your camp safety procedures allow them. The accumulated sound can be surprisingly fatiguing. Do not walk near cliffs or through difficult forest with your awareness unnecessarily muffled.

If supplemental oxygen is carried, it should be part of a professionally designed emergency plan that everyone understands. A small oxygen bottle is not a passport beyond the margin.

## Travel according to the Crown’s own logic

### Follow markers, not water

This is the most important local rule.

Water looks like a natural guide and is often the opposite. Runnels join basalt flutes, streams vanish over escarpments, and descending drainage may lead both toward a cliff and away from safe air. If lost, do not simply “follow the stream out.”

Likewise, do not assume that every visible anchor is a destination or that walking directly between anchors is safe. Stay on the verified route.

Human construction is not a guarantee either. Forty steps can end at a cliff. An old path may commemorate a destination rather than provide a continuation. A building may be disused rather than abandoned. Treat structures as evidence of former intention, not present-day routing advice.

### Expect visibility and scale to change abruptly

On an open saddle, you may be able to read weather across entire valleys. A short distance later, the forest may reduce your world to wet bark, leaves against both shoulders, and the next handhold. Allow much more time than the map distance suggests.

Near escarpments:

- Keep well back from edges concealed by vegetation, wet soil, or mist.
- Do not approach an edge merely because the rock is ancient and the slope looks settled.
- Do not improvise climbing when a route becomes technical. For an ordinary visitor, that is usually a turnaround point.
- Remember that the waterfalls may become mist long before any bottom is visible; the apparent softness of the cloud below says nothing about the drop.

The stone polished by splay-hoofed browsers is evidence of animal use, not proof of a safe human trail.

### Treat marshes, sinter, and tree reservoirs as systems, not amenities

Do not shortcut across a marsh because its bowl looks small or its outlet modest. Sedges and floating leaves can conceal the actual surface, depth, and firmness of the ground. Avoid disturbing the fishing birds and their nests.

Stay off mineral terraces unless a route specifically crosses stable ground. Sinter may be slick, hot, fragile, or all three. Do not test an unknown pool with bare skin, and do not assume filtration will make hot or mineral-rich water suitable to drink.

Cauldron-tree hollows are inhabited wetlands. Do not drink from them, put your hands into them, fasten equipment to their gutters, or use them as convenient washing places.

For ordinary water needs, use sources identified as suitable by your route leader and treat them appropriately. Abundant water is not the same as safe water. Carry enough between reliable sources, and do not forage or hunt unless local compatibility and sustainability have been established.

### Keep the party together through the noise

Rain, waterfalls, insects, wings, and low animal calls can swallow a shouted name. Delayed echoes near anchors may make distance judgment still worse.

Use close visual contact, agreed regrouping points, and signals that everyone has practiced. At junctions, wait until the whole party is present. Avoid splitting up to scout unknown ground. Camp before dusk unless there is a compelling reason not to; wet cliffs, hidden marsh margins, and animal activity make night travel a poor way to save time.

## Give the anchors a wide, respectful berth

There is no need for an ordinary visitor to touch an anchor, stand on its disk, scrape its seam, probe it with instruments, or camp beneath its roofless dry column. Observation and photography are sufficient.

Do not use cicada concentrations, delayed calls, rain behaviour, or the deep tone as improvised surveying instruments. They are intriguing features, not a calibrated map.

Nor should you approach a margin to discover where the change begins. Treat an unexpected, sustained drop in warmth or a change in breathing as a reason to return toward the last confirmed safe route—**before** ice, severe symptoms, or confusion provide clearer evidence. Seeing hoarfrost across a gorge does not mean the frosted side is a reasonable place to visit.

If someone develops serious breathing difficulty, confusion, loss of coordination, or dangerous cold:

1. Stop outward travel and keep the person accompanied.
2. Return by the shortest **known safe** route toward the last confirmed safe area; do not blindly descend.
3. Shelter them, replace wet insulation where possible, and use emergency oxygen or medical measures only as trained and planned.
4. Initiate extraction early. Do not wait to see whether severe symptoms resolve on their own.

## Camp as though damage may take centuries to repair

The abundance of the Crown can make it seem inexhaustible, but its timescale argues for the opposite conclusion. A cauldron tree may spend decades sealing a basin and centuries enlarging it. Damaging its crown gutter or roots could destroy an entire small wetland, not merely injure decorative vegetation.

Use established, durable campsites where available. Do not cut vegetation, strip moss, dig up sinter, move cairns, carve new markers, collect specimens, or build fires. Keep soap and waste out of runnels, marshes, seepage, and tree reservoirs. Follow local waste procedures; where saturated ground makes burial inappropriate, use a pack-out system.

Observe animals without feeding or handling them. Nothing about the stillness of a white fishing bird or the apparent tameness of a browser establishes how it will react to crowding.

For a multiverse visit, biosecurity matters in both directions. Clean mud, seeds, and organic material from boots and equipment before arrival, and do not carry water, soil, cuttings, insects, or samples into another world without proper authorization. Food scraps and dirty gear can transport more than intentions.

## A good first visit

A sound first expedition would look almost modest: arrive early, verify equipment and communications, settle into a known camp inside the safe region, and take only a short familiarization walk. On subsequent days, make guided excursions to an open saddle, a marsh overlook, or a nearby forest section, returning with daylight, energy, and supplies still in reserve. Do not spend the reserve merely because the weather remains mild.

Expect to be damp most of the time. Expect to speak more loudly and to find silence strangely difficult to imagine. Expect the great views to be intermittent rather than constant. Give yourself time to stop on the saddles, but do not become so intent on reaching one that you treat the forest between as an inconvenience.

Above all, do not wait for the landscape to advertise danger. There may be no dome, glow, or unnatural sky—only green leaves, black basalt, warm rain, and an old marker disappearing into vegetation. The ordinary light is part of what makes the place beautiful, and part of what makes it easy to underestimate.

**Stay inside the old accommodation rather than testing its edges. Keep to the markers, keep your party together, preserve one dry refuge, and leave the water and roots as you found them.** Then you can afford to notice the honey-coloured terraces at evening, the amphibians climbing from their tree cisterns, and the isolated peaks far below, moving through cloud like dark ships.
