# Preparing for the Country of the Standing Roar

Treat this as a life-support expedition, not merely an unusually stark trekking destination. Its stillness is misleading. Nothing needs to storm, stampede, or collapse for a visitor to get into trouble: thin air, glare, cold contact, thirst, a misjudged distance, and sleep deprivation inside the roar are quite sufficient.

Prepare in this order: **arrival, life support, sensory endurance, navigation, and only then itinerary.**

## 1. Make the arrival reversible

Do not let your first crossing be a traverse, and do not arrive assuming that an instantaneous journey has somehow acclimatized your body.

Before sending a person through, instrument the destination if at all possible. Measure:

- Ambient pressure and breathable-gas composition
- Temperature and radiation exposure
- Airborne sound levels
- Vibration transmitted through the stone
- Whether your navigation, communications, and return equipment actually function there

“High altitude” is not a complete specification. Supplemental oxygen may be sufficient in some conditions; in sufficiently low pressure it is not. If measurements indicate that ordinary acclimatization and oxygen equipment cannot keep an unprotected human safely conscious, use a suitable pressure suit or pressurized shelter—or do not go in person. Fitness and determination do not change ambient pressure.

If the altitude is within a range humans can approach through acclimatization, prepare gradually under competent guidance before crossing. Even then, keep supplemental oxygen available and treat the first exposure conservatively.

Your return arrangement deserves special redundancy. Elsewhere, an emergency high-altitude plan often includes rapid descent. Here, **“head downhill” may be useless**: passes can run for days without appearing to rise or fall, and the horizon may simply reveal more of the same roof. A person suffering hypoxia cannot count on walking quickly out of the country.

The first visit should remain close to a tested exit and be measured in a cautious, predetermined exposure, not in how much scenery you hope to cover.

## 2. Carry your entire habitat

There are no settlements, caches, fuel supplies, reliable shelters, or rescue services. Assume that everything necessary for survival must arrive with you and leave with you.

### Air

Carry primary and backup breathing support appropriate to the measured conditions, with reserves for exertion, delays, and equipment problems. Use visual gauges and alarms wherever possible; an alarm you cannot hear over the roar is not an alarm.

A pulse oximeter or similar instrument can provide useful information, but do not let one reassuring number override confusion, clumsiness, severe headache, vomiting, or deteriorating judgment. New headache, nausea, dizziness, or unusual exhaustion should be treated as possible altitude illness or hypoxia, not casually blamed on the sound.

### Water and food

**Water exists in the country, but it is not available to you.**

Do not look at the white domes and assume snowfields or meltwater. Do not plan to fill bottles from seeps, cup-sized pools, or films inside fractures. Apart from being uncertain and difficult to reach, those tiny reserves are habitats and parts of an exact hydrological balance. Removing or contaminating one could matter far out of proportion to its volume.

Carry all drinking water, divided among containers so that one failure is not catastrophic. Drink on a schedule: constant coolness and dry air can make thirst an unreliable guide. If long-duration breathing equipment dries your throat further, plan humidification using your own supplies, not local moisture.

Bring ready-to-eat food and enough for delays. There is nothing reasonable to forage, nothing to burn, and no ethical or practical reason to supplement your provisions from the hidden food web.

### Cold

The light does not imply warmth, and shade offers no special cold refuge. Dress for the measured temperature and for conductive heat loss.

Use insulated boots, gloves, and an insulated sitting or kneeling layer. Do not press a bare palm to the ground out of curiosity about the vibration; exposed stone can take heat through the hand within seconds. Avoid sitting directly on rock or allowing clothing to remain in prolonged contact with it.

### Light and time

Use wraparound eye protection appropriate to the measured radiation and visible-light intensity, with a spare. A hat alone is inadequate when both the pale ground and the horizon are painfully bright. The lenses must protect you without making pale-on-pale fractures and changes in slope invisible; test that balance before arrival.

Cover exposed skin and protect the top of the head, neck, and shoulders from the fixed, nearly overhead sun.

The sun is not a clock. Bring redundant timepieces and a written schedule for drinking, eating, rest, equipment checks, and departure. If you ever sleep there, you will also need something to exclude the constant light.

## 3. Plan for sound that cannot simply be shut out

Hearing protection is necessary if measurements show a hazardous airborne exposure, but it will not make the country silent.

Plugs, muffs, and active cancellation act mainly on sound reaching you through the air. Much of this roar arrives through your boots, legs, skeleton, and any surface touching your body. A helmet or pressure suit should not be assumed to solve that problem.

Consequently:

- Treat hearing protection as exposure reduction, not as permission for an indefinite stay.
- Do not enter clefts or chambers in search of quiet. They may be colder, slicker, and more strongly coupled to the massif’s vibration.
- Do not test the experience by lying down. A camp requiring you to sleep directly on the ground is a bad camp.
- Prefer to return elsewhere to sleep. If overnight stays are unavoidable, use a carefully engineered, freestanding, vibration-isolated shelter and test the complete system before the expedition. Do not assume an ordinary sleeping pad will be enough.
- Do not use alcohol or sedatives to force sleep in a hypoxic environment.

The passes requiring days to cross present a special problem: they are not merely long walks. They demand a workable way to sleep where lying down can make the roar feel as though it is inside your skull. Until you have solved that problem, those passes are effectively closed to an ordinary traveler.

Use non-auditory communication. Carry rugged text devices, agree on hand signals, and use visual status indicators. Keep companions within sight and perform scheduled checks. **Your partner may not hear you shout, fall, or approach from behind.** Equipment alarms, spoken briefings, and voice-activated controls should not be your only safeguards.

Pre-write important procedures. Under continuous vibration and sensory load, a short checklist may be more reliable than your memory or your ability to improvise.

## 4. Navigate by measurement, not by appearance

The country will repeatedly offer you false bargains: a dome that looks close, a saddle that seems nearly level, a basin whose far wall appears sharp enough to touch.

Do not accept any of these impressions as distance estimates.

Before travel, establish an actual surveyed route with measured distances. Carry redundant offline mapping and a return track. Satellite positioning and magnetic compasses should be used only if tested there; otherwise rely on a suitable combination of inertial, visual, and ranging instruments. An altimeter alone may tell you little about progress across the broad passes.

Use explicit rules:

- A visible landmark is not a destination until its distance has been measured.
- Turn around according to time, supplies, and human performance—not according to whether the next dome “looks nearly here.”
- If navigation systems disagree, stop and resolve the discrepancy or return.
- Do not follow wildlife in hope of finding water, shelter, or an easy route. A soarer’s range and a rockrunner’s needs are not yours.
- Do not assume that your footprints will remain visible on the pale, hard surface.

The scree’s stability is welcome, but it does not make every route safe. Fixed shadows and brilliant light can make slopes difficult to read. Move deliberately on open stone. Avoid deep joints and cleft lips, particularly where dark, damp surfaces lie below. Do not descend into a fracture for water, shelter, or curiosity without a separate technical plan; for an ordinary visit, route around such places.

## 5. Treat vibration, ecology, and contamination as related concerns

The apparent emptiness encourages carelessness. It should do the opposite.

A cushion plant may be the only plant on an acre. A tiny pool may support life you cannot see. A crack that looks like bare stone may contain several layers of animals whose sensory world consists partly of small interruptions in ground vibration.

Walk on bare, stable stone where possible. Avoid cushion plants, lichen-marked fractures, wet recesses, and cleft mouths. Do not hammer rocks, jump to test the vibration, or operate unnecessary vibrating machinery. What is an insignificant knock to you may be a substantial disturbance in a sensory environment already dominated by the standing roar.

Observe rockrunners and soarers without feeding, pursuing, calling, or attempting to provoke a response. Do not use carcasses as convenient observation posts or leave food to attract animals.

Most importantly, remain a closed system:

- Carry out food scraps, packaging, gray water, and sanitary waste.
- Never use a cleft as a toilet or disposal site.
- Leave no cairns, painted marks, ropes, stakes, caches, wire, or “temporary” equipment.
- Do not move stones to make seats, shelters, or route markers.
- Take no water, organisms, or souvenir rocks without a genuinely compelling scientific reason and an appropriate protocol.

Arrive with equipment cleaned and decontaminated according to your cross-world procedures, and do not clean it on site. Earth microorganisms introduced into a permanently damp joint could become a much more serious import than a visible piece of litter. Apply equivalent caution to potentially contaminated gear when you return.

A single discarded object would not merely be untidy here. It might become the country’s only persistent artifact.

## 6. Prepare psychologically for the absence of variation

There is no better hour to wait for. The roar will not diminish toward evening; there is no evening. The light will not soften, the shadows will not turn, and a distant objective will not gradually acquire atmospheric perspective.

A visit may therefore feel like this:

The land appears serene while your body is working hard. Your mouth becomes dry despite the coolness. Your companion’s gestures replace speech. The chosen dome refuses to grow larger. When you stop, the sound seems to become clearer rather than easier to ignore. You begin attributing irritation or poor concentration to your own weakness, when in fact you may be approaching a physiological exposure limit. Your watch reveals that your sense of elapsed time has been wrong.

None of this means the country has changed. It means you have begun to experience its actual scale.

Rehearse essential tasks while wearing your eye protection and breathing equipment, using only visual communication. Practice following checklists when tired and overstimulated. A simulation cannot certify that the roar is safe, but it can reveal whether your equipment and procedures are needlessly frustrating.

If you require silence to sleep or remain oriented, do not plan an overnight visit. That is not a failure of nerve; it is a sensible recognition of compatibility.

## A sensible first visit

A reasonable first human visit would be deliberately unimpressive:

1. Confirm environmental compatibility and a reliable immediate return.
2. Arrive fully supported, with a companion or base team monitoring the crossing.
3. Remain on a short, surveyed route over open stone.
4. Make no camps, collect no samples, enter no clefts, and touch no hidden water.
5. Check instruments, coordination, hydration, and comprehension on schedule.
6. Leave at the predetermined time, while you still feel well—not after the roar, glare, or distance has begun to degrade your judgment.

Extend later visits only after reviewing what actually happened, including sleep, cognition, equipment consumption, and after-effects. Do not measure success by mileage or sightings. Seeing no animal larger than a thumb is not a disappointing visit; it is a normal one.

The country will not provide a dramatic climax or a natural endpoint. It will simply continue. Your schedule, reserves, and willingness to turn back must supply the margins of safety.

Go prepared to endure a small, carefully measured interval of it. Stand long enough to understand that the apparent vacancy is populated, that the stillness is sounding, and that your own senses are among the least reliable instruments you brought. Then return with margin, leaving no cairn, cache, wire, waste, or disturbed cup of water behind.
