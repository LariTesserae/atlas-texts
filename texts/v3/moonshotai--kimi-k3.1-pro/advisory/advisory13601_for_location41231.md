# Preparing to Visit the Finished Garden

**Treat the visit as a brief, non-invasive EVA into an energized, resonant confined space—not as a botanical excursion.**

Nothing in the Garden appears hostile. There is nothing to hunt, fight, or flee from. The difficulty is that nothing there is arranged to keep a human alive, make room for a human, or necessarily recover from a human mistake. The machinery does not offer shelter, the vegetation does not offer food or water, and the smallness of the chamber does not make it easy to evacuate.

For an ordinary traveler, **observation from the threshold should be the default plan**. Physical entry should be the exception, permitted only if it can be done without forcing the foliage, trusting doubtful machinery, or making a destructive rescue likely.

## What the visit will probably feel like

The approach will be visually immense and acoustically empty. The plains will cease to look like terrain and become color and mineral pattern below you. The smooth black needle will offer few familiar clues about distance or scale. Outside, you will not be able to depend on hearing a companion shout, a rope snap tight, or equipment strike stone.

Near the seam, the Garden may announce itself through your equipment and body before you hear it properly: pressure in a handhold, then the wrist, then the teeth. Inside, the contrast will be extreme. The chamber is only seven paces long, but it will not feel open. Amber lamps, dense green surfaces, black fern-work, and the compressed ceiling will make it seem less like entering a room than putting your head inside a held chord.

Do not expect the sound to provide useful orientation. It has no rests, no developing rhythm, and no reassuring cycle. Do not expect touch to help either: stone, iron, and foliage all have the same coolness, and the engine is not a warm object around which you can rally.

Your own movements may be the only visible events. That can make even a small adjustment of your boot feel disproportionately violent.

Most importantly, **do not interpret “finished” as meaning that your own time, metabolism, or vulnerability is suspended**. Your oxygen will still be consumed, your batteries will still drain, and you can still fall, panic, or suffer hearing and vibration injury.

## Before arrival

### Survey first; arrive somewhere else

Do not use the chamber as your multiverse arrival point. A slight positional error could put you in rock, outside the needle, or inside vegetation that has no space for you. Arrive at a surveyed staging area with room to test equipment and abort.

Before committing to the ascent, establish:

- Local gravity, atmospheric pressure and composition, temperature, radiation exposure, and weather.
- Whether the chamber contains any atmosphere at all. Sound inside is **not** proof of breathable air or survivable pressure.
- Whether your pressure suit, transit equipment, radios, and life-support system function normally under local conditions.
- Vibration levels near the staples and seam, acoustic levels at the threshold, and possible electrical hazards around the foliage and housing.
- A return route that does not depend on the engine, the Garden’s continuing operation, or a rescue party reaching you quickly.

Any aerial approach must be designed for the actual atmospheric density; an ordinary rotorcraft may be useless in air this thin.

There is no quiet season to wait for, no dormant period during which entry becomes less disruptive, and no indication that the engine will eventually wind down. Schedule around your own consumables and exposure limits, not around an expected change in the Garden.

### Go with a team, but plan for only one observer

Do not go solo. A suitable expedition needs people responsible for the staging area, ascent safety, communications, and the return deadline. Only one person should consider entering, and only while another remains at the approach.

The confined-space problem is more serious than the chamber’s dimensions suggest. A shoulder-wide seam, a pressure suit, dense growth, and a springy root-and-cable floor make rapid extraction doubtful. A threshold attendant cannot simply rush in and pull you out.

**If an incapacitated visitor could not be removed without cutting roots, tearing up the pavement, or dismantling the housing, do not enter.** A rescue plan that requires destroying the site is not an adequate plan.

A compact camera or other remote observer may provide the best visit. This is not a lesser achievement.

## Equipment and personal preparation

### Life support and thermal protection

Until measurements prove otherwise, dress for the entire excursion as an EVA. An oxygen mask and cold-weather clothing are not sufficient preparation for potentially near-vacuum altitude conditions.

Use a closed pressure suit with:

- Independent oxygen supply and carbon-dioxide control.
- Thermal regulation and adequate insulation.
- A reserve sized for the ascent, threshold work, delayed egress, and return—not merely for a few minutes indoors.
- Visual warnings that remain usable when audible alarms cannot be heard.
- A helmet visor that can move from exterior glare to dim amber light without being opened.

The suit should be as clean, dry, non-shedding, and low-profile as safety permits. If a properly safe suit cannot pass the seam, the answer is not a thinner suit, a held breath, or removing your helmet inside. The answer is a camera.

The engine cannot serve as emergency warmth, shelter, or a power outlet.

### Climbing and approach equipment

The six staples are evidence of former access, not proof of present safety. Their dry tarnish and the absence of debris do not establish their load-bearing condition.

Use an independently planned access system. Do not make the staples your sole protection without assessment, and never clip safety equipment to the enamel tag, lamp cages, foliage, roots, or engine housing. Do not drill new bolts, clean away tarnish for convenience, or enlarge the aperture.

Smooth, grit-free rock should not automatically be treated as well-maintained rock. It may simply be finished.

### Hearing and whole-body vibration protection

Use properly fitted hearing protection integrated with the helmet, and measure exposure rather than estimating it from discomfort. But understand its limitation: **ear protection does not stop vibration arriving through boots, handholds, the suit, or your skeleton**.

Do not lean against the rear housing, press your helmet to the rock to listen, or maintain unnecessary contact with vibrating surfaces. Set a conservative exposure deadline before arrival. Habituation is not acclimatization.

Leave immediately for symptoms such as escalating tooth or jaw pain, headache, nausea, numbness, visual disturbance, disorientation, or difficulty coordinating movement. If the note seems to fade, acquire a beat, or change character, initially treat that as a possible change in your hearing, perception, or equipment—not as evidence that the Garden has entered a new phase.

### Electrical and contamination precautions

No visible motion does not mean the engine is inactive. Still gauge needles do not mean it is safe to touch.

Treat the crystal stacks, busbars, root-metal junctions, cables, and conducting leaf veins as parts of one potentially energized system. Do not improvise grounding, probe the machinery with metal tools, or attempt to recharge equipment from it. Have the suit’s electrostatic behavior assessed; absolutely dry conditions are not a reason to be casual about charge.

Your body is also a source of contamination. Breath moisture, sweat, skin oils, fibers, hair, microbes, and food residue would all be additions to a system that currently contains no dew, sap, animal waste, or organic debris. Keep life support closed, pre-dry external equipment, and conduct all eating, drinking, and hygiene at the staging area.

Bring no water for the plants. **Their dryness is not drought.**

### Time, light, and communications

Carry an independent elapsed-time display and have an outside partner enforce a second deadline. Use visual alerts; an audible timer may disappear into the chord, and a subtle vibrating alarm may be indistinguishable from environmental vibration.

Outside, use tested radios backed by prearranged visual signals. Inside, do not depend on spoken communication.

Bring steady, low-output personal illumination, since the three lamps are dim and an exterior-tinted visor may leave you nearly blind. Do not mount a tripod among the roots or attach instruments to the foliage. Record from the threshold where possible.

Carry nothing loose into the chamber. There may be no ordinary process there to conceal, degrade, or carry away a dropped lens cap, glove, fastener, or written marker.

## At the threshold

Remain suited. Make your final measurements without using your bare hand as a vibration sensor or inserting probes among the plants.

Look carefully before deciding that entry exists. A seven-pace chamber is not necessarily seven paces of available floor. The open area beside the rear housing is large enough for hands, not a promise of standing room, and it should not become your destination merely because it is the only visible vacancy.

Do not push leaves aside, tie back fern-work, roll roots beneath your boots, or assume that pliant vegetation will simply spring back unharmed. If every step must compress roots, cables, and stone together, or if your shoulders must force a passage, stop at the seam.

Leave unnecessary packs and tools outside, but never detach essential life support to make yourself fit.

## If you do enter

Keep the visit short, keep the exit in sight, and make no unnecessary contact with the walls or engine.

Specifically:

- Do not turn the handwheel.
- Do not touch the crystal stacks or test whether they are warm.
- Do not replace the ceramic fuse, fill the three empty bolt holes, renew the survey line, or restore the faded tag.
- Do not clean, lubricate, water, prune, warm, or “restart” anything.
- Do not introduce seeds, insects, soil, or other life in an attempt to make the Garden more natural.
- Do not take a leaf, root segment, mineral filament, gauge, fuse, or tarnish sample as a souvenir.
- Do not clap, sing, strike the housing, or play tones back at it to see whether the resonance answers.
- Do not leave an offering, plaque, coin, marker, or other token.

The handwheel is a control, not an invitation. The empty bolt holes and exposed fuse are not a work order. The apparent legibility of the machinery does not mean you understand its operating state.

Likewise, do not treat the Garden as a survival resource. Its vegetation contains no useful water, offers no established food, and has no animals or animal products to forage. Its apparent energy is not necessarily available energy for you.

## Prepare yourself psychologically

A human visitor is likely to search for a story: an approaching climax, a hidden message in the chord, a bud about to open, a needle about to tremble. Another common impulse will be to become its caretaker—to correct the tarnish, complete the missing hardware, or supply the water that an ordinary garden would need.

Resist both impulses.

There is no indication that the sound is communication, that the machinery awaits an operator, or that your arrival has been anticipated. Do not mistake dryness for thirst, stillness for dormancy, age for neglect, or the absence of animals for an ecological vacancy you ought to fill.

The combination of loud vibration, dim crowded foliage, uniform coolness, and unchanging detail may also produce claustrophobia, irritability, or a peculiar sense of being outside time. If that happens, check your actual clock and life-support readings. Do not turn a subjective impression into a theory about the place. Anyone likely to panic or dissociate under those conditions should choose remote observation without embarrassment; a panicked visitor in the seam becomes a danger to both the team and the Garden.

## Leaving

Exit at the predetermined deadline, at the first equipment problem, or at the first significant physical symptom—not after “one more look” at the rear housing. Do not linger simply because nothing appears to have happened. Nothing happening is not evidence that exposure is harmless.

Once clear, remain suited until you reach safe conditions. Check hearing, balance, communications, suit integrity, and consumables. Account for every item carried to the site. Clean and inspect equipment away from the aperture so that nothing is brushed, washed, or blown into it.

A successful visit is not measured by whether you reached the engine, obtained a sample, or discovered a purpose. It is measured by a safe return, a complete equipment inventory, and no fresh scar on stone, metal, or leaf.

Go prepared to be unnecessary. Carry everything you need, take no support from the Garden, add nothing to its circuit, and leave before your presence becomes an experiment. **The Garden does not require discovery; the task is to make discovery require nothing from the Garden.**
