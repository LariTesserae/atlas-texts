Go prepared for **an oven that looks like twilight**, and for **an oasis that offers no water**. Treat silence the same way: as a phase of the ventilation cycle, not proof that the place is inert.

Nothing suggests that you need weapons or that the town will collapse at a careless glance. The masonry is ancient but robust, and there are no animals to avoid. The serious dangers are more ordinary and easier to underestimate: heat, dehydration, dust and reproductive particles in the air, unreliable footing, difficult communication during the roar, and the absence of any local help or resupply.

## First, establish that entering is reasonable

The dark seam is not necessarily a human-sized doorway. Before packing for an exploration, determine whether there is an existing route through the mineral fault that you can use without widening it, breaking masonry, cutting roots, or clearing a conduit. If entry requires altering the ventilation system, do not enter. A visit that begins by changing the Thrum’s airflow has already damaged the thing you came to see.

If possible, observe from outside through a complete heating and ventilation cycle. Note:

- When the seam becomes warmer and when it emits its windless thread of dust.
- How long venting lasts and whether it reverses.
- Whether there are genuinely quiet intervals, rather than merely quieter ones.
- Whether surface conditions make waiting at the seam more dangerous than entering.

Do not put your face over the seam to listen or smell it. An active dust thread is an outflow, not a convenient signpost.

The chamber’s atmosphere should also be treated as unverified until tested. The description establishes heat and dryness, not human-breathable air. A particulate respirator does **not** correct oxygen deficiency or an unknown gas hazard. If you cannot establish that the air is breathable, your choices are properly designed supplied air or remaining outside. Supplied air would still do nothing about the heat.

## Make the surface camp your real lifeline

The salt pan offers no useful settlement, no reliable landmarks, and no reason to expect water or shade. At noon there may be severe glare; before dawn, the seam will be harder to find and the chamber darker still. Use redundant navigation and do not assume that satellite positioning, radio, or an emergency multiverse departure will work exactly when needed.

A small party is preferable to a solo visit. Ideally, someone remains outside with communications, additional water, and a practical way to assist an exhausted or injured traveler. Establish check-in times and a hard overdue procedure before anyone enters.

Your surface support should include:

- All drinking water for the approach, visit, and return, plus a separate emergency reserve.
- Electrolytes appropriate to prolonged sweating.
- Shade and a genuine means of cooling people after exit.
- Insulated water storage that will not leave you trying to drink dangerously hot water.
- Food for the wider expedition, though routine eating should happen outside the chamber.
- First aid and heat-illness supplies.
- Spare lights, batteries, respirator filters, and communications equipment.

Do not camp on the seam, pile equipment around it, or accidentally obstruct it with a shelter.

There is no useful local water-survival plan. Do not count on finding a hidden cistern, collecting overnight condensation, cutting roots, chewing leaves, or extracting moisture from the vegetation. The town does not merely lack accessible water; its entire infrastructure and ecology are organized without it.

## Personal equipment

For a short entry into air already known to be breathable, the important equipment is unglamorous:

### Heat and body protection

Wear loose, protective clothing suitable for hot conditions, with long sleeves and trousers to keep skin off stone and vegetation. Use sturdy boots with heat-resistant soles and good traction on rounded cobbles, powdery dust, and sand ramps. Gloves are useful for the access route, but should not encourage you to handle the town.

Remember that shade is not the same as cooling. The floor heats from below, the walls radiate warmth, and heat accumulates beneath the roof. A black doorway may look inviting without being a cool refuge. Neither sitting on the floor nor climbing to an upper walk should be treated as a way to escape the heat.

Arrive rested and normally hydrated, keep exertion low, and make the first visit much shorter than your curiosity wants. Protective clothing and respiratory equipment add to heat strain; carrying excessive water to justify a longer stay can do the same.

### Eyes and lungs

Use a well-fitted respirator appropriate for fine mineral dust, sand, pollen, and spores, together with sealed eye protection. Keep spare filters sealed until needed.

Protection should remain on throughout the visit. The great exhalation carries sand, pale seeds, and yellow pollen, but removing your mask when that cloud settles would be premature: the returning current carries finer spores. Nowhere inside should be assumed particle-free during a release, since seeds can cross nearly the entire hollow.

These substances need not be exotic poisons to cause trouble. Fine dry particles, unknown plant material, heat, and exertion are sufficient reasons to protect your airway and eyes. If you can only tolerate your mask by repeatedly removing it inside, you are not equipped for the visit.

### Light and communication

Carry at least two independent electric lights and spare power. At its brightest, the chamber is only comparable to late dusk; salt ledges will be visible while doorways remain black and upper wall surfaces disappear. A second, lower-angled light is useful for revealing curbs, gratings, worn thresholds, and changes in dust or sand underfoot.

Do not use flame for illumination. It adds heat and consumes oxygen in an enclosed space containing dry oil- and wax-rich vegetation, old resin, and fiber lashings.

The roar is described as less than deafening, but it will be fatiguing and may make spoken communication impossible. Agree on hand and light signals beforehand. Carry hearing protection for comfort if needed, but do not acoustically isolate yourself so completely that you miss the first low tone or a companion’s warning.

## Time the town, not your own schedule

Do not automatically choose noon because it is brightest, or the less-hot hours because you expect them to be cool. Noon may coincide with the surface heating that drives the exhalation; the darker hours offer no moisture and no assured comfort. Use actual observations of the cycle.

For an initial visit, stay near the entrance and explore only what can be reached without committing to a long crossing. Leave enough time to exit without hurrying.

The important sequence is:

1. **A low tone beneath the paving.**
2. **Separate flues joining at different pitches.**
3. **The full resonant exhalation**, with moving sand, streaming fronds, lifting shutters, rattling pods, and a combined seed release.
4. **A later pressure reversal**, carrying finer spores on the returning current.
5. **A weakening roar**, when individual taps, snaps, ticks, and contracting stones become audible again.

The first tone is a warning to execute your plan, not an invitation to stand over a grating and investigate. If you are close to a safe exit and your observations say there is time, leave calmly. Otherwise, move to a location you selected earlier, clear of flues, seed heads, shutters, counterweights, and the exposed central crossing.

If caught in the event:

- Keep your eye and airway protection on.
- Do not run blindly through vegetation or dark doorways.
- Do not try to hold a moving shutter or steady its mechanism.
- Do not assume the central vacancy offers shelter.
- Let the visible sand and seed cloud pass without releasing your mask.
- Remain alert for the return current before deciding that conditions have settled.

Many of the alarming noises are normal parts of the town’s operation. Pods opening together may sound like gravel striking tile; shutters knock; stones contract under the floor. Knowing this should prevent panic, but it should not make you dismiss genuinely new hazards.

## Move as though the architecture still has a job

The broad central vacancy is not simply a convenient plaza. It is the chamber’s principal empty feature, its acoustic return, and part of the space across which seeds travel. In quiet conditions it may offer an unobstructed route; during exhalation it offers little concealment and no reason to linger.

The perimeter has the opposite problem. Fronds can conceal entire doorways, flues emerge along walls, and sand traps and conduits complicate the paving. Do not push through curtains of growth merely because they look soft. Their leaves are tough, warm, and oil-bearing, and a broken stem may represent an extremely long investment of growth.

You need not treat the town as unbearably fragile, but do not use it as climbing equipment:

- Stay off the covered walk, roofs, and slender piers.
- Do not test counterweights, treadles, shutters, or polished handles.
- Do not lift gratings, clear sand traps, or reopen conduits to see what happens.
- Do not cut roots out of masonry; some of them are now helping hold the walls together.
- Do not assume that a doorway, interior court, or kiln is cooler or safer than the lane outside.

The apparent smallness of the town is deceptive. A few hundred paces is easy on a cool map and potentially serious in radiant heat, dim light, protective equipment, and airborne dust.

Set turnaround rules before entering. Leave at the first signs of headache, dizziness, nausea, unusual clumsiness, confusion, breathing difficulty, or escalating fatigue. Also leave for excessive filter resistance, failing lights, lost communications, or an unexpectedly early ventilation cycle. Do not consume the emergency water reserve merely to see one more workshop court. Confusion or collapse is an emergency requiring evacuation and active cooling, not a shorter rest in a doorway.

## Do not try to improve the Thrum

The most damaging visitor would not necessarily be a looter. It might be a helpful one: someone who waters the “parched” plants, clears a blocked flue, sweeps sand from a street, lights a kiln for warmth or light, or repairs a shutter so it moves properly.

The plants are not parched. The ventilation system is not waiting to be restored. The stable dust slopes, sand ramps, roots, seed capsules, and worn paving all belong to an ongoing balance. The old machinery is now also an ecological clock.

Accordingly:

- Do not spill or dump water, wash equipment, spit, urinate, or leave other bodily waste inside.
- Do not leave food, food scraps, containers, markers, or camping equipment.
- Do not clear growth or sand to make the town look more like its original plan.
- Do not take artifacts, seeds, pods, roots, or “dead” plant lace as souvenirs.
- Keep your visit brief; even an ordinary human body introduces heat, moisture, and foreign biology.

Multiverse travel adds another responsibility. Arrive with equipment free, as far as practical, of soil, seeds, insects, and plant material from previous worlds. On leaving, contain local dust and contaminated filters rather than shaking them out at camp or carrying them casually to your next destination. A place with no animal life and extremely slow growth is a particularly poor location for accidental biological introductions.

## What the visit will actually feel like

Your lamp will pick out chalk-pale salt, dark stone, bronze leaf margins, and perhaps a little suspended dust, while much of the town remains black beyond the beam. The greenery will not feel like a garden refuge. It will be substantial, waxy, warm, and almost motionless until the air begins to move.

The great sound may seem to arrive through the ground before it becomes a roar around you. Then the small town will feel acoustically enormous: sand lifting in sheets, fronds turning together, shutters hesitating and knocking, seed capsules rattling toward their simultaneous release. When the sound weakens, the heat will still be there. No birds will return afterward, because none left. No insects will resume their noise. Apart from the town’s small continuing ticks and taps, your movements will be answered mainly by echoes.

That can make a visitor want to stand still and listen longer than is wise. Choose where and when to do so.

A successful visit is brief, reversible, and almost disappointingly uneventful: you enter through an existing opening during a understood quiet interval, observe without operating anything, leave before your body or equipment reaches its limits, cool down outside, and remove everything you brought. The next exhalation then raises the shutters and opens the pods on the Thrum’s own schedule, exactly as it would have done without you.
