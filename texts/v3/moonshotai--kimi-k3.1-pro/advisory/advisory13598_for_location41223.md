Prepare first by giving up the idea that **visiting means entering**. For an ordinary human, the Seep-City cannot be a walking destination. It is smaller than your hands, its streets may be hairs-width conduits, and there is no entrance to widen. Multiverse travel may get you to the correct universe; it does not solve the last few handspans of basalt.

The right preparation is therefore twofold: ordinary field safety for reaching a cold, unmarked location, and an exceptionally disciplined way of observing a sealed system without becoming part of it.

## Before you go: establish whether you can observe without opening it

The ideal viewing method would make the basalt effectively transparent **without actually altering it**, and would let you hear the cavity without drilling, inserting a probe, or tapping the rock. It should add no light, heat, airflow, vibration, or moisture.

Do not accept “one tiny hole” as harmless. A hole that seems insignificant to you could:

- admit a draft into air that normally has no currents;
- release or redistribute pressure;
- change the seepage feeding the seven ceiling points;
- let the internal sound escape;
- introduce dust, organic material, or unfamiliar microbes.

An ordinary microphone pressed against the hillside may hear nothing. That does not mean the city is absent or your equipment has failed. Do not escalate to striking the rock, drilling a listening hole, or following a fracture inward.

If you have no non-interacting viewer, be honest about the kind of journey you are making. You can visit the rock above or around the city and conduct an exterior pilgrimage. You cannot truthfully call it a tour of the city. Sometimes the correct preparation is deciding not to make the trip until better observation is available.

There is no favorable season, hour, or ceremonial date to wait for. Choose your timing solely for a safe approach and return.

## What to pack

Pack for yourself and the approach, not for life inside the city.

- **Verified coordinates and a surveyed route.** The hill gives no sign, and you cannot locate the city by listening, smelling moisture, or searching for a doorway.
- **Approach-appropriate cold-weather equipment:** insulating layers, waterproof outer clothing, hat, gloves, suitable boots, and whatever route-specific safety equipment the exterior terrain actually requires. The city’s stable interior temperature tells you nothing about conditions outside.
- **Your own water and food.** The cistern is not a water supply. It is fingernail-sized, mineral-bearing, biologically inhabited, and essential to the closed balance.
- **A companion, first-aid supplies, emergency communication, and a firm return time.** There is no assistance available from within the cavity.
- **A low-light observation system**, if your method requires one, that amplifies the existing radiance rather than illuminating the city. Keep your own lamps for the exterior approach.
- **An audio feed with independent gain control and a simple mute control.** Do not connect your ears directly to an unregulated immersive feed.
- **A notebook and timer.** Short, deliberate observation sessions will be more useful than waiting indefinitely for something to happen. Record the real scale frequently; magnification will continually tempt you to imagine human-sized rooms and walkable lanes.

Leave behind excavation tools, sampling bottles, tracer dyes, bait, “just in case” sealants, and anything whose only use would be to test what happens when you interfere. You should not return with a water sample, a copper sliver, a bacterial swab, or a fragment of masonry.

## Do not treat miniaturization as automatic permission

Merely becoming small enough to fit would not make your presence harmless.

Even if the process kept you alive, you would still bring body heat, respiration, organic material, vibration, and physical force into a system whose normal exchanges are minute. Your breath could become a draft. Your warmth could create the city’s first visitor-made temperature gradient. Skin, lint, or microbes could become a substantial ecological input. A hand placed on a wall could redirect capillary flow; a step could obstruct a lane that is also a watercourse.

Nor should you assume that a miniature human could move safely through it. There are no reliably dry, warm rooms in which to shelter. Doorways may be crossed by water membranes. A drop may not behave like harmless rain, and the cistern should not be treated as an ordinary swimming pool. Sound arrives through floors, water films, and chambers, so shouted signals and struck stones would be poor safety tools.

If your emergency escape plan involves breaking out through the basalt, you do not have an acceptable entry plan.

A sensory avatar that exchanges no matter, heat, or force with the cavity could provide a closer view. But that is best understood as immersive observation, not permission to touch, walk, feed, or experiment.

## What the visit will feel like

**From outside, it may feel like nothing.** Cold rock, your own breathing, and no corroborating sound. Do not mistake this anticlimax for failure. Concealment is part of what preserves the city.

**Through a viewer, expect partial understanding rather than a reveal.** Even transparent basalt would not give you a single comprehensive view. Streets cross overhead, rooms obscure other rooms, and passages change category depending on whether you think of them as roads or plumbing. Begin with one bounded sight: the cistern rim, a cup beneath a seep, a copper clamp, or a drop suspended under an arch. Do not exhaust yourself trying to assemble a complete mental map.

Let your eyes adapt to the steady pewter dimness. Wet edges will carry most of the visible information; dry recesses may simply remain unresolved. If your view shows crisp new shadows, consider whether you are illuminating the city rather than witnessing its native light.

**Treat the sound as a sensory load, not a message.** It may suggest surf, machinery, milling grain, hammers, or a crowded market. Human hearing will repeatedly try to extract speech from it. That is an ordinary perceptual tendency, not evidence that someone is calling you. Do not answer, shout back, or add a signal. Use short listening periods, keep the reproduced volume comfortable, and take breaks in exterior silence.

A properly sealed visit should not provide the city’s smell. If you physically smell wet basalt, limestone, metal, and microbial sourness, ask whether containment has been breached rather than treating it as an atmospheric enhancement.

Finally, prepare for **nothing to culminate**. The transparent crustacean may not appear. The light will not brighten. The sound will not build toward a disclosure. No hidden population is likely to emerge because a visitor has arrived. A longer stay does not entitle you to an event.

This is not evidence of a temporal trap or a place literally frozen in an instant. Water and animals still move. There is no described need for protection against time distortion. Its peculiar stillness is an exact balance of continuing processes.

## Conduct while observing

- Do not feed the crustacean or try to attract it.
- Do not introduce dye to trace the water, dust to reveal airflow, or sound to test the acoustics.
- Do not adjust your viewing method by warming, flushing, pressurizing, or draining anything.
- Do not assume every dark chamber must be inspected or every unknown room assigned a purpose such as temple, prison, or house.
- Do not confuse simple tools with crude judgment. Look for the patience in the scratches, drilled channels, dry-fitted pieces, and organizational decisions.
- Do not interpret the city’s long-term stability as robustness against you. It is stable within its present sealed conditions, not necessarily under a visitor’s boundary changes.

Treat mist, a new draft, flickering illumination, a change in the cistern level, or an alteration in the sound’s pitch or loudness as reasons to stop and assess your apparatus—not as interesting new phenomena to pursue.

Pay attention to your own limits as well. Scale shifts can produce vertigo; the architecture can induce claustrophobia even when your body remains outside; the unvarying roar can cause listening fatigue; and the apparent absence of events can make people careless or strangely compelled to intervene. A companion who keeps track of time and your condition is useful.

## Leaving

A successful visit may produce no complete photograph, no specimen, no dramatic encounter, and no proof beyond careful records. That is sufficient. Keep exact location information discreet if publishing it would encourage excavation or souvenir hunting.

You are not completing the city by witnessing it, and it owes you no acknowledgment. Go warmly dressed, patiently equipped, and willing to leave with incomplete knowledge.

**The city will not remember your visit. Good preparation ensures that this remains literally true, not merely poetic.**
