Prepare for the High Catchment as a **self-supported expedition through a vast, uninhabited drainage system**, not as a casual visit to picturesque ruins. It does not appear to require alpine climbing skill or extraordinary protective equipment. The dangers are ordinary: cold, wet footing, concealed drops, repetitive geography, and being farther from help than your supplies can comfortably support.

Nor should you imagine constant flooding. Most of the old grades still work, exposed paving can dry quickly, and clear days may be bright and pleasant. The difficulty is that conditions vary from district to district and from one level of the stone to the next. You can be walking in sunlight toward a shower, sleeping on dry paving above a seeping wall, or crossing a clean court beside another court silted to its doorways.

The essential fact is: **water is plentiful; food, dry shelter, directions, and assistance are not.**

## Keep the first journey small

The plateau and its districts continue for weeks of travel, with no center or dominant landmark to reward a long push inward. Do not make your first visit an attempted crossing, and do not keep walking merely because the next district might contain something more definitive. It may contain only another, differently arranged catchment.

A better first journey would explore:

- one district and its stair streets;
- one sequence of roofs, courts, channels, and cisterns;
- a reservoir terrace or shallow mere;
- and, if reachable without a long descent, an outer terrace from which to see separate weather crossing the plateau.

Choose a mild season with long daylight. Winter travel adds thin ice on shaded steps, snow that can obscure edges, and continued seepage beneath apparently dry surfaces; it is best left to travelers already familiar with the place. Whatever the season, allow extra days for low cloud, slow walking on wet stone, and the simple pleasure of stopping.

Travel with at least one companion if possible. Solo visitation is feasible for an experienced backpacker, but the combination of solitude, fog, cistern edges, and repetitive lanes makes a second person unusually valuable. Leave a route and return deadline with someone responsible, and assume there will be no local rescue service.

If your multiverse crossing depends on a particular location or time, give yourself a large margin. Wet steps and low cloud can turn an easy return walk into a slow one. Be able to survive a delayed crossing, and protect any return device from immersion and cold. If it must remain at the arrival point, do not leave it in a courtyard, basin, or other convenient-looking recess that may belong to the drainage system.

## Dress for damp coolness, not just rainfall

Even sunny weather remains cool, and fine rain or condensation can wet clothing without the drama of a storm. Bring:

- a dependable hooded rain shell and rain trousers;
- quick-drying walking clothes, avoiding cotton against the skin;
- a warm insulating layer, plus hat and light gloves;
- several changes of socks and a completely dry set of sleeping clothes;
- sturdy, broken-in footwear with soles you trust on wet, uneven stone;
- sunglasses, sunscreen, and a sun hat for exposed clear intervals.

A pack cover alone is insufficient. Use an internal waterproof liner and separate dry bags for sleepwear, electronics, and medical supplies. Your last dry layer should remain dry even if you have walked through hours of rain and your pack has been set down on wet paving.

Trekking poles can help on cupped steps and long staircase streets, but they do not make polished limestone or calcite trustworthy. Take short steps, test sloping or darkened surfaces, and avoid rushing downhill with a heavy pack. Train beforehand for repeated stair descents; the country is not mountainous, but knees and ankles will notice the city.

You should be comfortable walking for hours with supplies, navigating without continual visual landmarks, and managing wet-weather camp chores. Technical ropework and mountaineering equipment are unnecessary if you remain on the ordinary grades and resist the temptation to explore escarpments or enter deep cisterns.

## Bring your own shelter, and choose its location suspiciously

The obvious shelter is often the wrong one. A dry court may be a court precisely because it temporarily holds runoff. A room that has remained dry through your arrival may hold rain for days after the next shower. A roofed space may drip, condense, or contain an unlit opening into a reservoir.

Carry a freestanding tent with a full rain fly and good ventilation, an insulated sleeping pad, and a sleeping bag rated for the coldest plausible dawn rather than the agreeable afternoon. The pad matters: stone and cold ground will drain heat even when the air does not seem severe.

Camp on durable, dry ground above visible waterlines, with drainage leading away from you. Avoid:

- basins and silted courts;
- channels, overflows, and ground beneath spouts;
- rooms with mineral tide marks or damp wall joints;
- retaining-terrace edges and escarpment rims;
- exposed swells when thunderstorms are possible;
- seams of grass or apparently bare silt where annual plants may emerge after rain.

Do not assume an old slab roof is safe to walk or camp on. The city’s stability is impressive, but nothing is newly repaired, and there is no reason to test loads the builders never intended. Never cook in an enclosed cistern hall or other poorly ventilated space.

Keep a compact emergency shelter available even on day walks. If low cloud removes your landmarks, waiting in a safe place may be much wiser than continuing.

## Carry food; treat water as a resource requiring work

Bring all food for the journey, plus a reserve sufficient for delays. The sparse plants and rare animals are neither a dependable larder nor an invitation to forage. There is no firewood supply worth planning around, and no reason to damage the vegetation that does survive. Carry a stove and enough fuel, and use it only in a ventilated, stable location where it cannot damage stone or ignite your own equipment.

For water:

- Arrive with enough for your immediate needs rather than expecting the first pool to be usable.
- Carry containers sufficient for a dry interval, not merely a single small bottle.
- Prefer rain collected directly into a clean vessel when practical.
- Filter and disinfect water taken from channels, cisterns, or meres; cold, clear, rain-fed water is not automatically sterile.
- Do not swim, wash cookware, or use soap in any pool or channel.

The water system should be observed, not casually altered. Clearing a blockage to dry your path, damming an overflow to deepen a pool, or moving stones around a campsite could change conditions well beyond what you can see.

## Navigate as though every district will eventually look familiar in the wrong way

There will be no central tower to walk toward, no lights after sunset, no smoke, and no useful noise of habitation. A long reservoir or stony swell can conceal the next district. After several turns, your own arrival point may be indistinguishable from scores of other grades and stairways.

Use redundant navigation:

- Make a sketch map as you travel, with distances, approximate bearings, junctions, and changes in slope.
- Record route notes in a waterproof notebook with pencil.
- Photograph important junctions looking both forward and back.
- Note time as well as distance; repeatedly stopping for water and weather can make progress deceptive.
- Carry a compass and, if it functions there, an altimeter or positioning device.
- Treat satellite navigation, communications, and electronic mapping as unproven conveniences until tested.

Keep electronics in waterproof containers and carry a headlamp, backup light, and spare power. Cloud cover makes solar charging an uncertain primary plan.

Do not leave permanent blazes, build cairns from moved stone, or scratch arrows into calcite. If temporary markers are absolutely necessary, make them non-damaging and retrieve every one.

Two rules are especially important:

**Do not follow water downhill to find your way home.** It will lead you through the catchment toward lower cisterns or a mere, not toward a center, exit, or arrival point.

**Do not descend an escarpment simply because lower country is visible.** Cloud can remove the view, the rounded steps can still involve considerable exposure, and the return climb may be much longer than expected.

If lost, stop somewhere dry and safe before fatigue and weather compound the error. Consult your notes and retrace only while you can do so confidently. Wandering toward the next “distinctive” structure is likely to produce more distinctive structures and no better position.

## Respect the quiet hazards

The principal physical risks are easy to underestimate because nothing looks dramatically ruined.

- **Wet steps and paving:** Rounded thresholds, worn hollows, dark seepage, and pale calcite deserve different foot placements. Thin winter ice is particularly deceptive.
- **Cisterns and covered reservoirs:** Treat every dark opening as a possible deep fall or cold-water hazard. Do not enter confined spaces, lean over unprotected edges, or explore them alone.
- **Standing water:** Avoid wading meres or flooded courts. Depth, soft silt, submerged lips, and old channels may not be visible.
- **Localized weather:** Sunshine over your lane says little about the district beyond the next reservoir. Carry rain gear even on clear mornings.
- **Low cloud:** Fog near basins and escarpments can erase precisely the landmarks you need. Stop before reaching an edge or unfamiliar staircase descent.
- **Thunderstorms:** Leave exposed swells and rims early. Do not stand in water or use a flooded route to reach shelter.
- **Cold:** Persistent damp, wind, and cool air can produce hypothermia without extreme temperatures. Persistent shivering, clumsiness, and confusion are reasons to stop, insulate, change into dry clothing, and warm the affected person—not reasons to push for one more landmark.

The stonework generally appears stable, so there is no need to move through the city in fear of imminent collapse. Simply avoid climbing walls, roofs, rubble banks, and terrace faces. Most of the old infrastructure is doing its job without maintenance; your task is not to interfere with it.

Wildlife does not appear to be a major hazard. Prepare for exposure and navigation rather than for herds, swarming insects, or large scavengers. Observe the occasional bird, amphibian, or snail without turning its particular cistern or pool margin into a campsite.

## Leave the catchment unchanged

The High Catchment’s apparent emptiness does not make it disposable. Its interest lies in small accumulations: mineral crust, worn channels, seed-bearing silt, a single seam of moss, or an amphibian occupying one cistern among many.

Stay on durable paving where possible. Do not scrape calcite, remove stones, clear channels, carve marks, dig up plants, or take “unused” water containers and basins out of service. Pack out all rubbish. On short visits, pack out human waste as well; the thin soil and intimate connection between courts and water make casual burial a poor default. Never dispose of wastewater in a dry basin merely because it is dry that day.

## Prepare emotionally for a place without a destination

The High Catchment may initially feel monotonous. There is no skyline to approach and no monument whose discovery will explain the rest. A traveler trying to tour it efficiently may experience only gray lanes, cold rain, and the frustration of repeated grades.

Its character emerges more slowly. Follow one small catchment, from roof to gutter to court to street channel, and notice how the sound changes as the water descends. Return after a dry interval and compare the dark seams with the pale stone. Watch mist leave a basin in the morning, or sunlight turn a series of overflows into brief white threads after rain. At evening, remain long enough to see that another district is receiving weather entirely separate from yours.

The silence can be restorative, but it can also make the scale feel oppressive. Small sounds remain distinct: a drop beneath a spout, gravel combing an overflow, wind turning a corner. There will be no human background noise to reassure you that someone else is nearby. This is another reason not to make solitude and excessive distance the tests of a successful visit.

A good plan therefore leaves time for inactivity. Keep one set of dry clothes protected, one route home unambiguous, one food reserve untouched, and one campsite safely out of the water’s path. Then you can afford to stop and understand what the city is doing.

The High Catchment was organized to admit weather rather than exclude it. Your visit should be similarly tolerant: do not demand a rain-free day, a fixed itinerary, or a conclusive view. Stay warm enough, step carefully, and let stone keep the grades while water keeps the seasons.
