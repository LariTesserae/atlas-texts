Prepare for the First Hollow as though it were three things at once: an unstable subglacial cavity, an exceptionally fragile cold-water ecosystem, and the exposed interior of a single responsive organism. Do not prepare as though it were merely a picturesque ice cave.

The most important part of your plan may be a firm decision about how far you will **not** go.

## Make non-entry the default

For an ordinary human, the best visit is probably a brief observation from a safe threshold, using no more of the interior than necessary. There may be no ethically acceptable place to put a boot. The root mass might bear your weight, but that would not make it a floor: you could crush several layers of leaves, animals, water-filled cups, and tissues that may require seasons or centuries to replace.

If proceeding would require you to:

- walk on the root felt;
- push hanging cords aside;
- pull yourself up by vegetation;
- clear away “obstructing” growth;
- or force either of the raw ends of the chamber,

then stop. You have reached the end of the visit, not an obstacle to overcome.

Do not set reaching the center as a goal. There is no warmth or revelation waiting there. Likewise, do not push toward the growing tip simply because it is the farthest accessible point; it is among the most delicate parts of the system.

Above all, do not treat the Hollow as emergency shelter. A human camp would need heat, light, bedding, food, and waste management, all of which would make it a damaging and probably dangerous place to stay.

## Plan the approach as a serious glacial and caving operation

The Hollow is a space **being made**, not a space that has settled into a durable final shape. Rockfall, crushing, flooding, and closure are not outside its normal processes. The plant vaulting supports vegetation; it is not evidence that anything can support the fractured bedrock and glacier above you.

If your method of multiverse travel permits it, arrive at a safe external staging area, not directly inside the chamber. A body-sized arrival in such a small, crowded volume could damage tissue or displace water and air before you had any chance to assess the situation. Keep any emergency recall available, but do not let it substitute for a safe exit plan.

For the physical approach:

- Use route-appropriate glacial and confined-space expertise, including crevasse or ice-cave rescue preparation where relevant.
- Do not go alone. Keep the entering party extremely small, with a safety partner and a prearranged check-in or surface-support plan.
- Establish a turnaround time before approach. Cold, fatigue, darkness, and curiosity all erode judgment.
- Do not depend on radios working through ice and rock.
- Keep rescue and rewarming equipment at the staging area. A rescue requiring a large party, heavy lights, cutting tools, and ropes dragged through the grove is not an acceptable basis for casual entry.
- Never use roots, cords, or plant-covered stone as anchors.

Assess the air before relying on it. “Almost odorless” says nothing about oxygen concentration, carbon dioxide accumulation, or other gases. Monitor from the threshold where possible. If the air is unsafe, do not solve the problem by ventilating the Hollow or by turning an ordinary visit into a supplied-air expedition. Leave.

The dim ice lens cannot be counted on for illumination; for much of the year it fails. Nor should you assume that a warmer season or better weather automatically makes the cavity more stable.

## Dress for cold, wetness, and sharp new stone

The liquid water is not evidence of warmth. The grove’s control of freezing protects its own living envelope; it will not keep your fingers warm, dry your clothing, or make a basin safe to fall into.

Use conventional cold-weather discipline:

- Wear moisture-managing base layers and insulation that retains useful warmth when damp. Avoid relying on cotton or on down that could wet out and shed feathers.
- Adjust clothing during the approach so that you do not arrive sweaty.
- Carry dry spare gloves and keep vulnerable skin covered. A surface can be wet, below freezing, and capable of sticking to skin at the same time.
- Wear appropriate head and eye protection for the approach, and protect your hands against the sharp conchoidal rock. Gloves are there to prevent injury, not to make touching the ecosystem acceptable.
- Use traction equipment on the approach as required, but do not wear crampons or swing an ice tool inside the Hollow.
- Carry two independent lights, with cold-rated power and protected spares. Use only the lowest illumination compatible with safe movement and exit.
- Secure every small item. Nothing should be able to fall from a pocket into a basin or disappear into the root felt.

Avoid flash photography, ultraviolet lamps, lasers, and unnecessary full-spectrum floodlighting. Light is scarce and can be shared through linked tissues; a bright lamp may not remain merely a local convenience. At the same time, do not compromise your ability to escape in an emergency for the sake of a darker photograph.

No open flame, stove, environmental heater, or hot drink belongs inside. Fire would add heat, soot, combustion gases, and oxygen consumption to a place that has none of the margins a human campsite requires.

## Assume that you are a contamination hazard

You are not merely a pair of eyes. You are large, warm, acoustically conspicuous, and constantly shedding hair, skin, salts, fibers, moisture, and microorganisms.

In this chamber, contamination may have unusual consequences. Foreign particles could provide sites for ice nucleation. A boot print could combine mechanical crushing with new chemistry and biology. The fact that the water remains liquid while you watch would not prove that your visit had no later effect.

Before approaching:

- Clean and dry equipment thoroughly, using methods that leave no chemical residue.
- Avoid bringing soil, organic debris, or damp equipment from another world.
- If crossing the threshold is justified, use a clean, non-shedding outer layer where practicable.
- Cover open wounds and avoid entering with an injury that could bleed.
- Do not wash gear in local meltwater or apply disinfectants near the grove.

Inside, do not eat, drink, spit, urinate, or leave used tissues, batteries, wrappers, or other waste. Do not rinse your hands in a basin. Do not drink the water merely because it is clear and ancient; its chemistry and biological compatibility with humans are unknown.

Keep bare skin out of all water. Some droplets may be poised close to freezing, and water carried beyond the roots’ influence can freeze abruptly. Do not test this by touching, collecting, or moving a droplet. The snap-freezing boundary is not a demonstration arranged for visitors.

Take nothing away: no water, sap, ice, mineral dust, leaves, cords, eggs, animals, or “already dead” tissue. Dead material is often simply material awaiting reuse. Afterward, clean and, where appropriate, isolate your equipment before carrying it to another world. Biosecurity applies in both directions.

## Be quiet, and do not experiment on the grove

Sound is not background decoration here. It is part of how the organism measures fractures, water, and open space. Your voice and equipment may reach portions of the Hollow that you never see.

Speak only when necessary. Do not shout to hear the acoustics, knock on the stone, use active sonar, fly a drone, or play recordings of the plant tones. Do not hum along or attempt to answer a call. An ordered tone is not necessarily an invitation, and a response hours later is not something a brief human visitor can responsibly interpret.

Likewise, do not investigate the grove’s coherence by introducing heat, changing the light, wounding a leaf, or watching how distant tissues respond. There is no engine, control panel, or buried mechanism to uncover. Do not excavate in search of one.

Other specific restraint is important:

- Do not use vegetation as a handhold.
- Do not touch moths packed along the cords or brush grazers aside.
- Do not put a hand into a leaf cup to inspect its occupant.
- Do not feed an eel, rescue a larva from a hunter, or otherwise “improve” an interaction you do not understand.
- Do not deliberately crush a leaf to experience the scent. The scent is associated with damage; it is not an invitation to create more.
- Do not leave markers, offerings, sensors, or recording devices behind.
- If you accidentally damage something, do not attempt to tie it back together, wash it, or prune it. Withdraw if appropriate and record what happened later. Further handling may enlarge the injury.

The question of whether the grove thinks does not need to be settled before deciding how to behave. Memory and control are sufficient reasons to treat it as a responsive whole rather than a collection of disposable specimens.

## Leave at the first sign that the visit is becoming unsafe

Immediate reasons to withdraw include new rock or ice fragments, visible movement in the ceiling or ice lens, a sharp fracture followed by changed seepage, sudden flooding, unexpected freezing around your equipment, or any need to damage vegetation in order to retreat.

Also leave for ordinary human reasons: loss of dexterity, numbness, persistent shivering, dizziness, headache, unusual breathlessness, an air-monitor alarm, or clothing becoming dangerously wet.

Do not interpret the glacier’s ordinary groaning as either a promise of collapse or a guarantee of stability. Conversely, do not treat animal silence after a major fracture as an all-clear. The plant’s changing tones are not a human-readable alarm system; do not remain inside trying to decode them.

Your route should be short and simple enough that you never have to choose between your life and a complicated retreat across the living floor.

## Prepare yourself for what the visit will actually feel like

You will probably see less than you expect and hear more than you are comfortable with.

The Hollow will not perform its slow processes for you. Leaves will not visibly unroll, basins will not deepen, and a predator may remain in exactly the same position throughout your stay. Much of the apparent emptiness is really motion below your threshold of attention. You may see only black wet leaves, dim silver edges, mist, and water—and that can still be a successful visit.

The sensory contradictions may be unsettling: breath glittering in air filled with liquid drips; rime and unfrozen seepage within a pace of one another; a floor that looks solid but contains black water and flexing life; no dependable shadows, yet countless small reflections. The chamber’s noises may seem to come from inside your own body. Drops, bladders, insects, grazers, pressure pulses, and glacial bass do not form a peaceful natural hush. They form a crowded, resonant occupation.

You may feel watched. More cautiously, you are a new physical, chemical, and acoustic event in a system that responds to wounds and fractures. That does not establish intention, and it does not give you a basis for claiming communication.

A good human visit would therefore be almost disappointingly modest: arrive clean, assess the approach and air, remain at the edge if the interior cannot be entered without damage, use little light, make little sound, observe without handling, and leave before cold or curiosity makes you careless. Make your notes afterward.

Do not try to match the Hollow’s patience by staying longer. You cannot. The achievement is not to reach its center or force it to reveal a purpose. It is to perceive, briefly, a place still learning how to become a place—and to leave it with no new human wound to remember, dismantle, or reabsorb.
