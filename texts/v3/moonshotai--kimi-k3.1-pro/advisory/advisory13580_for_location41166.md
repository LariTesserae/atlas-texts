# Preparing for a Visit to Senn

Senn should be approached as two expeditions at once: an alpine journey to a cold, exposed cirque, and a carefully supported visit beneath a bright lake surface. It should also be approached as a social call. This is not a drowned ruin, and its apparent emptiness is not abandonment.

For an ordinary human, the central fact is simple: **there is no air anywhere in town**. Not beneath a roof, not in the common hall, not in some convenient upturned vessel. Sunlight and clear water make Senn look unusually forgiving for an underwater settlement, but they do nothing to reduce the risks of drowning, cold, entanglement, or an ill-judged ascent.

You do not need to prepare for a lightless abyss. You do need to prepare for cold water, altitude, and your own complete dependence on equipment and surface support.

## 1. Arrange the visit before arranging the dive

Secure permission through a local liaison or someone who already has a relationship with Senn. A settlement of twenty-odd buildings can be overwhelmed by a small, clumsy visiting party. Keep the group small, and agree in advance on where you may go, what may be photographed, and whether your breathing apparatus, exhaust bubbles, lights, or other equipment will cause disturbance.

A local guide is valuable for navigation and etiquette, but should not be your only safety support. A guide who can cross Senn in a few unhurried minutes may not share your limits, recognize your early signs of cold stress, or know how much breathing gas you have left. Bring a properly trained human buddy and surface support as well.

Before choosing equipment, obtain actual information about:

- The altitude and relevant depths, not merely the fact that the shelf is “shallow.”
- Water temperature and expected exposure time.
- The safest entry and exit points.
- Seasonal snowmelt, spawning closures, and communal work.
- Conditions around the inlet and the deeper, unbuilt water.
- Whether any part of the town is temporarily closed to outsiders.
- How your party will be recalled if wind or weather deteriorates above the surface.

Do not expect visitor infrastructure. Senn has no dry guest room in which to adjust a mask, no heated refuge, no place to cook, and no human-oriented shop from which to replace a forgotten item. Everything necessary for warming, first aid, food, drinking water, sanitation, and evacuation must exist at the surface or along your route out.

The cirque is old and geologically settled, but the approach is still mountain travel. Acclimatize as appropriate, carry route-appropriate clothing and shelter, and do not let the heavy work of reaching the tarn consume the reserves you will need in the water.

If qualified cold-water and altitude-appropriate support cannot be arranged, the correct decision is not to enter. Senn does not become safe merely because you can see the paving from above.

## 2. Prepare for breathing and cold, not for darkness

Do not attempt to visit by repeated breath-hold dives. Cold, exertion, entanglement, and the deceptive clarity of the water make that a poor gamble, and there is no place in town to recover. Nor should you improvise with a long snorkel, an untested air hose, or the assumption that you can always reach the surface on one breath.

Use a life-support system chosen by people qualified for the measured conditions. Altitude can change diving calculations, and a plan that is routine at sea level may not be routine here. There is no universally ideal arrangement: conventional scuba introduces noise and bubbles, surface-supplied equipment introduces hoses and possible snags, and more sophisticated closed systems introduce their own complexity. Reliability and competent support matter more than elegance.

Your exposure protection should assume dangerously cold water until measurements prove otherwise. Depending on the conditions, that may mean a properly used drysuit or other serious cold-water protection, with insulation, hood, gloves, and suitable footwear. Test the complete arrangement elsewhere. Cold hands make simple buckles, clips, and knots surprisingly difficult; do not enter with equipment whose operation depends on fine fingerwork you will lose after a few minutes.

Other useful principles:

- **Streamline everything.** Gauges, hoses, cameras, and spare equipment should not dangle into roots or masonry.
- **Secure essential small items**, but keep tethers short and tidy. Senn’s inhabitants tether tools for good reason; a dropped object can vanish into the living mat.
- **Carry only emergency cutting equipment you know how to use.** Its purpose is to free you from your own gear, not to clear a path, harvest loomroot, or pry at a wall.
- **Practice buoyancy before arrival.** Good buoyancy is not merely a diving skill here; it is basic good manners.
- **Do not weigh yourself down so that you can walk the lanes.** The bottom is neither an inert floor nor an appropriate place to stand.
- **Keep photography subordinate to safety.** A small, secured camera is optional. Powerful lamps are usually unnecessary in ordinary daylight, and flash or prolonged illumination should not be used to disturb animals for a better image.

At the surface, have dry clothing, shelter, a safe rewarming plan, food and treated drinking water, and a medical and evacuation plan suitable for a remote cirque. The clear lake water should not be assumed potable. Plan washing and human waste disposal well away from the basin.

If your multiverse equipment includes water breathing or environmental adaptation, treat it as only one layer of preparation. Test it under controlled conditions, know its duration and failure mode, and retain an independent route to safety. Water breathing alone does not prevent hypothermia, entanglement, disorientation, or equipment problems. A return portal is not a rescue plan if you are snagged, unconscious, or unable to reach it.

## 3. Enter as though the whole bottom were alive and load-bearing

Use the agreed entry point. Do not anchor over the town, drop equipment onto the terraces, or throw stones in to test the depth. Once below the surface, pause in open water to check breathing, buoyancy, communications, and your companion before proceeding.

Move slowly above the substrate, with your feet and equipment clear of it. The weak current is misleadingly reassuring. It means that sediment disturbed by a careless fin stroke may hang in the water for a long time, spoiling visibility for you and for everyone working nearby. It also means that a visitor can become complacent about drift, separation, and the gradual approach of cold.

Do not:

- Stand or kneel on the root mat, seed beds, paving, or roofs.
- Pull on ribbon stems, cables of rhizome, or root walls for propulsion.
- Brush growth from a lintel to read its chisel marks.
- Tie equipment to pale cords, antler stakes, screens, or stone weights.
- Enter houses or explore doorways in roofs and floors.
- Swim beneath overhangs or into the common hall merely because the opening looks large.
- Follow the local practice of pulling yourself downward on stairs unless your own plan and training specifically permit it.

Roots are not decorative growth around otherwise independent masonry. They bind gravel, sew joints shut, and hold stones that might otherwise fall. Damage that looks botanical to you may be structural to Senn. A roof may also be shading a room, sheltering eggs, or interrupting the current; it is not a platform.

Keep clear of the inlet unless specifically told otherwise. Its white water, noise, sediment, and changing snowmelt make it a poor place for sightseeing. Likewise, treat the drop into deeper unbuilt water as a firm boundary. Senn has no enclosing wall, so “still seeing buildings nearby” is not an adequate navigation plan.

If a diving bird suddenly pierces the surface, or a school of fish turns around you in a commotion of bubbles, remain calm and avoid an unplanned ascent. The visible animals are not the principal hazard. Your reaction to surprise is more likely to cause trouble.

## 4. Learn the town’s visual grammar

Many things in Senn that look incidental are deliberate.

**Open water is maintained space.** The broad intervals between buildings, work yards, and hall are not vacant lots. They are part of the settlement’s circulation, light, ecology, and communal responsibility. Do not use them as equipment dumps, anchor zones, or places to stage a large group photograph.

**Every cord should be treated as meaningful.** Pale cords and cleared rectangles may be the intentions for rooms that no living planner expects to use. A cord across a lane may also mark spawning fish rather than construction. Do not duck beneath it because no worker is visible.

**Unfinished does not mean unsuccessful.** The newest district is not a failed building project awaiting modern materials. It is construction on a generational timescale. Offering synthetic rope, metal fasteners, or a method to “speed things up” may be neither welcome nor ecologically harmless.

**Untended does not mean neglected.** Some shelters are deliberately left for animals. Do not clean out crevices, turn over leaves to inspect eggs, move snails for a photograph, or charitably clear debris from a work yard. Maintenance is allocated carefully: one household may have the right to prune a wall while the lane beside it remains everyone’s responsibility.

**Objects may be tools even when they resemble natural debris.** A rounded stone, a weighted length of fiber, a lidded vessel, or a movable weir screen may be part of a working system. Leave them where they are. The same applies to storage recesses and the cold spaces beneath terraces.

The common hall is common to Senn, not automatically open to strangers. Clear water may allow you to see into rooms, but visibility is not permission.

Do not feed the fish, collect crayfish, take a root cutting, or carry away an “unattached” stone. If you wish to help, ask first and expect the answer to be that your best contribution is restraint. Unskilled pruning or cleaning could undo years of work.

## 5. Prepare for sound and communication

Senn may be visually calm and acoustically overwhelming. The inlet’s roar can seem to come through the terraces and your own body rather than from a particular direction. Stems knock, crayfish click, fish rasp at stone, and seasonal char drumming may gather beneath the hall. Your own apparatus may add a great deal of noise.

This has practical consequences:

- Agree on simple visual and, where appropriate, tactile signals before entry.
- Agree on what “cold,” “low on breathing supply,” “stay here,” and “end the visit” look like.
- Do not depend on acoustic direction to find your guide or the exit.
- Do not casually knock stones together. Struck-stone signals carry throughout the settlement and may have public meanings.
- Keep tanks and equipment from striking masonry.
- If a public signal is heard, follow the response agreed with your guide rather than guessing.
- Check in more often than seems necessary. A local guide’s easy pace may be too fast for a visitor in bulky equipment.

People sensitive to continuous low sound or vibration should know that ear protection may not make the experience quiet; much of the sound is conducted through water and body. A short first visit is preferable to discovering this after you are already cold and disoriented.

Light also requires some interpretation. At noon the square may be simply bright, and no diving light is needed. Reds will be subdued and distance gently blue. A passing cloud may change the appearance of the entire town without itself being an emergency. Conversely, calm conditions below do not guarantee that the wind above remains suitable for your exit. Surface support must keep watch and be able to recall you.

## 6. Let the first visit be small

A good first visit need not include every terrace.

Enter in full daylight with a generous margin of time. Pause above the paving and let your eyes and ears adjust. Follow the guide along one permitted lane, observe the square from an appropriate place, and perhaps pass one work area without entering it. Then stop trying to tour.

Watch the green fur on a stone, the movement of a snail, or fish passing through rooms without regarding them as enclosures. Notice that the solid surfaces are crowded with life even when the open water looks deserted. If a tender is working in the distance, give that person room. A worker resetting a stone may be audible across town; there is no need to approach for a closer look.

The temptation will be to manufacture an event: to cross the whole settlement, inspect the deepest-looking window, find the loudest drumming, or wait for something dramatic. Resist it. Senn’s important activities include roots thickening, sediment being guided away, a step rounding, and a planned room slowly becoming usable. Almost none of that can be witnessed on a visitor’s schedule.

Late afternoon may offer the most beautiful view: separate green roofs, dark windows, pale cords, and broad spaces of daylit water. The evening drumming may be worth hearing on a later visit. Do not extend a first immersion merely to reach dusk, especially if doing so complicates rewarming, visibility, or the surface exit.

Leave while you are still comfortable, still curious, and well inside your reserves. “I could have stayed longer” is a good ending. “I only realized how cold I was after my hands stopped working” is not.

## 7. Leave nothing behind except an accurate account

At the surface, rewarm and assess everyone before beginning the journey out. Any subsequent ascent, flight, or other pressure-changing travel should be compatible with the diving plan; an instantaneous route home should not automatically be assumed to solve physiological problems.

Pack out all equipment and waste. Check that no cord fibers, fittings, or other fragments have been shed. If you damaged a root, displaced a weight, or disturbed sediment badly, tell your liaison rather than quietly hoping it was insignificant.

Clean and decontaminate equipment away from the tarn, using procedures that do not discharge chemicals into it. Repeat the process before taking that equipment to another world. Loomroot fragments, larvae, eggs, and other small organisms are not acceptable hitchhikers, and a basin this distinctive should not be exposed to whatever arrived on your gear from the previous world.

Do not publish sensitive access details or interior images without permission. If a gift or payment is appropriate, arrange it through the liaison rather than leaving coins, metal objects, food, or manufactured cord as offerings.

The right preparation is therefore twofold: enough support that your survival never depends on improvising with Senn’s resources, and enough restraint that Senn does not have to recover from your curiosity. Bring more redundancy than luggage and more patience than itinerary.

You will visit for a brief interval in the life of a wall. Let that be enough.
