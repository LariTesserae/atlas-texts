Prepare for the Ninth Sump as **a hot, dark, confined industrial site containing a fragile ecosystem**, not as a cave tour, a rainforest excursion, or an abandoned ruin you can freely explore. The absence of animals and the scarcity of movement can make it seem harmless. It is not necessarily hostile, but its stillness tells you nothing about the air, the stability of the stair, or whether a human can safely remain there for hours.

An ordinary traveler should make only a short, supported visit—and only after independent checks. If you cannot verify breathable air and manageable heat, use remote instruments or do not descend. A respirator and determination are not substitutes for that assessment.

## Before going anywhere near the stair

### Establish that entry is reasonable

The description is not a complete hazards survey. Arrange, at minimum:

- **An inspection of the surface approach and the twenty-six-metre stair.** Check corrosion, damaged treads, hot surfaces, secure anchoring, and whether the exit can be kept clear. The stair is short on paper; it becomes much longer if you are dizzy, carrying an injured companion, or climbing in heat-soaked clothing.
- **A remote atmospheric test at the bottom.** Oxygen and carbon dioxide matter especially, along with carbon monoxide and any volcanic or industrial gases indicated by local knowledge. Continue monitoring during the visit. Do not use the visitor as the test instrument, and do not rely on smell: wet iron, ozone, and vegetation may simply conceal other odors.
- **A heat assessment, not merely an air-temperature reading.** Still air and poor sweat evaporation can make conditions much more dangerous than the thermometer suggests. Set a conservative exposure limit and a surface recovery plan before entering.
- **Confirmation that the rift and machinery present no uncontrolled steam, rockfall, electrical, or pressure hazard.** Quiet machinery can start remotely. Quiet rock is not automatically stable rock.

If local specialists impose stricter limits, follow them. Do not improve the ventilation, open a valve, or stop an engine to make your visit more comfortable; any of those actions could alter the groves’ water and chemical supply.

### Do not go alone

Use at least one companion, with a surface contact who knows your plan and expected return time. Ideally, someone remains topside with water, communications, and a genuine cooling and recovery area. Do not assume that emerging onto the black lava plain will itself provide relief.

Agree on:

- a maximum stay;
- regular check-ins;
- a simple turnaround signal;
- what happens if communications fail;
- how rescue will be summoned without expecting casual maintenance workers to appear.

Nothing in the chamber suggests that anyone is routinely coming back.

Keep your lights, communications, and means of returning home independent of the Sump’s electrical supply. The control cabinet is neither a charging point nor a source of useful illumination.

## What to wear and carry

Dress for **heat combined with cuts, hot metal, and occasional slippery patches**, not for sustained rain.

- **Helmet**, suitable for an industrial and sharp-rock environment.
- **A cool-running headlamp with a strong enough beam to scan the floor and walls**, plus a separate handheld light and a small emergency backup. Carry spare batteries. Three amber service lamps are landmarks, not lighting.
- **Sturdy boots with ankle support and protective soles.** Glassy basalt, conchoidal fractures, and angular blocks are unforgiving. Wet-looking and apparently dry mineral films near the groves should both be treated as potentially slick.
- **Lightweight long clothing** that protects against scrapes without becoming a heavy heat trap. Avoid a waterproof caving suit or raincoat unless a separate assessment requires one; the Sump’s rain will not justify the extra heat burden.
- **Gloves appropriate for hot handrails and sharp rock**, and eye protection.
- **A calibrated atmospheric monitor and a timer.** There is no daylight below to tell you how long you have stayed.
- **All your drinking water, plus electrolytes and a reserve.** Calculate this from the measured conditions and planned exposure, not from optimism. The condensate and warm pools are not emergency supplies.
- **Personal medication, a compact first-aid kit, and whatever communications equipment your plan requires.**
- Optionally, **a small camera and notebook**. Keep your pack close to your body so it cannot sweep through hanging root-webs or catch on machinery.

A towel can keep sweat out of your eyes, but wetting your clothing is not a dependable cooling strategy where evaporation is poor. Leave behind insect repellent, camping equipment, open flames, collecting jars, and anything you might be tempted to trade, plant, or discard.

Do not eat in the chamber. Attend to sanitation beforehand, and pack out everything you bring.

## How to move below

At the foot of the stair, stop before exploring. Check the monitor, your companion, your lights, and the route back up. Let the first wave of heat and stale-smelling air register without pressing onward simply because you have already descended.

Then move more slowly than the chamber’s apparent size requires.

The darkness will make the room feel much larger than it is. Scan at foot level before every step, and occasionally scan overhead and to the sides. Do not use the service lamps or the service rail as proof of a safe route. Most importantly, **do not expect the vegetation to gather around light**: it follows cooling circuits and reliable drips. A grove may be waiting in complete darkness beside a pipe, while a living lamp remains bare.

Stay on bare, stable rock or other clearly inert surfaces. Avoid:

- copper-colored cushions and dark root mats;
- clear mud, where your footprints could become the only tracks;
- translucent hanging tissue and root-webs;
- fallen fronds and glassy layers in old beds;
- dry cane lattices beneath failed pipes.

The dead-looking growth is still part of the site’s record. Each grove may effectively be one slow organism, and a single careless step could damage growth that took years to extend. Do not pour water on folded plants, open a frond by hand, shine a lamp on it to “feed” it, or add nutrients. It does not photosynthesize, and your well-meant water may carry incompatible chemistry or organisms.

Treat every pipe, cable, insulator, and engine as energized or otherwise hazardous. Do not lean on machinery, sit on the plinths, open the cabinet, scrape rust from maintenance numbers, or disturb the cracked lagging. The old stool, the root-bound spanner, and the incomplete warning sign should remain exactly where they are.

If a slight knock passes through your boots and a pump begins running, that may simply be the cycle described. Stand clear and observe; do not investigate with your hands. Unexpected steam, violent vibration, rock movement, rapidly rising heat, or an atmospheric alarm is different: leave.

Do not use the pools or seepage to cool your hands or face. They may be hot, chemically unsuitable, and ecologically important. The water is condensate only in origin; by the time it reaches iron, scale, rock, and growth, it is not drinking water.

## Plan around the Sump’s clock, not yours

The hardest preparation is accepting that **the place will not perform on a visitor’s schedule**.

A pump may run for several minutes, followed by a long interval in which the pipes merely cool. Condensation appears first as a silver bloom; beads gather; hours may pass before drops fall. A wet interval can last half a day. Fronds may then take days to open.

Consequently:

- Do not make your first visit a vigil for the rain.
- Do not extend a safe exposure limit because a pipe has just begun to cool.
- If conditions permit only a brief stay, accept that you may see nothing more dramatic than dark machinery, folded fans, and a wet seam.
- For longer processes, use repeated short visits or properly assessed remote observation rather than trying to endure an entire cycle underground.

If you arrive during a dry interval, the groves may look diminished without being dead. If an engine remains silent, it may simply be between cycles. And if your whole visit contains only one drop striking iron, that is a complete observation of the place, not a failed expedition.

Set a timer and keep to it. There is no dawn, dusk, or changing animal activity to remind you that you have stayed too long.

## Heat and emergency discipline

Hydration helps, but it does not make excessive heat safe. Fitness does not make you immune, either.

Headache, dizziness, nausea, cramps, unusual exhaustion, or irritability should end the visit. Do not sit beside an engine and hope to recover so you can continue. Confusion, stumbling, fainting, or collapse requires emergency evacuation and active cooling with the resources arranged topside.

Also leave for:

- any atmospheric alarm or unexplained change in breathing comfort;
- failure of communications required by your plan;
- a primary lighting failure that leaves you relying on reserves;
- uncertainty about the exit route;
- behavior by the rift or machinery outside the assessed conditions.

Do not wait until climbing the stair already feels difficult.

## Multiversal contamination precautions

The lack of animals does not mean the chamber is sterile, and human compatibility with its tissues, water, or microscopic life should not be assumed. Keep wounds covered, avoid touching your face, and wash or decontaminate your hands after leaving.

Contamination in the other direction may be even more important. You could introduce microbes or spores into small, isolated groves with no evident capacity for rapid recovery.

Use clean, preferably dedicated equipment. Do not bring soil, plants, food crumbs, pets, or untreated gear from another ecosystem. On return, do not shake basalt dust, root fragments, or dried mud out of your boots in your home world. Clean and contain equipment according to the applicable cross-world protocol, capturing removed material and wastewater rather than releasing them.

Take photographs, measurements, and memories. Do not take a “dead” cane, a cup of condensate, or a fragment of mineralized frond without explicit authorization and proper containment.

## What a good visit is like

A successful visit may be very quiet. Your lamp finds a plane of glassy basalt, then the rim of a pipe. Somewhere beyond it, a grove appears in layers of rust, soot, and old-bone yellow. Your light passes faintly through a sheet of hanging tissue. The air does not move. There are no insects at the lamps and no eyes watching from the dark; the silence is absence, not concealment.

You hear your boots, your breathing, perhaps a pipe contracting. Much later, perhaps, a drop strikes water.

Then you leave before the heat makes the decision for you, carrying out everything you brought and leaving the next drop undisturbed. That restraint is not merely good manners. It is how a brief human visit avoids becoming the largest event in the Ninth Sump’s very slow history.
