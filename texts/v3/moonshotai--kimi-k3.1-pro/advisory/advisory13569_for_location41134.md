# Preparing for the Dry Choir

Do not prepare for this as a hike, a cave survey, or the exploration of a ruin. Prepare for it as a brief, reversible insertion into a sealed and irreplaceable biological structure.

The Dry Choir will not accommodate an ordinary human visitor. It has no entrance, no breathable-atmosphere certificate, no quiet season, and no internal means of rescue. Almost everything that matters must therefore be arranged before arrival.

## First, solve access and return

At ordinary human scale, you cannot enter at all. The entire landscape is smaller than a plum stone and lies beneath an unmarked surface. A shrinking method that changes only your height is not sufficient: your transit must also account for mass, physiology, equipment, life support, and extraction at the visiting scale.

If your method cannot place you inside **without cutting, drilling, or otherwise opening the enclosing tissue**, do not make a bodily visit. Use a remote or otherwise non-invasive form of observation. The surrounding tissue is not packaging. It is part of the organism whose growth and maintained stress make the hollow what it is. Even a tiny incision might alter more than the outer surface.

A physical visit should require all of the following:

- Verified coordinates, since nothing outside marks the destination.
- A surveyed interior map at the exact scale of your visit.
- An external attendant or system retaining control of retrieval.
- Automatic return after a fixed interval, and automatic return if you become incapacitated.
- A return mechanism that does not depend on your voice, your hearing, a radio signal, or your ability to reach the original arrival point.
- A way to bring back everything you took in, including instruments and suit exhaust. No disposable probes, marker beacons, or “temporary” anchors.

There is no self-rescue plan based on finding another way out. Following the floor downward will not lead to a drain. Following the light will not lead to its source. Following the sound will not lead to a machine or opening. There is no door that might eventually appear.

## Treat the sound as terrain, not weather

The roar is the principal hazard and the principal navigation problem. It will not rise, fall, pause, or provide a rhythm through which you can dash. Earplugs and earmuffs may be useful supplements, but they are not a plan: parts of the basin impose pressure on the body itself, not merely sound on the ears.

Before entry, the loud and quiet regions should be mapped as **three-dimensional volumes**, not just as lines on the floor. A quiet seam may be extremely narrow, and a route safe for a probe may not be safe for a chest, helmet, or backpack. Your whole suited body must remain within tolerable conditions. A safe place for your feet is not necessarily a safe place for your head.

Do not test a boundary with a finger, and do not walk toward the roar to see how much you can endure.

The surface history can help corroborate a map. Smoky amber, rounded, burnished material is associated with the strongest resonance; matte surfaces with a fine crystalline nap occur along quieter seams. These are clues, not sufficient proof. Likewise, the nine plants trace portions of the quiet pattern, but nine locations do not constitute a path—and the plants themselves must never be used as stepping stones or approach markers.

Assume that voice communication will be useless. Use visual displays inside the suit, predetermined abort signals, and automatic retrieval. Do not rely on hearing a companion shout, a timer beep, or a warning alarm.

A suitable remote body with strictly limited sensory feedback is preferable to exposing an unprotected human nervous system. If no adequate pressure isolation or surveyed refuge is available, do not go bodily.

## Dress for brilliance, dryness, and confinement

The description establishes that the air is cool, still, and dry. It does **not** establish that a human can breathe it. Use closed-cycle life support unless independent examination has proved otherwise. Do not open a helmet to sample the wax-and-husk odor.

The life-support system should humidify air **for your lungs without releasing moisture into the hollow**. Drinking water, waste, exhaled humidity, and suit exhaust all need containment. The light provides no warmth, so use gentle thermal regulation rather than expecting the brilliance to heat you. There is no reason to dress for severe cold, but an unprotected human should not assume that “cool” will remain comfortable during exertion or stress.

The visor needs glare control and good depth assistance. The difficulties are unusual:

- There is no darkness in which to rest your eyes.
- Shadows do not provide ordinary spatial cues.
- Transparent and translucent laminae may make solid material look like open distance.
- The floor is visible only in fragments.
- White edges, straw-gold thicknesses, and blue narrow joins can be visually beautiful while giving poor information about clearance.

A separate flashlight is unlikely to help and may only add glare. Use passive observation and ranging methods that do not depend on shadows.

Keep equipment compact and integrated. The basin may be a plain in relief, but it is not an open walking field. Ribs, laminae, close ceilings, and nested surfaces make clearance more important than climbing ability. The danger is not principally falling from a cliff; it is becoming disoriented, brushing material aside, or discovering that your suit no longer fits the surveyed safe space.

## Bring your own time

Nothing in the Dry Choir will tell you how long you have been there. The illumination does not change, the air does not freshen or cool, the plants do not turn, and the sound has no beats useful for counting duration.

Use at least one visual clock that you can read without manipulating controls, with retrieval governed independently from outside. Keep the first visit short. A longer stay will not produce a weather window, a lull, a dawn, or a new stage in the plants’ growth.

Prepare psychologically as well as physically. The combination is not ordinary silence or ordinary sensory deprivation. It is continuous light, continuous sound, close spatial complexity, and an absence of responsive change. Some travelers may experience panic, disorientation, or the unsettling feeling that their own actions are no longer properly connected to passing time. Practicing with non-directional sound and featureless illumination beforehand is more useful than rehearsing a heroic survival narrative.

Chest discomfort, confusion, nausea, sudden fatigue, loss of positional certainty, or an urge to leave the mapped route “just to look” should all end the visit.

And if the place changes while you are stationary—if the light pulses, moisture appears, something moves, or above all the sound becomes silent—retrieve immediately. The cause may be your hearing, your suit, or your perception rather than the hollow, but that can be investigated afterward. Do not remain to enjoy an impossible improvement in conditions.

## Enter as a contamination-control problem

An ordinary human is wet, warm, biologically active, and constantly shedding material. You would bring moisture, skin cells, microorganisms, lint, heat, and force into a place where no loose dust remains and nothing has been left to decay.

“Leave no trace” is almost too weak. The aim should be **no exchange**.

Do not bring food, open drinks, collecting containers, adhesives, marker flags, climbing anchors, sample hammers, or anything detachable. Do not camp, cook, write on a surface, or set down equipment. Do not take a nodule merely because it resembles a loose grain: it formed in place. Do not peel resin, clear a growth boundary, or brush away what looks like dust. There should be no dust to remove.

Every footfall is on grown tissue, however much it resembles porcelain, bone, wax, or mineral.

The nine plants deserve particular caution. They are not provisions, souvenirs, or a tiny garden awaiting care. Do not water them. Do not touch their wax-sealed leaves, expose their roots, or attempt a cutting. They neither flower nor seed, and their roots are continuous with the architecture. Each one helps maintain the precise conditions around it. Their loss cannot be casually repaired by planting another.

Leave behind the ruin-explorer’s instincts as well. There is no mechanism to restart, no tool marks to interpret, no joint to pry apart, and no control chamber concealed behind the ribs. The Dry Choir is not broken, dormant, or paused. It does not need to be tuned, completed, or awakened.

## What visiting would actually be like

If you arrive successfully in a surveyed quiet region, do not expect peace. “Quiet” there means a bearable vibration, not silence.

The first impression may be that the light is coming through the substance of the world rather than falling onto it. The second may be that the sound has no outside. It will not seem to approach from a direction or remain politely in the distance. Elsewhere in the basin, it would press against the body; even in refuge, it will remain part of your physical situation.

The landscape may feel simultaneously minute and unreasonably extensive. There is no horizon to relieve the eye, but neither is there a broad, simple chamber. The ceiling stays close. The ribs divide and rejoin. Layers of clear material interrupt sight until openness becomes difficult to judge. The shallow floor offers no dramatic summit or depth, only slight steps and fragments visible between grown surfaces.

Nothing will respond to your arrival. Nothing will come to investigate. No plant will turn toward you, because the light already comes from everywhere. No gust, footstep from an animal, drip, or distant voice will interrupt the roar. If you wait for the place to reveal what it is “doing,” you will misunderstand it. It is not performing an action. It is maintaining a finished form.

The best visit is therefore modest: arrive at a known refuge, observe without contact, make a passive record if your equipment permits, and leave on schedule. Do not extend the visit merely because nothing has happened yet.

For an ordinary human, the essential preparations are non-destructive scale access, guaranteed retrieval, whole-body acoustic protection, closed life support, contamination control, and an independent sense of time. Without those, remote observation is not a lesser form of courage; it is the appropriate way to visit.

Bring back a record if you can. Bring back no specimen. Leave no moisture, scar, footprint, marker, or missing growth. The Dry Choir supplies no event to commemorate your arrival, and a successful visit should give it none to endure afterward.
