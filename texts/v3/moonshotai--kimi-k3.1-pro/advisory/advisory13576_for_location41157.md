Treat the Unfinished Range as a remote high-altitude expedition across active, route-poor rock—not as a trek through an unusually stark mountain park.

Its central hazard is **discontinuity**. A summit table may not connect conveniently to the next one. A basin may have no walkable outlet. A rill does not necessarily become a stream, a snowfield may not remain where you last saw it, and water can be visible on every cliff while remaining logistically scarce. Prepare for things to stop, shift, freeze, or disappear without replacing themselves with anything more reliable.

Also resist the psychological effect of its machine-like repetition. Cloud, rime, thaw, and refreeze may make the range feel scheduled or managed. Nothing is managing it. There will be no regular operating hours, maintained route, warning system, or sheltered place in which to wait out a mistake.

## Before committing to the journey

**Establish whether an ordinary human can actually breathe there.** Six kilometres of relief above the low country does not, by itself, specify absolute altitude or oxygen availability. Measure atmospheric pressure and oxygen conditions at your intended camps and route elevations. If the air is not independently breathable, acclimatization and mountaineering equipment will not solve the problem; you would need a genuine life-support expedition or should not go.

If conditions are broadly Earth-like and the elevations are high, treat hypoxia as one of the principal dangers. Arriving directly on a high table by multiverse transport would count as extremely rapid ascent. The ease of getting there would not acclimatize you. You could become dangerously ill without ever having climbed a step.

Use a staged approach, with time to acclimatize and a known, practical line of descent. Go with companions and monitor one another. Headache, nausea, abnormal exhaustion, clumsiness, confusion, or breathlessness out of proportion to exertion should not be dismissed as ordinary cold or fatigue. Severe altitude illness requires urgent descent and appropriate medical treatment; supplemental oxygen or medication, if carried by people trained to use them, is not permission to continue upward.

**Make your return arrangements part of your life-support plan.** Determine whether your exit depends on weather, visibility, reaching a particular landmark, or local infrastructure. Leave enough food, fuel, shelter capacity, and physical reserve to survive being delayed. Do not assume that a satellite communicator, positioning system, radio network, or rescue service will function merely because it works at home.

An inexperienced traveler should not attempt the high interior alone, and a equipment list cannot substitute for competence. At least some members of the party need real skill in high-altitude travel, technical route assessment, cold-weather camping, and navigation in complete loss of visibility. Everyone should know how to stop safely and survive an unplanned night.

For a first visit, choose an observation trip, not a traverse. A lower high shelf and one carefully scouted shoulder are quite enough.

## Dress for being wet without rain

Cloud will condense directly on you and your equipment. Rime will accumulate on exposed edges. During thaw, water will appear on rock and then freeze again after dark. You may therefore become thoroughly wet in weather that never produces recognizable rain.

Bring:

- A complete non-cotton layering system, with severe-cold insulation and a genuinely windproof, waterproof outer layer.
- Insulated, stiff footwear compatible with whatever traction equipment the route requires.
- Several glove and mitten options, including a dry reserve; handling cold rope, rock, and stove fittings with wet hands can end a trip quickly.
- Face and head protection, goggles for blowing snow and flat light, and dark eye protection for the occasional colourless brilliance above the cloud.
- A sealed, utterly dry set of sleep clothing and reserve insulation. Assume that nothing will dry overnight merely because you hang it up.
- Personal emergency shelter and enough insulation to remain stationary while injured, exhausted, or waiting for visibility.

The cold may often feel less like a dramatic storm than like continuous heat loss: damp fabric, pressure of wind, frozen fastenings, numb fingers, and a gradual reluctance to perform small tasks. Build your routines around that. Eat and drink before you feel inclined to, and stop to adjust clothing before a minor problem becomes an emergency.

Use sun protection as well. Altitude, ice, and thin bright overcast can damage eyes and skin even when there is almost no sensation of warmth.

## Carry a camp, not just a tent

There is no firewood, no dependable natural shelter, and no infrastructure. There may also be surprisingly few loose stones with which to anchor a shelter or construct a windbreak.

Your shelter should be suitable for severe exposed conditions, but “freestanding” must not be confused with “safe without anchors.” Bring purpose-made, removable anchoring equipment appropriate to the actual rock and snow, and know how to use it. Carry a sleeping bag and insulation mat system rated for temperatures well below those you hope to encounter, plus repair materials for poles, fabric, inflatable mats, and stove fittings.

Choose camps with unusual care:

- Do not camp beneath rime-loaded edges, hanging ice, overhangs, or fresh cliff faces merely because there is no old debris below them.
- Avoid fracture mouths and basin ends whose floors disappear into broken ground.
- Do not camp in a dry-looking depression fed by mineral seams or seep lines; the brief thaw may turn it into a chain of pools.
- Do not assume a flat snow surface is preferable to bare rock without assessing what it covers.
- Keep well away from vegetated seams and wet microbial films.

Cook with adequate ventilation and carbon-monoxide precautions. Carry all food and fuel, including a substantial reserve for days spent waiting. There is no realistic way to live off the scattered vegetation or brief animal opportunities, and attempting to do so would damage precisely the places where life has succeeded.

## Treat water as both everywhere and unreliable

The range is wet, but you should plan much of a journey as though confirmed drinking water were scarce.

A pool may be sealed by evening. A seep may shift between visits. Rime and old snow require fuel to melt and may yield disappointingly little water. Following a wet mineral seam may lead only to a fracture.

Carry sufficient containers for the longest plausible interval between confirmed sources, insulated storage to prevent your supply freezing, and enough fuel for melting ice or snow where necessary. Have more than one method of making water safe, remembering that filters can be destroyed or disabled by freezing and that batteries perform poorly in the cold. Perfectly clear water is not necessarily harmless, particularly where animals and concentrated microbial life use the same tiny pool.

Do not make an essential camp or daily schedule dependent on a snow patch marked during reconnaissance. Its boundaries may change before you return.

Most importantly, **do not use drainage as a route-finding principle**. Following water downhill will not reliably lead to a stream, valley floor, or low country. It may simply lead you to the place where the water vanishes.

## Move as though the landscape has not agreed on a route

The lack of rubble is not proof that the cliffs are stable. It indicates that little debris has accumulated; it says nothing reassuring about the next fracture, thaw, or strain release. Fresh, granular-looking rock may be solid, poorly attached, wet, or glazed with ice too thin to see from a distance.

Use helmets and whatever roped, climbing, snow, or ice equipment qualified route assessment indicates. Do not let possession of that equipment persuade you to attempt a connection that the terrain does not offer. Shallow or provisional ice is not automatically safer than a mature glacier, and a hanging white field should not be treated as an established travel surface. Thin snow can conceal joints without providing a meaningful bridge over them.

Other rules follow directly from the land:

- **Do not estimate travel time by apparent distance.** With no trees, buildings, or repeated forms, a cliff that looks close may require a day of complicated travel. Measure progress by time, elevation, and verified position.
- **Do not assume a basin has a continuous floor or a walkable lower end.**
- **Do not treat faults, joints, or bedding planes as paths merely because they are straight.**
- **Be wary below cliffs during thaw.** Loosening rime, falling ice, wet rock, and changing seepage can make a previously quiet face hazardous.
- **Do not interpret quiet as stability.** A deep report across a basin may be ice or bedrock taking a new strain. It deserves assessment, not curiosity-driven investigation.
- **Have a real descent route.** “Just go down” can lead into cloud, cliff bands, or fractured ground. Even when descent is medically necessary, it needs to be along the safest known line.

Carry at least two independent navigation methods and test whether they work locally. Satellite positioning may be unavailable; an altimeter will drift with changing pressure; a compass and electronic instruments should not be trusted blindly. Maintain a written route log with times, elevations, bearings where useful, and decision points. In flat light or cloud, a recorded track is only an aid—it cannot prove that the surface along it remains safe.

When visibility closes, stop while you are still on ground you understand. Do not continue toward the next supposed landmark simply because stopping feels unproductive. Avoid leaving cairns, paint, fixed ropes, or survey markers; besides altering an entirely unconstructed place, they may give later travelers false confidence.

## Plan around tendencies, not a timetable

When the described daily cycle holds, each part of it offers a different mixture of opportunities and hazards.

Cold conditions may make some surfaces firmer while covering others in transparent ice. Pools may be inaccessible, and flat light can make small steps and surface changes difficult to read.

The brief thaw brings water and visible life, but also wet stone, dripping overhangs, loosening rime, and changes around fractures. It is an excellent time to observe from a safe position, not necessarily to stand beneath the most interesting cliff.

Later cloud may erase whole walls and fill the air with fine snow. Dusk then narrows the seeps and seals the pools again. These are not dependable appointments. Use several days of local observation where possible, and establish firm turnaround conditions before leaving camp: deteriorating visibility, new rock or ice activity, a symptomatic team member, or simply falling behind the safe schedule.

Carry binoculars and a notebook with a pencil. They may be more valuable than additional ambitions. A record of wind, cloud, thaw, seep activity, and your own travel times will teach you more than a distant view can, though it still will not make a temporary watercourse permanent.

## Be exceptionally careful around life

The apparent sterility is misleading. A dark wet patch may be a living film. A sheltered fracture may contain plants rooted in dust accumulated over a long period. A clear pool may develop an active rim of invertebrates within hours, and several seasons of local abundance may depend on one seep that could shift or disappear on its own.

Do not walk through living pools, scrape films from rock, camp on green seams, collect lichens, or wash directly in seeps. Clean your equipment before arrival so that you do not introduce seeds, spores, or other organisms into isolated habitats. Do not feed birds or other animals, and secure all food and scraps.

There is little soil in which waste can decompose and no reason to expect buried waste to disappear. Pack out rubbish and, where feasible, human waste and hygiene products. Build no fires and leave no permanent camps.

The same caution applies when returning home: a multiverse traveler should not casually carry unfamiliar viable material between worlds on wet boots, clothing, or samples.

## What a good first visit would look like

A sensible first expedition would establish a conservative base after adequate acclimatization, spend time watching how cloud actually moves through one part of the range, and then make a short journey to a shoulder with a clear retreat. It might include one carefully chosen high camp, or none at all. It would not attempt to link several basins, descend by an unexplored drainage, or cross the range simply because a line looks possible on a map.

Leave unallocated days for waiting. If your schedule requires visible progress every day, the range will tempt you into poor decisions.

Expect long intervals in which the view seems almost empty. Then notice the smaller operations: a drop striking ice, snow grains shifting dryly, a rime vane whitening an edge, rock ticking as it cools, a compact flock feeding urgently before cloud returns. You may see one animal across an immense view and overlook thousands of tiny lives on the wet face below. A clear night above the cloud may be extraordinarily beautiful and dangerously cold; brilliance at midday may provide almost no warmth.

The successful visitor is not the person who makes the range behave like a finished mountain system. It is the person who brings enough margin to remain patient, turns back without needing a dramatic reason, and leaves having seen one small part of the work without damaging it. Go prepared to be cold, damp, disoriented by scale, and frequently unable to proceed—and let waiting be part of the visit rather than evidence that the visit has failed.
