# Preparing for the Still Multitude

Treat the Still Multitude as an extreme-altitude, wholly non-provisioned excursion into unreliable geography—not as a trek across an unusually quiet wilderness.

The plateau does not appear hostile. That is part of the difficulty. No storm will drive you back, no predator will complete its attack, and no sunset will warn you that you have stayed too long. The ground is stable, the daylight is constant, and the immense crowds are not going to charge. The principal dangers are more likely to be hypoxia, dehydration, injury, disorientation, and your own curiosity.

You may be the only thing in sight that becomes noticeably more tired.

## 1. Secure your return before planning anything else

**Do not enter unless your method of return is independent of the plateau’s geography.**

A return plan is inadequate if it requires you to:

- retrace your footprints;
- find the cairn, shelter, ridge, or gateway you passed earlier;
- walk toward something kept continuously in sight;
- reach the rim or descend to lower country;
- follow a rope, breadcrumb trail, or series of markers through the herds;
- wait for evening, weather, or some other local change.

None of those things constitutes a reliable route here. A compass may work perfectly and still be useless. Coordinates may describe places without describing how those places connect. Keeping an object in view is not known to preserve the path to it.

Ideally, your crossing method should permit immediate recall from wherever you happen to be, without line of sight to an entry point. If it has a range limit, stay well inside it. Arrange outside support and a firm extraction deadline measured **outside** the plateau. Test the return mechanism at the threshold before committing to a longer visit: cross, take only a few steps, return, and repeat after changing your position and viewing angle.

If that cannot be done, limit yourself to observation from the crossing point. An ordinary human has no business attempting a pan crossing or a journey toward the high roots on the assumption that the way back will somehow remain available.

A first visit should be a brief threshold survey, not a traverse and certainly not a camping trip.

## 2. Assume your body remains entirely human

Do not infer personal immunity from the condition of the inhabitants. The local animals do not breathe, drink, eat, or decay, but nothing about that guarantees you will receive the same exemption.

Plan on the assumption that you will continue to:

- require oxygen;
- lose water in the thin, dry air;
- become hungry and exhausted;
- bleed from a cut;
- develop altitude illness;
- suffer impaired judgment without noticing it.

Do not experiment with breath-holding, fasting, or deliberate injury to discover whether the plateau has changed these facts. If your physiology does begin behaving abnormally, treat that as a reason for immediate medical evacuation, not as an interesting reprieve.

Before going, obtain a medical assessment and acclimatize as far as is practicable. Because the altitude is described only as extreme, do not assume it resembles a merely difficult terrestrial mountain. If the actual conditions require supplemental oxygen or pressure equipment, use equipment designed for those conditions. If consciousness depends on oxygen, provide independent reserves and test the complete system before entry. Supplemental oxygen is not a substitute for an exit.

Carry:

- all the water you could need for the maximum plausible exposure, including a missed-exit reserve;
- electrolytes and compact emergency food;
- oxygen and delivery equipment where required;
- ordinary first aid, including bleeding control and sprain management;
- any personal medication, with reserves;
- two independently functioning elapsed-time devices;
- a written schedule for drinking, oxygen checks, rest, and mandatory departure.

Use timers rather than shadows, hunger, thirst, or perceived progress. At altitude, a pulse oximeter may help reveal a trend, but it should never be used as permission to ignore headache, nausea, breathlessness at rest, clumsiness, confusion, or unusual euphoria.

If altitude illness appears, **extract immediately**. Do not try to walk down through the rim. The descent is immense, the geography is untrustworthy, and apparent lower ground may not be reachable by the route you think you are taking.

If the oxygen, water, and contingency supplies required for safety are too heavy for you to carry, that is not a challenge to overcome with enthusiasm. It means the visit is beyond your means.

## 3. Dress for the ground more than the weather

The stillness removes many familiar mountain hazards. There is no need to plan around approaching storms, windblown ice, fresh rockfall, or darkness. Nevertheless, bring light insulation, sun protection, sunglasses, a hat, and clothing suitable for cool high-altitude exposure.

Your footwear matters more. Use sturdy boots and gaiters suited to glass sand, abrasive flint dust, sharp mineral plates, and polished bedrock. Carry gloves that would protect your hands in a fall, and eye protection compatible with whatever breathing equipment you use.

Move without stirring dust. Do not run.

The white pans require particular restraint. Their crust is not empty, convenient pavement. Beneath it may be densely packed shelled life. Avoid the pans where bare bedrock offers another route; do not stamp on the plates to hear them ring, crack one open out of curiosity, sit on them, or set a heavy pack down without checking the ground.

Do not light fires, build cairns, scratch marks, leave offerings, or discard anything. In a country where nothing weathers or decays, even small litter may become effectively permanent. Carry a means of packing out all personal waste. The fact that your footprints may already exist there does not excuse adding avoidable damage.

## 4. Never confuse observation with navigation

Maps, photographs, and sketches may be valuable records. They should not be treated as proof that two places remain connected.

If a cairn appears ahead after you passed it on the left, do not chase it to determine whether you have walked in a circle. If a shelter moves behind you, do not walk toward it merely because it now looks convenient. If walking away from a herd brings an animal closer, stop using apparent approach and recession as measures of distance.

There is no reliable evidence that staring at a landmark, walking backward, avoiding blinking, or performing some other private ritual will stabilize a route. Do not turn an anomaly into a tool simply because you need one.

If traveling with another person:

- do not split up;
- remain within continuous sight and, where practicable, easy reach;
- agree on stop and extraction signals beforehand;
- use communications tested at the threshold;
- do not rely on shouting, since the air carries sound poorly.

A companion is valuable for noticing hypoxia or panic, but is not a substitute for independent extraction. If separated, do not sprint toward the place where the other person appeared to be.

### If you find your own footprints

Expect that this may happen, and decide your response before it does.

The prints may match your stride, your boot depth, and the drag of one heel. They are not thereby a command, a prophecy, evidence that you are doomed, or proof that you must follow them to discover how you leave.

Do not follow them. Do not step into them, erase them, or alter your stride in an attempt to defeat them. Do not use them as a justification for continuing farther than planned.

For an ordinary traveler, encountering them is a sensible point to end the excursion. Record what you can without disturbing the ground, then use your established return method. They are terrain, not directions.

## 5. Treat every inhabitant as alive, solid, and not in need of your assistance

The multitude is neither a collection of corpses nor a museum of sculptures. A cool body and an unmoving chest do not establish death. The yielding flank and patient eye suggest exactly the opposite: you should be cautious about assuming that anything there is unaware of your touch.

Accordingly:

- do not feed the grazers;
- do not drive predators away from motionless prey;
- do not lower a raised hoof or straighten an interwoven horn;
- do not free a parasite, remove a colony from a giant’s back, or collect the small bodies beneath a pan;
- do not climb on an animal for a better view;
- do not touch a flank merely to verify that it yields;
- do not attempt resuscitation on something that is not breathing.

Above all, resist the urge to **complete** anything.

The browser does not need help finishing its bite. The hunter does not need to be punished or released into its strike. The toolmaker does not need you to bring the hammerstone down. The finished point is not abandoned property, and the obsidian flakes are not a souvenir scatter.

Nothing has been left undone in the sense a visitor might imagine. Mercy, curiosity, and tidiness can all become forms of interference.

The interleaved crowds also require conservative movement. If a sideways step or a lower viewpoint reveals another herd, treat every newly visible body as physically solid. Never step into an apparently vacant interval merely because it was empty from your previous angle. Do not crouch, tilt your head, or use reflections to make additional populations appear in search of a passage. A gap in your view is not necessarily a gap available to your body.

Move slowly, look at the near ground before committing your weight, and avoid abrupt detours through the horns.

## 6. Prepare for the psychological pressure

The silence may initially feel peaceful. Then your own swallowing, breathing, and equipment noises may seem like the only events in the world. Your footsteps will sound flat and close. The mineral hiss will offer no direction and no change by which to measure duration. The fixed daylight may make a short visit feel endless or a long one feel as though it has barely begun.

The crowds can produce a different strain. You may feel watched without being threatened, or compelled to count, classify, and find an edge to something that has no useful edge. Looking more carefully may reveal not clarity but further occupancy.

Some travelers will be tempted to test their reality: to touch something forbidden, follow an impossible landmark, see whether the same cairn can be produced again, or find their own tracks before the tracks find them. Treat a growing compulsion to perform such tests as a turnaround symptom, not as scientific virtue.

Carry a small card with a few unambiguous statements:

> I can still become hypoxic, dehydrated, injured, and confused.  
> The inhabitants are alive; nothing needs me to complete or rescue it.  
> Landmarks and footprints are not routes.  
> Newly visible animals are solid obstacles.  
> I leave at the scheduled time, not when I feel finished.

If an eye acknowledges you, no response is required. Being noticed is not a summons.

Also decide in advance that **change is a warning**. Wind, breathing, a lengthening shadow, falling dust, a completed gesture, or a rise and fall in the hiss would not be reassuring signs of ordinary life. They would mean that the conditions on which your entire risk assessment was based no longer hold. Leave.

## 7. Use conservative turnaround rules

End the visit immediately if any of the following occurs:

- symptoms of altitude illness or unusual exhaustion;
- an oxygen, water, timing, communication, or extraction problem;
- separation from your companion;
- repeated spatial disorientation;
- panic, euphoria, derealization, or an urge to test the anomalies;
- injury, however minor it first appears;
- discovery of your own onward footprints;
- any spontaneous change in the plateau itself.

Do not wait for a minor problem to become dramatic. There may never be a dramatic environmental warning.

If extraction fails, stop wandering. Find the safest available patch of bare, stable stone without breaking crust or pushing through bodies. Conserve oxygen and water, treat injuries normally, and use your prearranged external emergency procedure. Do not follow a familiar cairn, walk toward a shelter, descend from the rim, or pursue your own tracks in the hope that motion alone will eventually produce an exit.

This contingency is precisely why a visit without dependable outside recovery is difficult to justify.

## 8. After returning

Account for every person, piece of equipment, and item of waste. Inspect boots and clothing under contained conditions rather than shaking mineral dust or small organisms into another world. Do not bring back a scale-leaf, stone flake, shelled body, or “empty” fragment of pan as an experiment.

Seek medical attention for persistent headache, breathing difficulty, confusion, or other altitude symptoms; leaving the high country does not guarantee immediate recovery.

Compare notes with any companion without insisting that your routes must reconcile. Different memories of arrangement may not mean that one of you is lying, careless, or ill. Give yourself time before further travel. Ordinary wind, running water, breathing animals, and moving crowds may briefly seem extraordinarily loud or busy.

A successful visit will therefore appear almost disappointingly unambitious: you arrive supplied, verify that you can return, stand quietly, observe a small part of the multitude without touching it, and leave on schedule. You will not have counted the herds, reached the far ridge, solved the toolmaker, or explained your footprints. Those are not failures.

Your footprints may already be part of the ground. Your return should never be left to the same possibility.
