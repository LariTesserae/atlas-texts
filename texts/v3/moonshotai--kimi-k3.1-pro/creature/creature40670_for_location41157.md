No one lives in the Unfinished Range in the settled sense. Yet it is not merely visited by life. It has residents, though residency may mean a patch of stone remaining stable for a century, a fracture deep enough not to freeze solid, or a body capable of spending most of the year as a pause.

Nothing lives at the scale of the whole range. Even the widest travellers depend on things almost too small to see: a seam that stays wet past noon, a fall of insects onto snow, a patch of shelf grass exposed one day earlier than the rest. Most of the inhabitants did not originate here. Their ancestors came from older mountains, cold steppes, temporary ponds, and the air itself. They arrived already possessing the abilities the range requires—dormancy, flight, sure feet, tolerance of hunger, rapid reproduction when conditions briefly allow it. What succeeds is not permanence but the ability to resume.

The most enduring residents live in or immediately upon the stone.

Within pale, translucent mineral seams, sparse endolithic communities occupy the tiny spaces between crystals. Light reaches them weakened and colourless; moisture arrives as condensation held in pores rather than as flowing water. They may remain inactive through long cold periods, then complete a little growth during a favourable season. Their lives are so slow that the opening of a nearby joint can be, for them, a greater event than the passage of several years.

On exposed surfaces, the rust-coloured and charcoal lichens record a different kind of history. Their distribution is a map of stillness. They thicken on old summit tables, spread across blocks that have not moved, and stop abruptly at fresh scarps. A large lichen crust may be older than every pool in the basin below it. A cliff can be immense and ancient in human terms and still have almost none, simply because its face keeps renewing itself.

Where water returns often enough, microbial films spread over the lichens’ barer neighbours. Wet, they are slippery and nearly black, with green or brown visible only at close range. Dry, they can be mistaken for mineral staining. Their mucilage catches dust, spores, and fragments of windborne insects. This is not soil, but it is food, moisture storage, and a place for other organisms to attach. Much of the range’s visible abundance begins as this darker wetness on stone.

The cushion plants make a little more of their opportunity. Their living shoots are packed so tightly that a hand resting on one would touch hundreds of growing tips. Dead leaves remain among them instead of falling away, trapping mineral grains and slowing the loss of water. Roots bind the accumulated material into the fracture. Each cushion thus produces a small, private climate and the nearest thing to soil, then continues living within what it has made.

Their flowers are brief and inconspicuous. Some plants are self-fertile; others rely on small flies during the rare calm afternoons when insects can fly without being carried away. Many spread as much by slow division as by seed. When a seep shifts, a cushion cannot follow. It browns unevenly, sometimes retaining a green centre for another season. Seeds, fragments, and the bodies of tiny animals remain in its drying core. Elsewhere, a seed lodged in mud on a bird’s foot or a fragment caught in an ibex’s coat may begin the process again.

Lower down, the shelf grasses and waxy-leaved rosettes are equally economical. They put their growth into protected buds, roots, and short stems rather than exposed height. A sheltered pocket may support several generations of them while the open rock two paces away remains sterile. Their leaves provide food, but their greater importance is architectural: they hold snow a little longer, catch dust, shelter eggs, and give small animals something to crawl beneath.

A seep, when it remains active, gathers these scattered lives into a brief concentration.

Threadlike larvae graze the films in pools only a few centimetres deep. Their development is not steady growth so much as a series of advances separated by cold. Some can withstand partial freezing; others persist as eggs in protected sediment when the pool vanishes. At the margins, blue-black springtails gather on wet grains and along the water’s skin, feeding on microbial growth and organic matter delivered by the wind. Minute mites move through damp plant cores. Under adjacent flakes, flattened spiders wait beside a few strands of silk. They do not waste effort on broad webs in the open air. A springtail touching a line, a midge blundering beneath the stone, or nothing happening for several days: each is a workable event in a spider’s life.

Nothing that requires permanent water can belong to such a pool for long. Its abundance depends less on depth than on timing. A shallow basin that melts for several afternoons may produce more life than a deeper one sealed beneath ice. On a favourable day, adults emerge, mate in the lee of stone, and lay eggs before the wind rises. Birds may consume most of them. The remainder are enough.

Much of the food supply does not grow in the range at all. It arrives exhausted or dead: midges and other insects carried upward from lower country, winged seeds, fragments of leaves, organic dust. Wet rock catches some. Snowfields collect the rest, sometimes preserving a hatch until thaw exposes it. Springtails gather along these deposits, and birds find the springtails.

The small ash-brown finches travel in compact flocks because food is patchy and information is valuable. When one bird finds a productive seam, the others converge. They take larvae from pool margins, insects from snow, and seeds from sheltered plants, feeding with an urgency that makes them seem almost careless. A flock can descend on a seep, strip it of exposed prey, and leave within minutes. Then the stone looks untouched again.

Their nests lie deep in joints or on protected ledges, cups of dry vegetation lined with ibex hair and fine plant fibres. Nesting is a wager between shelter and supply. A ledge may remain safe for years while the food needed to raise young there appears for only a few irregular days. The finches respond by moving readily, abandoning attempts when necessary, and following thaws upslope rather than treating any one basin as indispensable.

The dark choughs use a larger territory and a more varied diet. They probe fractures for spiders, pick insects from melting rime, inspect carcasses, and work the air along cliffs where weaker fliers cannot manoeuvre. Their sociability is opportunistic. A dozen may appear around one rich shelf; the next day the same air is empty.

Behind these concentrations comes the falcon. It does not maintain a reliable daily hunting ground. It follows the conditions that make birds gather, crossing open air from cloud cover or from behind a summit table. Its presence is often known only when a finch flock rises as one body. A strike may feed more than the falcon: feathers and remains on a ledge attract choughs, ravens, foxes, and eventually the small decomposers of the next wet period.

Within the deeper fracture systems live short-eared, pika-like herbivores. Where other mountains offer talus, these animals use joints in bedrock, moving through passages that narrow and widen without pattern. Their calls are thin click-whistles, easily lost among the ticking of cooling stone. They emerge to crop grass and rosettes, carrying small bundles back to dry chambers. Their caches are modest because there is little surplus to gather. In a poor season, an entire fracture system can fall silent. In a good one, droppings, discarded food, and nesting material make its entrances noticeably richer than the surrounding rock.

The ibex operate at a still larger scale. Bands divide and re-form around snow cover, exposed forage, and the condition of the shelves between feeding grounds. Their knowledge of the country resembles a set of alternatives rather than a fixed route. A shelf that supplied grass last year may be unreachable this year; a new fracture may expose minerals they lick, or divert water toward plants that were previously dry. They carry seeds in their coats and dung, trample organic matter into protected pockets, and leave hair on rough stone. Their carcasses become major events. Frozen, visited repeatedly, and gradually incorporated into meltwater, a single body can enrich a small drainage for years.

The fox connects several of these worlds. It hunts the fracture herbivores, takes weakened birds, scavenges ibex, and at times feeds on insects concentrated along thawing snow. Its apparent aimlessness is often a sequence of remembered possibilities. A line of prints crossing an empty snowfield may join two widely separated fracture systems, with nothing edible between them. If the first has gone quiet, the fox simply continues.

Ravens and the broad-winged scavengers know the range through deaths, nests, and weather. Ravens can investigate small events on foot as well as from the air. The larger soaring birds depend more heavily on usable lift and may be absent whenever cloud and weak wind make inspection uneconomical. Seasonal migrants also contribute to their circuits. Most pass unseen; some die against cliffs or on snow, adding bodies and nutrients to a system that receives little from its own vegetation.

Each kind of inhabitant therefore keeps a different map. Lichens occupy a map of stable surfaces. Microbial films occupy a map of recurring wetness. Cushion plants occupy shelter. Ibex follow snow release and forage; finches follow hatches and windfall; predators follow the other animals’ maps. Nothing simply follows water downhill, because downhill so often ends in rock. Animals follow water’s appearances.

When a seep shifts, these maps do not move together. The adult insects die or emerge elsewhere. The spider may survive beneath its stone for a time and then disappear. The cushion browns. Birds stop landing. At the new seep, the first film appears, windborne organisms arrive, and birds bring additional cells and propagules on wet feet and feathers. The old community has not migrated. A new one has been assembled from some of the same possibilities.

During the long cold, most of this life becomes difficult to detect. Larvae lie beneath clear ice with almost no visible movement. Seeds and eggs remain in dry plant cores. The endolithic communities wait within stone. Fracture herbivores use their small caches, while ibex seek windswept shelves where snow is thin. Finches withdraw; ravens and other wide-ranging residents continue their circuits. Cloud keeps supplying moisture to a world temporarily unable to use much of it.

On one favourable thaw afternoon, however, a single seep may contain active films, grazing larvae, springtails at its rim, mites in the cushion behind it, and spiders beneath three separate flakes. Finches arrive. A chough crosses above. Far below, ibex graze an exposed shelf, and a falcon is already turning over the next basin. By evening the pool is sealed in transparent ice and the visible event has ended. Most of its participants have not ended with it. They are under stone, within plant tissue, in eggs and spores, or moving through the dusk toward another appearance of water.

That is the population of the Unfinished Range: not merely a sparse version of life elsewhere, but a life organized around interruption. Its inhabitants are not waiting for deep soil, continuous streams, or completed valleys. Those things may never come, and if they do, they will favour other arrangements. For now, existence here belongs to whatever can use a brief opening, endure its closure, and still find a way—by wing, hoof, wind, or patient dormancy—to be present when the next opening occurs somewhere else.
