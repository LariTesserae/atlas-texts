## The Rillfolk

Rill is home to a few hundred people, though counting its roofs would give a poor census. A roof may shelter an extended household, two households and a shared upper room, or one old person whose remaining space has been gradually surrendered to stairs, jars, and access passages. Children are minded by whoever shares the stair. Adults move between households after marriages, quarrels, deaths, and the occasional collapse of a floor. Water duties often outlast these arrangements: a person may sleep beneath one roof and still be responsible for a channel beneath another.

The Rillfolk are human, with no single physical type marking them as a separate people. What a visitor notices are habits acquired so early that they resemble instinct. They do not set down a vessel without glancing at the stone beneath it. They step over apparently empty strips of lane where an access slab lies flush with the mud. They wake when a familiar drip stops. Their hands carry small nicks from breaking fresh crust, and the hems of work clothes dry pale and stiff after a morning in the seeps. Indoors, possessions are kept high whenever possible. A dry sleeping mat, an unclouded drinking jar, and a storage shelf that has remained dry through the rains are forms of wealth.

They are not a solemn people, although visitors often mistake them for one. In the lanes they speak closely and without projection; walls return voices, and many conversations take place while someone is half-listening to water under a floor. On the roofs in plain sunlight, however, Rillfolk gossip, tease, argue about work badly done, and shout warnings across the shingles. Children can be extremely loud when no one is testing a channel. The town’s quiet is practical and intermittent, not a vow.

Nor do they worship water in any simple sense. They can curse a spring with considerable fluency. Their elaborate attention comes from living with consequences, not from seeing every seep as a spirit. A well-lashed joint, a sluice that falls cleanly, or a drain cover shaped to lift without breaking is admired as beautiful. The white mineral rind inspires no such affection. However lovely it may look to a stranger, to a Rillfolk worker it usually means labor postponed or a passage beginning to close.

Almost everyone performs some water work, but not everyone has the same skill. Some people are particularly good at reading a new stain, distinguishing rain leakage from a rising seep, or hearing the difference between a freely running hollow timber and one that is merely noisy because it has narrowed. These listeners and channel-keepers acquire considerable influence. Their knowledge is valuable, difficult to verify, and occasionally makes them insufferable.

Each important reach of the system has at least two keepers drawn from different households. One is expected to know where its water comes from; the other, where it goes. This does not prevent mistakes, but it makes it harder for one household to turn uncertainty into private advantage. No single person is permitted to become the sole authority over a spring, however well they understand it.

When a diversion must be debated, people gather around the settling court as best they can: at its edges, on the nearest stairs, and in the mouths of lanes, with questions repeated outward. There is no raised place for a speaker. By custom, the lowest household likely to be affected speaks first. The person proposing the change answers afterward. A plan is expected to account for four things—source, use, overflow, and escape. If someone can explain only where the water will be taken, and not where it will go when the arrangement fails, the plan is considered unfinished.

The custom does not create equality. Higher households can still speak pleasantly while sending their inconvenience downhill. Lower households bear more intrusive access work and more anxiety about floors. Experienced keepers sometimes become a small aristocracy of competence, respected until their confidence begins to outrun the changing ground. Rill has grudges, evasions, and quiet acts of selfishness. A board may be set to nudge water toward a neighbor’s wall; an unpleasant bend in a channel may be left uncleaned while the visible stretch is made immaculate. Usually the deception is eventually discovered, because water has little respect for a household’s account of itself.

Deliberately concealing a new seep is among the town’s serious offenses. The danger is not immediate enough to resemble violence, but everyone understands what it can become. A hidden spring may lift a sleeping platform, soften a shared wall, or emerge in the cellar of someone who had no part in the decision. Rill has no prison. Repair, sustained labor under the direction of those harmed, and the loss of trust are its ordinary punishments. Being thought unreliable with water is a deeper social injury than being thought quarrelsome or proud.

Because access stones must remain liftable, privacy has developed its own courtesies. A keeper entering beneath a private room states the channel and the work to be done. Water work is not supposed to become a social visit, and what is seen there is not supposed to become gossip. In practice, people sometimes fail on both counts. Households that value seclusion use screens, careful scheduling, and a great deal of tact rather than relying on a closed door.

For genuinely private speech, Rillfolk often go outside. Two people walking a little way onto the lakebed can remain visible to everyone and audible to no one. Courtships, delicate bargains, and family arguments are conducted along the mud paths or beside the raw gullies. From a distance, such conversations can look peaceful. They are not always so. The open basin provides room for feelings that would become intolerable beneath Rill’s low, connected roofs.

Children learn two maps of the town. The first is the visible one of lanes, stairs, roof gaps, and dependable dry footholds. The second is the invisible system of springs, pipes, overflows, and buried channels. The second map is more important and is never finished. They practice it by following the course of storm water, listening at lifted stones, and answering questions about where an overflow will reappear. Wooden chips may be raced in the outer rain channels, never in the clean seeps. A child who can remain still long enough to notice that one drip among several has changed is considered ready for more responsible work.

Older people are valued for remembering former arrangements, but memory is not treated as infallible. Someone who insists that water cannot enter a particular cellar because it never has before may simply be corrected by a wet floor. The most respected elders tend to say “the last time” rather than “always.” Their useful knowledge often consists of exceptions: the winter when a seam closed without warning, the diversion that worked until two storms came together, the old passage now sealed so thoroughly that everyone had forgotten it was there.

There is disagreement about Rill’s future. Some younger people see no reason to spend their lives maintaining passages that will only need opening again, and speak of building outward when the lakebed becomes firm enough. Others regard the exposed silt as no more trustworthy than a new crust over a drain. Some want to abandon the lowest rooms; others hear in that proposal an abandonment of the households that can least afford to move. These arguments remain unresolved. Meanwhile, the same people carry baskets, lift sluices, and repair the channels they have.

### The other inhabitants

Rill’s nonhuman lives are treated less as livestock or company than as scarce fellow occupants and, sometimes, as evidence.

The transparent crustaceans of the deepest spring pools are moved in shell cups when a basin is cleaned and returned before the work is considered complete. They are too small to be food and too delicate to survive rough handling. Their presence suggests that a pool remains cold, clean, and connected to its spring; their absence causes keepers to look again, though it does not by itself prove that anything is wrong. One of the first lessons children learn beside the basins is that clear water is not necessarily empty water.

The bitter water-cress is tended by rotation. Outer leaves are taken rather than whole plants, and the harvest is small enough that carelessness is noticed. It provides a sharp green addition to otherwise plain meals, not enough food to sustain the town. People can become remarkably possessive over a common trough. Its importance lies partly in its scarcity and partly in the fact that, unlike nearly everything else in Rill, it improves through gentle growth rather than through scraping, hauling, and repair. Moss on the roofs receives no such tenderness. It is tolerated where it remains harmless and scraped away when it begins to hold water against the shingles.

The black beetles in the settling court are mostly ignored. Eel-like fish entering the outer channels after rain may be caught when opportunity permits, but the gates are not operated for fishing. Their use belongs to the town’s water before it belongs to anyone’s appetite.

The gray heron is not considered sacred, and Rillfolk do not agree on any omen attached to it. It is simply large, wild, silent, and entirely indifferent to their arrangements. When one appears beside the outer pools, news of it travels quietly. Children are brought to a stair or doorway where they can see. Workers pause with their rods and picks. The bird stands in the wet emptiness beyond the roofs, belonging to none of the town’s systems and requiring no access through anyone’s floor. When it finally lifts away, work resumes, sometimes with a little embarrassment at how completely it had stopped.

A Rill upbringing is therefore an education in consequences that travel out of sight. The people can be tender, petty, conservative, adventurous, patient, and thoroughly tired of the whole arrangement—sometimes before breakfast. They have not mastered their small hydraulic world. They are its arguing, remembering, occasionally deceiving, and continually revising part. Even the most selfish among them lives where a private act may become public water. That, more than the pale crust on their hands or the crowded roofs above their lanes, is what makes them Rillfolk.
