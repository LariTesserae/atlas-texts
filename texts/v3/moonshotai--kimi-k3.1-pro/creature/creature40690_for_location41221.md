At first the census seems almost empty. Nothing calls, nothing darts away, and there are no nests, burrows, or paths made by feet. After an hour, however, the emptiness begins to look like a problem of scale and outline. The Rootworks is crowded with life, but most of that life does not keep a separate body for long.

It is convenient to call the largest resident the root-body, without assuming that it is one plant. Some of the reeds, broad-leaved climbers, and fine submerged stems probably begin as different kinds of growth. Once established, they graft so freely that their origins become difficult to trace. A shoot in the canopy may draw water through roots twenty paces away; a root beside an engine may be supplied by leaves it never shades. Following a single stem rarely settles the matter. It thickens, flattens, enters another stem, and emerges elsewhere with a different surface and another way of growing.

The oldest portions are not necessarily the largest. A waist-thick root may be a recent congestion of smaller growth, while a black cord no thicker than a wrist, deep in the mat, may have remained alive through decades of overgrowth. Age is visible chiefly as polish, compression, and the number of other structures passing through a thing.

An individual stem’s death is often only a change of occupation. A dead cane remains as a conduit until its walls collapse. Dead reeds continue to support the roofs made from their own leaves. Roots cease to carry sap and become braces, then dark cores inside younger roots. There are decomposers—bacterial films, fine fungal threads, minute animals grazing in wet seams—but they work in the narrow interval between death and incorporation. They are not allowed a season of loose litter and open rot. Almost nothing lies unused long enough to acquire the smell of decay.

Of all the rooted residents, the chambered canes have the most easily recognized lives. Young ones are soft enough to bend between two fingers, and many are crushed, shaded out, or rubbed away against the machinery. The survivors develop hard ribs and polished waists where iron has pressed them for years. Their chambers fill at slightly different rates, so the canes around an engine are never all in the same condition. One is taut and leaking beads of sap at its joints; another is slack; a third stands swollen against a yoke, apparently motionless, while the pressure within it approaches a stroke.

Old canes can become too woody to work. They continue swelling for a time against yokes that no longer respond, then split, seal themselves, and put out greener growth from below. Their dry upper chambers remain in place, carrying sounds from elsewhere in the hollow.

The other apparent specialists—the living belt-straps, gum-producing roots, oil-bearing growths around the cups—may not be separate species at all. Nor are they workers performing assigned tasks. They are, at least partly, the shapes that survive contact with the engines. A shoot entering a flywheel is worn away. One pressing against a moving yoke is repeatedly bruised and thickens. A root at a weeping joint remains wet while growth farther from the leak dries back. The machine prunes, selects, and polishes. Given enough time, its clearances become part of the vegetation’s anatomy.

This makes the maintenance look more deliberate from a distance than it does up close. There are old mistakes: a woody collar sealing a pipe that should have remained open, a root-gasket preserving the curve of iron that has almost disappeared, a belt-strap grown stiff and immobile at one end. Such failures are seldom corrected directly. They are bypassed or enclosed. The system persists not because every part performs its duty, but because there are more parts than duties.

The clear water contains another population. At each pump stroke, the level in some of the narrow pool-cells rises almost imperceptibly, and the white submerged stems bend before settling again. Rotifers, transparent worms, and other creatures too small to cast a visible shape in the pools live on the films coating those stems. A drop of cistern water appears empty until it has stood for a while under glass; then it begins to move. Some of this life is strained out by fine roots and returned to the mat. Some passes through the pipes repeatedly. The pumped circulation is therefore not only a water supply but a slow feeding current.

The colorless snails are the largest animals easily found. Most have shells no broader than a small fingernail, and many are much smaller. Their shells are clear enough to show a little gray or amber within; older ones acquire a faint iron stain. They prefer submerged brick, concrete, and the margins of the cistern rather than living roots. The preference is practical. A root can gradually overgrow a snail. Old mortar cannot.

They graze the thin films on these stable surfaces, leaving tracks that vanish as the film closes again. When water hammer shudders through the foundation, they draw tightly against the brick and wait. Their eggs appear as minute clear crescents in mortar joints. Empty shells do not accumulate in heaps; they separate, become waterlogged, and disappear among the root-cells. The snails do not seem to keep the machinery clean in any significant way. They simply live where the Rootworks cannot quickly grow over them.

The white eel is less easily assigned a place.

It usually appears behind the cistern grille with its head toward the current. Often only a hand’s length of it is visible. Its body has the translucent pallor of a submerged stem, and the narrow fin along its back looks like a loose strip of plant tissue. Its eyes, however, are small and distinctly dark. When it holds still, the movements of its mouth give it away. When it withdraws, it does so in a smooth succession of curves, as though a root were loosening and sliding back into the water.

No reliable description gives its full length. Its tail always remains in the dark beyond the grille or among the roots beneath the cistern. It has never been watched feeding in the open, though it may take the small transparent animals strained from the circulation, and perhaps young snails. At the crack of a trip valve it sometimes stops entirely; at other times the same sound sends it immediately out of sight.

No two have been seen together. This does not establish that only one exists. There may be several occupying separate lengths of pipe, or a succession of solitary animals. Nor is there any dependable way to compare present sightings with older ones: the accounts say little more than *white eel, alone, at the grille*. Its young have not been found, and no carcass has been recovered.

Unlike the canes and roots, it performs no obvious work. Its passage may help keep a small space open behind the grille, but that is hardly an explanation for its life. It is the one resident whose presence strongly implies an elsewhere—some reach of flooded pipe or root-chamber in which it spends almost all its time, doing nothing that an observer can turn into maintenance.

The scarcity of other animals does not seem to be enforced. There is no barrier at the rim, and nothing in the hollow appears to react to their arrival. Rather, almost every easy living has been removed. There are no flowers, fruits, dry seeds, loose bark, exposed soil, or accumulations of dead leaves. Open water is divided into narrow cells. Potential nest sites remain wet and are slowly enclosed. Even a substantial opening in the canopy is temporary on the scale of a bird’s lifetime.

Nothing in the Rootworks needs pollinators or seed-carriers. Nothing seems to require pruning by teeth. An insect or bird may enter, but there is little to induce it to remain. The hollow is not defended against animals so much as almost useless to them.

Its own young rarely look young. After storms, a gutter may briefly carry a faint gray-green dust of spores, most of which is caught before reaching the rim. Spores germinate in water films on established tissue; fragments begin growing where they have been wedged by current. Within the hollow, a new plant is often only a fresh green seam on something very old. Beyond the rim, fragments lose the reliable circulation, dry at their tips, or disappear among ordinary rushes. The Rootworks produces plenty of growth but few successful emigrants.

There is no human keeper. The pump house offers no dry sleeping place, no storage, and no task that must be performed each day. The ladder is instructive: it still leads upward, but its upper end terminates in packed foliage rather than a room or lookout. A person can clear a gutter, wedge a wheel, or refill an oil cup, but nothing in the long cycles requires such a visit. A frequently used path may remain open; an occasional visitor’s path becomes a rib of stems.

Nor is there any need to imagine a concealed gardener of another kind. Nothing investigates a footstep. No root hurries toward a leak. The quickest responses are hydraulic; living ones take days, seasons, or decades. What looks like care is largely the accumulation of forms that have not yet failed. The cistern is not a head, and the engines are not quite a heart. There is no single place where the whole arrangement is known.

By separate bodies, then, the census is a tangled population of plants that may amount to only a few ancient grafted individuals; an invisible crowd carried through the water; transparent snails on the old brick; and at least one white eel. By habitation, it is shorter still.

The snails and the eel live *in* the Rootworks. The canes, roots, leaves, and living straps live *as* it. And the engines, though they neither breathe nor grow, have been drawn so deeply into its circulation and repair that they can no longer be left out of its life. The iron has not awakened. It has been given a lasting function inside something that remains alive.
