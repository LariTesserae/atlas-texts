What lives in the Seep-City does not live in its houses.

A census taken doorway by doorway would find only wet thresholds, dark chambers, and water ticking through stone. No small hands tend the cups. Nothing sleeps in the vaulted rooms or keeps stores at the backs of them. The covered stairs carry no feet, and no creature looks out from the openings in the walls. Even the roar contains no concealed population. The ear makes voices from it because ears are practiced at that kind of hope.

Life is not distributed through the city. It is pooled at the bottom.

The bacterial film is the nearest thing to a settled population. It is not a continuous skin, and certainly not a carpet. At its margins it consists of separated cells and little holdings too sparse to discolor the stone. Only where these gather thickly does the cistern’s faint iridescence dull to gray. Under sufficient magnification, the stain would resolve into individuals and small aggregates with bare mineral still visible between them: less a mat than a crowd so thin that, from any ordinary distance, it appears to be one substance.

It lives on chemical differences between the seep water and the rock—on dissolved matter that can be taken in, transformed, and made to yield a little energy. The pewter radiance gives it nothing. It does not fringe the brightest part of the cistern or turn toward the shining walls. Its geography is determined by gradients no eye could see: where water lingers, where dissolved minerals are replenished, where a surface offers purchase, where grazing has recently passed.

The cold keeps its chemistry slow. Scarcity keeps it small.

This is not dormancy. Cells divide. A patch extends by increments. New material gathers among older cells and changes the way water lies against the basin. But nothing comes of these changes at the scale of the city. The film thickens and is grazed back, or extends beyond its supply and thins again. It neither blooms nor climbs. No cloudy plume rises from it, no spores enter the still air, and no succession of more elaborate growth follows. Its activity is real, but it does not accumulate into conquest. At the level of individual cells there are events; at the level of the basin, the events cancel.

The transparent crustacean belongs to another scale entirely.

It is not a miniature citizen moving through a miniature city. Many of the hair-width streets could not admit even part of it. A doorway cannot be a doorway to a body too broad to pass through it, nor a chamber shelter if the chamber is smaller than the animal seeking shelter. Most of the architecture is therefore not architecture for it. It is an intricate mineral obstruction surrounding the few places that matter: the interstice beneath a bridge, the accessible cistern rim, the patches where stone, water, and bacterial film meet.

When it emerges, it does not come out of a home. It comes out of refuge.

Its antennae move before the rest of it, testing a boundary that cannot be seen reliably. Then the legs begin their deliberate sequence. There are no dark eye-spots to give the body an easily found face, no pigment in the joints, no colored shell to separate it from the water behind. After it has fed, however, a narrow grayness may appear within it: material from the film passing through its gut. For a while, that internal thread is the most substantial-looking thing about the animal.

It grazes with motions too small to read as scraping from any distance. A little of the stain clears. The water’s iridescence returns there, not as a dramatic bright track but as a brief restoration of the condition surrounding the film. Then the animal moves on, or stops, or withdraws. Nothing about the rim records which.

Its slowness is not solemnity. At that scale, water has more borders than open distances. A meniscus is not merely a silver line but a surface that can resist, deflect, or hold. A drop is not a jewel hanging beneath an arch; it is an enclosing mass with a skin. Even a shallow film can alter the footing of a leg. The animal’s care is the practical care of a creature for which an apparently dry edge, a wet edge, and an open drop are three profoundly different countries.

Nor does it experience the city’s sound as a market, a storm, or a crowd. There is no silence against which it could have learned the roar as noise. The hard clicks and mineral booming are simply properties of the surfaces around it, as constant as cold. They neither summon nor warn. If another crustacean lives deeper in the system, the sound provides no convenient medium through which to find it.

There may indeed be others. Their existence would not make a society. Nothing suggests shoals, nests, territories, or gatherings. The city offers no abundance from which a crowd could arise. Even several animals would remain, in practice, solitary: clear bodies appearing at widely separated places and times, each involved with the immediate problem of food and water-skin. Their transparency is not a performance of ghostliness. There is no audience for whom they need to appear ghostly, and no hunter visible at the rim against which transparency might be a useful disguise.

The animal at the cistern is not evidently old, young, last, or first. It is not the city’s keeper. It does not repair a joint, clear a channel for the city’s sake, or inherit any purpose from the makers of the stairs. Nothing marks it as their heir. Its life does not explain the architecture, and the architecture does not explain its life.

The whole living economy can almost be stated in one sentence: dissolved minerals support the film; the film supports the grazer; nearly everything else is water and stone. If other animals persist deeper in the cavity, they must do so within the same narrow budget. A detached bacterial cell may sometimes be carried through a channel or held briefly in a drop, but transit does not make the upper streets another inhabited region. The wet lanes remain empty. The rooms opening behind other rooms remain empty. The little sea contains nearly all that can properly be called alive.

It is tempting to make the city itself the missing inhabitant. It has circulation, after all, and a stable internal condition. It takes in and gives out. One mineral face dissolves while another is restored. It fills every chamber with resonance and maintains that resonance without faltering. But these resemblances do not need to be promoted into life. The cistern is not a heart, the roar is not a voice, and the frozen accounting is not an intention. The city persists because its rates balance, not because anything within it strives to persist. It neither tends the film nor notices the grazer.

The literal inhabitants are stranger for being so slight.

Sometimes the entire visible animal population is a clear flex at the cistern rim, distinguishable from a bubble only by the sequence of its legs and the gray thread inside it. Then it withdraws beneath the bridge, and even that is gone. The film remains at the bottom, taking almost nothing from the seep and altering almost nothing in return. Water crosses the doorways, the cups fill and empty, and the roar continues at the same pitch.

What lives there keeps the Seep-City from being sterile without ever quite making it inhabited.
