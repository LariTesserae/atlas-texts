Anatomy is a poor way to census the Works. An inhabitant may be gravel while feeding, brine while traveling, and a traveling tick in the saturated ground between meals. What endures is not necessarily a shape, but a preference: for one kind of gradient, one route through the herd, one rhythm by which scattered matter repeatedly becomes a body. Animals recognize one another by these persistences. So, after a while, does anyone who watches them.

The rillbacks are easiest to know by their gait. Their wet armor changes continually, plates thickening with charged silt and thinning again as potential is stripped away. A dome may be porous with age, half occupied by smaller animals, and still cross three swells before its feet cease to agree that they belong together. Sometimes the agreement survives the dome. The gait continues in another part of the herd, gathering new mud around footfalls that no longer have an original body to remember.

Their young often begin as such disagreements. A group of feet falls slightly behind the rest. Brine lingers in the depression they make. Charge collects along the delay. By the time the larger rillback has passed, several low, unsteady domes are following it, each with too few feet and an exaggerated version of its limp. The herd does not nurture them. It simply fails to close over them quickly enough, and their continued avoidance of the surrounding flanks becomes the first thing they do consistently.

The backs are countries to smaller fauna. Pale back-runners live in the seams of the armor, flattening whenever a flank grinds against a neighbor. They feed on the weak discharge between plates and defend particularly productive cracks with quick, sterile collisions; little of either combatant remains afterward except the habit of returning to the same crack. When herds press together, back-runners emigrate by the million. Some spend their whole lives without touching ground. Their geography consists entirely of which animals have touched which other animals, and their offspring may inherit a route through bodies that will not meet for another several compressions.

Beneath the herd live the transparent animals of the foot film. Many survive by being trodden on. Pressure spreads one into a thin, bright multitude, and each fragment gathers enough silt to rise before the next footfall. Their knocking helps coordinate the rillbacks above them, though no single rillback depends on any particular multitude for long. When a hush passes, the feet continue and the knocking vanishes. Whole generations of foot-film animals lose their boundaries in the sudden failure of timing, and the mud glitters with burrowers coming up to claim them.

In the pools, individuality is still less dependable. A young wire eel may begin as a blue scratch in the brine, a short tension with no visible substance at all. Once it joins an alignment, its private current enters the common one. It can be difficult then to say whether the chain contains many eels, one eel with a great number of temporary bodies, or merely a route along which eels repeatedly occur.

Old chains carry kinks where earlier attacks interrupted them. New lengths preferentially precipitate near these kinks, and the pool’s hunters know it. They wait not for weakness in flesh but for hesitation in conductivity: a newborn length shivering free, an old eel slow to accept a charge, a narrow region crowded with animals feeding on the chain’s leakage. A strike at such a place can extinguish the pool from edge to edge.

Then the menders arrive. They are blunt, dark animals, visible mainly when the pool is dark, and they feed by gathering around severed tensions. Their bodies become briefly solid as they strip charge from the wreckage. Wire eels crowd after them, attracted by the small currents their feeding releases. New alignments form along the menders’ paths. Thus a pool may return to blue-white brilliance without anything having repaired it, and the menders may be eaten before the light has fully returned.

Along moving stripes of ice live the seam swimmers. Neither liquid nor solid brine is quite a medium to them; each is a different way of being enclosed. They gather where the two states meet, their bodies reduced to narrow exclusions around which freezing must pass. When the stripe vanishes, many vanish with it. Others continue in the liquid, carrying no substance across the boundary except a direction of travel and a tendency to turn whenever the brine begins to whiten.

A fog herd may be thousands of animals during one pressure phase and, during the next, a single broad hunger with thousands of dripping edges. Abundant sound makes its members spread until their separate condensations overlap. In that state they feed together, diving through a vent’s shriek or the grainy roar beneath a migration. When the sound fails, the herd narrows, divides, or rains partially onto the backs below. If two herds merge, there may be no subsequent fact of the matter about which one survived.

Their juveniles condense on whatever is coldest: rillback armor, exhausted eel chains, the porous outlines left by predators. Some unfold immediately. Others remain as heavy motes through several migrations, waiting for a cold that belongs to the proper part of their cycle. Reverse rain carries many of them upward again. A fog herd streaming low over the swells may therefore be surrounded by its own unborn young, some falling toward it, some rising away, and some arriving before the cloud in which they should have condensed.

Small thieves live within the condensation itself. They drink buoyancy rather than sound, attaching themselves to nothing more durable than a flier’s tendency to remain aloft. A heavily infested herd drips continuously and flies lower with every passing phase. Eventually its members begin alighting on pools and rillbacks, where the thieves abandon them. The stranded fliers are not necessarily dying. Many become other kinds of animals for a while, feeding as flat films until enough sound gathers to make them buoyant again.

Even a hush has an ecology, though nothing lives upon it.

Small hushes are common between one footfall and the next. Most consume no more than a tick, a scrape, or the brief whistle of a burrower before losing coherence. The great ones are those whose feeding has never been interrupted long enough for sound to close over them. Their appetite has acquired a boundary. Within it, they take not only noise but the coordination noise makes possible: feet forget their sequence, bodies solidifying in response to a call remain partly dispersed, and swimming multitudes lose the common shudder by which they had resembled one animal.

Fringe-runners travel just outside this silence. They are quick, low creatures, assembled mostly from mineral grains and alarm. They feed on the collisions at the hush’s edge, where fleeing animals strike others that have received no warning. Some follow a single hush for so long that their reproductive rhythm becomes another means of detecting it. Ahead of the silence, they are scarce; beside it, dense; behind it, they are already scattering toward the returning roar.

Within the center, scavengers enter the cold outlines almost as those outlines form. Death and habitation become successive names for the same occupancy. When the hush moves on, the Works’ sound returns through a crowd that is already several generations removed from the prey that had stood there.

Warmth supports a more temporary nation. The first arrivals are usually transparent spindles, each drinking not heat itself but the difference between one layer of brine and the next. Closest to the source, they become solid and crowded. Farther out, they are little more than orientations toward the center. Back-runners abandon their rillbacks to reach them. Eels coil into unusable knots around warm pools. Fog herds descend through the noise of the gathering, and larger animals follow the fugitives.

By then, warmth has ceased to be merely a resource. It has become a season with inhabitants, migrations, predators, and a particular kind of refuse. When the gradient finally weakens, the innermost animals lose their bodies first. The outer ones persist awhile by feeding on the dispersal of the crowd. Nothing remains to mark the event except altered routes, unusually dense young, and rillbacks whose armor briefly carries the scars of animals that never ordinarily touch the ground.

The pale anticipants have made a more exacting life from causal irregularity. They gather at carcasses before the corresponding deaths. A warning body in a pool is, to them, an early and rather poor feeding stage; the later corpse is the rich one. They raise one generation around the first and leave another waiting for the second. If the warning proves false, or belongs to a nearby reach of world, they do not migrate far in search of an explanation. They remain until other animals find a use for their substance.

Likewise, the wire eels that emerge ahead of their mineral pulse are not simply premature. The pulse often follows the conductive path their presence provides. Their early existence is part of the means by which their later bodies become possible. Older fauna treat the appearance of such young as reliable weather. Herds turn. Menders gather. Hunters take up positions around deaths that have not yet supplied anyone with a meal.

The inhabitants of newly inserted ground are never pioneers. Their rillbacks have worn feet and half-healed flank scars. Their back-runners already know which armor seams are worth defending. Their eel chains bear the kinks of old attacks, and their fog juveniles may be caught halfway through an unfolding that began on a surface which, until now, had not been present. These pasts are real in every way that matters to an animal: they determine hunger, route, fear, and which neighbor will be attacked on sight.

At the margins, herds sometimes meet and immediately resume an old avoidance. Burrowers flee from rillbacks that have not yet crossed toward them. A fringe-runner population may recognize the approach of a hush before any silence has entered the audible world. The additional reach relieves nothing. It arrives with its own crowding, its own unfinished feedings, its own deaths approaching through established channels.

Nothing in the Works is therefore young merely because its matter has recently assembled, and nothing is safely dead merely because its outline has failed. An old animal is often one whose habits have begun to be continued by other kinds of animals. A rillback’s gait persists in the mud fauna it fed. A fog herd’s route survives in juveniles that have never joined it. A great hush leaves little appetites moving through the footfalls behind it. There may be exceedingly ancient individuals here, though none of them has carried the same substance for long.

At the next compression, all these superimposed lives briefly coincide. Rillbacks lower their domes; back-runners vanish into seams; the foot film tightens. Wire eels draw into alignments that include lengths not yet precipitated. Fog herds spread in expectation of the blast, while pale anticipants turn toward a pool where something is already lying dead. Somewhere a hush takes the ticking out of a herd, and the fringe-runners close around its edge.

For that one stroke, the lowland can be mistaken for a single vast animal: mud gathering beneath it, brine flashing through it, silence passing among its multitudes.

Then the pressure releases. Herds divide, chains darken, fliers rise, and scavengers inherit every boundary that fails. The Works becomes a crowd again.

And on some reach of ground that has only just made room for itself, an old animal turns onto a migration route it has followed all its life.
