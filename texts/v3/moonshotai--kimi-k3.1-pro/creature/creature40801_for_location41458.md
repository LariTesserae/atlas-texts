The basin has an occupation before it has an ecology. Its visible inhabitants are the crews, and even they are less a settled population than a slow circulation of hands through the works. A stranger can walk for an hour and meet no one, then enter a district that seems crowded with labor: mallets approaching from several directions, stacks whispering down, someone scraping behind a wall. The entire population may be three workers and a cooling kiln.

The workers do not see in the dark. Their skill is memory organized by touch. They know the difference between a mold seam and a structural crack by the resistance offered to a fingernail; they can pass repeatedly along a bank without confusing two apparently identical sockets; they recognize a change in floor level through the soles of their feet before their pacing has had time to go wrong. Their hands are often surprisingly light for hands that spend the working turn striking things. A stranger watched by one of them may notice that the worker is not looking at the stranger’s face, but at where the stranger is about to put a foot.

Their camps are temporary almost to the point of denial: a dry ledge that has rung correctly, a covered vessel of water, tools laid where each can be found by weight and outline. When the work moves, the camp follows. A sleeping place may become part of a foundation within a season. Workers who say they are going home usually mean that they are returning to the other members of their crew.

Three is the ordinary unit because three kinds of attention are enough to sustain work: one person shapes, one moves and stacks, and one listens to what has already been done. Larger gatherings form around kilns, difficult excavations, or the laying of a survey line between districts. These gatherings disperse again. There are waterkeepers who know which condensation cups can be emptied without starving a mixing pit, line-keepers who maintain relationships between places hours apart, and listeners whose authority is largely negative. They cannot always order a bank to be raised, but they can stop one from being raised. In the basin, the right to prevent is often more respected than the right to command.

Few workers claim to possess the purpose of the whole. Precision and ignorance coexist comfortably in them. A crew may be able to reproduce a channel gradient to the thickness of a thumbnail while disagreeing about whether the channel is intended for a river, a flood, or a vibration not yet made. Plans survive less as stories than as gauges, molded lengths, habits of spacing, and sequences taught from one hand to another. An old gauge can outrank an old opinion. This gives the workers an unusual kind of orthodoxy: they may be strict about the curve of a rim and entirely undogmatic about the world to which that rim belongs.

Sound has shaped their manners as well as their labor. Speech is used for warning, close instruction, and the leisure of kiln warmth. At a distance, workers identify themselves with short figures of taps and scrapes. These signatures are practical at first, but some become personal names. A worker may be known across several districts by a pause rather than by anything spoken. Districts acquire similar names: Twenty Returns, the Mute Stair, Cup Rain, the Bank That Answers Late. Because construction changes acoustic behavior, such names are not always permanent. Directions therefore contain a certain amount of biography. To be told that a place used to be called Hollow Knee is to learn something about both the route and the people who named it.

Their time is similarly local. A waking is called morning whether or not anything outside the works could conceivably be morning. A long undertaking may be counted in firings, settlements, or rains. Warmth is too brief to schedule a life around, but kiln openings come closest to being social occasions. Several crews may gather where the heat will last, exchanging tools, news, and arguments about gradients. The dull red light is sometimes sufficient to show a face at close range. People who have known one another for seasons by footfall and tapping signature may then spend an unusual amount of time simply looking at one another.

Age does not necessarily remove a worker from listening. Hearing damaged by years of kilns and close impacts can be supplemented by touch: a palm spread on tile, fingers hooked lightly over a rim, a copper pin held against the teeth. Some old workers are particularly sensitive to the late vibration that travels through a floor after an audible echo has ended. They are also more likely to admit uncertainty. Among experienced crews, “I cannot tell what that was” is considered useful information, not an admission of failure.

Hospitality to strangers begins with safe standing room and a rhythm to follow. Explanations come later, if at all. A visitor may be given water before being told the name of the district, and will almost certainly be told when not to strike before being shown how to strike. Survey lines are treated with a seriousness that can resemble reverence, though its basis is practical. A slack line crossing an abandoned-looking pit may still be the only reliable relation between two embankments; cutting it could cause no visible damage and yet make months of work disagree. Workers will step over such a line without appearing to notice that they have done so. For them, this is ordinary courtesy.

The only other inhabitant whose body can regularly be offered as evidence is the joint-runner, and even its evidence is usually circumstantial: six punctures in damp clay, a faint click inside a wall, a pale shard where no shard had been a moment before.

Most runners are no longer than a little finger, though workers occasionally report old ones broad enough to cover the center of a palm. The shell is not ceramic, despite the sound it makes. At rest it has a slight unevenness and warmth that fired tile never possesses. The creature carries itself so low that its body is often mistaken for a flake of burst glaze. When disturbed, it may remain still for several breaths before vanishing with abrupt speed into a joint that looked too narrow to admit it.

Runners drink water too small in quantity for the workers to measure usefully. One will spend a long while beneath a cold tile where condensation has gathered, touching its mouth to separate beads. Its appearance on an exposed surface can therefore be informative. Crews have found hidden damp behind walls by noticing where runners emerge, and have learned to distrust a joint that shelters several of them during an otherwise dry working turn.

What they eat remains unresolved. The basin offers no obvious grazing, and the workers leave little that could support another creature. Some maintain that runners scrape nourishment from skin oils and salts left in palm prints; others point to their presence around cooling kilns and suspect something in the black scale. A few listeners, usually after a tiring turn, suggest that they gather wherever an echo has behaved strangely. This last idea has produced no useful experiment.

They are neither pets nor quite pests. A runner’s ticking can mask the ceramic chatter of a settling stack, but its step is irregular in a way a practiced ear can learn. Some crews regard one living near camp as a sign that their sleeping ledge will remain dry. Others immediately test the ledge again. Both responses may be correct. The runner’s habitat is not clay in general but imperfection: a badly fitted joint, a forgotten cup, a firing crack, a clearance that should have been closed and was not. If the basin were ever made exactly as intended, it might become less hospitable to the only animal known to live there.

Beyond the workers and the runners, residence becomes a matter of behavior rather than agreement.

The workers have several names for whatever produces the anomalous returns. In some districts it is the second crew. Elsewhere it is the late knock, the under-answer, or simply what is below a particular sealed sump. No consistent body has been described. There is only an extra beat after all surfaces should have answered, a blow returned from a direction with no open passage, or a familiar tapping figure repeated after the worker who uses it has stopped. Such events are rare enough that every crew tells a different version and common enough that the rules concerning them are nearly the same everywhere.

No one shouts into the dark to ask who is there. No one strikes a second time to make the phenomenon repeat. Work is moved, sometimes only a short distance and sometimes to another district. A survey line may bend around the place. A sump may remain sealed long after the water it contains would have been useful.

This is not worship. Nothing is offered, and no worker appears anxious to make the presence stronger, more frequent, or more communicative. What is given is space.

Every anomaly has possible explanations: trapped water, two crews working without knowledge of one another, a delayed release of stress, a flue connecting districts thought to be separate. Experienced workers can explain each incident separately and still refuse to explain all of them away. Their caution does not depend on deciding whether the basin contains a creature, an old instability, or some acoustic habit acquired by too many connected hollows. If the cause is instability, leaving is sensible. If it is a presence, then its territory consists largely of postponed work—narrow strips not cut, walls not raised, questions not asked twice.

When an entire district falls silent, these distinctions become less important. The workers stop. Runners withdraw into joints. Even the ordinary ticking of cooling material seems, to frightened memory, to have ceased. People put their hands or feet against the nearest solid surface and wait for movement that may never come. Sometimes a bank folds and the practical interpretation is restored. Sometimes nothing happens, and the crew nevertheless begins elsewhere the next waking.

In a sleeping turn, the whole population may therefore amount to very little: several workers lying near the last warmth of a kiln, a runner drinking from a bead beneath a tile, and a distant procession of blows passing from hollow to hollow. If one return arrives late, no one sits up to investigate. In the morning, a gauge may be set a little farther away.

That is how life fits into the basin. The crews inhabit sound ledges and moving camps. The runners inhabit failed clearances. The third population, if it exists, inhabits the interval between a question and its answer. None of them softens the works. Each has learned, in its own way, where not to strike. For now, that is enough to make a home: a ledge that holds, a drop that gathers, and a silence everyone agrees to keep.
