## Life on the Last Flat

Nothing lives on the Last Flat by holding a piece of it.

A nest would only be a temporary arrangement of silt. A territory would be a rumor. Even a thick body would give the collapses too much to act upon. The enduring forms are therefore thin, soft, intermittent, and able to resume after being buried, dispersed, or carried into rain. More precisely, they live on differences: a seep slightly warmer than the water above it, a made stone denser than the sediment around it, one film not yet chemically identical to the next. Where the Flat has done its work perfectly, nothing lives. Life begins where erosion has missed some small inequality.

Most of the living matter is in the olive sheen.

The sheen is not one organism, and it is rarely a continuous mat. Under magnification it resolves into clear cells, reddish chains, dark motes, and associations that hold together for a while before coming apart. Some of these use the weak light. More subsist on chemical inequalities: newly condensed water lying over water that has already passed through silt; copper seepage crossing an older iron-bearing province; warmth entering sediment that has not yet warmed all the way through. They feed, in effect, on the tendency of unlike things to become alike. Each cell is a brief delay in that process.

It is a meager diet, but the Flat supplies it almost everywhere.

No sheen becomes a floating meadow. If a film grows rich enough to darken, it also grows heavy enough to slide. A current takes it, a blister breaks beneath it, or the whole water skin stands up and carries it elsewhere. Its prosperity is one of the mechanisms by which it is dispersed. Resting cells remain in sediment, in the pores of stones, and in the hanging lenses of rain. A long-lived lens may contain several generations of microscopic life before it falls. When rain bursts sideways into spray, the inhabitants of one drop become the inhabitants of a province of air.

Nothing lives permanently in the air. The air only carries life between other conditions.

Within the films are tiny grazers: transparent coils, flattened sacs, soft bodies folded so completely around themselves that they can be mistaken for granules of sediment. The largest are visible only when they turn and catch the pewter light. They have no shells, no eyes, and no hard parts that might outlast the rest of them. Some unfold when a particular chemistry returns; others become active only when two films overlap. A pool may contain them in abundance for several minutes and then appear perfectly empty, though nothing has left it. They have merely become indistinguishable from what they were eating.

These are the animals of ordinary days. The great animal is something else.

Before it, though, there is seamwort, which remains the most common visible life because it is the least committed to being entirely where it appears.

A seamwort patch does not simply bridge two places. It bridges two incomplete agreements about the same place. Its lower filaments take up dissolved material from sediment; the rest, disappearing into air that is wet somewhere else, receives moisture and perhaps other substances across the discrepancy. The plant lives along the exchange. Its redness has little to do with the brightness of the sky. It deepens when the two versions of the surface draw apart.

The trembling comes from this strain. Each filament is being asked, very slightly, to belong to two movements at once.

When the versions close, seamwort may become colorless and almost vanish. This does not always mean that it has died. Days later, if the seam reopens, red threads may stand again in the same place. No seed has been certainly identified. Pieces pinched off by a closing surface sometimes appear already rooted along another discrepancy, as though separation were not an injury to the plant but one of its ordinary methods of arrival.

A patch never improves the ground for anything else. It makes no soil, holds no bank, and leaves no layer of dead tissue. Where it grows beside a made threshold, the stone gives the discrepancy a reference point and the patch may persist longer. Even there it does not spread far. It is not a colony advancing across the Flat. It is a locality repeatedly remembering how to support a plant.

The made stones themselves are the nearest thing to fixed addresses.

Elsewhere, one cannot return to exactly the same patch of life, because the patch may have been replaced without moving. On the stones, however, a particular black cup can be found again in a particular corner. This permits a kind of slowness unknown elsewhere. The cups may be very old.

Their shape is not for catching water. Water is the one thing they never lack. A cup defends a small volume of comparatively still air against constant condensation and dilution. Within it, the lichen carries on exchanges that the surrounding downpour would continually interrupt. When submerged, it waits. When exposed, it uses the faint light, the warmth lingering in the stone, and minerals brought out by the clear water weeping through the pores.

Its growth is not steady. It may add almost nothing for years, then thicken imperceptibly during some long occupation of warm weather. The rust-colored life in the joints follows another rhythm, advancing where seepage lingers and retreating from the exposed faces. Whether it is a companion organism, an earlier stage of the black cups, or something unrelated remains undecided. Neither produces soil. Beneath them, the stone is still bare and soapy.

The cups are the only life on the Flat that can afford to become old in one location. They do not found a succession. They merely endure long enough to make succession imaginable.

At stable seeps, the occasional leafless stem is usually called a seepwick. The visible stem is less the whole plant than an event in it. Most of the organism is a loose, nearly colorless web within the seep, spread through sediment that retains some slight constancy of warmth and flow. The stem is hollow and appears to be respiratory. Its tip keeps enough of the seep’s warmth to maintain a tiny envelope of clearer air, sometimes visible as a finger-width absence of haze.

It has no leaf because a leaf would offer the rain a handle.

A seepwick may exist for a long time as nothing above the surface. Then a stem rises, stands for an hour or a season, and folds back into the seep. Occasionally a clear bead swells at its tip. This may be reproductive tissue. It may also be only condensed water. No later stem has been traced convincingly to such a bead, and no two seepwicks grow close enough together to be called a stand.

Against this mostly microscopic and filamentous economy, the great sheet beneath the silt is enormous, rare, and almost irrelevant. It is usually called the underhide.

It cannot be common enough to determine the abundance of the films on which it feeds. Its importance in reports says more about the observers than about the Flat: a large animal is easier to remember than a temporary difference in microbial sheen. Yet nothing else alive is capable of becoming an event at that scale without making a sound.

The underhide has no settled front or rear. Any margin may become the leading one. It can reverse without turning, narrow itself around a buried obstruction, or spread so thin that the sediment above it scarcely rises. There are no eyes because there is no useful direction from which light might come, and no rigid skeleton because rigidity would give a collapsing dome something to break. Pressure appears to pass through it with little more consequence than through the surrounding mud.

Its whole surface may be sensory. The upper side responds to changes in load; the lower side, when it can be examined at all, seems adapted to taste the films along the sediment interface. It does not burrow in the ordinary sense. Nor does it quite swim. It proceeds by a local alteration of adherence and saturation, and behind it the silt is briefly pale and dry.

That dryness remains unexplained.

Where the underhide has fed, the microscopic films are not merely cropped. Adjacent films that were chemically or temporally distinct become less distinguishable. This has led to the suggestion that it consumes not only their living matter but the inequality between them. If so, its food is most abundant where the Flat is congested, and a well-fed animal would leave behind it a small region in which less was happening.

No carcass has been found. No skeleton, shed skin, egg, or unmistakable juvenile has been recovered. A dead underhide, if it retained its shape, would be conspicuous; therefore either it does not die where it can be found, or death is another kind of thinning. Reports disagree about its outline, its thickness, and even the number of its margins. Sizes do not sort neatly by region. The same enormous sheet has been described in widely separated places, while much smaller ones have been encountered along what might have been its later route.

Perhaps there are many. Perhaps there is one animal encountered at incompatible ages. Perhaps “underhide” names a condition that several kinds of organism can approach until their differences cease to be observable.

It gives no sign of intelligence beyond the intelligence necessary to remain soft. It does not investigate the made stones, avoid their lines, or treat the narrow doorways differently from any other dense obstruction. Nothing indicates mating, territoriality, or recognition between individuals. No call would be audible in any case, but the animal does not even disturb the surface in a patterned way. Its passage is silent because silence is the shape of its body.

Other animals are occasionally inferred: a film grazed into regular arcs, a black cup emptied of some inner tissue, a transparent knot clinging briefly to a seepwick. These traces may belong to the lens fauna, to young underhides, or to creatures never seen. The Flat does not repeat conditions patiently enough for zoology to wear its doubts away.

There are no evident people. The roofless cell has no occupant beyond warm water, rust growth, and the black cups. The steps bear no paths of return. Nothing has been observed placing one object upon another, or preserving an interval because the interval had meaning. If the makers have living descendants, nothing in their behavior distinguishes them.

Nor are the Flat’s own constructions secretly alive. The ribbed domes are not reefs. The mud vaults are not shells, and the temporary cities have no citizens too small to see. Their resemblance to organs and artifacts is real but brief; they do not feed, inherit their forms, or preserve a boundary against what surrounds them. It would be easy to call the whole Flat alive and be finished with the question, but its unquestionable inhabitants suggest a narrower definition. Life is what keeps a small distinction in force for a while. The Flat continually passes distinctions onward.

During the rare withdrawals of turbulence, nearly the whole visible living world can sometimes be taken in at once: an olive breath at the edge of a brown mirror; seamwort reddening beside a drowned threshold; black cups waiting under water; one seepwick holding its little absence of haze. Farther out, a pale seam closes without revealing what moved beneath it.

None of these responds to the others. There is no assembled community to be interrupted.

Then the mirror arches. Films are lifted, divided, and carried into rain. Seamwort is pulled between its partial locations. The seepwick folds back or is torn away. The underhide, if it was there, offers the collapse almost nothing to crush. On the made stones, the cups fill with water and wait.

Not everything survives every resumption. Continuity does not require that. Enough cells remain in drops and pores, enough divided filaments persist along other seams, enough of a seepweb stays below the reach of the disturbance. Life returns without recolonizing, because it was never entirely absent.

It leaves no leaf beds, shell banks, bone sands, or fossil soils. It is not a forest waiting under the mud, nor a seed from which the makers will necessarily come again. It is not the promise of another world. It belongs to this one.

On a continent being worn toward perfect levelness, living means only this: to remain, through one more collapse and one more rain, not quite interchangeable with what surrounds you.
