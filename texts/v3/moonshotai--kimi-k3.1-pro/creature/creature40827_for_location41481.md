## Life in the High Basin

Most of the High Basin’s inhabitants never enter its open water.

They live within the floor and the buried walls, in wet pores, in the narrow spaces between grains, and along fractures too small to cast a shadow. A plain that appears lifeless may be inhabited just beneath its surface. The visible sea is often the least crowded part of the Basin.

The colorless microorganisms come first. Their growth seldom resembles a mat. At its thickest, it gives a fresh fracture a faint slickness and clouds the water immediately above it with delicate floc. They draw energy from the meeting of brine and newly exposed binder, so their habitat is not simply the made material, but damage to it. A grazing scar, a fresh crack, or the raw face of a collapse can support a brief abundance while the untouched surface beside it remains nearly sterile.

In old, sealed pores, many of these microorganisms persist with almost no activity. Exposure wakes some and releases others. Thus a wound in the floor becomes a feeding ground, and everything larger ultimately depends on these scattered awakenings.

The masons move through the films and among the grains.

An individual mason is a soft, translucent fleck, flattened enough to turn inside its own cavity. A dark thread of gut and the pale particle forming behind it may be all that distinguish it from debris. It feeds on microbial growth, takes in mineral, sheds a delicate outer casing as it grows, and leaves cemented material in its wake. Its perceptions are immediate: the chemistry beneath its body, contact with a neighbor, the resistance of a pore wall, the drag of current at an opening. Beyond that, the Basin might be any size.

There is no common generation among them and no central stage at which a population begins a vault or decides upon a channel. Some masons spend their whole lives enlarging one cavity by a few grains. Others leave when crowding makes their natal surface poor. Carried away as nearly invisible specks, most are lost; enough settle on suitable material to continue the work.

Nor are the masons all alike. Different kinds handle different grains and leave different deposits—platelets, rods, hollow beads, thin layers that split readily, dense particles that endure long after the animal is gone. The distinction between a regular seam and a confused mineral knot is therefore partly a difference in current and partly a difference in bodies. In either case, the architecture is an accumulation of local accommodations. No mason experiences an arch. It experiences a place where the next particle can remain.

The flat grazers are among the few inhabitants easily visible on the open floor.

They are broad, soft animals with opalescent gray backs and clear, muscular margins. Beneath them, a wide feeding surface rasps and loosens the material over which they creep. Their tracks are not clean swaths but alterations in texture: a slight dulling of the surface, a shallow furrow, a line where loose grains have been pushed aside. They turn according to the richness of the microbial film, tightening their paths where food is concentrated and widening them across poorer ground. Over time, these movements produce the great spirals.

A grazer consumes countless microorganisms and masons, but it does not simply impoverish a surface. By worrying away old material, it opens fresh pores to the brine. A recently grazed field may look bare, then become richer than the untouched floor around it. Masons often settle densely in the furrows, where exposure and flow suit them. With repeated passage and deposition, even a feeding track may be cemented into relief. A record of hunger becomes part of the floor.

When a shear boundary approaches, the grazers stop. Some press themselves into hollows; others enter the sediment edge first until only their backs remain exposed. Many are too slow. Their torn bodies provide one of the irregular feasts around which the rest of the visible ecosystem briefly gathers.

The open water belongs, intermittently, to the sieves.

A single sieve is mostly aperture. Transparent lobes hold a filter so fine that it appears as a wrinkle rather than a net; its gut may be visible as a hair of gray smoke. Where the current supplies enough food, the animal needs to do little more than maintain its orientation. In stiller water, minute beating structures draw a feeble flow through the filter. When conditions worsen, the whole apparatus can fold close to the body.

At a current boundary, thousands of sieves gather because each finds the same advantage there. The resulting ribbon is not a colony. It has no common skin, no permanent membership, and no identity beyond the persistence of the water that feeds it. Seen end-on, it may disappear entirely. Seen obliquely, miles of empty water seem to acquire a faint, flexible surface.

Many sieves release their young in these gatherings. The ribbon is consequently both feeding ground and nursery, though it provides no shelter and no care. Smaller, thread-bodied hunters move through it, taking one animal at a time. They are almost as transparent as their prey and easiest to detect after feeding, when their guts cloud.

When the boundary weakens, the ribbon does not so much disperse as cease to be visible. Some sieves drift away singly. Others fold their filters and sink into slower water. Dead ones settle with their delicate structures collapsed, appearing on the floor as faint wrinkles until scavengers find them.

The long, pallid predators are shaped by the same scarcity, but at another tempo.

They have not merely learned to wait; waiting has become the ordinary condition of their bodies. Some lie along swells with their tails extended into hollows. Others rest with only the forepart exposed. Their eyes are small and of limited use. Pressure, vibration, and dissolved traces tell them more than the weak light ever could. A distant collapse may cause one to lift its head long before any visible sediment arrives.

The silt on their backs is not arranged as camouflage. They simply remain still long enough for it to gather. On the oldest and quietest individuals, that silt can become inhabited. Microorganisms film it, minute grazers cross it, and a few masons establish fragile crusts between the grains. The predator carries a small piece of floor. Its eventual movement is that community’s storm.

Feeding is usually a single contraction after a very long approach: a grazer passing too close, a wounded sieve sinking within reach, a lesser hunter grown careless near the bottom. Afterward, the predator may appear unchanged except for a thickening of the body and a darkening behind the head. It can then remain where it is through many further opportunities. When a great sieve ribbon forms, however, some of these animals leave their resting places. Their movement upcurrent is so slow that they seem less to approach than gradually to become nearer.

The scavengers that appear at a collapse are not all travelers.

The first have often been beneath the sediment all along. They are small, tough-bodied animals capable of reducing their activity almost to nothing: flattened forms that close themselves against the silt, smooth muscular ones that lie in burrows for long periods, soft cones with little more than a mouth and a means of retreat. The shock of shifting masonry, the chemistry of crushed bodies, and the exposure of fresh material wake them. Many travel only their own body length to reach the feast.

Others arrive later, carried along the disturbance or drawn from nearby refuges. They enter carcasses, strip filters from dead sieves, turn over newly deposited sediment, and consume the microbial growth spreading across broken surfaces. What they leave is not cleanliness but a finer distribution of remains: pellets, casings, fragments, and soft tissue already passing into the black sand.

A major collapse makes the whole ecology briefly legible.

First there is only the white suspension and the pressure of displaced water. Then the buried scavengers begin to emerge. Microbial floc gathers around fresh fractures. Grazers that survived uncurl or creep in from adjacent surfaces. Downstream, sieves thicken where the current carries food. The thread-hunters follow them, and beneath the hunters come the pallid lengths.

When the flow steadies, masons emerge from protected pores. Their pale seams cross fractured material, casings, and sometimes the remains of animals not yet entirely consumed. For a short period the ruin is crowded at every scale. Then the exposed surfaces age, the currents carry the floc elsewhere, and the gathering thins. Scavengers rebury themselves. Sieves fold and drift. Predators settle. The last conspicuous marks may be new grazing spirals; beneath them, construction has already resumed.

A sufficiently large fall can instead smother everything local. Then the first inhabitants are not survivors but drifting young and resting stages brought in afterward. Even these disasters export food. No event is purely destruction within the food web, though it may be final for every animal at its center.

The opening of an ice lead overhead produces no corresponding bloom. There is nothing green to answer the light. Its importance lies in changes to the brine, the movement of ice, and the currents that follow. Beneath the roof, dormant cells and the resting stages of small animals may lodge among crystals, but there is no rich ceiling fauna. At the rim, living things go over with the water: masons stripped from sills, folded sieves, grazing young, microbial films, and the contents of whole pores. None climbs back. The Basin continually leaks life into the freezing falls.

Because food arrives patchily and often as an event, nearly every lineage has some way of becoming less active. Microorganisms nearly stop. Masons withdraw into pores. Grazers bury themselves. Sieves close their filters. Scavengers wait in sediment. The largest predators simply continue waiting on a longer scale. Dormancy is not an emergency measure here; it is one of the principal ways of living.

From above, therefore, the Basin seems uninhabited because so much of its life has the scale of its texture. At close range, a wound can seem impossibly crowded because it has gathered lives that were previously separated by leagues of poor water. Both impressions are accurate.

A perfectly preserved Basin would eventually be a starvation. Its inhabitants persist because old surfaces are opened, grazed, crushed, suspended, and carried; because bodies become sediment and sediment becomes porous construction; because damage exposes what stillness conceals. They neither arrest the dissolution nor complete it. They live in the opportunities it creates.

The whole is built by animals, but no animal is its builder. Each occupies a pore, follows a boundary, empties a carcass, or waits beneath the silt. The arches, the ribbons, the sudden feasts, and the long quiet are what those lives look like when there are enough of them.
