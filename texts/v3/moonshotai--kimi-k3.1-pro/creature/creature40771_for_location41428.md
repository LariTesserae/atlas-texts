## The Inhabitants of the Brief Water

Nothing that lives in the cirque has made peace with cold so much as with interruption. Warmth is brief, water is movable, and shelter may be no more than the lee side of a leaf. Its inhabitants have become expert at two things: reducing themselves until the world can cover them, and recognizing the precise instant when it is safe to unfold.

The oldest residents are probably the cushion plants. A green hummock no wider than two hands may have been growing when the first cairn was built. Each year it adds a little to its rim and loses a little within. Its center is a dark compression of dead stems, trapped dust, old droppings, and roots—a sponge made from the plant’s own history. This interior holds moisture after the surrounding grit has dried. It also holds seeds. Sedges and mosses establish themselves in the body of the older plant, so that what appears from above to be one cushion is often a crowded succession of lives, some rising, some decaying, all sharing the same narrow purchase on stone.

The mosses can look ruined by night and be functioning again by midmorning. Frost makes them brittle; cloud-water restores them. Their black-green surfaces darken, soften, and begin the slow work of using the light. Around them, the yellow cups turn toward the sun, their curved petals gathering a little warmth around the seed organs. The garnet bells are warmer still within. A bee crawling into one may remain there after a cloud shadow has chilled the open mat, waiting in a space scarcely larger than its body. The white stars are less tolerant of wetting and often close before the cloud has fully crossed the lip.

The sideways shrubs are the slowest to forgive injury. Their resinous stems may creep for years before producing a hand’s length of woody growth. A climber’s single bite can remove a decade. Yet their old, half-dead branches are useful long after the leaves have gone: they trap windblown seed, anchor moss, and give beetles a dry refuge beneath the damp vegetation. Almost nothing here is merely itself. A plant is also a roof, a soil, a pantry, or a wall.

Beneath this living skin is another population, visible only when the water films are thick enough. Tiny translucent worms move between moss leaves. Mites and springtails graze on fungal threads and decomposing tissue. Creatures that seemed to be grains of debris unfold legs and resume feeding. For them, the distance between saturated moss and dry gravel is not a few finger-widths but the width of a desert. Many can endure being dried or frozen, but endurance is not activity. Their actual lives—the feeding, mating, and growing—are compressed into the intervals when the cirque briefly permits such things.

The black, furry bees take their cue from stone rather than air. A female may be motionless while the sun is already bright, then begin to stir when the slab beneath her has warmed. She basks, shivers her flight muscles, and makes her first short flight low over the mat. The flowers are so close together that her foraging can look like walking interrupted by hops. Pale pollen gathers on her legs; garnet dust sometimes marks her head.

She works alone. Her nest may be a chamber in sun-facing gravel or an abandoned recess near a hare burrow, its cells stopped with grains and resin. A season of flights becomes only a few provisions of pollen and nectar. She will not see the offspring that eventually emerge from them. They will feed, transform, and wait below ground through the long cold, while her own body is taken apart by weather and scavengers.

The copper beetles inherit much of what the other inhabitants leave unfinished: fallen petals, a damaged bee, the rich fragments around a hare’s latrine. They are most active on stone warm enough to sting a human hand. When the light fails, they disappear so completely that the mats seem never to have contained them. The midges, too, spend most of their lives out of sight, their young developing in the wettest moss. Their winged stage is brief. The loose threads above the moss are not aimless swarms but gatherings at the only warm, humid boundary where their bodies can remain active.

A cloud shadow closes all these doors at once. Bees back into bells or crevices. Beetles wedge themselves beneath stones. Midges settle. For a few minutes the cirque contains almost no visible animal movement, though it is densely occupied.

The rock-hares have made the largest alterations to this hidden world. Their burrows turn loose gravel into architecture: a resting chamber where the temperature changes slowly, a store of dry stems, a latrine, several approaches, and at least one exit that does not face the usual direction of danger. A burrow may be cooler than the sun-struck floor at noon and warmer than the air at night. The hares do not simply hide in the ground; they use it to choose among climates.

They gather stems when the plants are driest, sometimes leaving them on warm rock before carrying them below. Their stores are not lavish. A winter’s survival may depend on many small additions rather than one great heap. At dusk, the grass stuffed into an entrance breaks the wind and obscures the scent of the chamber without sealing it completely. A stopped hole is therefore a sign of occupancy, though not a reliable one; some entrances are maintained only as escape routes.

The young learn the floor as a network of refuges. Open gravel is something to cross, not somewhere to linger. Their pale backs take on the color of the stone, while the dark line along the spine breaks the outline of the body into less recognizable pieces. Even their feeding follows cover. They crop sedges near burrow mouths first, then venture along old runs to the richer patches enriched by their own droppings. In this way, their refuse becomes pasture, and their pasture gradually changes the distribution of green across the basin.

The ash-colored birds belong as much to the walls as to the floor. Their nests lie deep in clefts, cups of dry moss lined with hare fur and wool caught from the shrubs. They descend when insect activity is at its height, moving in quiet groups from one warm patch to another. Their single clicks seem insufficient to hold a flock together, yet the birds turn and rise almost as one. They take midges from the air, bees and beetles from the mats, and seeds when insects are scarce. In the hard season they leave. Not every life in the cirque is required to wait out its worst weather.

The split-hooved climbers are visitors on a still larger scale. The basin is one ledge among the many routes available to them, useful for its grazing and for the sheltered crossing it offers. They can cross without seeming to spend a thought on the plants beneath their hooves, but their passage has consequences. A cropped shrub, a patch of dung, a line of compressed gravel, a tuft of wool: each remains after the animals have gone. Hares later use the tracks. Birds steal the wool. The dung becomes a small, dark center of fertility.

The dun-colored hunter is not contained by the cirque either. Its range must extend far beyond the three walls, into country where enough prey exists to sustain it. The basin is a place it checks, a familiar room in a much larger territory.

It hunts the transitions. At dawn it examines burrow mouths and the places where several hare runs meet. At dusk it may lie still near a wall, letting the birds’ return to cover tell it where movement has been. Fresh grass plugs, a newly opened store, feathers caught below a cleft: these are more useful to it than a distant view across the glittering floor. Most of its visits leave nothing but prints. The kills are rare, but its influence is constant. It lives in the hares’ preference for short crossings, in the birds’ nearness to the walls, in the way every animal responds when a stone moves without wind.

Human life is more tenuous still. The people who built the low shelter did not make a home in the ordinary sense. They made a pause that could be reused. The cache suggests familiarity: work done while waiting for cloud to clear, a flake restruck, a tool hammered back into usefulness, cord handled until age or cold made it brittle. But nothing in the basin could have supported them for long. Food had to come from elsewhere or be taken quickly. Fuel had to be carried or gathered from animals. Even the shelter protected only a body already equipped to survive everything else.

If anything human can be said to live in the cirque, it is knowledge rather than a person: which cairn can still be trusted, which slope sounds hollow underfoot, where cloud tends to open, how to lie so that the semicircle takes the wind. Such knowledge exists only while someone carries it. When no one returns who possesses it, the shelter becomes stonework again, and the cairns become ambiguous heaps.

Winter does not empty the basin so much as reduce its address. The bees are below ground. Midge eggs and larvae remain in the moss. The smallest animals are dried, frozen, or folded into resistant stages. The hares draw on their stem stores and use the warmest hours sparingly. The cushions hold their living buds close to their old dead centers. Birds are absent; the hunter’s circuit lengthens. What looks like a lifeless shelf is a population contracted into seeds, cells, roots, burrows, and memory.

Seen in this way, the cirque is a place of vessels. A cushion holds water. Dead stems hold soil. A flower holds a bee through a cloud shadow. A burrow holds warmth. A store of grass holds an animal until morning. A dropping holds the beginning of another green patch. Even a cairn holds, weakly, the possibility of a direction.

Every vessel leaks. That is why the inhabitants seem so urgent when the light returns. Nothing can be kept for long in its useful form; it must be used, transformed, and passed on. Life persists here not by standing permanently against the mountain, but by fitting itself into the mountain’s momentary warmth—and by being ready, at any instant, to become almost nothing again.
