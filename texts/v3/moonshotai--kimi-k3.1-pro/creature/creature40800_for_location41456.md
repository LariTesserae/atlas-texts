The Lower Pumping Close is densely inhabited, but it has no population that could be counted door by door. Its residents do not correspond to the rooms, and many do not correspond to single visible plants. A fern-covered wall may be one old clone; a curtain of vine may belong to several separate rootings, or to one body that has reached the same ledge by different routes. Even the largest residents are only partly inside the cutting.

The oldest are the trees above the rim. Their trunks, branches, and most of their weather are elsewhere. Within the Close they exist chiefly as roots: a dim, inverted portion of a tree that elsewhere has leaves and seasons. A young root enters along a mortar joint, slender enough to follow its irregularities. It thickens, divides around an iron bracket, and meets another root descending from the same ledge. Where the two press together, they may join. Given enough time, the tree becomes architecture without ceasing to grow.

These roots do not simply support the district, nor simply break it. They do both at different ages. A root opens a joint; an older root becomes rigid across the opening it made; later growth stitches the whole region into a net. The cracked retaining arch has therefore not been repaired. It has been repeatedly inherited. Each new layer of living fibre includes the arch’s deformation among the conditions of its own growth.

The waxy vines are harder to separate into individuals. They do not merely hang from the rim. They root wherever a ledge holds enough leaf mould, send laterals along wet joints, and resume their descent from several points. What appears from below to be a curtain may be a net whose upper portion is out of sight. On some walls, the near-identical leaves and repeated patterns of growth mark the patient extension of a single clone.

The industrial lights have created peculiar local forms of that life. Around a bulkhead lamp, a vine can remain in a small, dependable day that never ends. Its leaves crowd close, glossy and regularly oriented, while the same plant farther along the wall may produce little but pale stem and root. A leaf beside the substation and a lightless root in the pump hall could belong to one body, though establishing the connection would mean dismantling much of the growth between them. The lamps are not bright enough to make gardens flourish. They are bright enough to make gardens gather.

The ferns map water more precisely than they map masonry. Their rhizomes follow the narrow conditions in which stone remains wet without being scoured: below a joint, beside a calcite seam, along the sheltered lip of an overflow. A change in seepage the width of a finger can brown one margin while another puts out new fronds. Spores settle widely, but few establish a genuinely new colony. Most new fern growth is old fern continuing.

The sedges and floating leaves lead more eventful lives. Water carries their fragments from gutter to channel and from channel to court. A stem caught by an intake screen may be torn free, transported a short distance, and lodged somewhere it can root. An entire patch in a lower drain may descend from such an accident. The revolving screens are the closest thing the Close has to grazing, except that nothing is consumed. Pruning and dispersal can occur in the same mechanical gesture.

Beneath all these visible residents live the decomposers. Their district is the apparent ground. With no animals to tear, swallow, or trample the fallen leaves, decay proceeds largely through fungal cords, bacterial films, saturation, and time. A leaf may remain recognisable long after it has fallen, darkening among old stems while fine threads enter its tissues. Water presses the remains into the root mat. The mat compacts, holds silt, and becomes firm enough to bear another generation of growth. The fungi seldom need to appear as mushrooms; their abundance is evident chiefly in the fact that centuries of intact leaf fall have not simply filled the lanes. Algae occupy another thin territory on glass and wet mineral surfaces, colouring the light before it can enter a room.

With so little arrival from outside, much of the Close’s ecology is biographical. The important events are extensions, severings, and inheritances. A vine uses the dead stem of an earlier vine as support. A fern colony dies at its oldest centre while its margins persist. A root obstructs the seam that once supplied it, and water opens another route nearby. There is competition, but it rarely resembles a struggle: one mat overshadows another by a frond’s width; one root takes space from another over decades. Often a plant’s principal obstacle is its own earlier growth. There are few clean beginnings and few complete endings.

The pumps stand outside this arrangement only in the narrowest sense. They do not grow or reproduce, but no account of the inhabitants can leave them out. Each machine maintains a climate. A motor housing keeps a margin of dry, curled leaves. A fan tears a ragged opening in the vines and keeps rewriting it. A lamp holds its glossy collar in place. The clean boundary around a flywheel is not an empty space but a line renewed at every revolution. Below the courts, the pumps’ small adjustments determine whether the root pavement remains merely saturated or becomes submerged.

This has not produced harmony. Roots still choke gutters, deform brackets, and prise masonry apart. Vines enter ventilator drafts and are shredded. A shifted channel can starve one colony while drowning another. Nor is the machinery benevolent: it prunes without pause, dries some tissues, and keeps others above a flood line they could never establish for themselves. The accommodation is made of failures that stopped short of finality. A bracket yields instead of tearing free; a root thickens around the yielded metal; a wall face holds. Growth reaches an intake, but not quite faster than the screen can clear it. No part maintains the whole. The whole is what has so far not come apart.

After long enough, it becomes reasonable to speak of the Close itself as the largest resident—not as a spirit concealed behind the greenery, and not necessarily as a mind, but as a body without continuous skin. Its living tissues are connected unevenly by roots, water, and contact. Its quickest responses belong to machines. Its memory is physical.

It remembers rain by the delayed darkening of a wall. It remembers an old route of overflow by a seam of silt and the ferns that followed it. It remembers a failing bracket as a bend in the metal and a root now thick enough to carry part of the load. These are not recollections. They are alterations to which later growth must respond.

If the Close knows the upper world, it knows it late and by proxy: weather as seepage, daylight as a weak green-grey change above the lower lanes, the seasons as slight differences in leaf fall. A footstep would matter less than a blocked culvert. A shout would disappear into the pump roar, and nothing would turn to investigate. Yet a failed lamp would gradually alter the leaves around it. A stopped fan would allow the vines to close its opening. A changed water level would redraw entire territories. The Close would not react dramatically. It would include the change.

At night, the inhabitants become hardest to distinguish. Above the cutting, the trees continue in darkness; below, their roots hold masonry. Fern margins rest against wet stone. Hidden decay works through intact leaves. Around the amber lamps, small gardens remain in a day that does not end. The pumps continue making their clearances and lifting water out.

The Close is not quite one organism. But it is no longer merely a structure with organisms inside it. Life has grown around the machinery, and the machinery has preserved conditions in which that life can persist. Down there, the distinction between resident and residence has become very old, very wet, and extremely difficult to find.
