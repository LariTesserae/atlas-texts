### The Seven Living Standards

Seven lives inhabit the Upper Standard, though *inhabit* is almost too spacious a word. They do not move through the valley, alter it, or make use of its distances. Each occupies a hand’s breadth of white pan and the hidden depth of its socket. The rest of the floor is not territory. It is separation.

They do not constitute a population, because nothing will join their number through birth or leave it through age. They are not quite a colony, because nothing connects them. Even *species* suggests too much: an ancestry behind them, variation among them, the possibility of descendants unlike themselves. They have no such depth in time. They are seven separately bounded acts of continuation.

Close up, a tuft has none of grass’s casual softness. Its dark filaments rise in a tense, narrow fountain, each blade composed of finer tubes nested and bound together. Their edges take the white panel light in colorless lines; their interiors remain almost black. They look wet but are not. They look as though they should chime or shiver under the acoustic load, yet their flexibility remains only a potential in the material. Nothing sways. Nothing turns toward the light. The seven shadows lie where they have always lain, sharp enough to seem part of the ceramic.

There is no sap within the tubes, no wet membrane holding a miniature weather of salts. Nothing in them needs to be a bag of water. Their organization is native to the valley’s dryness. Light is absorbed within their solid tissues; charge separates and migrates; mineral ions shift along pathways in the flexible matrix. At the roots, trace matter enters and bound products are withdrawn. There is no meal, no breath, no pulse of feeding followed by rest. Individual charges move, but no tide results. At visible scale, nothing happens. At molecular scale, nothing is allowed to happen for long.

That is the beginning of their aliveness.

A tuft is not merely matter that has been frozen in the correct shape. Its present organization is maintained from within. A displacement that would become weakness is answered before it can accumulate into damage. A depleted site is restored; a product that would obstruct further restoration is bound and carried rootward. There is no episode of repair because repair is not an episode. It is the ordinary texture of existence.

The constancy is therefore one of organization rather than of imprisoned substance. Matter may pass through without making the tuft larger, just as products may leave without making it lesser. Nothing is added to the pattern, and nothing is permitted to fall out of it. The standard is not the identity of every atom. It is the preservation of relations.

A crystal is stable without doing this work. A flame is sustained by flow without possessing the same internal reference to its own structure. The living standards lie uncomfortably between such comparisons. Each is a stationary state, but not a static object: an organized self spending energy so that its organization remains available to be spent upon.

They were manufactured, but manufacture only supplied their beginning. What keeps each tuft itself is no longer simply the tool that formed it.

The conditions of the cell are not hardships in the way they would be for an alpine plant. The cold is not winter. There is no water in the tissues to freeze, no dormant bud waiting for permission to open. The light is not day, and its constancy does not postpone a night. The roar is not a voice, a storm, or even a disturbance. It has always been the mechanical condition under which their chemistry is exact. Silence would not be peace to them; it would be a departure from specification.

There is no reason to suppose that they are conscious. They have no nervous tissue, no behavior from which to infer curiosity or preference. Yet it would also be too simple to say that nothing matters to them. A deviation matters in the most elementary way possible: their own organization answers it. Their relation to the world is not recognition but correction.

If they can be said to have a world at all, it is not the white pan, the black fins, or the other six tufts. It is the narrow difference between the state they occupy and the state they preserve. Because that difference is continually closed, even this world offers no narrative.

It is tempting to pity them, but pity easily turns them into ordinary grass with its future taken away. They are not necessarily lives of suppressed growth. No unmade seed aches within them. No branch exists as an intention. They have not been overtaken on the way to becoming forests, nor caught in some vegetable childhood and forbidden maturity. Growth and reproduction are not adventures they are prevented from having; they are absent from the organization they maintain. This does not make them less strange. It makes them more strictly themselves.

Their individuality is real, but thin. Each tuft maintains its own boundaries and could, in principle, cease while the other six continued. That is enough to make seven lives. Nothing passes between them to make a society. They exchange no signals through the gas, send no roots toward one another, and compete for nothing. Their spacing is not loneliness. Their resemblance is not communication.

The pale grains suspended above the pan are not their spores. Nothing hanging in the acoustic field is their unborn. There is no quiet reproductive drama in those incomplete terraces, no dispersal miraculously arrested on its way to colonization. The grains belong to the apparatus of position; the tufts belong to the narrower apparatus of self-preservation. Between them there is no relation at all.

Nor is there any less visible companionship. No mites live in the sockets, no fungal threads share the mineral exchange, no decomposers wait beneath the pan. There are no decomposers because nothing is permitted to become remains. The valley has not been emptied of an ecology. Its specification leaves no openings through which an ecology could begin.

In a functional sense, however, the tufts’ roots extend far beyond their sockets. Their existence reaches into power routes, preparation lines, recovery systems, and machinery whose wear occurs safely elsewhere. The tower laboriously surrounds seven small metabolisms with the conditions under which they need do almost nothing except remain organized. Still, this does not quite make the factory a vast body with seven green organs. The boundary remains meaningful. The tower supplies the terms. Within those terms, each tuft performs its own maintenance.

None of them is intrinsically deathless. They do not senesce, but they could fail. Deprive one long enough of energy or exchange, and its self-correction would stop. At first there might be nothing to see. The tubes would still stand. The green would still be green. There would be no wilt, no dramatic collapse, no brownness announcing the end. The shape would outlast the life. What had been an organism would remain, briefly, as an exceptionally faithful object.

That possibility reveals what the factory actually tends. It does not harvest their tissues or encourage their vigor toward some later abundance. Their yield is not growth. It is the continuing distinction between a green form that merely persists and a green form that is still preserving itself.

Everything enormous in the cell is a condition; everything alive in it is small. The cold has no self to lose. The roar, for all its pressure in bone and skull, has no organization it struggles to retain. The grains have no impulse toward another position. Only the seven tufts do work whose result refers back to the worker.

So the Upper Standard is inhabited, but nothing in it is waiting. It is not a garden paused before spring, nor a handful of survivors holding out against desolation. Its stillness is not the stillness of abandoned life. It is the stillness produced when living work is done with such exactness that no visible residue remains.

Seven dark-green interfaces stand in the white light, each maintaining a self without memory, company, or expectation. Their lives have no plot. They have only a present tense, and the present tense is *remain*.
