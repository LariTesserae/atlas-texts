The inhabitants are easiest to name by the spaces they complete.

A visitor calls the low, broad ones **pavement-backs**, the finger-thin ones **runners**, the long-limbed animals **spans**. Those held by the dense gas are **veils**; those fitted into walls and roofs are **tiles**. These are conveniences, not native names. Even “kind” must be used lightly. It suggests birth and descent, while the Deep shows only recurrent anatomies: the same solution appearing beneath one lamp and beneath another, with no young intermediate between the large and the small, and no remains after either.

The pavement-backs are the most numerous. Some are no wider than a hand; others could cover a table. Their plates are not identical, and the difference seems to matter. A narrow plate will fit beneath the raised edge of a neighbor’s shell, while a broader one takes the contact of several legs at once. When the animal flexes, its amber seams open in sequence rather than all together. The movement travels around the body and returns to the plate where it began. Within the widest opening, thin dark films slide across pale, gas-filled cavities. Nothing pulses like a heart. Nothing draws inward like a breath.

At intervals a pavement-back brings its mouth-parts against stone. The hard, spoon-shaped pieces close delicately over a crystal edge. There is no scrape, no powder, no fragment swallowed. Afterward the crystal is unmarked. The gesture resembles grazing until watched closely; then it seems more like listening with the teeth. Sometimes a plate shifts as the mouth closes. Sometimes a runner on a neighboring shell stops at the same instant. Whether either movement answers the other is impossible to establish.

Runners move across this living pavement without making it a road.

Each is a narrow succession of joints. Some of its limbs end in points fine enough to enter the seams between plates; others carry dry brushes so delicate that they seem to blur in lamplight. A runner will draw one brush across shell, another across mineral, then pause with its body spanning the join. Nothing adheres to the bristles. It is not cleaning the shell, gathering food, or laying down a trail. It may be comparing two surfaces. It may be completing some brief closure between them.

Their routes are elaborate but never become established. A runner may cross the same shell twice and take entirely different limbs over its edges. Another may reverse without turning, simply making the former front of its body the rear. The black pits along its sides do not follow a carried light. Only contact interrupts it: a boot placed too near, a finger’s electrical texture, the minute pressure of clothing against stone. Then the runner folds into a line no thicker than a cord, waits until the disturbance has been accommodated, and goes elsewhere.

Spans make rooms beneath themselves.

Their legs are so long and unequal that at first they look deformed. In fact each limb has found a particular contact. One narrow foot is wedged into a fracture; another rests across the shells of three pavement-backs; a third reaches far sideways to a tilted plate. The body remains almost perfectly horizontal above these negotiations. Its underside is ribbed, with shallow grooves in which runners occasionally settle.

When one of the animals beneath a span moves, the span adjusts several legs by nearly invisible amounts. The covered space remains intact. Smaller creatures pass under it, rest there, or leave. The span does not defend them, gather them, or respond differently when they are gone. It would shelter a stone as readily. The shelter is real, but nothing about it requires kindness.

Veils inhabit the provinces where the gas will bear them.

Their bodies are narrow frameworks from which dry membranes open in ribs. They do not beat these membranes as wings. A slight change in curvature is enough to alter their attitude; a succession of such changes turns them edge-on, then broadside, then returns them to their original inclination. In lamplight the membranes are smoke-gray or colorless, crossed by darker supports. Their inner lattices show through as fine, empty-looking cells.

Several veils may occupy one volume without becoming a flock. They maintain distances, but not formations. If one folds, others at different levels need not respond. When a traveler’s approach presses the gas against them, a veil may close to a narrow spindle and move aside the width of a finger. Later it opens again. The response has the economy of a balance recovering, not the haste of an animal escaping.

The tiles are easier to overlook. Their exposed plates follow the planes of a fracture so closely that only a regular amber line distinguishes a cluster of animals from a mineral seam. Along vertical walls they fit edge to edge; on roofs they overlap in broad, shallow scales. Dry hooked limbs brace them from within the cracks. When a tile shifts, it does not come loose. It exchanges one exact contact for another.

A tile small enough to cover with a fingernail is not an infant. A pavement-back no larger than a runner is not a miniature adult in any developmental sense, because there is no development toward which it could be tending. Size is simply one of the dimensions by which the available spaces are filled.

More difficult to interpret are the hooked animals called **cradles**. A cradle holds another creature against its underside, sometimes for longer than any visitor can remain to watch. The smaller animal may be almost entirely enclosed. Its brushes continue to move, so it is not inert, but it neither grows nor struggles. Occasionally an embrace has been seen to end. The hook opens, the smaller animal walks or climbs away, and the two do not follow one another.

Protection, parasitism, gestation: each word predicts an outcome that never arrives. The relation may be no more preparatory than the contact between two mineral faces.

All these animals are most elaborate at the boundaries of themselves. Their sensory pits, brushes, mouth-parts, and flexible seams gather where another body or the stone can be touched. There is often no clear head. A bulb crowded with brushes may look like one until a touch at the opposite end produces the first response. A dry tick in one shell may be answered by a click in another, but no observer has found a code in the exchanges. Each answer can also be explained as pressure passing through contact.

Anatomically, the individuals are distinct. In behavior, their boundaries are less certain. A group of pavement-backs may redistribute a displacement with the coherence of a single body. A span and the runners beneath it may move independently for days. At no point does the assembly need to decide whether it is one animal, several animals, or a temporary organ of the chamber.

Their relations are therefore real without being services. Runners do not keep pavement-backs clean. Spans are not fed by what shelters beneath them. Veils do not pollinate anything, and cradles do not bring anything into existence. Take away hunger, and a great deal of intricacy remains: fitting, bracing, touching, spacing, opening, closing. An assembly can be complicated without being an economy.

Only the rare broad-palmed animals seem, at first, to promise a person.

Visitors call them **palmers**. Most have low, crouching bodies and forelimbs that can fold almost completely beneath a domed shell. The palms are wide and dry, ringed with sensory pits. Their digits are of unequal length and can move separately, or flatten together until the whole hand resembles another body-plate. They can close around the haft of a stone flake without fumbling. They can fit a finger between the teeth of the mineral comb. Their hands appear made for precision, though precision here has no visible product.

One palmer holds a worked point against a wall. Another sits within a hood of four slabs, its palms pressed lightly against opposite sides, though the slabs need no support. A third curves its fingers around an old tool and then, over a long period, relaxes them without letting the object fall.

The posture looks intentional until watched long enough. Then intention begins to look like the observer’s impatience.

The point does not cut. The comb does not abrade. No groove is deepened, no cord added, no slab brought into a new alignment. Sometimes a hand moves by a fraction and returns. The worked edge remains as crisp as before. Nor are palmers invariably associated with the artifacts: other dextrous animals have been found holding them, and some palmers stand empty-handed with their fingers curved around nothing visible.

It may be that the artifacts had makers whose actions once formed a sequence. It may be that their manufacture is simply outside anything a traveler can witness. Or the error may lie in separating the object from the posture of using it. A span is not complete without the space beneath it; a tile is unintelligible without its fracture. In the same way, the hafted flake may include the palm around it, the comb the seam its teeth almost enter, the braided line the two needles and the width of emptiness between.

A tool need not always be a means of altering something else. Here it may be a completed relation.

This does not make the palmers secretly human. They leave no work half finished, keep no material beside them, and carry nothing toward another task. But it also does not make them mindless. They have alternatives. A disturbed contact may be answered in several ways, each precise, none obviously preferable. Their stillness is not the stillness of a mechanism with only one position left to assume.

If they remember, their memory does not present itself as recollection. If they communicate, no news survives the exchange. If they intend anything, intention never becomes visible as a plan.

A traveler, by contrast, arrives almost entirely composed of next things: the next breath, the next meal, the next chamber, the next weakening of the light. The inhabitants neither oppose these needs nor make provision for them. A runner tests the unfamiliar surface of a boot and steps aside. A veil folds at the pressure of a passing body. No palmer turns toward a spoken name. A shout may pass through a chamber as a brief closing of seams and a small adjustment of membranes; then the stone absorbs it.

When the traveler leaves, there are no tracks by which the encounter can be retold. For a time, bodies have accommodated an additional pressure. Then they accommodate its removal.

In the darkness after the lamp has gone, a pavement-back’s plates pass with a dry tick. Somewhere above it, a membrane opens and returns. A palmer’s fingers remain fitted around the haft, the stone point resting against the wall.

It neither cuts nor puts the flake away.

The gesture is not interrupted. It is whole as it is.
