For most things living in the Low Saltern, the important distinction is not between land and water, but between one water and another. A pan may be too hot, too saline, too deep, or too briefly wet; a channel a few metres away may support a crowded food web. The basin’s inhabitants therefore occupy an archipelago of narrow conditions. Even the people are mostly rotational, and many of the animals are visitors. The nearest approach to permanence is often dormancy: cysts in a dry margin, dormant cells in a flakes of film, a spider deep in the pipework, a bat roost used every year though the building beneath it has no other use.

At the base of the visible abundance are the microbial films. In the tolerable pans they form exceedingly thin, layered communities at the surface of the sediment and within the wet pores of the mineral crust. Some use light; others live on chemical transformations in oxygen-poor brine and mud. Their different metabolisms give the shallows their ochre, wine-dark, and nearly black staining. They make submerged concrete slick and lend certain sheltered sumps a faint sulphurous smell when disturbed.

The films are not indestructible. Drying can leave them brittle and apparently lifeless; a strong change in salinity can strip a margin back to bare mineral. But where even a little suitable moisture remains, they can darken and spread again after rain or flooding. Brine-fly larvae graze and rework them, while shrimp filter suspended material from the water above. Much of the animal life in the Saltern depends on these films, though a visitor could cross a causeway without recognizing them as anything more than stains.

The hottest intakes and strongest crystallizing brines remain outside this economy. There, survival is sparse and highly specialized at best. Life thickens along gradients rather than across whole pans: near a mixing gate, along a cooling margin, in the upper few centimetres of water, or where a film receives both light and regular wetting. A small change in level can move the habitable band several metres without moving it anywhere on a map.

Brine shrimp are the most numerous easily visible animals in the suitable pans, but their numbers are not stable. Their cysts persist through drying that would kill the swimming animals. When water and salinity become favourable, the first tiny swimming stages appear; if conditions hold, the population builds until clear water acquires a cloudy, reddish grain. If conditions do not hold, the pan may return to apparent emptiness while another generation waits in the dry margin.

Workers who have spent enough time there sometimes speak of a pan “coming on.” The phrase refers to more than shrimp. First the water changes in appearance, then birds begin arriving at particular depths, then predators gather where pumping concentrates the animals. At a sluice, shrimp collect in seams and eddies too dense for the current to disperse immediately. Small fish take those swept into the seawater channels. Waders work the shallower margins. When the gate setting changes, the whole arrangement can dissolve within minutes.

The brine flies carry much of this production into the air. Their larvae remain below, feeding within the films, but the adults make the pans briefly continuous with the space above them. A heavy hatch becomes a kind of weather. It enters clothing, collects in the corners of windscreens, and turns the sunny sides of signs dark. Although the flies do not bite, they make eating outdoors tiresome and breathing near a dense emergence something best done slowly.

The operations staff learn small precautions: cover a mug, keep the canteen door closed, avoid standing downwind of a hatching pan. Instrument glass is wiped clear with a finger or cloth, often without the worker looking away from a gauge. The clear patch clouds again. Afterwards, dead flies lie with pupal cases and shrimp remains in windrows, where they are eaten, wetted, dried, and gradually incorporated into the mineral deposits.

Not all the predators are conspicuous. Beetles hunt through the shallows and along wet stones, taking larvae and weakened animals. Crabs occupy the culverts more permanently than fish occupy the pans. Each cool recess beneath a slab may hold a crab that knows a limited circuit of shade, submerged refuge, and feeding margin. They emerge when pumping strands prey or when dusk lowers the glare. Around some culverts, dragged remnants and fine tracks in damp sediment reveal activity that is almost invisible during the day.

Fish are chiefly animals of the mixing system. They thrive where seawater, depth, and flow provide refuge from the extremes of the pans. They gather below gates when food is being delivered to them and retreat when temperature or salinity shifts. Their apparent sudden arrivals are often only movements within a network they already know. The birds understand this network in their own way; certain outfalls are rarely without watchers when pumping has been steady.

The spiders belong more to the structures than to the water. They occupy pipe-bridge angles, the undersides of walkways, lamp brackets, and sheltered spaces behind instrument housings. Their webs quickly become grey with dust and mineral deposition. During a fly hatch, some are so full of prey that they look like old netting rather than silk. The spiders repair what remains useful and abandon the rest. In lean periods their presence contracts to crevices and less exposed bridges; when the flies return, the webs appear again with surprising speed.

Birds convert these local events into movements visible across the whole basin. Their numbers are less a census than a map of current water levels. Long-legged waders take the deeper feeding grounds, while smaller birds work the wet crust and narrow margins for larvae, pupae, and other prey. Birds that take fish concentrate near channels and outfalls. Gulls distribute themselves more opportunistically among dikes, roofs, feeding grounds, and the occupied block, where they have learned the times and locations associated with food waste.

Some birds are present for only part of the year. Others pass through in numbers that make the notion of a resident population almost meaningless. A drawdown can turn an empty pan into a feeding ground and then, as it becomes too shallow or too saline, empty it again. The birds do not need the whole basin to remain suitable. They need enough suitable places within flying distance, and the works continually provide and withdraw them.

A smaller number breed there. Gulls and some ground-nesting birds use isolated dikes and undisturbed roof areas, making scrapes that are easily overlooked among stones and crust. Their eggs resemble the ground closely enough that the first warning may be an adult rising almost underfoot. During nesting, vehicle routes and roof repairs sometimes acquire small, unofficial exclusions. These accommodations are not always successful. The city was not arranged around eggs, and an operational decision elsewhere can still flood a dike or leave a feeding pan dry.

The bats are more closely tied to particular buildings. Different roof spaces offer different degrees of warmth and shelter, and the animals shift within them as conditions change. A roost may be betrayed by guano, a dry smell, or faint movement heard from below long before anyone sees the bats themselves. Some roof repairs are postponed when a known roost is occupied; elsewhere, bats simply endure the human habit of opening and closing buildings without regard for their preferences.

At dusk they leave for the channels, hatching pans, and lamps. The lamps are not deliberately placed as feeding stations, but that is what some become. Insects gather in the illuminated air, and the bats pass through it in repeated, economical circuits. When hatches are poor, they range farther and certain roosts become quieter. Their absence does not necessarily mean that a roost has been abandoned. Like much else in the Saltern, it may only be waiting for another season of abundance.

The human inhabitants live most thinly and affect the conditions most widely. The operating staff includes pumpkeepers, assayers, fitters, electricians, harvester drivers, storekeepers, cooks, and rail personnel when freight requires them. Some remain for years; others know the place only as a rotation. At any one time the staff is small enough that an unfamiliar vehicle at the occupied block is noticed immediately.

Their settlement is less a village than a shift with sleeping quarters. Rooms designed for families hold single workers or stores. The canteen provides nearly all the public life: meals, arguments, radio, shift information, and the occasional presence of someone simply sitting where other people are awake. Washing dries stiff. Food and small comforts arrive from outside. Nothing about the basin permits a garden, and there is little incentive to pretend otherwise.

Not every worker becomes interested in the animals. Flies foul equipment, gulls enter stores, spiders appear in electrical housings, and birds can make a roof repair unexpectedly complicated. Yet practical knowledge accumulates. A pumpkeeper may not know the names of the waders but knows that their arrival at a particular pan means the drawdown has reached feeding depth. A driver knows which outfall will have gulls after a change in pumping. An electrician knows which sheltered bridge will be thick with webs by late season. Notes about flies, birds, or roosts occasionally appear beside pressures and salinities in maintenance records, not as formal ecology but because they help predict what will be crowded, slippery, or fouled next.

This knowledge does not make the staff sentimental custodians. If the works require a pan to be flooded or dried, they generally flood or dry it. But small accommodations accumulate where they cost little: a repair delayed, a vehicle sent around a nesting dike, a roost left undisturbed in a building with no urgent use. The animals live under a regime that neither protects them consistently nor ignores them entirely. Human maintenance is one of the basin’s ecological forces, alongside heat, rain, and evaporation.

Nothing has reclaimed the Saltern in the usual sense. There are no roots opening the paving and no progression towards soil and scrub. Its ecology remains industrial and reversible, built from films, cysts, hatches, migrations, scavenging, and roosts. A change in water grade can erase one crowded layer and create another elsewhere. The people persist by continually adjusting those grades; almost everything else persists by taking advantage of the adjustments.

From inside the occupied block, it can still feel like a nearly empty posting. From the waterline, it is a city of immense numbers and very short addresses: this culvert, that roof angle, the upper water of one pan, the lee of one dike for the duration of a hatch. Most of its inhabitants could not survive the Saltern as a whole. They live, precisely and abundantly, in the parts that briefly permit them.
