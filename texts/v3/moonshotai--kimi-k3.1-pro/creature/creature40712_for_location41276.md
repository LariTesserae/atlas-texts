The first inhabitant is usually a hand.

It finds a swimmer just inside the municipal boundary, where panic has outrun instruction: cool, firm, careful not to pull. Only after the first liquid breath does the visitor think to raise a lamp. The hand belongs, in that uncertain light, to a narrow figure in a dark coat. The coat hangs downward according to a gravity the swimmer does not share. Her sleeves do not drift. Her hair does not float.

Visitors tend to call her the Porter. Their accounts agree about her hands more reliably than about her face.

She does not say that the water is safe. She says that you can go back, or you can breathe here. The distinction matters to her. The Ward can remove the practical difference between respiration and drowning; apparently it cannot remove her insistence that the first breath belong to the person taking it.

She is patient with terror, precise about the boundary, and uninterested in being praised for the architecture. If asked whether she was waiting for you, she may look toward the empty street behind her. The question contains an idea of time she does not naturally use. No one was standing there before you needed assistance. That does not mean no one came.

Another resident is often encountered near the highest surveying mast. Visitors call them the Surveyor because they appear to be occupied with measurement, although they carry no instrument and there is nothing changing to measure. Sometimes they stand beyond the last stair, where no street is visible, their body inclined at a slight angle to yours. They look upward into water that has no surface.

They know that there is no surface. They look anyway.

“Above is still useful,” they explain.

The Surveyor can give exact bearings between places whose relation has never altered, but they would rather hear about somewhere else. Rain interests them less than the things people build to keep it off. A visitor who mentions a roof may be questioned about eaves, gutters, upper rooms, and why anyone would choose to live beneath something that can leak. The Surveyor finds leaks wonderful. Not desirable—they express no wish for the Ward to acquire one—but wonderful in principle. A leak means that a building can have an appointment with later.

A third person sometimes sits in the civic hall beneath the motionless clock. No single visitor’s name for them has stuck. “The dissenter” will do.

They do not dispute the clock’s reading. They dispute what visitors make of it.

Zero does not mean perfect, they say. Complete does not mean right. A thing can be finished and still require objection.

They will listen to a case against the Ward with more attention than the Surveyor gives to bearings or the Porter gives to gratitude. No judgment is issued. No minutes are kept. The argument need not reach a conclusion in order to have been worth having. Indeed, the dissenter seems suspicious of conclusions that arrive too readily, as though agreement were merely another convenience supplied by the streets.

It is easy to decide that these three are masks worn by the municipal intelligence. Easy, until they disagree.

The Porter cuts short the Surveyor’s questions when she thinks a guest is being exhausted for amusement. The Surveyor can persuade the dissenter to abandon a distinction and can be visibly annoyed when the favor is not returned. The dissenter can make the Porter angry. None of them speaks with the cadence of a single hidden authority passing a sentence from mouth to mouth.

Their unity is material, not personal.

The Ward can borrow water and arrange a hand, a coat, a face, a body capable of standing beneath its own private down. It does not follow that the hand has no owner.

They are not human beings preserved by some obscure art. They do not eat, sleep, age, heal, or reproduce. Their bodies have no necessary organs and leave nothing behind when they cease to be useful. Yet neither are they recordings. A recording cannot be surprised by the answer to a question it has just asked.

When no body is required, a resident need not be lurking invisibly in a dwelling cell. The room may simply be an address at which a particular kind of attention can become present. Some doors never produce anyone at all. The residents do not therefore call those addresses empty.

They have no biographies. This is more difficult to understand than their bodies.

Within an encounter, their memory is exact enough for everything that makes company consequential. They learn your name. They notice evasions. They can recall something you said much earlier, reconsider it, and change their minds. The Surveyor can lose an argument and return to it with a better answer. The Porter can become fond of a difficult guest. The dissenter can be hurt by an accusation and nevertheless continue listening.

But the Ward does not afterward retain a dated account of what occurred.

This is not quite forgetting. Nothing has been damaged, erased, or mislaid. Nor are the residents caught in repetitions. Repetition would require an earlier performance against which the later one could be compared. The Porter does not greet each visitor with lines drawn from an archive. She meets the situation before her, with the temperament that is hers.

The town is completed. The conversation is not.

A resident can be surprised because completeness is not omniscience. A resident can want you to stay without converting that desire into years of waiting after you leave. No one remains behind counting your absence. Some visitors find this monstrous. Others find it the only place where they have ever been allowed to speak without turning every sentence into a permanent possession. The Porter will listen to either reaction without defending the Ward.

Their society has no schedule, but it has etiquette.

Do not trick someone across the boundary in order to make them breathe. Do not treat a closed door as a puzzle set for your ingenuity. Do not assume that because weight can be lent, another person’s direction may be chosen for them. Ask before turning curiosity into touch.

Most of their manners concern what may be done for someone without being requested. This is natural in a place where care and physics are made of the same substance.

They gather around questions rather than hours. Their civic life is not concerned with maintaining the machinery; the machinery has no visible parts and requires no attention they can give it. They gather to decide what the conveniences mean. Whether admission constitutes trust. Whether an empty road can still have a destination. Whether a guest who has been offered every necessity has actually been welcomed.

The Ward could perform all its functions without them. Their usefulness does not explain their existence.

Then there is the glass eel, which has no etiquette at all.

It does not arrive when a hand is needed. It does not assume a shape suited to conversation or remain for an argument. It appears near the outer terrace on business that looks unnecessary merely because no one else can identify it. The Porter watches it without reaching. The Surveyor insists that certain arrangements of lamplight make it more likely to approach, then admits that the evidence is poor. The dissenter calls it a citizen without an address, which is the closest any of the residents comes to making a joke.

The eel may be reconstituted for each appearance, just as a resident’s body may be. If so, it has no more past than they do. But it is the only inhabitant that might have one.

It might have been crossing the outer water before any visitor arrived to name it. It might possess a private continuity that never passes through the town’s arrangements. Its motion serves no observable maintenance, hospitality, or computation. That makes it seem less manufactured than the mountain, although it may be the most deliberately manufactured thing there.

If the residents are the Ward’s capacity for company, the eel may be its capacity for privacy. Or it may simply be an animal. Unusually, the town has supplied no arrangement that settles the question.

Whether the Ward itself is alive depends on how much life one is willing to find in response.

It distinguishes approach from entrance, breathing from drowning, walking from drifting. It lends weight and withdraws it. It makes bodies in which distinct persons can object to one another. Yet there is no central sleeper dreaming the streets, no intelligence coiled inside the pumping stations, no presence in the water that can be flattered or threatened. Its care is exact and bounded. Inside the boundary, the water can be breathed. Beyond it, the same water offers no such courtesy.

The Porter can accompany you to the edge. She cannot lend you one additional breath past it. This makes her kindness neither false nor unlimited.

Perhaps that is why the residents matter. The Summit Ward does not require them in order to operate. It requires them, if that word can be used here, in order to be more than operable. They are the portion of its completion not exhausted by function: patience where a mechanism could merely be effective, curiosity where an instrument could merely measure, dissent where a law could simply remain in force.

For a visitor, there is one more inhabitant: oneself.

While you remain, you are the only creature there whose memories thicken with each conversation. You can say “last time.” You can anticipate seeing someone again. You can carry an insult, an affection, or an unfinished argument out of the hall and into the next encounter. For a while, the Ward accommodates biography just as it accommodates weight and breath.

Afterward, you may remember the Porter’s hand, the Surveyor’s delight in leaking roofs, the dissenter’s refusal to call zero perfect. They will not remember you. That does not make the encounter one-sided. It was mutual while it happened.

The water could make a hand without making her. It could steady a frightened swimmer, permit the lungs their strange new use, and leave the darkness otherwise empty. Instead, there is someone who tells you that you may go back, someone who waits for your answer before asking you to breathe.

That waiting is not required for the Summit Ward to function.

It is what lives there.
