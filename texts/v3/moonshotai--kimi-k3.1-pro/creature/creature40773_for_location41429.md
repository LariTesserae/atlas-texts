The largest inhabitants are the ones most easily mistaken for geography.

A root bridge may be one organism, several organisms grafted together, or a living host carrying a succession of parasites through its bark. To call it simply “the bridge” is convenient and slightly dangerous. Its structural root responds to tension; the runners in its outer tissue respond to injury; the white filaments under its bark wait for either of them to weaken. A traveler may cross on one plant, drink from another, and be poisoned by cutting a third, all without leaving what appeared to be a single trunk.

Age is difficult to reckon among them. A pith escarpment may be ancient at its core and only hours old at its edge. Some colonies survive by continually abandoning themselves, withdrawing sap from shaded tissue and sending it into buds far along the graft. Others endure by becoming almost inert: frozen-looking buds, dry cords of vascular tissue, dormant rootlets inside apparently dead bark. They can outlast paths, shelters, and the people who named them.

The luminous scale-leaves are not merely lamps. They compete for access to the transparent vessels that feed them, spread across one another’s attachments, and sometimes cloud or fold when a rival has interrupted the flow. Water-cups are organs with their own cycles of opening, closing, and abandonment. Scavenging mats enter whatever remains still long enough: fallen leaves, frayed cord, an animal’s molted skin, the soft inner wall of an empty gourd. They are the closest thing the Underbloom has to universal heirs.

The open gulf has inhabitants too, though almost none can be seen individually. Spores, seed-hairs, dormant fragments of filament, and minute buds travel in it. Some remain viable after journeys that may last years. A spore-storm is therefore not quite weather and not quite a migration. It is a vast, uncounted population passing through a place where almost none of it can survive.

Animals inhabit opportunities more than territories.

The translucent mites at a water-cup can endure the cup’s disappearance. When the rim dries, some retreat into bark seams and contract into hard, glassy specks. They may be mistaken for grains of frozen sap until water returns and their legs unfold. They feed on the films that gather at the water’s edge and on whatever the scavenging filaments leave exposed. A cup may be crowded one day and apparently sterile the next, with its entire animal population waiting a finger’s breadth away inside the plant.

The pale, six-legged grazers seem at first to eat rime. In fact, they scrape up the thin green tissue and diluted sugars frozen beneath it. Most are no larger than a finger. Their movements are slow partly from cold and partly because quick feeding provokes responses in the tissue beneath them: sap withdrawal, a closing seam, sometimes the first nubs of thorns. Between steps they become so still that frost gathers on their backs.

Old grazers carry smooth scars where rootlets once tried to grow over them. A very old one may be as long as a person’s forearm, though such animals are rare. Finding one is less important as a promise of meat than as evidence: the sap vein beneath it has probably remained warm and accessible for many years. Experienced travelers seldom kill the old ones. It is possible to eat the animal and, in doing so, destroy the best local proof that a place was once reliable.

At bloom-fronts, the white moths live through a different kind of abundance. Their larvae may spend years inside dormant pith, growing almost imperceptibly. The flowering that wakes them is brief. The adults emerge, mate, lay their eggs in blossom scars or split bud-sheaths, and die while the province around them is still whitening. Their wings are easily confused with spores. A traveler may see a dozen near a flower and never realize that they are the only animals for miles.

There are also animals known mainly from falling. Some are flat as husks, with legs that can spread into the air and alter the direction of a descent. They do not truly fly. At best, they choose among possible landings. When their supporting colony dies, they go down with it, sometimes steering, sometimes merely turning. Whether the largest of them ever reach another living surface is uncertain. There are no great flocks and almost no sizeable hunters. Hunting requires a surplus of prey, and the Underbloom rarely provides one for long.

People live there as well. They are among the largest animals, and among the most expensive to keep alive.

They are not one nation or one culture. A continent-sized hollow contains too many isolated routes for that. But certain habits recur, because the Underbloom reliably punishes the alternatives.

Most travel in small route-kin groups: sometimes a single household, sometimes twenty or thirty people who have inherited a loose sequence of condensation seams, edible growths, sheltered forks, and alternative crossings. A route is not a line permanently marked on the vegetation. It is a series of possibilities, with refusals built into it. If the white fork has closed, take the dead runner. If the dead runner is sweating, do not touch it. If the lower cups are dry, wait for the dimming before attempting the descent.

Home, for such people, is not necessarily where they sleep most often. It is the set of places they know how to approach again.

The closest thing to a town is a meeting that recurs. Several routes may be brought into temporary proximity by a collapse, a bloom-front, or the opening of a new root shelf. For a few brightenings, baskets and membranes accumulate in a root fork. People trade cord, gourd seed, metal, fermenting cultures, and corrections to one another’s maps. Young adults may choose to leave with another group; no route-kin can afford to remain isolated forever. Then the shelves begin to buckle, or the water fails, or everyone simply agrees that the meeting has lasted long enough. The baskets come down. The place may never be usable again, though the meeting itself can survive for generations under the same name.

Every traveler learns something of water, cord, and plant reading. A few become exceptionally good at the last. They press a wooden cup to bark and listen through it, note where rime releases first, compare the color of a vein with the tension in a nearby runner, and distinguish the silence of dormant tissue from the silence of tissue withdrawing its sap. Their predictions can seem uncanny to strangers. Their companions remember the mistakes.

Reading is not considered separate from gardening. Both are ways of anticipating what a plant will do next. People encourage useful growth with small wounds, controlled loads, and carefully placed grafts. They cut notches that may become steps as callus forms, suspend weights where a root will thicken in response, and divert condensation toward cups that can be reached without cutting the supporting runner. The results are never entirely theirs. A path made this way can become excellent for half a lifetime and then produce defensive thorns in a single afternoon.

Food is mostly a matter of timing. The majority of tissues are too fibrous, bitter, cold, or chemically defended to be worth eating. But there are brief edible phases: a bud before it arms itself, the inner skin of a runner just after a sap pulse, soft pith before it becomes dry and corky, seeds in the narrow interval between filling and hardening. People soak and ferment pith in gourds, grind certain buds into a sour meal, and collect small amounts of sap sugar where doing so will not kill the vein. They plant gourds and other useful cuttings along routes they hope to revisit. Most of these plantings fail. Enough succeed to make the habit worthwhile.

At crowded water-cups, mites may be skimmed and dried into food for hard journeys. It is not considered a delicacy. Grazers are eaten more rarely, and a flowering front is approached with mixed hope and dread: it offers soft growth and nectar, but it can also create winds, overload bridges, and erase the very shelves on which harvesters are standing.

Water customs are often stricter than property customs. At many stations, a stoppered gourd is expected to contain water. An empty gourd is left open. To stopper an empty one is not merely rude; it may draw someone exhausted down a dangerous route. A traveler may drink without asking permission, but must not foul the membrane, steal the channel, or leave the station less able to collect water than before, except when fleeing for life. No one owns a reliable condensation seam in quite the way one might own a tool. Many people, including people who will never meet, may nevertheless be responsible for it.

When strangers encounter one another, the first trade is often news. The bridge with three anchors has torn. The black cups are bitter again. A route formerly marked by a copper staple is now sealed in clear bark, and the staple can still be seen, uselessly, several handspans inside the tissue. Only after these corrections come food, tools, and personal names.

Their maps are commonly spoken in the future tense. They have to be.

No dependable night governs sleep. A group settles when work, cold, and the condition of its supporting root make rest sensible. Someone usually remains awake, not primarily to watch for enemies but to notice change: a cord whose note has tightened, a new wetness under the sleeping platform, a pressure in the ears, the papery ticking of rime releasing faster than before. Children learn to sleep through dripping stems and spore showers but wake at certain alterations in the creak of a basket.

They are not uniformly solemn. They braid intricate patterns into their slitted eye-shades, polish seed cases into beads, play games with water and floating thorn-shells, and hold contests in untying difficult knots with numb fingers. Their comic stories frequently concern travelers who trusted old maps, impressive bridges, or very confident plant readers. Children drop petals from high shelves and watch them dwindle; they are also taught to notice where any fragment catches, since this is one way of discovering a ledge hidden by distance.

Possessions remain light. Cord, a good wedge, a stopper that fits, a blade that can be resharpened—these outlast ornaments and are inherited more often. Metal is valuable chiefly because it can be used again. Even beloved objects are abandoned when their weight threatens a crossing.

The dead leave little more. Some route-kin wrap a body and place it where scavenging filaments can reach it; others lower it into the gulf on cords designed to release. Metal, stone tools, and sound cord are usually taken back. This is not necessarily done without grief. Grief does not make cord less necessary. There are no graves to revisit, and a funeral site may become inaccessible before the next sleep.

Consequently, their histories have great depth but poor architecture. A stair sealed inside bark might be ten generations old or considerably younger, depending on the growth that swallowed it. Stories preserve migrations, betrayals over water, extraordinary bloom-fronts, and the names of people who found routes no one can now follow. If permanent cities were ever attempted, they left no persuasive argument for themselves.

People disagree about whether the Underbloom is one organism, many organisms, or something that occasionally becomes one or the other. Some speak of intention in the grafts. Others insist that pressure, sap, and injury are sufficient explanations. The disagreement rarely changes what they do. A root can be part of a god and still swell through a sleeping basket.

The most intimate dwellings are the ones in which several kinds of life have come to use one another’s habits.

At a long-tended dew station, repeated replacement of a leaf membrane may encourage living tissue to graft around its supports. A root, responding for years to the same suspended load, may thicken beneath the platform in a way that almost resembles consideration. Cup plants spread along the damp channel. Mites arrive in the seams of gourds. Scavenging filaments gather wherever food waste and warm bedding have repeatedly been left. The whole station begins to look grown for human use.

Such places are sometimes called kind.

Kind does not mean safe. In fact, kind stations can be especially dangerous because people return to them too confidently, add one more basket, clear one more seam, and remain through one more brightening. The association has no agreement in it. Each participant is merely doing what has worked before.

An ordinary station might therefore contain more inhabitants than a visitor would ever count: a handful of people asleep or repairing cord; mites waiting in the gourd seam; larvae motionless in the pith; a grazer paused above a warm vein; dormant cups inside the wall; fungal or plant filaments already entering a discarded scrap of food. Beneath all of them, root and parasite would be negotiating their graft without sound.

When the people leave, they may carry away every basket and tool, clear the membrane, and leave a corked gourd for whoever comes next. In a few years their cords may be visible only as dark lines inside new bark. Their passage will have changed very little.

But not nothing. A cutting will have taken. Mites will have traveled farther than they could have gone alone. A root will have thickened under a weight that returned for many years. For a while, all these separate lives will have made something like an inhabitant together.

Then the attachment will break, or be abandoned, and the bright emptiness will remain.
