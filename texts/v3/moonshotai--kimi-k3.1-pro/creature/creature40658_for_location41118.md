Most of the Crown’s population never appears in a view of it. It passes underfoot, inside the felt, or in the narrow interval between a structure and the organism instructed to maintain it.

The smallest inhabitants are not easily classified as individuals. There are wandering repair organs no larger than dust grains, temporary mouths that form around damage and disperse when it closes, and knots of conductive tissue with just enough memory to repeat the last useful thing they did. Some dormant valves contain several generations of potential inhabitants, each waiting for a different combination of pressure, charge, and vibration. A road leaking instructions into the ground may acquire a whole buried ecology that exists nowhere else. When the road is forgotten, that ecology does not necessarily die. Sometimes it follows the fading instruction downward.

The visible burrowers are only the largest and most coordinated of these multitudes. They travel in bands, exchange material through temporary connections, and separate again without appearing to notice the loss. A band may have a persistent route without having persistent members. Anther’s road-keepers can often tell what is wrong with a district by which burrowers have left it, which have arrived, and which have begun moving in small, distressed circles.

On the plains, spindle-grazers possess more recognizable social lives, though even those are permissive. Herds divide, merge, and exchange members along their routes. An individual may travel with the same companions for centuries, or leave after a season and root beside a basin. The herd remembers such groves. On returning, grazers often touch their outer valves to the old trunks and stand quietly while charge passes between them. Sometimes the grove answers by opening fans that have been closed for years.

Anther’s natural philosophers disagree about whether this constitutes recognition. The people who travel with the herds are less cautious. They give the groves names, avoid building across their approaches, and maintain that certain herds become unmanageable if prevented from making the visit.

Young spindles remain folded against an older animal until their legs can support them and their valves can hold a useful charge. The adult that carries them need not be the one that produced them. Herd membership is built from feeding, defense, and repeated proximity as much as from descent. When valves clap in alarm, the sound communicates more than danger. Experienced herders can distinguish a shear alarm from the irritated clatter caused by an unfamiliar rail resonance, or the complicated, almost mocking racket young spindles make when one of them has startled the others.

Kites inhabit the boundary between plain and settlement. In the open Crown they are solitary for long periods, but their city nesting colonies are crowded, noisy, and full of theft. They take conductive thread, fragments of survey line, and small pieces of instructed fiber for their nests. A nest built from stolen civic material can become a minor technical problem: it may retain enough instruction to repeat a signal, attract burrowers, or interfere with a tower’s tuning. Consequently, kites are variously protected, tolerated, and cursed. Some tower-keepers leave suitable nesting material in designated courts. Others spend their careers trying to outwit birds that have watched several generations of keepers come and go.

Individual kites can be recognized by what they do with mast echoes. Most simply repeat a call. A few add a turn or a delayed final note. These variations spread through colonies, disappear, and return decades later. Antherines who dislike kites call this contamination. Those who like them call it repertoire.

Shears remain rarer in experience than in fact. Their stillness is not sleep, and their resemblance to dead terrain is not merely camouflage. Small cleaners sometimes move across their folded plates, removing salt bloom and damaged tissue while the shear listens through the ground. It may allow this attention for days and then consume one of the cleaners without otherwise changing position.

A settled shear-grove is one of the few places on the open plain where small life becomes visibly crowded. Its old digestive chambers shelter burrowers; its buttressed limbs hold pockets of dust and dormant valves; kites occasionally nest in the upper plates. Such groves can be ecologically valuable and still dangerous. Rooting does not instantly abolish appetite, and an old shear’s reflexes may remain quick long after its intentions have become difficult to determine. Plains people approach them with bells and never assume that stillness has the same meaning twice.

And then there are the people.

The citizens of Anther and the graft-towns are usually called Antherines, although the word describes a civic condition more accurately than a single body or ancestry. They belong to the same dry continuum as the herds and the felt. Their remotest ancestors may have been organs that learned to depart and return; by now, so much instruction has been layered upon instruction that the distinction is mainly philosophical.

The commonest mobile form is upright and narrow, built around a flexible braid rather than a rigid skeleton. Two long legs carry the body. Two principal arms perform heavy work, while a pair of finer limbs usually lies folded along the ribs. The outer surface is covered in small, close valves, graphite-dark in some lineages and pale bone or tarnished copper in others. Their hands can be blunt and strong, or remarkably delicate when the terminal filaments are separated. With those filaments spread, an experienced worker can feel the difference between a wall’s ordinary resistance and its hesitation.

Mineral lenses give them sight, but light is only one of their senses. Through their feet and the conductive seams of their limbs, they receive the substrate’s continual report: footsteps, rail strain, burrower movement, the tiny uncertainty of a graft before passage. Their faces are comparatively still. Expression shows in the small plates around the speaking cavity, in the sensory fans behind the head, and in whether those fans are offered openly or held close.

They speak aloud, click, and sing. They also accompany speech with patterns of pressure and charge. This second channel is good for emphasis, direction, and the shape of a space being described. It does not transmit secret thoughts, and one can lie in it perfectly well.

Clothing is protection, insulation, and etiquette. Conductive seams are usually covered in crowds; exposed tending limbs may be considered intimate, aggressive, or merely careless, depending on the district. Graft-workers wear gloves with detachable tips. Plains travelers favor layered bast that keeps charged dust from accumulating against the body. Fresh repairs show a brief green iridescence, so a newly mended sleeve or healing hand can be conspicuous for a day or two before returning to the Crown’s usual subdued colors.

Antherines are not slow in their movements. Their bodies can be extremely quick when alarmed. It is injury that teaches caution. A fractured plate may remain troublesome for years, and damage to a sensory fan can alter a person’s experience of every room they enter. They are therefore capable of great patience without necessarily possessing patient temperaments. Their arguments can continue for decades and still become bitter over a single badly maintained door.

Not every citizen remains mobile. Injury, age, inclination, or an accumulation of personal decisions may lead someone to root. Legs thicken; tending limbs enter a wall or court; sensory filaments extend into nearby roots. A rooted person does not thereby become Anther, nor do they gain access to everything the city knows. They remain situated, limited, and sometimes extremely bored. Their attention may range farther than their body, but it still has habits and blind spots.

Visiting a rooted relative often means sitting against an outer wall and exchanging small pulses while waiting for an answer to arrive through a window membrane, a warming floor, or a sequence of tones in the room’s frame. Some rooted citizens become fluent in several architectural voices. Others retain one preferred door and refuse to speak through any other. Families learn not to confuse a delayed response with absence.

Citizenship accommodates these differences reasonably well in law and imperfectly in practice. A self that can be asked about its intentions, and can answer in some maintained fashion, is generally entitled to be considered. The difficulty lies in deciding when an answer is still a person’s answer. Old bodies preserve habits. Rooms remember routines. A wall may continue opening at the hour a dead resident once returned, long after everyone agrees that the resident is gone.

Antherines distinguish, at least in formal language, between a room that remembers someone and someone who can still remember the room. This does not prevent ghost stories. A foundation repeating a dead child’s exploratory knock is not considered evidence of survival, but that does not make it comforting.

Households include mobile and rooted members, young and old, and sometimes people whose contribution to a child’s upbringing matters more socially than their contribution of tissue. Kinship follows feeding, instruction, maintenance, and descent. A household genealogy often resembles a graft map more than a tree. There are formal relationships for the person who first taught a child to insulate itself before sleep, and for the one who repaired its damaged sensory fan, because such acts create obligations that mere ancestry does not.

Children learn practical caution early. Do not lean your feeding seams against an unfamiliar wall. Do not enter a room merely to discover why it has become larger. If your footsteps stop ticking on an ordinary road, tell someone. And never answer a subterranean knock until you know which side of the wall it came from.

An ordinary home contains at least one deliberately uninteresting chamber: fixed in shape, insulated from most civic signaling, and difficult for the rest of the house to repurpose. People sleep there, keep private objects, and entertain guests who do not wish to be in subtle conversation with a building. Wealthier households may have several such rooms. In the outer wards, a reliably dull room can be a greater luxury than a large one.

Meals are dry and unhurried. Dormant valves are milled into dense wafers; carbonaceous feedstock is pressed with reactive salts; conductive granules are taken from shallow trays. Feeding surfaces open along the hands or arms, and nourishment often continues while people talk, work, or sit together doing very little. A host offers a guest clean feedstock and a safe contact, but the more important hospitality is architectural. The first obligation of a good host is to ensure that a guest can leave by a route the guest understands.

Most employment is some form of maintenance, though it rarely feels as monotonous as that word suggests. Road-rememberers walk avenues that are trying to soften. Tower-listeners detect harmful resonances before they become visible damage. Freight handlers learn the personalities of particular husks and rails. Vault-keepers spend much of their time verifying that an entrance still has the obligations attributed to it. Herd-tenders, felt-walkers, graft pruners, and surveyors all regard one another’s methods as incomplete.

Before an important gate opens for the day, its keeper may strike three bells: one for the air, one for the court wall, and one for the route. The practice is old, practical, and surrounded by disagreement about interpretation. If the route-bell answers with the wrong interval, traffic waits. Freight operators complain, eat salt wafers, exchange news, and inspect the measuring rods. No one sensible hurries the gate merely because a line has formed.

Maps in Anther are consequently more than diagrams of position. They record reliability, load, approach, season of instruction, and the party responsible for maintenance. A route marked in an old hand may be precious even where it is geographically absurd. A beautiful new survey can be almost worthless if it fails to say who will sound the graft after a pressure reversal.

This gives Antherine politics a peculiar material force. Debates about what to preserve are also debates about who will remain reachable. The inner districts possess deep accumulations of maintained routes, redundant approaches, and old civic attention. Outer wards can find their journeys lengthened because a graft has been pruned to protect somewhere older. Wealth buys maintenance, but never certainty. Poverty often means depending on a route that everyone uses and no one is clearly obliged to remember.

Anther itself is treated neither as a god nor as a machine. It is a powerful, distributed, fallible participant in civic life. It can preserve a road after the last traveler who understood its purpose has died. It can also neglect a heavily used stair because several more urgent instructions have propagated through the same roots. When citizens say that Anther “wants” something, experienced listeners usually ask which district, under what load, and according to whose instruments.

Beyond the city, the inhabitants of the felt courts and herd routes often regard these concerns as needlessly involuted. Their houses may be planted freight husks, low braided courts, or shelters beneath old spiral plates. They use grafts where necessary but prefer a shelter whose interior remains where they left it. Their children learn grove routes and herd signals before they learn the names of Anther’s central districts. A family may occupy one basin for generations while its actual dwellings shift slowly along the root-lines.

City people accuse them of confusing endurance with wisdom. Plains people reply that Anther has mistaken a complicated door for a thought. Trade continues regardless.

During a pressure reversal, these differences narrow. Herds are brought into rigid formation or encouraged into sheltered bast. Graft traffic is reduced. Roof scales close, tower-keepers establish opposed resonances, and householders check the small domestic organisms that live along their walls and feeding courts. Everyone listens for failures beneath the storm’s enormous noise. It is not a serene activity. People shout, drop instruments, disagree over sequences, and make mistakes that will occupy repair crews for years.

When the front passes, Anther does not seek silence. The towers deliberately restore an ordinary chord: freight-rail hum, court resonance, the low pulse of roots under normal load. The sound tells residents that the city’s body is still answering in familiar ways. Public quiet is more alarming than public noise.

At night, the Crown’s inhabitants return to their separate scales of activity. Burrowers carry conductive dust beneath sleeping courts. Kites turn on their perches and tuck their angular vanes close. A spindle herd moves between old groves, its valves ticking softly now that no alarm is passing through it. Somewhere a shear shifts several meters and becomes terrain again.

In Anther, a road-rememberer comes home, covers their sensory seams, and touches the house frame. A rooted relative answers from another district, slowly and with the familiar omission that means they are tired. Someone in the next room is arguing about a survey. A child has been permitted to stay awake until the ordinary ground-tone changes.

For a moment, all of it stops.

No herd clatter. No rail. No subterranean movement. Even the small scavengers in the walls become still.

The citizens wait, not because they expect the same thing to be beneath every silence, but because experience has taught them to ask before continuing. Then something far below gives a long, deliberate knock. The felt takes it up. The ground-tone returns. Doors and valves begin to open.

Life in the Sere Crown is not measured by how much of the plain it fills. It is measured by what continues to answer: with hunger, with repair, with recognition, with a route kept open after its original purpose has vanished, and occasionally with the decision to remain beside another living thing long enough for the answer to arrive.
