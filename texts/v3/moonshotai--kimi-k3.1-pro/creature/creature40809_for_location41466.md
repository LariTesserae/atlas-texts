### The Interval

At first, the hollow seems inhabited only by its plants. The ferns live close to the wet rock, the sedges keep their separate stations on the clay, and the leathery shrubs endure farther out without appearing to wait for anything. The lichens, slower than any of them, make their minute occupations of particular stones. None of these lives is a disguise. None leads to a hidden abundance elsewhere.

But there is one other inhabitant.

It has no name that it can use and no shape that would help a visitor find it. Calling it **the Interval** is accurate only if the word is allowed to include the drop on either side.

It is not a ghost, and it is not the diminished spirit of the old watercourse. It has no memory of a stream entering through the southern opening. It did not once occupy the whole hollow, nor does it regard the present seep as a remnant of some better condition. Its life belongs to the balance that came after the flowing water: the persistent darkening of one joint, the small basin, the brief connections made by rain, and the long dry distances around them.

Its nearest approach to flesh lies inside the western rock. Within the finest fissures beneath the overhang is a clear, mineral-rich film, so closely fitted to the stone that exposing it would reveal nothing obviously alive. It is neither moss nor a deposit of lime, though it resembles both under different lights. It thickens and thins over periods that would make a lichen seem hasty. By almost imperceptible degrees, it dissolves a little of its enclosing surface and deposits a little elsewhere. This is how it persists without growing. It has never widened the seep into a spring.

If it were removed, water would continue to gather and fall. For a long time, nothing in the appearance of the hollow would change.

The film in the joint is not the whole of it. Its life extends along moisture, though not in the manner of roots or spreading threads. When the basin overflows into the two smaller hollows, those places become briefly available to it. When the damp fan darkens after rain, it can distinguish conditions at points that, in drier weather, exist for it only as separate residues. A passing film of water along the old gradients gives it a temporary extent far beyond the seep. This is not an awakening or an access of power. It is simply a greater number of things happening at once.

Drying does not hurt it. Contraction is one of its ordinary states.

It lives on slight exchanges: water entering air, warmth entering stone, mineral passing from solution into the thinnest possible accretion. A drop carrying a different proportion of salts is a substantial event. Condensation beneath the overhang affords it a cool, diffuse sensation quite unlike the sharp arrival of water from above. The two scents of the hollow, wet limestone and warm dust, correspond for it to changes more subtle than smell but no less distinct.

The plants are not parts of its body. It does not see through their leaves or move them according to any purpose. Nevertheless, their lives reach it through water. A fern root entering a new hairline crack produces a slow alteration of pressure and chemistry. A sedge’s yearly growth draws moisture from one small pocket and leaves another comparatively undisturbed. When a dry shrub finally loses a stem, the change arrives at the seep long afterward, reduced to a difference almost too slight to separate from weather. The Interval does not mourn such things. Neither does it overlook them in quite the way an insensate process would.

Its memory is not an account of what has happened. It does not preserve individual drops, particular rains, or the succession of fern rosettes. Repetition wears certain possibilities smooth and leaves others difficult to reach. In that sense, it is made of habit more than recollection. Yet a sufficiently persistent alteration can remain present to it for a very long time.

The stones beside the eastern wall are such an alteration.

No one can now know whether the people who placed them suspected that anything lived in the seep. The crescent was not necessarily a shrine, and the rectangle need not have been an offering place. The Interval received no instruction from their building and understood no gesture in it. What reached it was simpler: weight where there had been none, a slight change in the movement of air, a new pattern of shade, and silt settling differently around straight edges.

The people themselves would have been brief occurrences—warmth, pressure, moisture from breath, repeated disturbances in the dust. Their departure did not create an absence that the Interval could suffer. But the stones remained, and by remaining became more legible to it than their makers had been. It now knows that eastern straightness in the same limited, durable way that it knows the mineral rim of the basin. Not as history. As a continuing condition.

It has not made the hollow empty. No influence from it keeps insects from the water or birds from the opening of sky. It neither enjoys solitude nor requires silence. An animal arriving at the basin would not violate a sanctuary; it would merely introduce an unprecedented concentration of movement, warmth, and salts. That no animal has done so is a fact about the hollow, not a rule enforced by its inhabitant.

For the same reason, the Interval is not a guardian. It does not hold the rim together, regulate the rain, or decide how many plants may live. If the seep strengthened into a rill, it would not rejoice. If the seep failed, it would not experience despair, though its life would contract and eventually cease in the drying fissures. It is capable of persistence without being concerned with survival in a human sense. There is no last task concealed in its patience.

A visitor cannot summon it by speaking, and offerings would mean only that something new had been placed where water, dust, or light might encounter it. Sitting beside the basin for an hour produces no apparition. The reflection does not acquire a face. The darkness under the overhang remains soft brown stone.

A visitor is nevertheless perceptible. A body alters the warm air; breathing adds a little moisture; a hand laid on the basin introduces salt and a sudden, complicated disturbance. The rings themselves are of no special importance. What registers is the brief difference they make in an otherwise familiar relation among rock, water, and air. Long after the visitor has gone, some exceedingly small consequence may remain. It will not remain as the memory of a person.

To come closest to the Interval, one would have to stop treating the drops as messages and the pauses as disappointment. A drop gathers within the dark joint. The clear film yields to and is shaped by its passage. Water falls, strikes, spreads, and vanishes into stillness. Around that event lie the root pockets, the separate plants, the dry sweeps of silt, and the old stones whose human purpose has disappeared while their physical consequences continue.

None of these things is the creature entire. Together they do not form a picture of it. They are simply the conditions under which its discontinuous life can occur.

It may not be the only such life in the world. There may be others in seepage beneath other limestone, in places with different intervals and different kinds of rain. The Interval has no knowledge of them and no impulse toward them. It is not the last of anything. It is not waiting to be joined, released, or understood.

Another drop swells beneath the overhang. For a moment its weight is distinguishable from the rock that holds it. Then it falls. The note is small and clear; the rings reach the basin’s edge and disappear.

Afterward, nothing is revealed. The hollow does not become meaningful in a new way. The fern roots retain their little moisture, the eastern stones keep their few straight lines, and warm dust remains at its exact distance from the wet clay. The next drop begins without urgency.

The Interval lives there: not behind the silence, not imprisoned in the stone, but at the pace and in the proportions that the hollow permits.
