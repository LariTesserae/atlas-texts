Nothing in the Low Works keeps a house, and nothing waits beside a path to be fed. The six narrow houses on the upper terrace are empty; the lights behind their wet windows belong to the same automatic circulation as the pumps. Yet the basin has an ecology as particular as any forest’s. It is simply organized around temperatures, water levels, and machines.

Its first life is easily mistaken for discoloration. In the black sediment below the overflows, fallen reed fiber and fern debris soften into layers. Pale fungal threads bind the fragments; olive and rust-colored films spread through the warmer seeps, some living, some merely mineral deposited over life. There are no scavengers to hurry the process. A frond is not eaten. It loses its edges, then its veins, then becomes part of the silt on which something else takes root. Decomposition is quickest where warm water carries air through the debris, and slowest in the reservoir’s depths, where old leaves can remain recognizable long after their stems have vanished.

The wall gardens are more visibly precise. Around a leaking condensate pipe, life arranges itself by degrees of heat. Nearest the metal there may be nothing but scale and bare, dark brick. A little farther out, where the surface is warm but no longer scalding, moss forms a close green pelt. Ferns begin beyond that, finest and most delicate along the drip lines, coarser where their roots can reach masonry. Every frond is wet, but not every frond is tolerably warm. The result is a vertical meadow with boundaries sharper than any gardener could cut.

Such a garden lasts only as long as its conditions. A pressure change can make the leak too hot and leave a brown silhouette fixed to the wall. A cooler pipe may allow moss to advance over brick that was bare for years. When a machine fails and its body stops shaking, ferns enter the housing: first in the sediment behind a flange, then along a shaft that no longer turns, finally in a dense green mass beneath the open casing. Live machinery is ringed by exclusions—no growth across a belt, nothing rooted beside a reciprocating rod, no soft stem surviving in a steam throat. Dead machinery becomes available ground.

The tree ferns are the tallest inhabitants, but their height is misleading. Much of their life occurs below them, in root mats threaded through rubble and old ash. Their crowns intercept the low cloud and release it again as slower drips, keeping the ground beneath wet after the open terraces have begun to steam. Younger ferns gather in these manufactured pockets of shade.

Their distribution follows the plumbing as readily as the contours of the basin. Spores enter the intakes with spray. The heavier particles settle in silt traps and produce crowded, short-lived thickets that are eventually buried or washed out. The finest reach elevated cisterns and descend later with water into gutters, upper rooms, and cracked inspection chambers. A fern leaning from a high window may therefore have arrived by pipe rather than by wind. The houses are not separate from the basin’s vegetation; they are merely some of its less efficient cisterns.

The greatest living abundance, though, is in the black reeds.

Their visible stems are only the temporary part of them. Beneath the flooded courts, rhizomes lie through the silt in dense, interlocking beds, following old platforms and stopping where a channel is scoured too often. Some courts carry young growth, bright at the joints and easily flattened by a surge. Others, beyond the effective reach of the press mill’s cutters, stand dark and thick, their older stems polished where they knock together. Individual stems vanish under flood or cutting; the buried beds persist. The oldest living things in the Low Works may be these slow subterranean networks rather than any of the fern crowns above them.

The reeds also pass most completely through the settlement’s industrial cycle. Their resinous stems are cut, carried on the closed tram loop, and pressed into fuel. The fuel feeds the boilers. Boiler heat drives the pumps and helps return the basin’s water to the air. Ash goes back through its own channels and settles among the reed roots. Nothing has designed this as an act of cultivation, and nothing oversees it, but the reed beds occupy the place where several automatic processes happen to close around one another.

Their reproduction is just as unattended. Seed cases loosen into the water and float with whatever else the current carries. Many lodge uselessly against grilles or drift into water too warm for them. Some remain in a flooded court until a gate opens and takes them elsewhere. A few reach a new fan of sediment at the moment its surface is exposed. Nothing visits the reeds to carry pollen, and nothing buries their seeds. Water, pressure, and obstruction perform all the necessary accidents.

Copperleaf occupies the warmer routes between these communities. Its apparent preference for pipes is really a record of survival. Shoots that extend onto hotter metal wither; those that remain within the condensation put down roots. The living vine consequently traces an exact map of tolerable warmth, luxuriant beside one joint and abruptly sparse across the next span of masonry.

Its roots enter old mortar and fine concrete seams. They can widen a weakness, but they also bind loose material into a single fibrous mass. Where a pipe has cooled permanently, the vine may die back and leave a brown net over the wall. That net does not immediately disappear. It continues to hold dust and spores, and ferns often follow it. Thus a wall loosened by one generation of vegetation may be preserved by the next, its bricks unable to fall cleanly because roots pass behind and between them.

The gray rosettes belong to colder, quieter conditions. They gather along shaded inflows, in abandoned inspection channels, and in the lee of sluice aprons where clear water moves over black sediment. Their thick leaves shed the downpour instead of collapsing beneath it, and their low roots endure alternating exposure and submersion. They are absent from the warm, heavily coated pipes and from the reed courts where the rhizomes leave little open ground. Seen from above, their pale colonies mark the basin’s cool water almost as accurately as the enamel signs mark its pressure lines.

For all these inhabitants, the daily tide is a succession of habitats rather than a simple rise and fall. Drawdown exposes root curtains, lower steps, and bands of silt that were submerged an hour before. Water spreads through the marsh terraces; seeds change position; spores wash from one compartment into another. Then mist and rain erase distances, and the returning flood covers some seedlings while tearing others free. A plant need not survive the whole Low Works. It need only survive the particular sequence occurring behind one wall, beneath one crown, or downstream of one valve.

The vegetation also continually creates the conditions of its own removal. Reed beds narrow a channel. Roots thicken behind a grille. Moss holds enough silt for ferns, ferns hold enough for larger roots, and eventually the passage that fed the garden begins to fail. Pressure rises until water finds another route. The new current may clear the obstruction, drown it, or simply leave it behind in a quieter backwater. This is why the basin’s lushness comes in belts, and why its empty courts and broad streets are not unused space. They are the places where growth has repeatedly exceeded the circulation that permitted it.

It is tempting to call the whole arrangement one organism, with brick for bone and pipes for vessels, but that would give it too much unity. A stuck valve can boil a garden. A failed pump remains dead while ferns occupy its casing. A seed’s passage through a sluice has no intended destination. The pumps have no allegiance to the reeds, and the roots do not preserve the walls out of usefulness. Each process merely continues until heat, pressure, obstruction, or decay turns it into the beginning of another.

Over time, however, only certain kinds of life remain common: spores that survive the intakes, seeds that endure long floating passages, rhizomes that persist beneath cutting and flood, growth able to occupy the narrow interval between nourishing warmth and lethal heat. Life has not conquered the Works. It has become one of its redundant systems. Close one route, and water finds another; after a while, something green usually follows.

At night, this is clearer than the apparent emptiness. Orange boiler light ends against scorched vine and bare metal. Amber lamps pick out the reed courts, where only the jointed stems move, knocked together by pressure-born drafts. Fern water continues falling beneath roofs long after the rain has thinned. Along the cold channels, the gray rosettes disappear into darkness without ceasing to grow.

No figure crosses the causeway. No animal calls from the flooded courts. Yet by morning a wall meadow may be one frond wider, a seed case may be lodged against a different grille, and a newly exposed fan of silt may already carry the first dull sheen of film. Reed becomes fuel, fuel becomes heat, heat becomes rain, and rain returns to the reed beds. The engines supply the beats. Life persists in the narrow places between them.
