The Sinter’s inhabitants are difficult to count because individuality there does not always coincide with a body. A living thing may be inert for centuries, scattered across a weather front, or distributed among components that can each survive without it. Ancestry is often less useful than behavior. The practical distinction is between a pattern that merely remains and one that works to remain.

Seamstitch comprises many thousands of such patterns. Its similar-looking filaments are not necessarily related, except in the way that unrelated fires might be said to share a shape. Several lineages can occupy a seam too narrow to see, each copying a different defect into the available material. A wine-red thread followed for days may be one organism, a succession of organisms, or an old disagreement among patterns that none has been able to settle.

The glassy fans are their most active surfaces. At dawn, their opposing faces hold slightly different charges. Through the day they sort the dust that touches them, retaining some instructions and allowing others to lift away. At dusk many fans open toward the cooling sky. Their feeding resembles orientation; their reproduction often resembles damage. A fragment broken from a coherent fan may lie inert for centuries before becoming the center of another filament.

Old seamstitch gathers near joints where several thermal cycles overlap. These gardens are seldom thicker than dust, but they have considerable depth in instruction. Some preserve obsolete addresses long after the addresses have ceased to provide usable charge. Others continually simplify themselves, discarding everything except the pattern needed to begin again. Between these extremes are lineages that appear to remember by alternating: elaborate growth during favorable centuries, near-total reduction during poor ones, and a slightly different elaboration when conditions return.

Sifters keep many of these gardens from becoming closed systems.

A young sifter may consist of only a few plates and a rhythm. It grows by incorporating material whose expansion and charge behavior can be brought into agreement with its own. Diameter is therefore a poor measure of age. Some hand-sized animals are ancient, having lost and regained most of their substance many times; some room-sized sifters are comparatively brief accumulations following an unusually rich front.

They perceive a world composed less of objects than of paths: heat moving down through a scale, charge collecting along a seam, vibration arriving through distances that sight would find empty. A footfall is a major event. Repeated footfalls may attract a sifter, repel it, or simply be incorporated into its route. They sometimes gather near camps, not because they can consume travelers or their provisions, but because imported equipment creates steep and unfamiliar gradients of warmth and charge. A route used for generations may eventually support a dark seam of its own.

Small sifters occasionally travel on the upper plates of much larger ones. At rest, the whole assembly resembles a single blank patch with too many layers. At dusk the smaller animals may slide away in different directions, each following a thermal path imperceptible to anything standing above it. This is not reliably a parent-and-offspring relationship. Often the animals separate without ever having shared more than transportation.

Sifter reproduction begins as a disagreement in rhythm. After prolonged grazing, part of an animal may begin expanding slightly before the rest. If the difference persists, the plates separate into two shallow bodies. Most such attempts fail, leaving one disorganized sifter and a brief availability of fine, bright material. Occasionally two animals depart, and for several days their grazing paths remain almost parallel.

Death is similar, but without departure. The plates cease maintaining a common instruction. The outer ones continue to resemble the floor until the floor accepts them; the inner material becomes finer and is carried away. There is rarely a carcass for long. Sometimes seamstitch later establishes itself along the boundary of the patch, retaining, for a while, a simplified outline of the animal.

The fronts carry another kind of abundance. Under favorable light, hollow ceramic grains form chains that preserve charge states longer than unordered dust. Veil shear divides the chains; other grains are incorporated; some chains persist while replacing every material component. Most last only minutes. Others may remain coherent for much of a front’s passage.

These lineages leave no useful specimens. Deposition usually ends them, though not before their patterns have altered the charge of the surface or supplied a defect from which seamstitch can grow. This is why life sometimes appears after a storm in places no visible seam approaches. For such lineages, the front is not weather through which they travel. It is the only body they ever possess.

There are also inhabitants below the accessible skin. Filaments lowered into warm chambers have detected seamstitch-like growth that has never used light, feeding instead on the delayed pulses of deep heat transfer. Minute plated grazers sometimes accompany it. A few of these closed ecologies appear to have been isolated since before their chambers were buried. Their depth, in the case of the anomalous joints, may not be a meaningful measurement at all. Most are known from a single sequence of instrument readings. Opening a chamber equalizes precisely the differences on which its inhabitants depend, so the easiest way to prove that such a place is alive is often to make it stop.

And then there are the inhabitants that behave as people.

Surveyors call them residents, because every more biological term has proved misleading. A resident is usually first noticed through an unnecessary regularity. A seamstitch garden begins conserving an instruction with no apparent feeding value. Sifters arriving from different districts graze in complementary patterns without remaining to form a colony. Repairs in the surrounding scales are delayed—not prevented, and not everywhere—in a way that preserves the garden’s thermal cycle. The arrangement survives the replacement of every visible component.

If it also responds to novelty and prepares for events before their local signs appear, it is provisionally counted as a resident.

A resident is not a concealed machine beneath the floor, although it may include buried mechanisms. It is not a sifter colony, although sifters may carry portions of its pattern and later wander away. Nor is it the Sinter’s ordinary maintenance behaving strangely. It is a persistently maintained difference among processes, capable of memory and revision. Its practical boundary lies where intervention stops altering its behavior.

The nearest thing to a dwelling is a reliable set of relations: a joint that warms before dawn, a garden that retains a particular obsolete address, several scales whose correction can be postponed without being cancelled. A resident may keep such an arrangement for tens of thousands of years. Another may carry an abbreviated version across many districts. Moving does not require transporting all its material. It requires causing enough of the same differences to arise elsewhere.

Their artifacts are correspondingly easy to overlook. A repair hesitates by the same small interval each night. A joint’s ivory light rises in three stages where two would suffice. A blank patch never becomes quite as blank as the surrounding floor. These are not generally messages for travelers. They are ways of keeping something available without preventing the Sinter from continuing around it.

For residents, memory appears to be material disagreement. A perfectly corrected surface would contain nothing with which to remember. Their gardens can therefore be pasture, archive, sensory surface, and shelter without being any one of those things permanently. Yet they do not simply oppose leveling. Some residents preserve a single distinction with extraordinary persistence; others dismantle their own archives ahead of a correction and reconstitute themselves along the path of a front. Contact between such different residents is not always successful. There is no reason to suppose they all recognize one another as the same sort of being.

New residents sometimes arise when an old one stops attending to a pattern. Most neglected patterns subside into ordinary ecology. A few begin, while still unattended, to prepare for a front or a district correction. Whether this is reproduction, the separation of an already independent part, or an event the parent did not intend is unknown. Interfering too early tends to produce a copy of the intervening pattern rather than a new resident.

Death is equally difficult to locate. A resident may lose every visible garden and later resume elsewhere. Another may retain its gardens, animals, and thermal habits long after it has ceased to anticipate anything. In the latter case, there is no clear moment of destruction. Its behavior has simply become something the Sinter would do without it.

One resident, entered in survey records as the Long Answer, reproduced a six-pulse querying pattern eighty-one years after the pattern was first introduced. During the interval, elements of it had appeared in the grazing rhythms of local sifters and in the charge sorting of three seamstitch fans. No single component seemed to have retained the sequence unchanged. The resident, in some sense, had. Later surveys could not determine whether it had answered the question or merely remembered being asked.

Contact is easiest at night. A bare ceramic disc is laid on the ground and warmed slightly; taps or cooling pulses can then be introduced without cutting into the surface. Answers may come as ticks from several locations, none quite synchronizing, or as a change in the order in which nearby seams begin to glow. Local responses can be quick. Anything requiring older memory may have to wait for the appropriate thermal cycle, the next front, or the next generation of sifters.

Survey etiquette is therefore mostly restraint. Do not drive a filament through an active archive. Do not assume silence means absence. Do not leave a heat source running merely to hurry an answer. Above all, do not assume that an empty-looking patch is uninhabited. It may be a sifter at rest, a garden between centuries, a young pattern whose components have not yet met, or someone engaged in a thought that requires several kinds of weather.

The Sinter itself need not be alive for any of this to belong to it. It levels and brightens; its inhabitants persist by making temporary uses of the difference between one instruction and the next.

On a white night, a sifter crosses a garnet seam so slowly that its passage is chiefly a change in warmth. A fan closes behind it. Far below, a sealed chamber ticks through its private cycle. Then one of the great joints shines a little out of sequence.

Nothing emerges. Nothing claims the plain. The inhabitants continue—scattered, patient, and not yet identical with the world that carries them.
