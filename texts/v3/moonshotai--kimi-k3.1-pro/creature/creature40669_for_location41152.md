Life on the Old Green Height is arranged less by species than by tenancy. A hollow column is a vertical country. A browser’s back is a traveling island. A footprint can outlast the small creatures that settle in it. Many inhabitants spend their entire lives inside a volume of water that a larger animal could drink.

### The small waters

The clear swimmers of the leaf-cisterns are the young of several different winged animals, though in their early lives they are difficult to tell apart. Each is little more than a flex of transparent muscle, two dark eyes, and a green thread where food passes through the body. They graze the films that form on water and on the inner walls of hollow stems. Without them, many cisterns would stagnate; with too many of them, a cistern can be grazed clear and left unable to support its own inhabitants.

When the time comes to change, a swimmer climbs the pale roots inside its column. If the water falls before it has finished, it can flatten itself into the damp inner lining and wait, sometimes long enough for the plant around it to add another layer of growth. Those that emerge become bark-folders. Their wings collapse into narrow, weather-colored seams, and their outlines appear only when the body beneath them breathes. Most never fly far. A journey from one column to its neighbor, made through mist, may be the whole adventure of their adult lives.

The water-film amphibians are flatter still. Some can rest beneath the moisture on a leaf without breaking its surface, their feathery gills spread almost two-dimensionally against the tissue. They do not leap when disturbed. They yield. The body compresses, slides into a vein or fold, and seems to become another thickness of the leaf. Their hearts can be seen as a dull intermittent movement when light passes through them.

Along fallen fronds work the vein-walkers: many-legged grazers whose feet touch down in such long, delicate sequences that even collapsing tissues usually bear them. They eat the softened green matter between the veins, leaving the tougher strands as a skeleton on which moss and pale decomposers later settle. Their routes are not random. Repeated grazing opens the same small passages, and spores collect in the polished grooves left by their feet.

Vein-walkers drum before crossing one another’s paths. Two touches, a pause, another touch. If an answer comes, one usually turns aside. At this scale, communication is not ceremony. It prevents two bodies from tearing the same leaf, or two broods from exhausting the same film of food.

Much of the Height’s abundance consists of such successful avoidances.

### The walking gardens

The moss-backed browsers make geography at a larger scale.

Their gardens are not merely camouflage, nor are they accidental accumulations. A young browser carries only a narrow darkening along its spine. An older one may bear copper cushions, small translucent leaves, trailing roots, and the discarded shells of cistern creatures deposited by its attendant gliders. The growth is deepest in the hollows between its shoulders and hips, where water remains after the rest of the back has begun to drain.

Wallowing partly resets this record. It also exchanges it. Plants that cannot cross open ground unaided pass from one back to another when browsers crowd into the same basin. Amphibians leave eggs in the wet moss. Gliders move between bodies, carrying spores in the folds of their membranes. Some small plants are known only from browser backs and from the wallows where several browsers once met. Between those events, their whole range may be walking separately through the forest.

The gliders rest close to the browsers’ warm skin, often so deeply folded into the moss that only their breathing distinguishes them. In movement they are broad and brief: a spread of membrane, a shallow curve through the dim air, then another disappearance. They take the small winged things disturbed by browsing and also pick parasites from places the browser cannot reach. Their droppings feed the garden. Their young sometimes make their first glide from one animal to another without ever touching the ground.

A browser’s footprints become part of the same moving community. Water enters some immediately; others remain damp depressions for days. Clear swimmers stranded in a wallow may survive in them. Vein-walkers gather along their rims. The next browser to pass may drink from the print, obliterate it, or carefully step elsewhere. There is no single pattern.

Nor are browser routes trails in the ordinary sense. Often there is no herd to follow, only a succession of individuals separated by years. A path remains open because bodies keep arriving along it, each one finding the way by old compressions, scent in the roots, the shape of growth around previous passages. Their companionship is dispersed through time. Two browsers may use the same resting hollow throughout their lives and never meet.

When a browser dies, the route does not immediately forget it. The body remains a broad obstruction, then a sheltered richness, then a low assembly of roots and unusually vigorous leaves. For a while, paths bend around the place. Later they cross it. The garden on the animal’s back may outlast almost everything else, establishing itself in widening, irregular patches as the body beneath it ceases to be distinguishable.

### The patient hungers

The deep-pool hunters bind their lives to a single basin as closely as the browsers loosen the boundaries between basins.

They lie root-colored among submerged roots, their bodies flattened against the bottom. Some are enormous, but size is difficult to judge where most of the animal never moves. The occasional ventilation of the gills may be the only sign that a pool contains anything larger than its swimmers.

They can wait through long scarcity. Their flesh draws down slowly; their movements become rarer; even the gills seem to consult the water before opening. They respond less to daylight than to changes in level, pressure, and mineral taste. When a basin begins to receive water from elsewhere, new life may arrive before any visible flooding. Then the hunter feeds.

Such abundance is brief. Cisterns overflow, small animals are washed from root-vaults, amphibians gather to breed, and the still pool becomes crowded with events. The hunters feed, mate, and sometimes move. Their eggs are fixed deep among roots where later changes in water will either release the young or leave them waiting. Afterward the pool closes again around stillness.

The hunters do not keep balance in any deliberate sense. They simply ensure that a basin’s brief excess does not all remain where it fell. Much of it passes into a body that may then lie motionless for another age.

### The listeners

The Height also has inhabitants who deliberate. They are rare enough that their absence can seem more plausible than their presence.

They are called listeners because that is what they appear to be doing when encountered: crouched beneath a root arch with one palm against the mat, or standing beside a hollow column with two fingers resting on its wall. They are small, hairless, long-handed beings, usually stooped by the low passages they favor. Their skin is mottled and never quite dry. Their eyes gather what light there is, but their palms and broad bare soles are more useful.

Their sensitivity is not limitless, and they do not hear the thoughts of roots. They detect local, ordinary things: the difference between the settling of wet growth and the compression left by a heavy body; the altered tremor of a column whose water is falling; the faint repeated knocking of animals within a buttress. Much of their knowledge comes from touching the same places over long periods. A listener without that history can mistake almost anything for almost anything else.

At close range they speak through contact. Their names are short patterns of pressure and release given against a wrist or palm. Across a root-vault they drum softly. Scent is used in water only when the movement of that water is well understood; an uncertain watershed makes a poor messenger. Voices remain for infants, pain, sudden danger, and those rare occasions when someone must be found without being touched.

They also leave records in living growth. A young rootlet can be held, shaded, or lightly bruised so that it thickens in a particular way. Years later, another listener can read the resulting swellings by hand. A message begun in green tissue may not be complete when its maker dies. Roots distort it, animals interrupt it, and later listeners copy what they think it says. Their archives therefore contain not only knowledge but old errors, arguments, and corrections growing through one another.

A group of listeners is called a hearing, though it need not live together. A hearing may be five individuals sharing one root-vault, or several small camps scattered across neighboring basins whose members still answer one another’s messages. There are no villages and no fields. Their shelters are woven from fallen sheaths and soft stems; their vessels are hollow columns cut from already dead growth, or thick leaves folded and pinned with thorns. They possess stones for cracking hard seeds and little else that would survive long after abandonment.

They eat pith, oily seeds, soft growth from rotting wood, and animals taken carefully from crowded cisterns. They steep and ferment more than they cook. Fire is possible, but rarely worth the injury it causes and the attention it requires. An empty camp returns quickly to the mat: first a sourness beneath the leaves, then a richer patch of moss, then no evident camp at all.

Their gardening consists mostly of altering one small condition. A stem is notched so that it empties into a different hollow. A fallen frond is laid where its shade will preserve a seep. Water containing cistern young is carried from an overcrowded column to a new one. A little growth from a browser’s wallow is placed on another browser’s back. Afterward there may be nothing to do for decades except observe, remember, and refrain from repeating the intervention merely because its results are slow.

This does not make the listeners uniformly wise. Their records describe emptied cisterns, diverted springs, and gardens carried enthusiastically into places where they smothered the growth already present. They have hoarded food and falsified water-signs. Hearings have stopped answering one another over disputes whose original cause became illegible before the dispute ended. Slowness gives their mistakes time to become elaborate.

Their most serious arguments concern whether to intervene at all. A diminishing basin may be part of an exchange that will enrich another province, or it may be the beginning of a local collapse. Both possibilities can look identical for years. Some listeners insist on assisting what is already present; others regard assistance as a way of becoming responsible for consequences no one can yet read. There is no final authority beyond the next state of the water.

The scattered stones are not theirs. Their oldest readable records already describe the steps, corners, and broken walls as old. They recognize deliberation in the angles, but deliberation alone tells them little. They do not pull the ruins apart, because the roots often hold both stone and basin in place. Nor do they rebuild them. Rebuilding would require deciding what the original shape had been for.

The roofless cell with the single pale plant is visited by more than one hearing. Listeners clear fallen tissue from its floor and sometimes adjust the seepage around its walls, but they do not gather the plant. A few attempts have been made to establish it elsewhere. Each failed, though each failure took so long to confirm that the attempts never became a sustained practice. The listeners disagree about whether the plant depends on the stone, on some forgotten treatment of the water, or on an arrangement of roots that happens still to survive there. They continue to visit without settling the question.

Their own dead are placed in the mat and covered with fallen growth. No marker is needed at first. Later, the place becomes conspicuous: darker leaves, an unusual thickening of stems, sometimes a small pale garden where nothing similar grew before. Relatives visit that richness rather than a grave. In time it, too, becomes difficult to distinguish from the surrounding forest, and the hearing preserves the location only as long as someone still considers it worth copying.

Nothing in this requires the Old Green Height to be a single mind. Its inhabitants are connected without necessarily knowing one another, and much of what joins them is simply delay. A swimmer exhausts a cistern; a bark-folder emerges somewhere else; a browser carries eggs into a wallow; a hunter wakes when the basin changes; a listener misreads the change and leaves a warning that another generation must correct.

At the failing of the cloud-light, the listeners sleep as everything else does, often with one hand still resting against a root. Bark-folders close into the bark. Gliders disappear beneath wet moss. A hunter lets its gills settle. The last drops descend separately through the foliage.

Weeks later, beyond a broad swell, a spring rises. It wets a root, enters an old footprint, and reaches the edge of a cistern. Something small turns in the new water.

After a while, something touches the root and waits for the movement to come again.
