The Mound Plain has no single people, and no animal that knows it whole. A census taken at noon would be hopelessly incomplete. Most of its inhabitants spend their lives below ground, under cover, or in dormancy—as seeds, eggs, pupae, spores, or animals waiting in the cool dark for a better hour. The mounds are merely the largest visible parts of a population composed mostly of small bodies.

The principal builders are several kinds of mound termites. The tallest fluted towers belong to pale fungus-growers whose workers are blind, soft-bodied, and smaller than grains of rice. Unsheltered dry air can kill them, so their ordinary world is one of pressed earth, touch, vibration, and scent. They advance through covered runways, clip fallen grass and dead stems into portable pieces, and carry the harvest home. Soil is brought up pellet by pellet and fixed with bodily secretions. A buttress may begin as reinforcement around a damaged vent; a tower may grow because workers repeatedly deposit material along one favorable edge. Nothing like a drawing precedes it.

Deep within a living colony are nurseries, refuse chambers, stores, and gardens of chewed plant matter threaded with pale fungus. The fungus begins work the termites’ own bodies cannot complete, turning resistant dry vegetation into food they can use. Workers tend the comb, remove contaminated portions, and carry fragments of it to establish new gardens. Heat from all this crowded metabolism and decomposition lingers in the deeper chambers. Brood-tenders, foragers, and gardeners shift their activity through the nest as the outer shell warms and cools.

At the reproductive center is a queen, attended by workers and usually a lifelong mate, but she is not an architect and does not direct the works. Her immense body produces eggs; the apparent intelligence of the colony lies elsewhere, in countless local responses to damage, temperature, humidity, hunger, and colony odor. Soldiers defend breaches. Some shear at attackers with oversized jaws; others have blunt or plug-shaped heads that can obstruct a narrow passage while workers wall off the gallery behind them.

Not every mound has the same maker or the same interior economy. Broad ochre domes often belong to grass-harvesting termites that keep stores rather than true fungus gardens. Lower gray masses may house soil-feeders. Some narrow towers are only the active remnants of much larger nests, while others are composite structures in which a new colony has built upon the shell of an extinct one. Neighboring mounds may touch without their inhabitants mixing. Their frontiers are chemical and contested: sealed galleries, guarded runways, brief underground battles, and long stretches in which each colony simply avoids the other.

The plants above them make all this possible. Older districts are often shaded by anvil-thorn, a low tree with a broad, flattened crown and leaflets that fold during the worst heat. Its thorns protect the youngest growth, while its hard pods are taken by browsers. Its roots penetrate deep sediment but also follow old termite galleries, where the ground is looser and enriched by buried refuse. Between the trees grow waxleaf shrubs, their small leaves coated against desiccation, and bronze tussocks whose narrow blades roll almost closed in drought. Succulent rosettes occupy sheltered dust at the shrub bases. Beneath them lie dormant root buds and a seed bank far richer than the visible vegetation suggests.

These plants are not merely grouped around the mounds. They pass through the animal population. Termites take dead grass; harvester ants carry seeds into underground granaries; rodents empty pods and bury the contents; hoofed browsers swallow seeds that later emerge scarified in dung. Beetles bury the dung in turn. Some seeds are eaten at every stage. Others are dropped, forgotten, or buried too deeply for the animal that wanted them, and those accidents become thickets, grass clumps, and eventually new feeding grounds. Root-associated fungi extend through the soil, while bacteria and other minute decomposers persist in refuse pockets and resting stages. Even apparently vacant dust may carry a sparse living crust that helps hold it against the wind.

For most residents, there is no journey to water. That fact shapes bodies and habits more strongly than any single feature of the climate. Insects conserve moisture behind resistant outer coverings and within humid nests. Many remain dormant through the driest periods. The pale seed-rodents known as silt-mice forage after dark, produce highly concentrated urine and nearly dry droppings, and obtain much of what they need from seeds and the chemistry of their own metabolism. Reptiles divide their days between warm exposure and deep refuge. Browsers select leaves, pods, and succulent growth with more moisture than the air and dust would suggest is available. Predators inherit water from their prey. Nothing has escaped the need for water; the whole fauna has simply learned to live without treating it as a landscape feature.

A mound supports many more kinds of tenants than builders. Some beetles spend their entire lives in its galleries, flattened to pass through narrow spaces. Harmless species consume refuse or growths on old comb. Others steal food, attack eggs, or enter stores. A few carry the colony’s odor well enough to be tolerated, at least for a while. Mites, tiny soil arthropods, and other scarcely visible animals occupy the garden waste. Abandoned vents become spider retreats. Old exits lead into scorpion burrows. Ant nests riddle the apron of disturbed soil, and their occupants range from seed collectors to raiders that test the termites’ covered roads.

Where a runway is torn open, small lizards and birds may gather before the builders can close it. Tower lizards bask on exposed earth in the morning, each using a particular ledge or fallen slab, then withdraw into crevices as the shell becomes too hot. Slender burrowing snakes follow insect passages and feed on brood where they can reach it. Heavier snakes cannot use the intact galleries, but they enter breaches and old chambers to wait for rodents. Trapdoor spiders occupy firm soil near mound bases, while antlion larvae make their small conical pits in sheltered dust. At ground level, an architectural ruin and a hunting ground are often the same thing.

The silt-mice are among the most important tenants. Their burrows usually begin in an abandoned gallery or beneath a root-loosened slab. They carry seeds in cheek pouches and make more caches than they recover. Some caches are robbed by ants; others are exposed by wind; a few survive until rain. The mice themselves are hunted by snakes, small nocturnal cats, and birds of prey, so their movements follow cover with great precision. A lane that seems empty in daylight may, after dusk, be crossed at intervals by the same quick, low shape, always pausing at the same grass stem before the next open stretch.

The great breaches are made by moundbreakers, heavy nocturnal mammals with strong foreclaws, closeable ears, and long, narrow muzzles. One animal may test several shells before finding an active colony, then tear through a wall that has taken years to accumulate. It feeds rapidly on exposed termites with a sticky tongue and moves on, leaving broken comb, scattered brood, and a cavity open to the dry air. For the colony this can be a disaster. For everything else it is an opportunity. Birds and lizards take exposed insects; beetles enter the refuse; spiders occupy sheltered cracks; rodents investigate the deeper opening. If the termites remain, they seal what they can and build around the rest. If they abandon that portion, roots and other animals begin their slower work.

The larger travelers use the plain without belonging to any one mound. Pale, dry-country antelope move in single file along foraging lanes and the sheltered edges of thickets, taking thorn pods, shrub leaves, and occasional succulent growth. Their droppings feed beetles and return nutrients to the soil; seeds caught in their coats or passing through their bodies cross distances no termite could travel. Insect-eating birds nest in thorn crowns and hunt from low perches. Raptors use bare towers as lookout posts. A small, furred-footed dust-cat may spend the day in a chamber opened by a moundbreaker and hunt at night where two lanes narrow between shrubs. Larger predators occur, but thinly; the plain can support innumerable small lives more readily than many large ones.

The age of a mound can therefore be read through its inhabitants. A young tower belongs chiefly to its builders and their immediate enemies. An older one acquires beetles, lizard holes, root channels, and perhaps a rodent entrance. A very old dome may be living along only one flank, its heart hollowed by tenants and its upper cracks held together by shrub roots. Eventually the last builders depart or die out. The animal community does not then disappear; it changes. Shelter decreases, erosion begins, plant cover thickens, and material from the mound is gradually carried into other nests and burrows.

Rare rain reveals how much life has been waiting. The soft basin floor absorbs it quickly, without acquiring a lasting channel. Seeds that have lain in dust for years germinate together. Minute crust organisms spread through the upper soil; ephemeral plants flower and begin setting seed almost at once. Dormant insects emerge, and birds and rodents respond to the sudden concentration of food. Termites and ants may release winged reproductive forms into air that is briefly humid enough for them. Most are taken before they have flown far, by birds, lizards, spiders, and other insects. A surviving pair sheds its wings, excavates a tiny chamber, and begins a colony that may never raise a visible mound.

The builders also suffer from the rain. Fresh walls slump, runways collapse, and galleries fill with washed sediment. Repairs begin as soon as conditions permit. Then the surface dries. Ephemeral plants wither, insects return to refuge, and the brief abundance is packed away again into seeds, eggs, pupae, spores, caches, and the bodies of animals that fed well. The towers remain, stained and newly patched.

At night, occupation changes hands without ever becoming simple. Silt-mice visit their caches. Moundbreakers follow scent through the scrub. Dust-cats listen at burrow mouths. Moths and other small insects visit what flowers are available, while termite gardens continue their slow decomposition in the warm dark. Above them, an old mound may hold a snake in one chamber, roots in another, and an active nursery only a few walls away.

No creature inhabits the whole Mound Plain. Each occupies a small set of conditions: a particular warmth, a scent trail, a root system, a seam of seeds, a patch of shade, a cavity that stays cool until dusk. But those small worlds overlap so completely that almost everything becomes useful to something else. A lizard’s basking ledge was once a ventilation shaft; a mouse’s burrow was once a store; a tree rises from a ruined nursery. None of these lives is building the plain as a whole. Each is making it possible for something else to live there next.
