Everything alive in the Sump has had to solve the same three problems: how to hold on, how to wait for water, and how to find others of its kind without relying on sound. Their solutions divide the room into habitats far smaller than the room. A filter cassette may be soft with moisture along its lower seam and dust-dry at the top. A copper rib can support a wet green pasture on one side and nothing but dormant crust on the other. The open centre, by contrast, is all one habitat, and only the birds are equipped to use it.

The base of the living lining is not soil but film. Microscopic algae spread wherever condensation and light coincide: inside gauges, behind glass, along the wet backs of fern trays, in the narrow green margins of gutters. Fungal threads run through the filter cake, binding insect paper, old roots, rubber dust, and guano into something roots can use. Some of the copper is locked away in blue, gritty sheaths before it reaches living tissue; enough remains to stain the roots turquoise. In the deepest felt, where no light reaches, the fungi and bacteria live almost entirely on what has already died.

Between them are minute animals easily mistaken for dust. They graze the films, work through the fungal felt, and contract into crevices when a cassette dries. A wet seam may contain several generations of them. A dry one may appear empty until a drop of condensation rests against it for an hour.

The ferns are mostly root. Their visible fronds are generous, but their real labour happens underneath, where wiry mats stitch themselves through felt and around aluminium edges. Each frond also works as a roof and a gutter: condensed water runs along its ribs, collects at the tip, and falls close to the parent roots. In long dry periods, new fronds remain furled for months. The plant does not spend water merely to demonstrate that it is alive.

Their spores are not readily shaken loose. They adhere in dark, slightly tacky patches until wetting releases them, so most travel with water rather than air. Many establish themselves within a hand’s breadth of the parent. The black-green moss in the cooling fins spreads still more cautiously, often by fragments lodged between metal surfaces. When dry, it becomes hard enough to crumble under a careless finger. When wet, it softens from the edges inward, the green returning before the whole cushion has had time to drink.

The crimson vines look loose from across the chamber but are not. Every heavy festoon is secured at close intervals by pale rootlets. Each node is a possible anchor and, if the stem behind it dies, a possible new beginning. This is why the vines can hang so heavily without being walked, segment by segment, into the throat.

The small white flowers grow low against warm metal, where their scent can travel along the wall without being torn immediately into the central air. Their principal visitors are the clear-winged moths. At rest, a moth is often visible only as a slight interruption beneath a leaf: a body, a few grey veins, and wings that seem to have been cleaned of colour. Its feet remain hooked until the moment it flies. Most flights are short, made from leaf to flower or from flower to the shelter of a panel. A moth that travels far into the centre becomes easy for the birds to see.

The moth larvae live less conspicuously in the filter beds, feeding on dead fern tissue, soft films, and the papery waste that collects there. Their pupal cases are glued beneath lips and behind insulation. Generations of discarded wings accumulate in the same sheltered seams, contributing to both the smell of the panel caves and the substance on which the crickets feed.

Those crickets are pale, blind, and almost absurdly deliberate. Their antennae investigate several body-lengths ahead while their feet remain fixed to metal. They can jump. They simply do not. Alarm makes them flatten and grip rather than launch themselves toward an opening they cannot see.

Their courtship passes directly through the pipes. A male braces himself and taps a pattern into the metal; somewhere along the same run, a female receives it through her feet. The engines supply a continuous vibration against which the small interruptions remain legible. Different sections of pipe carry the patterns differently, and a cricket may spend much of its adult life beside one favourable length. Its entire known world can consist of that pipe, the cavity behind one access panel, and the scent of old moth wings.

The spiders also hunt by touch, though theirs is a more patient discipline. Their webs are inelegant by garden standards: over-guyed, asymmetrical, and often containing more anchor line than catching surface. They occupy the acoustic shadows where the worst shaking is interrupted. The spider usually waits at a junction of several strands, one foot against the web and another against steel. It does not need to hear an insect strike. It needs only to distinguish an irregular struggling from the endless tremor that was there before.

No spider lowers itself casually into open air. A safety line remains attached. After rain, damaged silk is often drawn in and eaten before the web is rebuilt, so that even a failed trap returns partly to the body that made it.

Inside the gauges, the snails pursue life at another scale. Their shells are small, flattened, and clear enough that a green meal may show through the last whorl. Each snail leaves a cleaned track through the algal film, then crosses old tracks as the film regrows. When its gauge dries, it seals itself to the glass with a thin membrane and becomes a brown speck beside the needle. Condensation softens the seal. The snail emerges and resumes grazing, sometimes on the same curve it abandoned.

A single gauge can therefore contain an entire visible economy: green pasture, grazing animal, a cluster of eggs like tiny bubbles, and the slow painted divisions of an instrument whose readings mean nothing to any of them.

The frogs make ponds of whatever will hold water. They are small enough to sit on a fingernail, with broad, closely striated toe pads that let them remain on wet steel and wired glass. In dry periods they withdraw into the cracked junction box, the deep gutter joints, or the damp shelter of roots. They do not call. Courtship is a matter of following, touching, and sometimes transmitting a small tremor through a surface already shared.

The two centimetres of water in the tin cup are enough to begin a life, though not enough to guarantee one. The translucent eggs there develop with the cup’s vibration running continually through them. If they hatch, the tadpoles keep close to the bottom and graze the film on the tin rather than swimming in open water. A roof rain may deepen their pond and hurry them along. Without it, some clutches simply fail. The cracked junction box is more dependable; the gutters offer more water and less security. The frogs use all three. Those that succeed leave as minute froglets, climbing the cup wall and sometimes the brass chain into a world made almost entirely of vertical surfaces.

An adult frog’s territory may be one gutter mouth and the undersides of two leaves. The two slate-coloured birds, by contrast, possess the whole chamber. Their hunting looks mostly like idleness: long, silent turns through the centre, with the wall life sliding around them. Occasionally a turn breaks. A bird folds, checks, and takes something too small to see from the walkway. Then the smooth circling resumes.

Their roosts and nests are wedged deep into insulator caps, built from root fibre, moss, fine moulted material, and stolen spider silk. Nothing merely balances there. At close quarters the birds communicate with posture, small openings of the wings, and touch. They have no use for a song carried across the roar.

Now and then one clips the edge of the central column. Its feathers lift unevenly; the clean geometry of the turn is briefly disturbed. It emerges slightly higher and continues hunting. Neither bird remains within the column, and nothing nests or webs there. It is not a habitat. Even water only visits it.

Rain is when the Sump’s separated little climates exchange inhabitants. Moss softens and fern spores release. Snails unseal themselves and lay eggs. Frogs can cross exposed metal without immediately losing too much moisture. Moths travel between patches of flowers that, for most of the year, might as well belong to different rooms. Spiders retreat beneath ledges while their webs bow and brighten with water. Above them, the birds hunt through the droplets, taking insects and drinking without landing.

Those few minutes are also when losses occur. Eggs wash into drains. A moth is carried too far from the wall. A web gives way. The Sump’s fertility has never depended on every attempt succeeding, only on enough material remaining caught somewhere.

Afterward, the drains carry off what cannot be retained. The birds return to their turns. Frogs disappear into wet seams. Snails resume their cleaned circles, and spiders begin reclaiming silk. Guano, dead wings, roots, and the bodies of the very small return gradually to the filter beds. The system leaks constantly downward through the throat and outward through the drains; it persists by being parsimonious, not by being closed.

Nothing here has answered the engines by becoming louder. Moths use scent, crickets use pipes, spiders use silk, frogs use touch, and the birds use sight. The plants answer drought with stillness and attachment with roots. Even reproduction often waits for a few minutes of water.

The Sump is not lush because life has overrun the machine. It is lush because, after a very long sorting, almost everything left alive knows exactly where to hold on.
