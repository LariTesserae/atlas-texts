## Inhabitants of the High Engine

Individuality is a loose condition in the High Engine. A creature may begin on one crown, feed from a dozen regions of rind, carry another growth’s spores beneath its armor, and end as part of a pumping organ whose tissues are later grafted into roots hundreds of kilometers away. Its life has a beginning and often an end, but not always a clear boundary.

The rind itself is not quite one organism. It is a patchwork of growth provinces, some ancient and extensive, others no older than the last storm. Within a province, pressure and charge pass readily, and injury can alter growth far beyond the immediate wound. Between provinces, signals weaken or take unfamiliar forms. Their borders may appear as glassy welts, bands of unusually fine growth lines, or seams where a gleaner’s drumming abruptly loses its carrying power. Roots cross these borders sometimes. They graft, compete, or digest one another.

This matters because nearly every mobile creature must eventually negotiate with the ground again.

The crowns support whole populations of easily overlooked buds. Around their rotating sleeves live flattened, crescent-bodied creatures with a root mouth on the inner curve and a variable number of hooking limbs on the outer. They spend most of their lives within a few turns of the same seam, consuming frayed tissue and packed mineral dust. Behind them, the crown lays down fresh bearing surface. Some are buds of the crown itself; others arrive with spore fall and establish their own lineages in the grooves. A crown may carry millions without any visible change except a difference in the sound of its rotation.

Other buds work below ground. These are blind, cordlike animals, little more than rings of contractile tissue surrounding a root mouth and a vessel of oil. They travel through hollow root channels toward disturbances in current. When one finds a broken conductor, it anchors, opens its body along the fault, and begins depositing conductive grain. Its mobile phase ends there. A repaired root may therefore contain the flattened body of the creature that found the damage, its vessels incorporated into the new lining.

Between the crowns, the gleaners possess a more elaborate geography.

No gleaner has a standard adult shape. A newly detached one may have six or eight limbs and a smooth, flexible back. Additional limbs bud irregularly as it grows; scales separate, thicken, and begin to overlap. An old individual may have thirty legs, with one side of its body noticeably broader than the other. Its feet end in clear, siliceous caps. Every footfall is both a step and an examination of the ground.

Their vision is limited to dark sensory pits along the forward scales. They perceive glare, silhouettes, and the bright signaling of fliers, but their detailed world comes through the feet. From the return of each small vibration, a gleaner can distinguish old rind from hurried storm growth, a full oil vessel from an empty one, a mineral inclusion from a buried root. An active conduit produces a continuous faint pressure. Another herd produces pattern.

To a gleaner, the white plain is not featureless. It is crowded with buried avenues, rejected territories, charged seams, and distant signals.

In the cold before dawn, a herd may resemble a scatter of low bark fragments. As the pale sun reaches their backs and the oil in their vessels becomes less sluggish, legs begin to move in ones and twos. For several minutes the herd seems unable to coordinate itself. Then a common rhythm passes through it, and the whole scatter walks.

Feeding is usually less violent than the storms that provide much of their food. A gleaner lays its mouth against a growth line and repeats a root signal until the rind opens a narrow seam. It strips out freshly exposed conductive tissue, leaving the pale armor largely intact. Where the ground refuses the signal, it can cut with its jaws, but this costs oil and attracts scavengers. What it eats is reduced and carried in an internal bladder. As the bladder fills, the animal’s back rises and its gait changes.

A heavy gleaner often seeks a crown. Among the buttresses it presses its mouth or belly against an exposed root and remains attached for hours or days, transferring concentrate into the network. The crown may leave a reproductive bud growing beneath one of its scales. If the gleaner detaches, it carries that bud elsewhere. If it remains attached too long, its own vessels begin to widen into the root system.

Herds are not families. They form around compatible traveling rhythms and useful responses to the ground, and individuals join or leave without ceremony. Most of their drumming is practical: a fresh seam, a dangerous rise in charge, a root that has refused attachment, the approach of turbulent air. The hind feet carry the heaviest signaling caps, and their blows can be felt far beyond the herd.

The oldest wanderers add complications.

Some gleaners postpone rooting for centuries. Their armor becomes gray and terraced, their oil bladders subdivide, and their pace slows until they seem less like animals than uneven ground in motion. These are the longbacks. Younger gleaners frequently travel behind them, not because longbacks command a herd, but because they remember. They return to roots that accepted them generations earlier. They avoid provinces where their attachment signals fail. After several storms, they begin to alter old routes before the changes in the rind become obvious to younger animals.

Each longback also drums with a recognizable irregularity. Young gleaners copy portions of its patterns, and the copies change as they pass between herds. When two longbacks meet, they may remain on the same ground for days, exchanging long sequences until certain phrases in their rhythms begin to correspond. Some of these sequences have no evident use in feeding or storm avoidance. They are learned, they vary regionally, and they can outlast the animal that first produced them. They are the nearest thing in the Engine to names, routes, and histories carried from place to place.

Whether a longback should be considered a person is less clear than that it is an individual. It has preferences. It recognizes former herd companions. Its behavior cannot be predicted entirely from the present condition of the ground.

Its final rooting, when it comes, is not a sudden metamorphosis. The rear feet remain fixed after a period of rest. At first the animal could still tear them free; later it cannot. The hind scales spread and join the rind. The body continues forward for a while on its remaining legs, dragging a rooted posterior that is already becoming conductive tissue. Eventually the mouth closes, the bladder joins a reservoir, and the back armor flattens into a low, weathered terrace. A longback may continue drumming with its forward feet after much of the rest of it has ceased to be detachable.

Some become pumping bladders or crown buttresses. Others produce a thicket of temporary masts and disappear beneath their own growth. A few never root at all, but stop too far from an active network and are slowly opened by the rind.

The veilwings occupy a different High Engine.

Their central bodies are narrow, oil-heavy spindles; nearly everything else is wing. Seen at close range, the translucent membranes contain green-black photosynthetic threads, fine conductive paths, and rows of siliceous cells that can turn toward or away from the sun. They rarely flap. Launching requires wind, a crown’s updraft, or a violent charge difference. Once aloft, they flex their wings in slow scallops and steer along their charged edges.

They gather spores on their undersides and take light directly through the wing tissue, but they must descend for oil and mineral concentrate. At rest, a veilwing folds around its central body and extends one root into a crown terrace or a favorable seam of rind. A brief attachment replenishes it. A long one can end its flying life. The wing bases thicken, the body puts out vessels, and what had been an animal becomes a mast, a seed chamber, or the beginning of a new crown.

Their mirror signals carry across distances that even ground drumming cannot always span. Some are simple relays repeated from wing to wing ahead of a storm. Others appear to identify individuals: veilwings that have flown together before often approach with the same irregular alternation of white and transparent panels. When different aerial lineages meet over a spore sheet, their signaling becomes mixed and, for a time, conspicuously inefficient.

The root-mouthed swarms are less a distinct kind of animal than another possibility of development.

They bud wherever escaping oil and exposed tissue provide sufficient incentive: from the rind, from a fallen crown, sometimes from the wreckage itself. The youngest are almost entirely mouth, a collar of white rootlets dragged along by three or four adhesive limbs. Feeding adds segments, legs, and temporary armor. No two need become alike.

At first the swarm is intensely mobile. Individuals strip material, carry it a short distance, attach to the rind, discharge it, and return. As the wreckage diminishes, more of them remain attached. Legs shorten into anchors. Mouths widen into digestive seams. The apparent swarm gradually becomes part of the ground, leaving only pale stitches of new growth.

They do not reliably distinguish wreckage from an animal that can no longer defend its boundaries. A gleaner with damaged oil vessels may attract them while it is still capable of drumming. Nor is every bud useful to the network that produced it. Some lineages counterfeit attachment signals and draw oil from crowns without returning concentrate. Others establish themselves in growth-province borders and live entirely on what fails to cross.

The High Engine is orderly at continental scale and opportunistic at the scale of a body.

Each inhabitant therefore occupies a different version of the place. For a sleeve bud, it is a circuit of turning grain and periodic darkness. For a common gleaner, it is pressure, conductivity, and the signals of nearby feet. For a veilwing, it is a field of light, spore shadow, and moving charge. A longback carries something closer to a map, though one written in routes and rejections rather than distances.

Sometimes a herd passes over the rooted body of an old longback and changes its step. For several minutes, its drumming reproduces the irregular signature once used by that animal. The behavior may be remembrance, or only a route habit maintained because enough young gleaners learned it. The pattern travels on regardless. Other herds take it up, alter it, and carry it beyond the horizon.

A herd crossing the white ground is not traveling over the High Engine. It is the Engine in a mobile phase, carrying oil, spores, damage, and a few learned rhythms from one part of itself to another.
