A census of the Seep City would depend on when it was taken, and on what the census-taker was willing to count as a body.

During a thaw, the closest thing to a population gathers at the work faces. The mobile mechanisms have no common shape. Some are flattened, many-footed bodies that clamp themselves to ceramic and move by altering, one contact at a time, their adhesion to the surface. Others are jointed strips that can stiffen into braces, or tools given just enough mobility to reach the place where they will be used. There is no standard worker because there is no standard damage. A mechanism laid down at the foundry in the morning may be unmade by evening. Another, having remained useful, may persist through many freezes. Age gives it no particular claim on preservation.

They do not swim, and they avoid open water when they can. A loose drop can close over one, carry it away, or pin it so completely that recovery would cost more than replacement. Their senses are local: the drag of a film, pressure through a foot, resistance in a joint, the slight vibration of a pump communicated through stone. None needs a map of the whole pocket. Much of what looks like coordination is supplied by the prepared world around it. A surface accepts water; a neighboring one refuses it. A lane has been left empty. A foundation will bear one kind of load but not another. These are instructions as much as any pattern stored in the foundry.

At the intake, no single mechanism confronts an arriving drop. A whole edge of the district responds. One track becomes water-loving, another sheds water, and the bead lengthens across the treated boundary until a measured portion can be admitted toward the cisterns. Nearby mechanisms remain clamped to stone, ready to release their loads if the water changes course. At the scale of the city, the event has the character of a flood successfully negotiated. In its record, it is an intake cycle.

Because bodies are temporary, continuity lies elsewhere. A district’s foundations preserve more than its former shape. They contain tolerances, alternatives, sequences of deposition: not merely where a tower stood, but how to choose a workable tower after the floor has been lifted by ice. Reconstruction therefore does not produce an exact city restored from memory. It produces the next city that can occupy the same pocket. What persists is less a picture than a way of responding.

The city is casual about bodies and strict about conditions. A worker can be disposable while a small quantity of liquid in the inner cistern is not. A tower can vanish while the empty volume needed by expanding ice remains inviolate. Its rules rarely have to be announced to its inhabitants because they are already present in their anatomy. A carrier that cannot release its load is not made. A seal with no allowance for freezing is taken apart. A repair whose completion would demand too much heat remains unfinished. Nothing has to be persuaded to sacrifice itself for the city; most mechanisms were never given a separate existence long enough for sacrifice to have much meaning.

The biological inhabitants live by an entirely different set of necessities.

The gray bacterial film is not a citizenry maintained by the city. It is a recurring occupation of favorable chemistry. Its world consists of gradients: sulfur-bearing seep water on one side, oxygen on the other, liquid remaining a little longer in the shelter of an iron-black vein. A surface that changes its wettability may spread the film into a new thinness or abruptly withdraw the water around it. Freezing may end activity in one place while a sheltered trace persists elsewhere. When the gray fades and later returns, it does not matter to the city whether the new film has arisen from survivors or from cells carried in by the seep.

To the bacteria, the city is geology with sudden weather.

It neither protects nor persecutes them. Their film may coat storage yards, cistern margins, and old repair zones without provoking any response. Only when it thickens enough to interfere with an intake does its presence become an engineering fact. Then water may be routed around the obstruction, or briefly across it, until the passage is usable again. Elsewhere the film remains. The city has not incorporated it into a garden or excluded it as contamination. It is simply one of the things that happens when stone, sulfur, oxygen, and water are kept in contact.

A tardigrade introduces something less predictable: a large body with its own movements.

It arrives without any understanding of the pocket, and the pocket offers it no welcome. At midday its translucent body can cast a shadow across the central court. Water gathers around its legs. Feet intended for films and minute debris come down on ceramic lanes, and structures designed for exact, delicate loads fail beneath them. It grazes where the bacterial film is accessible. To the animal, the city is rough country with occasional food.

The mechanisms withdraw. Water routes open so that reservoirs are not displaced into passages where they would cause greater damage. Behind the animal, sometimes while its foremost legs are still entering another district, deposition begins again. There is no pursuit and no attempt to herd it. Opposing such a body would spend more energy and endanger more of the seam than letting it pass.

If it dies, the event does not end cleanly. Its body remains a load, an obstruction, and a slow alteration of the cistern chemistry. The distributed record need preserve no likeness of the creature. Duration, damage, displaced water, and the cost of reconstruction are sufficient. In that record, the animal belongs with freezing and flood: weather with eight feet.

After sunset, the census contracts again. Ice takes possession of the outer lanes. Some mechanisms have withdrawn toward the inner districts; others are frozen where they stand. A frozen body is not necessarily waiting to wake. It may later be recovered as material, or left in place while a different route is constructed around it. The city does not confuse survival with keeping all its parts animated.

On a rare night, one small white light may illuminate a repair body laying down a pale layer beneath new ice. Around it there is no sleeping crowd. There are immobilized tools, films rendered inactive by cold, reservoirs held at the narrow edge of freezing, and the seam undergoing its own minimal changes. When the work light goes out, the city becomes almost indistinguishable from the materials from which it will make its next population.

If anything lives there continuously, it is that capacity to begin again. It is not an immortal worker or a mind concealed in one of the pressure towers. It resides in the continued availability of certain acts: accepting water without being destroyed by it, freezing without sealing, making a tool only when its task has arisen, abandoning a structure before its preservation can close the seam. The city survives not by preventing the loss of its parts, but by ensuring that their loss does not exhaust its possibilities.

Once a year, that persistence acquires emissaries. The altered drops carry no mechanisms and found no colonies. They are not the city’s offspring. They are simply water on which a precise chemical difference has been impressed, released into a watershed large enough to read it. Somewhere below, the message is separated from the mountain’s ordinary leakage: the small cold place is still maintaining itself.

At the next thaw, new bodies will gather wherever the ice has made work necessary. The gray film may return along one wall. A lane may exist for only a few hours. The city will assemble the population that day requires, use it sparingly, and let much of it go—while leaving, in bare rock and stored instructions, enough room for another population to become possible.
