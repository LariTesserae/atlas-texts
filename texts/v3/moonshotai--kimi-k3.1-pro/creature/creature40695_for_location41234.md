The loudest thing in the country is not alive, and most of its inhabitants have responded by abandoning hearing.

Not by becoming insensitive. The stone is read through soles, bellies, antennae, and shells. The roar is the continuous background against which a much smaller vocabulary can be detected. A mite’s step is not a sound within it but a slight failure of it. A damp joint returns a different pressure from a dry one. A body crossing a fissure interrupts vibrations that have traveled unchanged through miles of rock. Life does not speak over the country. It makes small flaws in the way the country feels.

Nothing here makes itself conspicuous for long. There are no herds on the saddles, no flocks settling in the basins, no colonies spilling onto the open domes. Most encounters between large animals occur at a distance, and many occur only between one animal and another’s absence: a stripped carcass, a cropped cushion, an insect concentration already thinning. Yet the country is not inhabited merely at wide intervals. Its census changes completely when area is exchanged for depth.

The gray-green cushions are the visible citizens. What looks like felt is a multitude of leaves so small, thick, and closely folded that the whole plant resists a finger like a piece of hard hide. The living surface is only a fraction of it. Within, older tissue remains packed in place, holding mineral grains and a little water without becoming soil. Roots and rootlike threads descend much farther than the cushion rises, following capillary passages toward dampness. A plant no broader than a palm may therefore occupy, in some tenuous fashion, a body-length of stone.

Nothing about it hurries. There is no annual flowering and no moment when the whole cushion opens. Cells divide, reproductive bodies form and are released, old tissue dies, and the dimensions remain nearly fixed. A fragment may lodge in a rockrunner’s coat and be carried beyond the plant’s original basin. Almost all such journeys end on bare, dry rock. Enough do not.

The black lichens are even less separate from the country’s structure. Their hair-fine lines follow the paths along which moisture can approach the surface without exposed stone ever becoming wet. The dark line visible in a fracture is only the lichen’s lit and photosynthesizing portion; colorless filaments continue deeper. At one edge they extend into newly accessible mineral surface while, elsewhere, older material is grazed or dies back. The line can appear to have been drawn once, in a single stroke, although every part of it is slowly being replaced.

In the wettest recesses, the translucent scales and dense rosettes seem less like vegetation than a different state of water. Each clear scale is a small reservoir protected by tougher skins. Light reaches some of them directly at a cleft mouth; others receive only what has passed through pale stone or reflected down a narrow joint. They are firm and cold to the touch, never lush. Below the reach of light, sparse films of colorless cells persist on dissolved minerals and on organic matter carried inward by moisture. These films support little individually, but they extend through an enormous volume of stone.

A hand-length of damp cleft can consequently contain several worlds stacked one beneath another.

Near the upper, drier lip, mineral mites creep over surfaces that appear clean. Their backs are armored with pale grains until they become almost indistinguishable from the stone’s fine texture. They graze lichen, microscopic films, and the discarded skins of other animals. Their eyes are poor; some have only a light-sensitive patch that tells the open lip of a joint from its deeper recesses. Each foot tests the vibration transmitted by the surface beneath it. When another mite crosses the same seam, the two often change course without ever touching.

Farther down, where the stone is slick, glassy springtails move within the water film. Their bodies are transparent except for a narrow darkness of food in the gut. At their size, water is not simply something to drink or avoid. It can close over an animal and hold it more securely than a web. The springtail’s forked spring, which on open ground might have been used for leaping, is here often used to break surface tension or to tear a limb free from a meniscus. Some species can flatten themselves until the film is deeper than they are thick.

Lichen snails occupy the boundary between these depths. Their shells are compressed into shallow spirals, allowing them to pass where a rounded shell would catch. A snail may spend its whole life within a few body-lengths of the same black line, rasping one side while leaving enough behind for the lichen to persist. It does not lay down a shining trail across open stone. Moisture is conserved, drawn back toward the body, or lost only within the joint. When it seals itself against the rock, the shell’s rim becomes another pale lip among many.

The flat hunting insects live between and around all three. Their legs articulate sideways, and their bodies can be thinner than the lichen lines beneath which they wait. Some hold their forelegs in the wet layer while the rest of the body remains on dry stone above. They detect prey by the tiny disturbances transmitted through water and mineral: a springtail struggling against a film, a mite stopping to feed, a snail beginning to rasp. The hunter does not need to distinguish these vibrations from the roar as separate sounds. It waits for the roar’s local pattern to become wrong.

Even the cup-sized pools are inhabited. Beneath their protecting stone lips live pinhead crustaceans, blind larvae, and other white animals whose entire geography is the curvature of a few drops. Eggs are fixed beneath the overhang, beyond the reach of the surface skin. Generations pass without the water level visibly changing. A pool that appears, from above, to contain nothing but a point of light may contain hunters, grazers, eggs, corpses, and the young of several species, all within a volume that could be held in a thimble.

A few cleft insects have an aerial stage. They do not emerge together. There is no hour or season when the country fills with wings; individuals simply complete their development one by one. Most die close to the stone. Others are carried up along warm rock faces, where moving air gathers them into concentrations far denser than anything visible over the open dome. A soarer can find these faint, drifting rivers of food in a sky that looks perfectly empty.

No common rhythm joins any of this. There is no dawn emergence, no nightly stillness, no breeding season, and no general rest. A rockrunner may sleep while mites feed in the stone beneath it and a snail lays eggs a little deeper down. Elsewhere, at the same moment, other mites are motionless, other eggs are hatching, and another runner is crossing a basin. Activity is an individual matter of hunger, development, and bodily condition. The country never collectively wakes.

The steadiness of the roar does not keep them in agitation. A constant stimulus becomes, for a nervous system born inside it, part of the shape of existence. Resting animals remain sensitive not to its volume but to slight alterations: the approach of feet, the shift of a body at a cleft mouth, the small, irregular vibrations of prey. A sleeping rockrunner may lie with one broad foot against bare stone. Its eyes can close; the foot does not stop listening in its own manner.

The rockrunners themselves are often first understood as stones that have changed position.

At rest, their compact bodies and pale coats merge with the rounded blocks. In motion, the length of their limbs becomes apparent. They cross open rock with an economical, deliberate gait, broad fleshy feet spreading over small irregularities and leaving no audible step. The feet are both support and instrument. Pressed firmly to a dome, they can distinguish the duller, closer return of damp stone from the freer vibration of a dry mass. Some cleft insects can be located in the same way, as thin and intermittent threads within the ground’s heavier foundation.

A runner’s visible coat is only part of its signaling equipment. Beneath it, along the inner limbs and in folds near the body, are areas of skin so dark that their sudden exposure seems to make a hole in the white light. By turning, rising, lowering the body, or holding one limb away from the flank, a runner can produce a flash visible at distances where its outline has nearly vanished. Other postures are used at close range. These signals regulate approach, withdrawal, mating, and the temporary tolerance of one animal for another at a feeding cleft. They do not develop into long conversations. Most encounters end with the two animals again alone.

Each runner keeps an unmarked knowledge of seeps, insect-rich joints, sheltered cavities, and cushions that can withstand cropping. This knowledge is not written into routes. Feet pass over the same stone without making a trail, and no object is placed to indicate that water lies below. An individual may return after a long interval to a cavity indistinguishable, to an eye, from thousands of others and put its tongue directly to the one damp portion of the wall.

Its feeding is precise. Cleft insects are drawn out with lips, tongue, or a narrow claw. The tough egg cases are more difficult. A runner may sit back, free a forefoot, and select an unshaped pebble. One stone is used to crack the case; another may be braced against a shell while the animal worries it open. The choice can be exact, the handling deft. Then both stones are dropped. The runner does not carry the better one, although it may have solved the problem with it only moments before. A young animal can learn something by watching its mother, but no implement survives the lesson. Their intelligence goes into memory, discrimination, and problems that disappear when solved.

For a while after a birth, the solitary animal becomes two. The young runner stays near its mother, at first within a natural cleft and later at a little distance on the open stone. It learns the meaning of her dark flashes and the patience required at a seep. There are no calls between them. If separated, they search by sight and by the faint vibration of movement. Eventually the young animal’s circuit and the mother’s cease to coincide. Nothing marks the separation, and neither range becomes a territory with defended edges.

The high soarers inhabit a less crowded geometry.

Their long, narrow wings and small bodies turn distance into their principal resource. A basin that requires days for a runner to cross may be only one portion of a soarer’s passage between a warm face, an insect concentration, and a carcass. Usually there is a single narrow cross above the stone. When another appears, the two need not be traveling together. Their paths merely share, for a time, the same invisible architecture of rising air.

A soarer’s bill serves both kinds of feeding. At a carcass it becomes a fine instrument for tearing tendons, skin, and dried muscle into pieces small enough to swallow. In flight, the animal can strain tiny insects from concentrations too sparse to appear as a cloud. On the ground it loses much of its elegance. The wings fold into awkward lengths, and it moves carefully over the stone, taking what it needs without the crowded scavenging seen in richer countries.

Its egg is laid directly in a suitable natural cavity. There is no nest to inspect afterward: no lining, rim, platform, or arrangement of stones. One parent remains with the egg or young animal while the other ranges for food, though the bond need not outlast the rearing. The chick grows in the same unvarying light and vibration as everything else. When it is ready, it climbs or is encouraged to the cavity’s lip and opens wings that have never yet supported it. Its first flight may carry it farther from its birthplace than a rockrunner travels in years.

Even death is absorbed without becoming a general event.

A dead runner on an open dome is not immediately surrounded. A soarer arrives, then perhaps another at another time. Small scavengers reach the body through joints or across the stone. Material is removed piece by piece, some of it carried back into the damp food web, some consumed where it lies. Hide, hair, and the hardest tissues remain after the softer parts are gone, but they are worked at until no clean skeleton remains to serve as a landmark. Nutrients too scarce to waste are returned to mouths and films. Elsewhere, while that one body is being reduced, other animals are being born at the same unspectacular rate.

This balance is not gentleness. Individuals miss food. Hunters take the young. Eggs fail, springtails become trapped in water, and soarers sometimes come down where they cannot rise again. A particular cleft may be rich for part of an animal’s life and unforgiving for another. The equilibrium belongs to the immense census, not to the experience of any inhabitant.

Nor do their lives make the country progressively theirs. The cushion does not become soil; the snail does not widen its crack; the runner does not improve its pebble; the soarer leaves no nest. Bodies pass through, knowledge dies with individuals or survives only partially in a young animal’s attention, and the stone receives no enduring instruction.

On an open dome, a rockrunner may stop with one foot pressed to the ground. Above it, almost beyond vision, a soarer adjusts a wing. Beneath its foot, mites alter their paths, a hunter waits with its forelegs in a film of water, and a cupful of white animals turns through the small dimensions of its entire world.

Nothing calls. Nothing gathers.

Lift a hand from the stone and the census disappears: pale distance, fixed light, perhaps one plant in an acre, perhaps no animal larger than a thumb for a day. Put the hand back, and the fine animal threads are there again, interrupting the deeper vibration in every direction. The inhabitants do not disturb the country’s one note. They live within it as brief, delicate irregularities, and the visible land remains empty only for as long as it is observed merely with the eyes.
