The keepers live in the thickness of the garden rather than around its edges. Their beds are dry alcoves let into the mineral walls, lined with old nest-fiber and mats woven from fallen fronds. Some are barely wider than the person sleeping in them. Others open beneath roots, so that a sleeper wakes with the slow weight of a braided trunk overhead. A household need not occupy one cavity, or even one contiguous place. It may consist of a sleeping niche below the eastern stair, a tool shelf half the garden away, and a small court where several related keepers take their meals. When the joins are sound, these places are consecutive. When they are not, someone may have to go visiting in order to reach their own eating court. This is treated as an inconvenience, not a marvel.

Only a few keepers are usually visible at any hour. One may be sweeping pollen from a seam; another may be almost hidden in the canopy, reaching through leaves to test a lashing with two fingers. Their clothes are close-woven and faded to the colors of tile dust, old cord, and dry leaf. Pollen gathers at their hairlines and in the folds behind their ears. Most work barefoot, because a tile’s slight rocking can be felt through the sole before it can be heard. At the waist they carry a stone mallet, a copper awl, perhaps a bone knife, and one or more coils of cord. The cord is often the most personal possession. A carelessly handled mallet can bruise someone; a carelessly handled route-cord can make a household difficult to find for several seasons.

Like everything native to the garden, the keepers are dry-bodied. They do not sweat, and mineral dust remains on their skin until they rub it away with oil. Their meals are crushed seed, bitter pith, salt, and the richer oils pressed between warm stones, usually wrapped in waxy leaves rather than served in vessels. A cut finger is sealed with the same amber resin used to close a damaged stem. They find the garden’s air pleasant. Wetter air, on the rare occasions they encounter it above the steps, feels to them heavy and strangely unresolved.

They are patient, but not uniformly gentle or serene. Keepers argue about whether a peg should be replaced now or left until the surrounding root has finished swelling. They complain about birds stealing fiber, conceal bad work beneath good lashings, and occasionally take an immediate dislike to someone whose knots are perfectly sound. Their patience is a craft learned through consequences. A keeper who has spent eleven years narrowing an ill-considered opening may still be irritable while doing it.

Children begin by sweeping, sorting, and carrying. They learn which fragments go into a planting bed, which can be twisted into fiber, and which must be laid aside because no one is yet certain what they have become. Before a child is permitted to tie a structural knot, they are given ruined cord to unpick. This teaches the hands more than dexterity: fiber remembers strain, a knot can conceal a weak place, and the other end of a line is not necessarily where it was when last seen. The other early lessons are simpler. Do not step over a hanging cord without looking at it twice. Do not lift a frond crowded with resting moths. If a distant mallet is not followed by a second blow, there is no need to go searching for the worker.

No keeper knows the whole garden. Knowledge is distributed among households, and even within a household one person may know the approach to a place while another knows the way back. Routes are learned as sequences of pressure, scent, and sound: warm tile, moss, the bitter smell of a crushed fern edge, a left turn at the copper pin, then the brief coolness beneath overlapping leaves. A child repeats these sequences while walking behind an elder, sometimes for years. A keeper who can identify every plant in the canopy but cannot return from it unassisted is still considered inexperienced.

When two keepers disagree about a route, age earns courtesy but not correctness. The person who walked it most recently usually has the strongest claim. If both have walked it recently and both are right, the route is treated carefully until its two versions can be given separate cords. There is no appeal to a plan. There is only walking, waiting, and the gradual discovery of which assertions the garden is prepared to keep.

When a keeper dies, the routes are attended to first. Their personal cord is walked by others, checked, and either divided among the household or slowly unpicked. The body is laid in an unused bed beneath dry manufactured soil, without resin. Beetles, roots, and time do the rest. The mallet passes to another hand; the sleeping alcove is cleaned and eventually occupied. No name is cut into a wall. Years later, someone may still set pegs with the dead keeper’s particular half-turn of the wrist, or avoid a certain seemingly convenient knot without remembering who taught them to avoid it. This is usually remembrance enough.

Of the smaller inhabitants, the copper-black beetles have the greatest influence on construction. The keepers treat them less as pests than as small, willful surveyors. Their customary crossings are paved, their processions are allowed to pass through work, and the narrow tiles beneath their feet gradually acquire a polished line. If such a procession begins to divide, with some beetles arriving before others that started ahead of them, a keeper may spend the afternoon following on hands and knees. The beetles are not assumed to be correct about anything. They have simply noticed something early.

The step-lizards are more individually known. Particular animals occupy particular warm edges for years, flattening themselves so closely to the mineral that their throats seem to pulse directly against it. A few acquire names based on old injuries or favored tiles. The keepers maintain that these names are merely convenient. They also know which lizard prefers the shade side of a broken corner and which will take a beetle from an open palm if the palm remains still long enough. Shed skins are gathered after the animals have finished worrying them loose, and nothing is done to discourage the birds from taking a lizard that has grown too bold in open light. Even a named lizard is not considered an exception to the garden’s feeding arrangements.

The pale moths are poor guides but useful evidence. They travel through seams that no keeper could widen safely, and they emerge carrying wing-dust, seed hairs, or pollen from beds whose current adjacency is uncertain. Orange pollen on a moth found beneath a western fern may be the only indication that a sunlit flower bed near the eastern stair is still, in some fashion, its neighbor. Keepers therefore note where moths appear but rarely follow where they go. A moth can afford to be casual about an opening that would take decades to repair.

Thread-snakes are the reason cord is never gathered hurriedly. They take on the slack of a line and the proportions of a knot with remarkable patience. A keeper lifting a coil waits first for warmth, movement, or the slight dry touch of a tongue. If a snake is living in a useful length of fiber, both are carried to a trellis and left there until the snake chooses another arrangement. The ash-colored birds cause more persistent damage. They steal loose fiber for their nests and have more than once flown off with the free end of something important. Bundles of frayed, attractive cord are therefore hung in the canopy as decoys. The birds accept these without becoming tame. At dusk they settle among the braided trunks, occasionally giving their single seed-click, while below them the day’s last lizards remain on the steps, unwilling to leave the stored warmth.

Other lives are known mainly by interruption. The felt-moss sometimes dimples in a narrow advancing line, though nothing rises high enough to be seen. Pollen is found neatly cleared from the interior of a closed tile joint. A dry scale, too broad for any thread-snake commonly handled by the keepers, appears beneath a frond and is gone by the next afternoon. Such signs do not automatically lead to investigation. An inhabitant does not need a name in order to be left sufficient room.

The keeper currently tending the shadow-bed is young, and knows the absent tree better than she knows many of the living ones. She knows which small fork in its shadow first reaches the wall, where the branches cross without becoming confused, and which darker patches the beetles prefer. She weeds around a central space she has never measured, because measuring it would require deciding how thick the trunk ought to be. Instead she leaves room, adds soil when the roots elsewhere begin to approach, and checks the three enclosing knots by touch.

Lately she has been setting wooden pegs near that central space. Most remain pegs. She removes those that would obstruct later work and leaves the others to dry, split, or be taken gradually into the bed. One has put out a single pale shoot.

She has not announced that the tree is arriving. The shoot may become an ordinary slender growth, useful eventually for cord or shade, or it may exhaust itself after one leaf. She has not tied anything to it. She has only moved a nearby weed so that its root will not cross the shoot’s root, and brushed away enough pollen to see whether the stem has swollen since her last visit.

When the shadow begins its afternoon climb, she gathers her cord and goes home. Behind her, beetles settle into the darkness beneath branches that are not there. A lizard takes its accustomed place along the edge. The pale shoot remains inside the precise, empty silhouette of the trunk, growing without being asked to explain what it is.
