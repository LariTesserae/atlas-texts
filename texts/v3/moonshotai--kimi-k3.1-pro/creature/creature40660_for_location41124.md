By living mass, Senn belongs to the orchards. Its people are too few to give the shell a human scale. A basket moving along a ledge, an arm emerging from foliage, a shaded face bent to a wall: these are brief events in a city otherwise occupied by leaves, roots, dust, and vibration. Yet without those few people, the green tiers would become ruin with remarkable speed. The inhabitants are less the city’s rulers than the small, mobile part of its metabolism.

They are dry-bodied, as everything enduring in Senn must be, though not brittle or plantlike. Their skin is supple and protected by natural oils; their food supplies what their bodies require without drinking. The black orchard seeds are milled into a dark, oily meal, pressed for oil, or worked with their own oil into dense pastes and baked. There are mixing bowls, storage jars, lamp reservoirs, and oven trays, but nothing that would suggest a people secretly waiting for water.

They have no special immunity to the light or the roar. During the white hours, many wear narrow-slitted shades woven from black fiber or stiffened with varnish. Waxed plugs remain in their ears through much of their work. Their most important adaptation is not an unusual organ but a cultivated sensitivity spread through the whole body. Soles, fingertips, lips, cheekbones, and teeth all become instruments. An experienced inhabitant can identify another person approaching by the slight rhythm transmitted through a balcony, and can distinguish the weight of a full basket from an empty one without turning around.

Their language has a full form and a reduced form. Face to face, it uses the hands, face, shoulders, and the distance between bodies; it can be precise, evasive, affectionate, or funny. The tapped language carried through rails and floors is not simply that language spelled out in vibrations. It is a compact body of instructions, warnings, questions, names, and acknowledgments, with longer messages relayed from person to person. Every personal name therefore has at least two existences: one visible and one tangible. A name tapped into a beam may arrive slightly changed by the condition of the wood, and people sometimes claim they can tell who relayed it.

Tapping is not always practical. Children elaborate messages until they become games, and adults sometimes send patterns whose only purpose is to be felt and answered. Laughter usually cannot be heard, but it can be seen, and if the person laughing is touching a railing, it can occasionally be felt three rooms away.

Senn cannot support specialists who do only one thing, but expertise still differs. One person is better at reading a wall; another knows the temperament of the old seed trees; another can repair a mill with the least waste of its wearing surfaces. Authority tends to last for the duration of a task. A gardener may direct six people during a dangerous repair and then spend the next amber period carrying away her own share of dust. A visually perfect repair is not accepted if it feels wrong. “Wrong” does not mean loud or quiet. It means that pressure catches where it should pass, or that a second vibration has begun to worry the edge of the first.

The public ovens are part of the same discipline. Their number is not simply evidence that Senn once held crowds. Some have not baked anything in living memory but remain essential because their chambers interrupt a harmful resonance. Others may be used only at particular phases of the shell’s expansion. A cook cannot merely choose the nearest oven and light it. Heat changes the wall, and the wall changes the gardens. Baking, masonry, and acoustics are not separate crafts.

There is no common night in which the whole population sleeps. People rest behind baffles and heavy fiber hangings, at different hours, often near their current work. The white hours bring leaf-opening and close inspection of growth. The amber hours favor other tasks, until a transition splits the dry capsules and harvest suddenly takes precedence over everything else. Then gardeners appear along several tiers at once, gathering black seeds from foliage and ledges while fresh mineral dust threads down the walls above them.

None of this makes the people uniformly patient or serene. They quarrel over favored cuttings, neglected tools, and who opened a flue too far. A gardener may become fond of a magnificent vine and postpone cutting it until its roots have entered a parapet. People who dislike one another can live out of sight for long stretches, which is easy in Senn; pretending that the other person’s work does not matter is much harder. Interdependence has not made them harmonious. It has made their arguments consequential.

The plants they argue over are not passive possessions. Some of the balcony vines may be the oldest continuous lives in the shell, although “individual” becomes difficult to apply to them. Stems are layered, rooted, cut back, and trained again until no original portion remains aboveground, while below the ledge the same patient system continues. A vine can be crop, cordage, future beam, and present structural danger. Its prunings may one day support the balcony from which it hangs.

The fan-leaved shrubs are living indicators as much as greenery. Their constant dry clatter is part of their circulation, and a change in their movement can reveal a blocked passage before masonry begins to fail. The black-green roof cushions bind loose grit and hold it against the tremor, but left alone they also work their roots into mortar. The coppery orchard trees are the most directly useful and the most closely watched. Their seeds sustain the inhabitants; their roots mine the very substance from which new bricks must be made.

Consequently, luxuriance is not always reassuring. A pale, rigid plant may mean too little vibration. A brilliantly lush one may mean that a wall is being consumed. Gardening in Senn often consists of reducing abundance by almost imperceptible amounts: narrowing a flue, withdrawing a root, replacing a few bricks, waiting. The shell also performs most of the work of shaking loose pollen and seed. The gardens do not depend on the rare moth, or on any other animal servant.

The belief that old sound settles in the low lanes gives those places a peculiar social weight. The inhabitants do not generally imagine that they can hear their dead there, or that whole conversations remain available to anyone sufficiently skilled. Nearly everything has been worn beyond identity. Still, the lowest lanes can feel occupied in a way the open center does not. Some people avoid them. Others go there when they want the company of a past too damaged to make demands.

The central emptiness has almost the opposite meaning. It is not a temple, and there are no offerings at its rim. Its dust discs and hanging curtains are useful: a change in their spacing may send someone to inspect a flue or stairwell several tiers away. But generations of restraint have given the hollow a character beyond acoustics. It is the only part of Senn that no one is permitted to make more useful. Nothing there needs pruning, lifting, mortaring, or replanting. A person sitting beside it has not escaped the noise, but has escaped, for a little while, the possibility of repairing the thing in front of them.

And then, irregularly, there is the moth.

Its early life is almost entirely hidden. The eggs lie in discarded seed capsules, and the larvae seem to live on traces of oil and dry tissue left in the husks. No change in seed crop, temperature, or flue work has allowed the inhabitants to predict an emergence. The adult is colorless except when its wings catch the light; then the mineral powder on them breaks into a brief gray or white glitter. It may actually be shell glass gathered at emergence, or something produced by the moth itself. No one has established which, and no one has made the matter urgent enough to justify destroying many specimens.

The moth appears to have no necessary office. It does not pollinate an orchard that would otherwise fail. Its arrival does not reliably precede a good harvest, and its absence for years does not produce a bad one. Senn could continue without it.

Nevertheless, when one flies, the news passes quickly from hand to hand and rail to rail. People leave mills and climb until they find a sightline. They do not usually try to catch it. They watch it move with an independence possessed by nothing else in the shell: not driven along a stem by vibration, not falling from a capsule, not carried in a basket for repair.

Sometimes it crosses the central hollow.

For those few wingbeats, the emptiness becomes distance around a small living body rather than a chamber in which the shell assembles its note. Then the moth disappears against the luminous roof or settles into foliage, and the work resumes. The orchards still feed and undermine the city; the people still keep brick from becoming soil too quickly and soil from returning to brick too slowly. But for a little while afterward, the messages traveling through Senn’s railings concern something that owes nothing to the balance, and was alive simply by going where it went.
