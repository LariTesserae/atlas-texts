The people who live on the Cistern Ledge are fewer than its rooms suggest. Usually there are a dozen or so, occasionally as many as twenty, distributed among households that may consist of a family, an elder and an apprentice, or several unrelated adults sharing a kitchen. They are not all old, not all related, and not sworn to remain. Some were born on the ledge; others came up the path to help with a season’s work and gradually acquired a bed, a round of duties, and a recognizable tread on the stairs. People leave. Others return. The town’s continuity has never depended on keeping the same bodies inside the parapet.

Outsiders call them keepers, and they accept the word because it is approximately true. Among themselves, however, keeping is more often a task than a title. One person currently has charge of the upper roofs, another of the public basin, another of the dry stores or the workshop. These responsibilities change with strength, skill, and absence. An elder whose balance has become uncertain may know more about condensation than anyone else on the ledge and still not walk the roofs alone. No one is keeper of the whole town. In a dispute, authority often belongs to the person who can kneel, point to grit or a darkening seam, and show the others what the water is actually doing.

Every dwelling is private at its door and public along its roof. A resident may shut out the lanes for days, but cannot treat the slab overhead as a personal possession. Before altering even a short length of channel, the people below it must be consulted. This produces a particular kind of neighborliness. Someone may know very little about another household’s private affairs and still know exactly where its overflow goes, which of its downspouts clogs first, and whose storage jar would suffer if a roof groove were deepened.

Children learn the settlement in this way: less as a map of streets than as a sequence of consequences. Grit swept carelessly from one roof becomes an obstruction in the next court. A pan emptied in the wrong place darkens a wall that should dry. A shrub allowed to root beside an overflow may be harmless for years and then lift one stone enough to matter. The rules are taught with their reasons, because a keeper who remembers only the rules will eventually meet a situation they do not cover.

Their expertise is mostly a matter of noticing departures. After a beaded dawn, a groove that remains dry can be as informative as one that overflows. An experienced keeper can distinguish water dropping into a deep cistern from water striking a shallow pool, and can hear when an ordinary interval has shortened. They know which north wall should remain dark longest after rain, which shutter always knocks twice before settling, and which patch of pavement becomes dusty first. None of this is mysterious. It is the accumulated result of paying attention to a small place for a long time.

The gatehouse holds some of that knowledge in physical form. Among the dry cord and timber are measured lengths, knotted gauges, and thin wooden patterns recording the profiles of slabs and the depths of older grooves. These are not venerated templates. When a better repair supersedes one, its pattern may be altered or replaced. Much more remains in practice: how to set a wooden wedge so that stone splits without bruising, how much mortar to use where a joint must later be opened, how to brush a channel clean without polishing its bottom into a shallower slope.

Repairs are not signed. Nevertheless, keepers often recognize one another’s work by the spacing of wedges or the finish left along an edge, and this can be a source of private pride or mild teasing. The praise they value most is retrospective. If someone inspecting a court years later has to ask where the crack used to be, the repair has done everything required of it.

The quiet does not make them uniformly patient or serene. They argue about stores, postpone unpleasant jobs, form unreasonable dislikes, and occasionally insist on being right after the evidence has turned against them. A person can sulk for three days on the ledge; there is simply no crowd in which to conceal it. When children are present, they sometimes run and shout. Laughter in one of the courts can be startling, not because it is prohibited but because the town gives it so much room. A shutter struck too hard in irritation may be identified by sound from several lanes away.

They dress for glare, cold, and work rather than for office: layered cloth brought up from below, patches at the knees and elbows, close wrappings when the wind rises, and soft footwear repaired until the uppers and soles no longer seem related. Stone dust pales the seams of their cuffs. There is no keeper’s robe. The clearest sign that someone currently has a responsibility is that they are carrying a brush, a wooden lever, or a coil of cord, and that someone else expects them somewhere.

Their conservatism is similarly specific. A better brush fiber, a new way of dressing timber, or a song learned from a porter may spread through the town in a season. A new roof pitch is another matter. They distrust any improvement whose failure would appear first in someone else’s cistern. This does not prevent change; it makes change answerable to its consequences.

The ledge cannot feed them, and they do not pretend otherwise. Grain, dried pulses, oil, salt, and fiber arrive at long intervals in back-loads small enough for the approach path. These supplies are paid for largely with work done elsewhere: keepers spend seasons in lower settlements repairing tanks, laying out channels, or teaching others to read the behavior of a roof. A delivery may also bring a visiting relative, a prospective worker, or someone simply curious to see whether the town is as empty as reports claim. It does not produce a market. Loads are divided, stores inspected, news exchanged, and the court becomes quiet again.

Their meals are plain, but scarcity is not treated as a virtue. Stores are checked so that people need not go hungry merely to appear disciplined. Water is not worshipped either. A sick person is given what they need; after sufficient rain, washing may be done more generously. To waste drinking water is considered foolish rather than sacrilegious. The patches receiving used wash-water are useful damp ground, not symbolic gardens, and no one tries to enlarge them into a promise the roofs cannot keep.

A guest is usually shown the public basin, the washing place, and the nearest overflow before being shown the view. Hospitality is real but not theatrical. There is food if food is available, a dry room, and very little ceremony. A visitor who stays begins by being told why each small task is done. One who stays longer notices that the explanations gradually stop. Belonging often begins there: not with a vow or a name entered anywhere, but with being handed a blocked channel stone and trusted to deal with it without disturbing the rest.

The keepers have few possessions, though not none. Most people own something useless by the standards of the ledge—a cup brought from below, a smooth stone of the wrong color, a length of dyed cord kept long after it has ceased to be strong enough for work. Such things are not confiscated or mocked. There is simply little reason to acquire duplicates of objects that already serve. When someone leaves, tools generally remain in the common workshop. Personal things leave with them, pass to another person, or remain on a shelf until their significance is forgotten and their use becomes plain again.

They keep no domestic animals. Every animal would require food carried up the path or water diverted from a system with little to spare. The slate-colored bird is therefore not a pet, though its visits are noticed. The keepers speak of “the parapet bird” without insisting that it is always the same individual. It comes, drinks if the coping hollow holds water, and leaves. No one feeds it in order to make its return more dependable.

The evening insects are watched only closely enough to ensure that they are not breeding where drinking water can be fouled. The shrub beyond the gate is neither harvested nor tended. It is not used as an omen, though visitors are inclined to treat anything surviving there as one. Moss and wiry grass are left where their presence does not obstruct a joint or channel; a seedling rooted where its growth may move stone is removed. This can look like indifference, but it is more exact than that. Life is allowed its established terms. It is not recruited into the town’s design, and nothing is encouraged to depend on a supply that cannot be promised.

What remains most precarious is the shared memory of how the ledge works. It cannot be kept entire in patterns, lists, or rules. A newcomer can learn in an afternoon where every cistern lies; learning which sounds deserve attention takes seasons. For that reason, inexperienced keepers are given real work while there is still someone nearby to correct them. A system explained but never touched produces visitors, not successors.

Late in the evening, when no one is visible and the courts have resumed their appearance of emptiness, a drop may strike within a covered cistern with a slightly altered note. Somewhere, a keeper hears it through an open window. They listen until it comes again. Perhaps it is nothing. Perhaps a channel has begun to fail. They get up to find out, without waking the town or making a declaration about duty.

That, more than careful speech or familiarity with stone, is what makes someone a resident of the Cistern Ledge: the willingness to have sleep, work, or an argument interrupted by a very small sound—and the practiced knowledge to tell whether it matters.
