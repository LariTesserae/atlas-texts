No one lives in the Thrum in the sense its doorways once implied. There are no sleepers behind the clay shutters, no keepers of the kilns, no hidden vermin making a second town below the first. Yet the hollow is inhabited. Its population is rooted, oil-veined, and almost immovable, and its visible luxuriance is only the outer part of a much older life underground.

Nothing there drinks. A broken stem does not bleed watery sap; it yields a slow bead of bitter oil, sharpens the air with pepper, and then seals itself beneath a skin of resin. Living tissue is built from waxes, tough fibers, and mineral-bearing filaments. Growth means adding substance cautiously and losing as little as possible. Even the broadest leaf is less like a surface plant’s brief green extravagance than like a tool intended to remain in use.

The oldest inhabitants are probably the white roots. They cross the salt in branching script, enter the paving, and follow the buried conduits until root and clay can scarcely be distinguished without breaking both. At their tips they exert a patient pressure and chemistry, splitting mineral grains and carrying away what can be used. Others extend between the hot floor and the less-hot air or masonry above it, drawing sustenance from the differences maintained across that narrow interval.

Where roots meet, some remain separate and some fuse. This makes any census uncertain. The growth around one court may consist of many individuals; elsewhere, a stand of canes, several bronze fans, and part of a curtain may share a body far older than any of their visible parts. The root binding a wall is not necessarily related to the root splitting the block beside it, but neither is it certainly distinct.

These roots hold the town together without preserving it. They stitch loose masonry, then thicken until a stone flakes under the strain. They line a conduit and alter its note, but heat and scouring kill back any growth that penetrates too far. At the edges of flues, living white tissue, dead black lace, and polished clay form narrow bands that have remained almost stationary for ages.

The first visible growth over them is often olive felt. Its cushions are made of filaments so fine that, from a few paces away, they resemble mineral stains. Close to, they have a dense, springy structure, with a living surface over layers of older material. They collect powder too fine for roots to gather directly and hold it against the scouring drafts. Slowly, the underlying roots reclaim it.

Nothing in the felt becomes soft with decay. Dead filaments compact into a dark, resilient mass, split when the temperature changes sharply, and are gradually worn free. A thick cushion may therefore contain only a thin living skin. The rest is its own history, still useful as support and shelter until it becomes dust.

From the felt rise the bronze-edged fans. Their black-green surfaces make use of the chamber’s weak pewter light, though light alone could not sustain them; much of their nourishment arrives through the roots. Each leaf is thick, slightly cupped, and warmed through by the wall behind it. The fine bronze seam is mineral-rich tissue, conducting heat along the edge and strengthening the places where thermal cracks would otherwise begin.

The fans move, but too slowly to watch. Over long intervals they alter the angle of a leaf, presenting a dark face to the dim light or turning a reflective underside toward a wall that has grown fiercely hot. What looks from one visit to the next like a fixed arrangement is, across years, a continuous adjustment. An old fan may carry leaves of very different ages: one broad and polished, one split and resin-scarred, one reduced to bronze-veined lace but still attached.

The wine-dark curtains are slower still. Their roots occupy high fissures around the covered walk and the backs of roof piers. From these anchored bodies the fronds descend, growing among the remains of earlier growth. A heavy curtain is not wholly alive. Its youngest tissues hang among layers of dead fronds that have hardened without falling, forming a fibrous architecture able to support still more growth.

During the great exhalation, these curtains do not simply tear or collapse. Their fronds turn edge-on, the dead layers flex, and the whole mass streams toward the wall. Sand hisses through them. Some of it remains caught in the lace, adding minute weight and changing the way the curtain will move during later cycles. When the pressure reverses, fine slits along certain fronds open and release spores into the returning current. The spores are so small that they travel less like seeds than like a brief discoloration of the air.

The ochre canes inhabit hotter, more exposed positions: beside the public kilns, around workshop yards, and near flues whose breath is strongest. Their joints record long pauses rather than rapid seasons. A cane may persist for an extraordinary time without adding height, thickening its old segments and concentrating oil around the seed structures at its summit.

Those seed capsules are the most exact inhabitants of the town. Their valves differ in weight, tension, and the width of their mineral lips. While closed, they rattle and knock in smaller drafts, gradually becoming polished at the edges. At last, when the surrounding conduits reach a particular resonance, a valve flexes beyond its limit and the capsule opens.

The canes supply many of the pale seeds that suddenly fill the hollow. Higher among the fans and curtain growths, smaller resinous structures release yellow pollen into the same current. Neither process requires a carrier. Fertilization and dispersal depend on being loose in the air at the right pressure, in a draft passing the right surfaces.

Different groups of canes answer different parts of the roar. Capsules beside a deep floor conduit have heavier valves than those growing near a narrow roof flue. Around a root-narrowed pipe, whose voice has lowered over centuries, the local seed heads have changed with it. No capsule hears the note. Its response is entirely mechanical; selection has done the hearing.

For a seed, the central vacancy is the great event of its existence. It contains no food and offers no place to anchor, but it provides an uninterrupted flight. A seed may cross nearly the whole settlement, passing roofs and courts without touching them, before striking a wall or dropping into the shallow dish of a threshold. Most seeds accomplish nothing. Some are lifted again during a later exhalation. A few reach olive felt, a root-lined fissure, or a pocket of powder protected by a curb.

Germination requires no rain. It requires a suitable mineral contact, sustained warmth, and a foothold stable enough to survive the next drafts. The first growth is usually white root. A visible shoot comes much later. By then, the seed’s pale coat may already have been reduced to a few flakes among the dust.

On the returning current, spores settle elsewhere: behind gratings, in the worn corners of shared stairways, along the shaded sides of conduits, and in the dead lace beneath old fans. Most fail. Enough persist that the narrow fringe around the chamber never quite disappears and never advances far beyond it.

The vacancy itself remains empty not because nothing living ever reaches it, but because nothing can complete its life there. Seeds find no enduring purchase. Roots extending too far from the perimeter lose the conditions that sustained them. The repeated sheets of sand erase small beginnings. Yet the vegetation depends on that emptiness. It gives the exhalation its depth, allows pollen and seeds to travel between otherwise separated colonies, and keeps the town’s note large enough to open the capsules. The bare center is both road and instrument.

Nothing grazes the fans or strips the canes. There are no nests in the curtains and no small bodies polishing the roots. Damage comes from heat, abrasion, weight, and age. Consequently, death is conspicuously slow to remove anything. A dead frond may remain suspended within a living curtain; a split cane may stand for ages beside its descendants; a fan’s oldest leaves may survive as dark outlines long after their interiors have become powder.

Nor does the town possess a season of general renewal. There are only the great exhalation, the returning current, and the smaller processes between them. At any quiet moment, a root tip is dividing a grain, a fan is turning by an imperceptible fraction, oil is moving through a cane, and a capsule is drying toward the tension at which it will open.

None of this is care or intention. The roots do not know they hold a wall. The curtains do not choose to shelter spores. The canes have not learned the town’s music. But architecture and life have become so closely fitted that the distinction between habitat and organism is no longer simple. Streets are dispersal corridors; courts are seed traps; kilns are warm ground; conduits are clocks and weather. Even the shutters, rising and knocking without hands, participate in the conditions to which the growth has adapted.

If the Thrum has a population, it is best counted in durations rather than bodies: a root’s slow extension, a leaf’s long persistence, a curtain’s centuries of accumulation, a spore’s brief suspension, a seed’s single flight across nothing. Nothing hunts, hides, or keeps house. Nothing emerges when the roaring stops, because everything alive was already there.

The builders’ town has not merely acquired a covering of plants. It has acquired a life for which stone is soil, resonance is weather, and emptiness is the distance between generations. That life is neither repairing the Thrum nor overrunning it. It is the part of the Thrum that still grows.
