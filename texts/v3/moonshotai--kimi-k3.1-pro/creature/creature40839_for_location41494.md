## The people of the covered water

Among them, belonging is reckoned by water before blood.

A household is not simply everyone sleeping beneath one roof. Roofs are too frequently shared, divided, and built over for that. A household is the group whose vessels may be filled beneath the same cistern cover, and whose members can be summoned when the clay lining cracks or an inlet begins to run cloudy. Some of its people are kin. Others have been attached by marriage, adoption, debt, or a winter of refuge that never formally ended. A person may sleep three courts away and still belong to the water at home.

They call this being *inside*. One can be inside while following a herd months beyond sight of the city, and outside while renting a warm room above the pass. The distinction determines where a sick child may be carried, who must contribute fuel after a hard winter, and who will come without daily payment when a channel must be opened. To lose a dwelling is a misfortune. To lose every obligation that connects one to a court is much closer to exile.

Travelers often call the people slow. They see them pausing on stairs, resting a hand against a wall before lifting a load, and refusing to hurry toward distances that are plainly visible. The judgment does not survive a summer rain. When water begins knocking in the roof gutters, the same people move with extraordinary speed: children scramble to flood drains, keepers throw back cistern covers, masons cut free a blocked runnel with a few precise blows, and men and women who seemed almost sleepy an hour earlier pass overflowing vessels down a stair without confusion. Their slowness is not inability. It is a judgment about what haste usually costs at that height.

There is no single plateau body or face. Old city families, far-ranging herders, and the descendants of lowland traders differ visibly from one another, and every caravan adds further variation. Many have wind-split lips, sun-darkened faces, and hands roughened by rope and stone. Herders tend to be lean from long absence and irregular meals; city workers may be broader through the shoulders from years of leverage and carrying. Age shows first in the fingers and lungs. What they share most strongly is an economy of movement learned in childhood: rise slowly, breathe before lifting, never run across unfamiliar scree, and never mistake a clear view for a short journey.

Their clothing is layered hair-cloth and hide, close fastened at the wrists and throat, with loose outer pieces that can be shed beside a fire or fastened against wind. Boots are soft-soled and repaired until little of the original remains. In hard glare they wear visors or narrow-slitted shades of hide, antler, or precious wood. Most garments are brown, gray, or black, repeatedly patched. Color is concentrated in small, portable things: a red clay mark inside a milk bowl, a bright thread worked into a braid, a strip of lowland cloth edging a child’s hood. Such a strip may outlive several garments.

The cistern keepers are practical specialists before they are officials. They know how frozen water sounds beneath a cover, how a new leak changes the echo in a settling basin, and why a seep that runs strongly after fog may still fail later in the season. Some can estimate the water level by the interval and pitch of drips. Children imitate them, crouching in dark courts with their eyes shut, trying to identify one cistern from another. A keeper’s memory includes not only repairs but mistakes: the inlet that looked well placed until a wet summer, the lining mixed by someone who used too little clay, the cover whose weight gradually opened a joint beneath it.

Consequently, their public memory is not entirely flattering to ancestors. A person may be remembered for an excellent flight of steps, but also for a gutter that has flooded every generation since. At the first thaw, when courts clean their channels, they recite the names associated with the work. The recitation is part genealogy, part warning. Concealing damage to avoid the labor of repair is considered worse than causing damage honestly; it passes a danger, unknown, to people who had no part in making it.

No palace stands above the other roofs. The nearest thing to a seat of power is an unusually open court with several covered cisterns, a grain loft, and enough dry wall space for people to wait out of the wind. There, representatives of the older water courts argue with herding speakers, masons, cliff fowlers, and those who manage the pass. Their authority is uneven. An old court with reliable water and many range relatives can make its preferences felt long before a formal decision. Pass factors may become rich by controlling storage and caravan space. Newer households, crowded into upper rooms, often maintain stairs and retaining walls whose failure would endanger people wealthier than themselves.

Wealth is correspondingly difficult for outsiders to read. It appears as a bed kept dry above an animal room, a deep stack of dung fuel under a sound roof, grain that need not be sold immediately, and the ability to summon thirty pairs of hands before a freeze. Jewelry matters less than a well-fitted door. Poverty appears as smoke that cannot be escaped, water carried farther than it should be, and repair obligations discharged with labor while others discharge them with stored goods. Knowledge gives many elders influence, but knowledge alone does not keep an isolated old person warm.

The city’s masons do not treat basalt as a single substance. They distinguish flows by their sound under the maul, the direction in which they prefer to open, and the kinds of flaws that remain hidden until winter. Some stone is said to split short, some to run, some to promise a clean face and then shell away beneath the hand. Their finest architectural work is often almost invisible: a course that takes weight without crushing the older wall beneath it, a drain lip set at the only angle that will not grow ice, a roof slab whose seam can still be lifted by descendants without bringing down the whole span.

They apply the same patience to the black stone vessels sent down the pass. These are ground and polished with abrasives, cord, and repeated work rather than shaped quickly. The best have thin walls, close-fitting lids, and interiors smooth enough to wash clean. Ornament is restrained: a groove where the thumb naturally rests, a pale mineral band followed rather than cut across, a lip made pleasingly precise. Lowland buyers may prize the vessel’s appearance. Its maker is more likely to speak of whether the lid still fits after years beside a fire.

Beyond the last wall, the herding companies live by a different scale of knowledge.

The great grazers are accustomed to people without being reliably obedient to them. Their broad hooves take the angular ground better than any boot, and their heavy coats allow them to remain out in weather that drives herders into stone shelters. They remember passes, accustomed handling places, and the presence of particular people. They also remember injuries. A frightened herd can undo a season’s arrangements in an afternoon. Ownership is shown by clipped patterns, ear marks, and records kept in knotted cord, but animals mix, stray, change condition, and sometimes simply refuse to go where their supposed owners require.

Herders milk them when the condition of the herd permits, gather dung along the routes, castrate or cull selected animals, and protect the young where they can. They do not pretend to command the migration. Their skill lies in anticipating where a herd can be turned, where it must simply be followed, and when the wisest action is to let animals go out of sight and trust the route that brought their ancestors back.

No one possesses a complete map of the tableland. Routes are carried as sequences of instructions, recited while walking and corrected by experience. Place-names often contain warnings rather than pictures: *Ice Beneath the Third Step*, *No Water After Clear Weather*, *Shelter Filled by Herd*, *Do Not Take the Straight Gully*. At maintained shelters, travelers are expected to leave certain fuel untouched for someone arriving in worse condition. Whether they do so is a recurring source of feud.

Some range people enter the city several times in a year. Others come only when a caravan, a marriage, a serious injury, or a failing water source makes it necessary. They may find the covered lanes oppressive and the animal smell indoors unbearable. City children, in turn, can be highly capable on roofs and utterly frightened by an open horizon. Marriage often links these worlds without merging them. A child may have sleeping space in one court, water rights in another, and range relatives who appear only after intervals long enough for the child to have grown unrecognizable.

The city does not rule the interior. There are smaller seep camps, seasonal shelters, and herding alliances that owe it nothing. Its importance comes from the conjunction of water and pass, not from an ability to send force across distances that even its own traders cannot cross quickly. Its inhabitants argue fiercely about privileges at home while remaining cautious about claims beyond the rim.

Their food reflects that dependence. Milk is soured, curded, or dried when abundant; meat is dried, pounded, and stored with fat; hides and hair are worked through the cold season. In the brief green margins around the seeps, thick leaves and sedges are gathered carefully, with some patches left untouched by custom if not always by hunger. Cliff eggs and nestlings provide a short, intensely anticipated season of plenty. Children set snares for the small furred animals of the scree, both for food and because the same animals plunder stored grain. Imported grain is too valuable for daily carelessness. A fresh grain cake may mark a wedding, a successful caravan, or the safe covering of the cisterns after rain.

Wood is treated almost as a form of inherited tools rather than fuel. A broken lever becomes a shorter handle, then a peg, then a spindle part. Salt is guarded against damp. Dung is stacked, counted, borrowed, and argued over. When pack trains return from below, they bring not only goods but new accents, disputes, songs, and occasionally young people who decide to descend with the next train and never become properly inside anywhere again.

The cliff fowlers form another body of specialists. They know which columnar ledges can bear a rope, which apparently solid projections will flake under weight, and which tiers may be harvested without emptying a colony. Their rights are old and contested. Fox-sized scavengers wait below their paths as they wait below herds and slaughtering places. Individual animals that haunt a court for years may acquire names and complicated reputations. They are tolerated until they begin taking young stock or digging too persistently near food stores. The great motionless raptors are used in speech as images of patience and attention, though people are less inclined to read omens into them than lowland stories suggest.

The quiet of the city should not be mistaken for subdued private life. Courts can be full of argument, laughter, recitation, and the outrage of someone whose drying rack has been moved three handbreadths to make room for a roof repair. Children play games of balance on parapets and games of memory in covered lanes. Adults gamble with bone pieces for small stakes of labor. Herders make merciless fun of city people who cannot read weather, and city people imitate traders stopping to breathe after every fourth step. A skilled route reciter can hold an audience through the description of an empty plain, provided the route includes one bad decision and a shelter reached almost too late.

Their beliefs differ by court and range, but few of them regard stone as eternal. They live among fresh fractures. They have seen apparently permanent stairs become scree. Water, by contrast, is never simply gone: it freezes, sinks, emerges elsewhere, or remains concealed behind a course someone was too impatient to examine. This does not lead all of them to the same religion. Some speak of the dead traveling beneath the buried contacts of the lava flows. Others find that idea fanciful and say the dead remain only in work still being used.

Bodies are carried to places beyond the settlement where frost and scavengers can do what soil and firewood cannot readily be spared to do. Tools usually return. A maul passes to an apprentice, a warm coat is cut down for someone smaller, a rope is inspected and put back into service. At later channel cleanings, the dead person’s name may be spoken beside the names of the living. This is not always tender. Sometimes it is accompanied by the old complaint about what that person did wrong.

For all their inherited obligations, the people are not merely preserving an ancient arrangement. Herds shift their routes. A dependable seep weakens. A wet summer turns a respected drainage system into a source of cracks and ice. Young households demand water rights commensurate with the labor they already perform. Pass factors grow rich enough to challenge older courts. Every generation contains people who want to open new channels and others who can recite exactly why the last attempt failed.

From a distance, this accumulated disagreement looks like stillness.

At evening, a keeper may lift a cistern cover with a child beside her, both listening. Below a stone floor, a herd animal shifts in its sleep and warmth rises through the room above. On a roof, a newly split block waits beside the course it will join, its fresh face blue-black against weathered masonry. Far out on the tableland, a relative neither of them has seen for two seasons is turning animals in snow that will not reach the city for days.

The keeper hears the water strike below and judges the level. The child hears only a note in darkness, but is beginning to recognize it as home. Then the cover is lowered, not quite sealing the sound away. It continues faintly through the stone, one drop at a time, while above it the city remains crowded, argumentative, unfinished, and inhabited.
