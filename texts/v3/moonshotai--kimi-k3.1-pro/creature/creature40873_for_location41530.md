## The Lives of the White Hollow

Among the Hollow’s people, a complete introduction has three parts: the pattern of one’s household, the cistern company with which one presently keeps water, and the place where one was born. The third is generally considered the least informative.

Outsiders call them Hollowers, but they have little use for a name encompassing everyone below the plateau. A person may belong to the Open Ring house, drink with the company of Ninth Cistern, and currently be camped beside a seep that did not exist last season. That is enough to place them. Surface-born spouses, adopted children, indebted laborers, and households that have split acrimoniously from other households all complicate the arrangement, but do not fundamentally change it.

They are not the blind, pallid cave-dwellers that surface stories sometimes make them. The Hollow is too bright for that. Their sight is ordinary, though many find the plateau’s heat and hard sunlight more distressing than its open spaces. Generations of work against white stone have made them quick to notice small movements: a crab no thicker than a fingernail crossing a court, dust sliding from a roof joint, the brief irregularity in a reflector’s beam that means its copper face has begun to tarnish. Their distinctiveness is more visible in their hands. Salt gathers in the creases. Lime dulls the skin around old cuts. Masons’ thumbs become broad and flattened at the nail; lightkeepers often have one palm polished smooth from testing copper.

They dress in layered hide, patched and resewn until a garment may contain pieces from several decades. Cuffs and hems are kept short where brine is likely to creep. Fur or hair side inward is used against the cold, but even heavy clothing cannot make damp stone comfortable, and a wet sleeper who neglects to dry their bedding may become ill in weather that never changes. Wealth shows less in the size of a dwelling than in the possession of a dry second set of clothes, good hide boots, unfrayed rope, imported fat, and water rights that survive the failure of a local seep.

A household is not quite a family and not quite a building. It is the people who carry a particular pattern, the stones marked with it, the tools and water shares attached to it, and the continuing obligation to help one another move. Some households are narrowly hereditary; others absorb apprentices, widows, surface traders, and laborers who have remained long enough to be given work in the household’s name. A child may recognize its own pattern before it can reliably pronounce the names of its parents.

When a house moves, the work is unsentimental but not casual. Blocks are knocked free, turned, inspected, and sorted. Softened stone is discarded. Stones carrying household marks are cleaned and loaded separately. On the protected undersides of certain blocks are names and short records: a birth, a death, a joining, a year when the household took in strangers. These are read during the move, sometimes ceremonially, more often while people are resting with the stones still on the sledge. Disputes about ancestry commonly begin at such moments. A house may possess no block that has belonged to it for more than twenty years and still be considered many generations old.

The fixed works have their own keepers, but not necessarily their own owners. Cistern companies inherit shares, admit new members, expel negligent ones, and argue endlessly over what constitutes maintenance. Carrying water may count for less than cleaning a catchment; supplying rope may count for more than attending a meeting. An old company with a reliable cistern can become prosperous without appearing rich. Its members simply do not have to reorganize their lives every time the moisture wanders.

Seep-readers are respected in proportion to their usefulness, not their mystery. They examine the shape of new crystal, the granular softening at a wall’s foot, the behavior of crickets around old drill holes, and the underside chosen by glasslice when a slab begins to dry. They compare surface reports with the delayed arrival of water below. Their records are kept in hide rolls, incised copper strips, and long recited sequences of events. They are frequently wrong, sometimes spectacularly so. A good seep-reader is distinguished less by never being wrong than by remembering what happened the last time.

The most serious water offenses are not always the most obvious ones. A thirsty stranger taking a cup may be fed and then questioned. Secretly altering a drainage grade, covering a neighbor’s catchment with fresh masonry, or shifting a marker so that future seepage will enter one’s own channel is another matter. Such acts are understood as claims against people who have not yet arrived. Punishments are commonly assessed in labor: clearing a furred drain, resetting a causeway, or surrendering a turn of light until a debt is worked off. This does not make the system impartial. Old companies know how to make labor obligations fall heaviest on newcomers.

Light, too, is administered. The reflectors belong to no single household in the ordinary sense, though particular families may have tended the same sockets for generations. Their keepers know how a shaft’s contribution changes with the season, how far a beam can be passed without becoming uselessly diffuse, and which courts can bear an hour of blue shadow without stopping essential work. There is no single noon throughout the city. One district may be drilling stone in borrowed brightness while another, only a basin away, is eating and waiting for its turn.

A reflector can also send a brief flash across several basins. The signals are practical—work stopped, assistance needed, a well open, a crew approaching—but children learn unauthorized variations and use them to conduct conversations across distances that would swallow a shout. Deliberately darkening another district’s court is a serious provocation. Quietly paying a lightkeeper for an extra turn is an older and more common vice.

The closest thing the Hollow has to public spectacle is an exceptionally fine alignment of light. Several keepers may cooperate to send a clear beam through a succession of empty spaces, touching a meeting hall, a white ridge, and finally a shaft filled with slowly falling dust. If chalkwings rise through it, people gather to watch. Most maintain that they have come only to inspect the reflectors.

Food begins with the films, though no household can confidently command them. People place suitable slabs where seepwater crosses reactive stone, leave them until their undersides darken with life, and then move them before the chemistry of the water changes too far. In this limited sense they keep pastures. Glasslice are brushed into hide bags; chalkwing larvae are gathered from certain blooms; small crustaceans are sieved through hide mesh. Much is eaten fresh. The rest is salted, dried, or allowed to ferment in sealed containers. Eels are more valuable and more difficult. They can be carried alive between districts in wet bags, but a bag of eels is not a portable food supply unless its owner also understands the water into which they are going.

A newly filled cistern may be seeded with water and animals from an older one. This is husbandry without domestication: no one knows whether the transferred life will establish itself, and an apparently successful cistern may become foul after a single sulfur-rich inflow. Some cisterns are deliberately kept simple, with few animals and little organic refuse. Others become crowded, productive, and precarious. Roosting bats are discouraged above drinking water, usually by screens of old hide. Their droppings are welcome in certain feeding basins and disastrous in others.

Salamanders are seldom eaten. They are too few, too slow to replace, and too easy to admire once a person has spent years seeing the same motionless shape along a rim. Districts give names to particularly old individuals and exaggerate their ages. A salamander’s survival is often cited as proof of good cistern keeping, usually by the keepers themselves.

Seep blooms provide feasts, but they do not free the city from the surface. A week of visible abundance can obscure how little of it keeps. The Hollow exports salt, sulfur, worked and unworked copper, and occasionally desirable stone; it imports hides, fat, rope-making material, and durable foods whose calories do not depend on the behavior of a drain. Almost every awning has therefore had a surface history. Fuel is too precious for casual use, and smoke is unwelcome in still air, so an ordinary meal is often cold. A hot meal offered to a traveler is hospitality with a measurable cost.

The people are not silent by temperament. Meeting halls can be extremely loud. Children shriek, boast, and argue over whose stone sledge is fastest. Adults gamble on whether a new channel will reach a causeway before crusting shut, spread malicious gossip about cistern companies, and tell increasingly implausible stories about the mistakes of rival seep-readers. Out in the open basins, however, they rarely use voices merely to fill space. Sound is poorly informative there. A shout may seem close and come from nowhere useful; a distant collapse may arrive first as a warning through the feet.

The other inhabitants accommodate the city without becoming parts of it. White crickets and spiders travel in dismantled masonry, so that a moved household may carry a small population of unmarked residents with it. Glasslice appear in newly damp storehouses and vanish before anyone has decided whether they indicate a leak. Blind eels learn the vibrations of regular feeding and gather at a rim where, a month earlier, nothing responded to footsteps. Chalkwing adults live briefly in the light wells, while bats hunt through them according to schedules no lightkeeper controls.

Nothing about this life has taught the Hollowers to despise permanence. They lavish generations of labor on cistern lips, drain grades, protected sockets, and safe routes across collapse ground. They simply do not expect permanence to resemble stillness. Their maps fail; their houses walk; their brightest streets depend on copper being turned by hand. Even their household names survive by being copied onto replacement stones.

After distant rain, a new seep may briefly gather the whole arrangement into view. A water company arrives first, then masons and traders. Glasslice collect beneath the slabs. Crustaceans spread over the court. Chalkwings rise, bats follow, and eels are brought in wet bags from a district where the water is failing. Hide awnings go up around stone counters, and for several days the place acquires markets, arguments, feeding hours, and a local noon.

When the seep withdraws, these will disperse at different speeds. The people may leave before the insects; the insects before the last brine tide mark dries. But for as long as the water remains, household, eel, film, bat, and city all live at the same address.
