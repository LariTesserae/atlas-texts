## The inhabitants of Avel

Avel is inhabited by people who have learned to distrust permanence without learning to live lightly. Their houses are heavy, their doors close firmly, and their household goods are made to be repaired. A family may keep the same rooms for generations while the official elevation changes, the street acquires a new joint, and the view from an upper window becomes a reservoir. Permanence is for obligations, not coordinates.

Outsiders often expect Avelans to be as austere as the plateau. The city’s silence misleads them. Inside the sheltered courts and covered passages, Avel is crowded with workshop noise, dishes, arguments, children running messages, and the continual knocking of boots against grates to remove salt. Outdoor clothing tends toward dark, close-sealed layers, pale at the cuffs with mineral rime; indoor clothing is often unexpectedly bright. A person can cross half a settlement without dressing for the weather, which gives some districts the appearance of being inhabited by people who have simply declined to acknowledge winter.

They are not all alike. Residents of the older pressure-tunnel districts value the steady temperatures, low doors, and absence of distant views. People in the newer upper galleries profess not to understand how anyone can live without seeing weather approach. Reservoir keepers and basin crews may spend weeks moving between outposts; for them, Avel is less a city than a sequence of heated rooms separated by stone and wind. They can be astonishingly ignorant of streets two districts away and intimately informed about a spring three hundred kilometres off.

Surveyors are therefore necessary, but not especially romanticized. People complain about them, quote their corrections with disbelief, and consult their maps constantly. Avelan courtesy includes closing thermal doors behind oneself, warning a neighbor when water has begun crossing a familiar route, and avoiding directions based solely on memory. A map whose date cannot be read is considered decorative.

Most residents never see a gradient engine. The deep crews who maintain them are regarded less as keepers of mysteries than as exceptionally specialized utility workers. When an adjustment passes through the city as pressure in the chest, conversation may pause. Someone glances toward an instrument; someone else waits to see whether a hanging lamp moves. Then the meal or argument resumes. It is not treated as an omen.

This does not make Avelans indifferent to place. They name things readily, sometimes excessively. A temporary pond may possess an official basin number, an obscene field-crew nickname, and the name given it by a child, all at once. Debates over whether a new settlement should commemorate a person, a survey expedition, or a particularly useful spring can continue for years. Meanwhile, construction waits for dashed blue channels on hydrological forecasts: watercourses that do not yet exist but must nevertheless be consulted.

The glazed tree courts are among the few places where the city permits itself an uncomplicated luxury. People visit them simply to sit near foliage. Gardeners are fond of telling admiring visitors that the real achievement is not the tree but the heat, water, and manufactured soil keeping it alive. Outside, a handful of spilled growing medium is worth sweeping up. Children raised in Avel may know green primarily as something under glass, while recognizing rust lichen, black microbial film, and orange-stained spring terraces as other, equally legitimate colors of life.

Numerically, nearly everything alive on Vey is still very small. The wet cracks contain films that revive for a few hours around solar noon and freeze again by evening. Dry salt and dust hold spores, eggs, and cysts through intervals when their basins appear empty. Lichens spread across fresh exposures so slowly that a hand-sized patch can serve as a local landmark. Cushion plants collect windblown organic dust in their centers. Hairgrass establishes itself wherever warmth and water coincide, including places the city did not intend to make fertile.

Avel is consequently an ecological source as well as a settlement. Its heat leaks, outfalls, sheltered ledges, and damp service seams create small climates. Every one is eventually inspected by something.

The temporary waters produce life with startling haste. A basin that was dry black stone can, within days of filling, develop cloudy algal margins and dense populations of microscopic grazers. Brine flies rise in seasonal pulses, their pale cast skins collecting in windows, against door seals, and in long shoreward bands. For a short time the shallows are crowded beyond anything suggested by the surrounding land. Then the outflow changes, the water drops, and most of that abundance either leaves, dies, or becomes dormant again.

The small silver fish of the deeper spring pools are called spring-silvers. They retreat into cold, mineral-heavy water when the upper shelves warm or shrink. Their occasional appearance in a newly filled pool is of interest to more than biologists: it may indicate a submerged connection that the surveys have not found. Reservoir keepers keep fish records alongside pressure and flow measurements, and do not always regard the instruments as more informative.

The common waders are known as ochre-legs for the mineral stains on their lower limbs. They arrive in loose groups, feed with their heads down in the shallows, and nest on low islands built from salt terraces and angular stone. Their colonies are wagers. An island may remain isolated long enough for the chicks to hatch, or it may join the shore after a night of drainage. A food-rich basin may empty before the young can travel. Whole nesting attempts fail; elsewhere, a new basin fills and the birds are already there. Their movements are useful signs, though not infallible ones. Avelans who trust them completely are said to have confused observation with prophecy.

The slatebacks are the animals by which visitors finally understand that the plateau is populated. Their broad hooves spread their weight on snow, salt crusts, and treacherous margins. Their slate-colored coats take frost along the guard hairs, so that a herd at rest can resemble a field of dark, steaming stones. They scrape lichen with hard mouthparts and tear hairgrass and cushion growth from seep lines. Their routes are traditional until the drainage makes tradition useless.

A herd is also a moving shelter. Small dark-furred animals called spindles run between the slatebacks’ legs, feeding on insects exposed by hooves and using the larger bodies as windbreaks. They cache dead flies in dry cracks and disappear into fissures when the herd bunches. Above them, sailwings turn on the rising air over dark stone, escarpments, and warm industrial shelves. They can wait for days on high ledges, then descend one after another when a hunt or death has left something worth opening.

Slatebacks alter more than they consume. Their hoofprints hold meltwater. Droppings collect in fractures with hairgrass seed and fragments of lichen. Spindles carry organic material into their dens. Around a repeatedly used seep, these small deposits form some of the first pockets of nutrient-rich ground—not soil in the lowland sense, but enough to make the next colonization slightly easier.

The predators belong to this system without being constantly visible within it. Riftcats use broken country as covered roadway, their long pale bodies often marked with rust where they have rubbed against stained stone. They take spindles, birds, and vulnerable young animals more often than they attack healthy adult slatebacks. The narrow-muzzled pack-runners cover longer distances along the migration routes, testing herds, following weakness, and scavenging when that is easier. Most Avelans know both chiefly through tracks, a sudden change in the behavior of a herd, or sailwings gathering over broken ground.

Around the city, wild animals have learned the heat map in considerable detail. Small birds nest on warm ledges and line their cups with hairgrass and shed slateback hair. Spindles den beside service conduits and become pests in seed stores. Ochre-legs feed along warm outfall channels when other shallows have frozen. In late autumn, slatebacks gather below the outfall terraces, neither tame nor especially trusting. They will use the city’s warmth and still bolt from an open maintenance door.

Avel’s arrangements with these animals are less harmony than continuous maintenance. Nests block vents. Grates need clearing after fly hatches. Road crews wait for herds. Conduits are screened, repaired, and screened again. During migration, the dark gaps in the infrastructure remain dark, and ground-level traffic accepts detours rather than cutting a lit corridor through the herds’ path. People complain about the delays, watch from the upper galleries, and record the crossings.

On an autumn evening, the whole arrangement can be seen from one of those galleries. Slatebacks stand below the warm terraces, almost indistinguishable from wet basalt except for their breath. Spindles flicker between their hooves. A sailwing settles onto a high ledge without a single corrective flap. Inside the court behind the glass, people are eating, repairing instruments, or arguing over a revised shoreline. The city lights stop cleanly at the migration gap. A deep adjustment passes through the chest, and for a moment everyone listens to nothing.

By morning there may be a new runnel across the survey road, fresh tracks on the dark ground, and another sheet of broken ice at the pond margin. Nothing announces itself as a beginning.

To live well on Vey requires a way to keep a little heat, a way to use abundance before it vanishes, and a route to somewhere else. Avel’s people have added a fourth habit in service of the other three: they write down the date.
