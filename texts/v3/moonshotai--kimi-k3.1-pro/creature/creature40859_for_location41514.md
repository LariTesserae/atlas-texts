## What Lives in the Small Weather

No animal owns the hollow, and no single census of it would be believable. A count made by eye would find two trees, a frog, several moths, and a narrow animal on the bench. A count made by ear would suggest hundreds of much larger creatures. Both would miss the population in the water films, the animals waiting in the stone joints, and the plants that spend most of their lives looking like dead cushions.

The oldest visible residents are probably the trees, though some of the cushion mats may be older still. A mat can die at its center and continue outward, generation after generation, until nothing remains of the place where it began. The trees grow more conventionally, but almost as slowly. Calling them cup trees describes their use rather than their ancestry. Their thick leaves are slightly folded, and the bases fit closely enough around the stems to hold small quantities of water. Where several leaves meet, the hollows are deeper. These are the copperthroats’ nurseries.

Not every bright morning drop is dew. Some water condenses from the sheltered air; some is drawn through a plant at night and released at the edges of its leaves when the tissues can hold no more. Either way, the drop is brief and valuable. A moth may drink from it. A frog may press its underside against it. A film of microscopic life may spread through it and be gone before the stone below has cooled.

One tree has followed reliability rather than space. Its roots have traveled away from the richer moss beds toward the pin now partly enclosed in wood. The root does not appear to draw anything from the pin itself. It occupies the zone where condensing water repeatedly wets the stone. Growth there is so slow that the pin has not been pushed aside; the root has simply accommodated it, fiber by fiber.

Each green island contains several altitudes. The upper moss is exposed, springy, and often dry at the tips. Beneath it lies a darker layer where water persists as a film rather than a pool. Fern roots stitch that layer to the rock. Pale fungal threads consume old moss and fragments of wing, and in the wettest seams live translucent animals visible only when they turn. Some fold themselves into hard little cases when dry. Others simply die, leaving eggs or nothing at all. The community does not survive because every member can endure the plateau. It survives because a few protected places always dry more slowly than the rest.

The split-seed singers spend their early lives in these lower layers, grazing on soft growth and the films over decaying matter. The adults climb into the ferns to call. The sound comes from a serrated surface inside the body case, worked so rapidly that the animal appears motionless. Some singers use the air alone. Others brace themselves against a stem or stone and send part of the vibration through it. This accounts for the notes that seem to originate inside the walls and for the faint, irregular tremor sometimes perceptible through a hand laid on the floor.

Their chorus has no conductor, but neither is it entirely independent. A singer often alters its interval when a near neighbor calls, avoiding the other note rather than matching it. Thousands of tiny avoidances produce the broad, shifting band of sound. They also make an individual animal difficult to locate. The whole fern bed appears to be calling, while the actual singer remains a seed-shaped darkness beneath one rib.

The copperthroats use a different kind of territory: not a fern frond or a patch of moss, but a supply of water that may last several days. When not calling, a frog can look like a wet knot of leaf matter. Its copper throat appears only when the body lifts and the voice sac fills. The wooden note carries much farther than the animal could.

Eggs are laid on the wet surfaces of the tree cups or just above their waterline. The tadpoles graze on the film coating the cup, growing quickly while the water is warm. Many broods fail. A cup that seems secure at dawn can be empty by the following afternoon, and the tadpoles have no mysterious ability to wait forever. The population persists because the frogs do not all breed on the same cue. Some respond to rain, some to a run of humid nights, some to water held near the rear seep. A few cups remain occupied while others fail.

The frogs answer the long, soft echo because its timing falls close enough to the pattern of a rival’s reply. They call toward it, wait, and usually fall silent when nothing further comes. No frog has been seen to approach the rear wall in search of it. Whatever the echo is physically, it already has a place in their behavior: an intermittent voice that can be challenged but never found.

The broad-winged moths belong mainly to the flowers and the hours around sunset. In dry daylight they rest with their wings closed, their outlines broken enough to resemble flakes of lichen. The clicking flight heard among the ferns is often theirs. At the cream flowers they unroll slender mouthparts and work deep inside the blossom; pollen collects along the soft scales of the head and forebody. They also drink from the pins, sometimes settling on metal that is plainly warm and remaining until a drop reaches the surface beneath them. Their young live out of sight in the older cushion growth, consuming tissue that has already begun to die.

The tiny browsers are easier to follow by damage than by sight. Each is a soft, flattened gray body carried on a fringe of deliberate feet. They travel mostly after sunset, when the exposed stone has cooled, following routes marked by traces left on the rock. A route may outlive many generations of the animals using it. At each island they take small, repeated bites from new growth, producing the characteristic nicks. Fragments of moss and fern spores catch on them and are carried across the bare intervals. Without intending to, they maintain connections between islands that would otherwise be ecologically separate.

The slender animal on the bench is the hollow’s most conspicuous regular resident. It is about the length of a human forearm, though much of that is tail, and its lichen-gray body can narrow itself into a crack that seems incapable of admitting a finger. It uses the bench because the old stone holds heat after the surrounding floor has begun to cool. It drinks by licking water from leaves and catches moths in short, abrupt dashes near the foliage. In cold weather it retreats into the roofless niche. Usually only one is seen, but there is no easy way to know whether this is a single long-lived individual or a succession of similarly shy tenants.

It has no special relationship with the old builders. The niche is simply dry, narrow, and close to a warm place to lie.

The hunter is a resident of a different kind. The hollow could not feed it every day, and it probably shelters somewhere among the outer fractures, but its hunting range includes the fern line. Its apparent flatness is not a trick of light or color. Seen edge-on, its body is genuinely thin, and its long joints break the outline into pieces the eye assembles poorly. Only when the head turns does the whole animal acquire depth.

It hunts by remembering sounds. A singing insect need not still be calling when the hunter arrives; the last note is enough if the animal has not moved far. The approaching hush is partly a response to the hunter’s faint footfalls and partly a relay among the prey. One singer stops. Its neighbors stop at the sudden gap. Others fall silent farther along, until the wave of quiet has crossed the hollow faster than any animal there could run.

The hunter takes what has not reached cover, usually singers, sometimes a moth or an incautious young frog. It does not dig long into the moss. When the remaining animals have become inaccessible, it leaves. The chorus that resumes afterward is changed partly because certain voices are gone and partly because the survivors call differently: more briefly, often lower, with longer intervals in which to listen.

Warm rain gathers nearly every population into the same short hour. Not all the winged insects that rise are moths, and not all come from the vegetation. Several kinds emerge from the stone joints, where their immature stages occupy damp pockets too narrow to examine without breaking rock. They fly, mate, feed if their kind feeds at all, and lay before the temporary water disappears. Their abundance is less a sudden creation than a movement of life out of storage.

The frogs feed heavily. The bench animal leaves its warm stone for the flower cover. The hunter may arrive, and after it come the gleaners: small, jointed scavengers that ordinarily live below the grit and moss. They cut wings into portable pieces, carry soft fragments into crevices, and drag the remainder toward the damp islands. Their activity is one reason so little debris remains on the central stone. A rain event ends not only with dry rock, but with nutrients moved, eggs placed, flowers pollinated, and several kinds of young beginning life out of sight.

The pins are treated no differently from any other useful feature. Insects crawl over them. Moths drink from them. The tree root incorporates one. The pin standing in bare stone never acquires a garden around it, but for a few minutes at dawn it may still be the busiest place in the hollow. Then its water is gone, and it becomes bare again. Nothing tends the pins, just as nothing clears the bench or repairs the basin. Their continued operation does not require the inhabitants to understand them, and the inhabitants do not require the pins to be anything more mysterious than reliable surfaces.

The long echo remains the only sound without an established owner. There are no tracks, feeding signs, or droppings to support the existence of some larger animal beyond the rear wall. There is not even a passage by which such an animal could reliably enter. Yet the frogs’ answers make it difficult to dismiss the reply as meaningless. The hollow’s known inhabitants already explain almost every noise. Almost is enough to leave the matter open.

What lives there, then, is neither a hidden crowd nor a solitary keeper. It is a staggered occupancy. Trees and cushion mats measure their lives in root-widths and slow changes of outline. Singers measure them in warm afternoons. Copperthroats measure them in the filling and failure of leaf cups. The moths gather at flowering, the hunter at silence, the gleaners at the hour after rain. Even the old human works have been assigned new tenants without being reclaimed.

By the time a visitor has reached the rim, the visible hollow may be empty again. Somewhere under a fern, one split-seed case opens by a fraction and tests the air. A tadpole turns in its swallow of water. A browser takes the next step along a route older than itself. The rest are waiting—not for a return to some wetter age, but for the next small, exact permission of warmth and water.
