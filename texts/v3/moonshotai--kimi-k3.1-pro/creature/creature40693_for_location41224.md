## What Lives in the Serein Catchment

People may still come to the Serein Catchment. The recessed doors open, the gauges remain legible, and certain repairs require judgment. But no one appears to have made a home of the basin. Its paths end where their work ends. Its shelters contain no accumulation of ordinary life.

The largest inhabitants are easily mistaken for a crowd.

A thicket in the central hollow may contain several hundred stems and still be one organism. Its apparent boundaries mean little. A dry stripe of scree, a service cutting, even a considerable interval of open ground may separate parts of the same body, connected below the stone or descended from a connection that has since died. There is no original trunk at the center, no oldest wood in which its age might be counted. It persists by ceasing to be where it was.

These great clones are not uniform. One may be failing on a bright upper slope while putting out vigorous stems in the cold shade of a mast half a kilometre away. Another grows densely around a buried main, its cupped understory holding condensation against the root felt, while its outlying stems remain small and hard-leaved. The maintenance tables call them clones because that is convenient. The word makes them sound repetitive. In fact each has a geography, and almost a temperament, imposed by fractures, cold, and the distribution of water.

Their young are usually accidents. A root fragment exposed by a valve shear remains viable in the damp dust on a mechanism. A stem broken during servicing is pressed into the felt by a returning counterweight. Years later, growth appears beside a pipe inspection port, far from any parent thicket. Seed is not impossible, but it is not how the forest principally travels.

Beneath and between these bodies live the stone-whitening fungi.

They occupy the thin frontier where root, leaf, and rock meet. Some pass through the root felt in cords no thicker than hair; others whiten the interiors of fractures, taking minerals directly from surfaces that roots cannot yet enter. They receive carbon from the forest and return phosphorus, stone-derived salts, and small, crucial improvements in the retention of water.

Their work on dead leaves is unexpectedly conservative. A leaf may remain recognizable for years after the fungus has entered it. Its tissues are loosened, its interior darkened, its shape preserved by the weight of later leaves above. Only when the sheet is finally opened does one see that nearly everything except the pattern of veins has been taken away.

Rarely, in the stable dark of a maintenance shelter, one of these fungi fruits: a white, translucent bracket behind a pipe, or a cluster of small cups in the dust beneath a gauge. By the next inspection there may be nothing left but a faint circular stain.

The animals live at the scale of such stains.

The transparent snail is usually noticed only after it has moved. On a leaf it resembles a drop with a dark thread inside; on ceramic it can be mistaken for a flaw in the glaze. The dark thread is its gut. It grazes the lichen bloom on metal markers and the softer fungal films at the bases of cupped leaves, then seals itself against the shaded underside of stone when the films disappear. Its waiting is not sleep exactly. Something in it remains capable of responding to moisture after intervals that would kill an active animal many times over.

Nearly colorless mites inhabit the water itself, though “inhabit” may suggest more room than they possess. They gather where a leaf cup narrows, where condensation joins two grains of dust, or where a collector has left a meniscus on the rim of a ceramic cup. A drop large enough to carry one may also crush it. For these animals, the daily wiping of a mast is migration, inundation, and catastrophe arriving as the same event.

The grey moth is the catchment’s nearest approach to a traveler.

Its larval life passes inside the compressed leaf sheets, where it feeds on tissue already softened by fungi. It grows slowly, sometimes surviving long intervals without feeding at all. The adult emerges only when the air beneath the canopy is wet enough that its wings will not dry before they harden. This usually happens before dawn.

The adult is small, weak-flying, and almost silent even against a collector’s metal frame. Its wings are the grey of cold ash, without the powdery patterns that might make it conspicuous on stone. It does not feed. It mates, lays where it can, and dies within a short time. The husk found beneath a fern is therefore not evidence of a population overhead. There may have been no other adult within hundreds of metres.

The forest does not need the moth for pollination. Its usefulness is incidental. Fungal spores adhere to its wing scales; fragments of the felt cling to its feet. A flight of twenty metres may join two patches of decomposition that had otherwise remained separate for decades.

In the slab pools, the pale creatures are slower still.

Old inspection notes call them efts, without settling what they are. Some are no longer than a finger. Their gills are so fine that they seem at first to be cracks along the body, and their internal organs show through the flesh as faint, suspended shadows. They have no visible eyes, but light contracts the muscles along their backs. Exposed to it, they withdraw beneath the slab or into a fracture without disturbing the surface.

They browse films from the stone. At the outlet of a pipe, where almost nothing emerges except a seasonal coldness and an occasional bead of water, they gather to feed on whatever the condensate has brought down. A turn of the body, a few mouthfuls, another period of stillness: this can constitute most of the observable activity of a day.

Each isolated reservoir contains a slightly different population. One has broad gill fans; another, almost none. In a clay-lined hollow the animals themselves are seldom seen, though their eggs persist in the damp margins when open water has contracted to a black seam. Their distribution follows the pipes more closely than it follows the shape of the ground. They did not walk between pools. Some, almost certainly, were carried in maintenance flushes or in water moved from one reservoir to another. Others may have arrived as eggs adhering to mechanisms that were never intended to touch living things.

This is why the machinery belongs in any honest account of the catchment’s inhabitants.

It is not alive, but it performs services that elsewhere belong to floods, hooves, feathers, and rivers. It gathers from wide surfaces and deposits in narrow places. It crosses divides. It opens a seam of light beside a service door once each year and keeps that seam open. It crushes one fragment of root felt and presses another into dampness. A collector’s wiper removes water from silver tubing, but it also removes moth scales, spores, snail eggs, and the fine organic dust that has settled among the condensate. What survives the wiping may later be washed into a different part of the root mat.

Until the prepared channels carry water, the maintenance system is the catchment’s river system: discontinuous, uphill in places, indifferent to living cargo, and effective over very long periods of time.

The forest has responded without understanding it. Roots thicken where an annual shear repeatedly wounds them, forming a dense collar around the clearance. Condenser frames enclosed by stems gain a little protection from wind and falling scree. At one distribution junction, a split support is now held in position partly by living wood. Beside a shallow main, the root felt has been kept swollen against an old seam for so long that removing it would cause the leak it presently conceals. A later hand has added two words to the inspection sheet: *keep damp*.

No root knows what a pipe is. No valve has been designed to prefer the forest. Nevertheless, some parts of the system can now continue operating only because the other part remains alive.

It would be easy to call the whole catchment a single organism. It is not, quite. Its parts share no purpose. The mite struggling at the edge of a drop has no relation to the pressure in the northern mains. The fungus whitening a fracture does not intend to make soil. The moth’s brief flight does nothing deliberate for the forest, and the forest’s shade is not a kindness to the machinery.

But their separate economies keep closing around one another.

Cold tubing makes a drop. The drop lasts long enough to wet a leaf cup. Leaf fall shades the tubing and slows the loss of the next day’s moisture. Roots clasp the mast, steadying its footing in fractured stone. Fungi enter the clasp and begin to make a substance that will hold water after both root and mast have changed. A snail grazes the marker nearby, leaving one almost invisible trail across the lichen.

Nothing in this chain was planned as a whole. The whole is simply becoming harder to dismantle.

For now, much of the catchment’s life remains strangely local. There are snails along one sequence of markers and none along another. A pool may contain hundreds of pale efts while a cleft beyond the adjacent ridge contains only microbial film. The great clones approach one another through the hollows without touching. Even the fungi have provinces, their white threads ending at dry stone or at the boundary of a different chemistry in the felt.

The prepared channels below the reservoirs will alter that.

One bright season, after more cold dawns than anyone has bothered to count, a hollow will fill beyond its lip. Water will cross the prepared sill and enter a bed that has never carried more than a stain. The flow may be no deeper than a hand. It may last an hour.

It will carry spores, eggs, mites sheltering in dust, and perhaps a living fragment torn from a root collar. Some will be stranded in places too dry to survive. A few will lodge in the green sides of the channel, where shade and prepared stone already offer a little protection.

Afterward the water will vanish. Sunlight will return to the pale bed. From the rim, nothing will seem to have happened except that a dark line briefly appeared across the catchment floor.

But beneath it, in a few damp fractures, white threads will already be following the course the water took.
