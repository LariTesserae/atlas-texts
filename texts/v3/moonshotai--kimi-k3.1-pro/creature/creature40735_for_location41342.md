## The Lives of Rill

Rill’s human population is small enough that an unfamiliar step on the slag paths is noticed before the stranger is seen. There are pump minders, condenser tenders, fitters, electricians, dredging crews, cooks, and people whose official titles are best understood as promises to do whatever else is required. A settlement that size cannot afford single-use people. The electrician may also keep the medicine cupboard; the repair-shop clerk may cut hair; the person who reads the water-level gauge may spend wet afternoons teaching children from books gone soft at the corners.

Some families have remained long enough for a patch in a cottage floor to be known by a grandfather’s name. Others come for one contract and leave before their boots have shaped themselves to their feet. Both sorts acquire the same local expression: a slight inclination of the head, even in sleep, toward the engines.

The first thing a newcomer learns is not a trade but a way of listening. At first the town is one crushing, saturated roar. Then the turbine separates from the falling water, the falling water from the pistons, and the pistons from one another. Eventually a particular chain can sound too loose from thirty yards away. Experienced workers do not always hear more than other people; many have lost some part of their hearing. They have learned where to stand, what to touch with the back of a finger, when to lay a spanner against a casting and feel the note travel up the steel.

Conversation in the engine halls is close and economical. Hands supply the rest: stop, wait, hot, live, clear. In the cottages, voices often become unexpectedly soft. People who have spent a shift being precise at close range may have little appetite for filling a quiet room.

Children learn the blue-banded pipe before they learn much geography. They learn that clear water may still be dangerous, that a grating is not a floor, and that no errand is urgent enough to justify touching an unattended valve. They know which cottage floors carry the generating shed’s hum and which drain holds frogs. Their games imitate inspection: chalked levels on porch posts, solemn tours with an empty tin cup, arguments over whether a sound has changed. When a child reports such a change, the adult response is not always convenient, but it is meant to be serious. No one is considered too young to have noticed something.

When a main piston falters at night, sleepers sit up in cottages along the far path before any bell sounds. For a moment the frogs seem louder, as though they have come inside. Then doors open, lamps move, and people go out pulling on boots. The deepest reassurance Rill offers is not that nothing will fail. It is that failure is unlikely to pass unattended.

Shifts, rather than daylight, determine the shape of domestic life. Meals are cooked for whoever is waking. Someone is always airing bedding near the boiler house, where it becomes merely damp. Letters from elsewhere are kept in tins; tools are wiped before they are put away, and often again after. Boots stand where they can drain. A gecko behind a porch lamp is tolerated as part of the household, though not necessarily by the same name from one household to the next.

The canteen is where Rill becomes less guarded. Its regulars can tell a person’s shift from the hour at which they ask for food. Conversation there often concerns somewhere else: families, prices, roads, weather that has seasons. Local events provide less material. A culvert is cleared, a bearing is replaced, the level remains at its mark. Consequently, small matters receive elaborate attention—a new kind of snail in a drain, a heron that has changed reaches, a fitter’s insistence that the latest patch is superior to the patch it replaced.

The people are not ennobled by their usefulness. They quarrel over rosters, hoard good tools, postpone unpleasant reports, and grow tired of being needed. Some find the fixed landscape deeply peaceful. Others experience it as a theft of the future. Young residents ask what lies at the far end of the condensate main, almost as if the pipe were a road. Those who leave are not always looking for wealth or excitement. Sometimes they only want a room that becomes dry, or a day whose tasks cannot be predicted from the sound outside the door.

The present gauge-keeper began on the dredging crew and still walks with the caution of someone who has seen supposedly solid margins give way. She is not Rill’s ruler, though visitors sometimes mistake her for one because she is consulted so often. Her authority concerns the gauge, its calibration, and the records kept around it. She is obliged to be unimpressed by tradition: if the old brass instrument is wrong, its age is irrelevant. Her ledgers contain levels, flushings, repairs, complaints, and the names of those who first reported faults. Read closely, they are less a history of water than a history of attention.

Below and around this human tenancy lives a much larger population, one that has no loyalty to Rill’s equilibrium but has learned to exploit it.

The midges are the true multitude. Their larvae crowd the warm margins where the outfall has lost its first violence; the adults rise in columns so fine that they are visible mainly when swallows cut through them. Snails work the wet channels, their shells almost the color of the stone until lamplight finds a translucent edge. Their grazing leaves wandering clear lines through the olive film. A flushing erases the lines, carries off some of the snails, and delivers fresh silt to the crabs. For one inhabitant, maintenance is catastrophe; for another, it is dinner arriving on schedule.

Each animal possesses a map of Rill more detailed than the settlement plan, though it is drawn in temperature, salinity, current, shelter, and light.

The frogs’ map is of fresh water. They keep to drains where seepage and condensate have diluted the lagoon’s influence, calling most vigorously in the gaps between steam releases. A visitor may think they are answering the engines. In fact, they are taking the opportunities offered to them. Their calls become part of the acoustic knowledge of a cottage: not merely frogs, but frogs in the near drain, frogs at the expected pitch, frogs where frogs ought to be.

The geckos’ map is made of illuminated walls. By day they flatten themselves behind lamp brackets, in pipe shadows, under the lips of concrete feet. At night each claims a small province of amber light and the insects that blunder into it. The yellow-eyed gecko beside a cottage lamp may be thick-tailed and scarred, or newly arrived and thin. When one disappears, the lamp does not remain vacant for long. Residents sometimes speak of “our gecko” for years without agreeing whether it is still the same animal.

Swallows build beneath the gantries with pellets taken from the silt traps. Their nests are darker than the steel around them and often sit beside bolts, flanges, or old timber survivors. A repair can destroy a nest in a minute. The birds begin again when the work withdraws. They are not dependent on kindness, though fitters commonly step around an occupied nest when stepping around it costs nothing. Their real security is that shelter and rising insects are produced again and again in nearly the same places.

High in the condenser house, the bats occupy another version of that security: warmth without open flame, height without trees, narrow roof joints beyond the usual reach of repairs. When the light thins, they drop from the roof spaces and cross the basin steam. Below them, herons work the ring drain with no appearance of haste. Individual birds favor individual reaches. They are not tame; they are well informed. A heron can withdraw from a dredging crew exactly far enough and return while the crew is still collecting its tools.

The fish below the sluices face into the current, taking what the water delivers. Crabs cross fresh boot prints before the prints have had time to fill. Herons take fish and crabs; swallows, bats, frogs, and geckos divide the insects according to height, hour, and light. None of this is gentle. A dependable niche may also be a dependable place to die. Hot water scalds, intakes trap, lamps expose, and dredging removes whole neighborhoods. Rill’s work does not preserve animals individually. It repeatedly recreates the conditions in which others can succeed them.

The plants live under an even stricter tenancy. Gray sedge persists in fissures where boots and dredging tools seldom strike. Ferns occur behind particular downspouts because roof water, shelter, and shade coincide there and not two feet away. A fern may outlast several occupants of a cottage simply by never obstructing anything important. That is what passes for a garden in Rill: a living thing that gives the work no reason to remove it.

For all the town’s apparent sameness, none of its inhabitants experiences Rill as fixed. To a snail, a flushing is upheaval. To a swallow, a rebuilt gantry is altered country. To a child, one year transforms the size of the engines and the reach of the paths. To an old pump minder, the same unchanging note can measure a gradual loss of strength. Even the gauge-keeper’s brass standard must be cleaned and recalibrated because devotion to a level does not prevent drift.

On an ordinary night, the off-going shift crosses the wet paths while frogs use a pause in the steam. A heron stands at the ring drain. Swallows shift in their dark nests; a gecko takes an insect from the wall beside a lamp. In the gauge house, the keeper makes a mark that resembles yesterday’s mark but is not yesterday’s. Somewhere a cottage door opens because someone has heard, or imagined, a difference.

Rill holds its ground at one level. Nothing that lives there is held so easily.
