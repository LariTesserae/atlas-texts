The island has no single inhabitant, and no clean boundary between an inhabitant and its habitation. The shearwaters live in soil partly made of shearwaters. The crabs shelter beneath trees whose fallen leaves pass through their burrows. The trees root among bird bones, coral skeletons, and the softened remains of older trees. To ask what lives here is to begin with the loudest animals and end, much later, with the rock.

At night, the shearwaters are the most insistent residents, although most of a shearwater’s life happens elsewhere. The open sea is its country of wind, distance, and food; the island is the small, dangerous place to which it must return to breed. On land, its grace deserts it. A bird comes out of the darkness with air rushing through its wings, misjudges a branch, and arrives in a clatter of leaves. It scrambles upright, finds the remembered entrance, and disappears underground. Another voice may answer from within.

For a chick waiting at the end of a burrow, that tunnel is nearly the whole world: warm darkness, the smell of damp soil and fish, the vibrations of approaching feet, the enormous noise of other birds beyond the entrance. Its meals arrive from waters it has never seen. Its first nourishment, its shelter, and sometimes its death all occur within the same few armfuls of earth. If it survives, it will leave the island and become almost entirely a creature of the sea. Yet the burrow remains the place that made its life on land possible, and birds of later seasons will enlarge and reuse the colony’s excavations until the nesting slope is honeycombed with old beginnings.

Above them, the tree-nesting birds occupy less sheltered addresses. Their nests are platforms of twigs set into forks and crowns, revised by every landing and sometimes diminished by a neighbor stealing material. Wings are repeatedly opened and folded; feet search for a better hold; branches spring under shifting weight. A nest may look careless from below, but for its occupants it is a precise little territory, known by its rims, approaches, and hazards. Beneath heavily used trees, dropped food, guano, eggshell, and fallen nest material create a different soil from that only a short distance away. The birds live in the canopy, but their influence travels downward.

The crabs belong more continuously to the ground. Individually, many of their movements are small: a claw tests the edge of a leaf, a fragment is pulled backward, a pellet of excavated earth is pushed aside. Multiplied across the colony, these movements make the land appear restless. A crab emerges from the humid security of its burrow and works through a territory of scents and obstacles—soft litter, hard coral, dropped fruit, feathers, carrion, the entrances and pathways of other animals. It drags some things below and tears others where they lie. It does not clean the forest in any tidy sense. It worries the forest apart and redistributes it.

Their tunnels join the older hollowness of the reef. Rain-made fissures, coral cavities, abandoned burrows, and fresh excavations intersect without forming any plan. Air enters. Water lingers a little longer in one place and vanishes quickly in another. Fine soil collects where there was once only loose grit. A burrow lip becomes smooth through repeated passage; elsewhere, a roof collapses and leaves a shallow pit for leaves and seedlings. The crabs live inside alterations they are always making.

Fruit bats inhabit a different map. For them, the open lanes between canopies are thoroughfares, and the forest is not a continuous roof but an irregular arrangement of feeding trees, resting trees, and useful landmarks. They cross spaces that a crab might never traverse and a shearwater knows mainly as an obstacle to landing. A bat carries fruit away from the branch where it ripened, drops fragments beneath a roost, and deposits seeds elsewhere in its droppings. Without intention, it connects trees that have no means of approaching one another. Some of the next generation of vegetation will begin along these nightly routes.

Only after the larger movements have separated does the smaller population become apparent. A gecko lies against a trunk with such stillness that only the slight working of its throat gives it away. Its world is a vertical territory of bark ridges, insects, and approaching vibration. A moth blunders within reach; the gecko’s stillness ends in a movement too brief to seem like a journey. Elsewhere, beetles pass through litter and decaying wood, moths strike leaves, and small hunters and grazers occupy surfaces that appear vacant from a few steps away.

The insects are not merely the island’s background noise. They are an uncounted population feeding, mating, hunting, emerging, and dying at different heights. Their chorus has no single rhythm because it is not a single chorus. One patch falls quiet while another continues beyond the trees. Under a fern, in the split of a branch, beneath loose bark, and within a warm piece of rotting wood are separate assemblies of lives, each governed by a slightly different degree of moisture and shelter.

Snails follow the dampness more closely than almost anything else. A frond’s underside, a shaded pit wall, or the deep groove of limestone can be enough to preserve a usable film of water. When conditions permit, a snail moves across surfaces coated with growth too thin to see, grazing with its rasping mouth. When the air dries, it withdraws and waits. Its shell is a small portable refuge, limestone’s substance reorganized into a form that can travel, however slowly.

The vegetation, too, is a population rather than a setting. Each broad-leaved tree makes a climate around itself: shade below, humidity held a little longer among the roots, bark that remains wet after exposed stone has dried. A fig may stand in deep soil while sending roots across bare pinnacles in search of another cleft. Vines borrow height from several trees at once. Ferns occupy the steep, sheltered sides of hollows where larger plants cannot establish themselves. On the windward rim, low trees endure salt and exposure; inland, their relatives of light and space grow beneath a canopy that changes from season to season.

A tree’s death does not remove it from the community. Its fall opens light, damages nests, crushes burrows, and gives seedlings an opportunity. Then fungi and insects enter the wood. The trunk softens, catches litter, and holds moisture. What had been a vertical resident becomes a long horizontal refuge and eventually a ridge of soil.

Within that soil live the inhabitants no census of wingbeats would find: mites and springtails in the litter, threadlike worms in moist films, fungal filaments penetrating wood and guano, bacteria multiplying around every particle of fresh nourishment. Their scale is minute, but they stand at the crossings between one kind of life and another. Nutrients brought ashore in a bird’s body do not become forest simply by falling to the ground. They are broken apart, transformed, released, retained, and taken up by roots. A fish caught beyond sight of the island may contribute substance to a leaf; the leaf may feed a crab or fall beside a fig root; the fig may later feed a bat. The sea is present even on the highest, driest ridge, carried there piece by piece.

This abundance is not peace. Crabs scavenge where they can, and an egg or helpless chick is food as well as a possible bird. Burrowing can undermine a tree. Trampling can prevent seedlings from establishing, while heavy enrichment favors some patches and leaves others harsh and bare. Nest material is stolen. Young animals die. A fallen adult becomes a gathering place for scavengers and decomposers. The island does not separate neatly between nursery and cemetery, or between damage and construction. Often the same event becomes each in turn, depending on which life is considered.

Beneath all of them, the ancient reef still exerts its influence. Its corals are no longer living inhabitants, but the forms they made continue to decide where water travels, where roots can enter, where soil gathers, and where a burrow must turn aside. A hollow that shelters a chick began as a space among skeletons. A fern’s refuge may be a solution cup formed in a coral mass. Even the dead reef participates in the habits of the living.

No resident experiences the whole island. The shearwater knows ocean weather and a crowded slope; the crab knows a circuit of burrows, litter, and stone; the bat knows feeding crowns and open passages; the gecko knows bark; the snail knows the approach and retreat of moisture. A tree knows its station through roots, shade, wind, and the slow contest for light. Their island is not quite the same place from one life to the next.

Toward dawn, the inhabitants change address. Birds leave for the sea while others remain below with eggs or chicks. Bats return to their roosts. Crabs withdraw into humid earth. Geckos vanish into crevices, and snails fasten themselves against the coming dryness. The noise changes, but the island does not empty. It closes thousands of small doors.

By daylight it may look less crowded. Most of its population has simply gone underground, beneath leaves, into bark, against shaded rock, or back over the horizon. The raised reef is not one home containing a collection of animals and plants. It is the place where innumerable partial homes overlap—and where, through feet, roots, wings, shells, decay, and rain, each resident helps make shelter for another.
