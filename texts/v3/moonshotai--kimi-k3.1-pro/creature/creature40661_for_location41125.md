Life in the pocket is patchier than the crowding suggests. The animals are abundant, but their abundance is made of innumerable small sufficiencies: one reactive crystal face, one pore where gas still leaks, one dust grain bearing a film rich enough to support a larva. A body length away, there may be nothing to eat.

The microbial films are not a continuous food supply. They are many different communities occupying slightly different chemical conditions. Some live where hot gas first reaches fresh mineral, harvesting the steepest and most dangerous reactions. Others persist just behind that front, using sulfur and iron compounds already partly altered. Still others inhabit the leakage between larger pores, growing slowly on differences too slight for animals to detect except by whether a surface is worth scraping. The mineral left by one community becomes the substrate of another.

Their success is brief by necessity. Where deposition is rapid, they are sealed beneath new crystal almost as soon as they multiply. Where flow fails, their chemistry becomes exhausted. The richest films therefore occur along narrow, unstable margins: active enough to renew themselves, not so active that they are immediately entombed. A grazer can find such a place by touch and chemical sense, but it cannot keep it. The quality of the feeding ground is always changing beneath its mouth.

Most of the animals are variations on a few flat, many-legged forms. Some remain lentil-sized throughout their lives and specialize in crevices. Others, after repeated molts, become broad as thumbnails and scrape the exposed faces of crystal blades. Their legs grip independently. When a crystal ticks or shifts beneath them, some feet release while the others hold, so that the animal seems to flow cautiously over the fracture rather than startle away from it. Their low shells take the abrasion that would otherwise destroy their backs. The wax at their joints is softer, but it flexes without cracking and seals the body against the hot, reactive dust.

They do not cool themselves. Nothing evaporates from them. Their internal oils and waxy fluids operate at the temperature of the surrounding stone, within limits set by species and age. On a favorable surface they feed for long intervals, the fine mouthparts beneath the body loosening microbes and mineral together. Useful material is absorbed; the rest passes as dry, compacted grains. Even their waste remains close to its origin, joining the sulfide grit below or lodging in the pore where it was produced.

Where several bodies lie deep, the arrangement is not cooperation. It is the temporary result of many animals seeking the same film and the same secure footing. The lowest grazer may still be feeding while another clings to its shell and a third bridges between that shell and the wall. The stack changes by small displacements. A lower animal finishes a patch and pushes outward; an upper one loses purchase and settles into its place; a juvenile crosses the whole group to reach a narrow seam beyond. There is no permanent possession of a trail or crystal face. Competition consists mostly of prying, resisting, and making another animal’s footing slightly worse than one’s own.

The rare electrical flashes reveal only the least important facts about them: their flattened outlines, the overlap of their shells, the way some walls appear paved with bodies. To another grazer, an animal is not primarily a shape. It is hardness underfoot, a chemical signature at close range, and a pattern transmitted through the mineral.

That mineral sense is exceedingly fine. Feeding mouthparts produce a repeated vibration quite different from the irregular ticking of crystal growth. Footfalls are broader and less regular. A shell deliberately rocked against stone makes a harder signal still. Grazers drum against one another and against the surfaces on which they stand. Their patterns are simple, but sufficient: close-range challenge, reproductive readiness, the presence of another animal of the same kind. A contest may end after an exchange of drumming, before either animal has been levered from its place.

A signal travels only where the solid carries it. Two animals separated by a finger’s width of gas may be inaccessible to one another, while others on opposite faces of the same crystal blade can communicate around its edge. Courtship often amounts to an answered rhythm, a gradual approach, and chemical confirmation when the animals meet. Then they separate. Eggs are left in existing dust-filled pores, enclosed in tough wax and flecked with mineral. They are not guarded, and nothing is prepared for them.

Molting is more dangerous than mating. A growing grazer becomes confined by its own shell until the plates lift and split along their seams. If a suitable pore is nearby, the animal wedges itself into it; if not, it molts among the others. Nothing about the new body is wet. It is soft with unhardened wax, its fresh plates easily distorted. For a while it cannot grip strongly or drum effectively. Predators take some animals in this condition. Others are crushed by the ordinary movements of the crowd. Those that survive mineralize their new shells gradually, incorporating material derived from the films on which they feed.

Many young begin in the dust. To their minute larvae, the floor is not a floor but a deep landscape of grains, needles, and shell fragments. A single grain can present several habitats: an exposed side touched by flowing gas, a sheltered side where different microbes persist, and pores small enough to exclude nearly every animal except the larva itself. The earliest stages have little mineral armor. They move between grains by successive contractions, feeding on films that larger animals could never reach.

Some remain dust-dwellers through much of their development. Others climb only after their legs and mouthparts have assumed their grazing form. Broods a short distance apart can meet entirely different fates. One pore becomes too reactive and its larvae cease moving; another cools and its films dwindle; a third remains suitable long enough for a few young to molt into the crowded world above. There is no synchrony among them except what the local geology imposes.

The wire-bodied predators inhabit the interruptions in this world of shells. They are not roaming serpents. Most occupy a small system of pores and trails, their narrow bodies folded along crystal edges or threaded through spaces no broad grazer could enter. Each section can grip while another extends, allowing them to move with very little vibration. When waiting, they are almost indistinguishable, by touch, from another irregularity in the wall.

They detect the minute repetition of feeding mouthparts and can distinguish it from the chamber’s other ticking and cracking. A grazer working steadily along a trail announces itself long before it reaches the hunter. The predator does not pursue it through the open crush. It waits until the body is close enough, anchors several parts of itself, and makes a short, exact strike at a joint or shell seam. Its hooked mouthparts open the vulnerable region rather than shattering the armor. It takes the wax-rich tissues and oils, often leaving much of the shell intact.

After such a meal, a hunter may remain nearly motionless for a long interval. Its stillness is not rest in any gentle sense; it is simply the most economical way to exist where pursuit costs more than waiting. Smaller wire-bodied forms hunt larvae among shell fragments. Larger ones sometimes die when a molt leaves them too soft to hold their anchorage, or when a grazing frontier shifts and the trails beside them fall silent.

The blunt scavengers work more slowly and less selectively. Their bodies are thick, their heads broad, their legs suited to pushing through loose grit rather than clinging to exposed blades. They scrape residual wax from molted shells, worry organic material from the seams of dead grazers, and consume the microbes that multiply briefly around a carcass. Several may work at the same remains without any interaction beyond shoving and occasional displacement.

A death therefore becomes another small chemical frontier. First the body’s own fluids alter the immediate pore. Microbes respond. Scavengers arrive or stumble upon the enrichment. Wire hunters may take a scavenger in turn. Eventually only resistant shell material remains, and even that is abraded, coated, and gradually incorporated into new crust. Nothing is deliberately cleaned away. The chamber simply continues to use what is available.

Animals also carry the films with them. Microbes persist in shell seams, on mouthparts, and in the dust adhering to waxed joints. A grazer leaving an exhausted surface may seed another without any change in its behavior. A predator crossing between trails can connect microbial communities that would otherwise remain separated. Their movements are too small and infrequent to distribute life evenly, but over generations they help keep the patchwork from becoming entirely isolated.

When a deep passage constricts and the chamber’s pressure-note begins its slow alteration, no general alarm passes through the animals. The first changes occur in the films. One rich crystal face becomes poor; another, previously unusable, begins to support growth. Grazers feed longer without gaining much, then move farther between feeding bouts. Some stacks thin out. Trails extend into pores that had rarely been crossed. Predators waiting beside the old routes go hungry, while scavengers may briefly prosper where animals have died after failed molts or unsuccessful movement.

The redistribution is neither an exodus nor a search for a promised refuge. It is the accumulated result of local responses repeated over generations. Animals remain where feeding is possible. Where it is not, some die and some wander. The smallest young enter passages unavailable to adults; microbes travel still farther. Most of these routes end in sealed mineral, exhausted chemistry, or heat beyond tolerance. Enough do not.

In a newly opened interstice, life is at first almost nothing: a few viable traces in fresh pores, a chemical richness where gas touches unaltered mineral, perhaps larvae too small to disturb the dust through which they move. Films establish themselves. Grazing follows where bodies can reach. Later, on some crystal face, one shell drums against another and receives an answer. The community has not remembered the old chamber. It has simply resumed the same narrow necessities under slightly different conditions.

Nothing here knows the shape of the mountain or the fact that the pocket is unfinished. A grazer’s world may be one productive blade and the backs of its nearest competitors. A hunter’s world may be three trails and the pore in which it waits. A larva’s world may be the sheltered side of a single grain. Their lives are no less complete for being so locally known.

Eventually the old films will be sealed, the last animal vibrations will cease, and shells will remain as disorderly inclusions within the filling crystal. Elsewhere, before that closure is complete, other animals will already be following other films. Their history will not lie preserved in any one chamber. It will continue in transit: molt by molt, pore by pore, through the mountain’s slow manufacture of stone.
