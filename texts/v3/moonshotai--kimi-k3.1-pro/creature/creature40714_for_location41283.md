## Life in the Breath-Cleft

Nothing large lives in the Breath-Cleft, and almost nothing lives there alone.

Its first inhabitants are scarcely distinguishable from the rock. The pale microbial films spread where warmth, moisture, and minerals overlap: a dullness on one stone flange, a greasy sheen in a recess, a softness that comes away under a fingertip. They have no foliage and make no visible display. During the mountain’s humid exhalations they extend a little beyond their old margins; when cold air is drawn inward, their outer edges dry and whiten. What looks from a distance like a continuous stain is actually a patchwork of feeding, dormancy, and grazing.

Springtails work through these films in numbers far greater than the eye first registers. A falling bead of water scattering them can reveal that an apparently empty patch of wall was crowded. In the wet seams lie translucent larvae, some clear except for a dark thread of gut, others clouded by what they have eaten. They feed close to where they hatch, leaving narrow, cleaner tracks through the films. They cannot cross the chamber if their seam dries. They crowd into the last moisture, or they dry where they are. Some of the larvae are young of the cleft’s small flying insects; some belong to the moths. Many never become anything but food.

Thread-fine mites move among them and below them, through dust compounded of mineral grains, wing scales, droppings, molted skins, and bodies too small to recognize once they have broken apart. Some graze. Some scavenge. Others fasten themselves to larger animals. Different kinds gather at the wing joints of bats, behind the heads of crickets, and around the eyes of lizards. The visible inhabitants are, in this sense, only the largest walking portions of the floor.

The pale crickets are the chamber’s principal cleaners, though they are not harmless cleaners. Wingless and long-legged, they feel their way with antennae that seem to occupy more darkness than their bodies do. They eat fallen moth wings, guano, shed skins, dead larvae, and any softer animal they can overpower. A cricket caught shortly after molting may be eaten by its own kind before its new casing has hardened. Their rasping comes not from wings but from legs and body surfaces rubbed together, so that their sound seems to arise from the floor and walls rather than from distinct musicians.

They are nervous of falling water. A drop striking nearby sends them flicking away in several directions, long legs kicking grit behind them. Then, one by one, they return to investigate what the drop has wetted, exposed, or killed.

Above them, the ash moths live so densely that their individual lives are difficult to follow. Each moth is small and unremarkable: wings freckled with stone-gray and dull brown, scales already loose, body narrow beneath the soft pile. Together they become the rear wall’s apparent surface. They crawl over one another to reach warmer stone. Those buried several layers deep hold the warmth around those above. A slow ripple can pass through the whole covering without any moth taking flight.

When seriously disturbed, the wall does not rise all at once. First it appears to crawl. Then patches detach, and finally the air fills with separate wings. Their scales dust everything nearby. The bats’ fur becomes gray with it; the lizards’ snouts pale; a wet basin may carry a brief, delicate scum.

Many of the adult moths do not feed, or take only moisture. They mate in the crawling layers, lay their eggs in protected dust and damp fissures, and die within the warmth that made their brief adulthood possible. Their fall is continuous enough that the crickets beneath them need not wait for a catastrophe. A few wings always come spiraling down. Whole bodies follow, some still twitching. The moths are at once a population, a food supply, and a kind of dry weather.

Around the three basins live the most patient of the larger residents: the flat amphibians. At rest they are almost impossible to separate from saturated grit. Their skins take on the colors of the mineral rings—ochre at the edges, black in the crevices, a sickly pale gray where the stone itself is pale. They can remain motionless while mites cross their backs and crickets step within a tongue’s reach.

They feed on springtails, larvae, young crickets, and small winged things that come down to moisture. When they call, the transformation is absurd. A piece of wet stone swells its throat and produces a single hard note with the force of a struck pebble. Another answers from beneath the grit. Then another. The calls overlap without settling into a common rhythm, until the basins seem to be ringing although they hold little or no visible water.

Rain changes them. When cloud strikes the summit and water enters sideways, the amphibians emerge farther from their hiding places and gather along the filling margins. Eggs and young appear in the saturated grit and shallowest water when conditions permit, but reproduction here is never secure. A basin may shrink before a brood is ready. Some young emerge very small; others do not emerge at all. Stranded eggs and bodies are eaten quickly. Not every rain gives the amphibians a new generation. Some rains give only a brief feast to everything around them.

The narrow lizards hunt the same margins with almost none of the amphibians’ noise. They lie with their chins against the stone, long tails fitted into shallow grooves, and wait for movement to cross some private boundary. Their stillness is not sleep. A moth descending toward damp rock, a cricket cleaning its antennae, a young amphibian making its first exposed journey: any of these may cause the lizard to become, for an instant, a short gray streak. Then it is motionless again, sometimes with only a slight working of the throat to show that anything happened.

They follow warmth by small distances rather than ranging through the whole chamber. During cold indrawn periods they flatten themselves into protected cracks or beneath warm grit and become sluggish. When the seep favors a different section of wall, their hunting places alter too. Their shed skins do not last long. The crickets find them.

The bats have a different relation to the cleft. For most of its inhabitants, the chamber is nearly the whole world. For the bats, it is an address.

They pack the ceiling so closely that fur, folded membranes, and small hooked limbs seem more continuous than the rock beneath them. Young, when present, are kept within the warmest press of bodies. Adults crawl over one another, argue over familiar places, and wake in brief bursts of clicking before settling again. Occasionally one loses its hold or is pushed from the edge of the roost. There is a leathery thump, a scramble, a burst of indignant sound, and the ceiling closes over the disturbance.

Some hunt within the chamber, taking moths from the air or snatching insects from the walls. But the cleft could not sustain the roost by itself. On favorable nights the bats leave through the sideways mouth and travel far down the mountain, returning with food gathered in colder valleys and lower slopes. What they bring back is not only in their stomachs. Their droppings fall onto the floor day after day, supporting scavengers and enriching the films. The ammonia in the chamber’s smell is partly the odor of this constant import.

The bats also carry the cleft’s smaller life with them. Mites travel in their fur and wing folds, invisible passengers on every journey. And when a bat dies, whether on the ceiling or after a fall, its body enters the same quick economy as a moth’s, only on a larger scale. Crickets gather. Larvae appear. Fine bones remain after the softer materials have gone.

Only at the entrance does light-fed life become important, and even there it remains grudging. The gray lichens occupy the outer lip with almost geological patience. The rust-colored rosettes endure where rain occasionally reaches them, and the cushion plant beside the outermost basin holds its rice-grain leaves close against the grit. In a difficult year, its visible achievement may be little more than not losing what it already had. These lives are survivors at the boundary, not the foundation of the multitude within.

The heavy-billed mountain birds come out of that brighter, emptier world to feed at the mouth. Their shadows alter the chamber before their bodies do: crickets near the entrance fall quiet, and loose moths settle. The birds pick exposed insects from the gravel and probe the edges of the floor. Hard cases are braced against an unshaped pebble and struck until they split. When the meal is finished, the pebble is dropped. It is not carried away, improved, or kept for next time. For a few minutes it has been an anvil; afterward it is merely one of the stones.

Other predators arrive still more accidentally. A favorable wind may bring a spider or hunting insect into the shelter of the overhang. One may survive for a while and prey on the cleft’s smaller residents. Most arrive exhausted, in air too thin and cold for them, and do not establish anything. Their bodies are absorbed by a community that owes much of its existence to arrivals but is not open to every arrival.

The whole assemblage changes with the mountain’s breathing, though never in unison. When warm, humid air flows outward, the middle films glisten, crickets spread farther from the rear, moths loosen their packing, and the lizards become quicker. The basin amphibians may call from under the grit. Sound seems to occupy more of the chamber.

When cold air is drawn inward, there is no general alarm. The films simply lose their sheen. One band of crickets stops rasping, then another. Moths crowd closer to the warmest stone. The bats compress overhead. Lizards cease their small adjustments, and the amphibians become mineral shapes again. The cleft’s life has not vanished; it has withdrawn by a few body lengths. For creatures so small, that is enough to constitute another country.

Over longer periods, the same slow restlessness continues. Wet years briefly enlarge the basins and leave new mineral rings. Dry years reduce the amphibians’ opportunities and widen the belts of dust. If the seep warms a different portion of the rear wall, the moth covering shifts first; the densest bat roosts follow almost imperceptibly. Old layers of wings, droppings, and chitin remain beneath the places that have been abandoned.

It is easy to mistake the chamber for a closed world, but it persists through leaks: minerals and heat from below, rain driven sideways, food carried home in bats, occasional bodies blown in from outside. Its abundance is not gentle, and it is never secure. It includes starvation at the edge of a drying seam, whole broods lost with a shrinking basin, and animals eating one another almost as quickly as they die.

Still, the result is unmistakably crowded life: a warm wall trembling with moths, a ceiling restless with bats, a floor ticking and rasping beneath them, patient hunters around water that may disappear, and unseen grazers consuming the paleness between. Nothing there is lush. Everything is compressed.

The abundance is real, but it is not plenty. It is a small allowance of warmth, used almost completely.
