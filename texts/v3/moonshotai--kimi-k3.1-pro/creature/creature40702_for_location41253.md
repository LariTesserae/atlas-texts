The people keep the census, but they are not the population.

Their names fit on shift boards, clinic cards, and the painted lids of dormitory lockers: pump attendants, pipe fitters, scalers, rail drivers, surveyors, cooks, screen menders, the nurse, the two men who maintain the ventilators and disagree about how to do it. Above them, the roof occupies another order of number altogether.

A bat roost looks undifferentiated only from the ground. Close up, it is an accumulation of preferences and disputes. Animals return to particular seams of warmth. Some endure close neighbors; others wake biting and shuffling over a space no wider than a hand. Bodies are groomed, folded, climbed over, and folded again. Wings flex without opening. The whole vault seems to breathe because so many small breathing bodies obscure the stone.

The swiftlets keep to their narrower places, except for the pump-hall trespassers. Those are officially an maintenance problem and unofficially known by location: the nest behind the west girder, the pair above the disused cable box, the birds that cannot be persuaded that a warm beam is inferior to a fissure. Fitters inspecting the roof steel learn to look before reaching.

At the daily departure, the people do not usually gather to watch. There is comparatively little to watch. Work continues while the upper darkness loosens and the air begins to move. Loose tags lift on their wires. Ventilator screens answer with a faint rattle. For a while, speech becomes more trouble than it is worth. A pump attendant may lay down a wrench and wait, not in reverence, but because he cannot make himself heard and does not care to shout. Then the fissure has taken the last of them, and the chamber feels briefly larger.

What falls from the roosts supports the town’s least regarded citizens. Where guano is scraped from steps and landings, it is simply filth. Where it lies on an untrodden ledge, it becomes pasture. Beetles work its crust; springtails occupy the finer spoil; fungal threads whiten and disappear into it. Cave crickets stand beneath the richest falls with their antennae extended farther than seems necessary, reading a room that human eyes would call empty. They are neither timid nor quite bold. A vibration sends them several body lengths sideways, where they stop and resume listening.

The crickets are the animals most likely to enter human domestic arrangements. A boot left below a shelf may acquire one. So may a food cupboard with a damaged screen. This is why a torn screen produces more immediate neighborhood concern than a stained ceiling. A ceiling can be watched. A hole in a screen is an open invitation to everything small, warm-blooded in appetite if not in body, and willing to explore.

Around the lamps, midges make their brief vertical weather. Lamp crews learn which colonies can be reduced by lowering a shade and which require the fixture to be cleaned, moved, or simply surrendered to darkness for a time. The green films at the base of the same lamps are scrubbed away without ceremony. Left alone, they make a step beautiful for perhaps a day and treacherous for much longer.

In the water, life becomes less visible and more difficult to count. Bacterial mats spread in colors too subdued to be called colors at all: cream, rust, the gray of old cloth. Some quiet thermal channels support growth that owes nothing to daylight. Elsewhere, fungal threads wait for organic matter to arrive from the roosts, the drains, or the town. Tiny crustaceans gather in clouds so dense that a held lamp seems to have stirred the water into sediment. They turn together, separate, and gather again.

The eyeless fish are not common enough to be emblems. A person may work beside the backwaters for years without seeing one. When one does enter the edge of a light, it does not resemble a secret revealed. It is merely an animal holding itself near cover, its flank more visible than its intention. A cart passing overhead, a changed vibration in a pipe, or the approach of the lamp may send it away with one economical movement. It returns to a darkness that is not, to it, darkness.

The human residents mostly arrive from above. Some come for a contract and leave before its end, defeated less by the heat than by the absence of any single thing to blame. They cannot sleep, or cannot wake properly, or grow tired of clothes that never feel entirely dry. Others discover that they can tolerate all this and simply hate the confined horizon. A few go up the incline at the end of their term, find the surface too bright and excessively weathered, and come back down before their replacement has learned the pump hall.

Staying produces no mystical transformation. It produces habits.

Hats acquire brims. Cupboards acquire feet. Valuable objects migrate upward onto shelves and hooks, and photographs are put behind glass before the paper can curl. Boots are placed on grates rather than floors, though their insoles remain damp anyway. Food is covered without being prompted. Spectacles are polished after leaving a cooled room. Nobody steps confidently onto fresh white mineral rind, however invitingly level it looks. Nobody describes a floor as dry without adding, at least mentally, *at present*.

Old residents do not learn to see in the dark. They learn where the dark contains a rail, where it contains water, and where an apparently straight route crosses a survey lot that has never been drained. They carry lights but use them economically. They recognize colleagues by footsteps on grating and can tell a loaded cart from an empty one before its lamp appears.

Most of all, they learn to listen through sound rather than despite it. A scaler with a hammer can hear whether vibration is traveling through solid pipe or through the accreted rind inside it. A pump attendant reads the machinery with a hand resting lightly on a railing. Ventilator people distinguish labor from strain. In the dormitories, sleep comes to depend on the combined noise of flywheels, water, insects, and the roof. Silence is not restful. Silence requires investigation.

This does not make the inhabitants especially solemn. Behind the screens, they can be as noisy and trivial as people anywhere. They argue about card debts, supply quality, the allocation of cooled rooms, and whether a particular datum stud has moved or merely been struck by a cart. News travels quickly because there is little of it and because everyone eventually needs something from the pump hall, the clinic, or the goods platform. A joke told in the screened mess may reach the outer causeway before the person who inspired it has finished shift.

Their civic pride attaches to modest achievements: a drain that remains clear, a bridge whose underside has finally stopped dripping onto the walkway, a dormitory in which no cricket has been found for a week. They can be fiercely impatient about a missing washer and strangely serene about a housing terrace that has been under consideration for nine years. The washer matters now. The terrace must wait for water, survey records, and the rock’s opinion.

Weather reaches them first as report. A supply driver mentions a storm on the plateau; someone in the pump hall writes down the date. Weeks later, when the flumes strengthen and another brown line appears on the sump stairs, residents say that the rain has arrived. No one looks up. The phrase has ceased to sound strange to them.

Near the incline mouth, the potted ferns are tended with a seriousness out of proportion to their usefulness. They are not quite a garden. They are more like a recurring experiment that several people have quietly decided must not be allowed to fail. Pots are shifted toward or away from the daylight; imported soil is replaced; blackened fronds are removed. Beyond the reach of the mouth, everyone knows, the attempt becomes sentimental and then brief.

The bats are not mascots, and the eyeless fish are not charms. The roosts foul equipment, restrict lighting, and make whole areas unpleasant to occupy. The crickets are pests when they choose to be. Yet a sudden alteration in the ceiling’s chatter can stop an experienced resident as surely as a changed pump note. These lives are part of the chamber’s working condition. People may curse them without wishing the chamber emptied of them.

The longest residents can sleep through wings, dripping water, the tick of insects, and the ordinary thunder of a goods cart. But let one heavy pump alter its stroke, and they wake completely. A foot touches the floor. Someone reaches for boots. A hand closes around a railing, waiting to feel whether the disturbance is local or whether another machine has answered it.

For that moment, nearly the whole human town is present in a few small actions. The roof shifts and resettles without comprehension. Below the walkways, crickets resume their antennae-work. In the sumps, crustaceans turn around some new and invisible abundance. The warm water continues opening and closing stone.

Then the pump finds its rhythm. The Low Works settles back into its scattered lamps and screened rooms—not into silence, and certainly not into peace, but into its own crowded patience.
