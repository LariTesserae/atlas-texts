The people of the Bright Cistern are not naturally solemn, though visitors often think so. Their quiet is a practical accomplishment, like a well-seated lid. In the lanes, where a public stair may pass within arm’s reach of a sleeping chamber and every wall contains another household, work is directed by gesture. Across the open floor, where speech would require shouting, people summon one another by personal signs: a turn of the wrist, two fingers at the shoulder, a hand briefly opened beside the head. These signs acquire nicknames, emphases, and private jokes. A skilled worker can receive an instruction, refuse it, and question the intelligence of the person giving it without pausing on the stair.

Indoors, when no one nearby is sleeping, they talk readily. They argue about goats, repeat gossip from three tiers above, tease apprentices, and tell long stories in which the same disastrous turning is endured by increasingly foolish ancestors. Laughter is usually muffled by felt and hoods rather than by principle. A household can be affectionate, quarrelsome, or tiresome in all the ordinary ways. Silence has not made them especially wise. It has merely made unnecessary noise difficult to excuse.

To a stranger, the bone goggles and dark eye paste give them an intimidating sameness. They recognize one another instead by gait, by the cut and patching of an outer garment, by the way someone carries a jar or takes a familiar grip on the stairs. At home, with the goggles hung beside the door, their hands are often more striking than their faces: cracked at the knuckles, stained around the nails, polished smooth where the same tools and ropes have been held for years. They warm their fingers against their bodies, beneath the arms of a neighbor, or sometimes against the side of a goat. A room is called generous not because it is large, but because it is warm.

Hospitality usually begins with a lidded cup of fresh seep water and a seat away from the brightness. To leave a guest’s drink uncovered would not suggest abundance; it would suggest that the host had forgotten how the place works. A visitor is lent goggles, shown where to put each foot, and discouraged from admiring any view that requires standing still on an exposed stair.

The forty-odd households are not simply clusters of blood relations. A household may include an elderly neighbor who can no longer climb to her old room, a child apprenticed into another family’s work, or several adults who have combined their goats and fuel because none could winter comfortably alone. Wealth is counted in healthy animals, intact felts, dry sleeping places, stored dung, and reliable help during a turning. Some households are considerably poorer than others. Their poverty shows in bedding that never quite loses its damp and in rooms where ceiling-rain must be caught rather than prevented.

There are feuds over fuel, roof rights, stray goats, and repairs done badly enough to become someone else’s problem. Shared necessity does not make everyone generous; it makes meanness unusually visible. Even so, custom is firm about emergency water and about warming dangerously cold hands. A person may be disliked for thirty years and still be let inside when their fingers have gone white.

The most respected practical experts are the seep-readers. They are not priests, and there is no single chief among them. Some are old; some are simply observant people whose predictions have survived enough turnings. They read the dark margins around pools, the behavior of eels, the posture of goats, the texture of salt on a wall, and the faint coldness felt through a knuckle held near stone. Their knowledge is less a map than a great accumulation of sequences: if this seam whitens, that stair will glaze; if those midges rise before the goats close ranks, water will probably return beneath the southern overhang; if a new felt is hung here, three rooms below may begin to drip.

Seep-readers are often wrong about small things. The careful ones say so. Their authority rests partly on knowing which uncertainties can be tolerated and which require everyone to stop work.

Consequently, building is a neighborhood matter. A household may own its slabs, felts, and roof pen, but a roof changes where light falls and where returning moisture gathers. A polished slab may make a lane unbearable. A new shelf may shelter one cistern while exposing another. A channel cut for convenience can deprive a pan whose dark pasture supports several families’ goats. Before old work is altered, people come to look at it, often silently, crouching one above another on the stairs. Sometimes they look for a very long time and then do nothing.

Their oldest place-names belong to dependable things: seeps, shelters, grips, gates, and channels. Transient pools may have descriptive names or none at all. Where an outsider says that a pool has dried, a resident is more likely to say that it has gone up, and then indicate where some of it may come down. A good house is not one that never becomes wet. It is one whose wetness arrives by known routes and can be answered without panic.

The objects they admire are rarely bright. Brightness is too common to be precious. They admire a cistern lid that can be seated in darkness with one numb hand, a felt seam that does not freeze stiff, a gate that moves after years of rime, a repair so well fitted that the next generation will scarcely need to think about it. Beauty and relief are closely related.

Children learn by being given small responsibilities before they are given explanations. They carry covered jars, fetch goat hair, watch the lower pans, and memorize which signs must be passed along immediately. Their first goggles are often oversized and held on with several turns of cord. During a turning, a light child may be sent across a roof to close a gate that an adult could reach only by taking down half a pen. Such children are not considered fearless. They are considered useful, which earns a different and sometimes greater pride.

The shag-goats are partners in this education. Every animal is known individually, and an old goat’s reluctance can carry more weight than a young person’s confidence. Goats remember routes beneath ledges, object to certain kinds of glaze underfoot, and close together before some turnings with a unanimity no one has taught them. At the shallow pans they crop the charcoal film from submerged stone, leaving pale tongue and muzzle trails that briefly reveal the size of the pasture. Grazing is moved from pan to pan, partly to protect the film and partly because a pool that was suitable yesterday may be bitter, stranded, or gone today.

Almost everything a goat produces is used. Its dropping is not an inconvenience but fuel that has not yet been dried. Dung is exposed on slabs where the fierce, cold light can draw moisture out of it, then stored under cover. Milk, hair, hide, and fat circulate among households; meat usually marks an accident, an old animal, or an occasion important enough to justify the loss of everything else that animal might have given.

Glass eels are gathered with fine hide nets when contracting pans crowd them together. Some are cooked on hot stones at the communal hearth-pits; some are dried for later use. Their condition is also read as news. Eels packing densely against a seep, settling early, or abandoning a shallow rim can change the work of an entire tier. After a turning, children and adults scoop stranded eels from disappearing pans. Some are eaten. Some are carried in covered jars to deeper water, partly to keep a pool from fouling and partly because tomorrow’s abundance is not something to waste casually today.

The slatewings are neighbors rather than livestock. Their sudden white openings are watched as closely as any human signal. Households leave useful roosting spaces beneath eaves, tolerate feathers and droppings, and take only limited eggs from accessible nests. Fallen feathers and stolen goat hair find their way into human bedding as readily as into nests. The rock shrews are less welcome but nearly as unavoidable. They raid stored food, line their nests with pilfered fiber, and leave tiny caches inside walls that may not be opened for generations. When their movements abruptly cease, experienced residents look for a pale marten. Often there is nothing to see. The pens and rookeries are checked anyway.

During a turning, all these separate attentions become one crowded performance. Signs travel from roof to roof: cover water, turn felts, move animals, watch the upper walls. No horn sounds. No one stands above the town directing it. People simply do the tasks nearest them while passing warnings to those who cannot see what they see. Lids are pressed down. Jars are carried inside. Goats resist on stairs. A felt is turned too late and someone below is drenched. There are mistakes, private curses, and occasional shouted names. Urgency does not make the people graceful; practice only ensures that most of their clumsiness occurs in useful directions.

Afterward come the comparisons and arguments. A reliable seep has shifted. A new channel has stolen from an old one. Somebody’s roof has sent ceiling-rain into somebody else’s bed. Repairs begin while drops are still falling, but major alterations may wait until several people have watched the next bright interval. The Cistern rewards decisiveness during a turning and patience immediately afterward. Its inhabitants need both, and do not always possess them in the same person.

Those who go beyond the outlet often find ordinary water difficult to ignore. A stream sounds wasteful, then threatening, then merely exhausting. Footsteps strike too sharply. Uncovered brightness seems to come with unnecessary heat. Some people born in the Cistern sleep badly their first nights outside because running water sounds like work left undone. They return with an appreciation for shade, enclosed rooms, and the soft, complicated labor of their own stairs.

After dark, when the goggles come off and the lamps are kept small for their warmth, the settlement becomes audible in pieces: a goat shifting against another goat, feathers settling beneath an eave, a shrew drawing fiber through a wall, one cold drop finding the mouth of a jar. Someone reaches out of bed to test a cistern lid by touch. Down in the pans, the eels gather over the dark film. Above the roofs, a marten crosses an occupied lane without troubling it. On the high walls, the orange and black lichens persist at a pace no household can usefully measure.

The people do not mistake their town for the whole life of the Cistern. They are one crowded, ingenious part of it, dependent on pasture they cannot see clearly, warnings given by animals, warmth borrowed from bodies, and water that may spend the morning inside a wall. The hush around them is not emptiness. Nearly everything living there has learned to take up less sound than space.
