## The Listeners

At first it seems that the Height itself must be the only inhabitant, and that everything moving within it is merely a process of its body. That is almost correct. The remaining life is not a hidden ecology awaiting discovery. It consists of a few precise suggestions of ecology, scattered through the valleys and never allowed to become a web.

The most elaborate of these suggestions are the listeners.

One might stand for days at the edge of a cistern without being recognized as an animal. Folded in on itself, it resembles several pale stems that have leaned together and grown along their points of contact. Its body contains more space than tissue. When it finally unfolds, the stems become limbs, the knots between them become flexures, and a clear central chamber appears where its trunk had seemed to be.

A green cord winds through that chamber, dividing and rejoining without forming a heart. At the upper end it enters a thin hood, not quite a head, shaped like a leaf just before it learns whether to open or remain a spear. The hood is usually turned a little away from the body, giving the whole creature an air of interrupted attention.

No two listeners are anatomically identical. One may have six limbs, another eleven. One carries a row of dark lenses beneath an unbroken membrane; another has no lenses, only a ring of shallow depressions around its hood. Several possess a fine seam resembling a mouth, though there is no cavity behind it. These are not injuries or deformities. The tissue surrounding each unrealized feature is smooth, exact, and complete. It is the next step that is absent.

Their skin is warm and translucent. Water moves visibly inside them, but not in pulses. It circulates in a slow, continuous direction, as though each listener were a small stretch of the same plumbing that serves the rim. Held against the ground, their many-lobed feet spread softly and take weight without gripping. When they lift, clear water rises briefly around them. No footprint remains.

A listener can move with decision. It may cross an entire marsh, stepping through the shining water without disturbing its level, and climb a terrace by flowingly rearranging limbs that seemed too long for climbing. Yet the movement never resolves into an intelligible journey. It does not lead toward food, shade, shelter, or company. The creature may stop halfway across an open field with one foot still raised. It may remain in that posture until the observer abandons the expectation that a raised foot must eventually come down.

Nothing in its stillness suggests fatigue. Nothing in its movement suggests urgency.

Sound is the one thing to which it unmistakably responds. The deep vibration of the outer buttresses passes through its limbs and causes the clear chamber to contract almost imperceptibly. A high leaf-tone sets the hood trembling. Beneath an unfinished arch, a listener may turn until its body is aligned with the hollow resonance, then produce a faint note of its own.

For a while, the note seems communicative. It rises through two or three pitches and holds the last with extraordinary purity. But it does not vary when another listener is present. It does not elicit an answer from the roots, the cups, or the far conduits. It is not a summons, a warning, or a song whose meaning has been lost. The listener is simply another hollow form through which the Height can sound.

The other animals are not its young, its mates, or its prey.

The colourless swimmer does not develop into anything with limbs. Its folded body contains no dormant listener, no compressed wings, no additional organs awaiting space. It turns, fastens itself to a wall, and turns again. The soft many-legged creature within a hollow stem is not sheltering there, either. Its back bears ridges corresponding exactly to the ridges inside the stem; when it flexes, water passes around it without friction. There need never have been an opening through which it could have entered. It is among the forms the stem has grown.

Swimmer, stem-creature, and listener can be arranged by an observer into a series of increasing complexity. The Height itself appears to recognize no such order. They are not stages. They are three separate approaches to the same unrealized condition: an animal that might belong here.

An ordinary animal would make belonging consequential. It would prefer one cistern to another, wear a route into the green pavement, carry matter from pool to pool. It would hunger, select, avoid, remember. Even its death would alter the place. The listeners do none of this. Nothing passes through them as nourishment. They produce no surplus and leave no remains. Their clear internal fluid does not cloud with age. A route travelled many times does not become a path, because neither the ground nor the traveller retains it.

On the rare occasion when two listeners occupy the same field, nothing social necessarily follows. They may stand close enough for their chambers to hold a common vibration. They may also pass at a distance without turning. There is no courtship in the first case and no avoidance in the second. Proximity does not become company. Separation does not become loss. When they are no longer near one another, the event has not become a meeting that either can recall.

This does not establish that they are unconscious.

A listener may possess an immediate and intricate awareness: the pressure of warm water against its feet, the green light coming through its own tissues, the distinct notes arriving from arch, cistern, and buttress. Its hood turns too precisely for perception to be ruled out. What seems absent is autobiography. One sensation does not join another to become familiarity. Nothing is anticipated because it happened before. There is experience, perhaps, but no accumulating someone to whom the experiences belong.

For that reason, the listeners are not as pitiable as they first appear. Their sealed eyes do not signify blindness endured through ages. Their mouthless seams are not starvation. Their incomplete limbs are not a promise of pain. They are not waiting to be born, and the Height is not cruelly withholding birth from them. Waiting would require the expectation of a different future.

Nor are they custodians. They do not tend the growth-fronts, clear the conduits, or regulate the cisterns. No arch continues after one has touched it. No basin fills more evenly because a listener has stood beneath its lip. The great structures were not built for them, and the hollow forms they occupy are not dwellings. The Height has no hidden people, no departed gardeners, no makers reduced to these last strange survivors.

If anything lives here in the fullest sense, it is still the continuous organism of the place. But it is not a central mind looking out through animal bodies. That would imply a kind of mastery the landscape nowhere displays. The listeners seem less like its servants than like brief local assumptions of animal posture: here, a body that might swim; there, a body that might creep; here, on the open terrace, a body that might hear.

One stands now beneath a half-closed vault of ivory stem. Its hood is lifted toward the exposed ribs. Water slides from the clear chamber and returns to the film on the ground without making the creature lighter. For an instant its whole form holds the low note of the outer buttresses, and the empty valley around it seems less like a landscape than the interior of an enormous ear.

The note continues. The listener neither understands it nor fails to understand it. Nothing comes in answer, and nothing is disappointed.

It is not waiting for the next sound. That, more than its translucent flesh or rootlike feet, is what makes it native.
