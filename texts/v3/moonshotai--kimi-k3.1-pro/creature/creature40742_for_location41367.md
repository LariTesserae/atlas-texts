Most of the inhabitants never see the court.

For a termite worker, the world begins at the reach of its antennae. It is known through pressure, moisture, vibration, and the odors left by other bodies. A passage is safe when its air is humid and still; it is damaged when dry air enters, or when light appears where no light should be. Fresh clay has a different feel from clay that has dried and hardened. A root is an obstacle, a support, or a boundary, depending on where the passage needs to go. The six turrets above may form a town to an outside observer, but no single worker needs to comprehend them. Its town is the next length of gallery, the pellet it is carrying, the opening that must be closed.

Some workers remain among the nurseries and gardens. Others travel through covered runways to gather plant material, returning along routes that are continually inspected and repaired. Still others rebuild. At a breach, one termite deposits mud and withdraws; another adds to it; a third presses the new material into the old. The repair has no ceremony and no moment of completion. It simply becomes sufficiently wall-like to be treated as wall.

The soldiers live closer to the possibility of rupture. Their darker, heavier heads and jaws make their purpose more legible than that of the workers, but they are not the town’s rulers. They are one of its vulnerabilities answered in flesh. When ants test an opening, soldiers may gather near it while workers thicken the passage behind them or seal it altogether. The colony’s response is partly defensive behavior and partly architecture. Often the safest victory is a route that no longer exists.

Deep below, where temperature and humidity change least, the queen occupies a chamber built around tending. Her body is more continuous with the colony’s future than with its daily movement. Eggs are carried away; nymphs are fed and groomed; the young pass into lives that may never bring them back to the royal chamber. The king remains part of this hidden continuity. Yet neither monarch directs the building. Their fertility is not government. No plan for a turret passes outward from them, and no worker waits for instructions. The town’s intelligence is dispersed among thousands of small responses to damage, crowding, scent, heat, and wet clay.

The brood includes more than replacements. When the colony is mature and the season favors it, some individuals develop toward a winged life. Their future is almost unimaginably different from that of the workers around them: light, air, flight, exposure. Nearly all who eventually leave will die beyond the nest. A very few may survive long enough to found another chamber elsewhere. The hollow thus contains not only the inhabitants of one town, but the unrealized beginnings of others.

Below the termites’ visible works lives the crop on which their crowdedness depends. The fungus gardens are pale because light has never been required of them. They spread through folded, moist combs, receiving plant fiber, being pruned and replenished, converting material that would otherwise remain stubbornly woody. The fungus is not merely stored food. It is another inhabitant, with its own requirements and vulnerabilities. Too much moisture, too little ventilation, contamination, neglect—any of these can alter a garden. The termites feed and tend it; the fungus makes their plant harvest more usable. Their partnership is old enough that neither life can be understood cleanly without the other.

Smaller lives are involved as well: microorganisms in garden tissue and termite bodies, in rotting wood and wet clay. They will never appear as a visible gathering. Their presence is registered as transformation—fiber softening, matter decaying, nutrients passing from one body or chamber into another. Even the richest refuse room is not simply full of waste. It is a place where other appetites are already at work.

The mud itself participates in this arrangement. It is not alive, but neither is it separate from life. It holds water that the workers cannot afford to lose. It slows the exchange of heat. Its passages admit and direct air. A plugged vent changes the climate of rooms that no builder may visit directly. A lengthened chimney alters the nest’s breathing after sunset. Collapsed walls are wetted, gathered, and returned to use. The colony repairs without sentiment; nothing needs to be preserved in its original form in order to remain part of the town.

Above and through all this lives the fig.

It is so large that the hollow can seem like a minor event beneath it, but the fig is resident rather than scenery. Its leaves take the rain first. Its bark holds the dawn condensation. Its roots support galleries, divert them, compress them, and occasionally threaten to break them. Each year it thickens slowly against structures whose builders work in minutes. The fig has no use for the termites’ plan, but much of their plan has been made within the consequences of its growth. It provides shelter without being stable, foundation without being still.

The ferns occupy a quicker, more fragile scale. Each one intercepts droplets and makes a small climate beneath its fronds. Moss crowds only where water arrives with sufficient regularity, forming cushions in which the distinction between wet surface and living body nearly disappears. Seedlings attempt the open clay and are repeatedly removed. Their failure is part of why the court remains bare. The damp film along one wall is alive too, though its inhabitants are visible mainly as a change in sheen.

That bareness can be misleading. The court is not the town’s meeting place. It is a region of exposure between entrances, a working surface after rain, a margin across which anything moving may be noticed. The termites’ social life happens in contact and darkness. Their crowds do not need to assemble above ground.

The smaller animals use the hollow according to their own necessities. Springtails gather where rotting bark keeps both food and shelter close together. Disturb their cover and their congregation becomes a brief scattering of leaps, then apparently nothing. After rain, the velvet mite crosses the clay with a brilliance that seems almost disproportionate to the hollow’s subdued colors. It is solitary and temporary, a vivid interval rather than a fixture.

The spider has made use of abandoned infrastructure. To it, the ventilation shaft is an opening of suitable dimensions; to the insects that touch its silk, the distinction between natural crevice and termite workmanship has no importance. Its web is the hollow’s most delicate construction and the only one maintained principally by tension. Rain and struggling prey damage it. The spider repairs what can be repaired and waits again.

Beetles work the margins of refuse and decay, investigating what the colony has put aside. The centipede beneath the fern roots is a purer concentration of predation: segmented, patient, capable of sudden speed. The frog belongs to the damp rim rather than to the nest. Motionless, it takes on the appearance of wet clay; when it feeds, its stillness is revealed as an economy rather than an absence of life. Even its small change of position after rain alters the dangers along one side of the court.

The ants bring another organized society into this one. A scout testing a runway is not merely an individual wandering. It is the first narrow extension of a different multitude. The termites answer with soldiers, pellets, closed passages, and abandonment. Their conflict often leaves no dramatic evidence—only a wall thicker than it was, or a route that ends abruptly in fresh mud.

The lizard is not an inhabitant in the same sense. It arrives from a larger scale of movement, and for a moment its head at the rim becomes the hollow’s most important fact. Surface life vanishes. Then it withdraws, and the smaller durations resume.

Rain makes a kind of census, because every inhabitant responds to it differently. The fig interrupts and redistributes it. Ferns hold it briefly and let it fall. Moss takes it into its cushions. The frog shifts toward a better wetness; the mite appears; the spider finds struggling insects at the edges of pools. Ants move their activity upward. Termites close openings, then use the softened material to build. Underground, the gardens experience the shower as altered humidity and temperature long after the court has ceased to shine with water.

No one experiences the whole event. There are only drops on bark, pressure in a gallery, silk under strain, a pool deepening beside a leg, clay becoming workable again.

At night the town does not settle into a single sleep. Tending continues. Gardens are reworked. Air moves differently through the shafts. A dark patch on a turret records a decision made after the light failed. By morning there may be no more visible animals than before, but the hollow has changed by a few pellets, a sealed opening, an arch extended far enough to matter.

What lives there is therefore not simply a list of species sharing shelter. It is a set of different durations occupying the same moisture: the queen’s slow continuity, a worker’s brief journeys, fungus digesting fiber in darkness, a spider renewing silk, a frog waiting beside the rim, and the fig root thickening almost imperceptibly through them all.

The termites make it a town. The other lives make it impossible to say exactly where the town ends.
