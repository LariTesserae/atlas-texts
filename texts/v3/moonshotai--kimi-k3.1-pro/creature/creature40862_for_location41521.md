Life in the Black Notch is specialized in interruption.

Almost everything there can become dry, cold, and apparently dead without quite dying. On exposed faces, the lichens harden into scabs. Microbial films in the deeper joints shrink to dark dust. Moss cushions contract until their crowded shoots feel like bristles. Then cloud enters the cut, and the community changes without anything so simple as a general awakening. A film becomes slippery. A lichen softens and darkens. Water passes from leaf to leaf through a moss cushion, and patches of its surface resume photosynthesis while the interior remains dormant. Each inhabitant lives according to the wet hours of its own small surface.

The first life on a fresh fracture is usually too slight to appear in lightning. In joints fine enough to retain moisture after the open stone has dried, dark films of bacteria and cyanobacteria spread over the andesite. When wet, some are gelatinous and almost black; when dry, they leave only a discoloration that might be mistaken for a mineral stain. They bind the finest ash, and some take nitrogen from the air. Their growth adds almost nothing that could be called soil, but their deaths enrich the dust caught beside them. A later spore, landing in such a crevice, finds a little more nutrient and a little less exposure than the bare rock would have offered.

Lichens occupy the harsher tenure. High on the walls and along wind-combed ribs, they form black, gray, and dull olive crusts, sometimes no thicker than the grain of the stone. They endure wetting, freezing, drying, and intense clear-sky radiation in quick succession. Their acids loosen minute flakes from the rock; their edges catch ash. Most of this work is undone by falling fragments of the surface itself. The lichens do not steadily convert the wall into a garden. They merely make scattered places where a garden can, by chance, begin.

Below them, the cushion mosses are the principal inhabitants and the principal makers of habitat. A cushion is not simply moss spread over soil. Much of its bulk is the moss’s own dead past. Living shoots crowd the surface, their narrow leaves holding droplets and drawing water along the stems. Beneath them stand brown, half-decayed generations; deeper still lies a compressed black layer, acidic and slow to rot. Ash settles among the shoots and is incorporated as the surface rises. The cushion thus grows upward on material it has caught and downward into material it has made.

Different parts of a single cushion can be living under different conditions. Its exposed crest may dry while the shaded base remains wet. A thin skin may be bright and active above an interior that has been dead for years. Water sometimes travels around the outside rather than through the center, following old stems and root channels until it emerges unexpectedly at a lower lip. The apparent plumbing of the Notch owes much to these buried differences. None of it is coordinated.

Clubmosses lace the cushions together. Their wiry stems cross from one moss body to another and root wherever a node remains damp long enough. On stable ledges they form a loose net over the entire surface; on overhangs they descend in cords, mixed with rhizoids, sedge roots, and the tougher remains of older growth. Some of the hanging “cables” are therefore less individual plants than bindings among several kinds of plant. When a stem is broken, a rooted portion may continue independently. What appears to be a colony of many small plants can be one repeatedly fragmented individual, and what appears to be one continuous mat can contain hundreds of unrelated arrivals.

The waxy rosettes live where the moss cover is thinner or where a root seam gives them a firmer attachment. Their stiff leaves account for much of the dry scraping heard in a gust. A pale bloom on the leaf surface limits water loss, while the folded arrangement of the rosette catches fog droplets and directs some of the overflow toward its center. Dead leaves remain for a time below the living ones, forming a close, fibrous skirt. Most rosettes increase locally by offsets. When they flower, the flowers are small and self-fertile; their dry capsules open to the wind rather than offering anything to a visitor. There are no bright rewards for pollinators and no defenses shaped by grazing teeth. The dangers here are desiccation, abrasion, burial, and loss of attachment.

The tallest growth belongs to the seams where volcanic warmth makes the root zone freeze less often. Even there, “warm” means only slightly less cold. Sedges creep through these fissures by rhizomes, often retaining dead leaves long after the living shoots have withdrawn. A flowering stem rising a handspan above the moss is conspicuous. Their pollen is released into air that is already carrying fern spores, clubmoss spores, ash, and grit. Nearly all of it is wasted. Local spread through the rhizomes is usually more dependable than the establishment of a seedling.

The ferns are more exacting, though not in the obvious way. Their spores can settle abundantly on ledges where no fern will ever grow. Before a familiar frond can appear, a spore must produce a small, flattened sexual generation on a surface that remains damp long enough for fertilization. That stage is easily overlooked: a green scale in a sheltered recess, vulnerable to a single thorough drying. The presence of ferns therefore records more than a suitable ledge. It records a particular history of moisture—long enough, uninterrupted enough, and often recent enough to have escaped the next collapse.

Beneath all of them, fungi and bacteria work without producing much visible evidence. Fungal threads ramify through the darker layers, and bacterial activity continues in wet pockets, but cold keeps decay incomplete. A dead leaf may remain recognizable long after it has been buried. The accumulating substrate is consequently as much an archive as a soil: ash bands, compressed stems, fragments of older cushions, and grains freshly broken from the wall. Nutrients are not rapidly returned to the living surface. Many remain locked away until water carries the layer downward or the entire shelf falls.

With no browsing, the plants are not kept open by being eaten back. They crowd, overtop, and bury one another. Moss creeps across the edge of a rosette; clubmoss shades a crust; sedge roots occupy a seam that might otherwise have held a fern prothallus. Yet the ledges do not sort themselves into a stable, predictable arrangement. Two shelves only a short distance apart can support markedly different gardens because different propagules happened to lodge there, or because one lost its early colonists in a fall. Conditions set the limits. Chance supplies much of the history within them.

Wind is the community’s pollinator and disperser, but also its great waster. It lifts spores from fertile tips and then throws most of them against wet stone, carries them out through the lower crack, or deposits them on the dry slopes above. Water transports heavier fragments downward. Within the Notch, reproduction by wreckage can be more successful than reproduction by seed or spore. A piece of saturated clubmoss, a living strip torn from a cushion, or a rosette offset wedged into fresh grit may already possess the tissues it needs to resume growth. It does not have to begin as dust.

That matters because every mature garden is becoming heavier. Its living surface intercepts more cloud water; its dead interior retains more fine sediment. Roots and stems bind the mass while adding to the load it must bear. Eventually a joint opens, a lip breaks, or saturated material loses its hold. Growth does not cause every failure, but it ensures that many failures carry away more than rock.

The fall is not always immediately fatal to everything carried with it. A cushion landing upright in shade may persist for a while. Crushed rosettes can leave viable offsets. Clubmoss fragments may root where the next temporary rill spreads fine sediment. More often, the mat is broken apart, buried by scree, washed into a basin, or left on a floor that dries too quickly. Its remains contribute to the black sand and to the next thin layer of substrate. On the wall above, the new scar begins again with wet mineral, dust, and films too small to see.

Even the floor’s brief pools support only life able to tolerate their disappearance: microbial films, dormant propagules, and fragments that can use a few wet hours without depending on permanent water. There is no stable aquatic community waiting for the rill to return. A flood may carry inhabitants from several ledges together, mix them in one basin, and leave them stranded under entirely different conditions.

The Notch’s inhabitants are thus difficult to count as individuals and impossible to understand as a fixed assembly. A ledge may contain a newly attached spore, a crust nearing the end of its substrate, a fern produced by one rare wet interval, and a moss mat already bulging toward failure. Their lives overlap without becoming synchronous.

All of them came, ultimately, as arrivals on the wind. Most of what now leaves the cleft will die on the mountain outside it. For the life circulating within, the Black Notch is not merely a sheltered patch in a larger landscape. It is nearly the whole habitable world: not rock alone, nor water alone, but their brief overlap with shelter and something to hold on to.
