People live in Serein, though a visitor could spend a first evening there and be unsure how many. They are behind wet glass, in vaulted workshops, or on the far side of a courtyard where only a moving fern betrays a passage. The basin-born are not a separate kind of humanity; centuries of cable traffic have brought too many lowland families for that. What residents share is mostly learned. They take stairs with a short, economical stride. They stand aside without thinking when someone carries a valve or a length of pipe. They notice an altered drip before they notice a changed face. A Sereinan woken by silence may lie still and listen for a pump that has stopped. One woken by running water listens for where it should not be.

Their clothing is made for damp rather than for downpour: short layered jackets, close cuffs, quilted plant fiber against the skin, imported wool or waxed cloth outside. Long hems are avoided because they wick water from steps. Work shoes have thin enough soles to feel a machine’s vibration through a floor, and most households keep a second pair drying near a heat pipe. In the blue-green shade of the lower lanes, everyone can look similarly dark and subdued. Indoors, color appears in tile buttons, mended cuffs, woven belts, and the bright ochre or red handles of personal tools.

Visitors often call the people reserved. This is partly breath and partly acoustics. Conversation on an open stair is conducted in short pieces, with pauses at the landings. In a warm kitchen, however, Sereinans talk readily and interrupt one another shamelessly. Their humor is particular and often concerns maintenance: the pipe installed perfectly according to a drawing of the wrong house, the conscientious apprentice who cleaned a vane until it stopped catching fog, the respectable councilor discovered to have been watering a private fern from a public overflow. Their ordinary speech distinguishes many kinds of wetness. There is water that beads and can be collected, water that creeps under mortar, water lost as vapor, and water that merely darkens stone. To call all of these a leak is considered imprecise.

Every resident belongs to a household and usually to a trade, but the more consequential affiliation is to a catchment. A catchment may comprise twenty roofs, several gardens, a stretch of channel, and a share in one or more cisterns. Its members need not be related or even friendly. They are the people who can reasonably knock on one another’s doors before dawn because a root mat has lifted or an overflow has begun to back up. An address often includes an outfall as well as a lane: not only where a dwelling stands, but where its water goes.

Gauge-keepers hold the most trusted public positions. They read reservoir levels, witness transfers between catchments, and maintain records going back through dry intervals no living person remembers. Their authority is narrow but real. A household may argue with a gardener about a tree and with a mechanic about a pump, but falsifying a gauge is treated as an offense against people who have not yet been born. Concealing damage to a common main is worse, in the public mind, than taking an extra bucket.

This does not make Serein equal. Wealth is visible less in height or ornament than in dryness and reserve. A prosperous household has inner rooms that do not sweat, a reliable heat loop, sealed cupboards, and enough cistern share to survive a poor season without selling tools. Poorer rooms tend to lie near overflow lanes or below older roofs. Their inhabitants may hear a great deal of water passing beneath the floor and have very little discretion over its use. In dry years, arguments over seepage become arguments over ancestry, old repairs, and who was once allowed to connect to whose pipe. The town’s patience is genuine, but it is not always gentle.

Most work is a mixture of tending and reconstruction. Vane-cleaners climb above the roofs and sometimes above the cloud ceiling, returning with wet shoulders and faces reddened by a sunlight the lower streets have not seen. Pump-listeners diagnose worn bearings and obstructed intakes by touch, sound, and changes in vibration. Root-cutters work with hooks, saws, and long flexible probes; the good ones can identify a plant from a brown fragment found in a valve. Pattern-keepers preserve drawings, wooden templates, broken castings, and specimens of materials whose composition is uncertain. A workshop may keep a failed gasket for fifty years because it is the only evidence of how one obsolete joint was meant to fit.

Gardening is part of the same technical world. A competent gardener can read a pressure gauge, and a competent pipefitter knows which roots will follow a warm condensation line. People change trades without changing this basic knowledge. Apprentices commonly serve in both a workshop and a catchment garden, if only to learn that a neat engineering solution can become a thicket and that a beautiful thicket can quietly dismantle a wall.

Domestic time follows fog, gate openings, and cable arrivals more closely than it follows any public clock. A good fog morning brings an understated increase in activity. Channels are inspected, reservoirs measured, work postponed during scarcity is taken down from shelves, and someone is sent to see whether a particular seam on the outer saddle has turned green. When water is plentiful, Serein becomes brighter and more sociable. Workshop windows remain lit later. Heated washrooms stay open. Gardens receive their full allowance, and meals are cooked with less anxious attention to fuel and water.

During long dry intervals, the town contracts without emptying. Freight is deferred. Lower lamps are dimmed. People visit one another less often in the deep courts, where sounds no longer have running water to cover them, and private conversations become easier to overhear. Tempers shorten. Yet this is also when old knowledge becomes valuable: which roof still yields a little dew, which pump can be run slowly without damage, which garden can be cut back and which will die if pruned. Elders are respected less for general wisdom than for remembering where a pipe ran before a particular wall existed.

Houses are arranged around the preservation of dry, warm interiors. Beds and books are kept away from outer walls. Clothes dry on communal racks near engine halls. Kitchens use heavy sealed pots, partly to conserve water and partly because boiling at that altitude is a cooler and less useful event than lowlanders expect. Public ovens and steam tables make use of workshop heat. Grain, salt, wool, and much new metal come up by cable; Serein sends down valves, insulators, fine wire, condenser fittings, and people capable of repairing machines no lowland manufacturer wishes to support.

The gardens supply tubers, beans, dark cooking leaves, mushrooms from warm cellars, and several sharp or bitter flavors that lowland visitors initially mistake for mistakes. Knotwood fruit, small and hard, is cooked into a sour preserve. A common meal is soft, dense, and fermented at the edges, with imported oil used sparingly. Offering a guest water is ordinary hospitality. Offering a dry seat beside a heat pipe is a clearer sign of esteem.

Children learn the public gauge marks alongside their letters. They are taught the overflow routes before they are allowed to roam far, and they know that putting a dam across a storm channel is not an amusing experiment. One permitted game is to race folded bark boats during an authorized release, retrieving them before they reach the grates. At a certain age, many children spend a season working in another catchment. This is meant to teach that water does not behave identically everywhere in Serein, though it also gives adolescents a useful period away from their relatives.

Some young people leave on the cable, impatient with a place where even celebration waits for a reservoir reading. Others return with lowland tools, unfamiliar seeds, new mathematical methods, or a determination to replace everything crooked. Serein needs such people, even when it exasperates them. Without periodic argument, maintenance can become mere repetition.

When a reservoir passes an old surplus mark, a catchment may hold what residents call a bright night. Lamps are left burning in the court, food comes from the common ovens, and musicians play plucked wire, low drums, and small instruments that do not demand too much breath. These are not solemn water festivals. People dance stiffly in their heavy clothes, gossip about the gauge-keeper, and eat too much preserved fruit. By morning, tools have usually been returned to their hooks.

Most of Serein’s visible inhabitants, however, are not human.

The knotwoods are the largest of them. In sheltered courts their trunks lean and braid around one another, seeking warmth leaking through masonry as much as light. Their thick leaves hold condensed fog along the margins, then release it in heavy, separate drops. A knotwood in an exposed saddle may remain a knee-high shrub for decades; the same species beside an engine hall can eventually spread above two roofs. Its roots are persistent investigators of nutrient-rich leakage. Gardeners value the tree for shelter, leaf catch, and fruit. Engineers regard an old knotwood with the caution due to a slow crowbar.

What residents call stair fern includes several plants with similar habits. Their fronds tighten in dry weather and open after wetting so quickly that they seem to be responding to being watched. They establish themselves in wall joints, gutter boxes, and the neglected corners of overflow stairs. Some are tolerated because they trap fine soil and delay runoff. Others are removed wherever their rhizomes approach a joint seal. The distinction is practical rather than botanical and changes from court to court.

Deep moss cushions are read almost as instruments. One common species turns from dusty olive to nearly black when saturated, revealing seepage on an outer wall before the interior plaster shows damage. Another remains springy through long dry periods and is used to pack certain garden beds, though never where its retained moisture would sit against structural mortar. Moss is transplanted, scraped away, dried, and sometimes stolen. A fine cushion on an old arch may represent thirty years of growth and one generation of disagreement about whether the arch is being protected or consumed.

On the open saddles, the coppery and olive cushions have different priorities. They withstand wind, intense cold, and occasional direct sun by remaining small and closely packed. Many spend much of the year apparently lifeless. A leaking pipe changes their world. Seeds and spores arrive from the basin on boots and freight, and a narrow community springs up along the moisture: first dark films, then moss, then a thread of fern or flowering green. These seam gardens are never entirely wild. Their extent records pressure, maintenance, and the length of time since anyone inspected the line.

The pale snails live most obviously on wet tile. Their shells are thin and almost translucent, and in dry weather they seal themselves into crevices with a film that hardens at the surface. After fog they emerge to graze on the nearly invisible growth covering stone and glaze. Their trails show where condensation has persisted longest. Children are taught to step around them, less from sentiment than because crushed snail is slippery on a high stair. Roof workers sometimes look for fresh trails before deciding which section of tile needs attention.

Ash moths have a more complicated place in the town. Their larvae live unobtrusively in deep leaf mold, especially where engine warmth keeps a bed from freezing. The adults gather on warm iron and around greenish lamps, opening and closing their wings as if testing the air. They also visit the small pale flowers that knotwoods and certain court vines open at night. In years when moths are numerous, fruit set is often better.

Engine-keepers and gardeners therefore have an ongoing disagreement about lamps and vent screens. Too much light near a dynamo room draws moths into machinery; too little near a garden may leave flowers unvisited. Fine gauze, shaded glass, and the placement of a single warm bed can become matters of extended negotiation. Nobody has designed a solution that satisfies both trades for more than a few seasons.

The gliders belong to the rim. Their nesting ledges are difficult to reach, and they rarely remain in the basin after hunting. When one crosses overhead, its body seems small and incidental between the broad wings. People look up, conversations pause, and moths vanish into fern. The appearance of a glider is not treated as an omen, though it often means that warm air is rising strongly enough to carry moths above the roofs. Children sometimes imitate the shadow with outstretched arms. Adults generally wait until the shadow has passed before doing so.

Even the covered reservoirs contain a few inhabitants. In the oldest, least disturbed cisterns there are colorless water-fleas and fine, pale worms living on organic films. They are filtered from machinery intakes and seldom seen by anyone but keepers. Their presence is not automatically welcomed or eradicated. A sudden change in them can be more informative than a clear gauge: new contamination, a broken flow pattern, or water standing where it once circulated.

The oldest condenser towers support stranger communities. Their tenders are mechanics, climbers, and gardeners in varying proportions. They tension fasteners, clear fern growth from braces, inspect copper vanes, and keep logs that may continue for centuries. The entries begin with dates, wind directions, and water yields, then often acquire more personal observations: taking east fog before dawn; dry after repair; giving again in still air. Engineers may dislike such language, but they rarely discard the books.

Some of the dark patina is certainly alive. Under magnification it contains black yeasts, mineral needles, bacterial films, and fine lichen-like growth, all bound into old corrosion and surface fractures. None of these organisms alone explains the towers’ behavior. The living layer may alter how droplets form and release. It may merely indicate some subtler combination of age, thermal movement, geometry, and wear. Transplanted patima dries, flakes, or becomes an ordinary stain. New towers polished to imitate old ones remain new.

The tenders do not agree on what this means, and the most experienced are often the least willing to settle the question. Saying that a tower must learn its weather is not, for them, an alternative to maintenance. It is an instruction to keep observing something whose relevant history may be longer than a human life. There are practical objections to preserving every inefficient structure, and younger engineers make them forcefully. Old towers consume labor, metal, and safe anchorage. Sometimes they are right. Sometimes a replacement stands dry beside the crooked frame it was meant to supersede, and the argument enters another generation.

At dusk, these different lives become briefly legible together. A gauge-keeper crosses a court with the day’s figures. A root-cutter leaves a basket of wet fragments beside a workshop door for identification. Someone on an upper saddle closes a tool chest while the first fog reaches the lower lanes. Snails are out on the tile. Moths begin to gather at the warm vents, and a gardener checks that the gauze is secure. The upper gates open; pipes shiver; the people nearest them feel the change through their shoes before the lamps strengthen.

Nothing in this is perfectly harmonious. Roots break pipes. Reservoirs favor some streets over others. Gliders eat the pollinators, gardens shelter pests, and human beings hoard, misread records, and grow fond of obsolete machines. But all of them have become involved in one another’s survival. The people maintain the condensers; the condensers wet the gardens; the gardens alter the runoff; the runoff determines which walls fail and which courts remain habitable. Around and through this arrangement live the smaller, quieter communities that no charter mentions.

Only the people call the place Serein. The rest inhabit it without needing a name, each keeping its own pace within the town’s long, leaking breath.
