What lives in the Low Dark is vegetal, but “vegetation” is too passive a word. Nothing there simply occupies a habitat. The living things are the habitat: floor, canopy, plumbing, power supply, and means of repair. The galleries shelter no separate population. They are spaces within the population, much as the inside of a sponge is still part of the sponge.

A census would quickly become meaningless. One could count trunks, leaves, valves, and reclaimer cups without learning how many organisms had been counted. A single trunk may contain tissues with several distinct internal chemistries. Roots from neighboring bodies may fuse into one conduit while remaining physiologically separate on either side of it. Elsewhere, one self-recognizing chemistry may extend through many trunks, across whole banks of pumps, and into condenser leaves some distance away. At shared junctions, double membranes admit water and selected solutes while refusing other substances. The border of an organism is therefore not always bark. Sometimes it is a membrane’s selectivity.

The smallest inhabitants may be the living nodules in the pools, each a thin body of exchanging tissue organized around a mineral core. The largest may have no compact body at all. It may be a pressure domain, a dispersed electrical lineage, or a chemistry persistent enough to recognize itself wherever it occurs. The Low Dark may contain many such beings in intimate physiological association. It may also contain one organism whose organs have retained the ability to behave like separate species. Its own boundaries do not settle the question.

All of them ultimately live from a difference: warm crust below, slightly cooler transpiring surfaces above. Thermoelectric tissues draw current from that difference. Electrode and electrolytic organs use the current to move ions, separate minerals, and sustain the chemistry by which carbon and nutrients enter sap. Other tissues receive water, current, or sap through the common network. There is no food chain climbing from plants to grazers and then to predators. Energy enters through the thermal gradient; materials travel around a closed loop. Nothing hunts, and nothing is shaped by the need to escape being hunted.

The first life beneath a footstep is the mat, which is not a passive tangle but a densely responsive skin. Its finest rootlets pack together into pile, felt, or springy enamel-like surfaces. Compression changes their turgor and opens paths through which expressed water can be taken up again. Load is distributed through lignin ribs rather than resisted by a single stem. At submerged fractures, transparent root curtains press close to the young stone, exchanging minerals across surfaces so thin that rock, root, and water seem to be participating in the same process. Even the apparently loose nodules are connected at their bases. Nothing is merely lying there.

Above this, the condenser plants live largely by surface and posture. Their leaves do not turn toward light or arrange themselves to outshade neighbors. Their angles govern wetness: where fog will settle, where a bead will remain attached while it grows, and at what size its weight will carry it into a groove. The black cuticle is part of a conductive, exchange-active surface, not a display color. The paler underside is crowded with vessels and ridges. In the highest chambers, fine filaments comb moisture from air that never quite becomes visible fog. A condenser’s slight coolness is not an accident of exposure. It is part of its work.

The pumping plants have the most conspicuous rhythms, but rhythm should not be mistaken for behavior. A pump begins as a seed-like node in the mat. Its early development is less like a shoot reaching upward than like a series of enclosed spaces coming into existence. A basal cavity forms; another cavity differentiates beyond it; the tissue between them becomes a valve that can flex in one direction and seal in the other. Only later do the outer chambers swell and the trunk acquire its industrial appearance. The sequence is embryology, not assembly.

In a mature pumping flat, contraction passes from chamber to chamber because the shape and pressure of each body dispose the next to follow. Flexible vascular bands keep banks of compression organs in phase. Piston rods slide within their own sheaths, and toroids turn in sockets that continually renew their bearing surfaces. Wear does not accumulate into damage, and repair does not require the organ to stop. A valve’s soft tick is not communication. The hum conducted through the larger roots is not a chorus. They are incidental sounds made by tissue maintaining pressure.

The galvanic thickets are quieter and more internally divided. Conductive bundles run beside tissues that must remain electrically separate, insulated by layers of dense lignin and nonconductive sap. Some leaves serve mainly as exchange membranes; others hold electrode surfaces against mineral-rich water. Current stays within vessels and chambers. There are no sparks in the open air and no luminous discharge. The only external signs are the faint tingle in a root bundle, the metallic taste of the water, and the precise distribution of deposited and withdrawn minerals.

In the fabricating provinces, life produces forms without ever producing objects. A hollow stem extrudes wet cellulose, but the fibre does not fall free as thread. Combed fronds receive and align it while it is still continuous with living tissue. A press consolidates a sheet directly into rootstock, or draws several fibres into a cable that is already connected at both ends. There is no moment when a finished product lies unused on the ground. A cable is not something the plant has made in addition to itself. It is a new part of the plant’s connections.

The reclaimer cups complete the other half of existence. Their enzyme solutions are clear and warm, and their beds have none of the disorder associated with rot. Old tissue is not left to collapse and then scavenged. Changes in its surface chemistry make it available to reclamation while its conduits are still attached. Walls soften inward; fibres release; mineral fractions enter solution. Sap, gas, and dissolved salts are taken away as quickly as they appear. Death, where that word applies at all, is not followed by a carcass. It is conducted as disassembly.

This gives the inhabitants an unusual relation to birth and age. A new pump node does not found a colony or claim unoccupied ground. A young condenser leaf does not represent a generation advancing beyond its parents. New organs arise within the capacity of existing circulation, while equivalent tissue is being taken apart elsewhere. There are young cells, fresh root tips, and recently formed valves, but no nursery province and no spreading frontier. A trunk that appears ancient is ancient primarily in organization. Its substance may have been replaced many times without interrupting the pattern that makes it that trunk.

In the inward root-ocean, even those patterns become difficult to assign to compact bodies. The buoyant upper mat carries dense vegetation; below it, fine filaments descend through warm black water. Some conduct between widely separated organs. Others form local exchange fields only a short distance across. Some help maintain tension and buoyancy, while transparent tissues near the crust continue the slower work of mineral exchange. Nothing swims among them. There are no plankton clouds, larvae, or buried eggs. The water seems clean because almost everything in it is either passing through a conduit or belongs to a living filament.

None of these inhabitants experiences darkness as deprivation. They have no eyes, no luminous signals, and no surfaces made to be seen. Their relevant world consists of turgor, strain, temperature, voltage, humidity, and dissolved chemistry. A condenser responds to the wet movement of air. A root follows a thermal or ionic gradient. A pump’s synchrony is enforced by shared pressure. Signals pass between bodies as changes in potential, solute concentration, and hydraulic state. Almost everything that one organism needs to “say” to another can be said without sound.

The coordination requires no central mind. No province commands another, and no intelligence sits behind the flywheels. If the network possesses memory, that memory is anatomical: the diameter of a conduit, the spacing of valves, the route taken by a recurring pressure wave, the chemistry by which one tissue accepts or refuses another. It is a memory of how to continue, not a record of events.

The most persistent inhabitants are therefore not cells or even trunks, but configurations—a circulation route, a self-recognizing chemistry, a balanced relation between production and reclamation. Molecules pass through them. Tissues are made and unmade within them. Nothing is born into an unoccupied future, and nothing leaves remains from which a later age could reconstruct its life.

The Low Dark’s inhabitants do not merely maintain its equilibrium. They are that equilibrium considered as life: condensing, conducting, pumping, weaving, and unweaving, with no part of their activity reserved for becoming something else.
