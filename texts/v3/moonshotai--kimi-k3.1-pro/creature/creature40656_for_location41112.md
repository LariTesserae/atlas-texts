## The lives of the Low Choir

Most of what lives in the Low Choir is small, and most of that is waiting.

Even when a basin is at full roar, its active crowds are only the exposed portion of its population. Beneath them are eggs with mineral seams, larvae folded around stored yolk, raspers sealed inside their armor, and blind coils reduced to damp knots in clay. The surfaces are crowded with life; the sediment beneath them is crowded with postponement.

### The films and the raspers

The microbial films are not a single living sheet. Sulfur-using films form pale, slick patches close to the freshest seeps. Iron-using kinds gather farther out, where their growth leaves rust-colored margins. Between and beneath them live darker films that can endure longer dryness, tightening into layered crusts and releasing almost no spores until water returns. A patch of apparently uniform stone may therefore contain several foods, each attractive to a different animal and each recovering at a different rate.

The raspers read these differences with their mouths and the sensitive front rims of their bodies.

A common pavement rasper has no projecting head. Its body is a low oval fringed with legs, its mouth underneath, its back covered by plates that overlap like the scales of some heavily compressed fish. The plates are not merely armor. Their flexible hinges carry vibration to sensitive tissue beneath, so that an animal experiences the chorus through much of its back. Its mineral-edged mouthparts are replaced throughout life; those of an old rasper are dark, broad, and polished, while a young animal’s are brittle and excessively sharp.

Different kinds graze differently. Some advance in a straight line until the film thins, then turn aside. Others rotate slowly, leaving spiral scars. Pool raspers have raised breathing slits and can remain submerged for long periods. Clay raspers are narrower and wedge themselves into sediment, feeding downward until only the tips of their rear plates remain exposed. On the crowded shell flats, the dominant animals are so broad and thin that a footstep rarely presses on just one.

Their crowding is not always sociable. They gather because food, moisture, and shelter coincide. Nevertheless, some maintain tiny feeding spaces with repeated shell clicks, and neighbors often answer by shifting their plates rather than moving their feet. When seriously disturbed, a whole patch can lock itself down: legs vanish, plates close, and animals become almost indistinguishable from rounded shell gravel. The isolated cracks that travel across the flats are often these clamping responses passing from one rasper to the next.

They molt beneath old shells. For a short time the new animal is pale, soft, and nearly silent, and it is then that many are taken by pool hunters or swallowed incidentally by larger grazers. If it survives, its discarded armor remains where it was shed. Eggs are frequently laid inside such empty shells. Their cases do not open reliably at the first touch of moisture. Many must remain wet while also being shaken; a solitary drip is insufficient, but condensation-rain ticking and trembling across the shell beds can open thousands together.

The shell flats are thus graves, nurseries, and feeding ground at once.

### The crowded air

The smallest abundant fliers are the veilwings. Their wings are framed with fine hollow supports and covered by membranes so thin that they offer little resistance except when moving. Their bodies are soft enough to collapse almost completely when swallowed. Most have no armor beyond small thickenings at the wing hinges and around the egg passages.

They feed with brushes surrounding the mouth. Some comb organic dust and film spores from the air. Others settle on dense colonies and take the fluids that collect there. A few specialize in larvae, picking them from wet shell grit or from the surface film of pools. At rest, veilwings cling in layers, their wings folded over one another until stone feels yielding and faintly adhesive beneath the hand.

Their wingbeats occupy several distinct bands. In an acoustic shadow behind a block or beneath an overhang, a mating swarm can gather densely enough to form a soft, moving body of its own. Elsewhere, individuals continually rise, lose their place in the congested air, and settle again. The brief courtship lights occur mostly within these swarms: a spark produced by one animal, answered at close range by another, and then extinguished. The exchange illuminates little beyond the pair.

Many veilwing larvae live in temporary water. Others develop beneath the throats of the great grazers, where folds remain damp and the constant passage through feeding grounds supplies food. After a chorus-rain, adults of several kinds may emerge together, but their lives are not equally brief. Some mate and die within the first surge of activity. Others enter a shrunken, nearly motionless state if the air dries, reviving once or twice before their wings become too damaged to use.

Above them hunt the shearwings. These are longer-bodied, with stiffer flight surfaces and rows of sensitive membranes along their sides. They do not search for individual prey within the lower wing-roar. Instead, they follow changes in its texture. A swarm thickening above a pool produces one kind of disturbance; a swarm dividing around an obstacle produces another. The hunter folds its wings, drops through the congested layer, and opens them only when it needs to turn or rise. Its mouth takes whatever the dive has gathered.

Shearwings also carry life accidentally. Spores adhere to their wing frames. Pipe-animal larvae sometimes cling to the damp joints of their legs. Eggs laid in one basin may therefore hatch in another, far from water and without any journey across the ground.

The dead of both populations fall continually. Shed wings, exhausted adults, and animals drowned in pools supply a fine rain of food that never reaches the intensity of the chorus-rain but is more dependable.

### The breathing walls

A pipe animal begins as a soft, free-swimming larva. Only after settling does it become mineral and fixed.

The adult’s principal body is a sac secured inside a low calcified cup. Its tubes are extensions of living breathing folds, hardened gradually at their outer surfaces. Air is drawn through them, passed over filtering membranes, and expelled. Dust, spores, and very small fliers stick to the interior. Larger animals are sometimes enclosed in mucus and moved slowly toward the mouth at the base.

No two tubes produce quite the same note. Length, thickness, moisture, damage, and the strength of the animal’s pumping all alter it. Young colonies tend to be reedy and irregular. Older ones acquire deeper tones as new tubes grow and old ones become partly blocked. A crushed tube may whistle for the remainder of its bearer’s life; a drying colony rattles before it falls silent.

Sound is partly an accident of respiration, but not entirely. Pipe animals can narrow their outlets, hold a tone, or stop pumping when pressure changes sharply. Neighboring animals often fall into phase because each responds to the others’ pressure pulses. The response need not be immediate. A note returning from the far side of a basin may arrive after the original animal has changed rhythm, causing another colony to answer a sound that is, in effect, no longer there.

The pipes contain smaller lives. Threadlike animals graze the filtering mucus; some keep passages clear, while others consume the living lining. Brood chambers around the bases may be packed with young in several stages of development. Under dry conditions the whole structure becomes hard and apparently vacant. When wetted, soft tissue extends, valves open, and the young begin climbing over one another. An overhang that seemed encrusted with empty mineral tubes can become furry with movement in minutes.

Not every larva succeeds in leaving. Many are eaten by their own parent’s filters.

### Pools, seams, and shell cover

The blind coils inhabit wet seams behind and beneath the pipe colonies. Some are no thicker than a filament; others are muscular animals whose bodies can fill a narrow cleft from wall to wall. Their skin detects chemical gradients, and their movement produces small pressure changes that return information about open water, soft sediment, and obstruction. They graze films, take eggs, and gather around injured raspers. A few are active hunters, ejecting a portion of the mouth into sediment and drawing it back with whatever adheres.

When a seam dries, a coil may knot itself tightly, reducing exposed surface and surviving within the dampness of its own folded body. Not all can unknot successfully when water returns. Pools after rain sometimes contain living coils feeding among the dead ones that made their revival possible.

The pool-crowns are more permanent animals. Most of the body lies below the water or fitted into a cavity beneath its rim. Only respiratory valves, feeding fringes, and occasional armored edges reach the surface. An individual may spend its entire adult life in one body-sized pool, growing until its shell reflects the shape of that confinement. It feeds on larvae, drowned fliers, and anything small enough to be drawn beneath the surface film.

Where several crowns share a pool, their respiratory openings crowd and jostle. Contests are conducted by pushing, valve-snapping, and sudden expulsions of air. These produce many of the poolside shrieks. After water joins formerly separate pools, animals that have never encountered another adult may meet, mate, fight, or simply remain pressed against opposite sides until the connection dries again.

Under loose shell flakes wait the flake hunters. Their bodies consist largely of a broad muscular margin around a surprisingly small central mass. Spread flat, they are difficult to distinguish from the shell beneath which they hide. When prey steps onto the flake, the margin closes and the whole structure becomes a shallow trap. Their few hard parts are concentrated around the mouth, allowing them to survive the drops they use to escape larger predators. The sudden impacts heard from walls and low overhangs are often these animals abandoning their holds.

The cold-wick is a smaller, more specialized waiter. Its body lies buried in mud, with one slender organ held just above the surface. At its tip is a chamber capable of producing a dim chemical point. The flashes resemble the courtship exchanges of certain small animals closely enough to draw investigation. The cold-wick does not illuminate its surroundings and gives no second display unless something approaches. Most captures leave only a brief contraction in the mud and a dark, lightless stalk folding down into it.

### The basinbacks

The great grazers, or basinbacks, are the largest animals that regularly cross the open flats.

Each walks on six thick legs ending in broad pads surrounded by short mineral toes. The mouth is a wide scraping band carried low at the front of the body. A basinback consumes films, loose organic sediment, dead raspers, and whatever living raspers cannot move aside. It does not crop efficiently. Its feeding leaves streaks, missed islands, and crushed shell, all of which are rapidly colonized by smaller animals.

The concentric growths on its back do not record age in any simple way. Rich wet periods produce broad, porous bands; dry years leave narrow, dense ones. Injuries become mineral ridges. Old armor may be polished smooth along the routes where the animal regularly passes beneath low stone.

These backs support whole communities. The grooves retain moisture after exposed rock has dried. Small raspers graze there, though their mouthparts are too weak to damage mature armor. Sessile filters establish themselves in protected angles and may survive for years. Some passengers remove irritating growths and damaged tissue; others feed directly on the basinback’s skin fluids. Winged larvae crowd beneath the throat, and flat brood animals occupy the sheltered spaces between leg and body. When a grazer rubs against stone, part of this population is crushed and part is scraped off to found colonies elsewhere.

The notes come from folded chambers in the chest. Inflation draws air across moist internal surfaces; contraction and release drive it through a narrow muscular opening. The resulting sound is so low that another animal may receive it through its feet before the air carries it farther.

Basinbacks use these notes to locate mates, avoid one another in confined feeding country, and judge the space around them. Returning vibration differs over deep shell fill, open sediment, and bare stone. An animal approaching an unfamiliar sink may stop after calling, stand with its legs spread, and then turn without taking another step. Its own note can also cause pipe colonies to answer, so that the grazer appears to be surrounded by responses from animals it will never touch.

They do not travel in close herds. Several may use the same polished grade at long intervals, drawn by film chemistry, moisture, or the remembered suitability of a route. Their bodies transfer eggs, larvae, spores, and dormant passengers between basins. Their droppings contribute pale mud; their footprints become tiny pools; their cleaned swaths determine where the next rasper front will spread.

A dead basinback remains inhabited for a very long time. Soft tissue is removed first, often from beneath. Raspers enter the armor seams. Coils occupy the damp chest. Young pool animals settle in depressions that hold condensation. The old sound chambers continue to distort echoes until sediment fills them or the structure collapses. Eventually the concentric back becomes just another low divide, recognizable chiefly by the fact that animals crossing it still prefer its worn, easy grades.

### The hushbacks

The great soft-bodied predators are called hushbacks for what happens around them, not for any sound they make themselves.

A hushback is low, broad, and supported by fluid-filled divisions within its body. Its thick skin hangs in fine folds over the feet and flanks. Those feet spread under weight and deform around shell and gravel rather than striking them. The sensory filaments are held just clear of the substrate, where they taste currents and detect slight changes in pressure. Almost the only hard anatomy is concentrated around the ventral mouth.

To animals that locate bodies by returning sound, a hushback is not a distinct approaching shape. It is an absence of expected return.

Before entering a crowded feeding ground, it may remain still for a long time, receiving the basin’s activity through skin and filaments. Dense rasper hiss, wing-roar, pool disturbance, and the breathing of pipe colonies together indicate where prey is concentrated. When it finally moves, the first response is often far ahead of it: a broken rhythm, a colony failing to answer, a patch of raspers locking down.

Then the hush spreads.

The predator uses that silence. Small sounds which would ordinarily be swallowed by the chorus—wet breathing, a leg withdrawing, larvae turning at a pool surface—become detectable. It does not need to chase. It moves toward the last known concentrations of prey and feeds where animals have clamped themselves to the ground or crowded into refuge.

The mouth opens beneath the body as a broad muscular disc. It can take raspers shell and all, strip a brood surface, or close over the respiratory crown of a pool animal. Mineral tooth plates within the throat reduce shells before the fragments are passed onward. Feeding may continue long after the original disturbance has ceased to propagate.

A satiated hushback often becomes nearly indistinguishable from a low clay bank. It can remain there through much of a dry interval, its body flattening and its filaments withdrawn. The clearest sign of its presence may be that close sounds fail to return from one part of the ground.

### Dryness, waking, and the whole crowded circuit

Dryness compresses the province into innumerable sealed rooms.

The films become crust and spore. Raspers close their plates with hardened secretions. Veilwing eggs remain in dust and empty shells. Pipe animals shut their outlets around the last moist tissue. Coils knot themselves into clay. Basinbacks stand for long periods in depressions, their passenger communities dormant in the grooves of their backs. Even the hushbacks become little more than soft, slow shapes beneath sediment.

A chorus-rain does not wake this multitude uniformly. Films respond first to chemistry and moisture. Some raspers unseal at once; others require prolonged vibration. Pool larvae hatch according to depth, oxygen, and the frequency of impacts on their cases. Veilwings emerge into air already becoming difficult to fly through. Hunters arrive not in response to rain itself but to the movements of everything that woke before them.

It is tempting to make a harmonious society of this. It is not one.

Raspers can strip a film beyond rapid recovery. Veilwings clog the breathing openings of the pipe animals that digest their larvae. Basinbacks crush nursery crusts and carry parasites across divides. Pool-crowns consume their own young when they settle too close. A chorus that brings abundance to one basin may leave the next untouched. Most eggs never receive the right combination of water and vibration, and most awakened animals die before producing replacements.

The circuit persists because enough appetites, accidents, and survivals close into a loop often enough.

Each inhabitant knows only a narrow version of the Low Choir. To a rasper, it is the resistance of film beneath the mouth and pressure arriving through armor. To a pipe animal, it is dust, airflow, and the pulses of neighboring breath. To a basinback, it is a broad country returned through the feet in fragments of its own voice. To a hushback, it is a map of crowded sound interrupted by useful silence.

The Low Choir is the overlap of these partial worlds. Again and again, one body becomes another body’s food, shelter, road, instrument, or weather: shell becoming sediment, breath becoming tone, tone loosening water, and water opening the next generation of waiting mouths.
