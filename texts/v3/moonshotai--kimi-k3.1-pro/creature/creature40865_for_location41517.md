Most of the Dry Court’s inhabitants could be carried away in a cupped hand. Few of its important lives could. The significant organism is often not the creature visible at a given moment, but the arrangement around it: an ant colony ramifying beneath the paving, a vine whose roots extend beyond the court, a web incorporating the work of spiders long dead, a roost shaped by the warmth and scent of many generations of bats. The wrens and geckos look like owners. They are tenants.

The wall mats make clear that luxuriance here is largely a record of what has not died. They are composed of many small, separate plants rather than one continuous carpet. Where a patch dies, it remains attached for years as a corky, brown island, catching dust and insect fragments in which a neighboring shoot may eventually root. The living edges are uneven, following old repairs in the mortar. Some patches have advanced no farther than a hand’s width while the stone above them has cracked and fallen.

Only their newest growth is briefly soft. The mature leaves overlap so closely that a falling seed may lie across several plants without touching anything mineral. During the dust days, the leaves press together and the mats become almost the color of the wall. Afterward their surfaces remain bronze for a while, but the green continues underneath.

The jointed vines are older and less replaceable. At their bases they are dark, woody cables, scarred where masonry has shifted against them. Dust is embedded in some of their resinous wounds. The fleshy upper stems are not soft stores waiting to be tapped; a broken end closes quickly, and even the ants seem unable to make much use of it.

Their flowers are the vines’ one extravagance. For a short part of the dusk, nectar and pollen are offered freely enough to dust the moths’ heads and legs. Several kinds of moth attend them. Some emerge from crevices within the court; others come over the walls from the surrounding lanes, following the pepper-and-honey scent. Their tongues fit the narrow flowers precisely enough that pollination looks almost like a mechanical act. By dawn the moths have returned to shelter, folding their wings until they resemble flakes of wax, bark, or stone. Those that fail to find shelter become part of the court’s morning traffic: a moth in a web, a moth beneath a gecko, or only a pair of wings on the paving, being examined by ants.

The northern shrub is a thicket of different ages. Living trunks twist among dead spines, old beetle galleries, and the split skins cicadas have left behind. Its small daytime flowers serve the bees. Later, its pods dry until their seams strain and then open with a hard snap. The seeds are too well armored for most creatures to treat casually. Ants and mice nevertheless manage them, and beetle larvae wait in the discarded husks.

Beneath the shrub, the cushion plants form green roofs over narrow spaces. Their fallen seeds gleam on top like a second, unsuccessful crop. A seed lying there is often waiting less for favorable conditions than simply to be moved.

Frequently, it is moved by an ant.

The seed-carrying ants have routes across the paving, up the vines, and over the ceiling. Architecture determines their method as much as instinct does. A seed too broad for a hole is turned sideways; a seed too heavy for one ant is abandoned, recovered by several, or dragged along a lower road. Their middens contain empty coats, broken husks, and seeds that have been damaged beyond use. Beetles work through these refuse piles. A few intact seeds remain in forgotten chambers or are dropped into cracks. Most never grow, but the court does not require many successes.

The smaller ants using the ventilation holes have another economy. They travel between their nests and the scale insects gathered at the vines’ tender joints. The scales exude sweetness in quantities too small to make anything visibly wet. A drop may harden before it has time to shine. The ants collect it, defend some of the colonies, and carry away the dead. Mites remain among the scales regardless, their rust-colored bodies clustered at the edges of the wax. At that size the court acquires another set of walls, pastures, and hunting grounds.

A third kind of ant, almost exactly the color of the dust, works mainly at night. It takes moth legs, fragments of bee comb, insect eggs, gecko droppings, and whatever the day traffic has overlooked. These ants are not peacefully separated from the others. Their trails cross, their territories overlap, and a disabled ant from one colony may become provisions on the route of another. Their boundaries are chemical, contested, and easily erased.

The bees in the lamp niche are small and dark, difficult to follow once they pass into the court’s brown light. They have closed the irregularities of their dwelling with resin and wax. The oldest comb is nearly black; newer cells are pale and precisely made. Pollen enters the niche looking like one more kind of dust. Nectar disappears into sealed stores. Nothing about the colony suggests exposed moisture, though the whole structure depends on having obtained and retained it.

The bees know the court by temperature and flowering time. They work the shrub when the sun reaches it and forage farther into the ruins when its flowers are exhausted. Beetles and spiders wait near their route, but not too near the nest. The colony’s lower hum is not simply the sound of flight; it is also the sound of a boundary being continuously defended.

Beetles are the court’s principal reducers. Some shred fallen leaves. Some occupy dead wood in the shrub and vines. Smaller species live in ant refuse, old nests, and dry guano. The shells in the basin have arrived without water: blown there, dropped by feeding birds, or carried and then abandoned. They are the durable remnants of bodies whose softer portions have already entered other lives.

The tiny wasps inspecting beetle burrows are searching for dwellings of a particularly intimate sort. They walk slowly, antennae close to the dust, testing holes and the traces around them. A beetle larva may become food and shelter for a wasp’s young as surely as a beam socket shelters a wren. The wasps themselves are brief. Their work outlasts them inside another insect.

The spiders’ webs are part trap and part inheritance. Fresh silk is laid across older lines, fastening together surfaces that no longer form a coherent plane. A wall may hold considerably more web than spider. Empty egg sacs, dust-loaded strands, and abandoned scaffolding remain between the newer, nearly invisible threads. From her present position, a spider can distinguish the heavy stumble of a beetle from the frantic signal of a moth and both from the meaningless touch of wind-carried grit. During the dust days, that distinction ceases to matter. The upper webs become loaded and brown, and their owners move below them.

The mantis among the flower stems resembles a dead leaf, but it is not endlessly patient. It changes its foothold, misjudges distances, cleans a foreleg after a failed strike, and sometimes abandons the flowers altogether. Its successes are abrupt and usually involve a moth or another insect attending the dusk bloom. When young mantises emerge, the court briefly seems full of them. They scatter through the vines in miniature, dry-leaf perfection. Spiders, ants, wrens, and larger mantises reduce that abundance. One or two may survive long enough to become the still shape among the stems.

The two kinds of gecko divide the court partly by surface. A pallid, slender kind keeps to the walls and higher masonry, flattening itself until its shadow is almost the whole animal. A heavier, granular-skinned kind holds the steps, the lower cracks, and the ground around the shrub. The shallow stair is where their maps disagree. Their disputes consist mostly of arched backs, pulsing throats, raised tails, and short advances that stop just short of contact. An injured toe or a lost tail costs more than possession of another step.

They hunt where the stone retains warmth, taking spiders, small beetles, moths, and young insects. Prey supplies moisture as well as food. Their eggs lie deeper in the masonry, and the hatchlings begin life at a size that makes the ant roads dangerous. Several adults bear blunt, regenerated tails; the originals, left behind during escapes, will have provided a brief richness for the pavement scavengers.

The thumb-thick snake is not the court’s ruler. For weeks at a time it might as well be another root behind the displaced paving stone. After darkness it emerges along a route so familiar that its passage seems less like exploration than the temporary opening of a seam. It takes a gecko when opportunity permits, or finds mice where their runs emerge from beneath the cushions. Then it disappears again. Its rarity is part of its success: the court cannot remain organized around an animal that is almost never visible.

The mice themselves are known chiefly through rearrangement. Their runs beneath the plant cushions are roofed with living growth and walled with compacted dust. Seeds tremble, roll, and vanish as the mice pass overhead or underneath. Their nests contain plant fiber, fine rootlets, feathers, and stolen fragments of silk. They gather seeds, steal from ant middens, eat insects, and move their caches often enough that no simple account of their influence is possible. They destroy far more potential plants than they create, yet an unrecovered cache occasionally places a seed deeper and safer than it could ever have fallen.

The wrens’ beam sockets are the centers of territories, not the whole of them. The birds forage through the court and beyond it, returning with spiders, small larvae, and other soft-bodied prey. Spider silk binds some of their nests; spiders also feed their nestlings. When a brood has gone, the old nest remains useful. Beetles enter it, mites establish themselves in its debris, and a gecko may use the cavity for shelter.

A wren’s scold from inside a wall can make the bird sound larger than the cavity that contains it. At other times the old basin serves as a dust bath. Afterward, for a little while, the dust holds a shallow impression of feathers and feet.

The cicadas belong to the shrub and to the hottest hours. Their cast skins remain fastened to its lower trunks long after the adults have climbed higher. When their trilling fills the court, the shrub seems to acquire a second, louder outline. The hunting bird that sometimes crosses the opening above is not a resident, but its shadow governs the residents. The cicadas stop in sections. Bees returning to the lamp niche widen or hesitate in their flight. Wrens disappear into the walls. The spiders continue attending to their private vibrations, and the ants keep walking.

The bats are the court’s great importers. Individually they are small, brown, and easy to overlook; together they give the vault the appearance of having grown an irregular inner skin. Each has preferred folds and crevices. At dusk the colony seems to loosen rather than simply fly away: a few shapes drop and turn, then the opening receives a stream of wings.

They hunt far beyond the court. What they bring back is not only their bodies but the proceeds of those nights. Their droppings dry quickly. Beetles break them down, ants carry particles away, and dust moves the residue into crevices. Some of the fertile pockets entered by roots may be the accumulated result of countless such deliveries. The court’s greenness is therefore fed, in part, on insects caught over distant roofs and lanes.

The annual dust does not merely interrupt this community. It redistributes it. Bees narrow their entrance. Ants close passages. Spiders abandon the upper walls. Mice continue beneath the cushions with their movements muffled, while geckos wait and the bats pack deeper into the vault. When the air clears, scent trails, territorial marks, and accustomed roads have all been weakened.

The inhabitants do not restore the old map exactly. An ant colony tests a passage it previously avoided. A spider keeps its new, lower position. A mouse digs toward a cache from another direction. The boundary on the geckos’ stair shifts by a step. Dust carries some seeds off the cushions and buries others just deeply enough to change their prospects.

Nothing here is deliberately maintaining a garden. The residents are eating it, boring through it, tying it together, robbing one another, and turning one another’s dwellings into refuse and shelter. Their abundance is not a surplus but a thoroughness of use. A moth is successively a pollinator, a hunter’s meal, a pair of wings on the paving, and several ant journeys. A bat’s night elsewhere becomes guano, beetle food, and dust in a root crack. A dead patch of wall mat catches material in which its living neighbor can advance.

Even vacancy is temporary. A cooled ledge, an emptied socket, a split pod, a thread of abandoned silk: each is already being inspected. That, more than any single species, is what lives in the Dry Court—a continuous, intricate occupation, fine enough to fit inside the stone.
