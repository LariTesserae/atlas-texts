Most of what lives in the High Remainder can be mistaken for the ground.

On the open swells, pavement cushions lie so close to the stone that they seem like stains left by old water. Their surfaces are made of thousands of tiny, tightly packed leaves, hard enough to turn ice and porous enough to catch dust. A cushion no wider than a doorway may be immensely old. Its living portion is only a rind; beneath it lie generations of dead growth, compacted into a black, spongy core. Frost can split the whole mass without ending it. The divisions separate, heal along their broken faces, and continue as cushions whose shapes still record the body they once shared.

Even some of the quartz is inhabited. Fine green threads occupy hairline fractures just beneath its surface, where a little light enters and condensation lingers. They are visible only when the stone is wet. Their growth is nearly imperceptible, and the gradual fracturing of a vein may be their principal means of spreading. They make almost no soil and leave almost nothing when they die. Entire populations can live on a bare outcrop without altering its appearance.

The mosses are the great gatherers. Their many lobes comb water from passing cloud and conduct it inward, drop by drop, into older layers. In dry weather they contract into rust-red and umber crusts. Rain restores their green from the outermost tips downward, so that a large colony may remain brown at its center long after its margins are vivid. They release spores not at the height of wetness but as they begin to dry, when the wind can lift them from the exposed lobes.

Where their water reaches a seam, sedges take root. Their dense, shallow roots do not tap deep reserves; there are usually none to tap. Instead they delay brief runnels of meltwater long enough for silt and dead tissue to settle. A sedge seam is therefore both a colony and a saved surplus. It can outlast the channel that created it.

The rain ferns are less patient in appearance but more patient in fact. Their folded growth can resemble a cluster of black scales for years. Given sufficient water, the scales loosen, stalks turn, and broad black-green fronds open over a few hours. The beads they hold are not merely ornament: water remains in the folds long enough to wet the roots beneath, and the last of it carries spores and fragments of older growth into the joints below. When the tarns disappear, the ferns fold again. Nothing about their dry state distinguishes a plant that will open next season from one that has been waiting for several centuries.

The tarns themselves contain no swimming things. Their water can be perfectly clear and still support olive films on the bottom, delicate mats around the margin, and translucent colonial growth attached to submerged stones. These freeze with the basin or dry into dark flakes under its mud crust. The life of a tarn is mostly at its edges, where wetting and drying occur unevenly and a colony can retreat by degrees rather than all at once.

In the sheltered hollows, the crooked woods accumulate.

A wood may contain no single old tree. Trunks split near the ground, lean until their branches touch the mats, and root where they rest. The original stem decays; newer stems occupy its shelter. After enough time, the oldest part of the tree is not any piece of wood but the continuing arrangement of roots, soil, and windbreak. The interwoven canopy is often a record of this process rather than the result of branches growing toward one another.

Their seeds are small and dry, carried by wind or washed into hollows. Most new growth, however, comes from division and rooting. There are no fleshy fruits, because there is nothing to carry them, and no tender foliage kept continually young by browsing. Nothing eats the leaves. They fall, freeze, are wetted, and remain. The deep soil beneath an old wood is not merely the place in which the wood lives. It is the wood’s accumulated dead, deep enough to alter the water and temperature on which its living descendants depend.

All this would be a slow, severe, but comparatively simple ecology were it not for the recurrences.

A recurrence is easiest to mistake for several widely separated clones. Ferns in two hollows open under the same conditions. Cushions on opposite sides of a watershed divide along matching lines. A sheltered wood and a few trunks in an abandoned court show the same pattern of frost injury, although no continuous root system joins them and the country between cannot reliably be crossed.

The resemblance is not only hereditary. A change in one portion can be followed by a corresponding change in another without any material passage that can be traced. Fronds in a dry hollow may open while rain is falling on another portion of the colony, then fold when that distant wetting ends. Growth after injury may repeat not merely the injury’s shape but its subsequent correction: a lobe abandoned in one place is abandoned in all, though the local exposure differs.

What joins the portions is not a hidden root in the ordinary sense. It is closer to the continuity of a stream whose next visible section lies in another basin. Here and here remain parts of one living body, even when between has ceased to have a stable geographical meaning.

This makes the number of inhabitants uncertain. A green basin may contain thousands of separate plants, or a few local plants and one visible portion of something whose other portions are beyond any current route. A newly appeared patch may be a young colony founded by a spore, or the first growth in that place of something extremely old. Only a history of rain, freezing, injury, and recovery can begin to distinguish the possibilities.

Nothing migrates in order to achieve this. The recurrences do not travel across the bare plateau. They gain and lose country. A portion may become inaccessible without dying; another may become visible after generations of absence. A census of all the living green patches would therefore not be a census of living individuals, and an empty basin would not establish that its former inhabitant was dead.

The towns make another kind of life possible.

Their value is not only shelter, deep soil, or stored water. In the wilderness, the relations among events cannot always repeat. Water may wet the same stone twice without reaching the same roots. A channel may preserve its shape while delivering its flow elsewhere. Within an intact town, however, the order of things can be depended upon: rain strikes a particular slope of slate, enters a particular gutter, fills a particular cistern, overflows along a particular edge, and reaches the same garden after the same interval.

Plants grow into that order. Moss thickens where the first condensation gathers. Fern roots occupy the joints wetted by overflow. Sedges hold soil at the mouth of a conduit, and their held soil makes the next overflow slower and longer. Water changes growth; growth changes the water’s subsequent effects. The town supplies repetition, while the living colony supplies change. Together they can preserve a history longer than any leaf, stem, or visible mat.

Rain may fall on a roof in an otherwise empty quarter. Water appears in a cistern beneath trees some distance away. Before the soil around them is wet, ferns along a roofed stair begin to open. Hours later, moss on an upper wall releases spores into a channel that has not yet filled. Each response has a material cause, but the sequence as a whole belongs to no single plant. When it recurs after long interruptions, in the same order and with the same small variations according to cold and dryness, it begins to resemble one inhabitant resuming an old habit.

These are the Remainder’s most persistent residents.

Some occupy whole districts: gardens, canopy streets, open reservoirs, and miles of pavement bound into a repeating pattern of wetting and growth. Others are almost entirely contained by one cistern. Such a resident may consist of a little green film, a dry black crust, sediment on the bottom, and a single fern rooted beside the overflow. It can remain inconspicuous through several human lifetimes, if such lifetimes are used to measure it, and become fully active only when an uncommon combination of rain and thaw fills its conduits in the proper order.

They have no voices, make no tools, and leave no inscriptions. They do not repair the towns. Where their growth looks like tending, it is usually the result of long survival rather than care. Colonies whose roots split foundations often destroy the deep soil and dependable water on which they depend. Colonies that spread along pavement, line an overflow without closing it, or leave the principal joints undisturbed are more likely to retain their place. After enough ages, the surviving growth appears considerate of the masonry. No gardener is required.

Nor do the residents preserve any account of the builders. Their memory is not a store of images. A flood may remain as altered root growth and a different threshold for releasing spores; a succession of hard freezes may remain as a changed arrangement of trunks. A resident can retain the consequences of an event without retaining anything that could be called a description of it. The hands that laid the conduits have left no corresponding impression.

It is possible to describe all this without attributing thought to anything. Stone conducts water. Plants respond. Dead tissue changes the soil. Repetition favors some responses over others. Yet a town resident is not adequately described as the sum of the plants currently visible in it. Its fronds can freeze and be replaced while its habits continue. Its wood can decay while the same sequence of wetting and opening persists in younger growth. Its body can be almost entirely renewed without entirely losing its history.

Conversely, the plants can remain alive while the resident ceases to exist. If a cistern is broken and its walls lose enough of their continuity, water still falls and vegetation may become more luxuriant than before. But the old order no longer repeats. The parts begin responding as separate colonies, or as portions of wild recurrences extending elsewhere. Nothing need die in the ordinary biological sense. What is lost is the reliable relation by which they had been one persistent thing.

Thus a green, reclaimed town is not necessarily an old or densely inhabited one. A nearly bare town with one intact reservoir may contain the older resident. Lifelessness, greenness, and ruination are poor measures of population.

The residents are not absolutely isolated. Water arriving from a roof in another quarter may carry spores, mineral dust, and the leachings of an unfamiliar garden. A stream section can introduce material from country to which no road presently leads. For a time, a town colony may include growth far beyond its walls, or share portions with another recurrence. Then the clouds return, the horizon changes, and the relation is no longer available.

Nothing suggests that all these lives together form one vast being. The plateau does not wake beneath them. The towns are not organs in a single body, and the green basins do not constitute a hidden population waiting to assemble. Life there remains plural, local, and frequently unable to agree with itself about boundaries—much like the country.

When rain comes, the extent of that population becomes briefly visible. Green threads darken inside quartz. Rust-colored moss softens on the open stone. Ferns unfold in basins and in roofless rooms that cannot both be reached from one another. Tarn films thicken. The cisterns begin their separate ticking, and in certain towns the responses follow old, elaborate orders that no surviving trunk is old enough to have learned by itself.

Then the water sinks away. Ice returns. The visible portions contract, dry, or disappear from their present country.

Nothing calls across the empty swells. Nothing comes to the doors of the towns. Yet the High Remainder is not uninhabited, and its inhabitants are not merely waiting in it. They persist by the same uncertain means as its places: continuing here, recurring elsewhere, and remaining themselves only for as long as something—root, soil, water, or well-founded stone—preserves the relations by which they can return.
