Nothing with a face lives in the Black Catchment. No remnant of the makers keeps the corbelled cell, and no animal has taken their place. Silt crosses the doorway; water climbs the stair. Yet the province is not lifeless. Its inhabitants are rooted, widely separated, and difficult to count. A hundred stems may belong to one old growth, while a single cushion of moss may contain generations of tissue that have never been wholly separate.

The veilgrass stands are the largest of these lives. What appears to be a crowded grove is often an ancient root mat following a mineral seam beneath the moraine. Individual columns rise, thicken, and are eventually stripped away, but the mat persists. It extends where seepage remains dependable and dies back where the seam fails. In this way a grove can shift its position without any part of it traveling. The stand that hisses against a bank in one age may, much later, be growing beyond the bank’s end, while its dead roots still bind the old ground.

In slack water the ribbons hang open, heavy and supple. The ascending current folds them lengthwise, one after another, until a whole grove seems to narrow itself against the flow. There is no alarm in this. Pressure draws the tissue closed; the returning rain presses it flatter still. The grass endures by offering as little resistance as it can while its buried parts hold fast. Those buried parts are tough, almost mineral in their density, and threaded through with fine dark filaments that gather the weak charge escaping from wet schist.

Beneath the grass, stone-moss lives closer to the rock. Its hard, silt-colored exterior is mostly old tissue and trapped sediment. The active growth lies within: clear, branching layers through which bitter seepage moves almost imperceptibly. A cushion grows toward moisture and charge rather than toward any opening in the darkness. Where ice splits the stone, the moss may eventually follow the new fracture inward. Where the surface becomes too heavily armored with silt, growth continues at one edge and abandons the other. Some cushions are therefore hollow crescents around the place where their first tissue once lived.

Cup-leaf occupies the boulders and the firmer debris among them. Its shallow vessels open during the ascent, when mineral dust is moving, and close as the first heavy rain arrives. On a large plant the closures pass unevenly from cup to cup, so that for a while part of the boulder remains pale and open while the rest has become a pavement of tight scales. The captured dust settles into the base of each vessel and is slowly taken up with seep water. A cup may be buried by later lobes while its rim remains mineral-hard, leaving the old leaf spread in concentric, broken arcs.

These plants make conditions for one another without forming anything so busy as a community in the usual sense. Stone-moss traps the silt in which veilgrass can gain purchase. Veilgrass slows the current and allows finer sediment to gather around cup-leaf. Later, falling fronds may smoother both. A root mat may seal the very seep that sustained a moss cushion, or raise a bed of trapped sediment until the rain can tear beneath it. Shelter, obstruction, burial: these are the gardens’ principal relations. Nothing grazes them, and nothing waits to consume their dead.

A collapsed grove consequently does not become a swarm of smaller lives. It remains. Ribbons stay folded in clear ice; stems darken where they lie; the lower layers compact under new growth. The living plants above are not nourished by this accumulation. They continue to draw from stone and moving water. The dead beds are less a store of food than an accumulation of unfinished persistence. Their layers record uprooting, burial, and changes in the current, but only as thickness, distortion, and scar. If the oldest gardens possess memory, it is entirely of this material kind.

The only living things that travel are spores and broken growing tips, and even they do not travel with direction. A spore has no means of choosing a basin or resisting a pass. It may be carried above a summit, held for a time in the crushing water there, and returned much later in a descending rope of rain. Some spores remain viable in the frond beds long after the growth that released them has vanished. Most are scoured against bare stone, carried into still depths, or buried beyond the reach of a seep. Their abundance compensates for nothing; nearly all of them simply come to an end.

On the highest slopes, life becomes correspondingly slight. The hairlike tufts in deep fractures often have more substance below the rock than above it. A tuft may add no visible tissue for many cycles, then extend one clear segment when ice widens its seam and admits a little more seepage. It survives at the margin of pressure, cold, and mineral supply, too exposed to become lush and too sheltered to be wholly stripped away.

The central gorge lies near the opposite failure. Its comparative stillness starves the rock of the shearing charge on which richer growth depends. Dust is not delivered repeatedly to the same holds, and fragments that settle there may remain undisturbed until sediment covers them. Life in the Catchment requires a particular amount of unrest: enough movement to bring minerals and charge, not so much that tissue is torn from the stone. The lush cirques occupy that narrow condition. The gorge and the open black slopes largely do not.

Occasionally the plants encounter something made. Cup-leaf may establish itself in sediment caught behind a cairn, or a veilgrass fragment may root below the seventeen steps. This is not habitation. The plants make no distinction between the stair and a natural rib of quartzite. No roots seek the shelter of the cell because it was once a shelter, and nothing tends the stone bowl or responds to the humming of its walls in rain. The makers belong to the history of the Catchment, not to its present population.

At the turning of another cycle, a spore lodges in a fresh fracture where schist weeps beneath a rind of ice. It puts out one colorless filament. The filament branches, finds the seep, and holds. For a long time there is nothing more to it than that: no stem, no sound, no visible garden, only a little living chemistry maintained against the cold.

This is the scale on which the Black Catchment is inhabited. A few enduring groves, a few closed cups full of mineral dust, a few threads beneath clear ice—and between them, immense reaches in which nothing living happens at all.
