The Senni themselves are easy to miss, even when one is looking directly at them. There are seldom more than sixty, and they do not gather unless something requires gathering. A person may work all afternoon within easy hearing of five others and see only one.

Nor do they have a single characteristic shape. An adult crouched over a wall, an infant clinging to a harness, and a swimmer extended in open water can look almost like three different kinds of animal. Adults are compact and heavy-boned, broad through the chest, with powerful legs and wide, webbed feet. Their hands are narrower and more delicate than the rest of them suggests; the last joints of the fingers are free of webbing and sensitive enough to distinguish the lay of a cord or a hairline crack in stone. Three long gill slits lie protected along each side of the ribs. During hard work they open, and the dark, feathery gills within pulse quickly.

Their skin is olive, slate, brown, or a mottled mixture of these, often paling around old scars. Transparent inner lids protect their eyes when silt is stirred. Their sight is suited to Senn’s real daylight: they can read a chipped edge or find the pale end of a cord at several body lengths, but at night they depend more on touch and hearing.

They are denser than they look. At rest they settle onto the paving rather than hovering above it. Much of their work is done half crouched, half anchored, with one hand on a root or stone. In open water the body lengthens, the feet stroke together, and the same apparently cumbersome person becomes a swift, economical swimmer. Even then, most Senni prefer to arrive at something solid. They are bottom-built people who happen to be excellent swimmers.

Clothing is mostly a matter of harnesses and tool loops. A working adult may carry a stone knife, two spare cords, a bone pick, and a lidded food vessel, each independently tethered. Wood and antler are precious enough that an awl or rake may be older than the house in which it is kept. Such tools accumulate repairs rather than ornament: a new binding beside an old one, a replaced wedge, a notch cut where a hand once slipped.

At close range, Senni speech combines soft sounds made deep in the throat with small, precise clicks. Much of it is felt through the chest as well as heard. When two people are working side by side, they often touch wrists and tap instructions directly into the skin, especially where a mistake with a blade would matter. At a distance, grammar is soon lost but rhythm remains. Public messages therefore use patterns rather than long utterances. A summons to the hall does not sound like a call to the weirs, and a damaged screen has its own urgent sequence. One need not know where a struck sound came from to know what must be done.

Privacy is achieved either by touch or by going farther out. Two people speaking palm to palm in the square may be sharing something no one else can overhear. Someone who swims alone beyond the last seed beds may be checking the boundary stones, or may simply want not to be available for a while. The open water has social uses as well as practical ones.

Senni infants can breathe water and cling almost immediately. For their first months they ride folded against a parent’s back in a soft sling, small hands and feet fastening reflexively whenever the adult turns or dives. Once children are loose, they are difficult to keep in rooms. They travel in little groups along the lanes, investigate every new cord, and return with silt on their knees and improbable reports about animals.

A child learns knots before knife work. The first useful knot is one that will hold under tension but can still be undone by cold, clumsy fingers. The first lessons in plants and animals are equally practical: which shoot may be cut, which root is bearing weight, which leaf shelters eggs, which patch of apparently empty paving is being used by spawning fish. This knowledge is not taught as general reverence for life. Some things are food, some are pests, and some must be killed before they damage a wall. The skill lies in knowing the difference.

Children frequently try to make pets of crayfish. The crayfish never agree.

Older Senni do not cease working so much as move their work closer to home. Hands that can no longer haul a terrace stone may still repair a fine screen or read an old agreement by touch. Sleeping places are shallow stone niches or root-lined recesses furnished with soft loops. Even a body that settles comfortably can turn or wedge itself awkwardly during sleep, so most people fasten one loose tether before resting. Infants sleep within the curve of an adult’s body, and the smallest children often remain tethered to the same loop.

Their food suits a town without fire. They crush the oily seeds from the beds, take silverfish in season, gather molluscs where silt collects, and eat crayfish and the tender inner portions of certain stems. The larger char are taken sparingly. Preparation consists of cutting, grinding, soaking, mixing, and waiting. Some seed pastes are deliberately allowed to sour in closed vessels; others are eaten fresh. A good cook knows the difference between a mixture that has become rich and one that has merely begun to rot. The cold recesses beneath the terrace extend the life of a meal, but they do not abolish seasons or make care unnecessary.

Most days are maintenance. A screen is lifted and reset. Sediment is coaxed toward a conduit. A root that has begun to narrow a doorway is cut back, while another is encouraged around a cord. Work is rarely allotted by a single central authority, though the common hall is where obligations are argued over and occasionally reassigned.

A household possesses tools, food, sleeping places, and various unfinished projects. Its relation to a wall or length of lane is better described as answering for it than owning it. Agreements are recorded on weighted cords kept in the hall, with knots and intervals that can be read by fingertip. When an agreement changes, the old sequence is often left in place and the alteration added, so that the history of a dispute remains available. Some of the chisel marks on the oldest lintels may be records of the same kind. Others may be names, tallies, or instructions. Different elders teach different interpretations, sometimes with considerable confidence.

The Senni are patient in their works, not necessarily in their natures. They can deliberate for twenty years over a doorway and lose their temper in an instant over a borrowed awl. Households become possessive. Skilled workers can grow vain. A young person may cut a living support because they want a room to look finished, then spend the following season helping to prop up the consequences. Patience can also become an excuse for postponing an unpleasant repair.

Their favorite old stories are often about mistakes that took decades to reveal themselves: the beautifully trained wall that closed its own entrance, the screen tied so securely that no one could open it during the next fish run, the lane kept so meticulously clear that three generations passed before anyone connected its emptiness with the decline of a spawning shelter. These stories are told with particular enjoyment when the teller is descended from the person at fault.

The largest and oldest inhabitant of Senn is not a Senni at all. It is the loomroot.

Whether the whole mat is one organism or many scarcely matters to the people working within it. They know its local temperaments. Growth on one terrace answers pruning by thickening; growth on another sends runners beneath floors and lifts stones. One kind of old rhizome becomes stubborn and woody, while another remains pliable long after it has reached the thickness of a wrist. A skilled tender recognizes these differences much as one recognizes neighbors.

The Senni do not regard every act of growth as benevolent. Loomroot can hold a wall together, and it can slowly pull the same wall out of alignment. It can shelter eggs or smoother a seed bed. Keeping it involves cutting some growth hard, leaving other growth strictly alone, and accepting that the result will not be fully visible for years. Some tenders talk to the roots while they work. Others consider this foolish and do it when they think they are alone.

Among the animals, a few are known almost as individuals. One very large char beneath the common hall has a torn fin and a pale mark behind its eye. It has rested there for as long as the oldest Senni can clearly remember. It is neither tame nor sacred, but its habits are considered when work is planned. It dislikes screens being dragged overhead, ignores ordinary pruning, and usually shifts a little when the evening drumming begins. No one catches it. Its flesh would not compensate the town for the peculiar emptiness of that shadow.

The silverfish are less individually known but more constantly present. They pass through open doorways, investigate disturbed silt, and vanish under roofs when a shadow crosses the ceiling. Crayfish clean some crevices and make others worse; they steal stored food, click inside walls, and occasionally emerge holding a fragment of fiber that someone had meant to retrieve later. Amber snails are carried off seed leaves and deposited on paving where their grazing is useful. Their empty shells become small scoops, counters, and counters again after children have stolen them.

A cord closing a lane for spawning is therefore taken seriously, though not always without argument. There have been cords left up too long by households hoping to avoid clearing work. There have also been necessary repairs delayed so long that the fish benefited and the wall did not. The hall cords preserve several vigorous disagreements on this subject.

A diving bird is the only visitor that enters without regard for any of these arrangements. Its arrival is too sudden for ceremony. People duck under the nearest stone, fish turn together, and bubbles tear upward through the bright water. Afterward there may be a damaged screen or a scattering of eggs to account for. The Senni do not generally treat the bird as an omen, but they discuss its size, direction, and success at considerable length.

When ice closes the tarn’s ceiling, the visible town contracts. People spend longer in their sleeping niches and more time working near the hall. Stored food matters; so do cord repair, tool maintenance, and the reading of old agreements. The settlement does not become lifeless. Loomroot continues slowly beneath the roofs, animals remain in their crevices, and the inlet’s voice changes by degrees. The Senni know that a quiet season is not the same as an empty one.

Their dead are carried beyond the last seed beds to a reach of dark stones where no rooms are planned. Bodies are not built into walls or kept within the living terraces. Root and small animals are allowed to do what they do. Useful fiber from a person’s harness returns to the household; tools pass on according to need and prior promises. The name remains wherever it occurs in an agreement, a tool history, or the intention for an unfinished room. Memory is affectionate in places, resentful in others, and never complete.

To keep something, in Senn, does not mean leaving it untouched. A wall may be kept by cutting it. A fish run may be kept by staying away from it. A child may be kept by being entrusted with a knife slightly before the adults feel entirely ready. Keeping always includes the possibility that one’s decision will later need correcting.

In late afternoon, the visible population may still amount to a single tender on a distant wall. The tender works with a stone knife, pausing to clear silt with one hand. Beneath the hall, two elders may be disagreeing by touch over an old cord while the scarred char rests below them. Somewhere near the work yards, children are supposed to be sorting shells and are probably not doing so. None of this needs to assemble into a crowd in order to constitute a town.

At the edge of the newest district, a Senni reties a pale cord laid before their birth. The rectangle inside it is still mostly leaves and illuminated water. Loomroot has thickened along one side and ignored another. The worker resets the stone weight, clears a little growth, and leaves the rest.

The person who will first sleep there has not yet been born. When they arrive, the room should be sound, but it should not be finished.
