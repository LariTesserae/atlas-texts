The gardener tying back the frond is one of the town’s people. They and their ancestors have never breathed air. Nothing in their bodies is a held bubble: no lungs, no swim bladder, no buoyant cavity that pressure might crush. Pressure is simply one of the conditions of being alive, like warmth or the weight of a tool. When completely relaxed, they are slightly denser than the water. Rest often means allowing a ledge to take their weight.

An adult, fully extended, is a little longer than a tall human, though they are seldom seen fully extended. At rest they fold into a low, heavy shape: broad head, deep and flexible trunk, four jointed limbs, and a muscular tail continuing the line of the back. The forward limbs end in hands with six blunt digits. The innermost pair can oppose the rest, allowing a grip precise enough to pick a volcanic-glass flake from a woven mat or strong enough to hold a harness under load. The hind limbs are chiefly for bracing. Their broad pads can find purchase in a fracture while both hands work and the tail takes the current.

Their skin is fine-grained rather than scaled, charcoal or olive or a brown so dark that its color appears only at local noon. Along the neck lie folded gill leaves. During exertion these open, revealing narrow edges of deep crimson; at rest they nearly vanish into the skin. Small changes in their posture also carry meaning. A stranger might see only a face without expression. Another inhabitant can recognize attention, fatigue, irritation, or a carefully concealed flash of amusement.

They wear little besides harnesses, tool cords, and pads of woven fiber where work would otherwise abrade the skin. Everything is secured. A loose stone knocking against a wall would be both wasteful and rude.

Their eyes are useful, but sight is not the sense by which they principally inhabit the town. It serves at close range: reading a hand sign, finding the edge of a root mat, distinguishing a screen’s weave from the darkness behind it. Beyond a few body lengths, other faculties take over. Sensory pits along the jaw and flanks detect small movements in the water and vibrations transmitted through stone. The gills and mouth read chemistry with great discrimination. Their vocabulary for water is much larger than their vocabulary for color. A seep may be iron-bitter, root-sweet, cutting, dull, or “wrong before the leaves know,” this last being a recognized condition rather than a poetic one.

A mason can rest a palm on a block and feel whether current is passing around solid bedrock or entering a new fracture behind it. A gardener can sometimes detect a badly set channel gate before any visible change reaches the terrace. To them, the streets are not merely aligned passages in black stone. One lane is the place where the wall answers late to a touch; another is where warm water crosses from left to right; another smells faintly of the old felt cultures kept behind its screens.

Their voices are low and brief. Most are used for coordinated work: hold, release, wait, something has changed. Ordinary conversation more often passes through the hands. At close range there is a visual language of economical signs. In darkness or privacy, there is touch-speech, delivered through pressures and releases on a hand, wrist, or forearm. It is not a simplified substitute for speech. Two inhabitants leaning motionless against the same wall may be conducting an intricate argument while appearing, to an observer, to do nothing at all.

The quiet is therefore not an absence of communication. Nor is it evidence that everyone is naturally solemn. Children are notoriously poor at it. They flick from landing to landing, misjudge descents, catch their harnesses on gateposts, and produce accidental knocks that carry through half a district. One of the first things a child is taught is how to come down onto stone without striking it. The second is that no water is private: whatever passes through their gills has already crossed someone else’s garden and will continue onward to another.

Adults can move quickly when speed is necessary. A startled inhabitant can disappear above a terrace with one stroke of the tail, and a failed gate or a shifted load can bring people out of screened chambers and side lanes within moments. For a little while the apparently empty town becomes crowded with muffled tools, rapid signs, and bodies braced against the same problem. When the flow is safe again, they disperse. The celebrated slowness of ordinary work is partly economy, partly caution, and partly the simple fact that many tasks improve when the worker stops long enough to perceive what has changed.

The long intervals between a mason’s blows are not necessarily meditation. The mason is listening. So are others nearby, whether they appear to be working or not. When the mountain gives its low internal note, everyone stops because small vibrations can no longer be read reliably and because seep behavior sometimes changes afterward. Some inhabitants also place a hand on the stone and keep still out of respect. Others consider that superfluous. The practical and the reverent explanations have coexisted for so long that most people no longer trouble to separate them.

Only a few hundred people live in the summit town in any one generation. Its apparent size is misleading. Chambers outlast their original uses; a sleeping cell becomes a store, a store becomes a fermentation room, and a fermentation room is eventually narrowed by mineral growth until only a child can enter comfortably. Many courts are common workplaces rather than the centers of crowded households. At any given time, some inhabitants are at outlying gardens, some are traveling, some are working alone, and many are asleep.

There is no common night to empty the streets. People rest according to work, digestion, age, and inclination. Behind a woven screen, an inhabitant may sleep in a shallow stone hollow or a fiber sling that keeps the gills clear and prevents the body from drifting. A closed screen can mean sleep, illness, private grief, stored food, or a wish not to be drawn into conversation. It does not necessarily mean that the room is vacant.

Households usually form around a shared warm court and shared food cultures rather than around a single building. Kin, mates, apprentices, and unrelated companions may all belong to one. Pair bonds can be lifelong, brief, or absent without affecting a person’s standing. Personal possessions are few: tools, harness, sleeping fiber, perhaps a cord of records or a small object kept for no defensible reason. More important is one’s relation to shared things—who may scrape a particular felt, who is expected to clear a sediment gap, who must be consulted before a gate is moved.

Children are born singly and infrequently. A birth is a substantial change in the labor of several households, not merely an addition to one. Adults remain involved in the rearing of children long after infancy, if only because a young person can take decades to acquire a reliable sense of water. A life of two centuries is full but not astonishing. Age is relative enough that someone of sixty may still be called young, especially by an elder who dislikes their masonry.

Long life does not make them uniformly patient or wise. It gives grudges time to become infrastructure. A lane may take an awkward turn because two households once refused to share a landing, and everyone since has simply learned the turn. A skilled gardener may repeat, for a century, the mistake that first earned them a reputation for carelessness. People who tire of being so thoroughly remembered sometimes leave for an outer vent garden or another cone. Solitude is one of the things the outlying settlements provide.

Their food comes almost entirely from cultivation. They do not directly consume the warmth or dissolved minerals of the seeps, though their bodies tolerate chemical conditions that would injure many other animals. The pale bacterial felts are maintained on stone surfaces and scraped in narrow strips, with ample time allowed for each strip to close again. From the larger plants they take oily root bodies, mature growth, and gels that can be collected without stripping leaves. Tougher foods are fermented in dark cells until they soften and change. Dead leaves supply fiber; living ones are cut only when a garden requires it.

There is little agricultural surplus, and this limits the population more effectively than any absence of building space. A meal may depend on several terraces, two different water chemistries, and cultures older than the person eating. Some households guard their felt cultures more carefully than their tools. A stone weight can be replaced. A felt adapted to one particular seep may not survive anywhere else.

The transparent grazers are usually too few to be pests. A gardener may carry one from a young fan to an older patch rather than kill it. Their transparency also makes them useful: held near a patch of luminous root-velvet, a grazer’s gut can reveal what it has been consuming. A good gardener can sometimes diagnose an unhealthy felt in this way before tasting anything amiss in the water. The pale coilers in the sediment basins are left to their work. A basin without them processes waste badly.

Some of the oldest continuous inhabitants are not people at all. A black-olive ribbon garden may have spread by root until no one can say whether it is many plants or one. Certain felt lineages have been transferred, divided, and transferred again since before the oldest surviving walls were laid out. A person may spend an entire life tending a garden that was already ancient when their earliest remembered ancestor first touched it.

Shared water makes some authority unavoidable. There are keepers of gates, listeners responsible for major masonry, and people entrusted with routes and records. Their authority belongs to the task and can be challenged. A gatekeeper who consistently misjudges a terrace will lose the gate, however old or otherwise respected they may be. The closest thing the town has to a universal law is that a change in shared water must never arrive unannounced.

This does not eliminate disputes. Warmth is wealth, and some districts are naturally better supplied than others. Gardeners disagree over diversions; masons object to channels cut near their foundations; households resent obligations imposed by work completed before they were born. Most disagreements proceed quietly, through inspections, touch-speech, and long sessions in the public courts. Quiet can prevent panic, but it can also allow an injury to remain unspoken for years. The town is workable, not innocent.

For durable records, they use pecked stone slips and shallow marks cut directly into working surfaces. A gate may carry notches showing its safe settings and small corrections added by successive keepers. Obsolete instructions are deliberately smoothed away; forgetting obsolete information is part of maintenance. Genealogies, years, and obligations are more often kept in knotted fiber cords. Routes beyond the summit are taught bodily. A traveler learns a sequence of descents, temperatures, textures, and exposures, not a picture of the region seen from above.

The dead are usually wrapped in old fiber and carried to cold outer basins, where the coilers and microbial communities make use of what remains. In a place with so little spare matter, this is not considered an indignity. Tools are cleaned and passed on. A name-sign may be added to a household cord, but a person’s most durable memorial is often a small alteration: a landing shifted after a load once slipped, a gate lip deepened, a route marker made readable to a numb hand.

Because everyone carries a few necessary tasks, a death is often noticed first as an omission in maintenance. A screen goes unrepaired. A sediment gap fills. A familiar adjustment to the morning flow does not occur. Only afterward does the absence become a face recalled clearly or a style of touch-speech no one else quite reproduces.

Their oldest stories do not begin with dry land, a flood, or an arrival from some country of air. They begin with warmth found in a fracture, with a garden that failed, with a passage made safe enough for a loaded basket. There are stories of the first successful lifted-water channel and bitterly different accounts of who actually designed it. Other stories concern errors: the terrace that turned bitter overnight, the traveler who followed a warm gradient into open water and returned with no memory of the crossing, the court abandoned because everyone waited for someone else to clear its drain.

Travelers from other peaks bring plant cuttings, living felts, new methods of abrasion, and occasionally new household members. They are questioned as closely about failures elsewhere as about successes. A town that has lost an entire kind of garden may possess knowledge more valuable than any surviving sample.

The great animals beyond the ridge remain unknown. The inhabitants call the events ridge-darkenings and record their years, duration, and any accompanying change in current. Some maintain that the same kind of animal follows the chain of peaks. Others believe the visits have nothing to do with one another. No one hunts them, and no wall is built against them. When the faint light goes temporarily absent, work stops; when it returns, there is rarely anything more to add.

Nor do the people agree about whether the mountain is alive. Some speak of it as a body, some as a set of processes, and some distrust any language that makes it seem interested in them. What they share is not a doctrine but a practice of attention. Nothing in their history makes emergence from the sea a promise or a desired conclusion. Upward means lighter water, greater exposure, and the faint bronze edge on leaves at noon. It does not mean rescue.

Yet they are not merely a maintenance system for gardens and channels. A mason may prefer one quarry because its stone carries a vibration they find pleasant. A gardener may preserve an unpromising crimson fan long after another would have replaced it, because it was the first living thing entrusted to them. People fall in love, become tired of one another, adopt apprentices they did not expect to miss, and keep small preferences that serve no public purpose. None of this is visible from the upper quarry.

The gardener in the first court finishes tying the frond and leaves the knot slightly loose. The stem will thicken. Whoever comes later should be able to free it without cutting the fiber. For a while the gardener rests with one palm on the gate, feeling warm water pass.

Behind a screen, a child lands badly and makes a single clear knock. From the nearest work court comes one brief note of reproof. The gardener’s gill leaves lift, just slightly. Then the frond moves, the water resumes its nearly soundless work, and there is nothing more for a visitor to see.
