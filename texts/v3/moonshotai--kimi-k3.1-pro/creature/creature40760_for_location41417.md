## The inhabitants of Sorn

Sorn has not had time to produce a wholly distinctive flora or fauna. Most of its inhabitants arrived from somewhere else: the coast, the retreating ice margins, older river valleys, and mountains where cold and disturbance were already ordinary facts. The basin has sorted these colonists less by simple hardiness than by their ability to survive displacement. A creature that can endure freezing but requires the same bank every spring is poorly served here. A willow that can grow from a broken branch is not.

The first life on new ground is usually invisible. Fresh gravel develops a dark, slightly greasy film of microscopic growth. Fungal threads follow through accumulated silt. At the orange seeps, rust-colored mats thicken around the dissolved iron, while tea-colored ponds fill with minute suspended life long before anything larger than a finger enters them. These films and mats are easily stripped away, but they also travel. A flood does not merely erase them; it distributes them.

Moss is often the first growth substantial enough to change the appearance of a surface. It occupies scratches in bedrock, the shaded sides of blocks, and the wet seams between sizes of gravel. It holds dust, then seeds. Willow follows with a completeness that can make a bar look planted. Some arrives as seed, some as fragments torn from thickets upstream. A stripped twig lying half in silt and half in water may put out roots before its leaves have entirely wilted.

The resulting thickets are not collections of securely established individuals. They are repeatedly repaired nets. Alder and willow roots cross and graft themselves into the same shifting masses of sediment. A stem may be buried to its upper branches and survive; another may be left standing on several exposed roots while the ground beneath it becomes water. When a whole section goes into the river, fragments lodge downstream and begin again. Much of Sorn’s vegetation is, in effect, older than the ground currently beneath it.

Sedge and horsetail occupy the ground where standing water would discourage less patient growth. Their living parts are often less extensive than their buried ones. Rhizomes run through mud that never properly dries, connecting shoots across hollows and beneath apparently open pools. Sedge tussocks rise as their crowns accumulate old stems and trapped silt. Some become firm islands; others are buoyant structures with deep water underneath. From above, the distinction is mostly a matter of color.

The spruce on the hummocks look more permanent than they are. Their crowns are narrow, their branches flexible, and their roots broad rather than deep. Pale lichen hangs from them in heavy, wind-combed beards. The trees grow slowly through the dark season and then, in the brief circling light of summer, put on growth with very little pause. A lightning flash may show a stand apparently packed trunk to trunk, but many of those trunks lean on one another or share the same root-bound raft. When one spruce falls, it commonly opens a much larger wound than its own crown occupied. The fallen wood then becomes shelter, fungal substrate, a trap for sediment, and sometimes the beginning of a new channel.

For a few bright weeks, all this crowded growth supports an almost frantic aerial life. Winged insects emerge from ponds, backwaters, and wet moss, though the wind prevents the calm columns and clouds associated with gentler wetlands. Most are immediately carried into lees and hollows. Small birds hunt there at ankle height, picking them from leaves and spider silk. Beetles and other wingless invertebrates work through saturated litter. Where a fallen tree has dammed a trickle, the surface may be dimpled by larvae, small crustaceans, and the fish that come to feed on them.

The small silver fish are creatures of connections rather than of particular ponds. While high water links the hollows, they spread through them. As channels retreat, they crowd into deeper water, their numbers often becoming greatest just before access is cut off. Some populations survive the dark season beneath ice; others are almost entirely lost when a pond freezes too far or drains through gravel. The basin persists as a network of such gains and losses. A pond that appears impoverished one year may be dense with silver backs the next, without any obvious local explanation.

Char are less conspicuous and less easily impressed by abundance. They hold in the seams of slower water beneath banks, behind large cobbles, and where a side current folds back against the main flow. In clear water they take drifting insects and small fish with precise, economical movements. In floodwater, when silt makes sight nearly useless, they keep close to structure and respond to pressure and vibration. They move before a failing channel becomes uninhabitable, but not always before it becomes impassable. Now and then a population is stranded in a pool that warms, shrinks, or fills with gravel. Elsewhere, a newly cut run is occupied within days.

Diving birds work the open leads and slower channels with an absence of ceremony that makes them easy to overlook. A bird surfaces, shakes once, and goes under again. Their plumage is wet at the outer edges almost constantly; their survival depends on what remains dry and warm close to the skin. They follow the silver fish into ponds when water permits and gather where char or other prey are forced into narrower runs. At freeze-up, open water becomes a form of territory more valuable than any nest site.

The smaller birds of the spruce endure storms by becoming almost part of the branches. They grip the leeward sides with disproportionately strong feet, feathers tightened and heads turned away from the driven rain. There is little flocking behavior to see at such times. The flock has been reduced to hundreds of separate acts of holding on. Feeding stops. Calling stops. Birds that lose their perch may be seen once, low over the alder, before the weather closes around them.

Then the wind drops, and the trees seem to come apart into individual lives. Wings are opened and shaken. Birds change branches. Thin contact calls pass from hummock to hummock, sometimes across distances that make the basin sound much larger than it looked in the lightning. These calls are not simply announcements of location. Birds answer, fall silent, and answer again, reestablishing a scattered company after hours in which each may have had no evidence that any others remained.

The otter-like hunters use both worlds, but they are most at ease where water has made travel complicated for everything else. Their smooth slides connect pools, root arches, and favored entries beneath undercut banks. In silty water they hunt close to the bottom, using whiskers and touch as much as sight. A den usually has more than one exit, and an animal may have several dens it uses irregularly. This is not caution in the human sense; it is the ordinary consequence of having entrances repeatedly flooded, buried, or exposed.

Their slides and scent marks are more often encountered than the animals themselves. In a lull, a bark beneath a bank may be answered from the next pool. More often, one animal calls and nothing replies. They take fish, birds, eggs, and small mammals when available, and they investigate carcasses before the river can remove them. Their young learn the basin during summer, when a route can be followed by sight and scent. By the dark season, portions of that route may no longer exist.

The heavy-furred browsers move through the willow in groups that are rarely visible all at once. A branch springs back; wet leaves shiver several metres farther on; there is a hollow sound of a hoof withdrawing from mud. Their outer fur carries water and can look almost black with it, while the underfur slows the loss of heat. Their feet spread their weight on soft ground, but no foot is broad enough to make a floating mat trustworthy. They test questionable surfaces, turn back often, and sometimes choose cold open water over a shorter crossing on apparently solid vegetation.

In summer they take leaves, sedges, and soft new growth. In the dark season they rely more heavily on buds, bark, and whatever evergreen material remains accessible. Feeding close together provides some shelter, and touch becomes important when calls cannot be heard. A young animal may keep contact with its mother’s flank rather than follow her voice. The group’s passage opens temporary runs through the thicket, distributes seed and dung, and breaks willow stems that later root downstream. Their trails are used by smaller animals, and occasionally by people, until rain or flood alters their meaning.

Reed mice live at a finer scale of the same instability. Their nests occupy the dry crowns of sedge tussocks, woven so that rain is carried away from the central chamber. A good nest is lined, cached with food, and provided with more than one way out. A better arrangement is to have several nests. During the worst cold, animals crowd together and remain quiet for long periods; during milder intervals they emerge to feed and repair damage. Floods drown nests, expose them to hunters, or leave them isolated above water. The mice persist not because each tussock is safe, but because the basin contains thousands of tussocks and because summer allows losses to be replaced quickly.

Human beings are only intermittent inhabitants. Small field crews come to maintain instruments, inspect anchors, retrieve records, or wait for work to become possible. They arrive with channel maps and leave with annotations that are already historical. Outdoors, much of their communication is reduced to touch and gesture: stop, test the ground, look upstream, come back. Even experienced workers can be surprised by how completely the river noise occupies thought. After several days, silence does not immediately feel peaceful; it feels exposed.

Their geography is consequently personal and temporary. Bars receive names that outlive the bars. Routes are described by what used to be there. A shelter remains a fixed destination even when its entrance has changed and the approach must be found again. People become attached to particular trees, clear pools, or dry hummocks, and return to find them buried, erased, or standing in the middle of a new channel. No crew knows Sorn in its entirety. At best, someone knows how it behaved during several previous visits.

The shelters and anchors are not alive, but other life has had to accommodate them. Birds perch on the grey posts. Moss enters the quiet texture around their bases. Within an anchor’s protected footprint, willow may grow unusually tall and sediment may accumulate into something resembling a durable bank. Animals treat this firmness as ordinary ground. When the field weakens and the bank is released, the whole small assemblage goes with it: soil, roots, nests, insects, and stored seed. Some is lost. Some lodges downstream and continues.

The installations therefore create refuges only in a limited sense. Mostly, they alter timing. A slope remains intact for another season; a shelter keeps an entrance above one more flood; a bank fails on a chosen afternoon instead of during an unknown night. Sorn’s inhabitants are already equipped to exploit such intervals. A brief delay is long enough for moss to spread, a fish to find a pool, or a willow fragment to put down its first roots.

Nearly everything that lives in Sorn keeps some second possibility: another root system, another den, another pond, another route through the willow. Residence here does not mean remaining in one place. It means remaining within the basin while places are exchanged around you. The lowland is crowded with life, but very few of its inhabitants expect to be found where they were last seen.
