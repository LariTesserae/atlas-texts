Nothing lives in the Rime Measure in the ordinary sense. Even the people who made its instruments cannot properly be called inhabitants. A human being would have been too warm, too wet, and too sheltering to reside there without altering the thing being observed. Breath would have fed rime on a cold seam. A body beside a blade would have narrowed the fog reaching it. A fire, however small, would have created a weather of its own. To live in the Measure would have been to make it measure oneself.

Those who came must therefore have lived elsewhere. It is convenient to call them measurers, though the word suggests a search for single, dependable results, and their work shows almost the opposite concern. They do not appear to have been trying to discover how much water the mountain received. They wanted to know how water behaved differently on neighboring surfaces, under what seemed to be the same sky.

The eleven basins were not eleven copies of one gauge. Each belonged to its slab, its drip, its exposure. A full cup did not simply mean a wet night; an empty one did not simply mean a dry night. Water might have arrived after the cloud withdrew, skipped the liquid state entirely, or vanished from a basin without overflowing. The measurers made no attempt to correct these inconveniences. The inconveniences were the knowledge.

Their records must have been difficult to transmit. The drilled holes and grooves could preserve an act of comparison, but not the whole circumstances that made it intelligible. The notation’s true vocabulary was distributed through the basin: this dark face, that shaded joint, the blade whose lower edge remained frozen after its crown had begun to drip. A successor could not simply inherit a table of marks. Someone had to be taught which differences mattered, and where to stand to see them. When that human chain broke, the marks did not become false. They lost their grammar.

The copper repairs suggest how carefully continuity was kept. A split slab could be fastened and remain itself. Replaced, it would have been clean, strong, and ignorant. The old surface’s fractures, worn hollows, and slight preferences for drainage were part of its history. Even the windbreak served that history. It did not keep a person comfortable; it kept one small record from being erased by windborne grit.

The little clay disc is harder to fit into such discipline. It may have been a counter, a marker, or the last fragment of some practical object. It may also have been personal: the one thing left there that did not need to work. Its presence prevents the measurers from becoming entirely a method. At least once, someone may have closed a hand around something small before leaving it behind.

Their restraint was not necessarily worship. Nothing requires that they imagined a god in the summit or desired blessing from its water. There was too little water to sustain a settlement, and too little shelter to reward conquest. The installations suggest a rarer motive: that an exact understanding of an inhospitable place was worth repeated journeys, provided the understanding did not overwhelm the place. Their most important act may always have been departure.

Between visits, and now between whatever visits still occur, the instrument has continued without them. Fog touches the cairns and enters along its old levels. Some cups receive moisture directly; others wait for delayed drips from high stone. Runnels accept only excess. Ice seals one basin while another empties into grit. No new marks are made, but the arrangement still separates events that would otherwise be indistinguishable. It has outlived observation without becoming inert.

If the basin can be said to have a changing population, that population is water in its temporary forms. Rime occupies the cold edges in white, granular shelves. Clear ice inhabits joints and sealed pockets. Black films appear briefly on sun-warmed mineral seams. Drops gather in quartz irregularities, hang, fall, and disappear. Vapor passes through without leaving any visible sign of its passage.

These are not generations. Nothing begets anything else, and no form struggles to preserve itself. A drop ceases to be a drop when it enters a cap of ice; ice thins directly into air while nearby rime is still growing. Even the longer-lasting ice is not stationary in the way the blades are stationary. Its surfaces gain and lose, its boundaries shift, and the water within the basin is always changing jurisdiction. The stone provides residence without providing rest.

Grit has a slower population of its own. It collects in level pans, fills old fractures, and is removed grain by grain along routes too small to watch. But the stone flour does not respond to the night as water does. It belongs to the Measure’s long memory, whereas fog, melt, and frost belong to its brief attention.

At first light, after a night no one witnessed, the eleven cups can still disagree. One may be empty without a stain of overflow. Another may hold ice although no cloud reached it directly. Around them, thousands of blades keep their separate accounts in white shelves, dark films, and tightening seams.

That disagreement is the closest thing the Rime Measure has to a native. It is not a creature concealed among the stones, nor a spirit requiring the human work to have been devotion. It is the recurrent fact that no single condition rules the whole basin. The measurers gave that fact a few vessels, a protected table, and a means of being compared. They could not remain with it. The water cannot remain either.

But the comparison continues. By morning the water has moved, the surfaces have changed their minds, and the old question—asked without hands now, and answered without words—is still awake in the cups.
