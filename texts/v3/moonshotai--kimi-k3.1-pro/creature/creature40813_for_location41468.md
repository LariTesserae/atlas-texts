## The Inhabitants of the Night Engine

Most of what lives in the Night Engine cannot be found by looking for movement.

At any one time, much of its animal population exists as eggs, encysted larvae, dry-bodied burrowers, or adults pressed so still against warm stone that they seem part of the crust. Even the microbial films can spend long periods indistinguishable from mineral staining. Dormancy is not an emergency here. It is the ordinary condition, interrupted by feeding.

The deepest and most nearly continuous life lives along boundaries: where brine touches sulfide, where a graphite vein narrows through insulating rock, where a crystal face has contracted enough to open a microscopic seam. These microbial veils are less carpets than populations of joints and edges. Dry, they resemble weathering. Wet and faintly charged, they extend filaments, exchange ions, divide, and lay down another hairline of mineral. Some use the chemical differences produced by the current; others appear to draw directly on the gradient itself. Their dead become food. Their precipitates become shelter. A seam may be inhabited, abandoned, mineralized, and inhabited again without ever becoming visible as more than a discoloration in the stone.

The lichens build upon this activity with almost geological slowness. They spread sideways rather than upward, following the leeward grain of the rock. A crust the size of a hand may contain hundreds of wetting and drying bands, each one altering its density by almost nothing. Slatebacks prefer certain old crusts, not because they are thicker, but because their microbial communities are easier to digest. Around frequently grazed outcrops, the lichens have become patchier and faster to recolonize. The grazers create their own pasture without ever tending it.

Ribbon plants occupy deeper fractures. Their conductive vessels draw charge away from tender growing tips, while their narrow leaves turn edge-on to the warm wind. In the dry phase they are brittle enough to snap under a beetle’s foot, but the apparent dead leaf often remains attached to living tissue below. After rain, the plant darkens, softens, and releases spores from seams along its lower surface. The rosettes are more self-contained. Each cups a little condensation at its center and holds it against a waxy, inward-folded leaf. That water is never merely water. It contains airborne spores, dissolved salts, microscopic grazers, and sometimes the eggs of a creature that will spend its entire life within the rosette. An overflow can carry this small community into a fracture, where the blind runners find it.

The dew beetles are the most conspicuous inhabitants of the dry intervals, though they often do nothing conspicuous for days. Their shells are ridged into channels with different wetting properties. Moisture beads on the high surfaces, gathers in the grooves, and runs toward the mouth when the beetle changes its posture. Some species direct the first water not to themselves but to a brood groove beneath the abdomen. Others simply drink and then remain motionless, allowing their shell to continue collecting water that evaporates from them more slowly than it would from bare stone.

They gather on mineral outcrops because crystals condense moisture before the surrounding rock does. An outcrop may appear knobbed and irregular until a vibration passes through the ground and scores of apparently mineral lumps adjust their legs. Dead shells remain among the living. They still collect dew, and young beetles shelter beneath them. In this way, even a beetle’s abandoned body continues the work of its anatomy.

Below the outcrops, the fissures have their own pastures and hunters. Blind runners move through them on flattened, many-jointed bodies, some small enough to pass beneath a loosened scale of lichen. Their sensory bristles respond to vibration, dissolved minerals, and slight differences in charge. A slateback’s distant stamp can freeze a whole fissure population. The runners feed on spores, washed-in crusts, dead eggs, shed skins, and smaller runners. Some seldom encounter open air. Their world consists entirely of surfaces: the underside of a flake, the wet side of a crystal, the narrow route around a grain too large to cross.

The flat burrowers wait higher up, beneath dust. A dormant one may be recognizable only by a shallow rim where its body has pressed the powder outward, and by a breathing pore no wider than a grain. Minerals slowly accumulate along its skin. When the right combination of moisture and ground charge arrives, the body swells, the rim breaks, and an animal that seemed little more than a fossil crawls out to feed with frantic efficiency. It may eat, mate, and bury a new generation before the last pool water has gone. Then it digs down again, taking a little organic richness with it into the dust.

Rain does not wake everything merely by making things wet. Dew would otherwise cause disastrous false starts. Many pool eggs respond only when water is accompanied by the electrical signature of a broad, connected brine film. Even then, only part of a buried brood hatches. The rest remain sealed. A brief storm can therefore produce abundance without gambling the basin’s entire future.

The first hatchlings graze on awakened films and on the organic dust suspended in the spreading water. Tiny crustaceans follow, beating through the black silt with limbs that also strain food from it. Hunting worms uncoil from mud pockets and search for the irregular muscular flicker of injured or struggling prey. The pool clouds with bodies while remaining too shallow to cover a slateback’s foot.

Some of the crustaceans mate by touch and release a single chemical flash close to the body. The light does not illuminate the pool. It barely reaches through the silt. It is less a beacon than a final confirmation between two animals already touching. Hunting worms sometimes converge on these flashes, so courtship often takes place half buried in mud.

The dark-legged waders arrive while the basin is still filling. They ride the pressure changes ahead of the storm, then land along the margins with their long wings folded and their mineral-sheathed legs trailing loose mud from the last basin they visited. That mud often carries eggs. Waders are among the few creatures that regularly exchange life between otherwise isolated pools.

They feed by touch, probing and snapping through the silt with narrow, sensitive mouths. As the water shrinks, prey becomes concentrated and feeding grows easier. The waders remain through this brief excess, then depart before the basin closes completely. Some leave eggs in the deepest mud; others carry their next brood within them until another storm offers a suitable place. The clicking of their feeding, the ticking of crustacean shells, and the small wing movements at the margin are usually the loudest things the pool ever produces.

The slatebacks approach more slowly.

An adult is broad, low, and carried on six splayed legs. Its flank folds open during sustained grazing, shedding heat along their dark inner surfaces. The mineral plates over its shoulders grow in irregular layers and are scratched by old outcrops, courser claws, and the occasional rubbing of another slateback. Its small eyes can register the brownish dim phase and little else. Footpads, jaw sensors, and the resonant bones of its legs provide a more useful picture of the world.

A slateback does not so much bite vegetation as unmake a very thin part of the landscape. The rasping surface of its lower mouth removes crust, weathered mineral, and microbial film together. Symbionts in its long gut separate what can be used. Young animals feed first where their mother has grazed, taking in her saliva and the microbes already established on the freshly exposed surface. They inherit a digestive community before they can manage a full range of crusts.

A herd is not always visible as a gathering. Dozens of animals may be spread across country so broad that no one body can sense all the others directly. Their stamps pass farther than sight or scent. A stamp is less a word than a disturbance introduced into a shared medium: one animal pauses, another answers, several turn their feet toward better-conducting ground. Direction emerges from these hesitations and replies. A calf learns the herd’s ordinary pulses before it learns the full grazing circuit.

Slatebacks carry smaller provinces with them. Mites live in the warm radiating folds, feeding on shed tissue and salt. Beetle larvae shelter beneath lifting plate edges. Spores lodge in plate crevices, and dung deposits gut microbes beside new grazing scars. When a slateback dies, its soft body disappears quickly, but portions of its shoulder armor may remain for a long time, collecting dew and sheltering rosettes. The animal continues to alter moisture after death, just as the beetle does.

Coursers inhabit the same signal world from the other side of it.

Their long legs seem excessive on level ground until one crosses a fissure without breaking stride or stands motionless while warm air moves beneath its narrow body. Sensory tissue along the feet and lower face reads the electrical untidiness of injured muscle. A courser can detect an animal that is limping before the limp becomes obvious, or feel the agitation passing through a scattered herd before the herd breaks into flight.

It often hunts by waiting for irregularity: an old slateback slow to answer a stamp, a calf separated from its mother’s grazing path, a body whose charge no longer fits smoothly into the background. Sometimes the courser runs. Sometimes it merely keeps a group moving until weakness becomes legible. When several coursers converge, they do not necessarily cooperate. Each may simply be reading the same disturbance, then adjusting to the others’ pace and position.

The kill is usually brief. Dust receives most of the sound. Afterward, the surviving slatebacks gradually resume their pulses, and the herd’s movement closes over the absence.

Hushwings find the carcass by scent carried on warm rising air. Their membrane wings make almost no noise in flight; the soft leathery report of folding is often the first sign that they have arrived. They feed, quarrel with their bodies rather than their voices, and carry scraps back to fissure nurseries or the few hard-rock cliffs suitable for roosting.

Those roosts are fertile in the upland’s restricted sense. Hushwing droppings collect in cracks, ribbon plants establish below them, and blind runners gather to consume what washes down. The cliff becomes a localized province of unusually rich life. During mating, a hushwing may open one wing slightly and produce a dim flash from a gland near its flank. The light is visible only to the animal pressed close beside it. The roost as a whole remains dark.

Nothing at a carcass is allowed to remain a single kind of thing for long. Coursers take muscle; hushwings take what they can tear and carry; beetles enter the drying cavities; microbial veils spread along moist bone and spilled gut contents. Plate mites leave their dying host for the legs of scavengers. Some pool eggs and plant spores travel the same way. Even widely separated bodies serve as connections between mineral provinces that water joins only rarely.

Because every region is out of phase with the others, there is no single scene that represents the upland. A slateback herd may take longer to change direction than a small pool needs to hatch, hunt, breed, evaporate, and return to salt. While one basin clicks with feeding waders, another remains sealed beneath mud that has not softened in centuries. Somewhere a courser is eating; somewhere else a calf is taking its first mouthful of inoculated crust; elsewhere a beetle has done nothing all day but allow one droplet to travel down its back.

This unevenness is part of what keeps the place inhabited. Not every egg hatches. Not every sleeper wakes. Not every spore germinates. A weak rain kills some of the lives it summons, but it cannot easily exhaust the reserve. The failure of one basin does not become the failure of the whole shield.

The inhabitants are therefore not simply the few shapes standing on the stone. They are also the runners beneath it, the veils within its seams, the mites in a grazer’s folds, the eggs in mud on a wader’s legs, and the dry generations sealed under salt. The beetle’s shell, the egg’s refusal to open, the immense spacing of the herd, the courser’s patience, and the hushwing’s silent flight are all answers to the same condition: connection is fruitful, but it cannot be assumed to last.

That is the Night Engine’s true abundance—not everything awake, not everything joined, but always somewhere a body ready to answer water, and somewhere else another body capable of waiting past it.
