## The High Cistern, Inhabited

An ordinary house in the High Cistern is occupied before its human residents are counted. Swifts cling beneath the catchment roof. Bats have found the expansion joint behind the stair flue. The courtyard overflow is paved with snails, and something transparent turns once in the ground-floor sump when the gate opens. The family lives above all this, behind copper screens, in rooms whose dryness depends on knowing which vents to open and which to keep shut. They do not expect the house to be empty of other life. They expect it to be sorted.

Sorting is endless and approximate. The same snail is livestock on a harvest tile, a blockage in a filter throat, and ceramic aggregate once its shell has been collected and ground. An eel is dinner in a trap and a catastrophe in a pump intake. A swift colony is welcome above an insect-rich sump until its droppings begin entering a catchment. Even bell-throats change category as they move: reservoir stock, avenue traffic, culvert damage. There are relatively few animals that the residents consider vermin in all circumstances. There are a great many circumstances in which an otherwise useful animal must be killed, discouraged, screened out, or scraped away.

The human inhabitants are no more uniformly patient than people anywhere else. There are gauge cheats, tile snobs, dishonest cull inspectors, herd sentimentalists, and children who must repeatedly be prevented from poking eels with wire. A resident may complain bitterly about a tram delayed for ten minutes and then accept, with only ordinary swearing, that a herd crossing will keep the same tram stationary until Thursday. What they share is not serenity but a practical literacy in levels, currents, sounds, and animal behavior.

An experienced resident can tell from the first cup at a public tap whether a filter is merely dirty or beginning to fail. A change in the knock of a pipe may mean air, loose ironwork, or a narrowing of stone. Children learn the overflow routes of their own courtyards before they learn the larger street plan. They know which warm sumps can be entered safely, which produce biting swarms after dusk, and where juvenile bell-throats lie motionless enough to be mistaken for submerged stone. Lowland visitors often exhaust themselves on the stairs and then wonder why everyone else works with such deliberate economy. The thin, warm air teaches pacing. Children run anyway, and pay for it.

Dryness is class. A wealthy household has upper rooms that remain dry through several days of rain, inner walls faced with glazed tile, screens fitted closely enough that they do not rattle, and water that can be drawn clear without waiting for the first vessel to run through. Its bedding smells of hot mineral fibre and clean hide rather than mildew and roost ammonia. Its wealth is audible as well as visible: no erratic dripping, no failing pump behind the wall, only the regular labor of machinery whose maintenance somebody else has been paid to anticipate.

Silence itself is not especially desirable. Silence may mean that something has stopped.

Poorer households often live closer to the fertile water. An outfall ward may eat well while its walls seep. Snails and eels can be abundant there; curd cultures may be vigorous; rendered oil may be cheap. Food is not always the principal deprivation. Dry bedding, uncontaminated storage, and a room in which the number of other inhabitants can actually be controlled are harder to obtain. A clean reservoir ward may be socially respectable and culinarily dull, dependent on vat cake and imported curd, while a rank industrial outfall supports crowded markets and several fierce arguments over who has the right to harvest it.

Cooks make much of distinctions that outsiders miss. There are snail broths almost black with oil and others pale enough to show the bottom of a metal cup. Eel may be crisped, potted, smoked over ceramic grilles, or eaten fresh from particular sluices whose water is considered sweet. Microbial curds range from soft and sharply sour to dense grey blocks that keep through a long crossing. Herd meat is associated less with luxury than with occasion and paperwork: a cull, a death, a distribution authorized after some long delay. The dense vat-protein cakes eaten every day are judged by texture, smell, and how much oil they can be persuaded to hold. Nobody praises them by calling them natural.

The city’s most respected skills are often diagnostic. A good pump wright can distinguish a worn bearing from an unsuitable intake by listening through several walls. Some old keepers, deaf to ordinary speech after years in compressor houses, can still locate a fault through the soles of their feet. Scale-cutters working beneath bridges learn to read tufa: where it is only an apron, where it has rooted itself in a crack, and where removing it quickly would bring down more than scale. Screen-smiths, filter setters, roost keepers, herd clerks, and sluice crews all possess forms of knowledge that look narrow until their absence becomes district-wide.

This does not make maintenance a harmonious civic priesthood. Crews curse offices that specify the wrong brick. Offices suspect crews of exaggerating breakage to obtain new metalwork. A scraping gang may be paid to clean stone that an outfall keeper has spent years making fertile. Reservoir officials want water clear; sump harvesters want it rich; roost wardens want insects controlled without allowing bats into the hospital wards. A bad decision entered into a durable district plan can keep generations of people damp.

The bell-throats stand outside most of these disputes by being too large, too long-lived, and too consequential to fit neatly within them. They are not tame, though they are administered. They are not quite property, though they are counted, culled, and eaten. Their routes have a practical authority older than many deeds. A street may be legally well planned and still spend several days each decade under water, feet, and slate-dark bodies.

Herd offices keep their records on hide leaves, fired-clay slips, and metal plates durable enough to survive changes in staff and orthography. A single animal may accumulate a working name, a route designation, and several nicknames bestowed by successive generations. No clerk expects to know the whole life of the animal being described. Knowledge is handed onward imperfectly: a pale jaw scar, a preference for one side of a flooded quarry, an old injury that changes the way water sheets from the left foot. The records are full of corrections made by people long dead.

When a herd enters a ward, private inconvenience flourishes alongside public procedure. Food sellers move onto the steps. Children crowd balconies. People whose work lies on the other side of the route discover how little civic explanations help them. Sluice keepers hold or release water according to instructions that may be obsolete by the time the last animals arrive. Behind the herd come inspectors, dung crews, shell collectors, and people simply checking whether their doors still open. Most accidents occur when someone mistakes deliberation for docility. A bell-throat is not usually malicious. It is heavy, its attention is elsewhere, and the architecture has been designed to yield before it does.

The young in canals are less impressive and often less predictable. Residents learn to give them room without treating them as monsters. Their presence is one reason canal gratings are built as structures rather than covers. Something small enough to enter a drainage basin may not remain small enough to leave by the same route.

At night, herd listeners claim they can distinguish distance, direction, and sometimes individual animals by the way calls arrive through stone. Whether bell-throats are answering pumping stations, responding to vibrations that resemble one another’s calls, or merely calling at hours when human listeners notice them most is a continuing professional argument. The belief that they answer persists because it is useful to nobody and satisfying to almost everybody.

Below the herds in size, though not in consequence, are the tended multitudes. Outfall keepers manage microbial films much as other peoples might manage pasture, except that pasture here has no roots, no season of flowering, and no necessary relationship with sunlight. They divert warm flow, rest exhausted patches, and introduce living felt to newly prepared stone. They know which colors and textures promise a good snail crop, which will foul a culture vat, and which indicate water that should be avoided regardless of how many animals it attracts. A well-meaning sanitation crew can destroy several livelihoods in an afternoon by scrubbing an outfall back to clean limestone.

Harvesting is often built directly into the waterwork. Fired-clay tiles laid in a channel become snail-encrusted slabs that can be lifted and replaced. Ceramic eel traps are set where a sluice opening will briefly concentrate movement. Shell is collected from gratings, middens, and abandoned pools, then sold back to the ceramic works. Winged insect columns are encouraged near certain factory roofs and suppressed near dwellings. Swift ledges and bat joints may be cleaned, relocated, screened, or leased according to the value of insect control, guano, and sleep.

None of this produces balance in the sense of stillness. It produces crowded arrangements with known failure points. A capped chimney starves one roost and causes another to form in a wall ventilator. A newly efficient filter removes food from a channel and leaves its eel traps empty. A bell-throat herd opens a route through silt and cracks three culverts while doing so. Residents can become attached to particular animals and still cull their populations without feeling hypocritical. Sentiment does not exempt a structure from inspection.

Human lives are fitted into this longer accounting without being wholly subdued by it. Family chronologies borrow dates from public works: a birth during the replacement of a lower gauge, a marriage in the year of the long crossing, a death before some promised reservoir stage was finally reached. The first clear draw after a major overhaul may be passed around in cups and tasted critically. People praise the water, complain that the pump still sounds rough, and argue over whether the old filter was really as bad as the office claimed.

Burial is difficult where cuttings seep and catchments must be protected, so many wards cremate their dead and keep the ashes in glazed urns or ceramic memorials in dry upper arcades. Others attach more importance to durable maintenance records than to monuments. A name on an inspection plate, a sound decision in an old plan, or a route that remains passable because somebody once insisted on building the arch higher can constitute a respectable afterlife. None of these guarantees remembrance. They merely give usefulness a longer reach than memory.

Residents are not thereby relieved of urgency. A blocked screen must be cleared now. A child must be pulled from a channel now. A cracked main cannot wait for the century in which its replacement was scheduled. The slow frame teaches them to distinguish what cannot be hurried from what is merely being neglected. Much of the city’s politics lives in that distinction.

Late at night, in an upper room, a child may wake because a familiar pump has stopped. For a moment the smaller sounds come forward: gutters ticking, a bat shifting in its joint, water finding the courtyard overflow, shells clicking beneath a grating. Somewhere below, an adult is already pulling on hide boots. No one yet knows whether the stoppage will last an hour or enter the district records under a name of its own. But someone will go to look, if only to establish whose silence it is.

Far away, a bell-throat calls across an unseen reservoir. After a while, another answers, or seems to. The child turns over and goes back to sleep.
