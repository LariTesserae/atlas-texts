A census of the Old Choir depends on when it is taken, and on whether one counts a skeleton as part of the organism that made it.

The overwhelming resident is the builder itself. It does not live inside the pale shells in the way an animal lives inside a burrow. The shells are not its houses, and the lanes do not lead toward some hidden chamber where the colony keeps its essential self. They are the accumulated body around which its living portion continues, retreats, and continues again. Most of what can be seen is dead. Most of what is alive is thin enough to overlook.

On a dry afternoon, that living portion may be little more than olive discoloration beneath a lip, a dark softness in a fold, a narrow margin where new mineral meets old. It clings to the outside of its own history. In some places the film is continuous across several shells; elsewhere it narrows along a damp seam and ends against pavement that has apparently been dry for years. The ordinary distinction between one inhabitant and many is difficult to apply. The colony is more easily mapped by moisture than by ancestry.

Its activity is likewise easy to miss. Cloud-water darkens it. Minerals arrive in dust, in solution, in the slow weathering of the surface beneath it. Tissue spreads; another grain is fixed; another paper-thin band is added around a pocket of air. Then the basin dries. Exposed growth pales and contracts. At night it freezes. Nothing about these pauses resembles sleep in an animal, but neither are they necessarily death. Much of the builder’s life consists of surviving intervals in which growth is impossible.

What it has done remains visible after what it is doing has become undetectable. A swollen green-stained band remembers a succession of clouded seasons. A seam of almost nothing records years in which persistence was the most it could manage. Its walls are therefore not simply supports but a kind of material recollection. New tissue grows over the record without needing to read it.

Vibration enters that record too. The colony need not hear the Old Choir in order to be shaped by its sounds. A rib repeatedly stressed by resonance thickens. A violent whistle narrows as growth accumulates around its opening. A chamber that merely holds a note may remain hollow for centuries; one that tears in storms is more likely to be closed, braced, or abandoned. The result resembles an audience’s gradual influence upon music, but there is no audience and no preferred harmony. The noise is weather acting on a body, and the body’s response is persistence.

Everything else lives in the exceptions to its dryness.

For the mosses, the Old Choir is not a basin or a borough. It is a seep line: a particular darkness spreading through a cushion after condensation, a depth of wetness that lasts slightly longer here than there. Their cushions pack into the seams until the mineral surface disappears. The tiny ferns occupy a still narrower version of the place, one in which shelter, water, and a little accumulated organic matter coincide. The liverworts know the basins as temporary ribbons of gloss and submersion. Red spore stalks rise where the mats remain wettest. Behind a perforated wall, the whole settlement may be reduced to one sheltered opening and the curtain of green depending around it.

These lives are crowded, but their crowdedness has sharp boundaries. A fern’s world can end within a pace. Beyond it lies gray pavement, wind, and a dryness that no amount of nearby architectural complexity makes habitable. After a wetting, the green zones deepen in succession as water reaches them. During drought, color and apparent activity withdraw beneath overhangs and into cracks. The inhabitants do not spread through the courts merely because space is available. Space is the one thing the Old Choir has in abundance.

The translucent snail belongs to the wet films rather than to the shells. Its occasional passage is so slight that it seems less like the movement of an animal through a settlement than a brief change in the settlement’s surface. It grazes; its faint trail begins, fades, and resumes where the film remains suitable. When the surfaces dry, it disappears from notice. There is no accumulation of shells in corners, no trail-worn network connecting the courts. Even its eventual mineral remainder may become indistinguishable from the colony’s fabric. It leaves no evidence that could be mistaken for a population.

In the warmest part of the year, one sheltered basin may briefly become more than a reservoir. A handful of midge larvae appears in water that otherwise contains only reflected sky and black depth. Their presence gives the pool an interior. Above them, beneath the stone rim, waits the pale spider.

For the spider, there is no reason to cross the basin, explore the arcades, or enter the towers. Its inhabited world is the edge between shelter and open water. Whatever reaches that surface may be food; whatever does not is effectively elsewhere. It lives beside a court without occupying the court, beneath a roof that was never built for shelter, at the center of a food web so slight that calling it a web almost overstates it. In most basins, even this much does not happen.

Nothing larger has made the Old Choir its own. A bird may pass over it and for an instant give an empty court the shadow of wings. A call may rise from the slopes below and return from several shells, seeming to come from within them. But there are no nests in the high galleries, no bones beneath the arcades, no habitual tracks between the pools. The towers are available and useless. Their height, shelter, and complexity do not add up to the things a larger animal would require.

Before dawn, when the accessible water has frozen and the wind has momentarily stopped, the entire population can seem to have been withdrawn. The plants are dark and still. The snail is nowhere to be seen. The spider waits without any visible sign of waiting. The builder is a contraction in the cold, an occasional tick passing through stone. This is not the silence of abandonment. It is the silence of lives whose active periods do not necessarily coincide.

Each resident, in that sense, occupies a different Old Choir. The midge larvae inhabit one basin during part of one warm season. The spider inhabits its rim. The snail inhabits a wetness that appears and vanishes. The mosses inhabit seep lines and bright afternoons of absorption. The builder inhabits mineral surfaces, connected hollows, and centuries.

The sound belongs to none of them. It is made by wind, water, and ice passing through the dead and living fabric of the colony. Yet over long periods the colony alters the conditions of that passage, closing one throat and leaving another open. The chord changes; the resident remains.

What lives in the Old Choir is mostly edge: living tissue on the edge of stone, green growth at the edge of water, brief animal life at the edge of a basin that usually holds nothing. Around those edges are air, skeleton, and cold. The place is not loudly alive because it is crowded. It is loud because the weather must continually pass through the body of the one inhabitant that has learned, without learning anything, how to remain.
