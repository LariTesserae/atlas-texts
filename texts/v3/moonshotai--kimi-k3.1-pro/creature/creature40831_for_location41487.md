No one lives on the crowns.

There are no burrows beneath them, no nests in the seams, no shy population that emerges when a visitor turns away. The High Clamor has no people, no herds, no resident hunters, and no hidden abundance behind its apparent sterility. To speak of its inhabitants, one must abandon almost everything visible about the place.

The inhabited map of the basins would not resemble their physical map. It would ignore the rims, the broad crowns, and the succession of hollows catching the light. It would mark instead a few narrow chemical boundaries: where seepage from deep within the rock still carries usable iron or sulfur; where oxygen reaches that seepage without immediately dispersing it; where the current is strong enough to renew the water but not strong enough to strip everything from the stone. A pool might be broad, sheltered, and beautiful, and contain nothing. A stained slot no wider than a hand might support life for generations.

The first inhabitants are the films.

They are not continuous skins, and they do not spread simply because wet rock is available. Some occupy only the downstream side of a mineral grain. Others form in pits too small to be seen without magnification, or along the submerged lips of overhangs where seepage and aerated water mix. A colony may be extensive in microscopic area and still amount to almost nothing on the stone.

Some of the cells obtain energy by transforming iron. Others use sulfur. Still others persist on the sparse chemical products of their neighbors. The cloud, the intense light, and the white glare of the crowns mean nothing to them. Their nourishment arrives from below, in water that has never needed the sun. Cold slows them, but it also allows the water to hold the oxygen many of them require.

Their growth is limited less by space than by supply. There is no shortage of stone on which they might theoretically live. There is an almost total shortage of stone where living would repay the effort. Thus they do not advance across the basin as vegetation would. They appear at the few places where a chemical imbalance can be maintained, persist there, and vanish when that balance shifts.

Even where they are alive, they rarely look organic. Some are transparent. Some are no more than a faint slickness within a roughness of the quartz. Iron-transforming cells may become lightly armored in the precipitates their activity helps produce, so that the only sign of their presence is a ochre line indistinguishable, to the eye, from an ordinary mineral stain. Dead films leave still less behind. The rock resumes its appearance of having always been bare.

Within the deeper seepage there are cells that never reach even this marginal exposure. They cling to crystal faces or persist in microscopic intervals of water, dividing slowly and contributing almost nothing to the pools above. They are not a secret multitude. The same scarcity prevails downward. Life follows the chemistry, and the chemistry is spare.

Where a surface film endures long enough, it may support one of the folded grazers.

These are the only inhabitants large enough to be recognized as animals without assistance, and even then recognition is not immediate. A small one looks like a thicken­ing in the water beneath a stone. A large one, approaching the length of a finger, might first be taken for a clear fragment of organic debris—except that there is almost no organic debris with which to compare it.

The body is broad, flexible, and usually much flatter than it appears at rest. Its back contains no shell and almost no pigment. The mineral beneath it can often be seen through its tissues. When it has recently fed, a faint branching cloudiness marks the gut; at other times there is little to distinguish interior from exterior except a subtle difference in the way light passes through.

Its outline is not fixed. Crossing an uneven grain, it can spread until part of the body seems to have no thickness at all. Entering a constriction, it narrows. If the current changes suddenly, the margins draw inward and the whole animal contracts into a smooth, heavy-looking lens. This is the folded posture in which it is most often found, secured beneath an overhang with its back close to the stone.

The underside is its means of remaining alive. A broad adhesive surface, aided by fine ciliated structures, allows it to hold against surfaces that would offer little purchase to a shell or a claw. It does not grip by a single muscular attachment that can be torn free all at once. Contact is made and remade across numerous small areas. When it moves, only a narrow portion releases at a time. The front margin is set, the body drawn forward, and the hold renewed.

Hence its deliberate slowness. It is not merely conserving energy, though it has little energy to spare. It is avoiding the one accident from which there may be no recovery. A grazer detached in a sheltered cup may be able to fold, settle, and fasten itself again. One carried through several constrictions and out into an active seam is likely to enter water where no film exists. It cannot cross a dry crown to return. It cannot swim upstream through the basin’s connected violence.

The animal has no eyes. It has nothing that corresponds to an ear, and no use for a call. Its forward sensory structures respond to current, contact, and minute differences in the chemistry of the water. The total roar of the High Clamor is too constant to provide it with news. What matters is whether pressure has increased slightly along one edge, whether oxygen has diminished beneath the overhang, whether the seep at the back of the cavity has strengthened or begun to fail.

Its feeding is correspondingly minute. A ventral mouth works over the film with a fine rasping action, removing cells in quantities too small and too dispersed to leave a visible trail. The animal does not clear a patch and move on. It works the same limited surfaces repeatedly, sometimes allowing a grazed area to recover before crossing it again. It does not cultivate the film in any deliberate sense, but its continued existence depends on not exhausting the little that is there.

Most individuals probably spend nearly their whole lives within a single cavity. This is not territorial fidelity. The stone is not defended, and there is no nest at its center. The animal remains because the habitable world ends close by. Beyond one edge of the overhang, the current may be too strong. Beyond another, the seep’s contribution disappears. A neighboring pool, only a short distance away as water travels, may be chemically empty.

Nor are the grazers social in any way that would make a colony visible. Two beneath the same stone may pass long intervals without contact. They do not cooperate in feeding, and they do not gather in response to danger. Their solitude is not preference or principle. It is simply the ordinary consequence of being few, small, and unable to travel safely.

Reproduction has no season. There is no warming month, no hatch, no sudden cloud of young in the pools. Eggs are produced sparingly and fixed in protected recesses where moving water can reach them without scouring them away. They are clear and easily overlooked. The young emerge without a free-swimming stage, already possessing the flattened form and adhesive underside they will need. Nothing in their life cycle is adapted for deliberate dispersal.

Dispersal nevertheless occurs, because water occasionally carries an animal where it did not intend to go. Nearly all such journeys must end in unsuitable water. A very few end in another habitable cavity. This is enough, across great spans of time, to account for the distribution: a hundred pools without a grazer, then one pool with a single animal folded beneath a stone. Its presence does not mean that the pool is generally fertile. It may mean only that a rare accident once succeeded there and that the seep has remained favorable since.

When a grazer dies, there is no enduring remainder. No shell settles into a cup. No bones collect in a fissure. The tissues lose their transparency and then their outline, and the films use what they can. The animal’s entire residence may leave no trace that could be distinguished from a slight variation in an old mineral stain.

Yet the inhabitants do not experience the High Clamor as the changeless place it appears to be from above. For them, events occur. A seep weakens. The chemical boundary beneath an overhang narrows. A film that had persisted on one face of a cavity becomes sparse while another face grows marginally richer. A shift too small to alter the sound of a seam can mean starvation, abundance, or the end of a local line of descent. Their calamities and opportunities take place entirely below the threshold at which the landscape seems to have stopped changing.

They therefore have histories, though nothing in the basins records them. An individual may grow, feed, repair an injury, reproduce, and age while the same white thread of water flashes over the same rounded lip outside its refuge. The pattern of the place repeats. The animal does not repeat; it persists, and persistence is not the same thing.

Nothing about it makes the High Clamor more hospitable. It does not clear the water, stabilize the stone, or maintain the films on which the next generation might depend. It is not a guardian of the basin, and the basin is not arranged for its protection. The current that feeds it can also tear it away. The seep that sustains it can fail without any visible alteration above.

Its whole world may be the underside of one stone: a few handspans of wet mineral, a direction of flow, a faint chemical gradient, and the dimming of usable film toward the edges. Beyond that lies not a larger territory but an increasing impossibility.

Sometimes, beneath such a stone, the animal extends its forward margin into the current. It holds there while water passes over and through the sensory structures along its edge. Then it fastens itself a fraction farther on and draws the clear body after it. The movement makes no sound that survives the roar and leaves no mark on the rock.

The High Clamor does not register it. Nothing gleams differently; no seam changes its note. But for that small interval, in that one cavity, the place is not merely being endured by water and stone. Something alive is reading the water, finding it sufficient, and keeping its hold.
