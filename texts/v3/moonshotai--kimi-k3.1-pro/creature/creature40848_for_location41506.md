The builders are beavers, but to call them the basin’s engineers is only half true. Engineering suggests that the place exists first in the mind. No beaver knows the basin whole. Each knows a small country of water with extraordinary precision: the depth at the lodge entrance, the turn where the current strengthens after rain, the submerged branch that can be gripped in darkness, the gap through which a kit can escape, the route home when an owl crosses the roofs.

At dusk, a canal that appeared empty begins to carry dark, deliberate shapes. A beaver’s broad body is cumbersome-looking on a muddy path but exact in the water. Its hind feet drive it forward; its tail steers, braces, stores fat, and, when danger comes, strikes the surface with a report that sends other animals down. Dense fur holds a layer of air against the cold. Ears and nostrils close. The lips can shut behind the iron-orange incisors, allowing the animal to cut and carry stems underwater without flooding its mouth. Those teeth never stop growing. Work keeps them worn; work also keeps the animal alive.

Most of its life is spent close to a few submerged doors. Inside the lodge, beyond the black water and the rising passage, there is a chamber lined with shredded vegetation and wood fragments. It smells of wet fur, sap, silt, and the sweet decay of old bedding. Here a breeding pair shelters with its young, sometimes alongside older offspring not yet driven or drawn away. Kits are born into a city already full of sounds: water worrying at a wall, teeth working somewhere inside the structure, bodies entering and leaving through the flooded passage, the faint shifting of branches under ice.

The young learn no plan of the basin. They learn entrances, depths, edible stems, safe shadows, and the particular habits of their family’s water. They follow, mouth at stems, push inadequately at mud, worry at a branch already being moved by an adult. Later they will repair a leak before they could be said to understand what caused it. Experience remains lodged in the body: in the resistance of a well-wedged stone, the angle at which a stem holds against flow, the feel of a bank that is solid and one that is about to slump.

Much construction begins with something immediately sensed. Water pulls too strongly through a gap. A food cache approaches the surface as a pond falls. A familiar route becomes shallow enough to expose a back. A chamber wall softens. One animal starts pushing material into place; another, arriving for its own reasons, may add more. What looks from above like coordinated public work is often a sequence of private necessities overlapping in the same current.

This does not make the basin peaceful. Families defend feeding grounds and lodge approaches. Scent marks on muddy heaps announce occupancy more durably than any boundary of stones. Stems are taken from unattended piles. A neighbour’s dam may flood a favourite willow bed; another’s neglected opening may drain a canal. Animals abandon chambers, quarrel at narrow crossings, or work within sight of one another without joining efforts. There is no civic agreement. Water level is the nearest thing to law, because it overrules territories and enters every house.

The family lodge is therefore both home and archive, though an untidy one. Its lower passages may lead into chambers long sealed with silt. A new floor lies above an old collapse. Fresh willow smells sharply beside wood that has been waterlogged for years. The young grow up among additions whose reasons they do not know. Some routes remain in use after the animal that first made them has died; others vanish in a single thaw. When young beavers eventually disperse, they carry habits rather than designs. An abandoned lodge, a new spring at the basin’s head, or a pond formed by rockfall offers a place to begin again.

Winter does not close the city. The beavers do not hibernate. Ice skins the ponds and locks the tops of dams, but beneath it the animals continue to move between lodges and food caches, more slowly and with fewer wasted journeys. Branches stored in cold water are drawn out and stripped. Above the waterline, bodies crowd into dry chambers while condensation gathers on the roof. Outside, a fish hangs almost motionless beneath an overhang, and the black sediment holds its own cold, sparse activity. By thaw, some caches have been reduced to pale, peeled sticks, and some dams have been torn open by shifting ice. The first open water is not simply a release. It is a summons to work.

Below the surface, the basin has a population far larger than its visible builders. Diatoms and algae coat stone and wood, making food out of light, seeping minerals, and the fine nourishment carried by the current. Insect larvae graze these films or hide in the branch mats. Snails travel the margins. Mussels draw water through themselves in the quieter ponds, their beds flourishing or failing according to the sediment a new dam delivers. Small fish use the interstices of the city as other animals use its chambers: beneath a lodged trunk, among willow roots, in the still shadow behind a weir. A flooded thicket becomes a nursery until the water falls and strands the unlucky.

A lodge wall is never only a lodge wall. Its crevices hold eggs, larvae, seeds, and the wet beginnings of moss. Its roof may carry a vole’s runway, a duck’s nest, or the flattened resting place of an otter. Dippers hunt along the stony runs, their bodies at ease in water that would numb most other birds. Diving ducks use the deeper canals, while swallows, present only for the brief generosity of summer, take midges just above the ponds. Frogs collect in shallow water that warms a little before the rest. Their spawn clouding a sunny margin is as much a sign of the basin’s success as any repaired dam.

Predators belong to the same network of passages. A heron learns which spillway gathers fish after rain. An otter learns which canals connect without a shallow portage and which roof offers a dry place to leave scent. Owls come over the rim at night, when a beaver crossing between lodges is visible as a moving darkness among darker shapes. The builders’ usual response is to vanish. The city has few defensible doors on land, but underwater it is full of them.

Not every life benefits. A raised pond can drown a vole’s tunnels or rot eggs laid beside what had been a stable shore. A drained channel can expose mussels and erase a fish shelter. Ice can crush a lodge entrance; rockfall can bury a family without leaving so much as a swirl on the water. Young beavers disappear, caches fail, and nests are taken. The basin’s abundance is not gentleness. It is the continual conversion of shelter into exposure, growth into food, and death into another layer of usable ground.

The plants, too, are inhabitants rather than materials. A willow stem driven into mud as a construction stake may root and leaf, turning part of a dam into a living hedge. Repeated gnawing makes willows and alders sprout from low stools, so a feeding ground becomes denser instead of disappearing. Sedges advance into an unused canal, their roots narrowing it year by year. Horsetails spread across fresh silt. Moss holds water against stone, and liverworts seal one damp surface to the next. Even the beavers’ abandonments are colonized while they are still visibly their work. A failed dam becomes a root-bound bank; an empty lodge becomes an island whose vegetation is already altering the flow around it.

Thus the oldest things in the basin are not necessarily its oldest structures. Walls slumps, canals fill, and family territories vanish, but certain conditions persist: deep water near shelter, shallow water near food, current broken into manageable steps, wet ground held open to light. These conditions are repeatedly lost and remade by animals with no conception of the whole, and by roots, eggs, larvae, ice, and sediment pursuing no conception at all.

On a rainy night, the distinction becomes especially clear. A beaver presses mud into a leak because the changed current has reached its own lodge. Upstream, the water rises almost imperceptibly. A fish moves farther into the flooded sedge. A frog shifts along the margin. Wetted willow roots tighten their hold on the bank, while a vole leaves a burrow that has begun to drip. By morning the breach is only one of hundreds of small alterations, and already water is finding a way around the new work.

Nothing has been completed. Everything has been continued.
