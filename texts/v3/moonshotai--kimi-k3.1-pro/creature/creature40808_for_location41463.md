The Crown’s people do not experience it as either empty or silent. Those impressions belong chiefly to eyes and ears. The inhabitants live just as much through their feet, their hands, and the electrical sensitivities along their backs.

A practiced resident can lean against a rail and distinguish the stroke of a kiln engine from an empty freight cage taking load. Sievebacks grazing on the far side of a wall produce a faint, combing vibration. Children running in an upper corridor feel, to someone resting below, like a brief change in the weather. The city’s great distances remain, but within a district every foundation is carrying news.

The common records call the citizens Crownfolk, though individual districts have older and more particular names for themselves. An adult is low, broad, and six-limbed. Four clawed legs carry the body; two narrow, three-fingered arms fold along the ribs while walking. Crownfolk can rear and brace themselves to work at a high bench, but most prefer workshops in which the important surfaces descend to meet them. This, rather than grandeur, accounts for the low doorways of the oldest chambers.

Their backs carry a mosaic of thin, flexible mineral plates, not a rigid shell. These are nearly black in the young and become pale, scratched, and individually recognizable with age. The rest of the skin is matte and pliant, rust-gray, umber, or bone-colored. Beneath it are hollow ceramic bones, oil-rich tissues, and enclosed ionic gels. Crownfolk are warm to the touch. A deep injury closes with a bead of amber gel rather than a free flow of liquid. They do not sweat, and their breathing valves can seal against bad dust.

They are not living statues, whatever a visitor may think after finding six of them motionless on a sunny terrace. They tire. They itch beneath their plates. They develop stiff joints, irrational preferences in food, and strong opinions about sleeping niches. Their stillness is often rest, sometimes concentration, and occasionally merely the wish not to be drawn into a conversation.

Voice is useful within a room or among a close work crew. Beyond that, the thin air makes it an unreliable vanity. Crownfolk speak in low sustained tones, clicks, and softly knocked consonants; their laughter is sometimes audible and sometimes only a rapid shaking of the forebody. Serious distance communication goes by wire or shuttered light. Even intimate language has a tactile form: a name can be spoken, tapped into a companion’s palm, or sent as a brief pattern along a private line.

Their bodies explain some of the city’s manners. Strong charge produces an unpleasant tension along the dorsal plates, and a badly grounded tool can announce itself before it is touched. A formal greeting therefore begins with both parties contacting the same grounded stud. Only afterward do they touch hands or forearms. Among close relatives the procedure may be abbreviated; among strangers, omitting it is not daring but boorish. The first hospitality offered to a traveler is a safe place to discharge. The second is shelter from the wind. Food comes third.

Their clothing is equally practical without being uniformly drab. Mineral-fiber wraps protect against abrasion; insulating aprons and glazed guards are worn in the kiln halls. Boots range from thick-soled terrace gear to thin, flexible coverings that leave the feet sensitive to vibration. Jewelry often serves as a shunt, key, counterweight, or identification seal. A beautiful piece is admired all the more if its electrical purpose is not immediately obvious.

Crownfolk grow slowly and commonly live for several centuries, but their reputation for patience is partly a misunderstanding. Their children are quick, noisy, and appallingly willing to experiment with charge in ungrounded corners. They run when adults would walk, become bored during cable journeys, and find the immobility of elders mysteriously easy to achieve. A citizen of forty may still be in a first apprenticeship. An old citizen may have outlived the trade they mastered, the district in which they were born, and several theories about how a particular furnace ought to be operated. Long life does not make them consistently wise. It merely gives their virtues and irritating habits more time to become established.

Their houses are usually held by households rather than by simple lines of descent. Kin, unrelated companions, apprentices, elderly former coworkers, and the occasional long-staying traveler may all share one warm foundation. Private rooms tend to be small; the common chambers contain the heat manifold, the grounding bed, the food cultures, and the tools too useful for any one person to own. Some households have occupied the same chambers for a thousand years without coming to regard them as permanent. A wall can be sound and still require watching.

A kitchen resembles an assay bench more than a hearth. Porous tiles carry cultivated films. Sealed columns contain dark oils populated by edible microscopic life. Jars hold pastes that look like preparations for ceramic glaze and are discussed with comparable precision. Cooking involves dry roasting, culturing, grinding, and the adjustment of mineral balances; nothing is boiled. Some districts prepare animal tissue, while others rely almost entirely on cultures. Crownfolk can endure long fasts, but they do not therefore treat eating as an unfortunate interruption. A worker’s meal may be brief and solitary. A household feast may continue for days, with people arriving, eating, sleeping, and returning to their shifts.

Most occupations are less heroic than the landscape suggests. There are film-tenders who decide which walls may be grazed and which must rest; dust-readers who can distinguish dormant food from an egg clutch or an electrical inscription; insulator surgeons; nest surveyors; tunnel listeners; archive copyists; kiln chemists; and clerks whose principal task is to ensure that an unpleasant uncertainty remains visible until someone resolves it.

A clean wall is not necessarily a well-kept one. It may simply be a wall from which every other form of life has been removed.

The metropolis has no single throne or capital. Its unity rests on the Common Span, less a constitution than a continually revised body of agreements: cable dimensions, freight priorities, grounding depths, tunnel signals, nesting protections, archive formats, and the circumstances under which one district may refuse another’s cargo. Local councils interpret these agreements differently. Districts have accents, rivalries, old angers, and wholly avoidable disputes over furnace output and cable allocation. The archives are not monuments to universal harmony. Some contain centuries of exquisite spite in the margins.

Nevertheless, certain offenses are treated with exceptional seriousness because their consequences can remain invisible for generations. Falsifying a ground inspection is worse than merely stealing material. Concealing an uncertain survey is worse than admitting that a route cannot yet be used. A clerk who schedules a repair beyond their own lifetime is not assuming they will personally finish it. They are leaving a legible obligation for successors, along with whatever doubts might prevent those successors from treating the schedule as certainty.

This does not make Crownfolk incapable of urgency. A fractured cable, a failed ground, or a collapsing nursery wall can bring a district into immediate, efficient motion. Crews run. Heavy machinery is stopped without consultation. Arguments are postponed rather than ceremonially resolved. Their slowness is reserved mainly for decisions whose reversal would be difficult or whose effects would fall on people not yet born.

Nor are they solemn. Jokes travel by wire. Kiln crews maintain elaborate rivalries over surface finishes. Small obscene figures appear inside freight mechanisms where no inspector would think to look until the mechanism is opened for overhaul. There is quick, knocking chamber music played in thick-walled rooms, and there are compositions conducted through entire buildings, with the audience touching rails or walls to receive them. Some glazes are made to change appearance as they cool over several days; others exist only to produce one brief, garish flash when a furnace door opens. An elegant repair can attract genuine admiration, particularly if the next worker can understand it without guessing.

Physical travel is uncommon enough to remain an event, but absence does not necessarily imply remoteness. Many residents maintain close friendships through messages, parcels, copied marginalia, and visits planned around freight capacity. A cable car becomes a small temporary household: passengers sleep, argue, share food, and learn the particular vibrations of the span. Long life does not make a missed visit painless. It only means that an apology may still be delivered in person.

The approach of an equalization alters domestic life before it alters the sky. Crownfolk become more careful about touch. Tools are covered, children are kept closer to the grounded chambers, and old aches in the dorsal plates become a subject of tedious conversation. When the luminous networks begin to spread, entire work crews withdraw into the galleries. They play games, cook, inspect whatever can safely be inspected, and tell highly inconsistent accounts of previous discharges.

Afterward come what some districts call the open-handed hours. For a little while, metal can be touched without ceremony. Residents run bare hands along rails that have required caution for decades. Children are brought out to feel the difference. Friends greet one another without first seeking a stud. Not everyone participates; inspection crews are often busiest then, and no competent kiln master treats the occasion as a reason to neglect a furnace. Still, the custom persists. For a brief interval, an entire district shares not only a potential but an unusual ease of contact.

The other inhabitants are accommodated more often than they are domesticated. Crownfolk do not harness veil kites or pen migratory spindrels. Sievebacks may become accustomed to a factory’s vibration cycle, gathering on a warm wall shortly before a conveyor starts, but this is learned convenience rather than obedience. Spindrels follow routes older than many streets, and the streets have been adjusted accordingly.

Some veil kites appear in tower records for generations, identified by damaged spars, old tears in the membrane, or peculiarities of flight. Crews give them names and occasionally argue over whether a returning animal is truly the same individual. The kites give no indication of caring. Their nests, however, are surveyed with great care, and their regional departure remains sufficient reason to stop work and ask what the instruments have missed.

Every slagfold attached to a municipal waste bed is mapped, and its last observed movement is recorded. An adult that has not shifted in sixty years is not considered tame. It is considered well documented. During hatching decades, entire industrial schedules may be altered around a warm bed that appears, to uninformed eyes, to contain nothing but clinker.

Even the small clutch animals in signal boxes are not merely vermin. Many boxes now have removable inner trays, allowing a technician to lift out an inhabited section, service the mechanism, and return the nest without dismantling it. Similar arrangements exist in ventilation shafts, cable anchors, and unused furnace ducts. The result is not perfect coexistence. Animals are injured, food walls are overgrazed, and some crews remain needlessly hostile to anything that interferes with a schedule. But interference alone is not considered proof that a life is in the wrong place.

Below the inhabited foundations, other grazers occupy the warm dark: eyeless, slow-bodied things feeding on chemical films around deep faults and heat leaks. Their burrows and molts accumulate in abandoned borings. The oldest chambers are never truly unoccupied, only unvisited.

Crownfolk death is likewise dry and gradual in its practical consequences. There is no rapid decay to manage. Specialist reclaimers receive the body, and the household keeps only those physical remnants the dead person wished kept: a tool, a plate, a piece of jewelry, sometimes nothing at all. More important is the examination of unfinished obligations. Notes are read, promises identified, and pending work divided among successors. Some tasks are accepted. Some are deferred. Some are formally ended.

That last act matters. A civilization with long archives requires permission to stop, or the living would eventually be crushed beneath the accumulated intentions of the dead. Grief does not disappear because a file has been closed. A sleeping niche may remain unused for years. A familiar vibration in a workshop may be missed more sharply than a voice.

Near evening, several residents may sit on a terrace with their feet against the same rail. To a distant observer, nothing is happening. Through the rail they are following a freight cage, a kiln engine, and children playing two levels below. A sieveback grazes within reach. Dust on the parapet contains a record that someone will eventually come to read. Above the summit, the kites are withdrawing one by one, though no instrument in the district has yet registered a reason.

The plaza can still look empty. It is not empty to them. They can feel who is at work, who has arrived, what is moving behind the wall, and which structure is carrying more load than it should. Their lives occupy the Crown without closing its distances.

The quiet is not what remains when life has been removed. It is one of the things life here makes possible.
