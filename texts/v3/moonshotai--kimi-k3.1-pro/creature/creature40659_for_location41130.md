Nothing in the cirque is native in the settled sense of the word. Much of the stone is younger than the animals standing on it. Every inhabitant is a colonist, and every colony has learned that occupation does not mean permanence. Their lives are organized not around seasons, but around intervals: rain and clearance, old film and new seam, one growth pulse and the next.

The greater part of the population is invisible. Beneath the violet skins of the older pools, the microbial films are layered like pasture and fallow ground. Some organisms draw what they need from warm mineral water; others live on the remnants of those. The copper-green films along fresh seams spread quickly, brilliantly, and briefly. They are less enduring than the violet growths but better at occupying stone that has only just become stone.

Between disturbances, much of this life waits. Spores lodge in the ash-colored lichen. Eggs survive in water-filled fractures. Films shrink into mineral pores too fine to distinguish from shadow. When resonance lifts droplets off the floor, it carries these dormant populations into the air; when the droplets return as rain, it sows them elsewhere. Construction does not cleanse the cirque of life. It stirs life through it.

The visible plants are small, but none is merely struggling to survive. Each has made an entire world out of scarcity. The lichen cushion is a refuge as much as a plant: its upper surface turns to ash again after every wetting, while deeper within it remain flea eggs, midge larvae, and damp pockets of violet film. The three waxy grass blades hold their roots directly in a seep, drawing nourishment from whatever the warm water has touched. The cup-plant keeps a private pool. Its translucent leaves close more tightly when the resonators deepen their tone, protecting the thimble of water inside from being lifted away. Within that water lives a little grazing community of its own. The plant absorbs what dies there. It does not photosynthesize in a garden; it tends a watershed small enough to fold between its hands.

When the rain slackens, the mica-fleas turn the larger pools into flickering fields. They feed along the meniscus, where film, air, and water meet. Their flat shells shed water so readily that a submerged flea seems to carry a bead of bright air with it. A flea can cross a pool in a succession of leaps too rapid for the eye to separate, pausing only to scrape at the surface with its mouthparts. For a few minutes there may be thousands. Then the kite-wings dive, the newts feed at the margins, and the pools become clear again. The fleas have not vanished. They have returned to crevices, lichen, and the narrow wet margins beneath overhangs, where a population can remain almost motionless for days.

The sieve-crabs are more deliberate. The largest can span two fingers, though most are smaller and so flattened that they seem to have no thickness until they turn. Their many legs end in soft gripping pads; their mouths are rows of mineral-hard combs. They advance sideways over new seams, shaving away copper-green growth and leaving behind the bright, clean trails that later growth so often follows.

Each crab carries a narrow garden beneath the rim of its shell: a reserve of the same film on which it feeds. Some of that garden is continually transferred to new stone by its legs. Thus the crabs are not only grazers but sowers, and perhaps surveyors as well. They reopen old routes after every pulse, testing each seam with their forelegs before committing their bodies. Occasionally an older crab will persist in following a path that no longer exists, stopping at a wall where there was once open floor. For a while it will make the same detour again and again. Behavior here can outlive the topography that produced it.

Rain-midges turn the microbial abundance into flight. Their larvae crowd the warm pools as transparent threads with copper-colored guts. At some signal in temperature, vibration, or the chemistry of the water, whole broods rise together. They cast their larval skins at the surface and ascend in columns, wings not quite dry, so that each swarm begins as a glittering extension of the pool beneath it. The adults are brief-lived. Their business is to mate, disperse, and lay before the rain closes over them again. Their sizzling is the sound of an enormous population concentrating nearly all of its existence into an hour.

The bell-newts live by a longer rhythm, but they are the most attentive to change. A newt resting on a ledge looks scarcely substantial: wet dark spine, translucent sides, bronze throat, toes splayed on stone still too smooth to offer obvious purchase. It hears with more than its ears. Vibration passes through the rock, its feet, its jaw, and the air held in its throat. What seems like foreknowledge when newts gather at a dry place is probably only exquisite listening. The stone has already announced that water is being prepared there.

An adult with its throat full of air produces the clear, hard ringing note. A brooding adult, its throat bronze and heavy with eggs, can manage little more than a blunt tick. Both sounds have their uses. The ringing newt calls and waits for the return. Its next note differs slightly according to whether the echo came from the eastern wall, arrived doubled, or seemed to issue from beyond the open rim. The calling is courtship and challenge, but it is also measurement. A newt knows its portion of the cirque less as an area than as a pattern of answers.

These patterns have to be learned again after the terrain grows. Young newts acquire the local chorus from adults rather than inheriting a fixed song, and each pool keeps a slightly different version of it. Old notes sometimes survive in courtship after they have ceased to correspond to any existing wall. A newt may therefore woo its mate with an echo of a cliff that vanished before either of them hatched.

When several adults approach a common pitch, the resonators often answer. Whether the machines require that pitch, recognize it, or merely find it convenient is impossible to tell. The newts, in any case, have learned to remain near the dry seams through the ragged beginning of a chorus. Afterward, brooding adults enter whatever water has appeared and release their eggs. The first larvae in a new pool may already be waiting before the pool itself exists. They must develop before it drains into pinholes, spreads into a different shape, or is partly sealed by another arrival of stone. Many fail. Enough do not.

The kite-wings belong partly to the cirque and partly to the clouds. Their bodies are small beneath the broad membranes, which fold so closely that a grounded animal looks like a little wet debris caught against the rock. In the air they are almost silent. They hang where the warm updraft meets colder cloud, watching not only for movement but for changes in the dim reflections below: the sudden glitter of fleas, the wavering column of a midge emergence, the pale interruption of a newt crossing wet stone.

They often arrive just before an emergence becomes visible from the ground. Before landing, even an old kite-wing makes a low pass over the floor, as though checking that the distances have kept their agreement. During a growth pulse, they do not attempt to outfly the vapor. They come down, fold themselves against the lee of a ledge, and wait with their membranes clamped over their bodies. Afterward they rise into air full of disoriented midges. Their visits also connect otherwise isolated pockets of life. Spores and eggs travel on their wet feet; the richest little growths on the upper ledges often begin where a kite-wing habitually rests.

The animals use the six structures without explaining them. Bell-newts favor the four descending steps because each returns a different note; a chorus there sounds as though several hidden newts were answering from inside the floor. Sieve-crabs gather near the forked pylons when their vibration is faintest and abandon them before it deepens. During ordinary rain, a kite-wing sometimes crouches on the low bench beneath the shelter’s water-outlined dome. It never remains there through a growth pulse. Midge columns bend around the black ring without touching it, though nothing visible obstructs them.

These habits do not reveal the structures’ purposes, any more than a nest explains the branch that holds it.

To the inhabitants, a growth pulse is not simply a catastrophe. It begins as a general rearrangement. The cup-plant closes. Crabs leave exhausted grazing grounds and press themselves into fractures. Fleas crowd the pool margins. Newts climb and begin calling. Kite-wings come down out of the updraft. Then the tone enters the teeth, droplets lift carrying their invisible freight of eggs and spores, and the cirque cracks into its new shape.

The event destroys pasture and opens it. It strands larvae and makes spawning water. It erases echo-territories, interrupts courtship, and gives the survivors different notes to sing. Afterward the crabs return first, then the newts, then the midges, and finally the predators. A pulse is at once harvest, spawning season, displacement, and construction. Its abundance depends on destruction remaining incomplete.

A finished cirque might therefore be a poorer home. The violet films need undisturbed time; the copper-green films need fresh seams. The fleas need old pools, the crabs new grazing grounds, the newts both anticipation and aftermath. Permanence would favor a few inhabitants and quietly starve the rest.

No single species directs this arrangement. The films feed the grazers; the grazers inoculate and mark the stone; the newts supply a common pitch; the resonators alter the conditions under which all of them must live. Even the machines have become difficult to regard as merely scenery. They may not feed or breed, but they wait, answer, and change their behavior in the presence of their neighbors.

The cirque’s inhabitants are not waiting for it to become a home. They have already made it one, in trails that outlast their paths, songs that remember absent cliffs, and eggs carried toward water that has not yet appeared. Home here is not the shape of the ground. It is the reliable expectation that, after the ground changes, something will answer.
