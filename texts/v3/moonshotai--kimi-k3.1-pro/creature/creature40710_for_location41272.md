The High Condensary is inhabited most densely where it looks most stained. Its population cannot be taken at doorways, or even at reservoirs. A ward may contain no visible animal and still be alive along every warm joint; another may hold thousands of bodies beneath black water and appear, until a lamp moves, perfectly empty. Life here has acquired the city’s own characteristics: thinness, patience, and an almost complete indifference to being seen.

The grey-violet film might be called **veinscale**, though it is neither a single growth nor easily divided into individuals. Colonies survive separation, dormancy, and long periods of apparent death. The active edge of a colony is nearly colorless. Behind it, the violet deepens and then dulls as the organism lays down its mineral shell. Where water flow and electrical gradient pull in different directions, the edge becomes braided: one set of branches follows the current, another leans toward a leaking cable or a charged pipe. An old power fault can therefore remain written on a wall long after the cable that caused it has corroded away.

Dry veinscale is not all equally dormant. During the hot intervals, the youngest branches often remain viable while the older heart of a colony dies back to grey tracery. When condensation returns, the wall does not revive evenly. Isolated hairlines become slick; a junction darkens; several days later, a broad patch between them may suddenly resume spreading. The process resembles recollection only because the colony’s structure preserves the routes of former growth.

Its shell is more than a remnant. It changes the way water holds to stone. Some shells seal a channel against loss; others create fine pores through which a climbing film can draw itself. Veinscale can gradually bridge a hairline cut intended to stop rising water, restoring a path that maintenance had closed centuries before. Elsewhere, its growth starves a seep by sealing the joint that fed it. The city’s drainage is therefore not simply old plumbing plus an infestation. It is plumbing continually revised by one of its inhabitants.

The **cistern lenses** live on this pasture.

At rest, a lens is slightly convex and held against the reservoir floor by a clear rim. It has no visible eyes and no distinct mouth; most of its underside serves as a grazing surface. Its movement is usually too slow to watch. Evidence of it appears afterward as clean, shallow arcs through the veinscale, often crossing and recrossing without ever quite touching. A covered reservoir may be extensively grazed while seeming, to a lamp held motionless above it, utterly sterile.

A moving lamp is more revealing. For an instant the light enters a lens at the proper angle, and its internal vessels appear: a few branching lines, a darker crescent near the rim, sometimes the narrow shadow of mineral particles swallowed with the film. Then the lamp passes and the water closes over nothing.

Lenses do not gather in shoals. Even when thousands occupy one cistern, they remain distributed according to food and flow. Pressure changes them before visible water does. A sluice opening elsewhere in the ward can cause a row of lenses to lift their rims, one after another, though the reservoir surface remains perfectly still. When the new current reaches them, they detach and drift. They do not swim so much as make small corrections to where the water carries them.

During a long wet period, a second rim forms across the body of a well-fed lens. At overflow, the disc constricts and separates into two thinner discs. Reproduction thus coincides with dispersal. Some of the new lenses settle in another reservoir; many are stranded on mineral shelves; some go over the plateau rim. Clear, folded husks high above the present waterline are often the only evidence that a channel once contained a population.

Heat drives the survivors into crevices. A folded lens can remain there long enough for its outer rim to become lightly cemented to the rock. When water returns, some tear free and unfold. Others remain as transparent flakes, indistinguishable from mica until touched. A crack may consequently contain both the living and several generations of the dead, all awaiting the same wetting but responding differently to it.

The **spillway wader** belongs to a different scale of time.

It is the only large inhabitant most districts ever see, and usually the reflection is noticed first. The body itself is easily lost among standpipes and pale condenser casings. It is not feathered. Its skin is smooth but finely crazed, like old glaze, and water runs off it in narrow, discontinuous threads. The torso is surprisingly small above the length of the legs. Its narrow head is carried against the body on a neck folded in two rigid-looking sections.

Its stillness is not merely concealment. It is listening.

Much of what the wader senses appears to arrive through its feet. The long toes rest on stone rather than in water whenever possible, and the animal can remain poised for hours while pumps labor distantly beneath it. A change in ground vibration may bring its head a fraction upward. A second change may send it, with no apparent haste, toward a spillway that will not begin flowing until the following day. Its routes through the city follow canal lines and pressure gates, not avenues.

When it feeds, the transformation is so slight that an observer often understands the posture only after it has changed. One leg bends. The folded neck lowers. The head enters the shallows and returns with a lens held almost invisibly against its underside. A meal may take an entire condensation period. The wader does not churn the water or pursue prey; it waits for the release to bring food within reach.

Waders do not flock, and nothing flies between the towers. Their passages are known mostly from needle-fine tracks, mineral knocked from a kerb, or a line of reservoir floors grazed in succession. Occasionally a much smaller and less regular set of prints leaves a covered cistern, so the animals must breed somewhere within the system. No nest has been identified. In the hottest intervals they disappear into condenser shafts or beyond the visible wards, and the open city is left with only their old reflections in the memory of an observer.

There are lesser lives in the return water: colorless knots around inspection glass, shells too small to distinguish from silica grains, occasional translucent casts caught in valve baskets. Some may be juvenile stages of the lenses. Others may belong to animals that never leave the pipes. The Condensary resists taxonomy for the same reason it resists a census—most of its habitat is enclosed, and opening a habitat often changes the pressure that made it worth occupying.

None of these creatures built the city. None appears to understand it. Yet they have become involved in its continued operation.

Leaking power feeds the veinscale. Veinscale alters the channels and the paths of climbing water. Lenses graze it back from brass inlays, collection slots, and the margins of control cuts, sometimes preserving clearances that automatic tools no longer reach. Overflow distributes the lenses and tears fragments of living film from one ward into another. Waders feed at the points where both become concentrated. The engines, meanwhile, impose the hot and wet intervals to which all this life is adapted.

The arrangement is not harmonious in any simple sense. Veinscale chokes valves. Lenses sometimes strip a channel so thoroughly that corrosion accelerates beneath them. A release can carry away nearly every grazer in a reservoir, leaving the next wetting to be colonized from elsewhere. Maintenance corrects some of these failures and ignores others. A clean gauge above a mineral-sealed facade is not evidence of contradictory priorities; it is evidence that only certain functions still matter.

Each inhabitant also possesses a different geography. For veinscale, a district is a pattern of charge, warmth, and wetting. For a lens, it is a chain of dark floors joined at long intervals by violent rivers. For a wader, it is a sequence of vibrations in the basalt and a widely separated set of feeding places. None of them knows the street plan. The numbered cisterns, plazas, and bridges belong to another way of occupying space.

The builders’ place among the living is less certain. The visible animals do not read gauges, replace gaskets, or send service carriers along the rails. A wader may wait beside a junction because its body feels the coming change, but it does not cause the change. Veinscale can preserve a channel or ruin one, but it does not polish a handle at the height of a hand.

If tenders remain in sealed rooms, their bodily presence has been reduced almost to nothing. They do not use the plazas. They leave less evidence of themselves than the waders. Their lives, considered from outside, would consist mainly of intervals between interventions: a gauge cleared, a seal renewed, a route kept open while an entire surrounding ward is allowed to mineralize. If no tenders remain, the same signs are the residue of mechanisms still capable of selecting a task without selecting a witness.

Either possibility leaves the avenues empty. There is no concealed multitude behind the repeated doorways, no busy hour at which the city becomes comprehensible as a settlement. The worn stairs prove that bodies once crossed these distances. They do not prove that bodies still do.

When the cloud ceiling descends, habitation becomes visible in stages. First the old dendritic scars on a wall darken at their tips. A climbing vein crosses them and divides. Beneath a cistern’s black surface, clear rims lift almost imperceptibly from the floor. Then the sluices answer one another across the wards, and the lenses loosen into the new shallows. At a spillway, a pale shape that might have been pipework unfolds one leg.

No door opens. Nothing comes down the road. The stain advances by a fraction; under the water, something transparent begins to graze; the wader lowers its head and becomes still again.

The Condensary has not suddenly become occupied. It has merely become possible to notice that it is.
