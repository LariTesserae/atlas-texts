The Ninth Cistern has inhabitants, but a census taken from the entry stair would miss nearly all of them. Its visible population is rooted. Its mobile population exists only when some repair requires a body. And its largest resident has no local body complete enough to be seen.

The gardens come first. They are not ornament, and they are not quite an ecosystem accidentally enclosed by machinery. They are part of how the Cistern handles water, minerals, and time.

The black-green mosses are the pioneers and the conservators. Their cushions occupy joints where warmth, seepage, and garnet light coincide. Each rain delivers fine construction dust and dissolved minerals; the moss retains a little of both, releasing the water more slowly than the pavement can. Within a cushion there may be several distinct generations: a dark living surface, a saturated green interior, and below that a compacted brown-black felt threaded through with fungal filaments. Almost no soil results. Nearly everything is used again before it can accumulate.

The ferns are less individual than they appear. A bed rising from several neighbouring drains may be one genetic organism, divided by ceramic and connected only intermittently by root fragments. Its waxy fronds shed the violence of the artificial rain while retaining a thin film against the saturated air. Spores travel in street water and condensate. Most perish on bare pavement or vanish through the grates, but enough reach another warm joint to make the beds slowly discontinuous with their original planting. The Cistern’s gardeners, if that function can be called by such a name, do not always restore the original boundary.

Along the thermal channels, the reeds perform a heavier labour. Their roots catch mineral scale and the pale dust washed from new cuts, binding both into a flexible lattice. Channel water therefore remains clear without the channels remaining bare. When a lattice grows thick enough to obstruct flow, the city does not simply strip it out. Sections are lifted, divided, and returned, so that a drain can remain an engineered aperture while also becoming the rhizome-filled heart of a reed bed. Some of these structures may eventually outlast the purpose for which the channel was first surveyed.

The reservoir plants live at another pace. Their broad leaves turn with convection and the small disturbances of falling condensation, but their root mats hold them in loose, almost stationary archipelagos. Below the leaves, roots hang through water that has no daylight and no seasons. Cold pulses pass around them; warm rain briefly troubles the whole surface; afterwards they resume their slow rotations. Nothing in the Cistern requires pollination. Mosses and ferns reproduce by spores, reeds and reservoir plants chiefly by division and extension. A garden can therefore persist for a very long time without producing anything that looks like an event.

Nor need these lineages be as young as the excavation. A root mat may be newly established here and still belong to a stock much older than the chamber. The Cistern contains young surfaces, but not necessarily young life.

Beneath the visible growth lives the population that makes death nearly invisible. Fungal cords and microbial films enter a fallen frond before its outline has collapsed. The frond yellows, clears, and becomes a skeleton of translucent fibres; then even the fibres lose their distinction from the mat below. On newly wet basalt, other films form sheens too slight to be called growth. They alter the stone molecule by molecule, preparing surfaces that moss may use decades later. After heavy rain they sometimes cloud the roots with a brief opalescence, then clear again as water carries their products away. There is no scavenging and almost no refuse, only a continuous transfer of material across very small distances.

The machines are harder to count because most of them do not maintain separate bodies.

One could watch for many cycles and see nothing resembling a worker. Then an access plate softens. Grey material moves across its damaged face, not quite flowing and not quite crawling, and the plate becomes whole. It is tempting to say that a repair substance has been sent to the plate. It may be more accurate to say that the plate has temporarily ceased to be obliged to hold a rigid shape. The film, the damage, and the repair are phases of one continuing obligation: *there is an access plate here*.

The same is true of a curb adding glaze-thin extensions to itself, or a street approaching a survey line across uncut basalt. The local material changes, but the operation persists. Drains, conduits, plaques, and galleries are less like possessions of the city than like repeatedly honoured descriptions of it. Most of the machinery required to honour those descriptions never has to appear.

The three towers encourage personification because they have separate voices. Their tones swell against one another, seem to approach, retreat, and finally agree. Yet they are not three hidden beings discussing the weather. Their convergence is the local shape of a larger process becoming coherent enough to make rain. Nor are they brains. If they resemble organs at all, they are organs of attachment: they keep the district from losing its relationship to the vastly greater substance elsewhere.

At that scale, the Cistern’s largest inhabitant begins to emerge.

It is the continuing city itself: not a spirit in the basalt, and not necessarily a mind, but an organisation capable of persisting through changes in its own material. It has memory in the practical sense. Addresses remain valid while plots stay empty. Named conduits retain their names before being connected. Work interrupted by rain resumes without confusion. Established roots are remembered by being accommodated rather than by being recorded anywhere a visitor could read.

It also has something like temperament. It names before it makes. It keeps services ready without requiring them to be used. It builds patiently enough that completion never becomes a final condition. It does not hurry to conceal the excavation’s newness or make the district look occupied. Most revealingly, it yields to roots.

That yielding need not arise from care. Preserving a root mat may simply cost less than replacing one, or the gardens may be too useful to the water cycle to treat as obstructions. But repeated consistently, the practice amounts to an ethic within the chamber: established life is infrastructure, and infrastructure may revise the plan. A tile is lifted and relaid around a root. A drain and a fern bed cease to have separate definitions. Geometry remains precise, but it is no longer entirely authoritative.

Whether this continuing city is conscious is not a question the Cistern helps a visitor answer. It offers no face, no greeting, and no observable response to being watched. It does not attend, but it accommodates. Its intentions are visible only as patterns of maintenance. The empty plots need not be homes awaiting people; they may be capacities kept open for later organs, gardens, reservoirs, or uses that will never require a building in the ordinary sense. An address can be a promise without being a promise of company.

Every seventeen minutes, the whole dispersed population briefly becomes one watershed.

Rain takes dust from the new cuts, spores from fronds and moss, fibres from the root mats, and minerals from every wet surface. For three minutes, distinctions among weather, feeding, cleaning, and reproduction largely disappear. Nothing comes out to drink; everything is already where the water will be. Then the drains open. The root mats retain what they can, the films receive their organic fraction, and mineral-bearing water vanishes into the machinery’s adjacent elsewhere. Fog returns. Grey repair surfaces resume their slow work. The gardens rise again in separate bands.

This is the closest thing the district has to a communal event, although none of its inhabitants needs to witness it.

The moss beyond the survey line on the far wall may therefore matter more than its size suggests. It is not an invasion, and it need not remain an exception. If its cushion survives, the illuminated line can be moved by its width. The unbuilt avenue can be surveyed again. Over sufficient time, a plot boundary may curve around a garden that was once only a damp joint. No machine will have decided to spare nature, and no plant will have resisted the city. Two forms of growth will simply have reached a durable arrangement.

A human visitor would be the only animal in the chamber, and perhaps the only presence capable of finding the empty addresses lonely. Their footsteps and voice would add new sounds to the rain, but nothing would come to investigate. When they left, no routine would alter in response. The tower tones would converge again. Ferns would bow beneath warm water, dead fronds would continue vanishing into their mats, and the moss would keep its narrow portion of the survey line.

For three minutes, all the inhabitants would occupy the same falling water. Then the drains would open, and the city would sort itself back into gardens, surfaces, and intentions.
