Nothing in the Low Cistern starts at a shadow. Nothing follows a vibration, approaches warmth, or withdraws into a burrow. The absence of animals is not an emptiness within the garden; it is one of the conditions that has made the garden what it is. Its inhabitants have never needed to flee, to withstand grazing, or to attract anything that might carry their young. They have needed to attach, to endure, and to survive on allowances so small that scarcity and dormancy are often indistinguishable.

The first life is usually a film.

On sheltered stone it lies beneath the leaf-dust, a thin microbial skin following variations in the masonry too slight to serve as shelter for anything larger. It collects in adze facets, in the crescents left by stone mauls, in mortar voids and the pores of fired clay. On smooth, current-swept tile it may be present only as a change in the way water wets the surface. The water remains clear not because it is sterile, but because so little of its life is adrift. Most of it is holding on.

These films are not uniform. Within a thickness smaller than a leaf, different communities occupy different chemical circumstances. Some consume organic matter leaking from the vegetation above. Others live on the contrast between water that has rested in deep mineral rubble and water newly arrived from an open court. Some slowly dissolve mortar and ceramic; others release substances that harden into scale nearby. The same film can therefore be taking a surface apart and sealing it, not in alternation but at once, pore by pore. Over long intervals, their activity gives the stone a new texture. That texture permits other lives to attach.

Above and within the films grows the low joint-felt.

Its minute leaves overlap so closely that a young mat appears continuous, but it has no true surface. Water passes between the leaves, through the older tissue beneath them, and along the holdfasts entering the masonry. The uppermost leaves are alive. Below them may lie several remains of earlier surfaces: leaves still intact but no longer growing, filaments compressed into dark threads, mineral grains caught among them, and finally a root-bound mixture of mortar dust and clay. A mat little thicker than a finger can contain an entire history of occupancy.

Not all of this history is dead. Some old stems retain living strands deep inside tissue that has outwardly become part of the substrate. A frond that has not grown for centuries may be dormant; another, fresher in appearance, may merely have been preserved by cold and stillness. Death here often means a change of function long before it means disappearance. A leaf ceases to gather and becomes shelter. A stem stops conducting and becomes support. A whole mat may become the soil in which its descendants root.

The holdfasts follow weakness rather than simply forcing their way through stone. They enter mortar first, then the fine spaces between ceramic particles, then fractures too narrow to be seen. Their growth can lift a pavement flag by increments so small that the flag remains apparently undisturbed while its support changes from rubble to living tissue. Yet the same growth binds what it displaces. A root-bound tile may fracture and still remain exactly where the roots require it to be. A pipe may lose its shape and retain its lining. Destruction and preservation are not opposite activities in the felt; they are different amounts of the same pressure.

Where the mats deepen, cupped fronds arise.

A cup has no need to hold water. Everything around it already does that. What it holds is a small region of reduced motion. Suspended leaf-dust settles there, along with mineral particles and the fine organic matter released by other growth. Delicate absorptive surfaces line the interior. In some forms, the cup also directs seepage across its base before releasing the water through a notch or porous rim.

Many cups eventually complete their lives by filling. Sediment accumulates until the interior is choked, and the frond becomes a low, vegetation-covered boss. New growth emerges from its rim or from stems beneath it. Repeated over immense intervals, this self-burial helps produce the broad domes over collapsed chambers. Their height is not the result of any single plant striving upward. It is the accumulated failure of innumerable small structures to remain open.

The jointed stems are more openly provisional. Each node is a possible beginning. If the stem remains intact, nodes contribute to a larger body; if it breaks, a node may attach and continue independently. Old stems seldom disappear quickly enough to clear space for younger ones. They become trellises, then internal braces, then dark lines within the compacted heart of a thicket. Some thickets stand largely on the persistence of material that no longer grows.

The long ribbons come closest to height, but they are not tall in the manner of trees. They have no massive supporting axes and make no common canopy. Their thin tissues are lifted by seed-sized floats and, where possible, supported by the growth beneath them. A ribbon may spend most of its length in absolute darkness while its uppermost portion receives the faint seasonal directionality of the water. During the brighter allowance, that upper tissue grows and stores. Through the long dark interval, almost nothing happens that could be called activity. The float maintains its slight tension; the lower stem persists; water brings what it brings.

Not all the lower vegetation can be nourished by that remote light. Much of it lives through materials delivered by seepage and through intimate association with the films around its holdfasts. Organic compounds leak from living tissue, escape from slowly decomposing leaves, or arrive dissolved in water from other districts. Microbes in the deep fillings add their own minute production, using chemical differences in the mineral waters. The garden has several incomes, but none is generous.

Its luxuriance comes from retention. A nutrient may pass from leaf to film, from film to water, from water to another mat, and back into growth without ever traveling far. A fallen ribbon becomes attachment before it becomes detritus. Detritus becomes a trap for further sediment. Even dissolution often returns material to a surface only a short distance away. The Cistern is not productive enough to explain the mass of its vegetation by rapid growth. It explains it by losing almost nothing, and by losing that almost nothing very slowly.

Inside the conduits, life takes other forms.

There is often no room for cups or ascending tissue. Instead, perforated linings spread across the ceramic: fine pipe-lace, low branching webs, and mats whose outer surfaces are molded by the passage of water. Some are distinct kinds of plants. Others are growth forms of lineages that, outside the pipe, would belong to the joint-felt. The difference between a pavement organism and a conduit organism is not always a difference of species. Sometimes it is a difference in the space available around the same persistent body.

The lace lives from flow, but it also diminishes flow. Its filaments strain particles from the water; sediment gathers behind them; new growth roots in the accumulation. A conduit can thus become nearly closed without becoming inactive. Water continues through it as seepage, emerging along roots and masonry joints to feed growth elsewhere. The plant has not simply blocked a pipe. It has changed the pipe into a slower, more extensive organ of the garden.

Occasionally a runner enters one opening, follows the dark interior, and emerges through another. After a collapse, the connection may be lost while growth continues at both ends. Whether those patches remain one inhabitant is not a question the Cistern answers cleanly. They may be genetically continuous and physiologically separate, or connected only through a thread of living tissue inside a wall. Some clonal bodies have the shape not of a surface but of an old system of voids. Their extent is a partial memory of how water once moved.

Age is correspondingly difficult to assign. A leaf may be centuries old. The clonal mat bearing it may be much older. A thicket may have occupied the same shallow rise through repeated replacements of all its visible tissue. Beneath it, preserved stems and mineralized leaf-traces record earlier arrangements of growth, but not necessarily earlier deaths. The oldest visible structures are often no longer alive; the oldest continuous lives may be microbial communities within rubble, where no single feature remains visible for long enough to suggest antiquity.

Reproduction does not arrive as a basin-wide spring. A patch releases spores when its own stored allowance and local water conditions permit, while neighboring growth remains dormant. Most spores travel only short distances. Most settle on surfaces already occupied: on older leaves, on living filaments, inside cups, or among the crowded rims of pipes. Many never develop. Others persist in the sediment until a change in seepage exposes them to a different chemistry.

Runners are more dependable, and fragments matter more than they would in a world with gentler foundations. When a slab settles or an old support fails, the sound has no audience, but the displacement has consequences. A mat tears. Stems break. A small area of uncolonized fracture appears, and viable pieces of vegetation shift into positions they could never have reached by growth alone. Nothing rushes to occupy the opening. Films alter it first. Felt follows where water and sediment permit. Much later, cups may rise there. The catastrophe becomes a rare act of dispersal and an equally rare offer of unattached space.

The arrival of a water front is usually less visible and more important. As water with another temperature or mineral history enters a district, the microbial films change before the larger growth shows any response. Ribbons take on a slightly different inclination. One side of a mat adds a denser layer of tissue. A patch that has long been dormant extends a runner; another stops growing without dying. The front may pass long before these responses are complete.

Old thickets preserve such events imperfectly. Layers of stems lean in different directions. Bands of compacted leaves alternate with intervals of more open growth. These are not annual rings, and no simple calendar can be recovered from them. They are a memory of water: which way it went, what it carried, and how long the garden had to respond.

No inhabitant apprehends the whole Cistern. A microbial film occupies a pore and its chemical boundary. A felt leaf occupies attachment, weight, and seepage. A ribbon has, in addition, the distant fact of above. Even the largest clonal body is only a set of local persistences connected, interrupted, and connected again. There is no central mind in the thickets and no hidden keeper in the ducts.

Yet at great scale their lives amount to more than a covering laid over the construction. Mortar becomes film; film gives purchase to felt; felt traps ceramic dust; dust supports cups; cups bind fractured flags. The vegetation makes the water visible by leaning before it, and makes the masonry durable by slowly taking it apart. Neither inhabitant nor substrate remains a fixed category.

The Low Cistern is therefore not simply a ruin with life in it. It is a construction in which life has become one of the materials, and a garden in which masonry still performs the work of soil. A spore settles among its predecessors and fails to germinate. Somewhere nearby, without witness or urgency, a holdfast enters a pore that has only just opened. That is sufficient. The garden remains occupied.
