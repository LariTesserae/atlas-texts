The Held Rain is not empty, but nearly all its animal life is small enough to overlook, and the rest is rooted, filming stone, or merely passing through. A census taken at noon would consist mostly of absences: a clear pool, a dark patch of sediment, a fern with one damaged stem. The inhabitants become apparent only when water begins to behave strangely.

The foundation of life is the film on submerged rock. It is not one organism but a layered population, changing with heat and minerals. Close to the fissures it is olive and almost grease-thin; in cooler pools it becomes green enough to darken the stone. Some of the hottest communities live more on dissolved minerals than on light. Others form pale threads beneath the rust-orange deposits, where water seems, to the eye, entirely bare.

The clear pools are clear because life here prefers an edge. Very little drifts freely. Almost everything adheres, waits, or buries itself.

The rice-grain grazers are the most numerous animals. Under magnification they are flattened and many-legged, transparent except for a narrow green thread of gut and, sometimes, a few mineral grains held in their bodies. Their feet are fringed with fine hairs that let them remain attached when a film of water accelerates across a slab. They scrape the biofilm in paths no wider than a hair. A heavily grazed stone looks cleaner than the surrounding rock but not polished: under raking light it is covered with tiny, intersecting routes.

As they grow, they shed their transparent outer skins. These husks, caught in sediment or hooked on fern roots, are often more conspicuous than the living animals.

The grazers do not swim well. When climbing water takes them, their response is simply to fold their legs and become compact. In a full condensation cycle they can be seen passing upward as intermittent green flecks, turning slowly in a runnel or gathering at an invisible corner before being carried on. Most survive the journey. The brief rain returns them to the basin, though not necessarily to the pool that produced them. Some strike wet ferns and crawl down the stems. Some fall into warm grit and die. A few reach hollows that have never held anything but condensation.

Their eggs make this dispersal more effective. They are laid in a narrow band around a pool, slightly above its ordinary waterline, where casual overflow will not wet them. There they can remain dry and almost invisible through clear weather. A complete cycle reaches them. Many hatch just as the basin’s separate waters are being mixed, so that a generation’s first experience may be a journey upward through open air.

A pool can therefore appear lifeless while carrying a whole population around its rim.

The flat hunter is the grazers’ only persistent predator. The largest individuals are scarcely wider than a hand. They are not truly leaf-shaped; their margins are too irregular, their surfaces too smooth. They resemble leaves already softened by decay, which is why a dark one at the bottom of a warm hollow so easily escapes notice. Buried in sediment, an individual may show nothing but a shallow oval depression.

It does not pursue. It detects some small disturbance—the repeated scraping of a grazer, perhaps, or the pressure of tiny feet crossing silt—and responds with a single movement. The mud dimples. A dark edge lifts and closes. Then the hollow is still again.

No one would call it abundant. There may be only a handful in the basin, divided between warm hollows with no dependable surface connection. Their world consists of mud, a few overflows, and the brief periods when release rain joins one pool to another. Only then do they commonly move. During that dense rain, a hunter may emerge whole from the sediment and slide into a young rill, steering with minute changes along its margin. It can cross stone in those minutes that would dry and kill it an hour later.

A hunter can remain motionless through many cycles without feeding. It is less the ruler of a food chain than a slow accident that may happen to anything small enough to approach it.

The plants lead more visible but no less intermittent lives. The moss along the seep lines can dry to a black crust that seems mineral rather than vegetable. Water restores it almost immediately: first a softening at the edges, then the return of a deep green nap. It holds dust and the finest sediment, so that a seep which once merely darkened stone gradually acquires a narrow, spongy footing for other things.

The red-stemmed ferns are more deliberate in appearance. Each occupies its pocket separately, with bare wet rock between it and the next. Their roots often look confused on dry days, turning abruptly or running parallel to stone for no apparent reason. During condensation, the confusion resolves. A bend coincides with a line along which droplets repeatedly gather; a root that seemed to end in bare grit is found at the foot of a suspended runnel. The fern has not perceived an invisible wall. It has followed the history of wetness left by one.

New ferns do not arrive as a general greening. A seam remains bare, then bears one crozier, then perhaps nothing more for a long time. The established plants shed fronds into the pools, where the tissue is reduced quickly and the transparent veins remain. Those remnants, together with mineral silt and the bodies of tiny animals, are among the basin’s first organic deposits.

The waxed rosettes occupy shelves that would scald or desiccate less guarded plants. Much of their living tissue is held low, close to the rock, while their thick leaves throw off water rather than admitting it. Their small flowers offer no nectar and little scent. They are self-fertile, some completing pollination before they fully open. This is just as well: the basin provides no reliable company of insects.

In the deepest cleft, the crooked trees are not crooked because of wind. There is too little wind inside the boundary. Heat and uneven moisture have shaped them instead. Stems lean away from hot fractures; whole sections have died where a seep shifted, while others have sprouted from cooler parts of the same root mass. It is difficult to be certain whether the cleft holds several trees or one plant repeatedly divided and renewed. They set little viable seed. Their persistence seems to depend more on resprouting than on reproduction.

The soot-colored bird belongs properly to the dry cliffs outside. It nests beyond the pressure change and enters the basin only briefly, yet it is probably the principal connection between this wet island and other places. Mud dries between its toes. Spores adhere to its feathers. Eggs too small to see may survive a journey in a drop held against its leg.

When a vertical stream is available, the bird drinks directly from it, sometimes clinging to the adjacent rock and leaning outward, sometimes taking the water in a quick pass on the wing. Otherwise it drinks from an ordinary pool, examines the ferns, and leaves. The recently stripped stems are usually its work; it occasionally tears away reddish fibers and carries them off toward the cliffs.

Its single call is dry and abrupt. The humid air does not give it back as an echo. For a moment the basin contains a voice, and then it contains only dripping water again.

Nothing dens beneath the low bench. Nothing gathers preferentially around the ceramic squares. To the inhabitants, the visible artifacts are less important than a seam that remains damp after the surrounding stone has dried. They do not read the marks left to clarify the system. They read its consequences.

On cloud-pressed days, when the condensation cycle becomes nearly complete, the whole sparse assemblage is briefly outlined. Moss darkens beneath lines of falling threads. Fern roots acquire silver edges. Grazers rise through the absent installation, their green interiors appearing and disappearing as they turn. A hunter may shift in the mud at a corner where release rain will shortly fall. Even the trees’ crooked growth seems momentarily legible, each stem avoiding a route of heat or water that no human eye would otherwise infer.

The click does not summon them. Nothing emerges to tend the workroom-sized volume, and no creature responds to it as a keeper might. The rain benefits some lives and destroys others. It hatches eggs, joins pools, and returns passengers; it also scours away film, dashes grazers onto rock, and tears sediment from around new roots. At the basin’s small scale, habitat and catastrophe are often the same few minutes of weather.

Most of these organisms need not have originated here. Their ancestors may have come from hot springs, cliff seeps, and isolated summit pools, already possessing habits that happened to be useful. The basin is too young for every fitting together of root, grazer, hunter, and rain to represent long adaptation. Much of its ecology is still coincidence being sorted by survival.

But the inhabitants are beginning to change what survival leaves behind. Biofilm catches mineral dust. Moss holds it longer. A grazer’s body adds a speck of organic matter to a crack; a hunter stirs fine sediment that the next rain carries away. Fern roots widen the fractures they follow. Fallen tissue briefly slows a rill, allowing one more grain to settle. Water usually resumes its appointed route, but it resumes it through a basin fractionally less bare than before.

An older wetland would conceal these beginnings beneath layers of soil and anonymous growth. Here they remain separately visible: a green thread rising through clear water, a root turning where no wall stands, a dark animal waiting beneath a hand’s depth of warmth, a bird arriving with dry-country mud on its feet.

Nothing lives in the invisible house. Life has moved into its behavior.
