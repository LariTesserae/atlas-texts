A census of the hollow can be made almost at once. Understanding the census is another matter.

Most of its inhabitants are the pale plants at the seepages. They might be called sheathplants, though even that name risks suggesting that the folded scales are merely waiting to become leaves. Their roots spread in fans because they follow the wetted stone, not because they are searching for deeper soil. Where the mineral film ends, the roots end. They do not pry into the volcanic core or darken its surface. Above them, the waxy threads lift their closed scales into air so saturated that the distinction between drinking and breathing must be a fine one.

Their pallor is not the pallor of starvation. Green would serve them nothing here. Nor are the scales necessarily buds. To call them buds is to import an outside expectation: that closed things are younger than open ones, and that the folded state must be justified by some later display. Nothing in the hollow provides that later state. The scale’s fold may simply be its mature form, a way of keeping one moist surface near another without exposing either. What looks like restraint may be anatomy.

Whatever growth occurs in them is replacement rather than increase. A rootlet can extend along a newly wetted grain while another thins and returns its substance to the water. A membrane can thicken without the clump becoming larger. No old scales accumulate beneath the threads, no dead roots bind the clumps together, and no plant uses the remains of another as a foothold. Each is a small, persistent act of organization, not the first stage of a garden.

The water-louse is the only inhabitant readily encountered as an individual. Even then, encounter may be too strong a word. It does not come to investigate a visitor or flee from one. Its transparency makes it less a body in the pool than a slight interruption in the water’s continuity. When it rests beneath the margin with its antennae against a root, it seems to be attending to something too faint for any other sense: a difference in mineral richness, perhaps, or some trace given off where living tissue meets seepage.

It may feed there. It may take dissolved nourishment directly from the water. Nothing about the roots shows grazing, and the animal leaves no visible trail of having eaten. Its slow circuit of a few millimeters is not obviously a search. It goes, stops, and resumes without the stillness between movements appearing any less purposeful than the movement.

It would be easy to call the creature dormant, ancient, or eternal, but all three words claim more than the hollow allows anyone to know. Dormancy implies an active life for which it is saving itself. Age requires a sequence against which age can be measured. Eternity requires evidence that the same individual endures. Here there are no eggs, young animals, cast skins, or bodies to establish a lineage. Generations may pass, each erased as completely as it arrives; alternatively, the same rare animals may persist for an unimaginable span. Exact replacement and exact endurance would look identical.

The louse’s stillness is therefore not necessarily an interruption of its life. Stillness may be most of its life.

People have never established themselves in the same way. Their objects are present, but their occupancy is not. The bowl, flakes, cord, crescent, and grooves need not even represent a single visit or a single people. In a place where edges remain fresh and fiber is not slowly buried beneath dust, the recently abandoned and the anciently abandoned can wear the same expression. Nothing arranges them into a history.

That may be why the objects resist practical interpretation. Each seems to begin a familiar human category and then stop short of it. The crescent is almost a seat, almost a wall, almost a windbreak in a chamber without wind. The flakes and cord are almost a cache for a task to be resumed. The four grooves are almost a tally, though there is no fifth to make counting feel inevitable. The bowl alone performs an understandable service, and even it becomes ambiguous beneath the seep: a cup, a measure, or an attempt to discover whether water can be persuaded to gather.

Perhaps the makers were testing the hollow. Perhaps they were simply doing what people do when a place gives no reply to ordinary questions. A person stacks a few stones to see whether they will still be stacked on returning. A person cuts one mark, then another, because touch has found a surface that will accept a mark. Such gestures do not require a priesthood or a settled tradition. They could have been invented separately by visitors who never knew of one another.

Nor is it certain that the objects were made by people who understood the equilibrium. They may have understood only that the chamber did not help them finish anything. There was no sheltered corner around which a camp could naturally form, no dry floor on which to arrange possessions, no growing supply of food, and no sequence of light by which work could be divided into days. Nothing invited a second stage. Their leavings are not the remains of a life lived there so much as the remains of intentions that never found a reason to become elaborate.

A person entering now is, for a while, the creature most burdened by sequence. The visitor has come from somewhere and will need to return. Their body is spending food, their thoughts are forming expectations, and their sense of duration begins almost immediately to trouble them. They ripple the pool and watch it become smooth. They speak and hear the close surfaces take the force from their voice. They wait for another drop and discover that waiting does not make the interval measurable. Soon the chamber’s *almost* seems addressed personally to them: the column will join, the scale will open, the bowl will fill, something will answer.

Nothing native to the hollow joins in that expectation. The sheathplant is not frustrated. The louse is not lonely in any sense that can be inferred from its rarity. The nearly closed mineral shell is not enduring suspense. Suspense belongs to a mind that has supplied an ending and is now enduring its absence.

It would also be too easy to say that the hollow itself is alive, as though equilibrium were a hidden animal or a patient intelligence. The chamber shows no preference for its inhabitants. It does not protect the louse, cherish the plants, or deliberately preserve the human marks. It has no apparent will to remain unfinished. Balance is simply the terms on which everything there occurs. Life persists only in forms narrow enough to persist without legacy: without spreading, crowding, shedding, hunting, or building a past out of its own remains.

That does not make the life unreal. The roots still take what they require. The folded tissue still maintains its difference from stone and water. The little animal still chooses, if choice is not too large a word, to move these few millimeters rather than those. These are not rehearsals for living. They are living, denied the privilege of becoming cumulative.

Waiting is something a visitor brings into the hollow, like a bowl or a coil of cord. The inhabitants have no use for it.

In the warm darkness, a pale scale holds its own surfaces apart. Beneath the pool’s edge, an antenna rests against a root and then shifts almost imperceptibly. A drop falls. For an instant there is one round note, then the same silence as before. Nothing has advanced, but nothing has merely been imagined. That narrow persistence—actual, sensitive, and without sequel—is what lives there.
