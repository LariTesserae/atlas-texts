The Highwater Massif has no native human society and, at certain hours, perhaps no people in it at all. It is nevertheless densely inhabited. The useful distinction is not between islanders and sea creatures, but between lives built around holding on, lives built around moving, and lives built around coming back.

The holders cover the stone. On rocks exposed at low water, barnacles seal themselves against the air, mussels wait with their shells nearly closed, and anemones contract into dark, granular knobs. Their stillness is deceptive. Each is fitted to a particular allowance of immersion, spray, light, and scour. A few metres can make the difference between abundance and impossibility. Lower down, where the rock seldom dries, limpets keep shallow home scars and sea urchins grind themselves into rounded pockets. Sea stars work through the mussel beds. Whelks, small crabs, worms, and nameless things with jointed legs occupy spaces that appear from above to be solid animal crust.

In the passes, attachment is an achievement. A larva carried through the South Race may have only a brief slackening in which to test a surface and fasten itself. Those that succeed grow into forms suited to the place: low sponges under ledges, flexible red turf, flattened encrustations, anemones sheltered behind rather than upon the rock. Filter feeders prosper where the current delivers food without tearing them away. They do not need still water. They need water whose violence has a reliable shape.

The kelp makes country out of that crust. Its shaded interior is quieter than the open water and full of small lives: snails on the fronds, amphipods among the holdfasts, young fish suspended nose-first in the weak flow, crabs waiting for something edible to drift within reach. Larger fish patrol the edges. Urchins graze the clearings, and lobsters occupy the boulder fields beyond, where each rounded stone may have a polished threshold and a deeper chamber behind it.

Eelgrass supports a different population. In the old silt-filled valleys, its meadows slow the water and conceal juvenile fish, pipefish, small crustaceans, and crabs vulnerable after moulting. Flatfish lie on the open silt nearby. The grass is both shelter and larder, but not a permanent refuge: a gale can bury blades, flatten a meadow, or expose its rhizomes, and its inhabitants must redistribute themselves accordingly.

Above these fixed communities, abundance is always arriving or leaving. A plankton bloom is not merely food appearing in the water. It is a vast, temporary population, followed by grazers, larvae, and then fish. The shoals gather where submerged shoulders force deep water upward and along the lees of obstructions in the races. At full flow they hold in narrow bands, making small corrections to remain in shelter. At slack they loosen and spread through the kelp.

Their predators understand the same boundaries. Diving birds work the shallower edges and suddenly appearing surface schools. Auks continue the pursuit underwater; gulls and other surface feeders take what is driven upward. Porpoises hunt along shear lines rather than in the white centre of the race. Seals move between offshore feeding grounds and familiar haul-outs, some of which exist only at particular heights of tide. On a falling shelf, a few animals may lie close together; an hour later the last dry space has vanished and the shelf appears uninhabited. Larger whales keep mostly to the outer slopes. Their passage is less visible than the birds’ commotion, but their calls can occupy a great deal of the underwater sound.

Even torn vegetation becomes inhabited. A raft of storm-ripped kelp carries amphipods, snails, and occasional young crabs away with it. Small fish gather beneath the shade, and birds land to pick through the tangle. For a while the raft is a travelling island. Then it strands and becomes food for shore scavengers, or breaks apart, or waterlogs and sinks. What reaches the deep basins is no longer a colony. It is a delivery.

The basin communities depend on such deliveries. Life there is much thinner than in the lighted water, but it is not absent. Worms and bivalves occupy the silt; brittle stars and other slow movers gather near its rocky margins; scavenging crustaceans and deep-water fish make use of larger falls. Most food arrives as a fine, intermittent rain from above. Occasionally there is an event—a dense sink of kelp, a dead fish shoal, more rarely a whale—and animals collect around it with a speed that suggests how much of the deep population normally waits. Their calendar has little to do with daylight. It is written in hunger and arrival.

The emergent islands support fewer large residents than their greenness suggests. There is little to sustain great land herds. The thickets are shaped more by wind, waterlogging, and salt than by browsing, and an alder or conifer no taller than a person may have spent decades producing its crooked trunk. Small birds nest in the densest cover. Insects appear wherever a lee offers a few hours of warmth; dark pools hold midge larvae, water beetles, and other small freshwater lives. Spiders and beetles inhabit the heath and the litter beneath it. Higher up, existence becomes progressively slower and closer to the stone: cushion plants, moss, lichen, and brief summer activity among them.

Seabirds are the great exception. For part of the year, suitable islands become crowded marine cities transported onto land. Birds return to particular ledges, crevices, and slopes, often after spending most of their lives elsewhere. Their colonies import food from a wide area of sea and concentrate it as guano, discarded fish, broken eggs, and dead chicks. The brilliant vegetation below a colony lives on that import. So do insects and the scavengers that work the colony’s margins. When nesting ends, the noise and traffic depart with remarkable suddenness. The white rock and enriched green strips remain.

Thus residence varies enormously in scale. A limpet’s territory may be a scar small enough to cover with a hand. A seal’s includes a circuit of feeding water and several haul-outs, each useful under different conditions. A colony bird may be faithful to one ledge but indifferent to thousands of square kilometres between migrations. A whale’s Highwater may be only one segment of a route along the outer basins. None of these animals needs to remain continuously in sight to belong there.

Human habitation is real but never quite becomes residency. A maintenance party arrives with food, diesel, spare seals, electrical testing equipment, foul-weather clothing, and the knowledge that its departure date may be wrong. The station was built for machinery, not domestic life, so much of the crew’s existence remains aboard the service vessel or in a few strictly practical spaces ashore. Work is arranged around access: growth cleared where it interferes with equipment, cable landings inspected, corrosion found before it becomes failure, lifting gear used when the sea permits. A task that looks straightforward on a work order can wait days for enough slack water and enough weather.

These crews develop local knowledge without developing a local population. It survives in logbooks, annotated charts, and the memories of skippers and engineers who return. The official names of passages are supplemented by working names for awkward eddies, reliable lees, seal shelves, and rocks that reveal themselves only under one combination of tide and swell. Technical entries sit beside observations of kelp fouling, birds on the gantry, whales near an outer shoulder, or a boat delayed because a predicted window failed to open. The most experienced skippers are distinguished less by the passages they attempt than by those they decline.

The station itself has become a small, noisy habitat. Growth colonises exposed metal and concrete wherever it can; birds use high fittings as lookouts; maintenance includes the continual removal or accommodation of life. None of this makes the machinery benign. Its vibration changes the acoustic world around the Race, and its lights and vessels impose movements of their own. But neither does it stand outside the province. Like everything else there, it is wetted, fouled, weathered, and obliged to keep time.

The older human traces have been more thoroughly adopted. Ferns occupy the sheltered masonry of the pump cottage, and small birds can nest where its roof once gave cover. The survey pillar is more useful as a roost than as a survey mark. The field wall is a perch above water and, below it, a hard narrow causeway through softer ground until the kelp conceals it. These things have not gathered a ruin-dwelling culture around them. They have simply become available surfaces.

Across the year, the census changes almost completely. The bloom water fills with small life; the nesting islands become deafening; fish and their predators thicken along particular slopes; autumn weather tears loose kelp and redistributes whole drifting communities. Later, the surface may look spare again, though the mussel beds, urchin pits, basin worms, and old wind-pruned trees remain. A bay empty on one morning has not necessarily lost its inhabitants. They may be in the next passage, deeper water, or another stage of the same circuit.

At slack tide, these arrangements briefly become easy to see. Seals rest on shelves that will soon diminish. Birds raft in loose groups. Shoals spread out of their lees, feeding fronds open, and a service boat crosses water that reflects the cloud ceiling. Then the current takes up. Kelp streams in a new direction, fish narrow into shelter, birds gather above fresh seams of disturbed water, and the station’s note deepens through the rock.

Only the stone has uninterrupted tenure. Everything else lives in the Highwater Massif by grip, by migration, by return, or by an unusually exact knowledge of when to move—and when to let go.
