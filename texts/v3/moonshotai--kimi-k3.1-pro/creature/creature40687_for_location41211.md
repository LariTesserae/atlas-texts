A census of the First Hollow would depend on the questions asked.

By mass, it contains almost one inhabitant. By number, it contains multitudes: mites beyond convenient counting, grazers beneath every mature leaf, moths layered along the cords, larvae and transparent things turning in the basins. By the standard of independent survival, it may contain no separate inhabitants at all. Everything animal lives within a physical exception maintained by the plants. The grove is food, shelter, road, nursery, and the reason blood and body water do not freeze.

Even the grove is difficult to count. A leaf cluster may appear self-contained until its cords are traced through three other clusters and into the common root felt. Some plants may have begun from separate seeds; some certainly arose as extensions of older tissue. Their distinctions remain visible in small chemical preferences, in the color of a bladder or the spacing of leaves, but not in their broader behavior. Wounds, light, water, and freezing are answered across boundaries that ought to separate individuals.

Sex has nevertheless been retained. The reproductive structures are inconspicuous, forming deep among the leaf bases rather than opening toward the dim ice-light. Their pollen is wet and heavy. It does not drift. It travels on moths, and occasionally in water moved from one cavity to another. Seeds are rare compared with the continual extension of cords and roots. Most increase is not a matter of descendants going elsewhere, but of the same living body slowly becoming more extensive.

The animals are correspondingly small, not merely because large bodies would be costly, but because smallness allows them to occupy the grove’s narrowest reliable spaces: the water film rather than the open air, the gap between two leaves rather than the hollow itself, the inside of a stem rather than its exposed surface. Their abundance is an abundance of duration and contact. Almost every suitable place is occupied almost all the time.

The glass-backed mites are the most numerous visible residents. Their processions are not migrations in any purposeful sense. They follow the continuity of water. Where a film broadens, they spread across it; where it narrows around a root, they gather into a line; where a drop swells and cuts the route in two, the procession divides and waits on either side. They glean exudates, loose cells, and the nearly invisible growth that collects on wet roots. Their transparent backs become clouded with age, so the oldest animals look like tiny grains of rime moving illegally through the liquid zone.

They are also among the first inhabitants of new growth. At the advancing tip, before there are basins, suspended cords, or enough old tissue to support grazers, mites already cross the simple leaves. They arrive slowly, generation by generation, never far behind the youngest reliable water. Some carry eggs on the underside of their bodies. Others carry nothing but the organic film on their feet, yet even this is enough to transport smaller lives from one part of the hollow to another.

Beneath the mature leaves live the flat, many-legged grazers. A grazer may spend most of its existence on a handful of plants. It works along a leaf margin cell by cell, leaving a wound too fine to see except where the wet surface changes texture. Its transparent shell makes the process appear almost self-consuming: the dark leaf, the clear body, and within it the green line of the gut slowly thickening.

Their feeding is destructive only in the simplest account. Left entirely alone, the oldest leaf layers become too closely sealed. Moisture and old tissue remain trapped between them, and the roots’ reclamation slows. Grazers open routes through that felt. Rootlets enter their feeding scars, and material that might have remained locked in a dead leaf is drawn back into living cords. The cast shells of grazers are treated in the same way, unless they fall into a basin first.

There is no fixed state of balance. Too few grazers, and a region becomes dense and stagnant; too many, and the grove begins sacrificing tissue faster than it can replace it. The plants answer chemically. Leaves become less suitable, roots become more aggressive around feeding scars, and egg chambers that once remained open are absorbed. From the animal side this looks like a changing food supply. From the plant side it may be closer to inflammation. Neither side needs to understand the other for the relationship to persist.

The pale moths are the hollow’s most conspicuous animals, though they do remarkably little. Most of their adult lives are spent roosting. Their wings overlap so exactly that a cord bearing a hundred of them seems to have grown a single pale sheath. They respond chiefly to touch and vibration. When one moth shifts, its neighbors do not startle; each adjusts after a slight delay, and the adjustment travels down the roost as a slow opening and closing of wings.

Flight is brief and laborious. Courtship often involves no flight at all, only antennal contact, wing clicks, and the exchange of position along a cord. Adults feed little. Their important journeys are made between the hidden reproductive structures of the plants, where pollen adheres to their legs and the fine scales beneath their bodies. A moth may visit only a few such sites in its life. That is sufficient in a place where successful fertilization can be decades apart.

Their eggs introduce a more exact negotiation. A moth inserts them into living stem tissue. Too shallow, and the eggs are exposed to grazers or displaced by growth. Too deep, and the plant surrounds and consumes them. At a narrow intermediate depth, the stem instead thickens around the clutch, supplying moisture and protection while leaving the developing larvae a route out. This looks like deliberate husbandry. It is also simply the depth at which the plant’s ordinary responses happen to benefit the moth. Over many generations, moths that lay there leave more descendants, and plants that tolerate a manageable number of larvae acquire more reliable pollinators. The arrangement has become precise without becoming gentle.

The larvae feed within tissue, usually in regions already undergoing slow reorganization. They are hunted there. A white needle-legged predator can detect the minute disturbance of one turning beneath a leaf and spend a day arranging its feet around the place. Its stillness is not an absence of strategy but the strategy itself. It matches the plant’s small movements so closely that the larva never receives a single clear signal of approach. Even the final capture is slow. Afterward, the hunter may remain over the same wound for days, feeding and then fasting.

The basins support more separate lives. Each is almost a country, with its own population of blind ribbon-eels and its own drifting larvae. The eels do not require sight. They respond to pressure, dissolved tissue, and the difference between the rounded vibration of a falling drop and the irregular disturbance made by struggling prey. They can remain motionless long enough for sediment-fine organic material to settle on their backs. When several dozen lie together, a basin appears empty until some shared change in the water causes the whole floor to flex.

Their eggs are lodged in submerged forks of root. The young drift before settling, and many are eaten by other eels. Those that survive may grow old without leaving a single basin. Overflow occasionally connects neighboring populations; at other times a root barrier persists for centuries. Consequently, eels only a few paces apart can differ subtly in proportions and behavior. Some basins hold slender, active animals that feed near the surface. Others hold broad-headed eels that spend nearly their entire lives against the floor.

Their movements are enough to keep nutrients from remaining permanently layered. What they stir up is taken by root epidermis, consumed by smaller animals, or carried into another cup. The eels are not external inhabitants of the grove’s plumbing. They are part of what makes the plumbing work.

In the smaller water-filled leaf cups wait the clear gelatinous predators. Each is little more than a muscular mouth surrounded by a body that barely resists the water’s shape. Mites may cross one without provoking it. A larva of sufficient size touches it and is gradually enclosed. Digestion can take weeks, during which the predator becomes cloudy and then slowly clears. Some reproduce by budding; the young are carried away only when overflow joins one cup to another. Until then, several generations may remain within a distance that could be covered by a single falling drop.

Nothing in the hollow keeps time primarily by day and night. The stronger rhythms are those of water pressure, light transmitted through the ice lens, and the grove’s own tones. During the brighter season there is no sudden greening or flowering spectacle. The leaves are already as dark as they can be. Instead, cords fill more firmly, feeding scars close a little faster, and the moths make more journeys. In the long dark, growth is not abandoned, but it becomes more selective. Whole regions may live by dismantling other regions.

Sound provides the closest thing to a common nervous system. The animals contribute to it whether the grove uses their contributions or not. A dense moth roost changes the vibration of a cord. Grazers alter the stiffness of a leaf. Eels shifting together change the return from a basin. Even a hunter standing motionless for a day adds a small, persistent load. The grove could not see these animals as bodies arranged in space, but it may register them continuously as modifications of itself.

Its tones are therefore not quite calls between separate plants, and not quite the sounds of one organism talking to itself. They resemble a body testing its own extent and finding that the extent contains other lives. A pressure pulse travels outward; the answer returns altered by open water, wet tissue, stone, eggs, insects, and things too small to have names. Memory persists in the alterations. An old fracture remains as a detour in the acoustic map. A frozen cord remains as an interval that no longer answers.

This does not make the hollow peaceful. Eggs are eaten. Larvae are absorbed. Grazers destroy tissue that took seasons to produce. A cord may die while its animals are still dependent on it. When freezing enters, the mites withdraw along the remaining films if they can, and the moths shift toward deeper growth. Eels trapped in a basin have nowhere comparable to go. The grove does not mount a rescue. It continues where continuation is possible and reclaims whatever can still be reached. Its persistence is not the persistence of every part, or even of most parts.

A new extension of the hollow passes through a recognizable sequence of occupancy. First come generalized leaves, wet root films, and a few mites. Grazers appear when there is enough old tissue to make their feeding more than simple starvation. Suspended cords and deeper leaf layers allow moth roosts to establish. Only after a basin has persisted long enough to acquire a reliable population of drifting larvae do ribbon-eels become numerous. Needle hunters and cup-mouths arrive later still, when there is enough prey to make waiting worthwhile.

Thus the distance from the raw growing tip to the crowded center is also a distance through ecological time. The mature community is not installed in a new chamber. It assembles itself, over centuries, behind the advancing roots.

No resident knows the whole result. A mite knows whether a film remains continuous. A grazer knows the chemistry of a few leaves. A moth knows cords, roosting loads, and a small number of reproductive cavities. An eel may know one black basin with extraordinary completeness. The grove touches all of these lives, but not as a scene laid out before an observer. It knows strain, return, chemistry, injury, and the slow redistribution of water.

The First Hollow is therefore not simply a refuge containing many species. It is an ecology in the process of becoming an anatomy, while remaining an ecology: its animals still hunger, breed, trespass, and die according to interests of their own. The grove has not absorbed them into obedience. It has only made their continuation so entangled with its own that the difference between inhabitant and organ is no longer always clear.

At the center, a glass-backed mite reaches the edge of a drop and turns along its surface. Above it, one pale moth opens its wings, and the movement travels gradually down the roost. Beneath the root floor, an eel’s heart contracts behind its transparent head. A tone passes through the cords. Hours later, an answer comes from the growing end, altered by stone that has only recently acquired space around it.

Nothing visible hurries. Nevertheless, the whole occupied exception is changing.
