Nothing that lives there knows it is a chamber.

To a glassback, smallness is not a property of the place. Smallness belongs to the inspection glass. Beneath its hooked feet, a pipe can be a long country: a region of pump-surge, a reach where water hammer ticks through the alloy, a cable sleeve in which the busbars’ vibration arrives softened by insulation. The animal crosses these countries without seeing them. The amber lamp means nothing to it. Its world is current, contact, and the slight warmth of other bodies.

At rest, a glassback looks like a droplet that has grown a skeleton. Its two transparent shells enclose a pale, jointed body and a fringe of gills that moves continuously in the film of brine. A dark thread of gut shows through. There are no eyes to catch the light, only the shallow sensory pits behind the antennae. It knows the nearness of a cluster by heat, the approach of another animal by vibration, the state of the engine by currents that pass through surfaces and shells alike.

When a pulse enters the busbars, the shells snap open. Their closure comes a fraction later: one small, hard sound, immediately enclosed in the sound made by thousands of others. A glassback does not hear the chamber’s tearing note as a human would. It receives the pulse through its legs, through the pipe, through the bodies packed against it. A cluster may loosen and tighten again. If the snapping carries information, it is information of the crudest kind—contact, displacement, another body here. Mostly it is simply what happens when living tissue has been caught in the machine’s rhythm.

Their crowding is not quite society. No glassback remains at the centre of a pile, and none keeps a place at the edge. They crawl over and under one another, drawn into the minute warmth already made by other bodies. None is warm by any ordinary measure; each is only fractionally less cold than the alloy. That is enough. The resulting heaps have the architecture of affection without requiring recognition: body fitted to body, the coldest surface covered by another living surface.

On the ceiling, they become part of the plumbing. Condensation travels along insulation and mesh, then across the shells of animals, gathering at a leg joint or in the groove beneath a gill before continuing sideways. A drop may cross three glassbacks before it falls. The animals do not carry it deliberately, but for a few seconds each is a watershed.

Feeding leaves almost no sign. Brine is drawn through the gill fringes; the microbial film is combed from it and passed to the mouth. On the pipes, in the troughs, over the wet mineral buttresses, glassbacks graze without producing a visible trail. The film closes behind them at the rate it is removed. They climb the ridge’s needle clefts and impossible ledges, finding purchase on surfaces that have existed in their present form for only moments. Nothing is worn smooth beneath them. There are no favoured paths, no nests in the clefts, no territory held long enough to become territory.

Even what they shed is food. A cast skin may remain for a while as a perfect, empty glassback, transparent shells still hinged around the absence of a body. Then another animal finds it, or the animal that left it turns back. The legs, gill-fringes, and shell are worried apart and consumed. Nothing is kept as evidence of growth.

Every glassback begins in the sumps.

The larvae are soft, pale dashes among countless other soft, pale dashes. They hang in the brine like suspended rice, feeding before they have shells to snap, turning in currents too slight to disturb the standing ridges on the water. For them, the sump is not a small pool but a volume without visible boundary. There are gradients of food, vibration, and pull. The intake is not understood as danger. It is a change in the water, then a stronger change, then absence.

Others leave the sump, climbing a wet rib or the body of a larger animal. They harden by degrees. Their gills divide and feather; their legs acquire hooks; the transparent shells close over them. A larva’s suspension gives way to an adult’s continual negotiation with surfaces. Yet the intake remains somewhere in the same brine. Animals of every size enter it. What comes back through the system is feed for the film, and the film becomes animals again.

The microbial film is therefore both pasture and soil: a soil so thin it can be swallowed, a pasture renewed partly from the bodies of those that crop it. Its cells divide along the wet walls and in the sumps, are grazed away, and divide again. There is no visible mat, no colour spreading across the alloy, no velvety edge advancing into a clean place. At its thickest it is little more than a change in the way brine clings. Within it, however, is the chamber’s other multitude—cells, cell-ghosts, exuded sugars, fragments of shell and flesh, all being taken apart and made available. A glassback may carry, in the dark thread of its gut, material that once moved through another glassback on the same pipe. Nothing in the loop remembers the previous body.

Only the moss has a use for light.

Behind the lamp guard, the eleven filaments stand in the amber leakage. None is tall enough to make a meaningful shadow. One is so dark against the alloy that it resembles a crack; another holds a droplet at its tip and seems built mostly of water; another bears a small zone of newer green, visible only because the wet edge catches the lamp. Each is performing the whole labour of vegetation at a scale that almost abolishes it. A little light is fixed. A little nitrogen enters the living chemistry of the chamber. Cold damage is repaired. Something is leaked into the brine and taken up elsewhere.

Sometimes another filament begins. It has no opportunity to become a twelfth. A glassback finds the soft growth beyond the guard and crops it before it unfolds. No appetite is counting. No regulator recognises moss. The number eleven is simply what remains when lamp, shelter, growth, and grazing have repeated themselves often enough.

The moss is not a forest waiting to happen. It is a forest reduced to the exact dimensions in which it can continue.

Seen from the inspection glass, all these lives can be mistaken for one restless tissue. Animals replace animals; larvae replace larvae; the film is eaten into existence and grazed back to the same thinness. But conservation is a property of totals. It is not the experience of anything alive.

At the scale of one animal, things happen in sequence. A larva leaves the sump. A shell hardens. A body finds its way into the warmth of a cluster. A drop crosses its back. It molts, feeds, turns from an intake or does not. There can be a first climb along the mineral ridge and a last current pulse. None of these events becomes history, but none is made not to have happened merely because the chamber afterwards contains the same quantity of life.

A glassback can live completely without leaving a trace. It filters brine, carries condensation, eats its own discarded surfaces, contributes its small hard click to the unbroken note, and vanishes. The number of animals remains correct. That is not the same as nothing having occurred.

The ridge remains a sketch of terrain, and the moss remains exactly insufficient. The glassbacks, however, are not sketches of animals. They are not an early generation waiting to become some later, richer kind of life. They are whole inhabitants, with senses sufficient to their country and appetites sufficient to its food, living brief and unrecorded lives inside a minute that has been forbidden to end.

The chamber has no future. Its inhabitants still have lives.
