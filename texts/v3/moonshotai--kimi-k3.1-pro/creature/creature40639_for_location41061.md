On the plain, a pool is not merely a place where animals live. It is an assembly, called together for an uncertain length of time. Some of its members fly in. Some walk. Some arrive as specks of life in the mud on a heron’s foot. Others were present before the water, waiting beneath a crust that looked capable of producing nothing but dust.

After rain, the first hatchings are often almost invisible. Translucent shrimp turn beneath the surface, their limbs beating so quickly that they seem to tremble rather than swim. Flattened creatures with shields over their backs plow through the sediment, stirring up clouds finer than themselves. In pans that hold water long enough, tiny fish emerge from eggs lodged in the mud and begin feeding before the pools have reached their greatest extent. Beetles arrive on the first warm nights. By the time wading birds gather, water that appeared to be empty rainwater has become crowded.

Many of these brief inhabitants will complete their lives between two receding shorelines. They feed, mate, and leave eggs while the margins are still changing daily. Their remains enter the mud with the next generation mixed among them. A dry pan may therefore be fullest of life at the very time it looks most abandoned: not active life, but life reduced to specks, cases, and resistant skins, able to endure heat and dryness until another rain gives it a few weeks in which to happen.

Other animals wait with their whole bodies. Burrowing frogs seal themselves into chambers below the clay, their skin thickened against moisture loss. Air-breathing fish survive in the deepest, coolest mud when shallow pools fail, provided some dampness remains around them. Snails close their shells. Turtle hatchlings stay buried until the ground softens. When these creatures emerge, they do so abruptly. A hollow silent at noon may, by midnight, contain several kinds of frogs calling from water, grass, and mud, each gathering around a different depth and a different expectation of how long the pool will last.

The dry rises support a more private company. Ants keep their entrances in the lee of stones and carry their brood deeper when the surface becomes hot. Beetles spend the day in the narrow darkness beneath flakes of weathered rock. Lizards flatten themselves against warm gravel in the morning and retreat to shrub roots by afternoon. Even the smallest shrub can be an address for dozens of creatures: spiders in the stiff twigs, larvae inside the stems, pupae fixed beneath the leaves, a bird’s old nest lodged where three branches meet. From a few paces away, none of this is visible. The rise appears to hold one shrub and a little grass.

Because no pool can be trusted indefinitely, many residents are specialists in leaving. Dragonflies find new water soon after it forms. Herons and storks carry more than their own appetites from pan to pan; seeds, snail eggs, and the resting stages of minute aquatic animals travel in the mud on their legs. Smaller birds move between the bands of sedge, taking insects and scattering seeds along the damp margins.

Fish also travel when they can. After heavy rain, when one hollow begins spilling into another, they enter water so shallow that their backs occasionally break the surface. Slender, air-breathing kinds cross wet grass and saturated mud at night. For a short while, the uncertain drainage lines become roads. Then the overflow sinks, the connections close, and each pool resumes its separate existence with a slightly altered population.

The grazing animals make longer journeys. Their wide tracks show where several have crossed soft ground, but the same route may not be used again that season. They carry seeds in their coats and dung, crop the growth around one seep, and leave nutrients beside another. Predators follow neither fixed paths nor fixed herds. They appear where animals have been forced together: at a shrinking pool, beside a strip of green growth, or along the firm ground between two expanses of mud. Much of their hunting consists of waiting near a place where some other creature will eventually have to come.

Among the oldest animals are the stonebacks.

An adult stoneback is a broad, low reptile, heavy through the body and armored with thick, irregular plates. The edges of these plates lift and weather as the animal ages, so that an old one resembles a piece of the exposed bedrock more closely than it resembles its own smooth-backed young. Its color changes with its habits. Rust remains in the seams after it has lain in an iron-rich wallow; algae darken the lower plates; a long stay near a seep leaves the whole animal nearly black. In the heat of the day, only the crown of its head and part of its back may show above a pool. The resemblance to stone can persist until the throat pulses or a nostril opens and clears the water with a small, deliberate breath.

Stonebacks crop submerged vegetation, dig out roots, take snails and carrion, and will seize a fish or young bird when opportunity makes it easy. They are not harmless because they are slow. A crowded adult can turn with unexpected force, and its jaws are strong enough to crush the thick-shelled animals that accumulate in drying pools. Most of the time, however, its energy is spent on immersion, grazing, and the long journeys between dependable water.

Over years, these ordinary habits alter the ground. A favored resting place becomes a wallow. Repeated entry and departure compress the clay and carry away small amounts of sediment. A trail across a wet hollow leaves a shallow depression along which overflow later runs. None of these changes is dramatic. But where an additional finger’s depth may decide whether water survives another week, a heavy animal’s habits can matter to hundreds of smaller lives. Frogs spawn in abandoned wallows. Sedges establish themselves along their rims. Fish retreat into depressions first deepened by bodies rather than by current.

Stonebacks lay their eggs on sandy rises above the usual flood line. The hatchlings emerge small, dark, and conspicuously soft, and many are taken before they reach water. The survivors grow slowly. An old animal may have outlived every tree presently standing beside its pool, as well as several camps, paths, and names by which people have known the place. It is not a conscious keeper of the wetland. It simply persists, and its persistence has consequences.

Where people have made homes, their settlements are easy to overlook. A few broad roofs stand on a rise above the wettest ground. Sleeping platforms are raised, but not high; walls are woven lightly enough to admit air and, when necessary, to be dismantled. There may be a clay hearth, racks for drying fish, sealed baskets of seed, and water pots sheltered under the eaves. Some households remain for years beside a reliable seep. Others move among several places as fishing, grazing, and plant growth change. A dwelling may be repaired for generations while its occupants spend whole seasons elsewhere.

Their equipment assumes water measured more often in handspans than in body lengths. Dipnets have long handles for reaching from unstable margins. Basket traps are set where overflow slips over a subtle lip of ground, not across channels that may exist for only a few days. Flat sledges carry loads over mud. On broader pans, shallow rafts ferry pots, bedding, and small children while adults wade beside them. People dig channels when they need to, but few expect a channel to remain useful indefinitely; a cut that drains one hollow may drown another, and a path raised this year may be an island in the next.

Their knowledge of the country is often kept as a set of pool histories. A useful account tells which hollow fills first, which one receives its overflow, where fish gather as it contracts, and which muddy margin remains dangerous after the surface has dried. It includes the movements of animals and the failures of memory: the seep that shifted around an outcrop, the camp abandoned after water stayed too long, the pan everyone continued to visit for a season after its fish were gone. Such knowledge is detailed without being infallible.

Children learn it by walking and listening. They learn that clear water over sand, brown water among roots, and still water beneath a seep-darkened rock offer different foods and different dangers. They learn which frog calls come from a pool likely to vanish quickly and which come from flooded ground with deeper mud beneath it. A route may be remembered less as a line than as a sequence of conditions: pass the rust-colored stone; continue while the ground remains gravelly; if the sedges beyond are green to their bases, expect water; if they are dry, bear toward the trees.

Stonebacks enter this knowledge as individuals rather than symbols. An old female’s preference for a particular seep may be familiar to households that seldom meet. People use abandoned wallows, avoid fresh ones when an animal is nearby, and give the larger stonebacks ample room when they are traveling. No one with experience treats one as a fixed landmark. They are neighbors whose habits have to be included in a plan.

Toward evening, all these ways of living become easier to hear than to see. Insects take up their trills on the rises. Birds dispute the last crowded feeding places. Frogs begin in separate pools, their choruses overlapping without ever becoming one continuous sound. Somewhere a stoneback exhales and resettles, sending a small wave against the stems. Beyond the last occupied camp, a pan lies dry under a skin of cracked clay, its buried eggs and seeds giving no sign of themselves.

Nothing has the whole plain to itself. Life passes from pool to pool, on feet and feathers, through mud and memory, while much of it remains out of sight. The apparent stillness is only the interval between one gathering and the next.
