The people are the easiest inhabitants to count, and the least useful measure of the place. A few dozen live along the two short streets, in houses whose walls slowly thicken around them. They can all be at work without making the chamber seem occupied. A figure crosses the working lane, another appears for a moment beside a gauge, and then there is only the open floor again, with moths turning in the distance. Yet human use is everywhere: in the shallow bowls of the steps, in handrails polished to the shape of palms, in the clear intervals between machines, and in repairs whose purposes no outsider could guess.

The Sump-born keep time loosely by the surface day. Morning matters at the freight shaft; below, there is the long shift, the fast note, before an opening, after an opening. Sleep is arranged around work rather than darkness. Woven partitions and heavy bed curtains reduce the noise, but nothing excludes the pump stroke in the floor. Children grow up sleeping through sounds that visitors initially find unbearable. An unexpected silence, on the other hand, brings people awake and into the street faster than any alarm. If the engines falter, something has entered the body of the place that everyone can feel.

Their speech has adapted without disappearing. In the houses there is gossip, argument, instruction, and the ordinary talk of people who have known one another too long to remain impressive. In the hall, language becomes economical. A sentence may begin with a touch at the elbow, continue in two hand signs, and end with the shuttered flash of a lamp across the court. Some signs belong to the whole settlement; others are private to a household or a working pair. Many residents have a hand-sign that serves as readily as a spoken name. During the loudest shifts, even tenderness becomes tactile: a hand sustained at the back of a child’s neck, two fingers tapped against a worker’s wrist, a forehead briefly pressed close so that words can be spoken directly into an ear.

Newcomers must learn stillness. Not reverence—stillness. A moth in the hair will usually leave without assistance. A beetle inside a collar can be lifted out more quickly than it can be crushed. Bats pouring from a beam are not descending upon the person below. Panic wastes time, tears clothing, and injures more animals than it avoids. Children acquire this knowledge early, along with the rules of the trolley track and the difference between beetles that may be swept from a doorstep and a shining file that should simply be stepped over.

Those born below sometimes find the plain more difficult than visitors find the Sump. Above ground, sound arrives without walls to give it size. Wind touches everything at once. There is too much distance in which nothing answers. At night the absence of vibration can feel less like peace than like a missing floor.

Human work in the Sump is never merely mechanical. An engine keeper must notice where cicadas thin along a main. Someone repairing a dwelling must know whether a stem can be cut without opening a seam above the next street. The cistern tender watches pressure, fish, seepage, and the behaviour of water on materials that do not appear in any surface maintenance manual. Even the person at the governor does not conduct the opening alone. Lamp signals come from the machine house, the cistern court, and the streets. Someone has seen the bats loosen early; someone else distrusts the stillness along a pipe. The adjustment, when it comes, may be too small for a visitor to hear.

A repair is not considered successful simply because a gauge steadies or a leak stops. It must survive being answered. A replacement pipe may carry water perfectly and still alter the warmth or vibration on which a dense band of nymphs depends. A new brace may strengthen a wall while giving the root a shape it will spend the next twenty years enclosing. Consequently, some repairs look unnecessarily cautious. Old material is left in place beside new. A vibration is changed gradually. A leak is caught and guided rather than sealed at once. No job is quite finished until wood, water, iron, and animals have all had an opportunity to object.

This does not make the residents gentle in every particular. They scrape guano from iron, clear wings from dynamo screens, prune roots that threaten a pressure fitting, and swear when beetles enter stores. The animals are numerous, but not infinitely welcome. The root seals one leak and crushes another. Damp rots what oil preserves; oil poisons what damp would keep alive. People can be ruthless about a house, a belt, or a blocked drain. What they have learned is not to confuse a local nuisance with an isolated problem.

The root is the oldest continuous inhabitant, and the only one no resident has seen whole. It may be a single organism, several organisms grown together, or something for which that distinction has ceased to be useful. Calling it “the root” is practical grammar, not a settled account of its nature. It has habits rather than intentions anyone can verify. It grows around loads, follows seepage, closes old voids, and answers the compound vibration with absolute regularity. It cannot be persuaded by a siren, a ceremony, or an urgent need for water.

Residents respond to it in different ways. Some touch a wall in passing as one might touch the doorframe of a family house. Others regard that as sentiment wasted on wood. Both sorts check the same seams and read the same stains. Rust-orange grain around a buried bolt, a pipe vanishing into a rounded braid, a doorframe narrowed by three generations of growth: these are records as much as obstacles. The root does not remember in any human sense, but very little that happens to it leaves no trace.

The moths make the chamber’s emptiness intelligible. The broad, apparently unused floor and the open air above it are flight room. A stack of abandoned crates would not merely clutter the hall; it would interrupt columns of wings and narrow the hunting space of the bats. People crossing the open chamber pass through drifts that divide around their bodies and close behind them. Transparent wings catch for an instant on oil-dark hands or cling to damp cloth. At the flowers, moth bodies pack the trumpets so densely that the blossom seems less to have opened than to have become occupied.

Individually, the moths are brief and mostly anonymous. Collectively, they are one of the ways the Sump remains in motion. They rise before an engine has completed its change, fall as wings onto the peat, feed bats, enter the work of beetles, and vanish into root beds. It is possible to describe all this as use and still miss them. A moth entering a flower is not performing maintenance. It is seeking what it seeks. The settlement has grown around the consequences.

The cicadas are more intimately bound to the engines. Their constellations mark warmth and vibration along the pipes; their nymphs crowd the wet elbows. An experienced keeper notices an empty stretch where bodies should be dense and looks for the cause before reaching for a brush. Their chorus has no conductor. It approaches unity without surrendering the fact that it is made from countless separate animals, each answering a little early, a little late, or at a slightly different pitch. That raggedness is not a flaw in the signal. It is the signal’s living texture—the part no siren can counterfeit.

The beetles inherit what falls. They keep drains from closing, strip wings, and chew back the pale rootlets that continually test the floor’s open routes. In ordinary times people walk around their files. When the beetles abandon the rails, a trolley driver pays attention even if no lamp signal has yet arrived. Above them, the bats turn architecture into a population. A warm rib may be solid roost one moment and a hundred unfolding bodies the next. Their guano is filth, corrosion, and food for a root bed; their hunting is both necessary and merciless. Beneath the cistern grille, the pale fish belong to a smaller world altogether. They turn in darkness, nose at new movement in the water, and are not required to signify anything beyond themselves. The cistern tender watches them anyway.

Nothing about the opening reconciles these lives into peace. When the hush comes, it holds caution, habit, appetite, and work together. Keepers listen for the engines exposed beneath the vanished chorus. Residents on the streets look for seams overhead. A cistern tender may be thinking only of pressure; a child may be thinking only of rain. Some people are tired and know that wet tools and slippery peat will follow. Still, nearly everyone stops. The silence is too complete to walk through casually.

Then the wooden click passes through the chamber, and what follows is abundance without gentleness. Rain falls. Flowers open. Moths lift in such numbers that the lamps acquire moving shadows. Bats dive among them, and torn wings begin falling almost at once. People move to valves and gauges while roots take up water and beetles begin their own work. For a few minutes, feeding, hunting, flowering, maintenance, and death are all the same event seen from different heights.

Within the hour, the opening has become another set of small tasks. Wet cloth is wrung out. Pressure figures are compared. Something that should have opened is inspected; something that opened too far is watched. The residents return to hand signs and close-spoken words. The root begins closing over what it can use.

No inhabitant understands the whole arrangement. The moths do not know about the cistern. The fish do not know about the governor. Even the people, who draw diagrams and keep records, must repeatedly discover that one of their certainties was only a temporary fit. They are not outside the system tending it. They sleep inside its noise, inherit its unfinished repairs, and possess the dangerous ability to alter the entire chamber with a small turn of the hand.

No one owns the note. It passes through iron, insect bodies, human bone, and living wood. By the time the root answers, it belongs to the whole chamber. That, more than the houses or the census, is what it means to live in the Ninth Sump.
