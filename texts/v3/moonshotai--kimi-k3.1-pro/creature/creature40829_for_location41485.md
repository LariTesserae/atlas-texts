The Basin of First Noon has inhabitants, but not quite a population. A population implies separable individuals, lineages, and the possibility of replacement. Here, even counting can become a geographical mistake.

Begin with the black-green felt. It is not moss, despite its softness of outline, and it is not a carpet of individuals crowded shoulder to shoulder. Its filaments branch, flatten, and rejoin. At the bronze rosettes, several filaments widen into a common tissue before separating again; whether a rosette is a small plant, a node in the felt, or an organ shared by many filaments depends on the scale at which one insists on an answer. Some rosettes have six lobes, others nine or eleven. The number is not an age. None has ever been simpler.

There is no earlier felt buried beneath it, no leaf litter worked into its depths, no dark organic horizon accumulating above the mineral crust. Where filament meets stone, the interface is exact. Root and substrate seem fitted together, though nothing ever fitted them. Charge crosses their closed membranes; ionic oils remain warm within their carbonaceous walls; every transfer is answered without depletion or gain. The felt does not draw down the crust, and the crust does not nourish it in the terrestrial sense. Neither is consuming the other. Neither is forming soil.

Above this ground layer, the apparent struggle for space becomes more elaborate and less historical. Creeping fans overlap translucent ground fronds. Broad shrub leaves curve around one another with exquisite precision. A leaf looks as though a neighbor has pressed it aside, but there was never a neighbor arriving before it, never pressure applied slowly over a season. The curve is simply its shape. A fan occupies a narrow interval of light, but it did not find that interval after failures elsewhere. Nothing has been crowded out. Nothing is enduring shade until an opening appears.

Even the groves’ scent is not a summons. The faint green-pepper sharpness and clean metallic sweetness attract nothing native, repel nothing native, and carry no message whose meaning might later be completed. The scent belongs to the foliage in the same way its color does.

The feathered canes are similarly without biography. Their crowded stands contain no old culms and no new shoots. Each cane is complete at its own height, its fine lateral leaflets held in ranks that never tremble. Among and above them stand the ceramic-grey trees. Their trunks are supple rather than petrified, but there are no scars where branches were lost, no healed injuries, no annual marks laid down through a succession of years. Their malachite and copper crowns are not mature crowns. They are not the outcome of growth. They are the trees’ form.

Every leaf is also more than a photosynthetic surface. The nanoscale lattices within it participate in the same living organization that maintains the basin’s impossible relations. A leaf’s location is therefore as much a part of its physiology as its pigments or the oil channels in its veins. The overlapping canopies are not merely botanical profusion; they are a redundancy of living specifications, repeated in felt, frond, root, cane, crown, and aerial lace.

The grove does not calculate this arrangement. Calculation would require steps. It is arranged, livingly, in the answer.

That is why some residents refuse a census. The copper-crowned tree beyond a nearby thicket and the copper-crowned tree on the eastern skyline should not confidently be called a pair. “Two trunks belonging to one tree” is also only approximate, because it imports a stretch of intervening ground that the tree’s own organization does not necessarily use. Its continuous shadow is often the better guide to its identity than the map.

The vine associated with the black seam is stranger still. It does not pierce the stone, and there is no concealed tunnel in which the rest of its body might be imagined. Its continuity through the seam is one of the relations the groves maintain. The vine is entire where it enters, entire where it emerges, and not divided in the ordinary sense between them.

Conversely, the three thickets in the single swale need not be reduced to one organism. Each is crisp, complete, and separately sunlit. Their leaves do not interpenetrate, and their roots do not contest a common volume in the manner of plants elsewhere. One resident may thus be entire at several distances, while several residents may be entire upon what appears to be one piece of ground. Names remain useful. Numbers become commitments the basin has not made.

The reproductive-looking structures offer no escape into a more familiar botany. The vermilion cups are not waiting to be visited. The violet chains are not approaching ripeness. The small white chambers are not buds, and their hinged lids are not held shut against an unsuitable world; the world cannot become more suitable. Closed is their completed condition. Likewise, the seed organs suspended among the branches are not falling too slowly for a visitor to perceive. They are not on a journey to the ground.

Nothing native is dormant. There are no sleepers in the basin.

The lensback is therefore striking less because it is still than because it looks as though stillness ought to mean something. A hand-sized animal beneath a leaf naturally invites an observer to wait for the next small movement: a foot adjusting, a transparent back lifting, a dark eye altering its regard. The wait says more about the observer than about the animal.

Its six limbs rest evenly around its flat body. Its smoky back resembles a curled plant scale and bends the outlines beneath it with a faint optical distortion, but the resemblance is not camouflage. There are no hunters from whom it needs to be concealed and no evolutionary history of having been overlooked. It is simply an animal whose surface shares the grove’s vocabulary of thin layers, filtered light, and precise interfaces.

Beneath, it is warm. Where its underside meets the host plant, animal and leaf form a closed physiological relation. Ions cross their common interface, but there is no meal taken, no debt accumulating, no slow exhaustion of the leaf. “Host” is convenient and slightly misleading. The plant does not spend itself supporting the lensback, and the lensback does not feed without giving anything visible in return. Their exchanges restore both partners to identical states.

The two eyes are the most difficult feature to leave unin­terpreted. They sit deep within the animal, dark and apparently receptive. Yet no blink is being suppressed, and no movement of attention is impending. Their stillness is not patience. To call them watchful may be to lend the lensback a visitor’s relation to time.

The repeated asymmetric fleck makes its individuality as uncertain as that of the copper-crowned tree. Perhaps there is one lensback, entire beneath every leaf where it appears, its body possessing a geography as over-specified as the groves’. None of its appearances would then be an avatar, a projection, or a remote extremity. Perhaps there are several, indistinguishable because no separate biographies have accumulated to mark them. The basin supplies no native sequence of events by which the question could be settled.

Other possible animals are even less decisive. A pale, jointed spiral is visible through the walls of a very few white chambers. Certain violet chains include an amber, articulated segment unlike the links around it. These may be animals whose whole visible life consists of a closed interface with a plant, or they may be plant organs whose anatomy merely suggests flesh to a visitor. They provide no feeding trace, no change of position, no second clue. They are less additional species than additional failures of taxonomy. The lensback remains the only native form one can confidently call a creature without first having to defend the word.

At the largest scale, however, the clearest resident may be the grove itself.

Not a spirit inhabiting the vegetation, and not necessarily a mind: a distributed physiology whose tissues include roots, leaves, aerial filaments, optical membranes, and the conditions under which locations remain compatible. No instructions need to cross the basin between places whose adjacency it maintains. No correction follows an error, because there is no preceding interval in which the error can arise and wait to be repaired. The correction is the exact compatibility itself.

This is why the grove’s instrumentality does not make it a machine, and its stillness does not make it dead. Nothing performs a task in order to earn continuation. The groves do not survive by holding the basin together. Holding the basin together belongs to the same completed condition as being green, warm, supple, and full leaf.

If that condition is called intelligence, the word must mean something closer to exhaustive coherence than to thinking. No pattern brightens toward a decision. No charge gathers around a problem. No memory is revised, and no intention approaches fulfillment. There is no evidence of anyone within the system who might speak for it.

A visitor is not exactly ignored. Ignoring someone would require the alternative possibility of noticing them. Footsteps sound among the leaves, and occasionally part of a step arrives again from far away, but no native inhabitant takes the sound as a cue. When the visitor stops, nothing resumes. The silence is not a hush; no one has stopped speaking. The stillness is not obedience; no command is being followed.

What lives there, then, is the felt and its bronze nodes, the clear fronds and crowded shrubs, the feathered canes and copper crowns, the aerial lace and the vine whose body geography cannot summarize. The white chambers live there without ever opening. The warm lensback lives there without ever shifting its feet. Perhaps these are many beings. Perhaps they are organs of one. The distinction may itself be among the contradictions the basin has made compatible.

Only a visitor thinks the seeds ought eventually to fall, the chambers to open, the animal to tire of its leaf. Nothing native shares that expectation. The basin’s inhabitants are not enduring their first noon. They are complete in it: wholly alive, and not about to do anything.
