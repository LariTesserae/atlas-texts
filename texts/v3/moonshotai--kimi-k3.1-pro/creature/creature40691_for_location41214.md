## Those Who Answer the Knock

Lowland traders call them Hollowers. Their own most serious term for a resident means, roughly, “one who answers the knock”: the hollow-beam alarm that summons hands when a grate packs with leaves, a basin overtops, or a walk begins to lift out of its pegs. Birth gives a person a bed beneath a particular roof. This other belonging accumulates more slowly, through hundreds of small arrivals, usually wet and often at inconvenient hours.

There are perhaps four or five hundred people in the hollow, though no count remains accurate for long. Traders winter in and marry. Young people go down the mountain. Rooms are divided, lofts acquire families, and an old terrace house may contain three households still cooking from one hearth. A person can be born elsewhere and become thoroughly of the hollow; a person born beneath its oldest spout can remain, in certain important respects, a visitor.

There is no single Hollower face. Their shared appearance is mostly a matter of use. They walk with short, deliberate steps on unfamiliar boards and with surprising speed on familiar ones. On the walkways near their homes, many go barefoot, not from poverty but because a sole can feel a sprung peg or softened plank before the eye finds it. Trail sandals are put on for the outer paths. Clothing is layered so that it can be dried piece by piece: short capes that leave the arms free, hems kept clear of the runnels, hair bound close enough not to trail in cooking or fiber work. Metal ornaments are uncommon. A well-hafted adze, a knife whose edge has survived years of careful grinding, or a chest with a lid that still fits says more about a household’s circumstances.

They speak loudly without necessarily being angry. Visitors sometimes miss the sudden quiet that follows, when several people pause at once to listen to something beneath the conversation. In the market, a lifted hand may mean not *wait for my turn to speak* but *wait—the third basin has changed note*. Across particularly loud water, people supplement speech with a practical vocabulary of gestures: blocked, rising, downstream, bring cord, bring a pole, come now.

Indoors, damp dictates etiquette. Packs are not set casually on the floor. Wet clothing goes near the entrance or cooking yard, never beside the sleeping shelves. Work and washing occupy the lower levels; food storage rises above them; the driest sleeping places are highest and closest to the beneficial warmth of the kitchen. Cradles and baskets hang from rafters. Even a poor house has an abundance of cord, hooks, spare pegs, and folded matting near the door.

Dryness is a form of comfort more valued than display. A guest is offered a place by the kitchen where cuffs and hair can steam, and the offer is made without ceremony. Privacy is scarcer. It is maintained by screens, lowered eyes, and strict conventions about what may be acknowledged. Nearly everyone can hear a neighboring dispute; good manners consist partly in not demonstrating that one has heard it. When a new household forms, there is seldom an empty plot to claim. A passage is closed, a loft partitioned, a store room rebuilt, and the new family takes responsibility for a stair or drain as part of taking possession of its walls.

Water work is organized by **courses**, though these are not simply administrative districts. A course is a particular length of flow and the people associated with it: a basin, a drop, a covered runnel, the walk above it, the washing place below. Its maintenance crosses household boundaries, so that an upstream family cannot remain entirely ignorant of who receives its water. Membership shifts with residence, marriage, and ability. The arrangement is practical, but it also creates acquaintanceships that would not otherwise survive the settlement’s crowded divisions into roofs and lanes.

The nine spouts have use-names derived from their behavior—the thin one, the one that knocks, the late water, the spout that strengthens before the others. The names change over generations, and people argue about whether an old name still fits. Each catchment also has its listeners. These are not priests, and their authority is neither absolute nor safely hereditary. Some work chiefly by sound; others are better at reading tremor in a rail, tension in a wetted rope, or the almost imperceptible skin of still water forming where there should be a visible current. They learn by walking the system at night, during storms, and in the dry weeks when a small alteration matters most. A good listener can save hours of searching. One who raises too many false alarms soon discovers how quickly public respect can become public comedy.

Children learn the permitted order of waters as a rhyme, then spend the rest of their lives learning its exceptions. Their bark boats are play, but also instruction: they discover where flow quickens, where an eddy returns a leaf, and why a boat released in one lane may appear beneath a cooking floor two houses away. A child’s first serious water duty may be nothing more than watching a grate during a shower and calling before trying to clear it. This is treated as real work.

None of this means the Hollowers are unusually harmonious. They shirk, favor their cousins, and quote old water arrangements with remarkable creativity. Households near the clean upper basins can become arrogant about it. Newcomers may find themselves assigned more than their share of unpleasant work. Feuds persist over gutters, party walls, roof drips, and who failed to arrive during some storm twenty years ago. Yet a blocked channel turns private resentment into public noise. Water backing into a lane is difficult to disguise, and the household that refuses help today may need its neighbors’ poles and pegs tomorrow. People have learned to quarrel extensively without allowing every quarrel to enter the plumbing.

The hollow feeds itself from a scattered, contentious patchwork rather than from broad fields. There are drained beds on the outer shoulders, tubers and hill grain in the drier openings, nut trees tended above paths and roofs, mushrooms encouraged on suitable fallen wood, and shoots gathered from the marsh margins. A tree fall is mourned if it damages a house and welcomed, more privately, for the light it admits. The marsh itself is not regarded as wasted ground awaiting drainage. Old slumps and abandoned cuts show what happened when earlier generations tried to improve it too aggressively.

Grain is toasted as much for storage as for flavor. Meals are built from it and from mashed tubers, nut pastes, mushrooms, sharp leaves, and whatever preserved goods have come up the path. Salt, dry grain, and metal are the most dependable imports. Food is kept covered and raised, and even then storage is a continuing contest with insects, mildew, and the exploratory intelligence of birds.

The principal craft is fiber. Managed stems are cut, their usable layers stripped, and the material carried through the downstream soaking and washing troughs. It is beaten, dried, twisted, woven, and repaired. The hollow’s best-known exports are not delicate luxuries but cordage, strapping, packs, screens, and channel matting that can be inspected and spliced far from the place where they were made. Fine work exists, especially in light screens and close-woven containers, but most production serves the settlement’s own appetite for replaceable parts. Nut oil, compact wooden fittings, and dried forest produce also go down the mountain. Little exported is bulky; everything must repay the effort of carrying it.

Repair is a trade in its own right, and almost everyone possesses some part of it. Certain peg and lashing sizes are shared widely enough that help arriving from another course does not have to begin by reinventing the joint. A cracked vessel may become a drain filter; a sound timber is reused until its remaining span is short enough for a bracket; old matting becomes packing, then kindling when it is finally dry enough to burn. This is not an absolute prohibition against waste. The marsh receives its share of failed experiments. It is simply understood that replacing an object may be easier than replacing the level on which it stands.

That understanding colors Hollower belief. The spouts are addressed familiarly, thanked, scolded, and sometimes spoken of as old relatives with difficult temperaments. No one expects scolding to clear a grate. Offerings that might obstruct a stone mouth are considered worse than useless; the proper honor is cleaning its catchment, maintaining its channel, and leaving its levels intelligible to those who come after. When a weakened spout strengthens again after the dry weeks, the first response is work, followed by a shared meal near the upper basins.

Their moral language borrows freely from water. To *keep the course* can mean to discharge an obligation even when no one is watching. To *trouble the lower water* is to make strangers bear the consequences of one’s convenience. These expressions are used sincerely, sarcastically, and by adults attempting to make children finish unpleasant tasks.

The dead are carried to dry rock shelters farther along the rim, beyond the intakes and the saturated ground. Back in the settlement, remembrance often takes the form of maintenance. A step is recut, a basin rim made sound, a dangerous walk finally rebuilt. Names may be cut where water will not immediately erase them: beneath a tread, on the sheltered back of a beam, beside an older name already softened by handling. Hollowers can be deeply attached to particular objects, but they do not generally regard permanence as the highest form of respect. A level terrace that still serves people whose names no one knows may be a better memorial than an untouched house rotting around its ancestral furnishings.

Their relationship with the other inhabitants is precise without being consistently sentimental. Animals are often discussed according to what they do: rack-thieves, gutter-nesters, grain-biters, pool-singers. Fondness does not prevent eviction. A frog admired in a marsh pool is scooped out of a drinking basin. Bats may be valued above the lanes and screened away from a food loft. Birds are welcomed for their insects and cursed for their seeds, sometimes by the same person in the same afternoon. Children learn which logs can be turned to look for salamanders and how to return them without leaving the soft wood exposed.

Every market seems to possess a frog whose voice people claim to recognize. It is probably not always the same frog. Nevertheless, negotiations pause for it, and someone invariably insists that it favors one trader’s stall. The hummingbirds along the sunny market edge are watched with a mixture of delight and exasperation; their tiny territorial wars are considered far more serious by the birds than by the people whose awnings they divide.

Not all life is maintenance. There are bark-boat races, wagers whose existence adults officially deny, kitchen storytelling, and loud responsorial songs made of short phrases that survive the background water. Dry beams become instruments. Covered work yards hold courtship, gossip, arguments about craft, and long stretches of apparently idle sitting during which someone is usually twisting cord. Not everyone loves the hollow. Some people are made sleepless by frogs, some hate the damp, and some leave chiefly to live where a private thought is less likely to be overheard. Others return with spouses, tools, or new techniques and discover that their feet still remember the boards.

Late one night, after a shower has passed, a runnel beneath a sleeping lane changes from a broad whisper to a strained ticking. Someone in a kitchen hears it and stops talking. A neighbor puts down a bowl. The beam is knocked, though by then three households are already coming out with poles and spare matting. They find a raft of leaves and fine roots packed against a grate. The work is cold, awkward, and accompanied by complaints about the person who last cleaned it. When the water breaks free, everyone steps back to avoid the first surge.

No one calls this a rescue. By morning the grate has been reset, a lifted board has been pegged down, and the lane is again mostly useful for gossip and drying baskets. The town has not been saved. It has been kept. Among the Hollowers, that is the larger accomplishment.
