### Life Between Breaths

The Cistern’s inhabitants do not survive by being hardy. No shell could resist every return, no root could hold against every possible path of the water. Everything living there has learned instead to be sheltered, to be dormant, or to be almost indistinguishable from water.

The dark green mats are the oldest visible lives. They are not moss, but close communities of filaments pressed so tightly together that a hand can pass over one without catching a single loose strand. Their outer layers are dead and resinous, a sacrificial skin polished by spray. The living growth lies beneath, against the stone.

They use the machine’s light extravagantly and everything else with extreme thrift. During the brightening before a drainage, they store more energy than they can immediately use. Their dead are not shed but retained, first as armour and later, slowly, as food. Almost nothing is allowed to leave the mat. A patch no larger than a hand may represent centuries of growth.

Occasionally a surge tears one free. Most such fragments vanish through the walls with the water. A very few lodge in sheltered cavities, already oriented against the flow, already possessing the hard outer skin necessary to survive. The great distances between the mats do not mark the remains of a former continuous covering. They mark how rarely dispersion succeeds.

The waxy rosettes practise a different economy. Their white roots are primarily anchors, creeping into flaws too narrow to be seen and swelling there until the plant and the stone cannot easily be separated. Only the finest root tips forage. The translucent leaves take up moisture from the air and minerals from spray; their toughness is the toughness of wet horn, not of delicate foliage.

They begin to fold before the worst spray arrives. The change is slight: the threadlike leaves draw toward one another, turn their rounded backs to the wall, and present fewer edges to the water. In the lull they open cautiously, not all at once. Sometimes several cycles pass before a damaged rosette fully opens again.

Nothing visits them, and they produce no conspicuous flowers. They increase when a rooted fragment survives transportation, or when an old plant divides at its base and one part is carried away. A new rosette may exist for years as nothing more than a white root and a single colourless thread. Consequently, plants that appear to be of the same age may differ by centuries.

The grazers beneath the causeway belong to the same economy of scarcity. After a drainage, when the protected pool is still, the silt sometimes appears to rearrange itself. Only then does one notice the animals.

Each is a transparent, flexible disc with a marginal fringe of short legs. Its body is so flat that, when it stops, water seems to pass over a bare patch of stone. A faint milky line runs through its centre and becomes visible after feeding. It has no obvious eyes. It finds food by touch and by following chemical differences in the silt, grazing a film so thin that the pool looks bare even where it is richest.

That film is built from light, dissolved salts, and organic dust shed from the walls. Much of it is stripped away during every return, but enough survives in crevices to begin again. The grazers take only a few mouthfuls during each emergence. They do not school, and nothing hunts them regularly; there is not enough food in the chamber to support a predator.

They also do not wait for the visible signs of danger. By the time the current strengthens around the basin rims, most have already withdrawn. Some vibration preceding the audible tone passes through the causeway and warns them. Each animal backs into a crevice, folds its legs beneath it, and becomes flush with the stone. During the return, pressure often helps hold it in place. Those that chose poorly are plucked free and disappear. There are no accumulating bones or shells. The soft, transparent bodies of the dead break down quickly, and the water carries away what remains.

But these grazers are not the adult form of their species.

The adult lives in plain sight, on the high shelf.

The bowl there may indeed be a made object. What fills it is not merely water.

Beneath a shallow surface of true water lies a single transparent body, shaped so exactly to the bowl that the two are usually indistinguishable. Its presence can be detected at the rim, where the apparent meniscus is fractionally too thick, and at the bottom, where extremely fine rays converge beneath the surface. When drops fall into the bowl, the rings they make stop against something a little sooner than they should.

During a drainage, the animal closes its tissues and retains its moisture. The machine can draw water through stone, but not readily through the sealed, living lining of the bowl. During the return, the animal relaxes. Microscopic cilia open across its inner surface, and it filters whatever the spray brings: cells from the green mats, fragments of rosettes, mineral-coated bacteria, the sparse organic dust of the chamber. It feeds on almost nothing, very slowly.

It does not climb, hunt, or tend the shelf. If it senses anything beyond changes in light, pressure, and the chemistry of its water, it gives no sign. An animal so clear is easily mistaken for an object; one so slow can be mistaken for dead.

At long intervals, small discs separate from its margin. They remain nearly invisible in the bowl until violent spray lifts one over the rim. Most are carried into the basin and lost. A few lodge on the middle terraces. Fewer still reach the protected pool beneath the causeway. These become the creeping grazers.

Their fingernail length is therefore no reliable measure of their age. Some may have spent decades at that size, waiting through thousands of cycles for enough food to add another imperceptible layer of growth. To mature, one would have to find a sheltered concavity above the principal scour, remain there without being torn loose, and gradually spread until its body could hold a permanent reservoir of its own. The Cistern offers almost no such places. The species persists less because its young commonly reach adulthood than because the one known adult endures and continues, patiently, to produce possibilities that nearly all fail.

Whether the bowl was an empty vessel in which the animal settled, or a mineral shell accumulated around it, can no longer be determined without damaging both. Mineral deposition has had ample time to make those histories look alike.

The ring takes no account of any of this. It is neither keeper nor persecutor. Its light feeds the mats and rosettes; its earliest vibrations warn the grazers; its returning spray feeds the bowl animal and scatters its young. But a mat that spreads across a required luminous vein is cleared as readily as silt, and a grazer carried into the central sand is treated as another object to be sorted. Life persists in the margins of the machine’s maintenance, not under its care.

Nor has anything adapted to the individual character of a surge. The shape of a sand fin, the path of a temporary waterfall, the particular wall that suddenly runs with silver—these variations mean little to the inhabitants. What matters is the underlying grammar: warning, drainage, return, lull. Everything living has fitted itself into those four events.

At the height of a return, the chamber can appear wholly lifeless. The mats are clamped dark against their cavities. The rosettes are closed. The young grazers have become parts of the causeway’s crevices. On the high shelf, the bowl stands full and motionless, apparently obeying one more inexplicable rule of the machine.

Then the water settles. A green mat softens almost imperceptibly. A rosette opens by the width of a thread. Beneath the causeway, a transparent back separates itself from the stone and bends over the silt. In the bowl, the adult resumes feeding so slowly that only the water seems to be moving.

The Cistern breathes. Most of its inhabitants live in the pauses between breaths. The one in the bowl has made a pause last for ages.
