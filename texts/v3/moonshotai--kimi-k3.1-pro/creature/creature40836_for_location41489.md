## The Tenants of the Manifold

The first mistake is to count the mills and call that a census. They are merely the largest residents whose movement can be seen from far away. Most of the population is small, flat, transparent, dormant, or living inside another resident. Even the distinction between an individual and a place is unstable. A cableleaf grove may be one ancient organism flowering through many trunks. A mill is one animal when it walks and a small watershed when it stands still.

Beneath the leaf pelt, the wind almost disappears. The temperature may be only a few degrees higher than in the open air, but those degrees are a country. Soft, flattened animals live there between leaf and leaf: grazing things with no hard parts except their mouthpieces, hunters whose legs fold so closely that they seem to have none, and minute brood-keepers that arrange their eggs along the warm edges of roots. Many are nearly colorless. Their visible organs hang inside them like dark seeds in glass.

Not all of them remain active between fogs. Some dry around a concentrated drop of salt until they resemble mineral scale. Their tissues stiffen; their internal chemistry nearly stops. They do not quite sleep, and they do not quite die. When water reaches them, the scale softens, a filament begins to move, and an animal resumes a life that may have been interrupted before the grove above it was born. Others live rapidly whenever moisture arrives, passing through several generations during one long fog passage and leaving the next century to their eggs.

The black brine seams have their own brief populations. Within hours of a fresh seep, transparent larvae are grazing the salt films. Flat hunters follow. Things that appeared to be grains of mica open into legs, mouths, reproductive sacs. They feed and mate while the seam remains liquid, then encyst as it darkens and contracts. Their lives are not necessarily brief. They are discontinuous.

A mill begins life without an industry of its own.

The newborn is soft enough to crease where it bends. Its plating is flexible, its tooth rings pale and insufficiently mineralized, and its small body cannot yet retain the heat of its chemistry. It lives beneath older animals, pressed against ventral surfaces where the temperature changes from plate to plate. It takes softened fodder and brine from adults, but warmth is the more important gift.

Not every carrier is its parent. In a dense herd, the young migrate from belly to belly as adults draw together, separate, and settle again. Some are lost when they attach to an animal leaving the herd. Others survive beneath old mills whose own reproductive years are long past. As a young mill hardens, it ventures farther toward the cold margins of the body, then briefly down onto the pelt, always returning with the particular vent rhythm and step-knock it has learned to mean safety.

Its first plates are nearly bare. Tenants arrive by contact. A few transparent grazers cross from an adult’s grooves; cleaner insects lay eggs beside a developing vent; dormant films adhere to the new shell. By the time its back is broad enough to catch weather differently from the ground, that weather is already being drunk.

Adult mills have small, heavily shielded eyes, but sight is not what holds a herd together. They feel the charge of the root mat through their feet. Vibration arrives through leg, shell, and bone: the approach of another herd, the settling of a grove under unusual current, the distant report of stone failing along the rim. Individuals can be distinguished by the spacing of their knocks and the rhythm of their exhalations. A basin’s apparent stillness is full of these recognitions.

The old animals are not leaders in any simple sense, but they are followed. They avoid talus that has recently begun to sing with internal fractures. They return to groves whose roots can support heavy feeding. Their habits, repeated long enough, become routes; calves learn the routes while hanging beneath bodies that learned them in the same way. Nothing marks the path. It persists as a sequence of preferences.

On an old mill, the back is crowded into climates.

The transparent groove-grazers show only a dark line of gut as they scrape films from the plates. Their bodies carry more water than the grooves often contain, and smaller animals drink from them without always killing them. Beneath the warm plate margins wait the seam predators, so flat that they resemble cracks until their legs unfold. Cleaner insects open their iridescent covers in the humidity of an exhalation, feed, mate, and close again before the plume has vanished.

Some of these animals spend their whole active lives on one host. Others cross whenever mills stand flank to flank. Herds are therefore moving archipelagos. When two herds meet, their small faunas mingle before the large animals have finished assessing one another; when they part, they carry away altered worlds. There are groove films whose entire range is the set of mills that regularly touch.

The host is not gentle with any of them. A shifting plate can crush a nursery. A vent can dry a groove that has held water for decades. A single stride passes through the shell as an earthquake. The mill’s value is not benevolence but reliability. Its body continues, and continuation is rare enough to be worth almost any risk.

Above the vents, the pinwheels and other charged flyers gather in their glittering sheets. Each animal keeps a minute distance from its neighbors, balancing wing charge, wind, and the electrical surfaces below. A sheet is not one organism, though it turns and tears as if it were. The shadow of a sailer can divide it into ribbons; a change in root potential can draw part of it downward until the pelt seems to be smoking with reflected light.

These animals drink from mill grooves, lay eggs beneath leaves, carry pollen between cableleaf flowers, and transport spores whose own dispersal organs amount to little more than a dry surface and a hopeful shape. They are eaten in enormous numbers. Water rises through the food web inside their bodies and returns elsewhere as salt crust, exhalation, excrement, or flesh.

A sailer is mostly a method of not falling.

Its membrane is both wing and feeding surface. The heavier organs are distributed in thick knots along the supporting cords, so that damage to one region need not stop the whole animal. Fog condenses on the finer fibers and is drawn toward absorbent mouths. In clear weather, the membrane filters eggs, spores, winged fauna, and organic dust from air that appears empty until a sailer has been feeding in it for a year.

Age is written across it in repairs: clear new tissue, dark scars, silvery crusts where minerals have accumulated around old injuries. Even the upper surface has tenants. Films grow where fog repeatedly condenses. Cleaner larvae occupy the seams. Eggs survive in the lee of the cords. A sailer may remain above one basin while entire lineages below it hatch, feed, and return to dormancy.

Its young begin as small, almost invisible kites, feeding among vent swarms until their membranes broaden and toughen. Most do not survive. Those that do may take decades to become more than transparent damage in the brightness.

When an old sailer dies, it does not necessarily fall at once. It loses station gradually, still filtering, still steering with whatever surfaces remain responsive. Its final descent can occupy seasons. At last the membrane drapes across grove and pelt, making one of the few broad, soft roofs on the Manifold. Condensation gathers beneath it. Animals enter. Roots follow. For a while it is shelter and food at the same time; later, only its tougher cords remain, threaded through vegetation.

During a basin phase, all these clocks overlap without becoming one.

For the cableleaf, it is a flowering. For an old mill, it may be a period of unusually good feeding, several successful calves, and a gradual deepening of the grooves on its back. For some seam animals, it is the only active interval between long states of suspension. For the flying fauna, it can contain a hundred generations that know no other condition than abundance.

A single cableleaf flower receives pollinators whose grandparents’ grandparents visited another part of the same inflorescence. The animal partnership is therefore not quite between a flower and a species. It is between a flower and a succession. Mills die during the event and their carcasses enrich it. Young sailers feed above it. Predators multiply without ever bringing the numbers under control, because control is not what happens next. Eventually the current weakens, the groves settle, and the separate clocks become distinguishable again. The phase remains as eggs, altered root chemistry, surviving young, and new patterns of tenants on old shells.

Nothing large makes a regular business of killing healthy old mills. Their enemies are usually smaller and more patient: infection in a tooth ring, scar tissue around a vent, cold entering through a wound, a calf separated from the herd’s ventral warmth. Once weakness begins, the tenants respond. Cleaners gather around damaged tissue. Seam predators move from ambush to entry. Organisms that spent decades maintaining a boundary between host and environment begin, without changing their nature, to consume what that boundary had protected.

A dead mill remains warm for a time because not everything inside it dies on the same schedule. Fermentation continues. Pressure finds new paths. The carcass steams without breathing. Water gathers in quantities that attract life from bare rock where nothing has appeared to move for centuries.

Then come the crowded decades: hunters under the plates, grazers in the grooves, winged things above the last warmth, roots testing the softer mineral fractions. Later the carcass is a wet province. Later still, a dark one. Tooth rings become shelters and mineral reserves. Plates roof small gardens. For a time, the vegetation that replaces the animal retains something of its outline.

It would be easy to call all of this automatic, but too much of it is learned. Mills recognize particular carriers and reuse successful routes. Calves inherit those routes bodily. Sailers return to productive air and compensate for injuries acquired long before. Root networks retain chemical alterations left by earlier currents. There are no artifacts, but there is inheritance: a habit, a scar, a prepared root bed, a fauna carried from one generation of bodies to the next.

The nearest thing to history is a distribution—where eggs lie, which groves are vigorous, which herds carry which tenants, and where an unusually dark patch of pelt marks the body of something that once kept a small climate warm.

To ask who lives on the Manifold is therefore to keep changing the scale of the answer. At the scale of a basin: groves, herds, sailers. At the scale of a stride: calves, plates, fugitive climates. At the scale of a droplet: cells, cysts, eggs, and the animals that will drink one another next.

There is no empty stage behind them. Stone is a future root bed; a leaf is a roof; a herd is an archipelago; a body is a passage for water. The inhabitants do not merely endure one another’s presence. Their presence is the condition of survival. The cold remains on the other side of a membrane, a leaf, a plate, or an old belly giving warmth to a young one—never defeated, only kept waiting by an immense number of persistent connections.
