The year-round inhabitants are few enough to know one another by footstep. Outsiders call them keepers. Among themselves, they usually say only that someone is *staying*: staying through the winter, staying until the upper cistern is reliable, staying long enough to teach another person the eastern drains. A stay may last a season or twenty years. No formal vow makes it permanent.

They are not the remnant of a vanished population, and they do not belong to a single lineage or religious order. Some arrive with work parties and remain after the others go down. Some come because they are good with stone, gardens, rope, or stores, and discover that those are secondary skills. The essential one is attention. Nor are they invariably patient or serene. They complain about the damp, resent unnecessary repairs, and sometimes argue for years over a course of masonry. A keeper may be perfectly capable of cursing a blocked conduit while understanding that its present obstruction began before their grandparents were born.

Listening is their shared discipline. An apprentice’s first lesson is often to stand in a court and say what is absent—not what can be heard, since any visitor can hear the clatter, but which customary sound has dropped out of it. On a cold morning, a silent channel may merely be frozen. After heavy rain, the same silence sends someone uphill with tools. Keepers learn to distinguish wind from water, an empty cavity from a full one, and the clean knock of a mason’s hammer from the irregular knocking of a rim beginning to obstruct flow. They also learn human sounds without especially trying. Privacy in the town is mostly a matter of not being seen; it is difficult to cross certain courts without being heard.

Much of the rest of their lives is ordinary and occasionally tedious. Cistern caps are cleared and reset. Mats are aired when the sunlight is dry enough to be useful. Rope is inspected, stores are counted, wooden tools are moved away from active seeps, and blocked gutters are opened before the water finds a less convenient route. Most staple food and fiber still arrive from below. The gardens are important without being sufficient, and no keeper considers it virtuous to pretend otherwise.

The gardens’ true wealth is their soil. It is handled more carefully than any possession likely to be found in a sleeping cell. Windblown dust caught behind walls, useful court sweepings, rotted garden stems, and fine mineral grit all have their places. Water used to wash a bowl is poured where its sediment can be kept. A little earth spilled on paving is gathered up again. Established beds are passed from keeper to keeper with instructions that may concern events decades old: which corner dries first, which overflow must never be diverted, which shrub was removed because its roots were making the bed too costly to hold. People can sleep in almost any empty room. Roots cannot.

When a work party arrives, the human population changes dramatically and the town itself usually changes very little. More hearth yards are occupied. There is news from below, hammering in several courts, and food cooked in larger quantities. Arguments that had remained theoretical among the keepers become practical disputes over leverage and stone. Then the party departs, leaving stores, mended rope, perhaps a cleared conduit and a single new wall course. The keepers are often both relieved and newly lonely. Occasionally a visitor improves a channel’s tone while ruining its usefulness as a signal; that repair is discovered later, with considerable irritation.

The full plan of the town is older than any living keeper, but it is not a promise that crowds will arrive on some foreseen date. Its chief purpose is to prevent small works from canceling one another. A court may remain empty because its levels govern a drain. A chamber may stay hollow because its resonance is useful, or simply because enclosing it further would serve no one. Not every vacant room is waiting for a sleeper. The plan allows one generation to leave a straight edge, a covered channel, or a prepared foundation that another generation can understand.

The keepers consequently use the word *finished* very little. Things **hold**. A wall holds through frost. A garden bed holds moisture. A tuned throat holds its note after rain. A repair holds long enough that nobody currently staying has to think about it. The finest work tends to disappear: first into reliability, then into mineral crust, until only a straightness here or a hidden cut there suggests that water was ever persuaded.

The other inhabitants make use of all this without acknowledging the plan. Moss is the steadiest settler. Where a gutter seeps, it may establish a saturated strip far lusher than anything on the open terrace beside it. Where a cistern is capped or a channel rerouted, another strip may slowly thin. Ferns gather in the sheltered joints of work shelters as readily as in natural rock. Shrubs find the older pockets, including pockets that exist only because a wall caught dust and a moss mat held it. In the marsh, sedge roots bind their own unstable ground, indifferent to whether the water reaching them last passed through a springhead, a garden, or a drain.

Thus the keepers are always making habitat accidentally. They are also continually undoing small pieces of it. Clearing a channel exposes wet stone; rebuilding a lip changes the depth of a seep; leaving a springhead unmolested allows a whole small community to persist. Their practical rules are not an ecological philosophy, but they have ecological consequences. A town that passes water through so many hands also passes opportunities to everything that can root, creep, or remain unnoticed.

The dark snails are probably the most numerous visible animals in permanent residence, though no one has counted them. A lifted slab may reveal several clustered where the stone remains wet even in bright weather. They graze the thin living films, leave minute dark refuse behind them, and contribute their bodies and shells to the same slow accumulation as everything else. Keepers who move slabs for repairs generally put them back as they found them. This is not a ceremony. Wet stone belongs wet side down, and there is no reason to make a dry killing ground out of a serviceable hiding place.

No one knows how many black salamanders the marsh supports. The keepers can reasonably disagree about whether they have seen several animals or the same wide-ranging individual over many years. A sighting is noted, discussed for a while, and then allowed to become uncertain again. The animal is not treated as an omen or a guardian. It is simply the inhabitant most likely to be mistaken for a piece of soaked wood, and the one least impressed by the noise of the waterworks.

The slate-colored bird is not, strictly speaking, an inhabitant at all. It drinks and leaves. Yet its brief visits may have consequences longer than a keeper’s entire stay. A seed can arrive in mud on a foot; spores and smaller things can travel by means nobody observes. Most such arrivals must fail. The stone is too bare, the pocket too dry, the winter too sharp, or the seep changes before roots can hold. Establishment is the rare event, not arrival. The bird matters because the basin’s future population may enter it in forms too small for anyone to recognize at the time.

Human absence would alter the basin without ending it. Certain conduits would narrow. Some gardens would dry while others spread beyond their beds. Walls would create shade and catch dust until their original purposes became difficult to read. Human presence alters it without mastering it. The keepers can hurry soil formation in one place, preserve moss in another, and persuade a spring to water a bed rather than a bare face of stone. They cannot persuade the terrace to stop depositing, or make the marsh deeper by declaring that it ought to be.

A gardener in the central court may therefore spend an afternoon moving no more earth than fits into a single basket. To a visitor, it looks like almost no work at all. Then one expected note fails beneath the paving, and the gardener sets the basket down and goes immediately. A small obstruction may be cleared in minutes. The bed will require generations. Both deserve attention.

The town has few people, the marsh few animals, and the soil almost no depth. But the basin is already inhabited. It is simply still becoming a place where more things can live.
