Almost nothing lives in the Unfinished Sea, and nothing lives there with tenure. Its inhabitants do not form a community spread through the gulf. They form a brief, recurring occupation of new stone.

The sequence is always much the same. A surface hardens. A film develops upon it, too thin and colorless to see except as a momentary slickness when the light changes. Much of the film is mineral, but not all. Within it are simple living cells, the sea’s most numerous inhabitants and its least conspicuous. They spread across a ledge while the ledge is still developing seams. They never become a felted mat or a visible meadow. Before they can acquire any thickness, the stone beneath them splits, and their entire world goes down in a pale trail.

Nothing becomes encrusted. Encrustation requires a future.

The olive algal cords are the only inhabitants easily visible from above. Their simplicity is deceptive. The small anchoring disc is the most elaborate part of the plant: tough at the rim, almost translucent at the center, and fitted so closely to the flaws in fresh rock that it seems less grown than set into it. The cord itself offers as little resistance as possible. It does not branch, broaden, or stiffen. When the current reverses, it turns wholesale; when turbulence catches it, it lashes, folds, and streams out again.

A long cord is not necessarily an old one. Its length records uninterrupted luck. Some anchors may persist while the cord above them is repeatedly torn away and renewed. Others bleach while still short, their ledges already whitening along a fracture. When the rock fails, the plant does not drift off to colonize another surface. Usually it simply goes down with the stone.

How new anchors arrive on newly formed walls is unknown. Nothing resembling a seed or spore has been reliably recovered from the water. The distances between plants make ordinary dispersal seem improbable, but the sea is full of improbable transports: a bubble diving where it should rise, a fragment carried sideways through still water, a current touching one small facet of wall and no other. Each cord may be less the descendant of a neighboring plant than the survivor of an accident.

The transparent crustaceans belong more to the anchors than to the open sea. In the lee of a disc there is sometimes a pocket of comparatively slack water, no larger than a cupped hand. There the animals flatten themselves against the stone. Most are smaller than a grain of rice, with hooked feet and a single dark thread of gut visible through the body. In calm water they make short excursions over the film, feeding and returning. At any change in pressure they draw their limbs inward and become nearly indistinguishable from grains of clear grit.

They are poor swimmers because swimming is not their usual condition. To be suspended in open water is already a disaster. One seen there does not dart toward shelter; it merely flexes, altering its course within the current, and disappears. No eggs, gathered young, or crowded molting shells have been found. They appear around a few anchors and are absent around most. Their life history is mostly inferred from the fact that, occasionally, they are there again.

Only the pale swimmer seems to use the sea itself rather than endure a surface within it.

Its body is widest behind the small head and thins gradually, without a distinct tail. The whole animal can make the slight, delayed correction that a tail would ordinarily provide. Its gill fringes stand along the forward part of its sides, opening softly when it rests against a wall and closing when it moves into rougher water. The eyes are minute and set low, useful at grazing distance but seemingly of little help in the larger gulf.

It feeds on new stone. Moving with painful slowness, it presses its mouth to the film and advances, leaving a narrow, cleaner track. It may spend hours on a ledge that formed the previous day. Then the wall shudders, or some pressure change passes through the water, and the animal releases its hold.

It does not flee by speed. It folds, turns, and lets the current take the force out of its own body. Seen from above, its gills draw in, its pale width becomes a line, and the line becomes a fleck. A moment later there is only green-gray distance. This looks like vanishing. It may be no more than an excellent use of clear water.

The absence of a second swimmer remains the strongest fact about it. There are no confirmed young, no paired grazing tracks, no bones, and no carcass drawn down through the water. The evidence supports an animal more confidently than it supports a population. There may be one very old individual. There may be several, each keeping to an enormous and separate reach. It may not breed in the visible basin at all, or may come up from depths whose stillness and extent cannot be guessed from the rim. Whatever the case, it is the only inhabitant that does not seem merely to have been stranded there. Its caution is not frantic. It feeds slowly in a place where slowness would appear to be the first luxury to disappear.

Nothing else has established itself. Insects, feathers, seeds, and occasional bodies of small land animals enter with weather and meltwater; they are arrivals, not residents. There are no surface schools, no nesting birds, no shelled things accumulating in crevices. The clear depths invite speculation about larger life, but clarity is not evidence. No shoal rises. Nothing calls through the noise.

People, too, were only conditional inhabitants. The roofless cell seems suited to one person at a time: a little fuel, a tool, a place to wait with the back out of the wind. Whoever came there returned often enough to leave equipment, but not to make a home. The weighted fiber line was probably used as much for feeling as for measuring. Held in the hand, it would have reported changes in tension, sideways pull, sudden slack—the water’s movements translated into something a person could compare from one visit to the next. Its clean break is the last measurement it gives.

The cuts on the cup need not record fear. Cold hands, failing light, and haste can make the same crowded marks. What they show more certainly is an attempt to find repetition: a depth that could be sounded twice, a ledge that survived a counted interval, a water level that answered rain or refused to answer it.

That distinguishes the human visitors from everything else living there. The algae required only a holdfast and a little time. The crustaceans required a pocket of slacker water. The swimmer required fresh film and room to turn. People required the sea to remain comparable to itself.

It did not.

And so the Unfinished Sea remains almost empty, not because life has failed to arrive, but because arriving is so much easier than remaining. It does not yet support an ecology. It supports the repeated beginning of one: stone, film, cord, a few transparent lives, a pale grazing passage—then fracture, milkiness, distance, and new stone again.
