No one lives in the Return Sump. But it is not uninhabited.

Nothing lives for long in the open water. A few cells pass through it, a spore sometimes turns slowly in the condensation ripples, and now and then something small is lifted from a trough and carried toward the weir. The pool is a passage, not a habitat. Its clarity is not the clarity of sterility, but of rapid removal: anything that might cloud it is either detained in the filters or sent onward.

The residents are in the troughs.

Take out one piece of coke, no larger than a walnut, and most of the sump’s population comes with it. To the hand it is merely wet and rough. Under magnification its pores are occupied. Bacterial films line the surfaces in layers too thin to see directly, patchy where the water moves quickly, denser in sheltered cavities. Fungal threads follow the remains of dead roots, crossing from grain to grain. In the better-lit places there are a few algal cells; deeper down, where light never reaches, the community lives on what the roots release, on fallen tissue, and on the meagre ration of dissolved matter brought in by the return water.

These growths are the surviving workforce of the filtration system. The station no longer needs them to be industrious, and no one comes to clean or replace their beds, but they continue the work for which the troughs were built. They take up substances from the water, convert some, store some, and release others. A root dies and is opened cell by cell. A frond collapses against the media and loses first its soft green tissue, then its finer veins. What remains becomes dark fibre, then particles small enough to be carried deeper. Nutrients pass back into the water film and are taken up elsewhere. The surplus goes over the weir.

That is why no soil has accumulated. The room has warmth, moisture, plants and time—the usual materials with which a floor might gradually disappear beneath litter—but production and removal remain too nearly balanced. Nothing stays long enough to become a deep, independent earth. The ferns still stand over black coke and slag. Their apparent luxuriance rests on an extremely narrow budget.

There are animals among the films, though not enough to make a second, hidden abundance. A drop taken from the lower bed may contain a ciliate rowing through a space between grains, an amoeba extended along a bacterial patch, or a rotifer attached by its foot and drawing particles toward its crown. Occasionally a thread-fine nematode works through a root channel. Another drop may contain nothing visible at all. The great roar that excludes birds, frogs and almost everything else does not exclude these. It reaches them as a condition of the substrate, if it reaches them as anything. They have no silence with which to compare it.

For such inhabitants, the falling ceiling drops are more consequential than the pumps. Each drop strikes without warning and without any audible announcement. Somewhere in the troughs it breaks a surface film, displaces a few grazers, moves a fragment of liverwort, or opens a temporary route between pores. Then the film closes. Nothing in the room records the event.

Above this hidden population, the plants are not a single green mass but several different ways of enduring the same restrictions.

The mosses hold water rather than seek it. Their cushions are thickest where the ceramic rims remain damp without being continuously scoured. The upper leaves are bright and closely packed; below them lie older brown stems and dead tissue, still bound into the cushion. Fragments broken off by dripping water sometimes establish a new cushion a short distance away. More often they lodge, remain green for a while, and then darken.

The ferns do the deeper work. Their pale roots pass through the moss without feeding on it and enter the angular filter beds, branching wherever a persistent film allows. Some rhizomes may be old, though the fronds above them are not. A broad frond and a finely divided one may be present together for months, giving the trough an appearance of established vegetation, while at root level old pathways are always dying and new ones replacing them. Spores form, but the humid stillness is not favourable to wide dispersal. Many fall directly into water or onto bare concrete. Most of the ferns’ slow movement is accomplished through the rhizomes, not through the air.

The liverworts occupy places too narrow and discontinuous for anything larger: the seam beside a brick, the join beneath a ceramic section, the shaded underside of a rim. Their flat growth follows the capillary film almost exactly. Where the film stops, they stop. There is no broad dry interval between the green edge and the bare surface, only a narrowing band in which smaller and simpler growth survives.

Pennywort makes the most visible attempts to leave. It puts a runner over the rim, then another, rooting where it can and holding its round leaves above the dark media. Some stems lean far enough for their leaves to touch the pool. A few extend into positions that look promising and fail there. The end browns; the runner withdraws by dying; another grows elsewhere. The fixed green margin is not restraint. It is the accumulated result of innumerable small attempts that did not succeed.

The bare vault, therefore, is not bare because life has only recently arrived. It is bare because arrival has never been enough.

Under the unchanging lamps there is no common season of growth. A fern may unroll a frond while another trough is shedding old tissue. Moss can brighten after a slight alteration in condensation without anything else responding. There is no spring in which the whole room participates. Nor is there any visible senescence of the whole. Individuals age, but the community does not advance toward a forest, a marsh, or a thicket. It remains a lining because every other possibility is terminated by dryness, lack of nourishment, or the overflow.

The snail on the weir belongs to a different category of life. It is not the sump’s secret custodian, and probably not the descendant of a long-established population. It may have arrived on a maintenance boot, in vegetation carried through an inspection opening, or by some improbable route through the return system. For a while it finds food in the film on the ceramic. Its shell turns almost imperceptibly in the plain light. Behind it, the condensation is disturbed in a faint line.

Then, on another visit, it is gone.

Nothing requires that its fate be mysterious. It may have died, been washed through the outlet, or withdrawn into some temporary refuge before being lost. Its track does not remain. It has visited a place in which almost everything necessary for its continued existence is present except sufficient food and the likelihood of another snail arriving at the same time.

Human visitors are briefer still. They bring heat, breath, perhaps a few spores or fibres on their clothing. They stand in the open centre because there is nowhere else to stand, and their presence is immediately disproportionate. When they leave, nothing follows them to the door. Nothing has become accustomed to them.

If the Return Sump can be said to have one native inhabitant, it is the whole wet margin: the roots, cushions, films and decomposers together, together with the sparse life grazing among them. That is a convenience of description, not the discovery of a larger creature. The margin has no gathered senses, no memory of the drawing office, no knowledge of the sea beyond the lowland. It does not interpret the pumps. It is not the mind or spirit of the station.

Its continuity is simpler and more exacting. Cells divide and are grazed. Roots enter spaces vacated by other roots. Leaves replace leaves. Material arrives, is used, and leaves. What persists is not ancient tissue but a set of rates that remain close enough to balance.

When the steel door closes, the room is therefore neither awake nor asleep. The hidden work continues at the edges, while the centre remains clear and unoccupied. The green does not advance. The filters do not fill with soil. The snail’s track, if there was one, disappears into the general wetness. Nothing there is waiting for release, and nothing needs the room to become anything else.
