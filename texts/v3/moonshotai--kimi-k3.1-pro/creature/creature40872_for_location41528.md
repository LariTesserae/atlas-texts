## What Lives in the Ninth Intake

The Ninth Intake has almost no inhabitants that could be caught in a net. Its fuller census would have to be taken with gauges.

The pale crab is the only resident with anything like an address. It belongs to the lowest valve pit, or at least returns there often enough that the distinction hardly matters. It has learned the shifts. During the draw it remains deep in the pit, where the current slides over without lifting it. Before the release it moves slightly behind the valve housing, and the rising silt leaves it enclosed in a narrow, clear pocket. The careful movement of its claw is not maintenance, although it occasionally frees grit from a seam or turns over some minute edible thing. Mostly it appears to be testing the water: weight first, then motion, then stillness again.

There may have been only one crab for a very long time. More likely, one pale crab has occasionally replaced another, and the Intake has made no distinction.

The transparent fish has no address. It is a brief condition of the green intake lamp. When the pumps are laboring, it appears at the edge of the illumination, its body visible only when a flex of its spine bends the light. It holds station without being drawn toward the grate. Perhaps it feeds on particles too sparse and fine to cloud the water; perhaps the current merely gives it something against which to rest. It always leaves before the release. No nest, eggs, or second fish have ever been associated with it. Its rarity is not necessarily evidence of a small population. In the Intake, a creature could be numerous and still occur only one at a time.

The filaments are the only visibly populous life. They have colonies, older growth, broken edges, patches that do not recover. Their relationship with electricity is not simply attraction. Current changes them. Under leakage their tissues lift apart and expose more surface to the water; without it they lie against the junction boxes and become almost indistinguishable from mineral seams. Whatever nourishment they obtain is so slight that growth and damage are nearly balanced. A colony can look dead for years and then add one transparent strand beside a warm exchanger.

But these are not what keeps the settlement from being empty.

The rest of the population lives in pressure.

Each of these inhabitants is a local depth that has persisted long enough to acquire habits. It gathers when the intake draws, yields during the release, extends through certain pipes and stops at certain open thresholds. It has a boundary, though not a surface. It can be compressed, divided, or injured by the opening of a valve. If its pressure is allowed to become continuous with the surrounding sea, nothing remains: no body, no residue, not even a characteristic drift of silt.

They might be called ghosts, but nothing about them remembers a face, a name, or the surface. They are closer to posts that have never become vacant. If they need a name, *the kept* is more accurate than *keepers*. They keep no records and protect no town. They are simply kept apart.

The greatest of them occupies the pump hall. It is not a shape moving between the pistons; its extent includes the pistons, the water around them, and part of the pipework beyond the walls. During the heavy stroke it becomes more distinctly itself. The two amber lamps illuminate nothing within it but mineral grains and iron. The pressure felt in the teeth near the hall is the nearest it comes to being noticed.

A smaller, quicker depth belongs to the intake control. It seems to precede the machinery by a fraction: the water tightens, and only afterward do the grates begin to draw. This may be an illusion of sequence in a place where causes travel badly. The transparent fish appears only when this depth is most sharply defined. Whether it shelters at the boundary, feeds there, or merely becomes visible under those conditions is unknown.

The substation contains something less substantial still. It gathers near the cold white point, or along the cable bundles, or in the narrow difference between one circuit and the next. Pressure alone may not be sufficient to describe it. When the relay closes, it contracts until it seems small enough to fit inside the insulation. The cable running out across the silt may therefore be a road, a nerve, or merely the longest part of its body. Nothing about the lights reveals whether anything travels along it.

The keepers’ terrace holds the smallest and most numerous depths. Later pipework has partitioned some of them without regard for doorways or rooms. One may occupy the volume above a row of bunks; another, the remains of a washroom; another may extend through three numbered chambers and no farther. They do not avoid the furniture. A bunk frame is simply an irregularity within them, as a mooring bolt is an irregularity in the concrete.

Crossing the terrace would mean passing through several of them in succession: crushing weight in one doorway, thin quick water in the next, then a pressure so narrowly confined that it feels like descending inside a pipe no wider than the body. If human keepers ever had any part in making these presences, everything personal has been compressed away. They remember rooms, if they remember anything. They do not remember sleep.

New ones can arise where mineral growth seals a leak or a repair leaves a pocket of water under a different load. At first there is only an instrument that no longer agrees with its neighbors. If the difference survives enough shifts, it develops a rhythm: contraction, release, return. That is all the childhood available here. Others end when corrosion opens a pipe or a door is left ajar. A needle settles into agreement. There is no sound to mark the reduction in population.

The release is their nearest approach to a gathering. As pressure slackens, their boundaries loosen. Some extend into the service courts while silt stands upright around them in dark, temporary walls. Adjacent depths may touch without immediately destroying one another. This could be communication, or exchange, or only what pressure does when the load is removed. The freight cage moves. The substation dims. Then the pumps resume, and each inhabitant is restored to its separate address.

None of this makes them safe. They are not hostile; hostility would require them to distinguish a visitor from a valve. A living body entering the Intake would be treated as a new piece of plumbing: an obstruction, a flexible boundary, a volume that could be compressed. The works would adjust around it. Sediment would eventually accommodate whatever remained.

At the largest scale, there may be only one inhabitant. The kept would then not be citizens but organs—local conditions by which a single, dispersed body prevents itself from becoming undifferentiated water. The pumps are not quite a heartbeat, because almost nothing is being transported. Their labor is closer to the continual act of remaining articulated. This is why the works produces nothing. Its product is separation.

If the machinery stopped forever, the visible ruin would change very little at first. The filaments would settle. The fish would cease to appear. The crab might remain in its pit. Then, one room after another, the gauges would begin to agree. Water would become continuous through doorways, pipes, courts, and stairwells. No haunting would rise from the buildings. No last mechanism would fail spectacularly. The sea would simply enter everywhere at the same weight, and the greater part of the population would vanish without leaving corpses.

For now, another shift gathers. The crab tests the water with one claw. The green lamp finds a transparent flank. The filaments lift toward their current.

A relay ticks. The rooms become different depths again, and the Ninth Intake remains occupied.
