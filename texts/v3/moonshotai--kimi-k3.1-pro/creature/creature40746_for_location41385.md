# The Lives of Iru

It is tempting to begin with the people, because they keep the records and have given names to the shaft-notes. But they are neither the oldest nor the most numerous inhabitants. Beetle lineages occupy walls older than the families that repair them. Some of the growths beneath the gardens may have been spreading since before there were houses above them. The living hollow is best approached from the warm stone upward.

The first lives are the **seam-felts**. To an inattentive eye they are only discolorations: a grey rind in a fissure, a violet bloom under a lifted slab, a bronze dusting where two minerals meet. In fact they are many different organisms, each suited to a narrow range of heat and stone. Some spread in thin, dry lobes; others make a pile like rotten felt, though nothing in them is rotten. They live on the slow chemical differences within the fractures and need no light at all.

People cannot make direct use of most seam-felts. Their tissues are too mineral-heavy, their oils acrid or inert. The felts are nevertheless tended as carefully as any crop. A gardener moving into an abandoned pit may grind a flake of old growth with stone dust and a little fresh oil, spread the mixture along a favorable seam, and then wait years to learn whether it has taken. Too much heat kills it; too little leaves it dormant. A successful new bed is often an inheritance rather than an achievement.

Beetles and grazing animals make the next transformation. Chief among the latter are the **tallowbacks**, the hollow’s most deliberate livestock. A full-grown one is no longer than a person’s forearm: low, broad, and covered in soft, overlapping plates the color of old bronze. Its mouth is a rasp. Its tiny eyes seem almost decorative, for it finds its way primarily by heat. A keeper can move a whole sluggish herd without touching it by warming a stone at the far end of the pen and waiting for the animals to become interested.

Tallowbacks crop seam-felt, fallen leaves, and certain dry reducers in the garden pits. Their bodies store pale, dense fat, and in lean times they can slow until they are scarcely distinguishable from the stones around them. This makes them easy to keep and infuriating to hurry. Their fat enriches seed cakes and medicines; their flesh is reserved for small, substantial meals. What passes through them, together with their dead and the remnants of their shed plates, becomes material in which roots can live.

Thus an Iru garden is not simply planted. It is built as a succession. Felt establishes itself along the warm bottom; beetles and tallowbacks crop it; their leavings accumulate; wiry herbs and cushion plants take hold; finally the dark, interlocking leaves of the larger growths shade the whole arrangement. Gardeners judge a bed by the resistance of a leaf between the fingers, by its aroma when bruised, and by which insects rise when a stone is turned. They feed, shade, warm, thin, and wait. They never water.

Several kinds of moth are also tended. Their larvae consume dry leaf matter that people and tallowbacks cannot use. The pupae are rich in oil, and the abandoned cases, after the adults have emerged, can be unraveled into a short, coarse fiber useful for stuffing seals and binding tool handles. The ash-colored moths flattened against public walls are their less manageable relatives. On certain still evenings they rise together, and the air above a warm court becomes full of soft, erratic movement without becoming any quieter.

Above and among these tended lives are the tenants who have made their own arrangements.

The beetles are not one nuisance but a whole citizenry of different appetites. Some reduce old fiber into garden matter. Some bore into seed cake. Some hunt the larvae of others. A few are welcomed into storage chambers because their ticking masks nothing and their presence drives out quieter, more destructive species. Granary recorders become adept at identifying infestations by sound: a dry stitch near the floor, a faint sawing inside a jar, a loose, irregular ticking that means something has found its way behind the wall.

The little **stair lizards** live on such insects. They spend the brightest hours beneath treads or inside the narrow gaps where masonry has shifted, then emerge to hunt along the mortar lines. Households often recognize individuals by a kinked tail or a missing toe. They are not pets, exactly, and no one feeds them regularly, but killing a large resident lizard is considered poor housekeeping. Within a season the insects usually explain why.

The furred **roof-gliders** are more admired and less forgiven. They nest high in the roof trees, folding their bronze-dark membranes around themselves like old leaves, and at night they descend toward the seed stores. Their narrow fingers can explore a fastening with extraordinary patience. They remember successful routes, investigate repairs, and occasionally appear to pass a useful trick on to their young.

Granary seals have consequently become some of the most ingenious objects in Iru: sliding stone plugs, resin-toothed closures, fiber knots that must be released in a particular order, lids whose balance changes when opened incorrectly. A seal that defeats the gliders for a full season is accounted excellent. No sensible recorder expects one to remain good forever. People admire gliders in the abstract and curse particular individuals with great fluency. An old female with a torn edge to one membrane may be known in three neighborhoods by three different names, each commemorating a different theft.

At the rim, the dark birds occupy another portion of the same economy. They take beetles from the canopy, snatch exposed pupae, and descend without shame on unattended food. Their nesting debris helps bind the protected root mats, and their cast feathers are gathered where the wind catches them. Still higher and farther out are the rare hunters whose feathers sometimes appear in the market.

The greatest of these is called the **hushwing**. Few people have seen one clearly enough to describe more than its broad, soft-edged wings and the sudden absence it leaves behind. It takes gliders, rim birds, and any small animal careless enough to cross an open roof. Its cry is not especially loud, but it carries. When it passes over Iru, silence spreads outward through the nesting chambers: gliders stop scuffling, birds tuck down, lizards freeze on the mortar, and even the insects seem to falter. People listen to that silence as carefully as to the cry itself. Then, after a while, one beetle ticks. Another answers. The ordinary noise reassembles.

The human inhabitants fit themselves into all this with considerable ingenuity and no realistic hope of standing outside it.

Their bodies belong to the same dry chemistry as everything else in the hollow. They do not drink, and they do not sweat. Their tissues conserve and rework the dense fluids they require; their food replaces what the body cannot remake. Hunger therefore means more than an empty stomach. It can leave a person’s internal oils poor and slow, while cold produces the dangerous sluggishness they call **thickening**. A traveler beyond the rim may carry food, protective fiber, and a small stone warmed on a slab, but never a vessel of water.

They tend to be deep-chested from the thin air, with pupils wide in the lanes and skin kept supple by oil rather than left bare to the mineral dust. There is no single occupational look, but work leaves recognizable traces. Pressers have toasted-seed oil in the creases of their hands. Stone-fitters carry pale dust in their hair and eyebrows. Vent-keepers often have burns on the sides of their fingers from testing slabs too quickly. Gardeners can be identified by the dark stains beneath their nails and by the insects that occasionally emerge from their clothing.

Ash, oil, and resin serve them in different grades for almost every kind of cleaning and repair. Skin is rubbed with fine ash, scraped, and dressed with a little fresh oil. Hair is bound close or loosely oiled to keep it from becoming a refuge for seed insects. A wound is scraped clean, dusted, and closed with resin-impregnated fiber. Tools receive much the same attention. Cleanliness does not mean the absence of oil; old, rancid oil is dirt, while a thin film of the right fresh oil can make skin, timber, leather, or stone ready for use.

Meals are small and dense: pressed seed cake, a little tallowback fat, crisped pupae, toasted larvae, aromatic leaves, and pastes that have ripened slowly in sealed stone vessels over a warm seam. A feast is not distinguished by great vessels or abundance of fluid, but by variety and freshness. One oil tastes sharply of resin, another of crushed leaf, another faintly of the mineral bed in which its seed plant grew. Skilled pressers can identify seed age and storage heat by aroma, and households argue over their judgments with the seriousness other places might reserve for law.

Clothing is woven from plant and moth fiber, layered loosely in the basin and wrapped close at the rim. Because color is difficult to judge in the lanes, identity and occupation are often worked into texture: a raised hem cord, a pattern of knots at the shoulder, a narrow ridge sewn into a sleeve. A person greeting someone in deep shade may recognize the voice first, the step second, and the scent of work third. Faces come last.

Children learn the hollow as a sequence of sounds and surfaces. Before they are trusted with a freshly knapped edge, many can follow directions such as *toward the double tick, up the stair with the hollow middle step, then left at the lintel with three close ridges*. They can sleep through bargaining, presses, bells, and seedpods knocking overhead, yet wake when a nearby shaft changes pitch. A person raised beside the granary drone may find the western roofs unbearably shrill; someone from the west may complain that the granaries make their teeth ache.

The signal stones add a public grammar to this private knowledge. Certain combinations announce press work, vent inspection, an open granary seal, or the need to keep children away from a roof edge. Children imitate ordinary work calls with merciless inaccuracy, but they learn early not to counterfeit an alarm. In the evenings, the same stones may be used less formally, their notes answered by voices, pod rattles, and the occasional small copper bell.

Vent-keepers are practical specialists rather than mystics, though their attention can resemble devotion. An apprentice learns to distinguish the ticking of insects from the tick of cooling stone, a partial blockage from a shift in the basin’s heat, and the harmless creak of old fiber from the beginning of a failed lashing. Much of the work is done with an ear against a floor and both eyes shut. When a repaired shaft is opened again, nearby households often stop what they are doing to hear its first clean note. There is no set speech for the occasion. The note itself is sufficient.

This attention gives vent-keepers considerable authority, including the unwelcome right to enter rooms, lift floors, and order a passage cleared. It also gives rise to some of Iru’s most persistent disputes. Someone wants a noisy duct narrowed for sleeping; someone else depends on the warmth beyond it. A new canopy improves a nursery but shades a neighbor’s oil-cushions. A press rhythm masks a foundation tick. A glider opens a seal that its maker had publicly declared perfect.

The equilibrium of the hollow is not maintained by universal agreement. It is maintained by repeated argument, inspection, bargaining, and repair. A room may belong to a household; the warmth passing beneath it never entirely does. A roof tree may supply one family’s seed and another family’s future lintel. Wealth shows itself in reliable heat, sweet stored oil, established garden rights, and access to old timber more often than in copper. The poorest rooms are not necessarily the smallest, but the ones whose warmth depends on somebody else’s diligence.

Nor are the people solemn merely because their material world is slow. They gossip, gamble on glider incursions, compose insulting versions of work songs, and complain bitterly about obligations inherited from grandparents. Children run where running is possible. Pressers race one another when no recorder is watching. The slowness lies in what their actions commit them to, not in their temperaments.

A new oil-cushion bed may be planted for a child who will see its first useful harvest only in middle age. When a roof tree dies, the uses of its timber may be debated for years: whether the straightest section should replace a bridge member, whether an old loom frame can serve one more generation, whether a beam already notched by three earlier uses still has enough heart for a lintel. To finish such work is satisfying. To begin it properly is considered nearly as important.

At night, the presses stop and the town seems to change population rather than settle. Moths lift from the walls. Stair lizards emerge. Tallowbacks arrange themselves along the warmest edges of their pens, and gliders begin their patient examinations of the roofs. People carry bedding toward nursery chambers, sleeping galleries, and the public rooms reserved on cold nights for those whose bodies thicken easily. Vent-keepers make their rounds without lamps where they can, pausing at each change in the chord.

Sometimes a hushwing passes, and for a little while the whole crowded hollow holds still.

Then the small sounds return: a scuffle in a duct, a pod knocking against a branch, a seed beetle at work inside the granary wall, someone laughing too loudly in a sleeping gallery. Beneath them the shafts continue their separate notes. Underground, the seam-felts grow by an amount no eye could measure.

By morning almost nothing appears to have happened. A root has entered another finger’s width of old mortar. A glider has learned something about a seal. A child has learned one more address. A tallowback has moved toward a warmer stone, and a repair begun before its keeper was born has advanced by the thickness of a slab. In Iru, these are not negligible events. They are the shape of a full night’s life.
