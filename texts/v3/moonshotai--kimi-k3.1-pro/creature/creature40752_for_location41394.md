The Old Height is not lifeless. It is almost entirely uninhabited, and the distinction matters.

Nothing there lives by increase. There are no generations pressing outward from a sheltered crack, no colonies advancing across the stone, no abundance hidden just beneath the apparent barrenness. Turn a wet slab and there may be lichen beneath it, or only the dark polish of long dampness. There will not be a crowded underside of insects, roots, eggs, and decay. The sparseness is not a surface condition.

Nor do the living things form a web. A web would require more connections than the Height can still support. What remains are a few small relations, complete where they occur: wet stone and lichen; seep and stem; tarn and the animal keeping its depth; lichen and the pale mouth pressed against it.

The lichen is the commonest life, though here *commonest* means only that a traveler is more likely to encounter it than anything else. Its ash-green stains occupy the sheltered faces of certain wet rocks. Their edges are sharp without looking newly defined. There is no paler, more vigorous margin and no dark, exhausted center. A patch is not young at one side and old at the other. Its whole extent seems to have been settled already.

Beyond that extent is not territory the lichen has failed to colonize. It is simply rock on which the relation does not hold.

The black cushions along the seep-lines are equally finished. Their stems crowd together tightly enough to resemble a single piece of saturated felt, yet none rises higher than a fingernail. They do not lean toward the dim sky or spread along the cracks toward the pool below. The seep wets only a few inches of stone, and the cushions occupy only what the seep wets. Sometimes even that is too generous a description: a black seam will descend beside living stems without quite touching them, and the stems will remain alive all the same.

Their darkness is not the darkness of charring or rot. Nothing about them is dead. Deadness would leave a husk, and there are no husks. Their growth has happened; their aliveness has not concluded.

The tarn animals are easier to overlook than the water containing them. They hang as transparent, jointed specks, some just beneath the surface, others close to the bedrock, each at a depth that appears to belong to it. Their stillness is not drifting. Water may receive a traveler’s hand or the wetting of the rain, and the specks remain suspended without scattering into shelter.

Only the nearly imperceptible movement of their gills distinguishes them from clear debris.

Nothing hunts them. Nothing visible is small enough for them to hunt. There are no eggs attached to the basin walls, no translucent young gathering in shallower water, no empty skins. Smallness is not youth here. The least of them is not a larva, and the greatest is not an elder. Their depths are not stages through which they pass.

A single tarn may therefore contain several animals without containing a population in any ordinary sense. There is no descent connecting them, no succession among them, no number that can meaningfully be added to the inhabitants of the next tarn across an impossible ridge. Each basin has its own living facts.

On the open tableland, animal life takes its largest and least explanatory form.

One comes upon the pale grazer without hearing it, though every other sound may be entering through the feet. At a distance it can be mistaken for a low boulder. Its back is broad enough to seem architectural, and its pale, nap-covered body receives the iron light without brightening. Only near at hand do the wet eyes and the unnatural fit of its stance separate it completely from stone.

Its divided hooves accommodate the small rises and sockets of the pavement. It does not appear to balance on the rock so much as to have found the precise configuration in which hoof and stone agree. There are no horns, no scars, no accumulated marks of contests or seasons. Its bulk gives no impression of stored plenty; its leanness gives no impression of famine. Those are changing conditions. The animal simply has the body required to stand there.

It breathes, and the breath makes no vapor.

Usually its mouth is pressed to a lichen-covered rock. Nothing rasps or crops. No green smear appears on its lips, and when the stone can be examined, the lichen is neither crushed nor diminished. To call the animal a grazer is a convenience imported from a world where feeding leaves evidence. Here, feeding may be the wrong arrow entirely. The posture may be the whole of it: mouth, lichen, wet stone, and the animal continuing.

An approach does not startle it. Neither does it respond with the permission or recognition of a tame creature. It does not quite ignore a visitor; that would require attention to be turned away. The encounter simply fails to become an event.

There are no herds, no paired animals, no small one keeping close to a larger. No two grazers have been seen together. This does not establish that only one exists. Repeated sightings cannot be assembled into a census, because the places in which they occur cannot reliably be assembled into one journey. An animal on a far pavement may be the same animal encountered in a cirque, another of its kind, or a distinction the Height has no need to make. Resemblance does not prove identity, and number does not survive the routes between sightings.

It is not the last of a declining race. *Last* would imply a sequence of losses. It is not a survivor in the sense of having come through some catastrophe, nor a guardian set over the tableland. Guardianship would give it work, and work would leave consequences. It remains rare without becoming rarer.

The rain and the noise are more tempting candidates for life, and less satisfactory ones.

The rain hollow has a boundary, an appearance, and an effect. It wets anyone who passes through it. For a while this can feel like the touch of something distributed through the air, too sparse to strike and too extensive to avoid. But nothing within the shower gathers, responds, or maintains a body. The pool beneath it contains no special inhabitant. The rain wets. That relation is entire.

Likewise, the roar does not belong to throats. No breathing thing labors beneath the central pavement, and no colony makes the gravel tick by turning in its sleep. The sound neither summons nor warns. It does not call the grazers, and the living patches show no preference for quiet basins over deafening ones. Comfort would require the possibility of relief. Even at the appointed loudness of a place, life remains exactly as sparse as elsewhere.

The made things offer no hidden people.

Nothing nests behind the body-length wall. Nothing shelters within the little ring of stones. The pale grazer does not use the three slabs as steps, and no animal passes through the unsupported doorway as though it still led somewhere. Chosen stone is not preferred by lichen merely because it was chosen. The works do not concentrate life, mark territory, or preserve a center around which a settlement might once have gathered.

If hands made them, the hands cannot be counted among the inhabitants merely because their decisions remain. A people would require repetition, descent, return, or at least the possibility of one maker succeeding another. The Height preserves only the local fact of placement. That is enough for a wall to exist. It is not enough for anyone to dwell there.

Indeed, *kind* may be almost as misleading as *people*. In a more coherent country, two similar organisms suggest a history connecting them. Here, two black cushions on opposite sides of a ridge need not share descent. They may simply be alike where they are. One patch of lichen need not have come from another. The pale animals need not constitute a species whose members could, even in principle, be counted. Lineage and population require the world to add up.

A census of the Old Height must therefore remain local: life on this slab; life along this seep; several clear animals keeping their depths in this water; one pale body standing on this pavement. Beyond that, the route has its own facts, and they cannot be brought home to complete the total.

None of this makes the inhabitants tragic. They are not enduring toward extinction, and they are not waiting for warmth, light, or some restorative catastrophe to release them into abundance. Nothing in them is straining against the limit. Their bodies are complete, though their being alive is not completed.

Between them lie enormous intervals of stone, water, and sound in which nothing lives at all. Then, once more, a small relation requires the word: ash-green tissue remaining tissue; a black stem not becoming a husk; a gill moving at its fixed depth; breath leaving no vapor; a mouth touching stone without taking a visible bite.

Everything else on the Old Height can be described without the word *alive*. For those few relations, the word is still necessary.
