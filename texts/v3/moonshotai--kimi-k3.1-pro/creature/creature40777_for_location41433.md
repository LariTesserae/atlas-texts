No one lives there, if that implies a keeper, a animal, or anything that might regard the chamber as a place. Yet the pocket is densely inhabited.

Its life is not organized around waking and sleeping, feast and famine, or even growth and dormancy in the surface sense. A cell may spend decades doing little beyond maintaining the conditions that allow it to remain a cell. A root that appears withered may still be exchanging ions across a narrow living region near its tip. A spore may be almost inert without being dead. Activity is patchy, minute, and unevenly distributed. At any given moment, most of the garden is doing almost nothing; somewhere within it, something is always doing enough.

The least visible inhabitants are the microbial films. They occupy the boundaries where chemistry remains possible: between root and stone, within the finest brine-wet fissures, and along the damp surfaces buried inside the peat. They do not form conspicuous slime. Many exist as sparse cells separated by distances great relative to their size, sustained by organic leakage from roots or by compounds released, extremely slowly, from old tissue. Others live in association with plant surfaces, taking sugars and acids and returning nitrogen, phosphorus, and minerals in forms the plants can use.

Their work is intermittent. When a brine film thickens, a cell may resume repairs that have been impossible for years. Enzymes act on a fragment of leaf cuticle or a strand of old vascular tissue. Then the film diminishes, salts concentrate, and the work stops with the process unfinished. Decomposition consequently leaves no clean boundary between fresh litter and soil. A leaf may be intact in outline, partly consumed in its interior, and already serving as a mineral source for a root that has grown through it.

Above and around these films, the cushion mosses provide most of the garden’s living bulk. Each cushion is a population rather than an individual: pinhead shoots packed so closely that their older portions have become a dark, compressed foundation for the tips still growing above them. The outermost leaves are alive. Deeper down, stems may remain structurally whole long after their cells have ceased to function. The cushion thus carries its own history inside itself, not as distinct annual layers but as an irregular accumulation of survivors, dead shoots, trapped mineral grains, and invading roots.

The mosses do not simply wait on the floor for moisture to arrive. Their finest tissues hold films against stone and prolong the existence of wet places too small to be called reservoirs. When brine reaches them, they do not become lush in any rapid sense. A few cells expand. A shoot adds tissue. Another shoot, damaged by salt or ice, fails and remains where it died. Over spans that would contain whole surface forests, the cushion changes its outline by almost nothing.

Through this growth pass the scale-leaved creepers. Their waxy, overlapping leaves are less like foliage spread toward light than like a protective covering laid over their own slender stems. The wax helps them endure brine, desiccation at exposed surfaces, and the destructive intimacy of ice. Older tissues carry the deep wine and copper that the darkness never displays. Where a node rests against a suitable film, it roots. Where a stem is separated by a shifting grain or a contraction of the surrounding rock, the detached portion may continue if enough of its root system remains intact.

A creeper’s territory is therefore not a neat patch. It may be a series of living fragments connected by dead stem, or several apparently separate plants that still share one buried ancestry. Some are gradually enclosed by moss. Others cross over older creepers and reach a root interface their predecessors never occupied. There is competition here, but no swift conquest. One lineage can exclude another from moisture or from a useful contact with the ordered material, and the consequence may take centuries to become visible.

The ferns rise out of this slow contention. Their height makes them conspicuous only by comparison. Each stiff, divided leaf is an old commitment of matter: easily damaged, expensive to replace, and retained for as long as its tissues can be maintained. Their open form is partly inheritance from ancestors whose leaves once had another use, but the leaves are not useless relics. They remain living tissue, exchanging gases with the still air and contributing, when they die, to the garden’s small stock of carbon and minerals.

A folded fern leaf is not a bud awaiting spring. There is no spring toward which its development is directed. Its crozier uncurls because cells continue to be added and because the tissues already present slowly change their tensions. Injury, scarcity, or an unfavorable shift in local chemistry can arrest it for long periods. It does not need to complete its growth on any schedule outside itself.

The ferns’ rarest mode of reproduction is even less eventful. Most spores never produce another fern. A very few persist until a wet lens and suitable surface coincide. Then a spore may germinate into a small, short-lived stage of life hidden within the film itself. Only where liquid persists long enough can that stage complete the sequence leading to a new fern. The entire occurrence might be contained beneath a single roof root and leave, centuries later, no evidence except an unrelated-looking stem in the peat.

The root-curtains are where the garden’s distinctive economy becomes most intimate. Some descend from the creepers and ferns. Others belong to plants whose visible portions are so reduced that the curtains seem to arise directly from the wall. In the deepest growth there are also lineages that no longer reproduce the forms by which their ancestors might have been recognized: living threads within old litter, dwarfed scales at a joint, roots that persist for immense periods and produce leaf tissue only after unusually rich seepage. These are among the pocket’s uncatalogued inhabitants. They are not specimens preserved out of place, but descendants that have become native to conditions their makers did not experience.

At root surfaces, the atomically ordered material maintains the tiny gradients on which carbon fixation and cellular upkeep depend. It does not feed the plants in the way soil feeds a surface crop, and it does not direct them. A root may improve its contact by growth; mineral deposition may later interrupt that contact. Another root may take its place. Over long successions, lineages better able to maintain useful interfaces leave more descendants, while others dwindle without any dramatic extinction. The system repairs enough of itself to continue transferring and storing energy, but it does not respond to a crushed frond, decide which plant should live, or restore the garden to an earlier arrangement. There is no gardener concealed in its distribution.

Nor is the whole growth properly one organism. Its boundaries are too entangled for easy separation, but its inhabitants do not share a single fate. Moss can smother a useful root surface. A creeper can fragment and leave part of itself behind. Microbes can exhaust a local supply and become inactive while cells a finger’s width away continue. Dead fern tissue may support a living cushion, and living roots may pass through the body of a plant whose leafy portion has vanished. Cooperation, consumption, inheritance, and abandonment occur in the same few millimeters.

The peat is the crowded residue of these arrangements. It contains legible leaves, root sheaths, spore walls, and tissues whose original forms are no longer represented above them. It is not an untouched archive. Every act of reuse alters it. Compounds are removed, structures collapse, films enter and withdraw. Yet because the cold delays disassembly, material can remain recognizable long after it has ceased to belong to any living individual. Much of the garden’s apparent permanence comes from this failure of the dead to disappear promptly.

Every few hundred years, when richer brine enters, the balance shifts. Microbial films become somewhat more continuous. Roots branch into places that previously offered too little. Moss cushions swell, and the creepers produce more new tissue than they lose. Spores that have remained viable through scarcity may encounter their only opportunity. Even then, there is no visible flourishing. The abundance consists of slightly more maintenance succeeding, slightly more growth being completed, and a larger fraction of old matter returning to circulation. Afterward, the garden does not die back as a whole. It simply becomes sparse in its activity again.

Nothing grazes these changes. No mite shelters beneath the waxy scales, no eggs wait in the peat, and no burrow aerates the cushions. There are no pollinators to attract and no animals to carry spores. A falling grain, a brine drop, or the slow extension of a root can move living material farther than any deliberate dispersal. The roof’s mineral tick startles nothing. If a frond breaks, nothing investigates the damage.

At present, beneath the ceiling socket, the brine bead continues to gather. Its eventual fall will matter only locally: a brief alteration at a few moss shoots, a change in a film, perhaps a slight opportunity for cells already positioned to use it. The folded fern leaf below will not open in response. Its few new cells belong to another process, proceeding on another clock.

The pocket’s inhabitants have not conquered its cold or darkness. They have become small enough, slow enough, and sufficiently intertwined to remain within them. The chamber is crowded with life, and almost nothing happens; here, those are not opposing facts.
