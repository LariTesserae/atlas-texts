Most of the Dry Choir’s population is hidden at the scale of a hand. A court can be empty at the scale of a road and still contain thousands of bodies: under the dust, inside the foliage, behind the roosting shelves, and in masonry that appears solid until a section of it moves.

The masons are the most numerous and the least often seen. The name covers several kinds of small colonial animal, alike in habits rather than appearance. Most are pale, soft-bodied, and blind or nearly so, with darker, hardened jaws. Some are slight enough to pass through the pores between grains; others have broad heads used to brace, crush, or block a passage. They avoid the open streets, where their bodies quickly dry. A broken wall may reveal them for a moment as a pallid seething around the exposed galleries, but they soon carry their young and their stored food deeper inside.

A worker carries one grain at a time, braces it against the old surface, and fixes it with a smear of plant resin. The act is simple. Its consequences are not. The animals respond to air moving over their antennae, vibrations conducted through their feet, the taste of old resin, pressure from neighboring bodies, and the narrowing or widening of the space around them. No individual perceives an arch as an arch. No colony possesses the shape of a district. Yet their repeated corrections keep passages passable, shed weight from fractured shelves, and turn weaknesses into buttresses. An old collapse is not a ruin to them but a store of loose material.

Resin is gathered from plant injuries before the wounds close, or patiently recovered from abandoned masonry. The masons themselves eat much of what the rest of the city discards: leaf powder, fine root tissue, cast insect skins, and the organic fraction of guano and dust. Their stores attract crickets and beetles; their exposed workers are taken by geckos. Even the builders are food.

The plants are the oldest individual inhabitants. Some of the rope-trunked trees have stood through the rise and abandonment of entire animal colonies. Their trunks are often several stems grown together, twisted around one another for support rather than height. A single curled leaf may outlast scores of moth generations. New foliage is rare enough to be conspicuous: deep plum at first, soft only by comparison with the older leaves, and soon armored in wax.

Their flowers are small, pallid, and usually hidden beneath the canopy. They offer little—oil, pollen, sometimes a film of nectar so thick it is almost solid—but enough to bring ash-colored moths from plant to plant. The hard, oily seeds are carried in fur, droppings, or the crops and stomachs of animals. Most groves, however, advance by roots and layering stems rather than by conspicuous colonization. A fallen trunk may root at several points and, after centuries, become a line of apparently separate trees.

The roots do not search for pools. They search for concentrations of organic matter. They follow beetle galleries, enter old guano deposits, and thicken where animals have died. A grove’s lushness is therefore also a record of roosts, feeding grounds, and deaths. Its soil has been carried there almost particle by particle.

Among the leaves, the moths are often indistinguishable from dust caught on the foliage. Their larvae feed within leaves, stems, or litter; the adults may be brief and fragile, but their wings are covered in scales that dull both color and sound. A grove can appear to hold a dozen of them until a hunter strikes a branch. Then the canopy seems to release its own ash, and hundreds of wings disclose how much of the visible leaf surface was actually animal.

Below them work the armored beetles. Some are smaller than pinheads; others are broad, slow, and heavy enough to leave deliberate tracks in the dust. Their wing cases fit closely against the dryness. Different kinds cut fallen leaves, consume seeds, work guano into powder, or specialize in the dead. Their pale larvae do most of their feeding out of sight. Between beetle jaws and the fine roots that follow them, almost nothing recognizable remains refuse for long.

The wall crickets are pale and long-legged, with antennae that can test the breadth of a doorway while the body remains motionless. Many spend much of their lives folded into cells too small for a grazer’s paw. When they call, the cell becomes part of the instrument. A dry scrape that would be negligible among leaves emerges from the masonry as a hard, far-carrying pulse. Dozens calling in neighboring chambers produce no common rhythm, only layers of insistence: courtship, threat, territorial spacing, and the continuous announcement of occupancy. A single resonant cell may be used by thousands of cricket generations.

The small furred grazers are the largest animals commonly encountered on the floor. None is bigger than a small cat, and most are much smaller. Their coats are dust-gray, brown, or faintly plum beneath the groves; their feet are broad and leathery, and their eyes seem disproportionately dark in their narrow faces. They follow the polished paths by scent, whisker, and memory as much as by sight. Often they travel alone. At a disturbance they become still so completely that the path appears to have acquired another rounded stone.

They crop the edges of leaves, take fallen fronds, and crack the oily seeds. Their feeding looks restrained, but it is not stewardship. They are simply too few, and the foliage too difficult, for wastefulness to last. All their water comes with food. Their droppings are dry enough for beetles to use immediately, and they rest for long periods rather than spending moisture on unnecessary movement. Young are born singly and at long intervals. To rear one, a mother must find enough leaf moisture for two bodies.

The paths between groves are not safe merely because they are empty. Geckos watch their edges from the masonry. Some are almost white, with mineral-gray eyes; others reproduce the mottling of old resin and dust so exactly that only the slow closing of an eyelid reveals them. They hunt by waiting beside cricket exits, moth-resting surfaces, and narrow openings used by the masons. They crush what can be crushed, consume small prey entire, and commonly eat their own shed skins. A well-fed gecko may do nothing visible for days.

The slender snakes use still narrower routes. Some are no thicker than a climbing stem and can enter a ventilation flue, follow it beyond several chambers, and emerge among the roosts. Others move along the floor during crescendos in the Chorus, when small vibrations are difficult to distinguish from the larger noise. They take geckos, young grazers, roosting birds, and bats according to their size. After feeding, a snake may remain in one crevice until its body has again become a line rather than a bulge.

Above them, the vaults belong to the bats and the swift-like birds. The bats hang in seams and behind ledges, their folded wings resembling flakes of dark stone. The birds have narrow wings, tightly fitted feathers, and very small feet; many are awkward anywhere except on a vertical roosting surface or in the air. They launch by dropping, turn between pillars with little margin, and catch insects above the groves and along the open streets. Their shrieks help maintain spacing and contact where sight is poor.

A roost at rest may look like a mineral crust. Disturbed, it does not merely empty—it changes substance. The ledge shivers, fragments, and becomes wings. The apparent multitude is not an illusion of echoes. Most of it was genuinely there, packed into heights and recesses that the floor never revealed.

Their dry waste falls into the same places for generations. Beetles and crickets work it; roots enter it. Where light also reaches the floor, a grove may eventually establish itself beneath the roost. Life overhead thus deepens the soil below without any animal needing to make the connection.

Death produces one of the few local hurries in the Dry Choir. A body contains water that cannot be allowed to escape into dry tissue and dust. Crickets find exposed softness; beetles open or enter it; larvae work within. Geckos and snakes may take the scavengers as readily as the carcass. Hair, wing cases, and other resistant remnants remain after the useful moisture has gone, but these too are clipped, abraded, and powdered. Roots often enter the galleries afterward. There is no open rotting and little waste, only a rapid redistribution followed by a long disappearance.

The city’s equilibrium is not kindness toward its inhabitants. Colonies starve when their foraging galleries fail. Young grazers die on sterile crossings. A closed flue can make a roost uninhabitable, and a grove may lose an old tree faster than its seedlings can replace it. The whole persists by permitting its parts to fail, and by ensuring that what fails becomes usable by something else.

No inhabitant knows the Dry Choir as a whole. A moth’s world may be one grove; a snake’s, a chain of scents and narrow passages; a mason’s, the pressure and resin around a single grain. Even the ancient trees know only the light and soil immediately available to their tissues. Their lives differ enormously in duration, but the city’s age belongs mainly to colonies, groves, and repeated lineages—not to a multitude of ancient individuals.

The visible city is their shelter, their residue, and the distance their survival requires. The audible city is their present tense: wings opening, crickets calling, jaws cutting dry leaves, mouths working inside the walls.

Under the roar, too faint to hear, a mason sets one grain against another. The click vanishes at once. The wall remains.
