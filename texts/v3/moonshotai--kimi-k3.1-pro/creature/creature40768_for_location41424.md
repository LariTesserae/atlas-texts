The Underhusk is inhabited, but not by anything that could be found sitting in one of its rooms.

A census would begin by counting the wrong things: the piers, the vanes, the cords in their niches, the scrapers, the active seams in the bast. It would mistake tools for possessions and rooms for dwellings. There is no body here that must be fitted into a house. The houses are among the bodies. A street may be less alive than the filament crossing it; a whole district may be an organ in dormancy; a chamber’s proportions may be the most enduring part of a sense.

Most of the life is dispersed through surfaces in layers too sparse to give them any softness. Beneath the corky pavement, conductive fibers maintain a slow inequality of charge. Along the mineral ribs, fixed enzymes loosen and remake bonds when strain or chemistry permits. The living lattices neither pulse nor flow. Their activity would be easiest to detect as an absence: a place where wear does not accumulate, where a crack stops widening, where a fragment’s substance gradually appears elsewhere.

The oldest resident is the inward life of the husk itself. It is not a creature enclosed by the rind, nor a root system hidden behind the walls. Floor, vault, bast, and older pavements are continuations of its body, though ages of grafting and local growth have made that body difficult to trace. Its remaining nourishment is the slight chemical difference inherited from its formation. Tissues near that boundary spend the difference with extreme restraint, passing a little energy into conductive strands, then into growth, repair, or the maintenance of some other tissue’s mere availability.

Nothing feeds abundantly, and nothing starves on a timescale that would resemble hunger. A patch may remain capable of growth for ages without exercising the capacity. Another may exhaust its local allotment and become structure, neither immediately reclaimed nor quite dead in the sense of being abandoned. Its custody has simply changed.

The nearest things to citizens are the temporary working growths.

When conditions along a seam alter, a small region of tissue may become different from its surroundings. Charge gathers more readily in its fibers. Its enzymes change the permissible directions of growth. It branches, not into a body with limbs and a head, but into the exact apparatus required by the local resistance: an anchor, a sensing tip, two opposed pads, a stem capable of storing torsion. If an old scraper lies nearby, the pads may eventually close around it. If no suitable tool exists, the growth may first spend centuries making one.

Such a worker has no journey to make. Its world extends from its anchor to the few fibers it can affect. It knows the task, if knowing is the right word, through changes in resistance and returning vibration. The scraper encounters a harder layer; the torsion stem slightly alters its development. A cut becomes shallower. Generations of microscopic thickening pass before the tool has traveled the width of a finger.

The worker does not experience this as delay. There is no hurried alternative against which its slowness could be measured.

When the work is complete, the manipulating tissues often cease to maintain their separate boundaries. Reclaiming filaments enter them. Their substance is taken back along the same dry paths by which growth once arrived. The anchored stem disappears; the pads become indistinct; the tool remains in its recess, clean and without dust. It is not necessarily waiting to be used again. It may simply be the last durable part of an action.

Birth here is differentiation. Death is often reabsorption. Between them may lie a single gesture lasting longer than many forests outside could endure.

Other tissues are occupied almost entirely by vibration.

The vanes are the most obvious of these, though obviousness would mean nothing in the dark. Each has a particular thickness, fiber orientation, and attachment to its wall. Some yield to a narrow range of notes. Others flex only under a pressure too low to be heard, transmitting strain into the tissue at their base. Root tips near them grow differently according to what repeats. A tone that occurs once may leave nothing but a momentary displacement. The same tone sustained or renewed across ages can become a thickening, a turn, a closure.

These are not ears reporting to a center. No membrane listens to the whole city. A vane responds where it is; a rib responds to the vane’s altered strain; a later growth responds to the rib. Distinctions travel without necessarily remaining the same distinctions. By the time a far passage narrows, nothing there need retain any trace of the original click except the fact that narrowing became possible.

If this is language, it is a language in which a sound need not stand for its consequence. A note is not a symbol for a rib. It is one of the conditions under which a rib comes into being.

Memory is similarly material. A soft mat remembers destructive resonance by occupying the place where such resonance once did harm. A dense pier remembers a stable load. A brace remembers a separation without preventing every trace of it from remaining. Even the tuned stairs are a kind of recollection: each tread preserves a small alteration in the path of returning sound, and therefore in the growth that sound may later influence.

The finest active tissues are the reclaimers. They are so slight that a fracture may appear untouched long after they have begun work on it. One filament establishes itself along an exposed edge. Another enters a layer whose bonds have already loosened. Material is not carried away as crumbs or dissolved into any hidden current. It is transferred piecemeal, through successive changes along living fibers, until a little of the old pavement becomes available to a new attachment far away.

They are not scavengers that have entered the city. Often they are outgrowths of the very tissue they dismantle. A structure begins, locally and without ceremony, to cease being itself.

This gives death in the Underhusk its peculiar neatness. There are no remains awaiting a separate community of consumers. There is no distinction between the cleanliness of the streets and the ecology of the streets. A fallen sliver may lie undisturbed for centuries because the work of reclaiming it is slow, not because nothing has noticed it. Eventually its outline weakens. Its substance enters other obligations. The place where it lay becomes smooth.

Much of the vegetation does none of these things. It simply persists.

A low bridge may retain only enough activity to keep its fibers available for future bonding. A thick beam may do no more than avoid becoming brittle. Whole tracts of old bast are neither building nor sensing nor being reclaimed. Their life consists largely in not changing prematurely. To call them dead because they do not grow would be to mistake the city’s principal achievement for an absence of life: almost everything here has learned how to remain.

It is therefore difficult to say how many inhabitants the Underhusk contains.

At the scale of present activity, there are many: a working pad at a seam, a sensing tip beside a vane, filaments within a worn curb, tissues maintaining the last chemical gradient. At the scale of ancestry and grafting, there may be only one ancient plant, elaborated until its organs no longer share an obvious outline. At the scale of tasks, there is a succession of brief residents, each brief only by comparison with the whole.

There is no evident center at which all this becomes a single mind. But neither is the city merely a collection of unrelated reactions. Responses outlast the tissues that began them. Tools remain fitted to tasks. Repairs take account of damage whose first cause has long since disappeared. Local changes become conditions for other local changes, and the accumulated result can look remarkably like foresight.

Perhaps intention here is not something that must occur quickly in order to be real.

Consider one ordinary event. A tension cable begins to fail. For decades, the failure is only a slight alteration in the cable’s return of sound. Soft tissue thickens near one anchor. A distant vane, excited by a note passing through the altered structure, deposits a little more material at its base. A root tip turns. Then, after a preparation longer than the history of many cities, one fiber slips.

The district booms.

Nothing startles. Nothing runs into the passages. The sound crosses empty rooms, enters ribs, wakes sympathetic resonances in structures that will not visibly change for ages. Its loudest moment is also one of its least important. By the time the report occurs, part of the response is already underway; by the time the response is complete, the report will have no remaining echo.

There are no mites in the tool niches, no burrowers using the old growth rings, no small opportunists nesting in the acoustic ducts. Nothing experiences the avenues as shelter or the chambers as refuge. The emptiness is not loneliness, because nothing has been removed from it. The hollows are not homes without residents. They are distances across which the resident acts.

What lives in the Underhusk is thus a vegetation that has taken on the functions usually supplied by an entire world: ground, shelter, instrument, worker, sense, inheritance, and remains. It is one body with many temporary occupations, or many tissues sharing one dwindling inheritance. Its tools are not evidence of vanished hands. Its rooms are not evidence of vanished crowds. Its noise is not evidence of celebration, distress, or speech that must be decoded.

Eventually the working growths will cease to differentiate. The last reclaiming filaments will stop transferring substance. The final resonances will die against vanes that no longer alter in response. What remains will not be a shell from which the inhabitant has departed. The shape left behind will be the inhabitant’s completed work.

Until then, the third braid of the newest brace continues narrowing toward its anchor. Somewhere, a filament accepts the substance of a worn edge. A vane yields by an almost immeasurable degree, and a root tip’s future changes with it.

The husk does not live inside its city.

Living is what the city is still doing.
