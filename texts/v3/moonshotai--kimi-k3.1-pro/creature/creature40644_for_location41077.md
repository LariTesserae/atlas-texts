## The Lives of Serein

Serein’s people are urban by habit and solitary by opportunity. They share reservoirs, paths, standards of measurement, and obligations across the whole basin, yet one person may work for a long stretch with only a goat on the cliff above and a bird in the channel for company. They are not a uniform population of patient water-priests. There are deft and clumsy people, taciturn stone-fitters, talkative gardeners, restless rim-walkers, sleepers who miss appointments, and keepers whose pleasure in a correctly filled measure is frankly incomprehensible to their friends.

Their clothing is layered against the cool air rather than built for severe cold: woven cloth, wool wraps, close caps or hoods, and cloaks that can be folded into a seat or sleeping cover. Cloaks are often the most colorful thing a person owns. Clay-red, dark green, brown, undyed grey, or an occasional surprising blue become means of recognition across Serein’s long, clear distances. Color belongs to persons, not offices; one may know “the red cloak on the upper path” before learning the wearer’s name. Stone workers carry pale dust in the seams of their clothes. Gardeners have dark cuffs and scratched hands. Condenser keepers are often recognizable by the cord and dry cloths they carry, and by their habit of glancing upward at nets and fins even when off duty.

There is no common dawn and no collective dusk. People sleep behind slab shutters and woven coverings while full daylight remains outside. At any given time, some households are eating, some are working, and some are dark and silent with sleep. Appointments are made by practical intervals: after a jar has filled, when a bridge cord has been inspected, when someone wakes. A person can be late to another person, but never to the sun. The city therefore never quite wakes or sleeps as a whole. Its quiet is not hush imposed by rule; it is the sound of relatively few lives not all making noise at once.

Almost everyone keeps something, but *keeper* is a responsibility rather than a caste. A water keeper may also make cord; a potter may be the person best able to judge whether a reservoir cover seats properly; a gardener may be called upon to read a doubtful level because she has an exceptionally good eye. Some keep condenser frames on the saddles, some inspect channels and bridge conduits, some maintain the covered reservoirs, and some do work with no obvious connection to water: cooking, weaving, teaching, tending the injured, replacing slabs, or making household objects that fit the spaces already waiting for them.

Their expertise is highly sensory. An experienced keeper can hear that a gutter is obstructed by a change in the interval between drops. Another can tell by touch whether a stone fin’s groove is carrying a continuous film or breaking it into beads. Reservoir keepers know the clean smell of wet stone and notice at once when it becomes flat or vegetable. Stone-fitters can often judge a replacement slab by its weight and the sound it makes under a wooden mallet. Such knowledge gives a person influence while the relevant work is being discussed, but little generalized authority afterward. No one becomes the owner of a seep by being its most skillful keeper.

Exactness does not make every object anonymous. Two jars may hold precisely the same measure and yet sit differently in the hand; two cord workers may tie equally reliable knots by different methods. A good repair is expected to restore the public form, but within that limit there is room for balance, texture, neatness, and personal habit. Among colleagues, the invisible repair is not literally invisible. They know where to look, and their praise can be exacting enough to border on rudeness.

Nor does public water make them saints. They quarrel over credit, avoid disliked relatives, leave tools where they should not, and occasionally do careless work. Someone who would never think of enclosing a seep may still be absurdly possessive of a copper needle whose particular bend everyone recognizes. Their disputes are often fiercest over very small matters: whether a replacement cover has always projected that far, whether a remembered dimension is trustworthy, whether a proposed improvement would quietly create more work for another ward. The roofed meeting ground is used for such arguments, as well as for instruction, shared meals, singing, and the simple desire to sit where other people are.

What they resist is not change in itself. Broken things are removed; worn cords are replaced; methods can vary. What they distrust is alteration that makes an unexamined claim on everything around it. A deeper channel asks for different falls, different bridges, different reservoir levels. A higher wall changes shade, catchment, and the proportions of a court. Even a larger jar may require a different shelf, handle, and carrying habit. Their conservatism is therefore less a fear of novelty than an unusually vivid understanding of consequences. A clever idea is welcome to prove itself, but cleverness alone does not give it precedence over the rest of the basin.

It would be easy to mistake them for solemn people. They are not. Children race along the broad civic steps, make inaccurate marmot calls, and grow bored during demonstrations of gauge-reading. Adults tell stories at one another’s expense, develop passionate preferences for particular work routes, and sometimes laugh loudly enough to send birds up from a channel. Their most durable beauty is usually something in use: a jar lip that pours cleanly, a stair that meets the foot without hesitation, a net seam that lies flat when wet. Songs, jokes, and small acts of hospitality are allowed to be complete without leaving monuments.

People remember, even though the city does not use memory to establish a direction. Events are located by place and company more readily than by date: at the eastern bridge, in the potter’s old court, during the repair in which two keepers stopped speaking to each other and then had to spend the whole interval working back to back. A person can acquire skill, regret, affection, and a rich private past without any of these requiring Serein to enlarge. Children are not taught that their purpose is to become the founders of a greater city. Older people are valued for useful memory and judgment, not treated as emissaries from a nobler age.

The stillness is not equally easy for everyone. Some find repeated work absorbing; others sometimes find it intolerable. Restless people may take the long saddle circuits, volunteer for work in the exposed clefts, or spend their free time near the eastern rim looking into the motionless cloud. Others prefer the lowest gardens, where leaves overlap and water makes several sounds at once. Neither temperament is considered a diagnosis. A private mood does not, by itself, establish that a public wall is too low or a garden too small. Solitude is readily available; so, usually, is help. This does not prevent loneliness, boredom, or dislike, but such feelings are difficult to lose in a crowd.

The animals belong to this social landscape without being turned into symbols. The rock goats are neighbors of varying familiarity. A few have learned which people will step aside for them and which will shoo them away; most remain cliff animals that happen to use certain paths, ledges, and cisterns. People recognize individuals by coat, horns, gait, and audacity, and complain about particular goats much as they complain about one another. The marmot-like animals are left their dry burrows, their soft clicks forming part of the local knowledge of a slope rather than an omen about weather that will never arrive.

Near the seep gardens, even small children learn that a stone lifted to reveal a dark salamander must be put back with its damp side down and its sheltering hollow preserved. The animal is not sacred. It simply lives there, and that is considered sufficient reason for care. In the covered reservoirs, the translucent shrimp are known mainly to keepers, who lift the covers just far enough to inspect them. Their small turning movements are part of the life of water that most residents experience only as clarity in a cup. Short-winged birds sometimes learn to wait near a person cleaning a channel, then descend to quarrel over the exposed insects. The larger birds crossing the basin may cause someone to stop and look, but no one is required to make the pause meaningful.

Gardeners hold a similarly exact relationship with the green pockets. They know the wet center, the usable shade, the firm stepping places, and the point at which ferns give way to rosettes and lichen. The dry edge is not treated as a failure of irrigation. A garden does not need to become larger in order to justify the attention given to it, and the basin does not need to become green in order to become kinder.

Consequently, the people of Serein do not understand themselves as holding back catastrophe or preserving a few remaining habits before an end. They eat, work, sleep, argue, take pleasure in familiar animals, and occasionally wish their work were different. Their public restraint is not continual self-denial; it is the shape their sufficiency takes. No approaching winter makes a full storehouse impressive. No expanding population makes an empty court look like wasted ground. No coming weather turns every repair into a race.

On an open stretch of pavement, Serein may still appear uninhabited. Behind one wall, however, two people are disputing the ring of a replacement jar. Someone else is asleep in full daylight with a cloth over their eyes. On a saddle, a keeper is retieing dark netting; in an overflow garden, a child is carefully returning a salamander’s stone; at a cistern, a person and a goat are waiting for one another with imperfect patience. None of this needs to accumulate into a greater Serein. It is already their life, and they are already the people who live there.
