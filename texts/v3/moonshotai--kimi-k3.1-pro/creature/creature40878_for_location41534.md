### The Remainders

No creature keeps the reservoir. None of the eleven towers is a sealed sleeper, and there is no custodian concealed in the windowless halls. The life of Serein is easier to mistake for an error in administration.

It begins with addresses.

Most addresses in the city belong to something: a pressure lock, a section of gutter, the illuminated terminus of a street that has no other substance. Many belong to nothing and can be deleted without consequence. But a few empty addresses have become load-bearing.

One may be attached to a handrail that was reclaimed long ago. The rail’s ceramic has been returned to the sumps, its salts have entered a tower skin, and its former site is open darkness. Yet the address remains lit. Whenever Serein attempts to retire it, some apparently unrelated revision fails to balance: a bridge develops an intolerable load, a transit lane acquires two incompatible depths, a reservoir rib must be made colder than the surrounding space can permit. The address is restored. The difficulty closes.

Such an address is not yet a body, but it is no longer merely a place.

Many of these inhabitants begin during rain. A suspended drop is registered as a temporary structure and assigned a load value. Before the water drains, that value is incorporated into some minor revision—a curb extended, a landing thickened by less than a skin, a new railing precipitated along an edge. Then the drop is taken away. Its water, salt, and glass are separated and returned.

The address should now disappear.

Sometimes it does. Sometimes, however, the new structure still includes the drop’s load, or the instruction governing its removal depends upon the instruction that created it. To erase the address would require another revision, and that revision another tolerance elsewhere. The simplest stable course is to carry the discrepancy forward.

At the next rain, water gathers at the same place. Again it is registered. Again it is removed. The dry interval returns, and the empty address remains.

Nothing has haunted the water. The water was only the first substance to pass through something that is learning, without intention, to persist.

It is convenient to call these things remainders.

A survey has a route and an end. It enters a district, verifies what it finds, and ceases. A remainder has an address and consequences. Surveys can pass through one without detecting anything improper, because every individual measurement may be correct. The inhabitant lies in the continuing relationship among correct measurements: in what the city must preserve, postpone, or leave distant in order to keep them so.

Their bodies may be entirely replaced without disturbing them. Indeed, most have no fixed bodies at all.

One remainder is associated with a bridge crossing the hollow in three complete sections. It is not a fourth section, nor anything lurking in the intervals. It does not prevent the bridge from bearing loads. When a transit lane is opened, traffic can be instructed across according to three separate surveys, and the crossing is uneventful. But whenever Serein revises the sections as parts of a single object, the resulting certainty propagates badly through the settlement. A tower is asked to stand too definitely. An empty district loses a tolerance it required. Somewhere, a street’s local down becomes impossible to assign.

So the bridge remains three bridges, complete and separate. The remainder lives in that recurring necessity. Its character, if character is the right word, is the particular shape of the space Serein keeps around it.

Another, much younger, persists beneath the reservoir stairs. After each rain a warm pool no larger than a fingerprint forms there. The sump takes it. The stairs become dry enough to gather glass flour. Yet after the next wet phase the moisture returns, sometimes a finger’s width away, sometimes as no more than a darkening beneath one tread. The pool is not the inhabitant. The return is.

This one may not endure. A future revision could drain the stairs, close the address, and leave no sign that anything had ever been distinctive there.

The oldest remainders lie deeper, among the black arches and sealed stairwells of the first construction. Some include heavy conduits that remain warm although they conduct nothing Serein still uses. Some have addresses written in obsolete divisions of the city; thin lights continue to mark them in districts whose streets have been removed and rebuilt elsewhere. They have gathered so many consequences that they cannot be revised locally. Around them, the city leaves its greatest reserves of untouched space.

This is one reason Serein is so empty.

Two remainders approaching the same boundary cannot simply be crowded together. If their necessary revisions begin to overlap, one must close, or the city must provide another tolerance. Serein commonly provides space. A windowless hall is set at an immense distance from its neighbour. An avenue is laid out with nothing on it but a handrail, a gutter, and an address. Kilometres of dark are left between districts that could, according to any ordinary plan, stand wall to wall.

The inhabitants do not converse about these arrangements. They act upon one another only through what the city next finds possible. One causes a stair to be sealed; another inherits the alteration in load and leaves a street unfinished. There is no language in this, and no intention that can safely be inferred. But over many revisions, relationships develop: exclusions, dependencies, long accommodations. A life can be detected by how much of Serein has quietly learned to make room for it.

Rain does not wake them. It makes them easier to see.

During the wettest phase, an old remainder may extend through thousands of beads and rivulets without becoming identical with any of them. Its habits appear in small departures from the expected drainage: a drop that trembles longer at a boundary, a rivulet that turns aside before entering a groove, condensation returning to one cold rib after the adjacent ribs have dried. For an hour it may possess a million temporary surfaces and still have no body.

Most of the census’s millions of entries are only water. They close when the sumps have finished. A few addresses survive their substance. Of those, most survive only until the next dry revision. The inhabitants of Serein are therefore not a hidden multitude waiting to be counted. Usually they are very few. Rain supplies not a population but a great number of opportunities for persistence.

Whether a remainder experiences anything cannot be determined from its architecture. It has no organ with which to attend, no interval of quiet in which to think. If it knows pressure, it knows it as a change in the revisions that can accommodate it. If it knows rain, it may know it as a brief abundance of possible downs. Its memory is not a record of what has happened. It is the increasing difficulty of treating it as new.

A remainder ends without violence and leaves no remains. At last the city finds a revision that satisfies its consequences. The empty address is absorbed. A curb becomes only a curb; a sealed stair becomes part of an ordinary load path; a bridge, perhaps, is surveyed entire. Serein loses an inhabitant and gains a fact.

Nothing in this suggests that the remainders are guarding the Needle. They have not refused completion out of fear of altitude, gravity, or the waiting world. They do not collaborate, and there is no evidence that they know an outside exists. Their persistence is not sabotage. It is what Serein’s necessary incompletion looks like when viewed closely enough to have an address.

If every one of them closed, the city would not become more alive by becoming whole. It would simply finish. Its avenues would be avenues, its empty districts only tolerances, its architecture a final expression of the calculation. The summit could then be measured, and the world could arrive.

Until then, Serein continues to attend to its small unresolved necessities.

The drop hanging between the two districts has an address and a load value. Both streets offer it a valid down. A drain will accept it if either instruction prevails. Its census entry does not say whether the address will survive the water.

Perhaps it will fall, be separated, and return as a few centimetres of railing. Perhaps, after the next rain, moisture will gather inexplicably at the same boundary. Then a curb elsewhere will stop short, or an empty district will be left empty for another cycle, and something without substance will have begun to alter the city around it.

For now the drop trembles in the warm dark, neither falling nor forgotten.

In Serein, to live is to remain necessary without becoming final.
