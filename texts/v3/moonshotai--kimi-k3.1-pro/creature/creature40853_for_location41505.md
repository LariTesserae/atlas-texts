People live in the Low Ward, though never in numbers sufficient to explain how occupied it feels. There are usually fifty or sixty of them, with a few more away on the plain. They inhabit the oldest, most continuous cells, where walls have remained walls for several lifetimes. No one sleeps there by choice merely because a room is new. A visitor can spend hours below the rim and see no residents at all; most of their lives take place indoors, around small stoves, worktables, beds, and ledgers.

Outsiders sometimes call them wardens or keepers. They dislike both words. They cannot close the Ward, command it, or reliably repair it. They are residents. More precisely, they are its witnesses.

Nothing about their bodies is strange. Their teeth ache in the cold. Their hands chap, their cuffs wick up moisture, and a child born below the rim has no instinctive understanding of an impossible corridor. They wear close-quilted clothing without loose hems to catch on brackets or condenser fins, and their shoes are made for slick, narrow footing. Under the flat daylight they look like members of any small, provision-poor maintenance settlement. Their peculiarities are learned.

Their oldest surviving register does not describe a founding. It opens in the middle of a disagreement about a drain, with one of the disputants already dead. This seems to the residents an appropriate beginning. They do not know whether their ancestors built the Ward, were hired to observe it, or simply arrived and failed to leave. What they have inherited is not a plan but a body of testimony.

The registers record which rooms could be reached from which thresholds, in what season, by whom, and whether anything unusual happened on the way. They note the intervals between drops, changes in sediment, the appearance of grit in a sealed room, and the behavior of doors that still open onto clay. Old observations are not scraped away when they cease to be useful. Later witnesses add to them. The residents do not make the past agree with the present by erasing one of them.

Their maps, consequently, are poor pictures but excellent itineraries. They consist of sequences, cautions, alternatives, and names of witnesses. A route attested boringly for eighty years may be trusted more than a shorter one surveyed last winter. When the rare note passes through the buried pipes, the residents compare where it was felt and where it was not. The resulting lists sometimes reveal adjacencies no one had suspected, though no one claims to know what the note means.

Naming rooms is the closest the residents come to construction. A name must be singular, memorable, and capable of surviving a great deal of boredom. **Lower Cup. Sixth Lock. Clay Door.** Once established, it remains even when it becomes misleading. New Lock has been old for as long as anyone can remember. A room called Wash Place has served as a bedroom for five generations. The residents would rather explain the name to every newcomer than risk giving the room another interior.

People, by contrast, are allowed abundance. A resident may have a childhood name, a formal name, a household name, and several nicknames used only by particular friends or enemies. No one has ever observed a person acquiring a second interior as a result. This is a source of jokes, but also of ease: people are permitted to be more than one thing in a way rooms are not.

Their speech distinguishes between two kinds of nearness. Rooms may be near by wall or near by going. A wall-near household can require half a day to visit; a way-near one may stand on the opposite exterior side of the Ward and open almost directly into one’s own corridor. It is possible to know someone intimately for forty years without ever seeing the outside of their home.

This makes address a form of wealth. A small cell reliably connected to water, warmth, and an entrance is better supplied with distance than a large suite beyond a repeating passage. Some families are rich in rooms and poor in access. Others have lived at dependable addresses for so long that their reliability has become a kind of inherited standing. Even so, isolation is not always poverty. A badly connected room may be exactly what someone wants if they wish to be left alone. The greatest domestic luxury is not space but a door that leads only to one’s own household.

Most daily work is thoroughly unmarvellous. The residents mend clothing, copy damp notebooks, carry stores, dry bedding, cook, and argue about routes. Furniture tends to be freestanding; putting a hole in a wall for a shelf is considered a much graver decision than putting up the shelf. Grain, salt, wool, and lamp oil arrive from the plain under old arrangements. In return, copies of the registers go out to surveyors and other interested readers. The Ward’s water becomes ordinary beyond the rim, and its distances cannot be loaded onto a cart. Testimony is almost its only portable product.

First water is never taken for the kitchen. It is allowed to reach its cistern untouched, not as an offering but as evidence: removing it would destroy part of the record of what a new junction did before people interfered. Drinking water comes from older supplies. The residents’ own cups are thick, brown, and ugly, chosen to hold heat and to avoid any possibility of confusing them with the white porcelain catch cups.

The common kitchen is the Ward’s nearest approach to a public square. A crowd there means a dozen people. It is often the warmest occupied room and sometimes the loudest. Visitors expecting a silent order of ascetics are usually disappointed. The residents gossip, burn food, complain about their relatives, and hold prolonged arguments over names proposed for rooms no one alive expects to enter.

They are not patient by temperament. They are a people whose impatience has had to acquire procedures.

Even for short visits, experienced residents carry a little food and a folded pad on which to sit. If a passage repeats, they stop and make themselves as comfortable as the passage permits. They may mend something, sleep, compose an angry speech, or simply stare at the wall. The instruction says to wait; it does not say to be calm. Appointments are commonly made by the day rather than the hour, and “the passage repeated” is a complete excuse, requiring neither apology nor elaboration.

Children are no naturally better at this than adults. They fidget, complain, invent silent games, and become extremely skilled at expressing boredom without appearing to disobey. They learn reliable addresses before they learn to read and are taught early not to chant numbers on stairs. Above the rim, where the wind returns, they run, shout, and count as loudly as they like. Trips onto the plain are valued less as educational excursions than as opportunities to be unbearable in an open space.

Because completion is so rare, the residents more often celebrate continuity. A drain that has behaved itself for a century, a door that has opened onto the same room for three generations, or a patch of lichen surviving unchanged on an exterior wall may justify a small feast. These are, as the residents readily admit, celebrations because nothing happened. A little sugar or dried fruit from the plain is brought out, and someone reads the earliest relevant entry. The reading is usually less interesting than the arguments that follow about whether the entry was accurate.

Not everyone considers such a life meaningful. Young people leave. So do older people who decide they have spent enough years attending to a door their grandparents also attended. The person most likely to curse a blank clay threshold is often the one whose family has watched it longest. There is no sacred obligation keeping the population in place. People remain for ordinary reasons: affection, obligation, useful work, an address they cannot bear to abandon, or the hope—rarely admitted to be reasonable—that they personally may be present when something changes.

Nor do the residents agree about whether the Ward is aware. Some speak comfortably of what it “wants” or “decides.” Others regard those words as convenient descriptions of processes that require no mind. There is no priesthood to settle the question. Both kinds of resident leave the first water, use one name, and wait when necessary.

The maintenance bodies are treated neither as servants nor as sacred animals. They cannot be called, trained, or persuaded to hurry. No one is even certain that each folded body should be considered an individual for the whole of its existence. The residents work around them and avoid putting weight on a handhold that might actually be a limb. A child may choose one particular body on a courtyard wall and check it for years, hoping to witness an unfolding. This is considered an odd but harmless interest. Some people working alone talk to the bodies; others find the habit foolish. The bodies have never expressed a preference.

The lichen and moss inspire a different feeling. They are independent witnesses. An address plate can be altered and a human account can be mistaken, but a wall that has supported the same slow life for seventy years has demonstrated something with its own substance. The little black moss beside the reliable drain is shown to visitors with a pride quite out of proportion to its appearance. Other settlements may have gardens. The Low Ward has proof that, in at least one place, water and surface have kept the same relationship long enough for something else to benefit.

The crustaceans in the oldest cistern are left strictly alone. A sighting is entered with a date, a witness, and usually very little else: **seen; alive; one.** Residents deny that the animals are omens. Their importance is more practical and more profound. Water in which a small creature can spend years growing has not merely remained liquid. It has remained sufficiently itself to support a life that does not belong to the engineering.

A bird crossing the basin causes more excitement than a newly opened door. Doors are part of the residents’ work. Birds are not.

The dead are carried above the rim for burial. Excavating a grave in the basin would mean making another cut in matter that learns from cuts, and no one wishes to discover what a grave might become. Nor are rooms named after the dead. At funerals, however, a person’s several names can be spoken without anxiety. Abundance, dangerous to architecture, is part of how a human life is remembered.

Their more durable memorials remain in the registers. Long after someone’s death, a resident may trust a route because that person walked it, recorded its peculiarities, and returned to confirm them over many years. The dead continue to serve as witnesses. A careful entry can protect people who will never know anything else about its writer.

The residents disagree about the Ward’s future much as they disagree about everything else. Some hope for the first stream and imagine what their descendants might do beside it. Others would prefer the basin to dry into an ordinary settlement, where every room is only itself. Those who have visited the plain often have strong opinions on that condition. Some find it a profound relief. Others describe it as a kind of loneliness and return.

For now, the stable rooms are not merely places in which to wait for a home. They are already homes, imperfectly and sometimes inconveniently. People are born in them, grow bored in them, fall out over names, bring hot food to one another, and leave notes for visitors who may not arrive until the following week. Around them, the folded bodies tend the seams, the lichen keeps its own slow account, and the nearly invisible animals filter dust from old water.

The Low Ward does not need to love its inhabitants for these lives to matter. It is enough that a few rooms remain reliably themselves: enough for a kitchen, a bed, a book of other people’s observations, and a door through which someone expected may eventually arrive.
