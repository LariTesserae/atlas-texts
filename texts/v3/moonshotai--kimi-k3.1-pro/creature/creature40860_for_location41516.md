# The Inhabitants of the Dry Loom

The Dry Loom is inhabited at every scale, but it is not populated in the ordinary sense. There are no operators living behind the mills, and the vaulted cage trees are not deserted dwellings. Most residents are fixed in place; many are also part of the place. A root mat may be the lining of a conduit, a mill’s gasket, and a portion of its power supply. A harvester may be assembled from the same plants it will later cut.

Even the distinction between one resident and another is provisional. Life here must do more than continue metabolizing. It must remain distinguishable. A body can persist in perfect health and nevertheless cease to be an individual by becoming too exactly continuous with another body. Consequently, birth, age, and injury have unusual meanings. New life must be given a difference. Old life carries its history as a defense.

## The living ground

The most abundant inhabitant is the pile, though it is neither a single organism nor a single kind of plant. In exposed beds it forms deep, interlocking cushions. Inside conduits it becomes a thin, dense lining, feeding on trace minerals and leaked charge. Around machinery it grows in lobes and collars, withdrawing fine filaments from moving clearances while thickening against surfaces that only vibrate.

Its smallest members are short-lived. The mat above them can be immensely old. Leaves die into it, roots thread through their husks, and new growth takes up the recovered material before the remains have time to become loose dust. A visitor standing on pile is therefore standing on several generations at once: living tissue above, elastic dead tissue beneath, and slow decomposition below.

Young mats propagated from a uniform stock can be geographically uncertain. For a while, dozens of separate cushions may respond as one body occupying many recesses. Only after abrasion, root pressure, and small differences in illumination have given those recesses separate histories can they be relied upon to remain separate. The pile does not need to be injured to survive, but some injury is necessary if it is to remain many things.

Within and beneath it lives the black root-lace. This is the Loom’s principal decomposer, a filamentous growth that follows wounds, dead vascular threads, and discarded animal tissue. It does not produce the wet decay of an ordinary world. There is no softening stench, no sudden collapse. The lace unthreads. It opens dry tissues along their finest divisions, takes up usable oils and carbon, and leaves mineral shell, alloy, and ceramic exposed for later recovery.

A dead cage-tree branch may thus remain standing long after it has ceased to be alive. Its shape persists while its substance is patiently removed. Eventually it becomes a brown, porous framework that crumbles under a harvester’s passing weight. Nothing in the process looks dramatic. The root-lace’s work is usually visible only as a clean black line advancing through dead tissue.

## The standing lives

Above the pile, rib-grasses, fan canes, banner canes, and ribbon-leaved thickets live as something between wild growth and perennial crop. Harvesting does not ordinarily kill them. Outer leaves are taken, conductive stalks are trimmed back, and the rooted body remains to replace what was removed. Some plants may pass through hundreds of harvests without ever becoming the large, obvious individual that a cage tree becomes.

Their apparent regularity conceals intense small-scale variation. Every cultivated leaf acquires a slightly different vein pattern. Stalks diverge around sockets by minute distances. Damage from spore fall, machine contact, and passing air bodies alters the timing of later growth. These differences are not ornamental. They are part of how a plant retains its own location.

The banner canes also carry power. Charge entering one stand may be passed to the root grid, stored in thick lower tissues, or spent in a sudden ordered unfolding of leaves. During dry storms they become dangerously inclusive: for a few seconds, every exposed stem seems to belong to the same luminous body. When the charge passes, the plants tighten again into their separate clumps and scars.

Cage trees are the oldest conspicuous inhabitants. Their lives cannot readily be counted by trunks. A tree grows around a bearing housing, divides into several supporting columns, joins itself again above a pressure chamber, and puts roots through decks that may already contain portions of its own earlier growth. Its architecture is a record of negotiations with machinery: a gallery narrowed around a replaced vent, a roof thickened where leaves once scoured it too thin, a root diverted after an old seam began to fail.

Some cage groves are probably colonies. Some are descendants that were once continuous with one another. Others may be single trees with widely separated addresses. While continuity persists, matching galleries can behave as portions of one interior. After enough differential wear, the same grove becomes several trees without any trunk dying. Its memory is not stored apart from its body. It lies in the particular ways its roots and branches fail to be regular.

Nothing demonstrates that these old trees are conscious. They can, however, preserve conditions across spans of time that make ordinary reflex and intention difficult to distinguish.

## The grown mechanisms

Mills and harvesters are not plants, but it is misleading to describe them as merely machines. Their structural members are pressed from dead growth; their belts may be living tendon; their seals and gaskets are renewed by roots. They are repaired by the same tissues they process. They have assembly, maturation, wear, periods of paired operation, and eventual reclamation.

A newly fabricated mechanism does not automatically become an independent resident. If it leaves production as an exact and sustained copy of another unit, it may remain a position of that unit. This is useful when paired mills must share motion or wear. It is undesirable when a harvester is meant to begin a separate maintenance circuit.

The final step of manufacture is therefore often a controlled discrepancy. A casing is scored. A harmless tolerance is altered. A root loop is trained around the wrong side of a socket and left there. One unit’s resting rhythm is allowed to drift from another’s. These are not flaws awaiting correction. They are the means by which a new mechanism acquires a history that cannot simply be replaced by its parent’s.

Paired mills are the deliberate exception. During an operation they are not two machines cooperating across distance. For that duration, they are one mill with several stations. When the operation ends, slight differences are permitted to accumulate again until the stations can safely pursue separate work.

Harvesters themselves have no cabins, seats, or accommodations for riders. Their long bodies follow service gaps with a patience that resembles grazing more than travel. They cut, spool, clear, and reseed along circuits that may take seasons to complete. Their behavior can appear cautious: an arm stops above a young cane, a wheel waits while pile withdraws from a seam, the whole body folds before a weather front arrives. There is no need to suppose that it feels caution. Its life is organized around avoiding injuries whose repair would cost more than the delay.

The smallest mobile mechanisms are sometimes called surveyors, though they carry no instruments and draw no maps. The marks they leave are the survey.

A surveyor may have an abrasive wheel, a pruning beak, a row of unequal scratching teeth, or nothing more complicated than a hard casing edge dragged at an angle. It travels through districts where repetition is becoming dangerous. It nicks arch borders into unlike rhythms, scuffs socket lids, prunes leaves whose patterns are converging, and encourages roots to take slightly different paths. It does not make the district more orderly. It makes the district more reliably itself.

Surveyors do not form communities. A group of them is a maintenance event, not a settlement. When two work near one another, their rhythms tend to drift apart; an observer might think they were avoiding company, when in fact they are avoiding agreement. Old units acquire patched limbs, eccentric routes, and scratches that have been incorporated rather than polished away. Some return repeatedly to particular marks long after those marks require attention. Whether this is preference, degraded instruction, or simply the consequence of wear in their guidance systems is not clear.

## The rare animals

The broad browser sometimes called a plateback is the animal a visitor is most likely to encounter, though even that encounter may be separated from the last by years.

It is low, wide, and six-legged, with a dry shell resembling boilerplate softened and rounded by use. Its joints are flexible leather rather than exposed metal. Beneath the shell lies dense, oil-veined tissue. Dull pits around its forward rim respond to vibration and electrical charge. Before an obstacle it often stops entirely, then rocks slightly and tests the ground with one foot after another. That hesitation is frequently the first thing that distinguishes it from a displaced machine housing.

Its feeding is audible only at close range: a faint papery crackle as a ventral rasp takes brittle lower leaves. It does not clear a swath. It bites one stem, passes several, returns to another after some distance, and leaves apparently suitable growth untouched. It may remain within a single feeding thicket for years while its actual path becomes enormously complicated.

This irregularity gives the animal an importance far beyond its rarity. In a young or over-standardized district, its feeding introduces differences that roots and surveyors can preserve. A stretch of causeway troubled by shared positions may become stable after a plateback has spent a season wandering beside it. The animal is not repairing the road. It is selecting food. Distance is repaired as a consequence.

Mills do not appear to gather platebacks or direct them, but harvesters work around their freshest trails, and surveyors sometimes widen selected feeding scars while leaving others untouched. An old plateback’s shell is itself deeply individual, crossed by abrasions and distorted where past growth has closed over damage. No two old animals are likely to remain similar for long.

There are no herds, calls, or conspicuous courtship gatherings. Their reproduction is poorly understood, and their population may turn over far more slowly than the vegetation around them. When one dies, no carrion community assembles. Root-lace enters the body and takes it apart with the same patience it applies to a fallen leaf. The shell remains for a while, clean and hollow, until weather, pile abrasion, or a recovery press removes it.

Other animals may live in the deeper decks, but their traces are difficult to interpret. In the Loom, machines also drag, roots also travel by growth, and repeated mechanisms can leave matching marks in separated places. The plateback is not necessarily the only animal. It is simply the one whose life is easiest to distinguish from maintenance.

## The oldest residents

The oldest inhabitants may be neither trees nor animals nor individual mechanisms, but persistent associations among them: a cage grove, its root grid, the pile in its vents, a mill, and the surveyors whose routes keep the entire complex distinct.

Such an association can survive the replacement of every component. Its continuity lies in an inherited pattern of differences—old scars, unequal spacings, root diversions, particular maintenance rhythms. It can gain new organs and new addresses. It can also divide without a death, as formerly continuous portions acquire incompatible histories and become separate residents.

These complexes regulate themselves in ways that can resemble care. Roots reinforce a housing before it cracks. A harvester’s route changes around weakening pile. A vent is cleared before the air body that will need it arrives. Yet none of this requires a central mind, and there is no evidence of one consciousness extending across the whole Loom.

If any of these old associations are aware, their individuality would have little reason to resemble an animal’s. Their selves could have discontinuous geography. Their memories might persist as addresses rather than recollections. They could survive fission and regard the addition of another position as growth rather than travel.

On an old causeway, most of this life passes without acknowledgment. Pile tightens around a vent. A surveyor deepens an existing scar. A cage grove lets two matching leaf patterns diverge. A plateback bites one stem and leaves its neighbor standing. None of these acts is ceremony or conversation. Together they keep the vegetation productive, the machinery repairable, and the district separate from every other district.

The population of the Dry Loom therefore cannot be counted simply in bodies. Some bodies are positions; some positions are individuals; some individuals are districts. Its plants provide continuity, its mechanisms keep repetition under control, and its rare animals generate difference without designing it. Birth often begins with a deliberate flaw. Memory persists as wear. And to live there is not merely to continue functioning—it is to remain sufficiently oneself to have a place in which to live.
