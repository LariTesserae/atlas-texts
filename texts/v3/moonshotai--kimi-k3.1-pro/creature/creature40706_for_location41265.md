The Low Work is inhabited chiefly by life that does not look separate from it. Its makers are still its principal residents, although only a minute fraction of their continental body is alive at any one time.

The building colonies spend long periods as dull, protected films in joints, beneath overhanging lips, and within the pores of younger material. Dry, they are easily mistaken for water stains or weathered mineral. Some exposed patches are dead. Others are merely inactive, their living portions withdrawn into shelter too small to see. Rain is the surest test: one dark seam remains only a dark seam, while another softens, deepens slightly in color, and begins to accumulate a clear bead along its margin.

Not every wetting is sufficient. A brief shower may cool the plate without reaching the protected film at all. A little dawn condensation may permit survival but no growth. The colonies add material only when moisture remains long enough for their activity to continue at the surface. Then they leave their mineral-rich gel, and drying completes the process. Heat is not simply the enemy of their work; it is also what fixes each new layer in place.

Their active lives could be measured in accumulated hours of dampness rather than in years. A colony in a frequently wetted joint may thicken its edge steadily through a season. Another, equally old, may have done almost nothing for generations because a nearby rim now sends water elsewhere. No colony contains the form of a plate or rib. Each responds to the local duration of wetness, the shelter of a pore, the drying of one side before another. A rib kilometers long is the residue of innumerable such local events, many of them separated by centuries and performed by colonies that never touched.

The builders therefore inherit more than their own dormant bodies. They inherit slopes. A previous deposit determines where the next water lingers, and therefore where the next film can live. A growing lip may keep a seam supplied, starve it, or eventually cover it. Farther down the same almost imperceptible grade, a pan may begin holding water for the first time. The colonies have no connection with one another except through these consequences. Their continental community is drainage.

Not every living film builds. The dark crusts around temporary pools have a quicker and less enduring existence. In drought they may be indistinguishable from old tide marks. Within hours of wetting, some flush olive and spread into delicate films over the wetted surface. The pool above them can remain perfectly clear; its life lies against the bottom or at the margin rather than swimming through the water. Nothing darts away from a shadow. Nothing rises to the surface.

As the pool shrinks, the living area contracts with it. The outer film dries first and returns to darkness. Successive wettings may leave several such zones nested within one another, though mineral stains and deposits soon obscure their boundaries. These crusts add little that will endure as structure. Their importance is brief: they use light and water while both are present, contribute a trace of organic matter, and then become nearly indistinguishable from the pan again.

The rooted plants are more visibly separate, but only slightly less provisional. The gray-green rosettes keep their living centers close to the surface. Their roots do not bore into the Work; they occupy dust-filled seams, old deposits of their own dead leaves, and the narrow pockets where wind has lodged a little fine material. The waxy blades are equally economical. At noon they tighten or roll until a patch of them can look like a few dry scratches. After rain they open, but they do not make the seam lush. Even at their best, they remain small events surrounded by bare mineral.

Some plants persist through drought as closed living bases. Others disappear entirely above the surface and return only from seed. A joint may look empty through many ordinary storms, then produce a thin line of growth after one exceptional wetting. Flowers follow only when conditions last long enough. They need no visitors: pollination is completed by the plant itself or left to the wind. Their seeds are correspondingly unattended. Some are blown until a rib stops them. Others adhere briefly to moving water and are left along a tide mark when the water stalls or sinks.

Most seeds come to nothing on the open plates. A few land where condensation repeatedly gathers. For those, a seam only slightly wider or deeper than the surrounding surface can be an entire favorable district.

Because nothing grazes or tramples them, the plants retain their dead parts for a long time. Dry outer leaves may remain arranged around a living rosette. Last season’s flower stem can stand beside new growth, bleached but otherwise almost unchanged. A narrow blade may collapse without being broken and lie exactly where it fell. Age is therefore visible without being easy to interpret: a plant can look half dead when its center is merely closed, or appear intact long after its living tissue has gone.

The pale decomposing threads work within these remnants, but only where dampness is sheltered from the full return of heat. They enter old leaf bases, packed seed husks, and organic dust caught beneath a lip. Their advance pauses whenever the material dries. In an exposed place, dead vegetation may simply persist; in a protected seam, the threads slowly reduce it to finer matter that can be used again.

This does not produce soil in any deep or continuous sense. It produces local enrichments: a little darker dust, a slightly greater capacity to hold dawn moisture, a pocket in which the next seedling may survive a few days longer. A single dead plant can matter greatly to the narrow seam that contains it. Beyond that seam, its influence ends.

Nothing burrows beneath the plates, mixes these pockets, or carries nutrients from one district to another. Nothing opens the sealed hollows in the older deposits. There are no grazers to keep the plants small, no pollinators to shape their flowering, no bodies to transport seeds or awaken pool sediments. The absence of animals is not merely an absence of sounds and tracks. It leaves the ecology almost entirely in the hands of water, wind, drying, and accretion.

Those forces can connect lives without bringing them into contact. A building film thickens an unfinished rim; years later, water no longer reaches a seam of rosettes, and their seeds accumulate without germinating. The diverted water enters another pan, where a dark crust has time to flush olive before evaporation. Elsewhere, a shallow deposit covers an old stain but creates a shaded joint where condensation now survives until sunrise. None of the inhabitants perceives the larger pattern. There is no larger pattern except the one left behind in material and moisture.

For much of the year, most of this life is invisible, dormant, or dead beyond recovery, and the dry surface gives no reliable indication of which. At noon the Work can appear entirely sterile. After rain, the same ground does not necessarily contain more life; more of its life has simply become apparent.

At dusk beside a lasting pool, several of its inhabitants may briefly be visible together. An olive film lies beneath a few centimeters of clear water. Open blades stand along one seam, their old dead tissue still attached. Pale threads remain at work in a damp leaf base. At the unfinished lip, the building colony’s translucent bead is already losing its wet shine.

A week later, the pool may be only pale rings. The crust will have darkened, the blades closed, and the bead hardened into another narrow addition to the rim. Some lives will have ended there. Others will be waiting in the same few pores and grains of dust. Farther away, where the water went instead, another seam may just be darkening.

That is how the Low Work is inhabited: not continuously across its surface, but in scattered resumptions—life becoming active where water pauses, and the plain retaining, long afterward, the slight alterations made during those pauses.
