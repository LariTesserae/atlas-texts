People live in the Upper Sump, though from the plateau rim it is easy to mistake them for a handful of operators tending an otherwise uninhabited works. The figures crossing the yards go home. Behind the double walls are cooking rooms, narrow stairs, beds built away from the pipes, and courtyards where laundry dries slowly in the mineral air. There are children who have never heard an unaided voice carry across open ground, and old people whose working lives can be traced through the repair history of a single pump hall.

Outsiders sometimes call them Sumpers. Within the city, the word is too broad to be useful. A person belongs to a court, a rib, a crew, and usually a watch: the western reservoirs, the lower mill rooms, the night gauge round. The population is small for the area and changes slowly. Houses are subdivided, repaired, and handed on rather than replaced by new districts. Some young people leave for the lower country and find that they cannot sleep there; others leave and are relieved never to hear the chord again. New mechanics, surveyors, and mill hands arrive by rail. A newcomer begins to seem local when they stop asking when somewhere will be made dry and start asking where its water ought to go.

Nearly every occupation is, directly or indirectly, a negotiation with seepage and load. There are pump keepers, turbine crews, gauge readers, pipe fitters, masons, scale-cutters, silt-pressers, block-makers, and people who maintain the ceramic route posts. There are also route-walkers whose whole duty is to notice differences: a warm patch in cold masonry, a new rust trail beneath a flange, sedge flattened by a runnel that did not exist the previous week. Cooks learn which stores mold first. Teachers learn the lamp sequences, because lessons sometimes have to end when a parent’s watch is extended. Even the makers of ear guards understand water; their felt and leather fail quickly if they are not dried and dressed properly.

The Sumpers dress for warm wetness rather than for rain. Their working clothes are light, close at the cuffs, and easily washed, with heavy boots and ear guards carried even on short crossings. Mineral stains remain at wrists and ankles. Scale leaves pale lines in the creases of hands. People walk deliberately on the exposed stone. Running is reserved for genuine emergencies; a hurried fall beside machinery is considered a more likely danger than the few seconds saved. It is poor etiquette, and often unsafe, to approach a worker from behind. One steps into view and gives the sign for attention.

Their hand language began with work signals, but it is no longer a limited code. It can express pressure, delay, uncertainty, affection, exhaustion, and several degrees of disbelief. Crews can argue across a roaring yard about whether a vibration comes from a worn bearing or from movement in a gallery below. A sign may be made broadly enough to cross a spill-field or discreetly enough to remain private at a crowded table. Lamps and colored paddles carry official instructions; hands carry most of ordinary life. In the courtyards, where speech becomes practical again, people often continue to use both.

Hearing is carefully protected, though hearing loss is still common among older workers. It is not treated as a glorious mark of service. The celebrated keepers are not simply people gifted with mysterious ears. Their skill lies in comparison and memory: this pump under this load, at this season, against the same conditions last year. A keeper may touch a rail, frown, and then spend half an hour confirming the impression with gauges and contact rods. When they are right often enough, the confirmation is gradually omitted from the stories told about them.

Children learn the city’s sounds before they understand their sources. They can sleep through the crushing roar of a mill room beyond the wall but wake when one familiar note drops out. A sudden quiet brings adults upright more reliably than an alarm. During rare, planned reductions in load, the open grounds seem unnaturally exposed. Footsteps become audible. So do clothing, breath, and small birdsong from the few courtyard trees. Many residents find these interludes less restful than the usual noise; silence gives them too little information about whether the city is well.

Public authority is centered less in a palace or council chamber than in the Gauge House, where levels, temperatures, pump loads, and seep reports are copied into long series of books. There are hierarchies and old resentments—between the central works and the outlying ribs, between those who assign watches and those who must take them—but the officials most distrusted are not necessarily the harshest. They are the ones who order a change in load without warning the districts whose walls may answer it hours later. A decision that looks hydraulic in one building can become domestic in another: water beneath a kitchen floor, a child’s bed moved away from a wall, a week of scale-cutting added to someone else’s work.

Most households keep a seep book alongside their ordinary domestic records. It notes stains, trickles, warm patches, pump changes, rainfall, and the dates on which old openings returned. A house is inherited with this book, and a buyer would be foolish to value it less than the deed. The central surveys are respected, but they do not invalidate household knowledge. When the two disagree, both accounts are usually kept. On the large charts, obsolete arrows are crossed through rather than erased; a rejected path may become useful again when the water resumes an old habit.

This produces a distinctive caution in ordinary speech. A repair is said to be *holding*, not finished forever. A channel is *turned aside*, not defeated. A wall is *dry at present*. Children who make grander claims are corrected without much ceremony. The habit is not fatalism. Sumpers can be forceful and ingenious when action is needed, and they take considerable pride in a well-cut joint or a cleanly rebuilt foundation. They simply distrust language that promises more than the next inspection can support.

Private life gathers around the courtyards and their small exceptions to the plateau’s general severity. Clean condensate is saved for drinking cisterns and for the lined beds in which a few trees grow. Their roots are kept shallow and cool, their soil renewed with material brought up from below. The trees are narrow from pruning and seldom bear heavily, but their leaves are the most generous green many residents see from one week to the next. A healthy tree is not merely decorative. It demonstrates that a household can maintain a little volume of soil at conditions different from those of the ground beneath it. Fruit is often shared less for its quantity than for the proof that the tree has survived another season.

Many families also place small name-blocks in later repairs. These are made from the same pressed and fired silt as ordinary building block, with a name and dates cut plainly into one face. They may commemorate a keeper, a mason, a child, or an entire crew. There is no requirement that they remain forever in the first position chosen for them. When a wall is rebuilt, the inscription may be copied into a new block and the old one broken up for fill. Remembering, like drainage, is something that has to be renewed.

Beyond the courts, wild life is sparse but particular. The coppery sedge establishes itself where seepage has cooled and where enough fine material has collected to hold its roots. A clump catches dust and silt, making a slightly deeper pocket in which another clump may follow. Lichen spreads almost imperceptibly over old basalt. The fleshy, salt-edged plants remain close to the ground, thriving in places that would exhaust less economical growth. None of this softens the city at a distance, but route-walkers learn to read it. A fresh line of sedge can reveal a runnel before the runnel is obvious on stone.

The rust-red and olive films are the closest thing the plateau has to pasture. They are thin, discontinuous, and easily overlooked, yet their combined area must exceed that of all the courtyard gardens many times over. They grow, dry, regrow, and take part in the slow formation of deposits around channels and outfalls. Crews spend their lives removing some of the material their presence helps create. In that sense, much of the Upper Sump’s life is engaged in making small obstructions for human beings to clear.

The pale snails graze within cool, wet joints in masonry. Their shells are so lightly colored that they seem at first to be flakes of the surrounding deposit. After rain they may appear several together; after a hot flush, a seam can be empty again. They are mostly ignored until they enter a narrow gauge line or gather where a sample is meant to remain clean. The moths are more visible. A few circle nearly every service lamp, gray or soot-colored against the steam, and at dawn their wings leave dust on the housings and on the gloves of those who clean them.

The dark lizards live on these small margins of abundance. They hunt insects near the lamp shelters and courtyard beds, then flatten themselves on warm parapets with their legs spread and their bodies almost flush to the stone. Expansion joints give them refuge. They often vanish just before a relief valve opens, not through foreknowledge but because they feel the preliminary shudder that human beings have insulated themselves against. When a bird passes high above the plateau, people sometimes stop to watch it. It is not generally treated as an omen. There is simply very little else moving in that part of the sky.

Then there is the inhabitant that may be only a manner of speaking.

Residents say that the Sump has “taken” water, that a rib is “holding” it, or that the western galleries are “giving back” what a quarry pond lost. They use much the same grammar one might use for a large animal moving cautiously through rooms. This does not prevent them from drawing meticulous charts or arguing over pressure and fissures. They do not erect shrines to the water, and most would be impatient with the suggestion that it possesses intentions. The expressions persist because they describe something the simpler mechanical vocabulary often misses: an action here may have its answer elsewhere, much later, and the answer may arrive by a path no one has yet succeeded in keeping on the map.

There are no offerings made to this body. There are gauge books, spare gaskets, watches kept through bad weather, and a strong reluctance to seal an unexplained seep before it has been observed through a seasonal rise. Whether that constitutes reverence, prudence, or merely long experience depends on the resident asked.

Life in the Upper Sump is therefore a collection of maintained exceptions: a bed that remains dry enough, a root run kept cool, a snail’s seam, a clear gauge glass, a courtyard in which one person can answer another by name. No one who has lived there long mistakes these exceptions for permanent victories. The pumps change load. Hours later, water rises in a well beyond the ridge. And somewhere a sleeper wakes before the signal lamps change, because one familiar note has gone missing from the chord.
