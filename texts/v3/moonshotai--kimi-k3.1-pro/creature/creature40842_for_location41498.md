### The Brief Tenants

Nothing in the hollow is an ancient cave creature. There has not been time. No blind, colourless fish hangs in the pool; no generations of nesting animals have stained the roof or softened the ledges. Its inhabitants have arrived from the river, the peat, and the wet undersides of stones. Nearly all could live elsewhere. What is singular is the arrangement they make here, during the few years in which cold water, darkness, and bare mineral surfaces coincide.

They do not occupy the room evenly. They occupy transitions: oxygen-poor seep water meeting air, light failing along a joint, silt becoming just stable enough to hold a burrow. Because those transitions move, residence is never quite fixed.

Most of the population is not merely small; it is thin. Within the rusty veils live the iron-oxidizing bacteria, the hollow’s most continuous tenants. To one of these cells, the chamber is not a room but a narrow chemical opportunity. The seep supplies dissolved iron; the air supplies oxygen; between too much of one and too little of the other, growth is possible. Some of the bacteria form delicate sheaths and stalks, which become coated with ochre as they grow. Water tears pieces away. New films extend over the damage. What looks like an old mineral stain is therefore also an accumulation of brief lives and the structures they leave behind.

Nearer the entrance, where the gray light can still be used, the diet changes. Diatoms and other algae spread over the wettest rock, thin enough to seem at first like a darkening of the stone. Many anchor themselves in mucilage. Under clouded noon they grow slowly, manufacturing tissue out of light that would seem useless to anything larger. Their glassy remains collect in the finer sediment. Grazers scrape paths through them, and the paths green over again. A film may be established, cropped, buried by a pulse of silt, and begun elsewhere before the bare eye would have decided that anything lived there at all.

The finer silt looks dead until it is watched through a change in water level. Then it stirs. Some of the red threads within it are true worms; others are the larvae of midges. They swallow sediment for the bacteria and organic dust it carries, leaving the mineral grains to pass through. Their small burrows and tubes admit water a little farther into the deposit. When a pressure surge rearranges the surface, they are buried, exposed, or shifted bodily. Afterwards they resume without requiring the previous arrangement to be restored.

The stonefly nymphs and other flattened larvae keep to the undersides of the angular stones. Their shape lets them press close where the flow is quickest. Gills move almost imperceptibly; feet test the rock. Some scrape at the films. Others wait for smaller animals to loosen their hold. To them, the deep double knock is not a sound coming from behind a wall. It is a pressure arriving through water, stone, legs, and gills. They brace, retreat a fraction, and later return to the same grazing ground—unless the slab itself has shifted, in which case there is no same ground.

Water mites hunt through the clearer shallows with their bright, abrupt pauses. Each crossing is a sequence of searches rather than a journey. Above them, on the damp grit, springtails graze another microbial pasture. Their habitable country may be only a finger’s width above the flood line. When the pool rises, that country narrows or disappears; when it falls, a new strip becomes available, already stocked with whatever the water has left behind. The springtails speckle it like dark grit until disturbed, when a few vanish in sudden, springing jumps.

The sticklebacks are the most conspicuous residents, but their tenure is uncertain. They arrived by water and may leave by water, if a flood reconnects the pool generously enough. Until then, each keeps to its own patch. One uses the shadow of the slab; another works the brighter margin where drifting larvae are easier to see. They do not school. There is too little food to reward crowding, and too much bare water in which a chase would waste warmth and effort. A fish takes a midge larva, investigates a disturbance, then hangs almost motionless again. When the shrew enters, the fish become pieces of the pool’s darkness.

The midges show how little of a life need be spent in the form by which it is known. Most of their existence passes as red threads in sediment or as small aquatic larvae among the films. The winged adult is a brief departure. At the surface, one struggles free of its old skin while its reflection trembles beneath it. A fish may take it before it rises. A nearly invisible web at the entrance may take another. The rest go out into the sedges, where mating and egg-laying bind the hollow to places it never touches. The works’ output of wings is not a flourish. It is one of the few products that leaves under its own power.

The water shrew knows the chamber intimately without belonging wholly to it. Its dry nest is elsewhere in the peat; the hollow is one station on a hunting circuit that also includes the river margin, the undersides of overhanging turf, and other cracks too small for anything heavier to enter. For the shrew, darkness is not much of an impoverishment. Whiskers read the edge of a stone. Scent identifies a recent flooding. Water carries vibrations directly to its feet. When it dives, air caught in its fur gives it a momentary silverness, but there is nothing leisurely in the visit. Cold water draws away heat, and the shrew’s small body requires frequent feeding. It searches fiercely, takes what it can, and is gone. No other animal so repeatedly travels the chamber from mouth to rear wall, yet the shrew knows nothing of the bacterial veils except as a texture beneath its feet.

The dipper’s knowledge is briefer and louder. It arrives with weather still clinging to its wings, bows on the slab out of an old habit belonging to open streams, and makes the stone answer its call. Then it is at the water, or in it, and a larva that has spent months avoiding smaller mouths disappears into one large one. The dipper nests elsewhere. Its young may receive some portion of the hollow secondhand, carried out in a parent’s beak.

At the entrance, the rooted inhabitants work more slowly and with greater consequences. Moss holds water against the peat. Liverworts spread where every surface remains wet. Sedge roots bind one patch while dwarf-willow roots probe another. Between the moss leaves, in films of water too slight to see from a step away, rotifers, nematodes, and tardigrades feed, drift, freeze, and resume. This seam is crowded with lives that can wait through conditions the pool animals could not survive. The plants are not deliberately reclaiming the chamber; they are following light and moisture. Their effect is reclamation all the same. They hold the roof together for a time, then help open it.

No inhabitant experiences the whole works. The bacteria know a chemical boundary. The worms know burial and release. The nymphs know pressure and foothold. The fish know movement against pewter light. The shrew knows a route through hunger, and the dipper knows a hunting stop. They do not form one organism, but material passes continually among them: iron into ochre, light into algal tissue, tissue into larvae, larvae into fish or bird, fine sediment into water leaving by the rear fissure.

When the slab falls, there will be no single death to mark the chamber’s end. Some residents will be washed downstream. Some films will persist on wet rubble. The shrew will alter its circuit; the dipper will hunt another stone. Fish that cannot escape may remain until the pool no longer holds them. Moss and sedge will cross the floor, and the special assemblage will disappear as its edges disappear. Elsewhere, another fracture will be colonized without ceremony—not by a procession of refugees, but by whatever the next seep, the next flood, and the next hatch happen to bring.

For now, on a quickening thaw night, the pressure cycle shortens. The worms draw down; a nymph braces; the sticklebacks abandon their spacing for a moment and then resume it. A fresh pulse crosses the ochre veils. Before morning, a midge leaves the water, and the shrew makes one brief silver movement at the limit of the light.

None of them witnesses the whole. The whole is only this: separate lives using the same cold water while the hollow lasts.
