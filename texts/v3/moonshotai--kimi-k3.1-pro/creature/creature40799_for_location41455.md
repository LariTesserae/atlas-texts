The largest inhabitant of Crownwater is the one most easily mistaken for Crownwater itself.

The consortium has no central organ in which the whole basin is represented. No buried heart coordinates the crowns, pillars, roots, and halls. Its unity is practical rather than pictorial: sap crosses old grafts, salts are adjusted at the roots, and tissue responds to damage where the damage occurs. A feeding pore narrows and is reopened; a current slows and the cilia along its path increase their beating; a surface thins and is replaced from within. The plant’s apparent care for the whole is composed of countless local corrections.

If it has any experience of its own extent, it cannot be a view. It must be a distribution of pressure, hunger, chemistry, and flow.

Everything else lives on the small surplus produced by that maintenance.

The pale grazers spend most of their lives against the plant skin. Their flattened bodies are flexible enough to follow the shallow curves of a pillar, and their margins can adhere closely enough that even a strong local current does not readily lift them. Most are small, though old individuals occasionally become broad, thin disks whose outlines are difficult to distinguish from the surface beneath them. They neither see nor glow. Their pallor has no use in their own darkness.

A file of grazers is not quite a procession. There is no leader, and neighboring animals may be separated by several body lengths. The files form because many grazers respond to the same lines of sap-richness and the same open feeding pores. One advances, stops to rasp at the new tissue around a pore lip, and advances again. Another finds the same favorable course some distance behind. Their common direction only becomes apparent after watching for a long time.

Their feeding is slight at any one place and continuous across the basin. They do not gouge the pillars or expose deeper tissue. They take the growth that would otherwise close the pores, and the plant replaces what they take. The faint sizzling around them is the sound of many tiny mouthparts working out of rhythm with one another. The closer clicks occur when a grazer shifts its grip, meets another, or releases its hold and allows the current to carry it elsewhere. Most never travel far. A pillar may contain a grazer’s entire world: taste under the body, pressure above it, and the slow approach of other appetites along the same surface.

The sieve-bodied swimmers belong to the water between surfaces. Their bodies contain more passage than flesh. Water enters them, crosses membranes where spores and fragments of living tissue collect, and leaves through lateral openings. They feed while coasting, then give a small muscular contraction and coast again. What looks from nearby like a shoal is usually a formation with no dense center: individuals scattered through a long volume, all moving in approximately the same direction, none needing to remain close to another.

Such a formation may take a long time to pass a fixed point and still never obscure anything. There is nothing to obscure. Its members are known chiefly by the showers of tapping made as filtering structures flex and clear. Those taps are not necessarily messages. Nevertheless, other animals use them. Cavity dwellers orient toward them, larger swimmers alter course because of them, and the sieve swimmers themselves can remain loosely aligned by listening to the shifting distribution of sound.

They turn around pillars before touch becomes possible. The pillar’s drone strengthens; the pressure field changes; the formation opens slightly and closes again beyond it. Their feeding grounds are not places where food lies waiting, but closed paths along which the plant’s suspended spores and tissue fragments continually become available. Home, for them, is a circulation.

The ready-grown cavities hold animals with a different relation to stillness. These are the eyeless, many-finned creatures whose fins seem too numerous for swimming until one sees what they actually do. Each fin makes a separate, minute adjustment. Together they allow an animal to hang in a chamber without bracing against its walls, correcting for currents too weak to move its body visibly.

Their sensitivity to pressure is so fine that an approaching animal may be detected first as a change in the water at the cavity mouth, then as a pattern along the fins, and only much later as sound or contact. A distant call from one of the great open-water animals can make every fin fold at once. A small swimmer passing nearby produces a different response: the cavity dweller contracts, feeds, and returns to apparent immobility.

They do not excavate, enlarge, or furnish their shelters. Young animals occupy narrow clefts; larger ones use deeper chambers. A suitable cavity may remain empty, while another holds the same occupant through much of its life. When that occupant leaves or dies, there is no nest to mark it. There is only water resuming its former path.

Between these conspicuous kinds are softer and quieter lives: minute animals developing among suspended spores, slender scavengers following chemical traces through open water, larval forms that spend one part of life swimming and another attached to plant tissue. Few make sounds that carry. They are important less as presences than as interruptions in the persistence of things. Through them, dead tissue does not remain dead tissue for long, and loose organic matter rarely remains loose long enough to become debris.

The great animals of the open middle are harder to know as bodies than as events.

One may approach as a broad pressure before there is any sound peculiar to it. The drone of a nearby pillar alters. Something immense passes at a distance that cannot be estimated, and then the ordinary notes return. Even a direct encounter offers little sense of the whole animal: a head or flank continuing beyond touch, a slow displacement of water, a wake that remains after the creature itself has gone.

They are built for continuation rather than speed. Their movements are economical, and their feeding is correspondingly unhurried. Some strain suspended matter during long passages; others take isolated animals from the edges of sieve formations. There are no thrashing pursuits through the pillars and no crowded feeding grounds. The cold water and sparse food favor long intervals in which very little appears to happen.

Their routes are not trails. Nothing is marked, and the water retains no impression of their passage. A route consists of acoustic relations: one pillar’s note held to the left until an arch’s rushing tone rises ahead; the open floor’s dull reply beneath; the rim’s roar gradually separating into layers; then the sound of another pillar, distant but recognizable, by which the next basin can be entered without touching a wall.

The routes are old because the acoustic geography is old, and because each generation finds the same dependable features. A young animal may travel for a time in the acoustic company of an older one, far enough behind that the two rarely see—though sight is not the relevant sense—or touch. Eventually it turns away. What it carries on is not an object or an instruction, but a practiced way of recognizing where it is.

Their calls work at several scales. A single knock may precede a small alteration of course. A descending groan can remain audible across an immense reach of open water and be answered, or simply cause another animal to change direction. The rare booms seem to belong especially to the open middle, where the rim’s delayed reply gives back the size of the surrounding space. Whether these distinctions constitute meanings in any deliberate sense is less clear than their consequences. Animals remain separated, find one another when necessary, avoid arches already occupied by another large body, and orient within water that offers no visible horizon.

What sounds like a chorus is often a number of solitary animals negotiating distances too great for meeting.

Death briefly alters this dispersal. A small animal may be taken apart before it reaches the floor. The body of a great swimmer can become, for a while, the most crowded place in Crownwater: scavengers arrive along chemical gradients, feed, and disperse, while root hairs spread into whatever settles against them. Nutrients are drawn away and distributed. Eventually the floor resumes its smoothness. There is no mound, no bed of hard remains, and no enduring change in the sound of that district. One voice is absent. Others continue.

None of this is peaceful in the sense of being free from appetite. Tissue is eaten; swimmers are taken; cavity dwellers starve if too little passes their mouths; grazers are lifted away before they can reattach. Nor is the steadiness the result of cooperation consciously maintained. The grazers do not know that they preserve feeding pores. The sieve swimmers do not clear the water for anything else. The plant does not reward its animals, and the great swimmers do not keep their routes for the benefit of those that follow.

The steadiness lies in the accounting. Removal meets regrowth, reproduction meets loss, and every life is small enough in its demands—or rare enough in its occurrence—that the basin’s larger balances remain unchanged.

Each kind of inhabitant therefore lives in a different Crownwater. For the consortium, it is a body extended through gradients of flow and damage. For a grazer, it is taste, adhesion, and the nearness of living surface. For a cavity animal, it is a chamber defined by pressure. For a sieve swimmer, it is a moving current populated by taps. For the great swimmers, it is a country of echoes, routes, and distant replies, most of which never need to become touch.

No inhabitant possesses a complete view of the sea. The plant is coextensive with it but has no vantage point; the animals have vantage points but almost no views.

To float among them is consequently to be surrounded by evidence of life while remaining, almost always, alone. Crownwater’s inhabitants do not fill its maintained emptiness. They give that emptiness distance, direction, and scale.
