The Brine Hollow has a population, but scarcely a community. Its visible inhabitants amount to fewer than a dozen tussocks of wire sedge and one small fern. They make no meadow, no thicket, and no promise of either. Yet they are not scattered at random. Each occupies an address in the plumbing.

The sedges stand on the far side, with several paces of bare crust between one tussock and the next. They do not crowd the brine pool, despite its deceptive fringe of color. Each grows where dependable condensation has made a little fresh water available: beneath a drip, in the small diluted halo where moisture reaches stone often enough to keep the salts from simply taking it back. There is scarcely any soil around their roots. They hold to wetted cracks and hairline runnels, living in a temporary correction of the hollow’s chemistry.

Their narrow leaves are gray-green rather than lush. Salt has burned the ends transparent, so that some of the older growth looks as though it has been tipped with glass. A tussock may put out a short increment of new growth, then lose as much when a drip weakens or shifts. It does not prepare the ground for successors. Between the plants, the crust remains bare, hard, and unwelcoming.

Nothing comes to use them. No insects work among the stems; no animal browses the leaves or carries them away for a nest. When a stem falls, it stays where it landed. If brine reaches it, it may stiffen beneath a translucent mineral coat before it has had time to collapse. Thus the newest organic thing in the hollow can come to resemble one of its oldest objects, while the living tussock beside it continues its slight, uncertain gains.

The fern survives under even narrower terms. Its mat, no broader than a spread hand, lies in the shade of a leaking flange. The leak supplies the water; the flange provides the shadow; the smallness of the failure keeps both from becoming lethal. A sound repair would end the fern by drying it. A much worse leak might scald it or lay salt across its fronds. Its existence depends on the metal continuing to fail at approximately its present rate. Its hand-breadth is not necessarily a measure of youth. It is the size of the habitable interval.

No other ferns follow from it in any visible advance. No sedge forms a continuous margin. There is no food web worth the name, and little of the ordinary business of consumption and removal. The hollow’s noise does not conceal a quieter living murmur. When the pressure valve closes and the latest concussion has returned from the walls, nothing living answers.

If the pump is counted as an inhabitant, it is by far the dominant one. It is not alive, though it possesses more of the apparatus associated with life than anything else there: intake and output, a repeated pulse, by-products, and a dependence on energy supplied from elsewhere. The motor takes current. The three plungers draw hot brine. Nearly everything they produce is sent away through the insulated main. Heat, leakage, condensate, steam, and noise remain behind.

The plants live entirely among those leftovers.

The pump does not tend them. It does not quicken when a sedge spreads or falter when one dies. A small loss of water that means nothing to the machinery may be the whole basis of a tussock’s survival. A change in load, a shift in condensation, or the slow sealing of a leak can redraw the living map without altering the roar appreciably. The pump’s only gift is repetition: it maintains conditions stable enough for a few accidents to continue.

Human beings are present without being residents. The valve wheel, the fallen insulators, the rail disappearing beneath white crust, and the enamel number wired beside a cut-back pipe do not constitute habitation. There are long bare intervals between them and no road down. Even the working pump-house is not a home. People made the hollow and still act upon it remotely, but no person has joined its small population.

All the visible life lies between two opposed waters. The nearly fresh condensate sustains roots while patiently dissolving stone. The hot brine excludes vegetation and builds mineral over whatever it touches. One water opens hairline spaces; the other closes them. A plant may be fed by the first while its fallen stems are preserved by the second. Life and fossilization are not separated by any great distance here, only by temperature, salinity, and the route taken through a pipe.

There is therefore no clear progression toward marsh or meadow. The orange, olive, and green margins promise fertility that is not there. Most of the floor remains mineral and empty. A new drip might make another sedge possible while an older halo fails; the fern might persist for years without becoming larger. Given enough time, mineral deposition could close over the entire arrangement. The plants have no influence on that ending.

For now, fresh water gathers, falls, and is intercepted by a few roots. The fern remains its small green fact in the metal shade. The sedges hold their transparent tips above the crust while the pump drives on, deafening and indifferent.

Life has not reclaimed the Brine Hollow. It has found the leaks.
