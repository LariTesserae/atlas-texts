Life in the Level Spring is mostly a matter of interfaces. It occupies the places where warm water meets air, where a current becomes a film, where a film is just thick enough to shelter something that can feed.

The bacterial growth at the inlet is therefore not merely the spring’s simplest inhabitant. It is the inhabitant on which the others depend. It lives protected within a few narrow seams, taking no energy from daylight and little from the water’s passage. Its nourishment is the faint chemical difference between freshly risen water and the surfaces across which that water spreads. The film extends so slowly that its persistence is easier to detect than its growth. Calcite closes over portions of it. Elsewhere, a seam remains wet, or a new route opens by a fraction, and the film continues there.

Now and then something from those seams is carried into the overflow: a cell, a fragment, a trace of organic matter too slight to discolor the water. This is enough. The Level Spring’s animal life is built from almost nothing, but it is not built from nothing.

The blind crustaceans are the largest permanent animals. Most of their lives are spent out of sight, pressed against the submerged side of a basin rim or beneath a shallow ledge where the current cannot easily lift them. Their bodies are clear enough that the stone seems visible through them. Only a disturbance in a reflection, or the brief articulated movement of a limb, separates one from a particle of calcite.

They are eyeless, but the chamber is not blank to them. Their antennae and fine sensory hairs register currents too weak to move the surface. They can tell the approach of overflow from the stillness before a drop. Organic matter carried from the inlet gives one thread of water a different meaning from another. A strike elsewhere in the chamber arrives as pressure through stone and pool alike. When a person steps near a basin, the animal may remain motionless long after the human ear has forgotten the sound.

They feed without haste and travel without anything that could be called exploration. A pool may contain a lineage for centuries, though not every pool contains one. Young animals, and occasionally an adult dislodged by overflow, pass from basin to basin. Some are carried into cracks from which they cannot return. Others find another rim, another thin delivery of food, and settle. Reproduction is infrequent enough that there is never a swarm, never a season in which the water seems full of them. Their transparent molts settle onto pale stone and gradually disappear beneath mineral films.

No individual is ancient. The antiquity belongs to the chain of replacements.

For these animals, the spring is neither silent nor unchanging. It is a world of pressure, current, and scarcity, in which the height of the water matters chiefly because it determines which surfaces remain submerged. They know nothing of the level as a whole. Each lives within a very small portion of its consequences.

Nothing larger has been able to make a living there. Human visitors brought warmth, skin, oil, fibers, and the microscopic life that travels with a body, but these did not establish another ecology. A hand left a little organic material; the water carried most of it away. Breath briefly altered a pocket of air. Then the visitor climbed the steps, and the spring closed over the interruption without needing to erase it.

The human objects have different degrees of belonging. The bowl and hammerstone remain possessions without possessors, set aside in a niche. The stair still belongs mainly to the act of visiting. But the clay trough has been incorporated. When people first placed it, water followed a slightly more deliberate route. Then the joints sealed, calcite thickened along its course, and the rectangular receiving basin found its own place in the overflow. What had been an alteration became one of the paths by which the chamber continued.

The spring remembers routes better than it could ever remember faces.

There is, however, one further inhabitant. It is neither the film nor the animals, and it cannot be discovered by looking behind the columns or waiting in the darkest pool.

It is the level.

This is not a spirit concealed in the water, nor an intelligence that might be coaxed into taking a shape. It has no separate substance from which a body could be made. Its body is the continuing relation among inflow, basin, outlet, evaporation, and return: the whole arrangement by which water is always being lost and the pool is never quite lost with it.

It acts, if that word can be used, at the speed of stone. Deposition narrows one route. Dissolution opens another. A submerged seam accepts a little more of the outflow; a rim holds a little longer before surrendering its surplus. No individual change resembles a decision. Over spans longer than any visit, the changes together maintain a boundary so precise that it can be read in the mineral stains.

A living body also persists without keeping the same matter. Its tissues are replaced, its fluids exchanged, its surfaces continually surrendered and renewed. What endures is not the material but the organization of replacement. In that sense, the level is less a feature of the spring than its oldest physiological act.

It has no center. It would not be found in the warm inlet alone, or in the deepest buried outlet. It exists most completely in the connections between them. The visible pool surfaces are simply where its condition can be seen most easily.

Nor is it eternal. There was a time before the linked basins and buried passages came into their present balance, when water stood at other heights and followed other routes. The inhabitant formed gradually, without a birth that could have been witnessed. It could end just as impersonally. A diverted inlet, a deeply opened drain, a sufficient change in the limestone below: any of these could make the old balance impossible. Nothing would be released. Nothing would linger in the chamber afterward. The stone would remain, and some of the smaller life might persist for a while, but the level would no longer be there.

Whether anything like experience accompanies its persistence is not a question the chamber answers. There is no need to imagine an ancient mind dreaming beneath the plain, slowly composing thoughts from drops. Its strangeness is more complete than that. It need not dream in order to continue.

A visitor can become briefly part of it. Warm skin gathers condensation; for a few minutes, the person is another surface on which the spring’s lost moisture returns. A dipped vessel removes a little water. A ring crosses the pool, reaches the crowded stone, and disappears among the rims. The surface settles. Inflow makes good the difference. Nothing recognizes the visitor, and nothing needs to.

Taking water is not theft from it. Leaving water is not a gift. Both are merely small disturbances within the range of what it can absorb. To damage it, one would have to alter the terms by which disturbances are absorbed.

So the Level Spring is inhabited at three scales. The films live where water arrives. The small blind animals live on what arrival releases into the overflow. The level lives between arrival and departure, in the continual making-small of their difference.

None of these lives possesses the chamber. None stands apart from it. Even the oldest among them is not something that has resisted replacement, but something that replacement has repeatedly restored.

Its whole biography could be written in one sentence: water enters, water leaves, and the level returns.

That is also the closest thing it has to a pulse.
