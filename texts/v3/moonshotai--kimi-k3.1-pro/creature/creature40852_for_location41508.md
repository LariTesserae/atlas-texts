Nothing in the Low Works keeps house. Even *residence* implies too much: a repeatable address, a tolerable interval between departures, ground that remains related to itself. There are no native settlements, no domesticated plants, no animals concealed in the green murk. Yet the basin is densely inhabited. Its life simply does not depend on staying put.

The vegetation has developed without mouths. Nothing browses the crowns, drills the seeds, or carries pollen in exchange for food. Consequently, little in the Low Works is shaped to attract or resist an animal. There are no fleshy fruits, no nectar, no burr-like seeds made to catch in fur. Where tissue is armored, the armor answers pressure, cavitation, hot rain, or the impact of fresh casting. Where it is thin, it can be extravagantly thin. Color is mostly chemistry or light-harvesting, not display. Nothing is sweet because nothing needs to be persuaded.

Reproduction belongs instead to the machines. A pump surge does the work of wind. An intake fan becomes a dispersal season. Pollen travels in dense threads through the pressure rings underwater; spores are sucked from one thicket and plastered against cable sheathing in another. Some seeds remain indefinitely inert until a particular turbine harmonic makes their coats flex and split. A change in engine speed can therefore produce a change of forest. There is no spring, but there are frequencies at which certain hillsides become possible.

The stilt-crowned trees are the most visible inhabitants, though their size makes them look more permanent than they are. Their near-black leaves gather the weak green light and shed rain in heavy ropes. Below them, the trunks divide into roots that treat concrete, clinker, pipework, and older root mats with complete impartiality. A warm conduit is especially favorable: not because the tree feeds on its current, but because heat leaks from it, mineral water collects around it, and its length offers purchase across ground that may otherwise be only precipitate.

These trees are not built to withstand every revision. Most do not. Those that survive a reassignment of submergence often survive by changing the employment of their tissues. A crown leaf driven fathoms down may separate along preformed veins into narrow pressure ribbons. Roots lifted into steaming air close their wettest surfaces and develop the open, corky texture ordinarily found nearer the crown. The tree does not right itself afterward. It remains bent, partly drowned, sometimes flowering below what is temporarily its root line. Its form is an account of what has happened to it, written without any expectation that the account will stay legible.

Individual survival matters less than the quantity and variety of seed left behind. A heavy crown may contain hard seeds for deep water, buoyant seeds for pump travel, and thin-coated seeds that must germinate almost immediately on hot new ground. The tree is brief. Its possibilities are less so.

The copper-colored reed beds are less stands of separate stems than detachable fabrics. Their color comes from mineral scale laid down in the outer tissue; old stems become brittle and weighted, while younger growth remains flexible enough to lie flat beneath a surge. Their nodes root readily when torn free. Thus a crosscurrent that destroys a reed flat may also plant several others, and an intake that shreds the mat can distribute its viable fragments more efficiently than intact growth ever could.

Between the reeds, the broad ribbon leaves make still less resistance to events. They stream, twist, collapse, and occasionally fold around a stem rather than tear. Some cross the boundary between two bodies of water, so that one part of a plant absorbs minerals in a lower lagoon while another puts out spores in the water hanging above it. During the passage of a vertical sea surface, the same leaf may be twisted through several pressure regimes without changing its location very much. To call it upright or inverted afterward is usually a courtesy to the observer.

The smaller growths live closer to the machinery’s immediate effects. Green cable-threads begin in warm seams and damaged sheathing, at first no more than a damp discoloration. Within a few production cycles they can fur a whole span, their reproductive nodes opening unevenly as load and temperature travel along the cable. The translucent fans in the lee of concrete are not quite leaves and not quite roots. They are absorbent surfaces flattened against the current, intercepting dissolved tissue, pollen, lime, and mineral fines before the flow can carry them away. A fan torn loose does not necessarily die; pressed into another root mat, it may resume as a root.

The waxed rosettes in the eyes of whirlpools are among the briefest inhabitants. Each is a little floating raft organized around one heavy seed. The rosette persists only while the whirl provides a comparatively calm center and a steady delivery of floating material. When the eye shifts, the plant is pulled apart. Its leaves spread across the water, and the seed drops. Some seeds must be wetted, dried in steam, and wetted again before they can open. Others require the violent pressure change of being carried from an upper body of water into a lower one. None of this is travel in the animal sense. The rosette has no destination. It has only a sequence of conditions.

Beneath all these forms is the root web, which might be called the Braid if a name were needed. It has no dependable outer boundary. Roots from different lineages meet, fuse, separate, and grow through the remains of earlier fusions. It holds the youngest mud together, wraps pipes, fills fractures, and occupies the narrow interval between soil and machinery. There is little humus in the Low Works; soil formation is too slow. The Braid is soil while alive and solution shortly after death. Warm water breaks down fallen fronds and drowned stems, and the fans and fine roots intercept what can be used before it settles beyond reach.

The Braid is also the basin’s seed bank and, in a limited sense, its memory.

Its roots do not form annual rings. They thicken according to surges, heat exposures, and intervals of deep pressure, so a section through old root tissue is more likely to record pump behavior than years. Its dormant seeds preserve a different archive: the proportions of hard coats, resonant shells, quick-germinating forms, and propagules made to be torn apart reflect earlier machine states. A turbine note long absent may still find seeds capable of answering it.

This is not thought. The Braid does not recognize an intrusion or pursue a plan, and there is no reason to imagine a forest mind waking beneath the casting flats. But neither is it quite memoryless. It cannot remember places, because places are among the least durable things available to it. It remembers processes in the only ways vegetation can: by altered growth, unequal dormancy, and the repeated production of whatever happened to leave descendants.

The deepest dormancies are associated with revision itself. Certain seeds open only under the low three-note chord. Their layered coats respond to the notes in sequence, each layer releasing the next. Others are held shut by continuous vibration; in the common silence, when every engine stops together, the tension fails. Less than a second is enough. When the noise returns, those seeds are already open.

For such life, a phase-locked catastrophe is not merely an obstacle to reproduction. It is also one of reproduction’s necessary conditions. Revisions expose fresh minerals, fracture slabs, mix seed beds, and return accumulated tissue to water and rain. Left in genuine peace, parts of the Low Works ecology would not flourish. Some seeds would never open; some fast thickets would exhaust the usable material around them; some lineages dependent on shredding and redistribution would dwindle. Disturbance has ceased to be an interruption of their life cycle and become part of its mechanism.

During the worst revisions, even descent ceases to be straightforward. Ordinarily a thicket comes from an earlier thicket. But *earlier* is not always available as an organizing principle. Seeds can be washed out of growth associated with a forest whose ground has not yet been extruded; later, that forest may contribute propagules to the same surge. Such a stand has no first generation that can be reached simply by going back far enough. Its lineage is not a line but a loop with lives attached to it.

This does not make the individual plants supernatural. Most still germinate, grow, are struck, and die in an intelligible order. It means only that the ecology as a whole cannot be mapped solely by ancestry any more than by geography. Persistence belongs to viable material and recurring forms, not necessarily to biographies that can be narrated from beginning to end.

The buried engines participate without being alive and without tending anything. Root mats narrow intakes and alter their surges; the surges tear mats loose and plant the fragments elsewhere. Growth around outfalls redirects warm water into new pans. Cable-threads trap sediment that would otherwise wash through, while their destruction returns that sediment to suspension. No intention closes the loop. The machines are not partners, and the plants are not engineers. It is an ecology with one living kingdom and many kinds of weather.

At full production, its inhabitants can be distinguished by how they receive force. The black crowns shed it. The reeds divide beneath it. The ribbon leaves stream with it. The fans flatten and drink from what it carries. The rosettes are taken apart. The Braid holds, or lets go in sections, or is drawn through an intake and scattered into green rain. Seeds split at notes too deep or too high to be separated from the rest of the noise.

Then the engines stop.

For that fraction of a second, nothing new is struck into growth. Vibration-held seed coats loosen. Suspended filaments begin to settle. No creature emerges to take advantage of the quiet, because nothing has been hiding. The life of the Low Works was already everywhere, exposed and making use of the blows.

What follows may drown the seeds, expose them to steam, or cast ground beneath them. It does not matter to the pattern, though it matters utterly to each seed. Enough will open somewhere, under some revised arrangement of water and noise.

The Low Works is not inhabited despite its continual manufacture. It is inhabited by means of it.
