On a stair tread in the oldest district, a copper-shelled mite opens a seam in the fiber.

The stair is alive, though it has been planed flat and drilled for a handrail. The mite finds the dead tissue first: a few papery cells crushed by decades of feet. It cuts these free, works them into its body, and leaves behind a pellet too small to see without kneeling. A decomposer cord will find the pellet. Rootlets around the cord will take what the cord releases. Somewhere above, a leaf wafer will thicken by an almost immeasurable amount.

Most life in the Rootworks happens at this scale, and at this degree of indirectness.

The grey-green skin is not one plant, nor quite a community with a common purpose. It is a population of populations. Felt-formers bind loose dust into a surface that can bear weight. Braided rootlets seek fractures and conductors. Hard-stemmed species climb wherever the current is suitable, while low, bitter growth persists around black salt and is avoided by almost everything except mites. Some varieties respond quickly to a pulse and then stop; others continue for days after the circuit has been removed. A road is a multitude held flat. A tower wall is another multitude persuaded to stand under load.

The engineers know this, even when district plans make the growth look singular and compliant. Two patches that appear identical from a tram may require different instructions. One will thicken into a clean structural rib. The other will fork around it, leaving a hollow that was not wanted and may later prove useful.

The pale grazers move over all this with the assurance of animals that have never needed to hurry.

Residents call them hollowfeet. A herd crossing an open basin looks, at first, like a scattering of stones that has changed position while no one was watching. Their knee-high bodies are the colour of old dust, with darker pleats behind the shoulders and along the browsing mouth. Their hooves ring almost imperceptibly when they strike exposed mineral, but on the felt they make no sound at all.

A hollowfoot does not simply crop what is before it. It tastes the age of a shoot, the mineral charge in its tissue, and the faint electrical irritation left by a nearby conductor. Herds therefore concentrate along some service paths and avoid others. They keep lesser routes open, but not as a public service. They are seeking growth at the stage between translucence and hardness, when it can still be torn.

Within a herd, decisions travel by contact. Shoulder touches shoulder; a head turns; a forehoof presses twice against a stem. Several minutes later the whole group may be moving in another direction, with no visible animal having taken the lead. People who work outdoors learn to watch their feet rather than their faces.

Occasionally a herd finds a survey filament at the frontier and crops a neat corridor through a line intended to remain undisturbed. Occasionally, in the settled districts, it opens a shortcut so convenient that maintenance crews begin using it too. A few service roads have inherited their gentle, impractical curves from routes first chosen by grazers.

At ventilation intake, the moths rise.

They spend the cool stillness packed into crevices, behind lamp housings, inside discarded casings, and in the narrow spaces between structural stalk and metal frame. When the pressure changes, their broad matte wings unfold. Dust adheres to them in pale strokes. Pollen adheres more deeply, in the grooves of the body.

The moths do not wander evenly through the hollow. They gather along warm machinery, follow the lee of towers, and stream above open basins where the current is unobstructed. A concentration over one district may be visible from the next as a soft, shifting grain in the lamplight. Then the small hunters begin to appear.

These are many-legged, jointed animals, most no longer than a hand. They can flatten themselves almost to the thickness of a cable sheath. In the mills they move through spilled fiber; in the switchyards they run along insulators and disappear into seams that look too narrow to contain anything alive. They hunt by touch and by the scent of wing scales. To a maintenance crew, an unusual gathering of hunters beneath a housing is not an infestation to be cleared immediately. It is information. Something in there is sheltering, nesting, dying, or chewing.

The larger membrane-winged predators wait higher up. They roost with their wings collapsed along their bodies, nearly indistinguishable from torn insulation or a folded length of structural sheet. When moth numbers thicken sufficiently, one drops from a gantry, opens, and crosses several hundred metres with only a few adjustments of its wing surfaces. Its passage does not fill the cavern with sound. A moth vanishes; the predator rises elsewhere.

People argue about these animals only when their habits intersect with a plan. A roost above a public gallery may be tolerated for generations. The same species above exposed switchgear will be encouraged to move, usually by offering it a better roost somewhere less inconvenient. A cable burrower is a pest inside a live conductor and a useful fissure-cleaner at the excavation face. Even the mites, universally depended upon, can become a nuisance when their pellets accumulate where a mechanism requires a clean contact.

No one has succeeded in drawing a durable boundary between helpful and harmful life.

The people themselves are easy to overlook, not because they are few, but because their city gives them so much room. A residential gallery may contain hundreds of inhabitants and still present an outsider with several minutes of empty corridor, a closed freight door, and the low vibration of a press somewhere below.

They are dry-bodied animals, like the grazers and hunters. Their tissues carry sealed mineral gels rather than watery fluids. Their skin has a fine, matte grain, and a clear inner lid protects the eyes from excavation dust. At close hand, their apparent stillness is misleading. Scent changes at the collar and wrist, small pressures of the fingers, and vibrations picked up through feet or railings carry much of the ordinary business of attention: recognition, caution, amusement, the request to repeat something.

They speak as well, especially in workshops and across distances where touch would be impractical. Their voices are not hushed by custom. There is simply seldom much noise to speak over.

Their clothing is made from the same growth they cut into houses and gears, though processed differently: combed fiber for warmth, thin laminated husk for outer work clothes, translucent growing-tip tissue treated into flexible seals. Garden-engineers wear insulated soles and cuffs studded with contacts. Freight workers favour matte yellow or iron-red panels so that they remain visible across the long floors. At home, people often wear undyed sage-grey, and in a quiet gallery a group of residents can seem to emerge from the walls only when they move.

Meals bring out warmer scents. There are crisp cakes of milled seed tissue, slivers of oil-rich pith, cultured gels served from sealed containers, and sharply aromatic preparations of young leaf. Nothing is boiled. Nothing needs to be. Cooking is a matter of pressure, dry heat, grinding, fermentation within closed vessels, and the careful combining of mineral charges.

A home is never quite a finished object.

Its address usually includes a district, a gallery, and a plan revision. The revision matters. A room may have been enlarged since the last visit of a friend; a passage may have become a shallow alcove; a stair may now turn around a rib that did not previously exist. Most alterations are modest and announced in advance. The inhabitants do not wake to find their furniture wandering on roots. Nevertheless, everyone who lives in growing architecture acquires a habit of noticing seams.

To add a shelf, a resident may simply bolt one to the frame. To add a series of shelves intended to bear weight for decades, it can be better to alter the wall’s instruction and let the necessary thickening occur behind them. Children learn the difference early. Their first construction exercises are small frames in which a shoot must be guided around an opening without closing it. The results are generally crooked. A child who has produced a merely usable bracket has learned something about current, load, patience, and the fact that living material does not share a child’s sense of urgency.

Garden-engineering is correspondingly both more and less mysterious than it appears from a distance. There are load calculations, insulating gloves, junction boxes, surveying instruments, saws, planes, and long arguments over whether a hollow is structural or accidental. There is also the experienced hand laid against a stem to feel whether machinery beyond it is running evenly, and the experienced eye that notices hunters collecting around a cable fault before any instrument reports one.

The best engineers are not those who expect perfect obedience. They are those who can tell which departures matter.

One may spend a morning protecting a young shoot from freight wheels and the afternoon planing several tonnes of related growth into sheet. To an outsider this can look contradictory. To the engineer, one plant is in the right place and the other is not. Tenderness does not preclude harvesting; harvesting does not preclude care.

The same practicality shapes the people’s attitude toward age. They do not possess picturesque ruins, and they do not greatly regret their absence. Old material is useful material. Retired rails disappear into foundations. Compressed root-felt is lifted, combed, pressed, or instructed to become something else. Sentiment attaches instead to small offcuts, obsolete plan sheets, the outline of a former door preserved in a wall, or a peculiar fork that an earlier engineer chose not to correct.

A person may be remembered by a detail left in the public growth: a seat-sized hollow retained where someone habitually waited for a tram, or a pattern of ribs whose unusual spacing commemorates no event except that its maker thought it beautiful. Such memorials are not guaranteed permanence. If the loads change, they may be cut away. This is considered unfortunate, not sacrilegious.

District meetings spend a surprising amount of time on very small living things. A proposed avenue may be delayed because its conductors would erase a warm refuge where moths gather during the rest period. A factory extension may be redesigned around a grazer route, not out of reverence for the herd, but because forty years of attempts to divert it have already produced forty years of damaged fencing. Elsewhere, roots are cut back without debate, burrowers are sealed out, and an elegantly unintended chamber is filled because it would otherwise compromise an arch.

The residents do not describe themselves as living in harmony with the city. Harmony suggests an agreement that has been reached. Their situation is more continuous than that.

Industry keeps creating opportunities that no plan has anticipated. Behind the pressworks, where waste heat prevents leaves from folding during the cool period, grazers linger longer than they should. Hunters follow the moths that follow the grazers’ disturbed pollen. A warm pocket intended only as the side effect of manufacture becomes a little vertical economy of folded wings, droppings, dry pellets, decomposer cords, and unusually dark felt. Eventually someone puts a railing around it because workers have begun using it as a place to sit.

In another district, an alternation error leaves a row of narrow hollows along a warehouse wall. They are too small for storage and too awkward for machinery. Moths nest in them. Then children begin visiting at intake to watch the wall appear to exhale wings. Years later, when an engineer proposes correcting the error, the warehouse crew objects. The hollows have become part of how they tell where they are.

At the frontier, the sequence of arrival is especially clear. First come the instructed filaments and the animals that clean after them: fissure mites, tiny burrowers, decomposer cords carried in sealed cases by the excavation crews. Dust clings to the work lamps. Before a roof arch has closed, something is usually living behind the lamp housing. A future station can acquire its first hunters before it has a platform, and its first moth roost before its name is used by anyone except freight dispatchers.

The house-sized bud at the end of the track is therefore not empty while it waits. Mites explore the planed valve where the platform number has been stencilled. Fine roots adjust themselves around its internal conductors. A grazer occasionally tests the outer tissue and finds it too hard to be interesting. Somewhere within the raw wall beyond, roots are swelling into fractures, and small animals are already occupying spaces that did not exist a shift earlier.

The people who work here learn to distinguish the ticks. One is a cutting head somewhere out of sight. One is an animal turning in a fissure. One is the wall yielding by a fraction too small to measure without instruments. They do not treat every sound as a promise. Some fractures are useful; some require a crew to stop and reconsider the roof.

Back in the central galleries, all these lives remain widely spaced in the clear, workable light. A tram crosses an empty basin. Two residents talk beside a freight cart while a hollowfoot herd passes behind them. Copper mites turn dead fiber into the beginnings of new growth beneath both. High above, a folded predator shifts its grip on a gantry.

Then the ventilation reverses.

Mites draw into seams. Moths lift or settle according to their district and temperature. People near the collectors look up, not in ceremony but because a blocked frond has a different sound from an open one. Across the towers and low roads, millions of leaf wafers flex. For a few moments the inhabited region makes its soft, dispersed turning of pages.

Afterward, the separate sounds return: a relay, a hoof on exposed mineral, a dry valve closing underfoot. The city has not acted with one will. It has no single will to act with. Its plants, animals, machines, and people have simply answered the same change in pressure, each according to its own interests.

That is how most of the Rootworks gets made. Something is instructed. Something responds. Something else takes advantage of the response. The people correct what they can, preserve what proves useful, and learn to live inside the remainder.
