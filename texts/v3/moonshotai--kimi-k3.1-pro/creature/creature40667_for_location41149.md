## The Life of the Unfinished Hollow

What lives in the Unfinished Hollow is small, translucent, and mostly concerned with water. Nothing there behaves like an omen. The animals do not guard the threshold, interpret the incisions, or tend the little crescent of stone. They feed, clean themselves, wait out dryness, and go on doing so while the chamber around them changes its mind.

The first living thing to become apparent is not an animal but a change in the surface. The colorless microbial skins begin wherever damp mineral persists beneath the light. A skin spreads grain by grain, too thin to cast a shadow, until the marl acquires the faintly altered sheen of something recently touched. The light sustains it in some manner, though without warmth: a skin kept wet beneath an opaque flake stops spreading, while another beside it continues across the open wall. In dry weather the skins do not so much die as cease to be surfaces. They retreat into the contacts between grains, where neither snail nor springtail can reach them.

The green filament, the scale of liverwort, and the arrested rootlet contribute almost nothing. They are three small accidents of vegetation, not a garden. The rootlet in particular seems less a resident than an arrival that has stopped to consider the conditions.

After rain, the glassy springtails emerge to harvest the clear pasture. They come from pores so fine that they appear to step out of slight variations in the wall’s color. Most are visible only by the minute darkness of a full gut moving inside an otherwise transparent body. Each shaves a narrow path through the microbial film, wandering and returning until its feeding becomes one of the looping tracks revealed when the clay darkens.

Around a fallen drop, several may gather without ever becoming a swarm. They touch the water, feed near its margin, and spring away at disturbances too slight for a larger animal to detect. Their jumps are usually merely too quick for the eye: a springtail is at one edge of a glistening bead, then somewhere else. The hollow’s distances make even this small disappearance seem consequential.

The blind mites work more slowly. Their forelegs test everything before their bodies commit to it: a grain, a film, the rim of a drop, the empty joint of a shed husk. A mite may spend a long while over such a husk, touching each leg and then its own, as though comparing measurements. It may be grazing the last microbes from the empty shell. It may simply be investigating the only other object in the hollow with approximately its own shape. Some husks belong to completed molts. Others appear before the molt that would explain them. The mites give both kinds the same meticulous attention.

Threadworms occupy the water held between grains. They do not leave burrows; the wet grit closes behind them. Their passage is visible only as a succession of fine bends, like a crack repeatedly changing its mind about which direction to take. When the seep spreads, they rise toward it. When it retreats, they settle deeper into the mineral seams and the wall appears uninhabited. They are food for the larger hunters, but also passengers through the pasture: microbial films often reappear richest along the routes they have taken.

The glass-snails are the easiest grazers to follow. Light catches their shells, while within each shell the animal remains a soft, curled comma, slowly clouded by what it has eaten. They rasp the skins from damp walls and from the undersides of flakes, drawing their translucent bodies forward with a patience that makes even the mites seem impulsive. Where moisture lasts, they may leave a few clear eggs in the shelter of a stone lip. More often the drying wall moves them on before they have remained anywhere long enough for that.

They leave grit beside the crescent of fitted flakes because grit adheres to them and later falls away. They do not select it, fit it, or return to improve the arrangement. A snail crawls over the crescent’s newest stone with exactly the same indifference it shows toward raw marl.

The two pseudoscorpions give the hollow its only suggestion of separate households, though each household is merely the underside of a stone.

One is hasty. A droplet’s tick brings its claws open; the passage of a snail draws it partway into the light. It lunges at small movements, misses often, and afterward cleans its claws with fussy thoroughness. It seems to hunt as much from irritation as from hunger. The other can remain motionless through nearly an entire cycle of wetting and drying. Springtails pass close to it. A mite investigates one of its old husks. Still it waits. When it finally feeds, it does so once and withdraws.

They have never been seen hunting together, and there is no reason to think they recognize each other as anything more than another possible disturbance. Their territories do not touch. Once, the hasty hunter was seen to close its claws on a shallow silver shadow just before a springtail arrived. It caught nothing. By the time the animal itself appeared, the pseudoscorpion was still again.

The milk-pink salamander belongs to the pool’s cool margin, though “belongs” may imply more attachment than it shows. It lies with its fine toes splayed on the wet clay and its head partly beneath the ledge. Sometimes the pool barely covers its back. Its color is so close to that of damp marl in certain lights that an observer may notice the slow movement of its throat before noticing the animal around it.

It is not a ruler, a guardian, or a larger intelligence wearing a small body. It is a salamander: alert to vibrations, inclined toward shelter, capable of remaining still for extraordinary periods, and interested chiefly in threadworms and the other soft-bodied life concentrated near water. Its hunt, when it finally occurs, consists of one deliberate step and a movement of the head too quick to resemble the stillness before it.

Its shadow is less dependable.

Sometimes that shadow slides forward over the grit, lowers its head, and returns with the minute tug of something held in its jaws while the salamander itself remains motionless beneath the ledge. No prey disappears. The pool does not stir. Much later, the salamander may perform the same hunt in the same place, or it may turn away and vanish beneath the stone. The shadow leaves no tracks and requires no food. It is not a second animal so much as a local failure of sequence.

Only one salamander has ever been seen at a time. Whether the hollow contains a succession of them, or repeatedly makes room for the same one, there is no reliable way to determine.

When the brightness densifies and the chamber becomes solid marl, none of the animals is crushed or fossilized. A springtail remains visible in mid-step; a mite continues lowering one foreleg; the salamander’s throat completes its slow pulse. They are suspended in stone without being imprisoned by it. Fossils do not finish their movements.

Nor do the inhabitants emerge from these intervals as though waking. There is no general rush toward the seam, no panic, no mass death afterward. A grazer resumes feeding if its pasture remains and searches if it does not. The salamander may find the pool shifted by a hand’s width and cross the intervening grit to lie down at its new edge. For the animals, even solidity does not quite succeed in becoming a catastrophe.

The architectural marks enter their lives only incidentally. Springtails feed along the three incisions when dampness gathers there, their brief lines diverging as the cuts do. Snails cross the crescent. Nothing pauses at the threshold. When the crescent gains another flake, no animal comes to inspect it.

Likewise, the pool’s reflections are not a trustworthy census. They may be crowded with snails that have not yet descended the wall or with springtails around a drop that has not fallen. Some of the reflected animals eventually arrive. Others never do. The living inhabitants drink, graze, and hunt among them without reacting to the discrepancy.

Yet beneath all these uncertainties, an actual ecology persists. Dampness and light produce the clear skins. Springtails, mites, worms, and snails crop them or move through them. The pseudoscorpions and the salamander take the smaller animals. Husks and bodies are worked over until almost nothing remains to accumulate. Rain expands the whole arrangement; drought contracts it toward the last wet seams. It is abundant only when examined closely, but it is not merely a collection of stranded creatures. They feed one another. They reproduce. They endure.

The least certain resident is the one heard inside the wall.

Its claw is very small. The tick it makes could belong to an ordinary pseudoscorpion testing a fracture, or to some other minute animal working at a seam that has not yet opened. Sometimes a later opening reveals a fresh scratch or a new pore; sometimes it reveals nothing that can be connected with the sound. No body has been found that certainly belongs to it.

Whatever makes the ticking does not seem to be knocking for release. The sound is intermittent and purposeful: touch, withdrawal, touch again. It is already behaving like an inhabitant.

The others are not waiting for the hollow to be completed. Their lives are brief, local, and entire in themselves. The grazers are already grazing, the hunters already hungry, the pale salamander already breathing beside whatever pool the chamber currently provides. They are ready for a world.

It is the room that is still learning how to hold them.
