Most of the basin’s inhabitants never enter the view from the rim. Even beside a pool, the greater part of them remains beneath the apparent surface: in silt, in the film on the water, in root hollows, or in disturbances so brief that they are easily mistaken for light.

The pale mud is inhabited to the depth of a hand and, in places, farther. Threadworms no thicker than rush fibers keep narrow passages open through it, drawing down oxygenated water and feeding on the fine organic dust that collects in their burrows. Other animals use and abandon the same passages. A single bubble rising through a pool may mean that a worm has turned, that a buried beetle larva has shifted, or that an old burrow has finally collapsed beneath the weight of a snail. The mud’s stillness is mostly a matter of scale.

These burrowers help preserve the open ground. A seedling that escapes the grazers may nevertheless tilt and die when its small root system is undercut. Its remains are then carried below, fragment by fragment. Pale silt lice perform a similar service above ground. They gather under fallen leaves, first along the veins and then across the softer tissue, reducing a waxy leaf to lace while it is still green. Shrimp and snails finish what the lice leave. This is why leaf fall produces so little black rot, and why the manyroot trees’ harder fallen branches are so anomalous: the same decomposers that strip a leaf in days work on their dense, bitter wood for years.

The fern-gilled snails are the basin’s principal grazers. In water their gills open behind the shell in delicate, branching plumes; in thinning water the plumes collapse and lie flat against the body. They scrape the brown-green film from submerged stones, keeping pace with its growth without ever quite eliminating it. Their feeding trails are visible only for a little while, as slightly brighter curves across darker algae. They also take the seed leaves of plants that venture beyond the hummocks. The snails are therefore gardeners whose gardening consists entirely of prevention.

Transparent shrimp work through their leavings and through the richer dust that accumulates behind roots and fallen wood. Some carry their eggs visibly, a cluster of green or amber beads beneath the abdomen. They are conspicuous only when they cross something dark. Over pale silt, a shrimp may be reduced to two moving black eyes and the brief jointed flicker of its legs.

The veilfish occupy the water above them. Adults are no longer than a finger, nearly colorless except for their eyes and a narrow silver line along the gut. The clouds of fry contain several hatches rather than one, each age keeping a slightly different depth. When the fish turn, the movement passes through these layers in succession, so the whole school seems to thicken and thin without changing its outline. They feed on midge larvae, tiny shrimp, and insects caught in the surface film. The larger individuals make the minute suction sounds that can be heard at the edges of quiet pools.

At the surface, the skaters maintain their irregular spacing with pulses too slight to carry more than a few handspans. These ripples serve for courtship, warning, and the brief disputes that occur when two hunting areas overlap. Each leg’s dimple is therefore not only a footprint on the light but part of a close-range conversation. Bubble beetles pass beneath that conversation, carrying their bright air stores down to hunt weak fry, take dead animals, or probe the upper burrows. Sometimes the bubble is the only visible part of the beetle: a point of silver moving with apparent purpose several finger-widths below the surface.

The midge columns are mating assemblies, each held over its own patch of warm shallow water. The adults do not blood-feed and live only briefly. Their larvae are more consequential, grazing and burrowing in immense numbers at the boundary between water and silt. Glasswings hunt them at both stages. The flattened aquatic young take midge larvae among the roots; the winged adults wait for the same prey to emerge into the air. A glasswing thus follows one food supply through two elements. Its dry feeding crackle is often the last event in a pursuit that began underwater.

The only animals that belong specifically to the standing rain are the rain treaders, though even they do not live inside the drops. A suspended drop is too pure and too short-lived to be a little pond. The treaders are wingless, pinhead-sized arthropods, silvered by water-repellent bristles. At ordinary times they browse the surface film along wet silt and pool margins. Where condensation recurs, their behavior changes.

As a drop grows, the air and surface immediately beneath it cool slightly. Treaders gather in that coolness. Often their waiting comes to nothing: the drop slides sideways, joins another, or remains suspended while the congregation slowly disperses. When one falls, however, its impact breaks and spreads the surface film, throwing up specks of organic matter and microorganisms too small to be separated by sight. The treaders caught directly beneath it are flung outward on the bright ring, usually without being wetted through, and then turn back to feed.

Their eggs endure long dry intervals in the silt. Simple flooding does not always hatch them; many seem to require the repeated chill-wetting produced by an active condensation field. Consequently, the treaders are better evidence of where the standing rain has been than any brief inspection of the pins. A patch that looks vacant at noon may be densely inhabited because drops formed there before dawn. In drought, these small, scattered feeding grounds become important to hushfrogs and other minute predators. They never make the basin look lush. The life around them remains almost entirely within the silver mud and the surface film.

The groves are the basin’s high country. Their manyroot trees add roots at least as readily as they add wood, sending dark arches outward and down until each hummock is bound into a lattice. A trunk that dies may be replaced by another shoot from the same root crown, so an apparently young tree can belong to a very old individual. The waxy living leaves resist most grazers and shed water into the fern rosettes below. Copper moss holds some of that water; reed stems and root hollows hold the rest. Long dripping after a shower is therefore not a single event but water being passed slowly from one living reservoir to another.

The smaller hummocks are not necessarily young groves waiting their turn. Some are old, limited associations of fern, moss, reed, and stunted rootstock whose edges have remained nearly fixed. Seedlings appear around them repeatedly and disappear just as repeatedly into mouths, burrows, or seasonal water.

Hushfrogs live mostly within the groves, though they sometimes hunt along the wet margins at night or after rain. They are dark leaf-green above and pale beneath, an effective coloring in a place where reflected light strikes upward. A frog may spend its whole adult life within a few root-lengths, feeding on mites, tiny larvae, rain treaders when available, and anything small enough to be taken without a prolonged struggle.

The hushfrog’s single click is a close-range signal, not an advertisement sent across the basin. Most encounters between frogs are silent. Eggs are laid in water held by fern rosettes or in deep root cups, where the tadpoles feed on leaf dust, drowned midges, and the film that grows on the cup walls. During drought, the adults retreat into root fissures that remain damp long after the surrounding silt has lost its shine. Because young frogs usually disperse along wet roots rather than across open ground, the populations of the three principal groves differ subtly in color and in the pitch of their clicks.

The white waders, or stillwaders, use the groves but hunt in the open. Their stillness serves two purposes. From underwater, a white body held against the white-gold glare is difficult to distinguish from reflected sky; from beneath the silt, a bird that has not shifted its feet for an hour has given a silt-hunter almost nothing to follow. Their toes are broad and flexible, and a walking bird lowers each foot so gradually that the water often closes around it without making a visible ring.

They do not defend pools with calls or displays. If two birds approach one another too closely, one simply moves away, a few slow steps at a time. Even a captured fish may be held underwater until its struggling stops before being lifted and swallowed. The few nests are built in high root forks, above the reach of the ordinary seasonal rise. Mates greet with posture and brief touches of the bill. Chicks flatten and become motionless at disturbance long before they are capable of flight.

The silt-hunters explain why so many of the basin’s other inhabitants seem cautious out of proportion to anything visible. An adult has a thick central body and a soft margin that can thin until it resembles a fold or shallow depression in the mud. There is no shell and no rigid fin. Sensory pits edge the underside, and the mouth opens below. A hunter usually takes prey by settling or rising beneath it, trapping it against the bottom rather than pursuing it through open water.

The young are transparent ribbons that live among grove roots and feed on shrimp, larvae, and fry. Only as they grow do they take on the color and habits of the buried adults. Mature hunters are few, widely dispersed, and capable of long fasts. They do not maintain calls, trails, or visible territories. Their principal sense is vibration carried through water and mud.

Ordinary rain appears to confuse that sense more than it excites it. Impacts arriving everywhere at once erase the distinction between near and far, large and small. Veilfish often feed higher and more boldly during such showers. A single stone, a landing bird, or a fish thrashing in one place is another matter: it stands out sharply against the general noise. When the rain stops, caution returns at different rates. The skaters move first, then the fish, then the birds, and the basin’s separated small sounds resume in an order determined by hundreds of independent decisions.

The apparent communal pause around a moving hunter is similarly deceiving. Skaters feel a pressure change through their feet. Fry see roots tremble or watch the shrimp vanish. Glasswings drop lower when their prey columns sink. A stillwader may respond only to the sudden contraction of a school at the other end of the pool. No alarm passes through all these animals. The pause merely travels faster than any one of them.

Nothing inhabits the ruins as ruins. The roofless chamber is valuable because its low walls provide rare vertical surfaces and because leaf dust collects behind them. Glasswing nymphs climb the walls to emerge, leaving their empty cases fixed just above the water. Silt lice work the leaf dust below. Fry using the chamber have two exits, the doorway and the gap through which the pool enters, and are therefore less easily cornered there than in a root pocket. The four steps form a sequence of depths: bubble beetles frequent the upper edges, snails graze the middle treads, shrimp work the lower joints, and burrowers occupy the buried end.

The black pins are almost the only surfaces that offer nothing. A glasswing may rest on one; rain treaders may congregate in a condensation field nearby. Otherwise the animals treat them with complete indifference. There is no avoidance, no congregation except where food or moisture provides a reason, and no attendant creature that cleans or guards them.

In drought, the veilfish and shrimp contract into the remaining threads and root pools. Snails follow the water downward. Burrowers close their chambers with silt and mucus, hushfrogs disappear into the roots, and the silt-hunters settle into long stillness. The standing rain does not rescue these larger lives drop by drop. Its direct beneficiaries are mostly minute. Its wider importance lies in the fact that the contraction stops.

After prolonged rain, the process runs outward again. Pools join, and young fish, shrimp, and silt-hunters use the temporary passages to disperse. Seeds are carried onto open mud; some sprout before the water settles. Most are then grazed, undermined, or drowned beyond the old hummocks. The animals do not require one unchanging water level. They require the variation to remain bounded.

None of this constitutes knowledge of the mechanism beneath the basin. It is inherited expectation. A hushfrog’s egg cup remains viable because the groves do not dry quite beyond a certain point. A stillwader’s nest survives because high water stops below the old root forks. A rain treader’s egg waits for a chill that ordinary flooding cannot provide. For different creatures, the standing rain is a breeding condition, an occasional feast, a slight stippling of the surface, or no perceptible event at all. It has become a season that need not agree with the sky.

A drop falls. The rain treaders flash outward on its ring and begin to return. A stillwader swallows once without lifting its feet. On an upright black pin, a fern-gilled snail reaches the last bare finger’s width, finds no film to graze, and begins the long descent toward food.
