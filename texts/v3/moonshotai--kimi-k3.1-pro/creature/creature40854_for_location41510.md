What lives there is best approached by subtraction. Nothing crosses a street to feed. Nothing shelters beneath a covered walk. No young are left in cisterns, and nothing returns to a familiar court. The city’s inhabitants do not occupy its rooms or travel its distances. They occupy much smaller intervals: a difference in temperature across a wet film, a chemical boundary between seepage and rain, the brief persistence of water against an edge.

A wall evenly soaked may be barren, while a narrow seam within it supports life. The surface of a pool may offer nothing, though the water entering it from below carries a usable reaction. The geography of the residents is therefore not the geography of courts and quarters. It consists of warm against cool, clinging against scour, retention against release. Most of the basin provides no such difference for long enough to matter.

The pale felts are the most persistent growths. What appears to be a single patch is mostly accumulation: old filaments, empty sheaths, silica deposited around both. The living portion usually lies close to the stone, where capillary water remains after the visible seep has failed. It feeds on slight chemical inequalities between the buried water and the water reaching it from above. One supplies what the other can accept. The reaction yields little, and the felt uses little.

During the long seep, a patch becomes increasingly composed of its own remains. Its outer layers whiten and flake. Beneath them, a small amount of tissue stays viable in a warmth that never permits complete rest. Dormancy is not free there. Each surviving patch spends its stored chemistry almost imperceptibly, and many are exhausted before the next useful rain. Along a seep line, living felts, dead felts, and mere mineral tracings may be indistinguishable until water returns.

The fans require a rarer conjunction. Two waters must meet without one immediately sweeping the other away. The flow must be sufficient to renew the reaction but not strong enough to strip tissue from the stone. This is why they stand beside certain overflow stones and not beside others that appear identical. The shape of the ground matters less than the thinness and constancy of the current.

A closed fan resembles a flake of translucent wax caught in a crevice. Much of its rigidity comes from mineral laid down inside it. When it opens, the living layers do not reach toward the rain; they simply absorb water and separate along old folds. The fan’s two faces come into contact with slightly different currents. Its clarity increases as the spaces between those layers fill. None of them turns green, and none responds to the brief gray visibility.

The clean, sweet odor released at opening has no recipient. It is not a signal or an attraction. It comes from the dissolution of the seals between folded layers, a byproduct escaping into air that carries it only a little way. By the time the odor is noticeable, most of the fan’s opening has already occurred.

At their fullest, the fans release threads. Each thread is mostly sheath, with living material held at widely separated points along its length. It cannot swim, choose, or attach at will. Many threads are dead before they leave the fan. Others remain viable only while the water maintains a particular chemistry. Their apparent delicacy is misleading: the mineral sheath lets some endure transport that would destroy unprotected tissue.

Their routes give the basin’s life its strangest geography.

A thread released beside one overflow stone may appear later in a pool with no surface connection to it. Another may pass through a court, ascend a stair in a thin film of runoff, and enter a district that lies an hour away across dry ground. The thread experiences none of this as distance. It remains in current, or it does not.

Consequently, the kinship of the fans does not follow the dry map. Two growths separated by a low wall may be profoundly unlike, their lineages having received different water for ages. Two fans in remote precincts may be nearly identical, founded again and again by threads traveling the same recurrent connection. A genealogy of them would resemble the missing map of the wet city: first this overflow, then that cistern, then the buried court, then the stair where water climbs. Their heredity preserves adjacencies that the pavements do not.

When a passage narrows and a connection finally fails, the fans at either end do not immediately change. They may continue opening for many cycles. Only their threads reveal the isolation, returning to the same stone or accumulating in pools where nothing can receive them. A lineage can thus outlive its future without any visible alteration in its present.

In a few deep cisterns, there is also life without a surface form. Threads that neither attach nor dissolve have entered the upper sediment and persisted there as colorless nets. These nets do not open with rain. They extend through pore water, drawing on the difference between warm water below and the cistern water above. Their growth is so slight that several cycles may pass without any measurable change.

They are the closest thing the basin has to old residents. Yet none is an ancient body in the ordinary sense. Living material fails locally, is replaced, becomes mineralized at one edge while continuing at another. Their age lies in continuity rather than in substance. Nor do they form a hidden foundation beneath the city. Many cisterns contain none. Between them are great volumes of sediment in which nothing metabolizes at all.

During the fullest rain, water briefly removes some of the isolation among these scattered growths. Water that has passed a felt in one district may alter the chemistry surrounding a fan in another. A pulse of dissolved material may reach a cistern before the rain directly above it has changed its level. Threads from separate precincts may arrive in the same temporary pool.

This is not communication, and the wet city does not become an organism. Nothing responds in order to affect anything else. A fan does not announce its opening; a cistern net does not receive news. The changes are simply consequences of shared water. Still, for a short interval, life that has been confined to separate seams acquires a kind of discontinuous circulation. Its activity extends across districts without becoming concentrated anywhere.

The gray visibility, when it comes, adds nothing to this. No growth turns toward it. No pigment has developed to gather it, and it is too brief and weak to provide useful energy. If a witness could see, the residents would hardly alter the appearance of emptiness: a clear fan against dark stone, a pale seam below a weeping wall, perhaps a faint milky thread in water that had seemed untouched. The city would not look inhabited. It would look faintly marked.

Outflow separates the growths according to the same schedule that joined them. Some fans close while water still stands around them, because the useful current has ceased. Others remain open on stone that appears dry, sustained for a while by seepage arriving from a district already withdrawing. Felts contract at different times along the same visible seam. The local appearance of wetness is not a reliable account of their condition.

Threads suspended in pools that cease to occupy their depth may later be found on the paving as pale, nearly weightless strokes. They do not always mark a former waterline. Some lie above any level the remaining pool could have held, set down as the water became absent around them. The next rain may lift them again, though by then most are only mineral filaments.

Almost nothing scavenges the dead. There is no rapid succession of consumers, no rich decay in the sediment. Heat, dissolution, and burial perform nearly everything. A felt dies by exhaustion or entombment. A fan dies when its overflow stone ceases to provide the necessary meeting of waters. A net may persist until sediment closes its last pores. The city inherits their remains before any other life can use them.

Those remains occasionally matter to the stone. A lattice holds a film of water slightly longer than a bare surface would. Deposition thickens one side of a rib. A dead fan leaves a fluted weakness along which later seepage travels. Such effects are real, but small and local. The residents did not build the city, and they do not maintain it. If every living patch vanished, the courts would continue to accumulate and dissolve, the cisterns would still fill, and the wet watershed would still remember its order.

Even taken together, the growths do not constitute a hidden abundance. A precinct may contain one viable seam. A chain of courts may contain none. There is no crowded darkness behind the walls, no second population awaiting sufficiently close inspection. Life shares the city’s proportions: considerable extent, very little gathering, and long intervals in which continuation is difficult to distinguish from residue.

Nothing there knows the city’s extent. Nothing requires its stairs. A fan does not inhabit a court; it inhabits a condition that occurs beside one stone in that court. That the stone is a curb, or part of a bridge, or older than the terrace beneath it, makes no difference to the fan. The city’s scale belongs to an observer it has never produced.

And yet life is not entirely separable from the place. Its dead lattices enter the walls. Its dispersal follows connections no dry map can show. Its lineages divide when passages close and spread again when water establishes a route elsewhere. Without perceiving the watershed, the residents have come to bear its history in where they are and where they are not.

The rain keeps the city. It does not keep the life within it safe; most beginnings fail, and no connection is permanent. But by returning, and by restoring the ancient routes in their recurrent order, the rain leaves a few surviving reactions somewhere to resume. That is all the population the basin has: not a hidden citizenry, not a mind in the stone, but a sparse persistence that becomes briefly connected whenever the city does.
