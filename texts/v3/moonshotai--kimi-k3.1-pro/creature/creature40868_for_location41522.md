Most of the basin’s inhabitants are overlooked three times: once for their size, once for their stillness, and finally because much of every population is not, at any given moment, active. It exists as hard eggs, folded young, stacked wings, and dormant embryos in root thatch. The moving animals are merely the portion of life currently being spent.

The nine trees are shelters before they are food. In clear sunlight their outer leaves open in tiers, and the animals spread upward and outward with them. When dust thickens or the light fails, the foliage closes in stages. A crown does not become lifeless then; it becomes crowded. Its inhabitants retreat toward the trunk, where conductive fluid keeps narrow seams of bark a little warmer than the air. Some inner thickets are so packed with bodies that the tree seems to possess a second, slow pulse.

The wirebacks make the trees’ waste into usable terrain. At rest, their stiff fur gives each one the outline of a small seed. In motion, the seed separates into six hooked feet and two combing mouthparts. They cut fallen leaf scales into flakes and drag them along routes ending among the upper roots. Their droppings—blue, green, or rust-bright beads, according to the foliage they have eaten—collect along these runs. New roots grow eagerly into the bead-rich zones. A wireback lineage thus leaves its tree a map of former feeding ground, written in a material the roots can consume.

Wirebacks have no useful sense of airborne sound. They press the underside of the body against bark or stable root and receive a narrow band of tremor through it. When that private vibration alters, they do not flee across the floor. They clamp down, draw their fur into a dense conductive cage, and wait. This saves many from ordinary concussions and none from a revision that takes the bark itself. Their continuing insurance lies elsewhere: inactive broods cached separately in the root thickets, sometimes so far from the feeding routes that the adults never revisit them.

The bark seams belong to the seamsters. They are so flat that their numerous legs appear as two staggered fringes, and a large one can insert itself into a crack no wider than a leaf edge. They graze the mineral crust that forms where conductive fluid reaches the bark surface. Left alone, that crust would slowly insulate and choke the warm seams. The seamsters keep it open, carrying the removed material inward and depositing it in hard little tiles around their brood chambers. A mature colony turns a split in the trunk into an inhabited corridor, roofed with old skins and warmed from both sides.

They are also the first animals to disappear when a crown is viewed casually. Their bodies take on the color of the bark beneath them, and they can remain motionless through an entire daylight period. At night, however, a seam may seem to flow. Thousands of legs move in sequence while the colony shifts its brood away from a cooling section of trunk.

The smokewings spend still more of their lives concealed. Inside a hollow twig they lie in dense stacks, body against body, each rigid wing fitted into the narrow space left by its neighbors. Their tissues become almost electrically quiet. They can remain in that state through the death of the branch around them, provided some part of it stays enclosed.

Flowering wakes them. The stimulus is partly the bitter oil released by the flowers and partly a change in the twig’s charge. A crown may bloom twice in quick succession and then not again for decades; the twigs retain smokewings for both possibilities. On emergence, their wings do not beat in the ordinary way. They snap between two fixed angles, carrying the animals in brief, hard glides from leaf to leaf. Pollen adheres to them electrostatically. Their flight is therefore a dark, crowded event inside the foliage rather than a cloud above it. From the open floor, the tree may appear to shudder and nothing more.

The adults live only long enough to feed, pollinate, and place eggs in several separate hollows. Then their wing cases dry, split, and add another kind of flake to the crown’s litter. A revision that isolates a flowering tree does not necessarily interrupt this cycle. Sometimes the altered electrical conditions simply wake a second reserve of smokewings, and the crown flowers in a storm of wings after the visible flowers have already begun to close.

The side-flashes are the nearest thing the basin has to a people.

Individually, they are long, cautious climbers with six grasping limbs and a tail that can lock around a leaf stem. Their colored flank patches are shutters rather than lights: plates lift, turn, or fold away, exposing mineral colors that remain visible in plain sunlight. They hunt seamsters and torpid smokewings, take oil from hard seed cases, and spend much of their time moving along narrow routes kept open through the foliage. At an alarm, every patch closes. A colony that was flickering with messages a moment before becomes a collection of dark shapes pressed against the trunk.

An individual side-flash is not obviously thoughtful. A colony is harder to dismiss.

Each colony maintains an identifying sequence of flashes. Young animals learn it before they learn the full feeding circuits. After a major revision, the survivors gather on stable bark and perform the sequence from beginning to end. Members answer with short signatures. The colony then repeats the signatures that received no answer, sometimes for several days, while nurses open reserve nurseries and newly active young are brought into the foliage. The practice may be only a method of accounting for survivors and redistributing work. It may also be the simplest possible form of history.

Their signals refer more often to relationships than to places. There are signs for inward, reserve, hold, stranger, and all-answer. Signs attached to particular landmarks are less durable, for obvious reasons. Different crowns possess different versions of the colony sequences, and migrants occasionally introduce fragments from elsewhere. The side-flashes also leave a temporary architecture: bright leaf flakes wedged at forks in their routes, or smokewing wings positioned so that a flank display can be seen around a curtain of foliage. Leaf-fall erases these arrangements. Nothing about them could survive long enough to become a ruin.

Their reserve young explain the apparently spontaneous return of colonies. Dormant animals lie folded into narrow, jointed cases in the root thickets. Nurses wake them with fluid taken from active root nodules, after which they unfold limb by limb over several hours. A tree that has stood silent can therefore appear to generate a colony. It has not done so. The colony was present, but not yet spending itself.

The mantles are the only inhabitants that routinely turn the space between crowns into a road.

Folded against an upper trunk, a mantle resembles an old sheath of bark. Its broad body is drawn between elongated limbs, and its fur lies in thick, irregular layers. That fur is both insulation and a pressure organ. Every hollow filament registers a slightly different component of the air moving across it. A mantle can wait through years of dust and concussion, feeding sparingly on seeds and dormant animals, until some combination of pressure and ground tremor brings it to the crown’s edge.

It does not fly by beating the thin air. It falls, adjusts the curvature of its body, and allows a concussion front to discover it. The furred surface opens by degrees. Limbs that were folded close to the body become spars; the whole animal changes from a falling weight into a broad, responsive surface. Its steering is mostly a matter of refusing or accepting pressure.

A landing is not gentle. Foliage snaps inward, side-flash colonies go dark, and wirebacks pour toward the trunk. Then the mantle closes itself among the leaves and begins to feed.

Its arrival matters for more than what it eats. Mantle fur carries seed husks, smokewing eggs, seamster brood plates, and the dormant young of wirebacks. Some passengers lodge accidentally. Others occupy protected folds where dust and charge cannot easily reach them. Occasionally an active side-flash clings beneath a forelimb as well. Thus a single dark shape crossing the basin may be the first mixing of two crown populations in generations. Seen from the rim, it is a loosened leaf. Seen from within the destination tree, it is an immigration.

Even the death of a crownwood does not necessarily end its animal estate. The foliage becomes dry plates and powder, which the wirebacks continue to process. Side-flashes withdraw toward the roots. Smokewings remain in whatever hollows survive. Dark living films around the deepest roots persist through the tree’s death and colonize its replacement. When new leaves finally open, animals are already waiting to occupy them. A crown is therefore not quite synonymous with an individual tree. It is a persistent association that a tree may join, support, and eventually leave.

No native behavior suggests reliable foreknowledge of the floor’s revisions. There is no dependable omen, no evacuation to safer ground, no wisdom that makes the noise intelligible. The response comes afterward: close, clamp, count, open another nursery, resume. The governing adaptation is not foresight but multiplication.

Nothing now living shows a special relationship to the gray cell, the eleven steps, or the black needles. Side-flashes do not direct their long displays toward them. Mantles do not preferentially shelter in the cell, and no present animal’s resting posture explains the depression in its bench. The basin’s inhabitants are not visibly custodians of its machinery. They do not repair it, worship it, or avoid it. They use the conditions it produces, much as they use sunlight and chemical gradients.

None of them burrows into the open floor. A burrow there would not be shelter but a wager on one particular piece of ground. Their nests are instead rooted deeply, carried inward, distributed among several refuges, or capable of traveling inside another animal’s fur.

This gives the basin its peculiar abundance. A silent crown may contain thousands of wings in its twigs and several generations in its roots. A mantle folded against a trunk may be a migration before it has moved. Even a colony’s identity is dispersed among signatures, nurses, migrants, and dormant young. Individuals are fragile; whole local populations vanish. Life persists because it is never entirely gathered in one place, or entirely awake.

The floor revises geography. The inhabitants answer by revising how much of themselves is present.
