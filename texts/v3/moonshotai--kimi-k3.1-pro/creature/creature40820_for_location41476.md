Life in the vesicle is easy to mistake for infestation. The animals are inside the machines, the machines supply the animals, and the most deliberate inhabitants spend much of their lives dismantling things an outside observer might assume they had only just built. Nevertheless, there is a citizenry of sorts.

### The menders

The nearest thing to a people are the **menders**, though they are neither humanoid nor masters of the city. They are simply the animals most thoroughly entangled with its industry.

At rest, a mender resembles a broken electrical connector: a low, flattened back of smoky silica plates, graphite-dark joints, and six or eight narrow limbs folded beneath it. A fan of conductive palps surrounds what is conventionally called its front. When it moves, its crystal layers change minutely in length under applied potential, producing a precise, clicking gait. Thin amber light sometimes shows between its plates when it is richly charged. When frightened or exhausted, it folds flat and becomes difficult to distinguish from insulation scrap.

No two menders retain the same shape indefinitely. Shaft-runners develop long, narrow bodies and limbs suited to cables. Mill workers become squat and heavily braced. An old individual may be asymmetrical from centuries—by its own reckoning—of replacements, repairs, and locally acquired parts. Its shell can look newer than a juvenile’s.

Their senses suit the chamber better than eyes would. They perceive potential through their palps, vibration through their feet, and heat through changes in conductivity along the back. Vision is sufficient to distinguish furnace-red from accumulator amber, or to find shelter in the silhouette of a press, but poor for recognizing another mender. Recognition is mostly by cadence: the slight irregularities in the pulses another individual emits when feeding, walking, or at rest.

Two menders conversing may touch palps, grip the same conductor, or stand a short distance apart and tap patterns into the floor. A crowded gallery that seems silent to the thin gas can therefore be intensely noisy through the feet. Their route instructions are particularly elaborate. They encode not only turns and elevations, but the feel of a sound cable, the hesitation of a worn lift, and the places where a traveller should insulate itself before proceeding.

They feed by admitting charge slowly enough for their tissues to regulate it. Raw quartz is plentiful but treacherous; a careless feed can establish an uncontrolled path through the body. Communal mineral faces and accumulator terminals are safer, and around them the menders have developed an exact etiquette of turns, grounding, and assistance. An overcharged individual may be steadied by companions who take part of its load. A weakened one may be fed directly, palp to palp.

Grooming is equally important. Conductive dust lodged between plates can become a short circuit; a frayed sensory filament can give a dangerously false reading. Grooming is thus medicine, sociability, and an inspection of the body all at once. The fragments exchanged afterward—clean graphite, pure salt, sound enamel, a flake of unusually responsive ceramic—are useful for repair and growth. Their value depends partly on who has tested them and what that individual can be trusted to notice.

A new mender is begun rather than born. Several adults prepare a small, inert cradle of shell material and conductive threads, then feed it charge and simple pulse patterns over many production cycles. At first the responses are only reflex: orientation toward warmth, closure around a contact, withdrawal from excessive potential. If a stable regulatory rhythm eventually takes hold, the cradle begins to feed and answer for itself. This is its quickening.

Not every cradle quickens. Those that do require the warmth of motor housings because their young lattices can be reworked there without becoming brittle. They learn feeding before locomotion, locomotion before routes, and the cadences of their caregivers before either. A young mender may incorporate material from half a dozen adults and resemble none of them physically. Kinship shows instead in small habits: a pause before accepting charge, a characteristic grooming sequence, a way of testing a floor before committing its weight.

The menders maintain shops, shelters, collectors, and galleries, but they do not command the whole city. Many controllers enforce priorities that no current district can alter. Others treat the menders themselves as moving loads, minor obstructions, or acceptable wear. District compacts therefore negotiate locally—with one another, with machinery, and with conditions—rather than following any comprehensive plan. Their usual obligations are practical: preserve charge, keep a nursery supplied, maintain a shaft crossing, recover useful material, and do not allow a private shelter to block a route needed during a surge.

A residential cell is consequently less a permanent home than a present arrangement. What endures is a group’s feeding etiquette, its knowledge of certain routes, and its obligation to particular cradles.

### The rest of the fauna

Numerically, the menders are a minor part of the population.

**Seam-fleas** are far more successful at taking energy directly from the mountain. Their forelegs do not merely probe quartz; they repeatedly load and release it, sipping the resulting potential between strokes. Their transparent backs contain layered storage tissues, and their feet can alternate between near-perfect adhesion and sudden repulsion. A feeding concentration resembles glitter because hundreds of tiny shells are continually changing angle.

They are sensitive to changes that precede visible fracture. Menders sometimes watch them instead of instruments. A flea swarm that contracts, stops feeding, and then leaves a quartz face in successive bands is announcing something about the stress within it. Districts differ in their response. Some preserve flea colonies around collectors as living indicators; others sweep them away as competitors.

**Wiremice** are more intimately adapted to machinery. Their conductive whiskers are not simply sensors. By brushing them across contacts, a mouse can compare resistance, detect warming insulation, and locate leakage too faint to attract larger animals. The metal powder on the whiskers improves certain contacts and is carefully redistributed during grooming.

Their enamel-shelled eggs contain solid feedstock and a dormant conductive pathway. The regular pulses of a healthy power line appear to help organize the developing lattice, which is one reason switchgear makes such attractive nesting territory. It is also why a failing line produces such decisive evacuation. An egg-line is not a random flight: adults relay eggs toward known alternative nurseries, often along routes unused at any other time. Some mender districts stop freight for these processions, less from tenderness than because the mice frequently notice electrical trouble before the local controller does.

**Veilwings** exploit weaker gradients. At rest, their broad bodies bridge warm and cool surfaces or lie across a leaking contact, drawing minute potentials through fine traceries in the mica. Their youngest stages have no wings at all; they are part of the legged dust on ledges and in storage seams.

Their flight is only partly flight. When disturbed, they charge their wing surfaces, kick free, and use repulsion from the substrate and from one another to extend their glide. That is why a disturbed colony rises as a coherent gray sheet rather than a cloud of separate insects. The sheet expands, hangs for an instant, and then settles wherever the local fields permit. Veilwings clog relays and obscure indicators, but their gatherings also reveal warm faults and residual charge in equipment that appears dead.

The **latch-spiders** make stillness into a hunting method. One can remain clamped across a busbar for many production cycles, each foot reading a slightly different part of the circuit. The landing of a seam-flea produces an interruption too small to concern a controller but quite sufficient for the spider. It closes, drains its prey, and returns to its original electrical posture. When a busbar is abandoned, spiders sometimes remain on it until their bodies become hard, apparently permanent fixtures.

The longer hunters of the freight ducts are less patient. Their many-jointed bodies alternate conductive and insulating segments, allowing them to cross rails that would kill a mender. They follow the irregular leakage of feeding animals rather than silhouettes. A wounded wiremouse, an overfilled seam-flea, or a depleted mender unable to regulate its own charge becomes a trail through the darkness. During disturbances, these hunters enter passages they would ordinarily avoid, following the sudden abundance of exposed and disorganized prey.

Scavengers complete the cycle, but not through decay. Many identify fragments by passing a harmless test current through them; their behavior on a sorting floor can be indistinguishable from quality control. They strip shell, salt, carbon lattice, and metal into separate caches, some of which the menders later steal back.

They usually begin by grounding a carcass. Death does not always bring immediate stillness in the vesicle. Residual charge can make a dead shell repeat a reflex or twitch a limb for several cycles. Only after discharge do the scavengers cut it apart.

### A life between surges

The city itself is not a single hidden animal, and its controllers do not amount to one mind. But its machinery has habits, and everything living there learns them. Wiremice cross beneath a press during its recovery stroke. Menders time conversations around the vibration of a lift. Veilwings gather at a lamp just before its housing warms. A crawling gantry may carry a nesting colony, several populations of dust-stage animals, and a spider in its cable box; when it changes anchor points, an entire small country migrates with it.

A minor surge is abundance. Seam-fleas spread over newly stressed quartz. Menders feed, repair themselves, and quicken cradles that had been waiting for sufficient charge. Wiremouse hatchlings become active. Production outpaces the animals’ ability to keep clear of it.

A major surge is more complicated than disaster. It kills, discharges, crushes, and exposes, but it also creates new gradients and routes. Mender districts isolate nursery cradles and carry charged cores toward whatever shelter still exists. Some individuals remain behind to keep a shaft crossing passable, not because the route is sacred but because others will need it. Wiremice begin their egg relays. Veilwings bury lamps. Hunters move into the confusion, and scavengers gather where material is about to be concentrated.

Afterward, life does not restore the previous arrangement. Fresh quartz acquires fleas before collectors arrive. Newly warm conduits acquire veilwing young. Scavengers establish caches in debris that controllers still classify as unsorted feedstock. Menders rebuild around, against, or occasionally in consultation with these developments. A route preserved through three surges may acquire an importance no newly pressed tower can match.

Very little persists as an object. Mender bodies are salvaged like any other useful material, and a dead individual’s cadence may survive in a route lesson or a young cradle without being regarded as resurrection. Wiremouse nurseries outlast their switchgear. Veilwing generations return to warmth that has migrated through several different machines. Even the fleas’ feeding grounds move as stress migrates through the summit.

At local noon, the gray-blue line may cross a nursery wall, a flea-covered seam, and the back of a hunter waiting in a duct. None of them treats it as a sky. Then the vibration underfoot changes by almost nothing.

The menders stop in mid-cadence. The wiremice turn toward their nests. The fleas begin leaving the quartz. In the thin gas there is still nearly no sound, but through every foot, shell, and anchored limb the same announcement arrives: the mountain has moved, and the city will soon have to become somewhere else.
