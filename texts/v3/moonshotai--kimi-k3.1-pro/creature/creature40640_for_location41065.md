Nothing in the Summit Nursery is native in the old sense. There has not been time for that. Everything alive arrived as seed, spore, cutting, egg, or unnoticed life in the prepared humus. Some individuals have since been born there, but belonging is still less a matter of ancestry than of use. Each machine makes a climate; each climate offers a few narrow opportunities. Life has begun to find them.

Most of the population is invisible as a population. Bacteria and microscopic algae occupy wet films on ceramic and metal. Fungal cells, pollen, and fine fragments of tissue circulate through the root mats. In the basin, transparent water fleas are the visible portion of this suspended world. They filter it, rise toward daylight, and drop among the black glass grains when a leaf dimples the surface. At their scale, the small rain is violent weather: each drop a shock, each shallow current something to resist. Between rains they gather in slack water behind stems and aggregate. Some are drawn toward the intakes when the pumps reverse. The basin is not a placid pool to them, but a place of tides, caves, and a periodically falling sky.

The pale snail inhabits another clock. Rain scarcely changes its conditions, because the cold side of a condenser core is always wet. It grazes the thin growth gathering there, rounding service bolts and following the seams where moisture lingers. Its trail is a narrow cleaning as much as a track; on an algae-filmed retaining block, a casting number may reappear one digit at a time behind it. It drinks from beads of condensation. The warm, mossy side of the same boulder is another country, only a hand’s breadth away and rarely crossed. For the snail, the boulder is not a machine pretending to be stone. It is a range of climates with a reliable spring on one face.

The white moth lives in the intervals. During rain it waits beneath the waxy creepers; afterward, when the foliage has finished its brief applause, it resumes a route among the flowers. Its slowness is possible in a garden without birds, but its importance is magnified by the absence of almost everything else. It may be the only animal carrying pollen from one terrace to another. Much of that work comes to nothing. Pollen reaches an incompatible blossom. Nectar is taken from a flower that will set no seed. Or no moth appears while a particular shrub is blooming, and its scent goes unanswered. When a flower that cannot pollinate itself does produce seed, the event may have depended on one white insect choosing to cross open air.

The plants and fungi are not background to these animals. They are the principal settlers, and their successes are uneven. Self-pollinators can increase without waiting for the moth. So can plants spreading by runners, roots, or stems that strike wherever they remain pressed against wet humus. Others flower abundantly and leave only fallen color. Fan-ferns turn before the mist; mosses take up water almost immediately; creepers treat irrigation cables as the trunks and branches they were never given. The young tree in the valve column has found the equivalent of a cliff ledge. Its roots are still an opportunity. Later they may become an obstruction. That distinction, rather than any preference for the original planting plan, will decide whether it remains.

The fungi govern many of the endings. A fallen blossom can lie intact for days while its structure is being quietly emptied of strength. Then, seemingly all at once, it collapses into wet lace. The same fungal felt threads through the humus, binds fragments around roots, and helps keep the thin living layer attached to its constructed ground. Without it, the Nursery would gradually suffocate beneath unspent flowers, dead roots, and its own preserved beginnings. With it, even failed reproduction becomes material for something else.

The machines are not alive, but nothing living encounters them as inert. The pumps are weather. The radiator combs bring a brief, localized cold. A pipe is a warm boundary, a housing is shelter, a condenser core is simultaneously cliff, spring, and grazing ground. The maintenance mechanisms are the nearest thing to catastrophe, though they do not hunt: a stem crosses into a moving joint and is simply removed. The crawler at the unfinished end produces empty habitat. Its new ribs are at first only pale mineral foam. Then the cups darken with rain, moisture persists in a few seams, and films begin where the eye has little reason to look. The ferns leaning across the gap look like pioneers, but the first settlement will already have happened beneath notice.

It would be easy to mistake the Nursery’s quiet for balance. It is not balanced, quite. There are too few animals, too many flowers going unused, roots advancing toward joints, and whole small habitats dependent on a pump continuing to reverse. A population might vanish through an intake; a plant might spread densely and leave no descendants; a useful root mat might become, by growing another finger’s width, an obstruction. This is not a finished miniature wilderness. It is an unfinished set of dependencies, some intended and others only just being discovered.

No resident experiences the whole forty-pace room. To the water flea, it is a basin. To the snail, one cold face of metal. To the moth, a sequence of flowers and shelters. To the fungal felt, it is a constellation of dead petals, root surfaces, and damp humus. Their territories overlap without yet forming a complete community.

At the escaped rill, those separate lives are beginning to have a common consequence. Water sorts the white grains into a crescent. A surface film can make them slightly harder to move. Fungal threads cross spaces roots have not reached; moss can hold moisture after the surrounding material has drained. A root follows, then another. If a bank forms, and later a hollow beneath it, no single inhabitant will have built either. They will result from many small acts: filtering, grazing, binding, rooting, surviving the next rain.

If anything can already be called native to the Summit Nursery, it may be that relationship rather than any species. None of its participants originated on the tower. The arrangement did.

The machines continue to repeat the afternoon. Life, almost imperceptibly, has begun to make history.
