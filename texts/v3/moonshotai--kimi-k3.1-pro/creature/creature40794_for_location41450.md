Nothing in Sere is merely a resident. Nearly everything is also part of the residence.

The oldest citizens are the vault-stem colonies. One colony may be green and budding at its crown, ivory within its shaded chambers, and stone among its oldest roots without ceasing to be a single continuous life. It does not build an arch and then withdraw into it. The arch is the colony’s own body, thickened where its stems have leaned together and fused. Its living tissues creep over mineralized ancestors; its hollow dead become towers, stairs, and flues. Even its refuse remains useful. Fallen sheaths soften into flooring, old walls support younger growth, and abandoned chambers fill slowly with clean, odorless meal.

Each colony knows its own chemistry and accepts it. Stems of the same lineage touch, join, and reinforce one another. Between unrelated colonies, growth stops just short of contact. The resulting seam is seldom planned to any width, yet it is often exactly wide enough for a browser. Where several such refusals meet, a court opens. Sere’s streets are therefore not passages through the vegetation so much as agreements the vegetation has failed to make, kept open by appetite.

The plants provide almost everything the other citizens require, though never in one simple form. Their leaves hold pale oil and gas; their flowers offer dry pollen and golden beads of food; their young shoots feed the browsers; their roots sustain a finer population in the stone. Beneath the visible green, root filaments and microscopic partners occupy clear films within mineral pores. Together they etch glass and sulfur stone, releasing what the colony can use and leaving the cliffs inscribed with white traceries. This partnership determines the border of Sere more exactly than any wall. Beyond the last root, the chemistry fails. The mountain resumes being bare.

The veilwings are the first animals likely to catch the eye, though they rarely hold it. Their wings are broad and dry, colored like pale horn, old ivory, bottle glass, or tarnished bronze. The fringes along their edges divide the air so finely that even a close passage makes no sound. In the warm courts they seem not so much to fly as to be repeatedly mislaid by gravity: rising a little, sliding beneath a vault, catching another slow current, and disappearing into shade.

They feed at the waxy flowers, taking oil and carrying pollen in the fringes of their wings and along their narrow bodies. A veilwing may remain faithful to one flowering crown for days, then cross a lane and visit another. In this way they carry messages between colonies whose stems will never touch. At rest they hang high in hollow towers or beneath old bridges, wings closed. Hundreds together can resemble nothing more animate than a roof layered with dead sheaths. Their courtship is scarcely more conspicuous: two animals hanging near one another while dim bands of color travel, over hours, across their wings.

The browsers provide Sere with its visible crowds. They stand about hare-high, though their long, low bodies and six soft feet make the comparison useful only at a distance. Their hides are usually stone gray, dusty green, or warm brown, but color gathers along their flanks when they meet, follow, or give way to one another. A encounter at a narrow threshold may consist entirely of two animals touching shoulders, waiting, and slowly changing shade until one steps aside.

They crop shoots with padded jaws and inherit their routes more surely than they inherit any fixed territory. Young browsers follow older ones through the courts, learning the scent of junctions, the feel of nacre underfoot, and the difference between a passage that opens into another district and one that ends in a chamber gradually closing. Scent left at a turn may outlive the animal that left it. A well-used route can thus persist through generations, even as individual memories vanish.

Their feeding is the other half of Sere’s street-making. The plants’ chemical refusals open the seams; the browsers prevent those seams from healing shut. Shoots along a lane are taken again and again, while growth farther inside the colony is left to thicken into wall and roof. In some places the stems produce especially tender growth at the cropped margin, as though the colony had learned to offer its appetite a acceptable direction. Neither plant nor animal intends the arrangement. Nevertheless, a lane may remain open for centuries because both continue to benefit from it.

Where lanes meet, the browsers gather without quite forming herds. They feed, touch, change color, and stand warm flank to warm flank. Nothing is bought or displayed in these market-like courts. Pollen, scent, and small fragments of leaf are exchanged without intention. At night many of the animals withdraw into hollow stairs or low chambers where the mineral walls retain the day’s heat. Their oils thicken, their colors dim, and a crowded court can become apparently empty without anything having hurried away.

Wherever an interior wall is exceptionally smooth, masonbacks have lived upon it. Each is no larger than a grain of rice, with a small wedge-shaped back colored to the local stone. They graze films of growth and fine mineral dust, and as they move they leave behind exceedingly thin scales of secretion. One animal’s work is almost nothing. A colony’s work, continued through thousands of generations, becomes nacre.

Masonbacks have no queen and no common design. They respond to roughness, chemical traces, and the paths of their neighbors. A new chamber is gritty underfoot; an old one takes the daylight evenly and feels almost soft despite being mineral. Scratches disappear not because anyone repairs them, but because disturbed surfaces attract fresh grazing and fresh deposition. The finest floors are also cemeteries. Empty masonback shells and the dust of their backs remain within the layers, so that a browser crossing an ancient court walks on a polished accumulation of minute lives.

The suede hunters inhabit another kind of attention. They lie beneath leaf bases, along the dark sides of steps, or where a roof meets a wall, their skins taking on the color and dry softness of their surroundings. Some remain in one place for days. Their stillness is not sleep but a way of allowing the rest of Sere to become careless around them.

Usually the eye betrays them first. It changes from ivory to bronze, or from bronze to near black, as some small movement crosses its field of view. A veilwing descends too close; a young roof animal follows the wrong scent. The hunter’s intervention is brief, and the silence closes immediately afterward. Then there is only the leaf base again, or the wall.

They do not appear to defend areas so much as particular opportunities: a shadow that forms at noon, a fold where veilwings settle at night, a warm current along which small animals drift. Even their courtship can occur at a near standstill, two hunters resting within sight of one another while each slowly repeats and alters the colors passing over the other’s skin. For most of Sere’s inhabitants, a hunter is not a pursuing danger. It is a possibility that has been part of the scenery all along.

The pale scavengers perform the town’s longest labor. Some are visible as fine jointed threads in the litter; others are so small that their presence is known mainly by the changing edges of dead tissue. A fallen frond does not blacken or collapse. It becomes thinner, then papery, then delicately perforated. Its fibers separate. Its oils are taken up by one population, its mineral content by another, until what seemed a single object has been distributed through the floors, roots, and walls.

A dead browser is treated with the same patience. For a while its body remains an unmistakable animal in the lane. Later it is a dry form beneath the sheaths. Eventually it has ceased to be gathered in one place. Nothing in Sere calls this cleaning, and nothing calls it burial. It is simply the final stage of being useful.

The census of Sere depends on how long one watches. A single noon suggests a town of veilwings and browsers, with flowers open and pollen suspended in the warm air. A night suggests folded plants and empty courts. A season reveals the scavengers’ work. Longer observation makes the masonback colonies visible as a slow resurfacing of the world, and longer still brings the vault-stems to the foreground. An arch advancing by less than a hair may matter more to the future town than all the animal journeys made beneath it.

No inhabitant knows the whole. A veilwing knows warm columns of air and the flowering roofs. A browser knows inherited turns, edible margins, and the faint give of old nacre. A masonback knows the chemistry of a few grains. A hunter knows one dependable shadow. A vault-stem knows light, pressure, its own tissues, and the refusal of its neighbor. Yet these partial lives fit together without administration. The lanes remain open, the courts remain sweet with pollen and oil, the walls thicken, and almost nothing leaves the system.

That may be what citizenship means in Sere: not understanding the settlement, but altering it in a way that other lives can use.

From a little distance, the animals seem to live inside a town made by plants. Closer, plants and animals appear to live inside one another’s leavings. At the longest view, the distinction weakens further. The green growth is skin and bone; the nacre is continual healing; the browsers and veilwings are brief movements through streets older than their lineages; the scavengers ensure that even death remains within the anatomy.

The arches are not quite houses. They are the part of the population that has slowed down enough to become a place.
