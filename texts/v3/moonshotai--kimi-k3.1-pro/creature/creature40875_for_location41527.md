## Life at the Warm Margins

Most of the caldera’s inhabitants are specialists in margins. Scalding water, deep forest, open lake, and exposed ridge each support life, but the greatest concentrations occur where one condition leaks into another: hot water into fresh, mineral crust into soil, forest into marsh. A species may be abundant there and still occupy almost none of the basin.

The inhabited part of a hot spring often begins some distance from its source. The first gray-green films are sparse and tough. Farther along the overflow, where the water has spread thin and cooled, they thicken into rust-orange and emerald layers. These are not simply stains on the rock. They trap sediment, bind mineral particles, and provide food for the smallest grazing animals. Their dead sheaths and sticky surfaces also retain a little silica, so that life contributes, incrementally, to the terraces on which it lives.

In the cooler outflows, midge larvae graze the microbial mats. Some build loose cases from sinter grains and become almost indistinguishable from the bottom. Translucent flatworms move across the films, and tiny water beetles hunt around the edges. Where a fresh stream crosses the flow, snails appear, scraping the warm stones clean in irregular trails. If the spring migrates, this whole assemblage contracts, dries, or moves with it. An abandoned channel may retain its colors for a season, but the orange becomes dull and the emerald cracks into curls. Fern spores and grass seeds are already waiting in its hollows.

Coppergrass is usually among the first higher plants on such ground. At first it grows not in meadows but in lines, each tussock marking a seam where roots can remain cool and sufficiently free of minerals. Its waxy blades lose little water even on exposed, shimmering flats. Terrace ferns occupy older sinter, particularly behind deposited lips where windblown soil collects. Their roots bind the dust; their fallen fronds add a little organic matter; moss establishes in their shade. A patch of ground that appears barren from a distance may therefore contain the beginning of soil at several separate stages.

The forest is a much larger version of the same layering. The broad-crowned trees are not exceptionally tall, but each trunk supports several distinct habitats. Moss occupies the permanently wet base. Ferns root in branch forks. Orchids and other small epiphytes cling higher up, where there is more light but less reliable water. Beetles bore into softened wood, mites and springtails work through the moss, and tiny frogs hunt among leaves that never touch the ground.

Tree-fern crowns and the cavities beneath old roots hold small reserves of rainwater. These contain midge larvae, aquatic mites, and the tadpoles of seep frogs. Such pools may be no larger than a cup, yet they are valuable because they are fresh, fishless, and usually cooler than the ground below. A tadpole developing in one may spend its entire aquatic life above the forest floor.

There is no single, general emergence of insects. Rain, shade, and local water temperature produce separate pulses from basin to basin. One afternoon, a column of midges may rise over a pool while the next marsh remains almost clear of flying life. Dragonflies gather quickly at these places. The larger ones patrol fixed circuits along the shore, taking insects just above the water; smaller species hunt among reeds and fern stems. Their nymphs live a more concealed existence in quiet sediment, preying on larvae, tadpoles, and one another.

The small silver fish of the lake—the silverlings—are equally sensitive to local conditions. They gather where hot inflow has been diluted but not entirely lost, feeding on drifting larvae and insects washed from the margins. A school seldom remains in exactly the same formation for long. When a thermal plume shifts, the fish scatter into the clearer water and re-form a few metres away. From above, the movement appears as a brief disturbance of light rather than a visible shoal.

They spawn in the gravel of cool inlet streams, where the chemistry is steadier. The young remain among reeds and drowned roots until they are large enough to venture along the shore. The deep, clear lake is important to them as refuge and passage, but its open water is not their richest feeding ground. Like many of the caldera’s animals, they prosper at an edge rather than in the largest available space.

Frogs are more particular than their numbers suggest. Warm water alone is no use to them, and many mineral pools are lethal to their eggs. The little seep frogs of the forest lay their eggs in root cavities, water-holding plants, and other places protected from both fish and geothermal surges. The larger marsh frogs breed in fresh shallows and rain-filled hollows. After heavy rain their calls spread unevenly through the reeds: isolated clicks, short trills, and a low bubbling note that stops as abruptly as it begins. Their sudden silence is often more informative than their calling.

On drier basalt outcrops live small basalt skinks. They use the warmth of the rock but not its full midday heat, emerging in the morning to hunt beetles and other insects and retreating when the surface becomes too hot. At night some shelter in fractures that remain warmer than the surrounding air. They are among the few animals regularly seen on otherwise bare stone, though even they tend to vanish into a crack before they can be examined closely.

The slate heron is the most deliberate hunter of the shallows. It stands for long periods with its head drawn back, then takes one slow step and waits again. It follows the shifting boundary between milky geothermal water and clearer inflow, taking silverlings, frogs, and large aquatic insects. Its nests are platforms built on reed-bound root islands in the freshwater inlets. A colony may use the same group of islands for years, then abandon it after changes in water level or vegetation.

Old, inactive terraces have their own birds. Ash plovers nest directly among sinter fragments, their speckled eggs difficult to distinguish from the pale gravel. The open ground gives them a clear view of approaching danger, while nearby seeps provide insects for the chicks. As shrubs close over a terrace, the plovers disappear from it, though they may still nest on newer mineral ground only a basin away.

Rim swifts belong largely to the air. They nest in gorge crevices and drink from the lake on the wing, rising in wide arcs when insect swarms lift above the marshes. Larger cloud kites nest on the outer cliffs and cross the summit on thermals. They take ash mice, skinks, and young ground birds when opportunity permits, but also find carrion. These wide-ranging birds connect distant parts of the caldera; smaller forest birds and the grazers carry seeds across barriers that many frogs, snails, and stream insects never cross.

The most visible large residents are the caldera tahr. They live in small herds, usually no more than a dozen animals, though several herds may use adjoining ridges. Their short coats darken almost to black in rain, and their deep chests and steady breathing are conspicuous at close range. Their cloven hooves have hard outer edges for broken rock and broader, softer central surfaces that give some purchase on wet clay. They can move quickly when alarmed, but their usual unhurried gait conserves effort in the thin air.

They crop sedges, soft shrub growth, young fern fronds, and the grasses around cooled seeps. At certain exposed banks they also eat small quantities of clay, obtaining minerals missing from their usual forage. Their paths are not chosen according to any visible plan. They are simply the easiest routes repeated often enough to remain open: along a root-bound ridge, below a hot slope, around a patch of unstable sinter. Seeds travel along these corridors in dung and wet coats. Dung beetles and flies arrive soon after the herd has passed, and much of the remaining material is drawn into the soil by fungi and rain.

Beneath the same root masses live smaller mammals. Ash mice feed on seeds, fungi, and fallen insects, nesting in dry pockets above the saturated ground. Water shrews hunt along fresh streams, often entering the shallows for larvae. Both are common enough to support predators, yet either can spend its whole life beneath vegetation without becoming conspicuous.

The largest ground hunter is a low, heavy forest cat. Rare glimpses show a coat the color of wet basalt, broken along the flanks by muted chestnut markings. Its broad feet leave clear prints in soft mud but little trace on roots or stone. It is solitary and thinly distributed, with a range that may include wooded ridge, marsh margin, and several tahr paths.

It does not ordinarily run down healthy grazers. Instead, it waits where a path narrows between dense growth and hot or unstable ground. Calves, exhausted animals, and weakened adults are vulnerable; most hunts nevertheless fail. Between such opportunities, the cat takes ash mice, ground-nesting birds, and carrion. It usually spends daylight beneath a fallen root plate or in deep fern cover. Its presence is more often established by a sequence of small signs than by a sighting: frogs ceasing to call, a heron leaving the shallows, tahr bunching on a path, or fresh tracks crossing warm mud before dawn.

Rain temporarily rearranges all these lives. Dragonflies settle beneath leaves. Silverlings move away from turbulent inlets. Tahr stand close under the canopy or continue feeding with water streaming from their coats. Many frogs become active, and some forest cats hunt while the rain covers small sounds. Afterward come the midges, the hunting birds, and a general movement back toward pools and open margins.

Night does not bring an equivalent pause. Bats replace the swifts over insect-rich water. Shrews and ash mice forage, marsh frogs feed among the roots, and silverlings spread into shallows they may avoid by day. The tahr descend from exposed ridges to feed in lower cover. The forest cats follow at no fixed distance, using the same general passages but not necessarily the herds’ exact trails.

When subterranean pressure alters the flow of a spring, the inhabitants respond at different speeds. Fish move almost immediately. Midges and other aquatic insects establish themselves in new outflows within days or weeks. Wading birds alter their feeding routes. Plants respond more slowly, and their response can look paradoxical at first: beside a newly warmed stream, a thinning tree canopy admits more light, ferns flourish briefly, and grazers may increase their visits even as the forest itself is dying.

Elsewhere, an abandoned terrace cools. The microbial colors fade, ferns and coppergrass spread, and ash mice begin using the shelter of the new vegetation. Plovers may nest there for a time, then lose the open ground to shrubs. The cat gains or loses an ambush site; the tahr path bends; herons continue hunting elsewhere. None of these adjustments is restoration of a previous state. Each produces another local arrangement.

Death is incorporated just as unevenly. In warm forest litter, a small carcass may be reduced quickly by fly larvae, beetles, fungi, and scavengers. In waterlogged peat, wood and bone can persist for years. A fallen laurel may remain solid enough to carry moss and ferns while its interior becomes a network of insect galleries. Even the largest hunter eventually enters these smaller cycles.

For its inhabitants, then, the caldera is not one continuous green refuge. It is a shifting collection of usable places, separated by water too hot, soil too mineral-rich, forest too dense, or open ground too exposed. The birds cross many of these barriers; the tahr walk around them; the frogs and snails may never cross them at all.

To remain there, a lineage must do more than tolerate heat or thin air. It must be able to live within a narrow allowance of conditions—and, when that allowance moves, find another. The apparent equilibrium of the basin consists largely of these small retreats, returns, and recolonizations. Even the forest, which looks most settled, is only moving too slowly to watch.
