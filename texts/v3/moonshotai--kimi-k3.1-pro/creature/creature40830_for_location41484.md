The black water beetle lives there on its own account. So do the mortar moss and the iron bacteria at the seep. The grey bird only visits. None of these, however, accounts for the Mere’s peculiar sense of occupancy.

The other resident is easiest to miss by looking for a body.

There is no large animal beneath the central silt, and nothing coiled behind the motor. Most of the resident’s substance appears to be thin: dark films within the pipes, fine threads in the ochre at the seep, mucilaginous seams through certain lenses of clay. It favors places where two materials remain wet against each other—rust and water, silt and brick, cold metal and condensed air. If all its living matter could be stripped from the installation and gathered, there might be no more than a barrowful of it. The rest of its apparent bulk is borrowed.

Open a pipe away from the basin and one finds scale, black slime, and nothing that moves. Within the circuit, the same materials participate in behavior.

Whether that behavior belongs to one resident or many is not a settled question, even in principle. When a valve isolates part of the basin, activity there often becomes simpler rather than stopping: a seep pulses, a rim of clay rises and subsides, a vent draws water a little way up its throat. When connection is restored, those small rhythms alter and are taken into larger ones. It is almost as accurate to describe a population that sometimes becomes an animal as to describe one animal that is frequently divided.

Pressure is its quickest means of reaching itself.

The flywheel is not its heart, and the pump house is not its head. The motor supplies movement, and movement allows distant parts of the circuit to affect one another. But a valve closing is neither simply something the resident does nor simply something that happens to it. By the time such an event has passed through water, scale, clay, and another valve, the distinction has become unhelpful.

Its most visible organs are the temporary constructions in the silt.

Where several currents meet, something finer than the muddy water reveals can be drawn upward into sheets and filaments. Particles collect along them. Arches brace them; partitions increase their surface; concentric rims slow the flow enough for finer material to settle. For a few seconds, the resident has edges in daylight.

These are not dwellings, and probably not attempts at building in any human sense. They are closer to feeding surfaces, or to the shape fingers make when following an obstruction in the dark. Their crispness comes from constraint: a thin structure in moving water can persist only in certain forms. When the current changes, the support is withdrawn or redistributed, and the clay folds back.

An arch is not an unfinished house. It is usually a finished gesture.

The rectangular repetitions are more difficult. Some plainly follow buried footings, with the resident feeling its way along brick it cannot see. Others recur after the visible current has moved elsewhere. Perhaps old paths remain easier within the clay. Perhaps the films themselves retain a preference for one route over another. This is the nearest the resident comes to displaying memory: not an image kept, but a passage made slightly more likely to happen again.

It appears to feed on dissolved iron, traces of organic matter from the peat, and whatever can be strained from the circulating water. The cold cleanliness of the outfall suggests how thoroughly that water has been worked over. But its deeper requirement is not merely food or water. It is changing exposure.

A permanently open channel would scour its films away. A permanently closed one would leave them without exchange. Still water would allow some districts to choke and others to dry. The resident persists in the narrow condition between those outcomes. Throttling is not something the machinery does to it; throttling is very nearly how it breathes.

The stable notch on the sluice gauge need not be its purpose. It may only be the accumulated result of countless local acts of feeding, withdrawal, and investigation. The resident is not preserving the old installation or trying to complete its work. Its healthy condition is the Mere’s indecision.

It knows solid things chiefly as resistance.

A brick chip entering a runnel alters pressure, gathers a brief collar of silt, and is passed onward. A fragment lodged against a grille may be investigated repeatedly until its every edge has become part of the local flow. A visitor’s boot is a larger event: weight transmitted through peat, water displaced below, a new hollow left behind. The response need not occur near the foot. A pipe across the basin may sound; a runnel behind the visitor may narrow; clay may rise delicately around the print after its maker has gone.

Nothing approaches in the manner of an animal coming to look. Attention seems instead to pass from place to place, often arriving where the visitor is not.

A repeated knock on a pipe can sometimes be taken up. There may be no answer for several minutes. Later, three small pressures may disturb a pool, or a valve may give two ticks and then, after a pause, a third. This is not necessarily imitation, and there is no reason to treat it as language. A new rhythm has entered the day’s circulation and has not yet been worn away. Usually it is altered before it disappears.

The resident is neither tame nor hostile. It does not hunt, and nothing suggests that it desires company. But it investigates obstruction from both sides, and it can put pressure behind an investigation. A hand held across a grille or thrust into a vent would be, at first, simply a change in resistance. It would be unwise to wait while that resistance was examined more closely.

Stopping the motor would not kill it outright. Much of it would probably retreat into the western seeps and the wetter scale, surviving in smaller and simpler districts. A long drought might reduce it further without quite ending it. Scouring the pipes, drying the clay, and restoring the installation to efficient cleanliness would be more dangerous. A century of neglect might only alter its reach; a conscientious renovation could destroy it.

Its age is therefore difficult to infer from the age of the works. It may be older than the pump house, a life of the original seeps that spread into the new surfaces offered to it. It may be younger, an association made possible only after the outfall began returning water to its own supply. Either possibility leaves much the same evidence. The generations of human repair are now part of its surroundings, as indifferent to their intended purposes as buried brick is to grass roots.

Not everything in the Mere belongs to it. The beetle sometimes breaks a fresh mud rim while working the sluice margin, and nothing retaliates. The moss dies back and returns according to its own requirements. The grey bird’s departure causes no discernible disturbance.

On a quiet afternoon, the resident may be most evident in an inconsequential sequence. A drop falls inside the pump house. Somewhere across the water, a valve ticks. The nearest runnel draws back, and the ochre film at the seep leans a little harder into the current. Three low ribs of clay rise around an old heel print, touch its sides without preserving them, and soften away.

Nothing emerges. Nothing needs to. The resident has already examined the place where someone stood, insofar as it examines anything: by pressure, by resistance, by the brief shape taken on in mud. Then it has let that shape go, and the whole of its attention is elsewhere.
