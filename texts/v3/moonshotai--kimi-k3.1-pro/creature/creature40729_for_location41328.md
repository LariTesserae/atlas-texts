## A Census of the Hollow Brick

A census of the garden would be short, and almost entirely misleading.

One translucent snail. Three midges. One pinhead spider. After that, the census-taker would have to decide whether the vine climbing its own earlier stems is one resident or three; whether the duckweed belongs to the basin, the ceiling, or the rain presently traveling between them; and whether a mallet stroke waiting for its dimple to appear should be counted as an inhabitant, an event, or merely a noise.

Most of the visible life is green, but none of it is passive.

The plants have adapted to a world in which surfaces do not necessarily remain surfaces. Moss settles deeply into the palm whorls, not simply covering the old impressions but thickening according to their ridges, so that certain walls seem to have hands buried just beneath their green skin. Where two ferns have uncurled against each other, each keeps the other’s pressure in its adult frond. Later, when one has shifted, the remaining fern bears a glossy, indented portrait of its absent neighbor.

The vine is the most difficult to understand. Its first ascent supplies the dead-looking but still living framework for its second; the second carries the third above the ferns. Yet the uppermost leaves are not always the youngest. Sometimes a lower heart-shaped leaf opens into a shadow that has been waiting for it, while a leaf near the ceiling remains furled for many rains. The vine does not grow toward the vent. Nothing does, except occasionally a root that immediately turns back from the dry light. The plants grow toward room: a hollow between fronds, a seep beginning in clay, a shadow already occupying air.

Their apparent foresight may be nothing more mysterious than accommodation. In a place where effects arrive late, any life that insists too firmly on the present will be repeatedly bruised by it. The successful fern is the one that can bend before the blow. The successful root is the one that does not mistake a new wall for a permanent wall.

Completion is especially poor habitat. When the finished terrace ceased to be interior, no abandoned mat of moss was left behind. Either the moss vanished with it or had already withdrawn to the wet margins. Since then, roots have been thickest wherever workmanship approaches neatness. They enter a channel just before its joint can close. They thread themselves through the last tidy binding. They do not break the construction; they merely keep it weeping, swelling, and receptive to another gesture.

This looks like a pact between the garden and its vegetation. It may only be survival. Whatever fastens itself exclusively to finished surfaces is liable to become exterior with them.

Of the animals, the snail has the largest consequences.

Its whole visible territory is one potsherd, sharp enough at its edges to cut, though the snail passes over those edges without injury. Through its translucent foot, the algae it has eaten shows as a faint green thread. It grazes with extreme slowness, sometimes remaining motionless through an entire ceiling rain. Then it turns aside for no evident reason and lays down another clear ribbon.

Later, that ribbon darkens. Clay begins to sweat along it. A thread of seepage finds the route, then a root, then enough black leaf mold to support a little moss. In this way the snail’s meals become watersheds. A ledge may eventually swell beneath a place where it once paused.

There is no reason to think it intends any of this. Still, its pauses are difficult to dismiss entirely. It often stops at the point where one more stroke of grazing would join two trails and make a closed, clean circuit. After a while it turns back, leaving the circuit open.

When rain reverses, the snail fastens itself to the shard and lets water stream upward around its shell. During silence it withdraws, but not before extending its body once toward the nearest reed channel, as if testing whether the water there still remembers its work.

The three midges occupy much smaller territories.

One keeps beneath the vine’s lowest leaves, where the condensation is warmest. One is often caught in upward rain and can be seen struggling inside a rising drop, its wings held open by the water until the drop reaches the clay and vanishes. Some time later the midge reappears beneath the ferns, wet but otherwise unharmed. The third prefers shadows. It will settle on a section of bare wall and remain there, occasionally walking a few steps, until the sound of wet clay being pressed sends it flying. A handprint subsequently rises beneath the place where it stood.

There are three midges whenever anyone thinks to count them. That is not the same as saying they cannot die. After the spider catches one, there may be two for several rains. Then a third appears at the basin’s edge, crawling from the duckweed with its wings still crumpled. No eggs have been found, though the leaf mold is full of places where eggs could be overlooked.

They could pass through the vent. Occasionally one reaches its inner lip. Immediately its wings take on the dry, frosted look of the outside air, and it turns back into the heat.

The spider owns the only animal dwelling that can easily be recognized as such.

Its web is not a finished orb but an irregular open fan, stretched between the reed gutter and the half-lashed tool handle. One long strand remains loosely looped rather than fixed. Every hard rain changes the web’s shape; every reversal of rain turns it briefly into a hanging net of bright, trembling water.

The spider drinks from the largest drops and plucks the rest free. This may be maintenance, but the resulting plinking also travels through the reed bindings and returns from places much farther away than the web. Sometimes a reply comes: a single drop in an otherwise dry corner, or a faint tightening of the loose strand.

Before silence, the spider gathers what silk it can into a small bundle against the tool handle. It does not attempt to preserve the whole web. When the work resumes, the gutter and handle may no longer stand at quite the same angle, and the spider begins again with the bundle it saved. Its home is therefore not so much a structure as a practice.

The hunt, when it happens, is almost shockingly ordinary. A midge strikes a wet strand. The web stops singing. The spider runs out, and for a few moments the entire roaring chamber contains nothing more important than that small struggle.

Then there are the briefer residents: the gestures.

It is tempting to populate the garden with invisible potters, but nothing else supports them. There are no tiny footprints, no crumbs, no resting places, no paths worn by repeated passage. The stone blades remain in their basket. The mallet is heard, not seen. The workmanship does not seem to be performed by bodies concealed among the leaves.

Instead, the sounds themselves occupy the interval before their effects.

A mallet stroke has a heavy residence in the chamber. It can be heard beneath the basin’s bubbling after the initial blow has ended, recurring more faintly until the terrace dimples. Cord-creak travels through the reedwork, sometimes passing from binding to binding before a new lashing appears. The sound of a basket being set down is broader and softer. Midges have been seen resting on a floor where that sound persists, then rising gently as the basket-mark lifts beneath them.

These gestures are not alive as the snail is alive. They do not feed, flee, or reproduce. Yet the garden’s undisputed inhabitants respond to them, avoid them, and occasionally make use of them. The spider repairs during certain delays. Ferns shed their held water at a scraping sound. Roots enter clay just before it is smoothed.

Perhaps each gesture is simply on its way to becoming an object. Perhaps the interval is the only habitat it has.

Finally, there is the possibility that the garden itself is alive, though not in the manner suggested by a breathing vent or a wall that swells. Nothing indicates that it watches, chooses, or cares. It can deform a fern as readily as nourish one. It can carry away the snail’s watershed or leave the spider without two points between which to fasten silk.

If it is an organism, it is less like a heart or a mind than a growing tip.

Finished surfaces depart from it as brick. What remains inside is wet, open, and capable of further form. Height accumulates beneath the summit while the soft interior persists at the top. The garden does not repair the mountain; the making is its growth. Its newest tissue is always unfinished, and its oldest visible surfaces are never old.

The plants and small animals live within that growing edge. They are not ornaments in a container, nor priests serving a hidden intelligence. Their effects are modest but real. Roots keep joints from closing cleanly. The snail’s trails give seepage unexpected routes. Rot makes soil from fiber. The spider’s web holds water briefly where water would otherwise merely fall. Each life makes the garden slightly less straightforward to finish.

None of this requires cooperation. It may nevertheless be why so much life can persist in a place that would cease to contain anything if its last gesture were ever completed.

When the vent shows the immense wet palm, none of the residents worships it. The snail remains clamped to its shard. A midge struggles inside an ascending drop. The spider holds its bundle of silk. To them, the palm may be no more meaningful than weather, or another new surface too large to comprehend.

Then the delayed stroke arrives. Warm rain resumes its disputed direction. Roots tighten around wet cord. Somewhere far below, another hand begins smoothing clay.

And in the brick, life continues as it always has: attaching itself to what is still damp, still yielding, and still capable of being touched again.
