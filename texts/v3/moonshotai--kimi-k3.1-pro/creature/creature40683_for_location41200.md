Life in Serein is concentrated, but it is not concentrated around people. It gathers where water lingers, where warmth and root space coincide, and where the ground has acquired enough irregularity to keep a small history. A precinct garden may contain only a dozen human residents and still be densely inhabited. Most of its population is underfoot, in the cistern, or dormant in a seam, waiting for the next brief wetting.

The people live at close quarters socially and enormous distances physically. A resident may know everyone in the precinct by footstep, yet need an hour to reach the next inhabited node. The cistern courts serve as informal meeting places because they already contain the things people need: shade, tended seating, water nearby, and usually a path that maintenance keeps clear. A second cup left on a bench, a pair of shoes outside a gallery, or the ceramic click of an arriving carriage can constitute news.

Not every resident is a gardener or surveyor. People cook, read, repair small things, care for one another, grow old, and do work whose relation to the basin is not visible to a visitor. The distances suit some temperaments and not others. Those who remain learn a kind of privacy that does not require secrecy: conversation happens at close range, while beyond the court the leaf-hiss and condenser pressure quickly absorb it. An empty hall is not necessarily a lonely one. It may simply be a room used less often than its size suggests.

Other custodians have no bodies at all. A cistern’s level controls, a transit hall’s routing functions, and the mechanisms that open condenser ribs can each develop recognizable habits without becoming persons. A court begins clicking softly before rain; a carriage waits without passengers; a filter clears itself during the night. Residents do not generally call these functions alive. They do learn what to expect from them. Human solitude in Serein is therefore not the same as being untended.

Gardeners have the most intimate relationship with the living ground. Their work begins below the garden, often before there is anything visibly green. They carry moisture probes, soft brushes, cultures in damp cloth, and small samples from older root mats. A handful of mature soil is handled less like bulk material than like a community that must be persuaded to survive relocation. It contains fungal threads, bacterial films, mineral grains altered by acids, and microscopic animals whose loss would make the sample much less useful, however rich it remained chemically.

On young ground, their first task is not simply fertility. It is complication. Fungi enter clean fissures and make their walls less uniform. Roots follow condensation unevenly, then die back in places and leave narrow organic passages. Microbes establish films where water pauses. Mineral filaments bind some of these variations together without erasing them. Salts accumulate in one pocket, acidity in another; a dry void remains beside a saturated one. Gradually the substrate stops responding as a single, freshly described object.

This is why white rootlace is protected. It is not sacred exactly. It is load-bearing, water-bearing, and alive. A clean cut through it may sever more than a root: it can interrupt a fungal pathway, drain a moist pocket, or remove one of the irregular connections that took years to establish. Gardeners will prune a vine or lift a fern readily enough, but they step around exposed rootlace with the care other people might give to a foundation cable.

Above it, the veilleaf trees perform slow hydraulic work. Condensation gathers on their broad leaves and runs toward the central gutters; after rain, water travels down the stems in narrow threads and disappears into the mat without ever spreading across the paving. Their canopies make a smaller climate beneath them—slightly cooler, wetter, and sheltered from the dry basin wind. At local noon the leaves appear nearly black, but their dark red tissues are still working in the diminished daylight. Below, their roots occupy warm ground and draw what subsidy the buried lattice offers.

An old veilleaf may outlast the architectural purpose for which its court was resolved. When an unneeded facade thins and disappears, the tree can remain standing among opened galleries, its roots still counted among the foundation’s assets. Vines then cross the abandoned arches, making sheltered, humid passages between beds. Copper ferns respond to wetting almost theatrically, uncurling within minutes, while wine-red moss changes more quietly: dry and inconspicuous one day, swollen and nearly black-red the next. Each plant alters where the following drop will go.

The oldest beds acquire inhabitants too small to notice without disturbing them. Threadworms move through water films around mineral grains. Mites and springtails occupy fragments of dead leaf tissue. Fungal threads pass through both, retrieving what would otherwise have to be removed and replaced. Gardeners record these arrivals carefully. Their presence means that dead matter is beginning to be processed locally, and that a garden is no longer merely maintaining the nutrients with which it was planted.

The cisterns contain separate settlements. A newly cleaned one may be optically clear and almost empty. In older, warmer water, films establish along the walls and minute swimming organisms gather near them. The pale newts belong to this later condition. Nearly blind, they respond to vibration, scent, and the small currents made by falling drops. Much of the time they hang motionless beneath the reflected sky. Then one flexes at the tail, slides a short distance, and becomes difficult to find again. Their presence is evidence that the water has remained chemically and thermally stable long enough for a food web to form around them.

Dusk moths connect the cistern courts to the foliage. After rain they emerge singly rather than in swarms, their broad wings holding the color and visual uncertainty of the dim stone. One may seem to detach from a tree trunk, cross an amber lamp, and vanish again before its full size registers. Some visit the nearly colorless flowers that open on wet vines; others settle where water remains in leaf gutters. Their larvae live among soft fern growth and decomposing tissue in the wetter mats. By the next dry period, the most visible evidence may be a single wing left on a step.

The low browser belongs only to the oldest garden belts, or passes through them often enough to leave evidence there. No agreed likeness of it exists. There are prints instead: a broad, soft impression held briefly in fine sediment. There are fern tips stripped at a consistent height, a narrow disturbance through moss, occasionally droppings at the shaded edge of a path. Residents may live for years without seeing the animal itself. When a sighting occurs, it is usually partial—a low movement beneath fronds, already withdrawing.

Its rarity limits its appetites, but not its influence. Spores and fragments of living mat can travel on its pads. Browsed ferns leave small openings where moss or a different shoot may establish. Its droppings return plant tissue to the ground in a concentrated, microbe-rich form. Gardeners do not try to make it common; the garden belts could not necessarily support that. They note its passage because it connects places without being asked to, and because its routes sometimes reveal humid corridors that human plans had treated as separate.

Beyond the green archipelago, life is mostly dormant, transient, or being carried. Viable spores occur in the sharp fans of dust behind survey stakes. Brief condensation can wake a thin film in a sheltered hollow for a few hours before dryness returns. A fern may germinate beside a leaking seam and persist only until the leak is corrected. Carriages, damp tools, and the hem of a gardener’s clothing move small organisms between precincts. Most arrivals fail. The open basin does not threaten them so much as offer them almost nothing with which to continue.

The little channel below the overflowing cistern is different because failure there has begun to take longer.

Each overflow carries fine sediment, leaf fragments, fungal cells, and water already enriched by the mature garden. The stream does not repeat its route exactly. It leaves one shallow fork dark with moisture and cuts a little farther into another. Rootlace follows the persistent dampness without having been placed there. Copper ferns appear from spores rather than from a gardener’s tray. On the shaded underside of a displaced crust, a film survives after the exposed stone has dried. Tiny animals arrive eventually, though whether on a root fragment, a boot, or the browser’s soft feet no one can determine.

The channel is still usually dry, and nothing large lives in it. The newts remain in the cistern; the moths only use its brief humidity. Yet every wetting leaves the ground slightly more capable of retaining the next one. Organic matter catches in a bend. Fine roots hold a grain fan in place. A hollow stays moist a few hours longer. The community is beginning to alter its own conditions of arrival.

Gardeners visit without improving it. They do not straighten its course, inoculate its bare patches, or give it a precinct designation. Its water still comes from city infrastructure, and its organisms descend largely from planted stock, but its boundaries are no longer being set by either. For now its population is a few ferns, a following lace of roots and fungi, films of microbes, and whatever else arrives with the next overflow. It has no garden plan and no fixed extent. Of all the inhabited places in Serein, it may be the first whose residents will decide how large it becomes.
