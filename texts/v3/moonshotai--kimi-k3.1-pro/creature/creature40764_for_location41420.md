Nothing in the Cistern Ward lives quickly, and very little lives separately.

The most numerous inhabitants are almost indistinguishable from the ward’s mineral skin. Along the warmest seams, the colorless thread-gardens are dense populations of filaments, sheaths, and microscopic bodies occupying the narrow chemical difference between water rising from below and water already circulating through the streets. They do not simply cover the limestone. They interrogate it, spreading into its finest openings, thickening where dissolved minerals are richest, thinning where an intake draws the seep away. Their older parts die and remain as pale, stony veins. New growth advances over them, so that a living garden may be only the soft outer fringe of a much larger body made by its predecessors.

These gardens have no stable boundaries. A change in pressure can extend them across several courses of brick or reduce them to a few threads sheltered in a crack. Some become sealed inside the deposits they helped create; others are torn from an intake and settle nearby, not as a catastrophe but as a small relocation. They feed the films that grow over silt and scale. They also narrow passages, foul grilles, and retain sediment where the pumps would prefer it to remain suspended. The ward’s circulation supports them, and they continually make that circulation more difficult.

The visible vegetation makes different uses of the same crowded conditions. The olive felt belongs to still horizontal surfaces: paving, the steps of disused stairs, the broad backs of machines. It holds silt in place and gives the streets their soft, almost fabric-like footing. The burgundy ribbons favor narrow warmth and the slight movement around conduits. Their frayed edges are not merely damage; fragments detach, lodge elsewhere, and occasionally establish another dark-red length along a pipe. The waxen leaves are more local still. A masonry joint, a little seepage, and access to a lamp may be enough to support one for years.

Residence is intensely particular. To a root, one flawed joint can be a sufficient country. To the felt, the sheltered underside of a footbridge differs profoundly from its upper surface. Two growths of the same kind may live a few body lengths apart and experience different warmth, different minerals, and different degrees of light. The ward is small only when measured across. From within, it is large with distinctions.

Each lamp garden is therefore an individual place, even when it contains many kinds of growth. Its shape records the cage around the bulb, the heat leaking from a nearby pipe, and the direction in which fronds are drawn when an outlet exhales. Leaves closest to the light are thick and deeply green. Behind them, older stems live in their own shade, slowly becoming brown and rigid. Dead growth traps dust; dust holds moisture and mineral matter; new roots enter the mixture. In time, the garden acquires a core that is part vegetation, part sediment, and part masonry.

The garden’s dimming of its lamp is not quite suicide. As light weakens, growth slows. Leaves near the bulb may still receive enough illumination to persist, while the outer mass becomes increasingly self-supporting and mineralized. The result is often a stable, leafy sleeve with an amber heart. From one side the lamp appears nearly extinguished; from another, a single green vein may shine with startling clarity. These gardens are among the ward’s longest-lived residents. They have grown around light without ever knowing darkness as an event.

The machines live among them in the sense that reefs live among currents: not metabolizing as the gardens do, but altering everything within reach. Each mechanism has a small territory determined by heat, vibration, and movement. Felt does not establish itself easily on a vibrating housing. Pale threads cluster at a warm flange. Burgundy growth trails from the downstream side of an outlet, while the metal immediately beside an intake remains comparatively bare. Even the great flywheel participates. Mineral scale accumulates differently on the advancing and returning faces of its spokes, and delicate growth survives only where the displaced water is gentlest.

The maintenance mechanisms are stranger residents. Not all of them travel. Many are little more than an arm, a carriage, a weighted brush, or an old instruction with just enough body left to obey it. Their original surfaces survive in protected recesses; elsewhere, scale and growth have thickened them into shapes their makers might not immediately recognize. A moving mechanism may be easiest to detect not by sight but by the narrow clearance it leaves behind: a crescent in the silt, a patch of exposed grille, a fringe of filaments slowly withdrawing from a disturbed joint.

They perform no general stewardship. One clears an intake because a contact still closes. Another returns foundry grit toward the same place it has always gone. A carriage may remain stopped against an obstruction for years, then advance a short distance when warmth, pressure, or corrosion changes the conditions around it. Their work benefits other lives only incidentally. Clearing a grille may improve circulation through a whole court; scraping a pipe may also remove the mineral sheath that was protecting it. A brush can open a road for a crab and crush the growth that sheltered snail eggs.

Occasionally they transport residents without any possibility of knowing it. Filaments travel in their joints. Silt rich with microscopic life is pushed from one room into another. A snail attached to a housing may be carried beyond the cage or court in which it hatched. In a district with almost no traffic, even these accidental journeys matter.

The blind snails are the foundry’s most dependable animal inhabitants. They are not numerous, but the storage cages give them broad surfaces, shelter, and a continual supply of fresh material on which films can grow. Six or seven on one cage make it seem conspicuously occupied. Their shells are chalky toward the older whorls and almost clear at the growing lip. Against a pale tablet, a snail may be visible mainly because the tablet has a shadow.

They do not subsist on stone alone. They rasp away the thin living films that spread over the tablets, though their feeding also loosens grains and deepens tiny fractures. Their paths cross, vanish beneath new deposits, and reappear on another face. When a tablet cracks, its grazing marks enter the silt with it. When fragments are cleared back toward the intake, part of the snail’s small geography goes too. Nothing leaves the foundry as a finished product, but some of its material passes briefly through flesh before returning to the floor.

The snails’ eggs are laid in protected angles: behind cage wire, beneath a curled fragment of scale, in the seam between a tablet and the conveyor housing. Many are mineralized before they can hatch. Others are dislodged by the press’s vibration or carried away in a weak current. This scarcity is not an interruption of the foundry’s cycle. It is one of the reasons the cycle remains slow enough to persist.

The pallid eel, or eels, are harder to count. The ward offers no vantage from which separate sightings can be compared. A body beneath a heat exchanger and a similar body seen years later beyond a switching house may be the same animal, another generation, or simply the same solution to warm darkness. The eel tends to remain where the temperature suits it, its length following the lines of weed and loose cable around it. Its movement is often less noticeable than the slight displacement of sediment when it has gone.

It feeds infrequently and without drama: soft growth, eggs, small bodies uncovered in the silt, whatever can be taken without a chase. When a long pressure cycle alters the exchanger’s warmth, it may shift position or disappear into an interstice too narrow to inspect. Its absence does not make the shadow empty. The narrow place where its body rested remains for a while, a smooth impression slowly filling with dust.

Crabs provide the ward’s few visible journeys. A cable tray offers them firm footing above the softer floor, and a pump court can become a considerable expedition. They pause beneath obstructions, test growth with their claws, and sometimes remain motionless through an entire change in pressure. There is no migration in this, no season calling them elsewhere. Food, shelter, and the next usable edge are sufficient reasons to continue.

Older crabs become difficult to distinguish from pieces of the ward. Scale collects in the grooves of their shells. Burgundy threads may attach where the surface is rough enough. One crossing beneath a lamp can look like a fragment of encrusted machinery that has briefly acquired a purpose. Their molted shells remain behind on trays and ledges, hollow and almost perfectly attentive, until silt fills them or a maintenance arm knocks them down. For a time, the ward appears to contain two crabs where there is only one traveling and one abandoned shape.

None of these animals forms a community in the usual crowded sense. A snail may never encounter an eel. A crab may pass through a court, open a few holes in the felt, and leave conditions altered for years. Their rarity gives each trace unusual importance. A feeding path, a resting hollow, or an empty shell can remain evidence of a life long after the animal itself has moved elsewhere.

No human community remains to interpret these signs. The street plaques are not read, and no doors are opened in expectation of workers returning. But this does not make the ward abandoned in any simple sense. Abandonment describes a relationship to people. The ward has developed other relationships. Its oldest instructions still produce actions; its lamps still organize growth; its pumps still create conditions to which living things respond. A change in one room is answered, hours or months later, in another. The whole has reflexes, though no necessary self.

It would be misleading to call the result cooperation. The residents have not become gentle. Roots open mortar while binding walls together. Thread-gardens foul the circulation that nourishes them. Lamp gardens consume the light and then reduce it. Snails help return the foundry’s product to sediment. Maintenance mechanisms clear obstructions and scrape away protective crusts. Every resident is slowly damaging something.

More exactly, they have become slow enough to survive one another. Injury and repair proceed at comparable rates. Nothing is harmless, but nothing is so effective that it gets to finish.

The ward is inhabited, then, by more than a list of species. A warm seam and its living fringe constitute a lasting household. So do a lamp and the garden dimming it, an intake and the mat repeatedly closing over it, an old switching instruction and the arm that still answers. The eel’s warm shadow is a residence. The crab’s cable tray is a road. For a snail, one storage cage may be an entire geography.

A pump answers a pressure change. Somewhere a frond inclines. In the foundry, a snail begins another path across a tablet already softening beneath it. Nothing announces a new age of the Cistern Ward. The next one is already being lived, in small clearances, at the speed of roots, scale, and patient machinery.
