## What Lives in Sinter

Nothing in Sinter is quite wild, and almost nothing is tame. The distinction has little use there. A fish may spend its whole life beneath a cistern cover, rising when it feels footsteps through the stone, yet belong to no household. A bird may nest in a hole cut expressly to suit it and still abandon the colony without warning. Even the people are best understood as inhabitants of the waterworks rather than as builders standing outside them. They maintain the conditions in which everything else lives, and are fed, warned, plagued, and kept awake by the result.

Life sorts itself according to heat, flow, and foothold.

The smallest lives are among the most closely watched. The microbial film is not one substance but a shifting company of organisms, each with its preferred water. Near the hottest spring mouths it forms cream or iron-red threads, delicate as wet hair but able to stain a whole shelf. Farther along the cooling runs it becomes gray, grainy, or softly slick. It sloughs away, drifts, catches again, and spreads across new stone almost as soon as water reaches it. Keepers judge a spring partly by the appearance of this growth. In an open run, water that looks too clean can be more worrying than water that looks foul: it may be too hot, carrying some harmful mineral, or simply failing to feed anything.

Where the film can be grazed, the stone swarms with small animals. Sievebacks, flattened crustaceans rarely larger than a fingernail, cling to the downstream faces of slabs and comb food from the current with their legs. Their cast shells gather in corners as transparent grit. Among them live insect larvae, some slender and quick, others blunt and almost immobile, sealed in cases of mineral grains. A hand lifted from a shallow pool may bring up dozens of animals and still appear to have disturbed nothing; the surface closes behind it, and the grazing continues.

Larger, paler relatives inhabit the drains. These drain crabs have weak eyes, broad claws, and a tolerance for water that people will not touch. They find carcasses, scraps, and the softer accumulations around waste inlets. A few are useful. A multitude usually means that something has died upstream or that matter is collecting where it should be moving on. Their abundance is therefore read as a symptom rather than celebrated as a catch. When removed, they are eaten; their thicker shells become scoops, small cups, and lids for stone vessels.

The fish of the open channels are narrow, alert animals, pewter or charcoal above and pale beneath. They hold position in the aerated water, taking larvae and insects as the current brings them. People fish them less by pursuit than by arrangement. A slab is shifted, a side pocket opened, a stone grate closed, and a portion of the run becomes a temporary enclosure. The rest of the channel remains open. Skilled keepers can take fish without emptying the run or stirring up the film on which the next crowd depends.

The covered cisterns hold other kinds. Some are whiskered and nearly blind, finding the inlet and one another by touch. Some rise at the vibration of a particular cover being lifted. The largest are the hidefish: slow, thick-skinned animals of deep, warm water, capable of gulping air and swallowing almost any animal matter that reaches the bottom. An old hidefish may be longer than the person responsible for its cistern and may outlast several such keepers. Districts sometimes know these individuals by scars, missing fin rays, or peculiar habits. Killing one is uncommon, not because it is sacred, but because a living animal that cleans a deep cistern is often more valuable than a single heavy meal.

When a hidefish does die, almost nothing is discarded. Its skin is scraped, repeatedly worked with fat, warmed, and softened until it can be used as leather. The heaviest door curtains come from such skins, though many are patchworks of smaller hides sewn with sinew. Skin bags, protective aprons, infant slings, and quarry foot-wraps all come from the same patient secondary labor. A hidefish is thus both an inhabitant of the waterworks and, eventually, part of their furnishing.

Frogs occupy the intervals between fish water and human shelter. Some are no larger than a thumb joint and live around seep-warmed steps. Others crouch in cistern overflows, broad as two hands, their bodies the color of wet ash or rusted stone. Their tadpoles graze the film; adults take insects, small crustaceans, and one another. Eggs, tadpoles, calling adults, and dead frogs can be found in the same basin at once. No collective breeding moment empties or fills them.

To the people, the frog chorus is not music and not merely noise. Each cistern has a customary depth and texture of sound. A keeper does not need to identify every animal. They need to notice that one knocking voice has stopped near the inlet, or that the overflow basin has become thinner and harsher in its calls. People harvest frogs, sometimes heavily, but a prudent harvest does not leave a basin silent. Silence removes both food and one of the means by which the water is monitored.

Above the pools, insects rise in columns so dense that birds and bats can feed without searching widely. Their emergences overlap rather than coincide. Some species trouble people hardly at all; others bite, enter sleeping chambers, or gather around sores and eyes. Hide curtains are as much barriers against them as against steam. Certain warm vaults are left to the bats because closing every opening would mean more insects below.

The bats themselves fill the high dark with another social world. They roost in crowded, warm clusters, hunt through the insect columns, and return to vaults where pups, nursing mothers, old animals, and carcasses are always present together. Much of their communication passes above human hearing, though the roof voids still contribute small screams, chuckles, and scratching sounds to the city’s roar. Their droppings fall into stone catchments or onto ledges from which they can be washed toward the settling pools.

Builders know which high holes belong to bats and which to birds by the staining beneath them, the debris on the sill, and the sounds within. When a vaulted roof must be repaired, the work is often done in sections. To strip the whole roof at once would not merely displace animals; it would remove a major control on insects and leave a quantity of exposed droppings and carcasses to be dealt with elsewhere.

The roof birds are smaller and harsher-voiced than the great waders of the outer flats. They nest on parapets, under corbels, and in holes too narrow or high for a person to reach. With no twigs or reeds available, their nests are scrapes, rims of small stones, accumulations of shell, or cups fixed with droppings. Some eggs lie directly in the roughness deliberately left on a ledge. A parapet built too neatly may stand empty until weathering, damage, or a keeper’s stone pick makes it usable.

A rookery keeper walking a permitted route can pass an egg, a newly hatched chick, a half-feathered juvenile, a bird repairing its nest, and a carcass being worked over by insects within a few paces. Harvest therefore cannot be governed by a calendar. Sections of roof are left alone while others are visited, and repairs move around the colony rather than waiting for a season when the colony will conveniently disappear. Marks scratched beside access stairs indicate which ledges may be entered and which must remain undisturbed.

The outer waders are less manageable. They pack the rookery flats in such numbers that an approaching person can become a moving disturbance in a single enormous body. Their bills take fish and crustaceans from the shallow water; their droppings whiten the stone and enrich the pools below. Keepers who work there suffer the ammonia, the mites, and the relentless cries. Turns on the flats are often shorter than work elsewhere. The birds are harvested, but they are not herded. The useful skill is learning how to approach without setting the whole flat into flight.

Human beings move through all this as the quietest large animals, at least in their intentions. There is little point in shouting across a street. A person who wants another’s attention approaches, enters their field of view, or touches them. Public hand-speech uses the hands, arms, shoulders, and orientation of the body; at close range or in darkness, related signs can be given against a palm, forearm, or back. It is not a collection of emergency gestures. Jokes, arguments, instructions, courtship, and extended stories all have their forms in it.

Spoken language remains intimate and necessary. It belongs to deep rooms, to faces close together, to people working shoulder to shoulder, and to things said directly into an ear. Caregivers keep infants close not because a cry would be ignored, but because it might simply not be heard. Children learn early to distinguish being out of sight from being out of reach.

Hearing often deteriorates with age, especially among people whose work keeps them beside dense rookeries or resonant cisterns. This does not exclude anyone from ordinary public life, which never depended wholly on hearing. It does matter to the specialist listeners. Their expertise is intensely local: one knows a series of roof voids, another a chain of frog basins, another the vibrations associated with a difficult stretch of drain. No listener comprehends Sinter as a whole. Each knows what belongs to a small part of it and notices when that part changes.

Most people go barefoot in familiar districts, where the feet can read warmth, moisture, and the difference between sound foundation and thin mineral crust. Hide coverings protect the feet in quarries and on the sharper outer stone. Clothing is made from hide panels stitched with sinew, easily repaired and easily removed for wet work. There is nothing woven about it. Mineral water leaves pale or rusty margins; rookery workers carry white dust on their shoulders; stonecutters’ hands become crossed with fine cuts; vent workers often have skin roughened by repeated heat.

The important fellowship in a district is often the run. A run is both a physical sequence—spring mouth, channel, nursery pool, cistern, overflow, settling pond—and the group of people responsible for keeping that sequence functioning. It may contain several households and more than one kind of specialist. Its boundaries are hydraulic rather than simply territorial. Food taken from it carries obligations: an inlet to clear, a cover to maintain, a fish population not to exhaust, a section of roof to leave rough.

Vent keepers understand the covered cooking chambers and the dangers of shifting heat. Stone workers know how to replace a step, a span, or a cistern mouth without interrupting everything around it. Rookery keepers, hide workers, listeners, and people skilled at moving living fish between pools all have their place. Most also know how to perform the ordinary work: scooping sediment, replacing a small slab, cleaning a grate, carrying waste to the correct pond.

A settling pond is not treated as a sewer at the end of the system. It is a feeding ground with a location, a keeper, and limits. Different kinds of waste go to different water. A carcass in the wrong pool can foul a district; too much richness can be as damaging as too little. The recycling of matter is skilled work, not an automatic virtue of the basin.

This arrangement does not make the people uniformly wise or gentle. They take too many eggs, postpone unpleasant repairs, quarrel over credit, steal choice food, and sometimes resent the animals on which they depend. The waterworks do not abolish selfishness. They merely make its consequences difficult to keep abstract. A neglected inlet becomes someone’s silent cistern; an overharvested roof becomes an absence of cries and then an increase in insects below.

Nor is everyone equally comfortable. A household beside a dependable vent, with a productive cistern and sleeping chambers several walls away from the roofs, possesses advantages more important than stored objects. Quiet sleep is wealth. Reliable water is wealth. A place in a well-maintained run can matter more than the number of tools or skins a person owns. The deepest sleeping rooms are prized by households and often sought for the sick, the exhausted, and those caring for very young children.

Food arrives without a harvest season. Meals draw on runfish, frogs, small crustaceans, eggs, larvae skimmed during pond cleaning, and less often a roof bird or an old cistern animal. Covered stone chambers at the vents hold steady heat; food is steamed, baked, or left to cook slowly in hollowed stone. No woodsmoke hangs over the vent courts. Much of the food is eaten near where it was living, and live water remains the most important form of storage. A well-kept cistern is more useful than a room full of perishing meat.

Consequently, hunger in Sinter is usually local and technical rather than seasonal. A spring shifts. A film dies. Fish cease entering a channel. A colony abandons a roof, or waste backs up into the water that once supported a nursery. There is no general lean time to which the whole basin submits. There are failed runs, and the difficult work of discovering why they failed.

Public time follows much the same pattern. People sleep and wake out of phase with one another. Work is measured by the filling and clearing of a pool, the cutting of a slab, the repair of a wall section, the hatching of a particular clutch, or the interval between inspections. Longer records are scratched into stone: changes to an inlet, past vent failures, obligations attached to a cistern, warnings about thin crust. When a wall is refaced, important marks are cut again. An inscription may therefore be very old on very new stone.

This gives human memory a peculiar material form. Objects do not necessarily become venerable by surviving. They become venerable by being accurately renewed. A channel keeper may inherit no ancient tool and no old building surface, yet still possess a detailed account of mistakes made by people long dead. An elder whose hearing can no longer separate the higher frog calls may remain valuable because they remember what happened the last time a spring moved beneath a wall.

People themselves age, and this is one accumulation the city cannot repair away. Children outgrow the small tasks by which they learned a run. Strong hands stiffen. Listeners lose part of their hearing. Bodies become less able to tolerate heat, ammonia, or a hard sleeping shelf. Against the perpetual renewal of steps and roofs, human change is conspicuous. It does not necessarily make life feel futile. A person’s work can persist as a condition: clean flow, a stable span, a healthy nursery, a warning copied clearly enough that someone else will understand it.

There is art in this, but not only in maintenance. Formal hand-speech can hold an entire vent court without a voice being raised. Carved instructions acquire borders, repetitions, and flourishes that hands as well as eyes can follow. In deep chambers, people make sounds that do not attempt to overcome the city: low voices close together, rhythms passed through palms and stone, stories spoken for only a few listeners. Elsewhere, the same story may be performed entirely in movement for a crowd.

The people are not reverent toward every living thing. They curse biting insects, dread a crust collapse, drive crabs from blocked drains, and sometimes hate the roar with considerable sincerity. They can also become attached to a scarred hidefish, take pride in a difficult bird colony, or sit beside a cistern longer than its inspection requires. Affection and use are not considered opposites.

Most of them do not experience Sinter as the image of a world beginning. It is simply where their feet remain wet, their food lives beneath stone, and someone is always awake. What would overwhelm an unfamiliar ear is, to them, a population of separate and meaningful presences.

When a keeper rests a hand on a cistern rim, they are not hoping for silence. They are attending to the frogs below, the insects at the overflow, the clatter of birds above, and the small tremor of water carried through the stone. If all are present in their proper proportions, the run is alive. If one is missing, there is work.
