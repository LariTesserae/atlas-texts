Nothing in the Still Hollows lives everywhere. Life has particular addresses: a crowded ceiling, a fall of guano, one wet joint in an otherwise dry wall, a basin small enough to cover with a hand. Between those addresses, an animal may be only a crossing, a track in dust, or a sound arriving from somewhere else.

The bats are the great exception to the stillness, though not to the localization. Most of the principal roosts are formed by small, narrow-winged freetails, their bodies packed so closely that the colony appears to have a single breathing surface. Bent-wing bats occupy looser clusters along fractures, while long-eared gleaners often hang separately near the margins. These are different ways of using the same shelter: the freetails depend on multitude and shared warmth; the solitary bats depend on remaining unnoticed.

To them, a dark gallery is not featureless. It is a sequence of returning sounds. A low roof answers one way, an open hall another; a side fracture interrupts an echo that would otherwise continue. A bat can travel kilometres through apparently vacant passage by following distinctions no light would reveal. At the roost, the same acuteness becomes intimate. A mother can find her pup among thousands by call and scent. Individuals dispute a few centimetres of ceiling, then settle back into the general density. Mites move through their fur and wing membranes, and small bat bugs wait in nearby fissures for the warmth and blood to return.

Every night, the colonies carry the Hollows outward. The bats feed on insects beyond the plateau and return with the cave’s food supply inside their bodies. Almost everything eaten in the deep chambers was first caught in the open air. Guano, shed hair, an occasional fallen young animal, and the bodies of the dead are the forms in which that food arrives.

Beneath a large roost, abundance changes scale without diminishing. Colorless fungi and bacteria begin on the freshest material. Fly larvae work through the wettest patches; mites graze, pursue, and scavenge among them. Springtails gather where fungal growth is richest, sometimes so densely that a patch of deposit seems to pale. A touch sends them jumping, and for an instant the floor appears to disperse into grains.

The cave crickets are larger and more deliberate. Their antennae, many times the length of their bodies, sweep ahead of them over grit and guano. Some feed on the deposits directly; others take fungi, dead insects, or the softer remains of a fallen bat. They molt beneath roof-flakes, leaving pale, hollow likenesses of themselves among the debris. Their rasping can continue when no individual insect is visible. Beetles move through the same island in different ways: rounded scavengers burrowing into older material, quick rove beetles hunting across its surface, larvae concealed below. Under the plates lie pseudoscorpions, flat and patient, and small predatory mites. A pale centipede may be the largest hunter beneath a particular roost, though there need not be another in the next hall.

These communities have edges sharper than anything visible in the stone. Beyond the reach of regular guano, the same dust supports almost nothing. Crickets and beetles sometimes cross that bare ground, and their tracks show that the islands are not absolutely sealed, but they do not establish the same multitude there. A dead bat beyond the feeding floor may draw animals for a while. Once it is gone, the passage becomes vacant again.

In the isolated pools, life is slower and much easier to overlook. White amphipods walk or flex along the mineral bottom, investigating by touch and chemical trace rather than sight. They do not school. A basin may contain a handful moving independently through water so clear that their bodies seem to be small interruptions in it. Minute worms inhabit the clay or the organic film along the rim. Other eyeless animals occur so sparsely that a pool can appear empty until a specimen is disturbed and its whole population seems to consist of the one animal now moving.

There are no plants in these basins and no fish. Food consists of microbial films, fine organic particles, and the rare drowned insect. A springtail washed from a nearby ledge can be a substantial event in such an economy. The animals endure scarcity by needing little, remaining inactive for long periods, and using what arrives almost completely. Because the pools do not connect, each has its own census: amphipods in one, mostly worms in another, only a sparse film of life in a third. Proximity does not make them alike.

The larger permanent predators are correspondingly rare. A centipede may patrol an extensive guano island. A long-legged spider may occupy the one damp crevice where insects regularly pass. Only where several wet chambers adjoin do a few slender, pale salamanders occur, sheltering in saturated fractures and visiting pools or cricket ground to feed. Their presence never becomes general. An immense hall may contain one such hunter, or none.

Even the blue-green lights are animals living at the limit of their food supply. They are the larvae of small cave gnats, each settled in a little silk-and-mucus cradle beneath a moist overhang. From it, sticky threads hang into the air. The larva’s glow attracts minute flying insects; the light is a trap, not an illumination offered to the chamber. A fed larva may dim and remain motionless. A hungry one can shine for hours above stone that receives no other light.

Most overhangs, even suitably damp ones, have no colony. Too few insects pass to make waiting worthwhile. Where a colony persists, it may consist of six larvae, or twenty, separated by dark stone. The adult flies are brief and frail compared with the long larval life. They mate, lay eggs, and disappear, while generations of larvae keep the lights close to the same few locations. Nothing else can use that faint radiance. It grows no plants and reveals no road.

At the roof openings, life can afford to be green, but only separately. A fern occupies the precise ledge where reflected daylight and occasional moisture coincide; it is not the edge of a fern bed. Moss cushions endure drying and revive briefly when wetted. A root may emerge from a fracture with a few fine hairs and then stop, its tip drying in air that offers nothing further. Small insects and mites use these pockets, and hunting spiders sometimes wait beside them, but the barren ledge a short distance away remains barren.

The cleverest visitors also stay near this gray light. A plateau crow may crack a snail against a stone, turning the slab momentarily into an anvil, or use a loose stick once to worry a grub from a fissure. The stone is not flaked and the stick is not shaped. When the bird leaves, there is no tool among the debris, only objects that could be used again or never touched. Owls and snakes may take bats near an opening, but they belong chiefly to the plateau and do not carry their hunting into the deep system.

Thus the Hollows’ inhabitants do not share one way of being cave animals. The bats live by numbers and travel. The guano communities live by converting a concentrated, continual fall of food. The pool animals live by slowness and interruption. The glow larvae live by waiting almost without motion. The plants at the openings survive within exact, separate allowances of light and water. Whiteness, lost eyes, and elongated feelers appear where they are useful; they are not a uniform costume worn by everything below.

None of these lives enlarges its island into the surrounding country. The bats do not improve their ceilings, the insects do not spread their abundance across the dry floors, and the luminous larvae do not make the chambers brighter for anything else. Habitation persists by replacement rather than construction.

A single ceiling may therefore hold hundreds of thousands of bodies while the next kilometre of passage contains a few tracks, one hunter, and sounds made elsewhere. Both belong to the Still Hollows. Most residents belong intensely to one small place; the bats and a few wanderers make their lives by crossing the distances between such places. The rest is not quite emptiness, but distance—crossed more often by sound than by bodies.
