Nothing in the Dry Choir has a life cycle simple enough to draw as a circle. A thicket may be a browser’s old age; a swarm may be a trunk’s brief way of becoming elsewhere; a seed case may outlive its seed by centuries as an instrument. Oil and warm gas nourish the life. Sound does not feed it so much as give it impatient instructions, and those instructions are often misunderstood.

Almost everything hears, though very little possesses anything that could be called an ear. Leaves hear through their ribs. Roots hear through stone. Browsers receive vibrations separately in each foot, so that a procession of them may turn toward a call at slightly different moments, like a field bending in successive gusts. Even the discarded husks in the open grit can tighten when a note passes over them.

The violet felt beneath the islands may be the oldest continuous inhabitant. Each filament is hollow near its base and divides into dry, resinous tips that catch spores, powdered shell, and beads of oil escaping from root-bark. Beneath a healthy mat, the grit is bound into a dark crust crowded with folded cases. Some contain dormant animals. Some contain the beginnings of fans or filaments. Many contain nothing but a shape that life has not yet finished using. When the felt is torn, the pepper smell sharpens, and the exposed filaments coil inward with a faint, continuous whisper.

The felt survives most catastrophes. A thicket can be stripped, split, and ground back into the floor while buried violet threads remain alive beneath it. Fragments also travel in the coarse fiber of browsing animals and around the toes of larger feet. An island’s new position sometimes begins as no more than a faint discoloration caught in a footprint.

Above the felt, an island is not necessarily a grove of separate trees. The braided trunks often belong to different ages of one repeated growth, each younger stem having tightened around an older hollow core. The broad doorleaves take the brass-colored light on their green faces; their crimson backs are more sensitive. A leaf may turn toward a new call, shiver until its edge splits, and seal the wound with black glaze while still trembling. Bladder-fronds fill with warm gas and lean against one another. Some collapse harmlessly into rubbery parchment. Others, when pierced, release not spores but a tight, ticking rain of minute legs.

The rubbery fans are harder to place. They layer themselves over old shells, abandoned legs, and half-dead roots with equal readiness. Their tissues can remain apparently unchanged for years, then produce a transparent vane in an afternoon. Most such vanes tear loose and vanish into the haze. A few fold down, develop fine articulations along their veins, and crawl.

Between the islands, the open ground is not empty so much as stored. The pale grit includes mineral fragments, but also lentil-sized cases, jointed coils, wing scales, and flat discs that might be seeds until something causes them to unfold. Most never wake. They are rounded by dust and footsteps until they become indistinguishable from the stone. The danger in a quiet place is not that it contains no life, but that too much of it may be waiting for the same signal.

The pale **unbinders** are among the few inhabitants regularly at work there. They are low, segmented animals, some no larger than a grain and some as long as a finger, with salt-white backs and mouths surrounded by stiff circular brushes. They do not rot dead tissue. They unweave it. Fiber is teased apart, shell is fretted into powder, and glazed oil is scraped away in dark curls. After a collapse they arrive in numbers that seem impossible for the bare floor to have concealed. A few hours later there may be nothing left of them but minute translucent skins among the grit.

The browsers are easier to follow because they persist long enough to have a before and an after. Visitors usually call them **manyfoots**. They begin as leafed knobs among the fans, each held down by one black root. During this stage they are almost indistinguishable from the surrounding growth, except that their leaves are arranged around a flattened shell and their root occasionally flinches.

When enough oil has accumulated, the shell lifts. The root tears with a dry report, and rootlets around its margins become the animal’s first uncertain legs. A newly freed manyfoot still carries green or crimson foliage on its back and drags the curled stump of its root behind it. Older individuals are bark-hided and bare, though their joints remain tufted with coarse fiber.

They feed by rocking their flat bodies against felt and fan growth, scraping with brushes beneath the forward shell. They do not appear to form herds, but their footsteps make a scattered clatter, and other manyfoots often approach it. Thus a solitary animal can acquire a procession without any evident social intention. Pods sometimes spring up along the route such a procession has taken. Sometimes, instead, the route fills with thorns.

When frightened, a manyfoot clamps its legs beneath it and becomes a piece of layered ground cover. If stillness lasts too long, the pose can cease to be a pretense: the legs thicken, the shell opens along its back, and roots begin to descend. Very old browsers may walk more and more slowly over several seasons before committing to this final change. The thickets they become often retain a circle of hollow legs. Wind entering them produces small, uneven flutes. In this way, animals that once crossed the hollow answering the Choir remain behind as part of its instrument.

The **glasswings** are much briefer. They pour from split trunks in transparent thousands, their wings held rigid by amber veins and their bodies little more than black, oil-heavy knots. Their first flight is usually also their feeding flight. They settle on new leaves and unseam the tissue along its ribs. One animal makes almost no sound; a swarm produces the hiss of grit being poured, pierced by wing-cries too fine and numerous to follow separately.

Their abundance can strip an island while it is still growing. Yet they do not simply destroy it. The high irritation of their wings often coincides with fresh transparent growth, filament sails, and tissues so thin that they tear free and join the swarm. Whether these are plants becoming animals, animals being imitated by plants, or neither is not a distinction the hollow seems obliged to preserve. When their oil is spent, glasswings drop. Wings detach first and stick to trunks like flakes of mica; the bodies click softly on the grit.

The **pod-springers** are even less interested in individual survival. A crushed pod may release thousands, each thumb-sized and folded around a single powerful pair of hind legs. Many have no functional mouth. Their entire inheritance is the oil stored in their shells, and they spend it jumping. They strike stones, seed cases, one another, and the legs of browsers. Their impacts crack further pods, so that a patch of ground can seethe, fall still, and then seethe again from a second generation before the unbinders have finished with the first. A few springers survive long enough to grow violet fuzz around their leg joints. These are sometimes found rooted days later, their leaping legs already stiffening into woody forks.

Other musicians are longer lived. Narrow, saw-bodied insects inhabit hollow stalks and abandoned seed cases, each scraping out a call that seems disproportionately loud for its size. They are among the few creatures whose young consistently resemble their parents, although their instruments change as they grow. A young insect’s thin click may become a sustained rasp when its case hardens. When the stalk breaks, the insect often remains inside a fragment, calling from the floor until something steps on it.

None of these familiar sequences explains every event. In particular, they do not explain the old wall-record of a motionless thicket becoming several hundred running animals in moments. The Choir has inhabitants for which there are no stable names because they have not yet happened twice.

The largest island has its own resident, or at least its own recurring evidence. Browsers commonly turn aside from one deep root-shadow. The air there pulses with a warm, resinous exhalation, and the booming, accordion-like cry that sometimes underlies all other sound seems to come from below the buttresses rather than from any mouth among them.

For convenience, the unseen maker is called **the Bellows**.

Its occasional foot is broad as a shield, with three blunt toes and a sole scarred white by salt-grit. Fine rootlets inhabit the cracks between its horny plates. It does not stride out or reveal a leg. The foot merely presses beyond the shadow, remains while dust gathers around it, and withdraws. Once, according to the shelter marks, violet filaments had begun stitching themselves across its upper surface before it moved.

No one has seen the Bellows feed. It may be an animal sheltered by the island, an immense and ancient browser that never completed its rooting, or an organ the island extrudes when it requires a foot. Its low call often precedes a thickening of the buttress roots, but often enough it does not. Nothing in the hollow appears to obey it. Its distinction is not authority but duration: thickets rise around it, are stripped, become dust, and rise again, while the same slow inhalation continues underneath.

The quiet crescents support a different company. There the felt lies close to the stone, and the doorleaf growth, when it appears at all, remains as small crimson buttons. Translucent pods stay no larger than fingernails. A narrow ash-colored insect lives in the best-preserved baffle and produces one dry tick followed by a thin rising note. It can be heard entire there. If another insect answers, it usually stops, sometimes for hours. Beneath the dust around it lie densely packed husks, including the folded shapes of animals never seen in the quiet pocket itself.

Whoever maintains the crescents and repairs the forked frame has become part of this ecology without becoming its master. No settled people live in the hollow. There are no stores, no hearth, and no path that remains reliable from one visit to the next. The repairers’ work consists largely of maintaining exceptions: one place where growth stays low, one frame whose stiff cords rattle irregularly rather than keeping a beat, one clapper wedged so that the wind cannot make it strike continuously.

Their marks are less like declarations than records of failed experiments. Intervals are counted. Pauses are deepened where an observation had to be corrected. Black glaze has been scraped from a few stems and certain pods have been cut neatly rather than crushed, so the visitors may come for materials as well as knowledge. Whatever their purpose, they arrive during confused passages, do a little work, and leave. They have learned enough not to sing while doing it.

Even their interventions are eventually taken up. Roots lift the fitted stones. Springers shelter in the stair’s crooked treads. A discarded length of cord becomes the axis of a rubbery fan. The quiet insect may depend on the baffles now, which means the repairers preserve one small life precisely by preserving a place where almost nothing else can decide to grow.

From the rim, none of this is visible for long. A manyfoot pauses at the edge of the grit with one last green leaf attached to its back. Glasswings fall around it, already losing their wings. Beneath its feet, dormant cases tick without opening. From the largest island comes the long intake of the Bellows.

If the browser walks on, it may carry the felt to the beginning of another island. If it roots, its legs may stand for years as a ring of little flutes. Either outcome leaves the stone cup looking nearly unchanged.

The mountain keeps its shape. Within it, life is not a population of finished creatures, but a series of brief agreements about whether to root, to walk, to fly, to unbind—or to wait in the grit and listen.
