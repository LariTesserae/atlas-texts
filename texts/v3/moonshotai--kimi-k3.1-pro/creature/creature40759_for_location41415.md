The shelf is inhabited, but not occupied. There is no hidden fauna behind its silence, no keeper of the wet crowns, no creature that has learned to use the cupped stones. Its residents are the visible ones, and even they are fewer than the greenery first suggests. A many-stemmed tree may be one old individual; a hummock may carry generations of moss beneath a single fern. Life covers only those places where, at some unlikely moment, something small managed to take hold.

The hummocks are the oldest living structures, though not necessarily the oldest living bodies. Their surfaces are a thin, working layer of moss, roots, and new growth. Beneath that lies the accumulated remainder of earlier surfaces: dead stems still interlaced, root fibers persisting after their plants have vanished, red clay caught and held until it resembles soil. Nothing tunnels through this material. It compacts under its own weight and swells again when wet. A hummock can therefore grow outward and upward while its center remains almost unchanged for decades. Its shape records successful growth more faithfully than it records decay.

The black-stemmed ferns rise from these accumulations like events that have lasted unusually long. A solitary crown reflected in a pool is not necessarily young, and its isolation is not a preference. Spores reach many of the shelf’s damp grooves, but nearly all come to nothing. Some dry on open rock; others wash into clay too dense for their first roots. The few that survive may spend years as inconspicuous green filaments before producing a recognizable frond. After that, growth remains deliberate. New fronds stay folded through long periods of apparently perfect warmth. Old ones keep their full outlines, unchewed and untorn, until their tissues brown and they settle gradually into the mat below.

The rosettes live by smaller margins. Their concave leaves receive rain directly and sometimes catch the fall from a stone crown, but the water standing in them is not a nursery and contains no little world of swimming things. It is simply held water. It warms, gathers a little dust, and is lost by overflow or evaporation. As the plant adds leaves, older ones bend outward and downward, forming a layered basin whose center slowly rises. A rosette may look almost unchanged from one season to the next while its root has been thickening in a seam no wider than a finger.

On the pale ribs, establishment is narrower still. Moss occupies a groove; a small fern finds a pocket where wind has left spores and rain has left just enough mineral matter. These plants experience the stone’s inverted wetness most intimately. Their roots may sit in material that appears dry while the surface around their crowns remains damp. Yet they do not cause the seep, regulate it, or draw it upward by any exceptional act. The water would rise without them. They merely persist where its arrival makes persistence possible, and die back where a crown’s pore structure changes or a thin layer of sediment cuts off their hold.

The four copper-brown trees are not a grove in any meaningful sense. They cast no shared shade, and their roots have no reason to meet. Each is a separate solution to the problem of becoming large on a shelf that offers little depth. Where a root crosses stone, it broadens and flattens rather than penetrating; where it reaches clay, it divides into progressively finer threads. The visible trunk is supported by a body much wider and less regular than the trunk suggests. Stems can be lost without the tree dying, and new ones can arise slowly from old wood near the base, so the age of the whole plant cannot be read from any single stem.

Their small dry seeds are abundant compared with their descendants. Wind carries most beyond the rim or onto bare rock. Wash takes others into pools. A very few lodge in existing root mats, where they must compete not with animals but with established roots, deep shade close to the ground, and the simple absence of unoccupied space. The trees consequently remain four for long stretches without being frozen at four. A stem dies; a seedling persists for several years and then fails; elsewhere a shoot detached by heavy rain roots unexpectedly and begins the long, uncertain business of becoming something more permanent.

Nothing maintains the open intervals between all these lives. They remain open because they are difficult. The plants neither cooperate to preserve them nor advance across them as a front. Their exchanges are slight and material: a fern’s dead frond adds fiber to the place where a moss later spreads; a tree root redirects a thread of water; a hummock gives a seed better footing than the surrounding clay. Warmth removes one interruption, but it does not abolish scarcity. Growth is easy only after establishment, and establishment is rare.

So the shelf’s stillness is not dormancy. Its inhabitants are active at scales that offer almost nothing to the eye: a root pressing a fraction harder against stone, a folded frond lengthening within itself, another living layer forming over dead moss. A bead gathers at a pale summit and eventually falls. Nothing turns toward the sound. Later, perhaps after many such falls, one more root hair enters a damp groove, and the shelf acquires a resident where, for all practical purposes, there had been only stone.
