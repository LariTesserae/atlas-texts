No one lives at the centre, because there is no centre to spare. Nor is there a queen sealed in some smaller cell, a pale guardian coiled beneath the water, or a single ancient animal whose dreaming explains the rest. The hollow has no principal inhabitant. It has a population so closely fitted together that, from within, one creature’s body is always another creature’s wall, ceiling, instrument, or weather.

The silver springtails are the most restless of the visible lives. Their folded springs carry the whole possibility of a leap, compressed beneath their bellies and rendered useless by proximity. One has its spring lodged beneath a mite’s knee; another’s is braced against the yielding shell of a snail. They do not struggle to free themselves. Their antennae move with exquisite concentration over whatever lies immediately before them: a bristle, a crystal edge, the dark hinge of a neighbor’s leg. One springtail grooms the antenna of another, drawing it through its mouthparts with a care that looks like affection until the same movement is seen applied to a grain of silt. Beneath the silver cuticle, a minute heart shows garnet-red. Its beating gives off no warmth.

The mites belong more closely to the light. At rest, their faceted backs would be indistinguishable from the crystal nuclei beneath them, but none is quite at rest. A palp tests the air. Two legs clean a third. Mouthparts work at some irregularity too small to be seen, and no particle comes away. The clear mite sealed within its exact air-cell seems at first to have been cut to fit the cavity, its transparent back repeating the cell’s angles. When its legs shift, the entire cell appears to flex, though nothing in the stone does. Its body contains a dark, tightly folded gut and a delicate arrangement of muscles around each leg: an astonishing amount of machinery devoted to movements that cannot alter its surroundings by so much as a grain.

Other mites cling upside down, or what would be upside down if the chamber possessed a convenient orientation. Some graze with their whole faces pressed to the stone. Some are engaged in an obscure mite-courtship of touching forelegs and aligned backs. No display succeeds, and none fails. Success and failure would both require a next posture.

The blind white worms occupy the narrowest places. They are not merely in the passages; in several places, they are the passages. Their bodies curve around angular grains with no space in which to turn, their heads resting against their own flanks or against the armored side of some other animal. To call them trapped would imply an open place from which they had come. There is no such implication in their bearing. Each maintains a strong, slow grip on the mineral around it. Contractions gather in the pale muscles and spend themselves without translating the body elsewhere. Through the thinner worms, a red vessel can be seen keeping its small, steady pulse. Their tightening around the grains gives the choir much of its depth: a gritty pressure below the ticking and rasping, felt more than heard.

The snails seem softer than anything else except the green scales. Their shells have the delicacy of gel and none of the reassuring hardness that shell usually promises. Yet nothing presses them out of shape. The three sharing one cell lie with their translucent whorls touching, each spiral distinct, each animal afforded exactly enough room to remain entire. Their eyes are dark points at the ends of tentative stalks. One works its rasping mouth across the clay film; another has the edge of its shell against a crystal face, so that every minute adjustment of its body produces a fine, glassy note. A third is partly associated with one of the ribbed mucus tubes, though it would be impossible to say whether it is emerging, withdrawing, or simply occupying the posture between those ideas. The tube opens onto the back of a winged creature. It is a doorway with no available threshold.

The winged creatures are the most numerous and the most various. Some have four narrow wings edged with hairs finer than the light’s blue seams. Some carry lacquered wing-covers over bodies almost entirely transparent. Some are little more than a pair of dark eyes, three pairs of mechanical legs, and a trembling quantity of wing. All hold their flight surfaces half-open. This is not readiness. Nothing in them is gathering itself for departure. Half-opening is simply the complete and permanent expression of their wingedness.

They sing without throats. Wing-cover rasps against wing-cover; a leg draws across a ridged abdomen; the edge of a wing touches a snail’s shell and makes it answer. One produces a sound like a pin drawn slowly over glass. Another maintains a dry, dense whirring. A third, almost hidden beneath a springtail’s body, contributes a pulse so slight that it exists mainly as vibration in the bodies touching it. Two face one another with antennae inclined and forelegs lifted in the intricate attitude of courtship. Their wings shiver violently. Neither advances, retreats, accepts, or rejects. The display is not a failed version of courtship elsewhere. Here, display itself is the whole occupation.

There is no melody, because no note yields its place to another. Every apparent gap is already filled by a smaller sound: the cleaning of a mite’s foot, the minute contraction of a worm, the tick of a springtail’s mouthparts against clay. The loudness comes from this absolute adjacency, not from any single powerful voice.

The six liverwort scales are not pastures, although animals graze on them. On one, two mites and a springtail work their mouths with every appearance of appetite; not a green cell is lost. On another, a half-wing cleans one wing against the soft edge, leaving neither a mark nor a polished place. The plants do not spread beneath this attention. They do not feed the multitude in any measurable way. Their greenness is simply present, intense and difficult, and the animals’ mouths are present around it.

The exposed root hair in the roof serves briefly as an illusion of a road. Several feet are set along its pale length, and a worm’s blind head rests close to its tip. But it leads only back into stone. Nothing travels it. Nothing turns toward it as toward an exit, and no root follows it down. Its softness is used without being understood as refuge.

Likewise, the little structures never quite become homes. Beneath the dome of glued silt, a winged creature holds one cover raised against the curved ceiling, giving its own rasp a harder tone. A mite at the rim has a foreleg set against a single grain in the posture of a builder, but there is no next grain to be placed. The two cast legs and their mica flake make an arch beneath which nothing can pass: the far side is occupied by the flank of a worm. Near the spiral scored in the clay, one of the soft snails is asleep, or as near sleep as is possible without any change of state. Its stalks are half-drawn. A faint movement remains in its foot. It does not complete another circuit, and it does not wake to explain the first.

A natural history of the place would therefore have to dispense with nearly everything that ordinarily makes natural history possible. There is no food web to trace, no succession of inhabitants, no distinction between the young and the old that would promise development. Mouths work, but nothing is consumed into absence. Bodies court, but nothing follows from courtship. The cast-off legs do not require a carcass or even a completed moulting; they are simply two more articulated objects among the articulated living. Even hunger, if anything here feels it, can neither deepen nor be relieved.

This does not make the multitude tormented. No one claws at the enclosing chert. No one is crushed beneath the crowd. No one starves while performing the gesture of feeding. Their busyness is not a collection of failed efforts, because failure belongs to actions that might have been completed. Each animal is wholly absorbed in the small condition of its own body: this antenna against that grain, this shell touching that shell, this wing held at exactly this angle.

Nor are they a single organism. The mite does not know the snail’s spiral. The worm has no sense of the winged multitude above its back. No command passes through them, and no labour is divided for a common good. Their unity is more accidental and more complete than cooperation. The snail’s shell is the mite’s resonating wall; the worm’s flank is the springtail’s boundary; one creature’s private grooming supplies another creature’s continuous vibration. Each life remains stubbornly particular, while none can be separated from the physical fact of the rest.

If the choir itself is counted among the inhabitants, it is the only one without a body of its own. It lives in the contacts: claw to silica, shell to shell, wing-cover to air, soft muscle to hard grain. It has no score, no conductor, and no need of an outside listener. It is what their separate aliveness sounds like when there is no space left between one life and another.

Outside, the nodule gives no sign of any of this. Within, the springtail grooms, the mite mouths its point of light, the blind worm holds its grain, the snail sleeps beside the spiral, and the half-wings maintain their brilliant almost-flight. Every heart is visible. Every note is taken up by another note. Nothing is accomplished, but nothing is idle. The multitude is entirely occupied with being alive, and the black stone stays cool in the palm.
