The basin’s most consequential inhabitants never bellow.

They are the seamroots: plants whose black-red fans and armored climbing stems are only the smallest, most exposed portions of their bodies. The rest lives in the fractures. A seamroot enters stone as a thread, swells on intercepted moisture, and wedges its way downward until the rock fails. Later roots bind the broken pieces together. Thus the same organism can open a cliff and make a hummock from its debris.

Seamroot leaves are shaped to catch mist. Water gathers in their waxy channels and runs toward the stem, though much of it is stolen on the way. Mites stand in the grooves to drink. Minute, soft-bodied things shelter where leaf meets leaf, emerging to scrape at the film of fungal growth that develops there. A plant may support several generations of tenants without any of them touching the ground.

Between the seamroots grow jarstems, their swollen joints cool inside even when their outer skin is hot with dust. Insects tap the thinner tissue at the joint seams. Larger animals tear the stems open. The plant’s defense is not to remain intact but to grow in such crowded profusion that no single wound matters. Folded strap-leaves provide other refuge. Their narrow dry-season tubes contain whole little populations of legless larvae, leaf mites, and hunters small enough to pursue them through a space no wider than a finger.

The mats bridging hollows are likewise inhabited throughout their thickness. Their upper surface feels firm; below it is a damp fibrous darkness full of shed root skins, droppings, insect cases, and white fungal cords. Some animals there have no eyes and almost no pigment. Others are fiercely colored, though nothing ever sees the colors. They navigate by vibration and by the odors of particular rots. When a herd animal’s hoof plunges through, the mat does not merely tear. For a moment, an entire buried community is exposed to air, light, and appetite.

In the brown-green twilight, flowers cannot rely on being noticed.

The cupbells remain closed while they are being pollinated. Each is a leathery, hollow chamber with a narrow seam in its petals. Its pollinator, the keyfly, hovers against it and changes the speed of its wings until the chamber begins to resonate. Only then does the seam flex enough to admit the fly’s forelegs. Inside, it finds a smear of thick nectar and is dusted with pollen before forcing its way out.

Different cupbells answer different wingbeats. A keyfly can worry at the wrong flower until it exhausts itself without opening it. At dusk, patches of vegetation tick and shiver with insects testing blossoms, while unsuccessful flowers remain shut and silent.

Other plants have entered into an equally exact arrangement with the herds.

Herd-pods grow along repeatedly used corridors, often at the height of a browser’s shoulder. When mature and dry, their seed chambers are tense, woody, and tuned to low vibration. A close herd-bellow makes the pod walls shudder; after enough exposure, the seams split and the seeds are flung down among hooves. Some are eaten. Some are crushed. Enough fall into punctures in the root felt or adhere to wet hides to make the arrangement worthwhile.

A pod soaked by rain becomes temporarily unresponsive. It must dry again before sound can open it. Its seeds can then wait in dust for the water that will finally make them germinate.

The rumblebacks themselves are heavy, wedge-bodied browsers with small tearing mouths and bare, sloping shoulders. They do not crop vegetation neatly. The animals at the front of a herd seize mouthfuls while forcing aside stems; those behind strip what has already been bruised. Creepers catch on their shoulders and are dragged until they snap. Old sap, dust, and plant fragments accumulate on their hides in a crust continually scored by new wounds.

A herd is constantly counting itself by sound.

The adults’ lowest calls pass through flesh and root mat more effectively than through dense foliage. Young animals answer with shorter, higher notes, often while already running to catch up. Individuals recognize one another not simply by pitch but by rhythm and by the small irregularities of damaged throats. When corridors divide, groups may separate for hours and then find one another again by following answers transmitted through several compartments of growth.

Their bellows are not always alarms, and their alarm is not always a bellow. A single animal discovering water may call repeatedly. A herd compressed by panic may become almost silent except for the knocking of bodies and the high cries of separated young. During a rattlewing emergence, when wing-hiss and feeding noise overwhelm their contact calls, rumblebacks often crowd together until their combined movement breaks a new path.

Every herd travels with a disproportionate share of the basin’s smaller life.

Flies gather at the moisture around their eyes and at wounds in the shoulder crust. Other insects follow for dung, shed skin, or the creatures attracted to both. Fine-winged hunters take these insects on the wing. Still larger hunters move above them, visible only when a gap passes through the swarm. Rumblebacks are therefore never merely consumers of foliage. They transport pollinators, distribute seeds, churn dormant eggs into water pockets, and carry feeding grounds with them.

They also leave animals behind. A young rumbleback that drops into a narrow sink may call for a long time after the herd has passed. Such sinks acquire their own inhabitants: flattened scavengers able to live on intermittent falls from above, larvae that breed in the moisture of decay, and fungi that spread through whatever the scavengers leave. A sink can remain ecologically busy long after the cries at its bottom have stopped.

The rattlewings are among the few creatures capable of temporarily outweighing the herds’ influence.

Their wingless young feed within thick leaf joints and soft climbing stems. Usually their damage is scattered. In an emergence year, whole stands of foliage yellow, split, and collapse under them. Then the survivors climb toward warmth and break from their old skins in such numbers that branches bend with the concentration.

The winged adults eat little. Their business is brief: call, mate, lay eggs, and be hunted. Males produce the dry rattles that give the animals their name; females are quieter but heavier, and their arrival on already loaded branches often brings the branches down. Their discarded skins remain caught in foliage, clicking faintly whenever the vegetation moves.

Browsers panic partly because the swarms foul their eyes and breathing passages, but also because the sound makes distance and direction unreliable. Longhands descend to feed on fallen females. Ground birds work through the drifts of bodies. Shriekwings gather above the opening canopy. For several days, nearly everything seems to be either eating rattlewings or eating something distracted by them.

The shriekwings occupy almost every hunting height.

The smallest have furred bodies, narrow membrane wings, and a flight full of abrupt checks and reversals. They hunt among the insect halos of the herds. Larger species roost against trunks in close-packed clusters, so that a tree may appear to have grown a pelt. When disturbed, the pelt detaches itself in pieces and becomes a storm of wings.

Their screams are not wasted noise. A shriekwing calls, folds its broad ears briefly against the returning blast of its own voice, then opens them to the altered dark. It reads branches, prey, and the movements of other hunters from the returning fragments. In pursuit it may fall silent until the last instant.

The broadest shriekwings call less often. Their presence is more commonly inferred: midges drop, keyflies abandon their flowers, and small aerial hunters swerve away from an absence moving through them. Around their roosts, discarded wings and other leavings collect in the root forks. These deposits produce flushes of sweetrot, a soft fungal growth that smells almost fermented and attracts beetles before it has finished spreading. The beetles bring mites; the mites bring their own predators. By the time the fungus collapses, its former body has become a crowded feeding ground.

Longhands hunt through all of this without belonging wholly to any one layer.

They are light canopy omnivores with long, narrow fingers and an ability to cling to stems too slight to support their whole bodies for long. Their loose family parties keep contact by tapping wood and lianas. A vibration passing along a climbing stem can be felt several trees away by an animal holding the same stem with one hand.

They eat insects, soft fungal growth, eggs, fruit, and the watery tissue of jarstems. They also investigate anything newly broken. Their curiosity is useful but not cautious: longhands are taken by large shriekwings, injured in canopy falls, and occasionally trapped when a root mat or intertwined mass of climbers gives way beneath them.

Their seed-opening is a matter of judgment rather than equipment. A longhand wedges a hard seed into a forked root and strikes it with a convenient stone. The fork is crucial. One too springy lets the seed jump free; one too rigid allows the blow to pulverize kernel and shell together. Young animals watch adults, steal seeds from poorly chosen forks, and repeat the procedure with leaves, soft sticks, or stones too large to control. Some become skillful. Others continue to rely on easier food.

The stone is dropped when the hunger has been answered. The root fork is not improved. Another longhand may use it later, or the seamroot may grow through it and close the fork entirely. Knowledge of the technique can pass between generations while every physical trace of a particular meal disappears within days.

Thornbirds practice an equally temporary craft on the ground.

They are compact, strong-legged birds that hear the chewing of larvae inside softened wood. A bird may stand motionless beside a fallen trunk for some time, turning its head through small angles before making a single exploratory opening with its beak. If the larvae lie beyond reach, it may pick up a fallen thorn and probe with it, withdrawing the thorn now and then to take whatever clings to the point.

A suitable thorn is used intensively and then abandoned. If it bends, the bird does not repair it. Young thornbirds often try leaves or splinters first and appear perplexed when these fail. Adults sometimes feed near them without intervening, except to drive them away from the richest part of the log.

The rare rains reveal how many inhabitants have been present only as possibilities.

Dry hollows contain eggs that can outlast the disappearance of every trace of water. Some hatch at the first wetting; others wait until they are submerged. The first larvae strain spores and organic dust through combs on their limbs. Later hatchlings include slender predators little more than jaws, gut, and a violent tail. A pool can become crowded, then clearer, then crowded again as one brood consumes another.

Those that mature quickly enough produce winged adults. These mate over water already shrinking at the edges and fasten their eggs beneath stone lips or deep in drying root felt. Stranded larvae are taken by thornbirds, beetles, and longhands. What remains is worked into the mud by hooves and eventually into dust.

For a short time, the basin’s inhabitants rearrange themselves around drinking, laying, feeding, and escape. Rumblebacks foul pools almost as soon as they find them. Shriekwings hunt along temporary streams. Seeds that may have waited through several herd passages split their coats. Then the water goes, and each species returns to its own method of waiting.

When the great silence comes, none of them mistakes it for absence.

Keyflies settle beneath closed flowers. Rattlewings clamp their wings. Rumblebacks draw their young inward and stand with their weight low. Longhands cease tapping and hold the stems that connect them. Even the shriekwings stop calling, deprived for a moment of the returning sounds by which they know the dark.

The crowded stillness may end in rain, in a fracture shifting under the root felt, or in nothing more dramatic than the slow resumption of wings. Whatever happens, the noise rebuilds unevenly: a drill note, a hoof in litter, a seed pod cracking, an answering bellow several compartments away.

Nothing here maintains itself by remaining separate. The seamroot’s tenants drink its water; the herd opens the pods that feed on its passage; the hunters feed the fungus beneath their roosts; the fungus feeds the crawling life that will enter the next fallen trunk. Even technique survives chiefly in attention, memory, and imitation.

The basin’s inhabitants are not simply voices within its weather. They are how that weather is made.
