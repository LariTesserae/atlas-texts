Most of the Last Level’s population lives at or below the apparent surface. Its stillness is largely a failure of scale.

Press a hand into the mat and the first movement is almost too small to see. Transparent grazers, each carrying its few organs like grey seeds in a drop, work along the boundaries of aging cells. They eat tissue that has already begun to loosen from its neighbors, and the mat closes behind them without a scar. Black, many-legged inklegs flatten themselves into water films and swim between leaves. Where they gather around a pump intake, they look like a crack in the stone until the crack changes its mind. Soft burrowers move through the deeper root layers, feeding on exudates and abandoned tissue. Their passages do not remain empty. Living roots follow the chemical trace of a burrow, fuse along its walls, and sometimes turn it into a vessel.

Much of the Last Level’s plumbing may therefore have begun as the itinerary of something small looking for food. Nothing was designed for circulation, but certain paths were used so often that circulation became necessary to everything around them.

The pump-stands are habitats, but they are inhabitants too. A stand is not exactly one plant, nor is it usefully several. The visible columns have no oldest wood and no indispensable chamber. Valves thicken, wear, and are replaced; stems fuse; failed tissue is eaten where it stands. The continuity of the organism lies in pressure, root connection, and rhythm. Three columns rising together may be independent above the mat and indistinguishable below it. Animals recognize a stand less by its shape than by its particular sequence of claps, pauses, and sighs. That sequence changes with age, but slowly enough to serve as an address.

Inside the chambers live the valve-keepers: flat, bone-white animals shaped somewhat like folded leaves. During compression they cling in crevices or press themselves against the chamber walls. During intake they hurry over the valve lips, biting away small irregularities and clearing inklegs from the seals. The stand feeds them with sap released around the chambers. A stand with too few keepers becomes leaky and arrhythmic; one with too many begins to lose valve tissue faster than it can grow it, and the excess keepers are driven into the outflow, where eels and waders wait.

Their larvae can survive being vented in cold mist. Most come down within the same basin, but a few travel far enough to colonize another stand. A newly matured pump may thus acquire its maintenance animals before its crown has fully opened.

The heavy-looking hoverers are leadwings. Their wings are dark because each is a mesh of black veins around almost nothing: clear membrane, a little water, and air. They do not truly hover for long. Their flight is a succession of small, arrested falls, assisted by the pressure plumes around venting crowns. Between one exhalation and the next they often hang from a frond, wings folded and draining.

They are sensitive to the electrical change that precedes the opening of the pale flowers. A leadwing can arrive just as a flower fills with spores, feed for the few minutes before it collapses, and move on to the next. Many spores are digested. Others survive in the gut or lodge in the wing grooves, to be deposited beside another pump-stand or in the shallow water carried on a grazer’s back. Leadwing larvae develop separately in leaf-held pools, each leaf axil a nursery with its own temperature, population, and population of hunters. At the approach of an eel, a larva may release its hold and drop into the root-water below rather than remain in open water.

The pale eels are never entirely confined to pools. When open water contracts, they withdraw along root vessels and become flatter, slower, and difficult to distinguish from the pale tissues around them. Pump-driven flow brings them back. In a pool they hunt largely by electrical interruption: an inkleg swimming across the root-grid’s faint current is more conspicuous to an eel than a dark body against dark water.

This is why a newly formed pool can appear empty for hours and then contain an eel without anything having crossed the visible ground. The hunter was already present in the saturated country beneath it. Under ordinary conditions, eels move only short distances with changes in pressure. Their real dispersal waits for the storms.

Of the animals visible from far away, the rillbacks are the most numerous, though this does not mean they are often seen. An adult is low, long, and rounded, usually less than a metre high but several metres from its blunt grazing lip to the end of its body. Its six broad feet discharge small currents as it walks. Its back forms a shallow basin in which water, bone-white liverworts, inklegs, and occasionally leadwing larvae travel together. A rillback is not merely wet-backed. It is a moving piece of the basin system.

Its grazing removes only the oldest accessible layer of the mat. For a short time the animal’s passage leaves a smoother, more reflective band; then roots rise through it and the band disappears. Rillbacks move slowly and can remain motionless between steps long enough for their back-water to settle. In the thin air, their economy is one of pauses. Even feeding is intermittent.

They travel in lines because a line is an efficient living circuit. A foot-current passes from animal to animal, and each repeats it with small alterations. There are signals for deep water, rich grazing, damaged root-flow, and the first electrical instability of an overproducing stand. There are also individual signatures: slight asymmetries in discharge by which one animal can be distinguished from another. Some signatures remain in a herd’s relay long after the animal that produced them has died and been absorbed by the mat. They recur at particular stands or along particular migration routes. Whether this is memory, mourning, useful procedure, or something for which those distinctions are too coarse is not evident.

Rillbacks also call, placing their low sounds carefully between pump-beats. Calves, however, learn early to answer a foot-current before they answer a voice. Voices can be imitated. During storms, they can also arrive from somewhere other than their source.

A migration route is remembered as a sequence of rhythms. One stand’s rapid double clap, another’s long pressure-sigh, the interference produced when two groups are heard across the same basin: these are the rillbacks’ landmarks. Their geography remains usable when cloud hides everything above the mat.

The mirror waders are more solitary and more conspicuous, though a wader can stand in silver water for an hour without appearing to be an animal. Its body is narrow and pale, supported on two long, dark legs. Its head is a horizontal blade. Often the reflection is easier to see than the body, and the wader seems complete only where the two meet at the water.

It hunts by counterfeit. A wader learns the hydraulic rhythm of its local stand and reproduces it with throat, feet, and small changes of pressure in the water. The imitation is usually imperfect in one precise respect: it gives the sequence that causes eels to rise toward an intake, but omits the final venting phase. An eel emerges expecting a change in flow. The wader takes it and then may remain motionless for the rest of the pump-cycle. Larger animals are rarely deceived for long, because no crown turns and no mist vents. Eels, feeling the signal through water and root, are less well placed to notice the omission.

Waders use the same talent to address one another across enormous distances. Two solitary birds may spend days exchanging versions of a stand’s rhythm, each answer slightly closer to the other’s tempo. They can be socially engaged across half a region while having no visible neighbor at all.

Some of the soft burrowers in the mat are not worms but young veilwings. They spend years below the surface, feeding, growing, and contributing their mucus-lined passages to the root network. At the end of that buried life, each seeks a pump-stand and climbs it, blind and heavy. In the shelter of the crown, the adult body unfolds. At first its wings resemble the extravagant growth of a storm: broad, translucent sheets veined with blue. As the water drains from them, they become grey, light, and almost indecently large for the body suspended between them.

An adult veilwing is mostly wing and sensory tissue. It hears pump-beats through the spars and feels electrical charge along the trailing membranes. It does not navigate by seeing basins. It navigates by listening to their interference and by feeling, dimly, the buried grid beneath cloud. It glides on slope winds and the pressure disturbances around stands, flapping only when necessary. Its food is spore-mist, leadwings, and the smaller animals lifted accidentally out of venting crowns.

At rest, a veilwing wraps itself around a column and looks like a dead frond that the stand has not yet reclaimed. Adults are solitary because each requires an enormous feeding range. They come together reliably only when the grid begins to overproduce.

For the animals, these storms are not simply catastrophes. They are irregular seasons of abundance, and each species reads their approach differently. Rillbacks feel the root gradients rise. Eels detect pressure arriving out of sequence. Leadwings respond to flowers opening before the usual signals. Valve-keepers withdraw deeper into their chambers. Veilwings appear from cloud, one at a time, sometimes converging on a stand before its visible crown has begun to accelerate.

Then there is more food than the ordinary landscape ever provides. Rillbacks crop blue-veined sheets of foliage and crack the hollow antlers for the fluid inside. Their back-gardens enter brief, uncontrollable bloom. Eels spawn in the new pools. Waders feed until their stillness deserts them. Leadwings gather so densely around the translucent bells that they resemble a second mist, and veilwings carry spores away in their wing grooves and bodies.

None of this is cooperation, but it has a common effect. What the mat cannot support as foliage is carried off as muscle, eggs, gut contents, and passengers. The animals’ appetites are among the means by which surplus becomes mobile before grounding.

Once location begins to fail, their behavior diverges.

Eels enter joined pools readily, but they test the water first. A surface connected to living roots carries a pulse, however confused; a pool that is only a shadow may have none. An eel will often keep its tail in the entrance water until its head has found current on the other side. Those that cross successfully may emerge several basins away, carrying eggs, parasites, and the chemical signature of another stand. This is their great opportunity and their great risk.

Veilwings use folded mist to cross country that would otherwise take days, but they do not remain airborne simply because the passage remains open. As the regional charge becomes continuous, they seek an old swell or a stand and fold their wings. A glider caught above the mat at grounding is less likely to return than one clinging to living tissue.

Rillbacks stop trusting visible herds. They close their lines on the oldest available mat, put the calves between adults, and maintain a steady exchange of foot-currents. If a reflected grazer steps out of a pool and joins them, they test it as they would test any stranger. If it can receive and return a current, the line may make room. At grounding, the line is often shorter again. The animals do not appear to require an explanation before resuming their journey.

Mirror waders cease calling altogether once sounds begin arriving before their sources. They plant their feet and strike only at prey that physically touches them. Leadwings crawl into leaf axils. Inklegs descend. Even the transparent grazers move deeper into the mat, though whether they respond to charge, pressure, or some chemical anticipation is unknown.

These are not theories about unstable geography. They are simply the storm behaviors of creatures whose ancestors survived by performing them.

Grounding does not neatly reverse the storm. It restores the difference between near and far; it does not necessarily restore the original occupants of either. An eel may remain in a distant pool. A veilwing may land carrying spores from a stand it has never visited in ordinary space. A rillback line may include an unfamiliar animal whose pulse-signature is nevertheless present in the herd’s oldest relays. Sometimes a wader remains beside its own reflection until rain troubles the water. Sometimes only one of them remains when the water clears, and there is no test for which has survived.

After the regional silence, the smallest lives resume first. Grazers return to the loosening cell walls. Inklegs investigate the intakes. A valve-keeper crosses a wet wooden lip and begins removing an irregularity no larger than itself. Then a rillback sends a cautious current through its feet. A wader counterfeits an intake. Somewhere above the cloud, a veilwing opens wings that still hold a faint blue brightness.

The closest thing to a settlement on the Last Level is a pump-stand with its keepers, nurseries, hunting grounds, and listening animals. The closest thing to a road is a current through continuous roots. The closest thing to a calendar is the interval between one grounding and the next. Nothing builds because nothing needs to remain separate in order to be useful, and nothing leaves monuments because even a body is promptly given back to growth.

Yet the inhabitants are not merely components. An eel has its own hunger and takes risks the grid does not require. A rillback herd preserves patterns whose original purpose may have been forgotten. A wader elaborates an imitation beyond what hunting demands. A veilwing can choose the wrong length of mist and vanish. The system depends on them without exhausting what they are.

A far stand claps. Beneath the wet vegetation, an answer travels without sound. The Last Level is once again almost empty, which is to say that everything living in it has resumed being difficult to see.
