The Roof only looks uninhabited. Most of its life is underfoot, inside the groves, or along the cliffs, and nearly all of it has been shaped by three necessities: to drink from the air, to keep hold of the ground, and to feel a change coming before it can be heard.

The green skin is not one being, but neither is it merely a crowd of separate plants. Fern rhizomes stitch through the thin soil; fungal cords bind them to rosette roots and wet stone. In places, water collected in one hollow can be drawn away along that buried web and appear, hours later, at the base of a plant several paces off. A torn corner of turf does not simply expose soil. It breaks a system of storage and exchange that may have taken centuries to establish.

The wax-leaved rosettes are the most conspicuous water-harvesters. Their leaves are hinged close to the base, though the movement is so slight that it seems at first to be a change in the light. When moist air is being drawn across the table, they open their pale, finely furred undersides. Mist gathers there and runs toward the central cup. When the current strengthens or turns dry, the leaves fold upward, protecting the stored water and presenting their smaller, waxier surfaces to the wind. A whole slope can pass from gray-green to emerald and back again without a drop of rain.

Their flowers live briefly and urgently. The whites, rust-reds, and bruised yellows are intended for eyes only a finger’s breadth away. Wingless mites and small, soft-backed crawling things carry their pollen, climbing from the root mat when a blossom opens and retreating when it closes. Other plants abandon close pollination altogether. They cast adhesive seeds into the root felt, or loose downy seeds during an exhalation and take their chances. Most of those seeds are lost. Some go over the cliffs. A very few strike wet vegetation far away, catch, and begin the slow work of becoming another green patch.

The groves are where this diffuse life becomes crowded. Many are older than any trunk standing in them. Trees grow, fall, and rise again from roots that have occupied the same sheltered fold or warm seam for centuries. Black bark holds moisture; pale fungi whiten the rotting wood; vines lay one wet surface against another until the interior becomes a complicated apparatus for intercepting fog. Water dripping within a grove is a form of wealth, and almost everything there has arranged itself around a drip.

Dew snails no thicker than a fingernail pairing travel the fronds, sealing themselves with a translucent membrane when the air turns. Small translucent hunters wait beneath wet bark, so flattened that they seem part of it. In the darkest root hollows live blind, many-legged animals that follow warmth and the taste of decay. None of them needs the Roof to become quiet. Their communications are taps, touches, chemical traces, and minute tensions passed along a stem.

Even the fissures have inhabitants. Rust-colored and sulfur-pale films spread where warm mineral air reaches the stone. Root-felt hangs into some openings as far as a light can be lowered. Around smaller mouths, silk-spinners construct nets that can be furled against the rock. While a fissure inhales, the net catches seeds, mites, and fragments of vegetation drawn toward it; when the current begins to turn, the spinner gathers the whole apparatus into a narrow gray packet and holds on. An old colony can make a well mouth appear bearded.

The ancient pools contain stranger evidence of connection. Some hold blind white swimmers, seldom longer than a finger, with limbs folded so closely against their bodies that they resemble moving roots. They feed on what settles into the water and retreat into crevices when disturbed. Occasionally, after a great exhalation, one is found in a basin that was dry the day before. Whether the swimmers travel through flooded passages below, survive as eggs in damp stone, or are sometimes carried alive through the air, no one has established.

The slate-colored browser is the animal by which the Roof is most often remembered. Those who stay there commonly call it a stonefoot; its palm-sign is four fingers pressed firmly down and held.

Its visible ears are small and can close almost entirely. Its more important senses lie lower. The broad soles of its feet are soft enough to mold themselves around roots and stone, and it can remain for long periods with all four planted, reading vibrations that a human notices only as a headache or an uneasiness in the chest. It is not predicting the weather in any general sense. It is noticing particular movements deep below it.

Stonefeet follow newly opened growth rather than seasons. One may spend days cropping a single bright patch of rosettes, then walk half a night to reach ferns that have just unfolded elsewhere. Mud and spores collect in the crevices of their feet, so their journeys bind distant patches of vegetation together. Their resting places compact the mat into shallow basins where dew later gathers. Even their absences become useful.

Adults are usually alone, though a young stonefoot remains with its parent for years, learning sheltered crossings, dependable drip basins, and which groves are likely to be stripped when it arrives. They have no effective call and apparently no need of one. When two meet, they generally stand close without touching, then either feed together or move apart.

Before a hush, a stonefoot often changes its stance. It spreads its feet, lowers its body, and stops chewing. People who know the Roof watch for this. The animal is not always right, but it is right often enough to be worth obeying.

The long-winged shadows at the rim belong more to the cliffs than to the table. They launch by falling and rise on the warm air pressed upward along the rock. Their wings are fringed with fine, separately mobile filaments that register small changes in the current. They feed on seed-down, insects, silk, and other light life carried over the edge, and they rest on ledges below the lip.

They do not cross the open sky above the Roof because they cannot afford to land there. Their wings are made for cliff air, not for taking off from level ground. A rimwing brought down on the table would have to drag itself days toward the edge. Thus they remain immense but marginal presences: inhabitants of the Roof’s abundance without ever standing upon it.

People are rarer still, and their presence is not continuous enough to make a history easy to read. At one time there may be a handful living long circuits between cells; at another, only seasonal visitors; at another, no one but those who left intending to return. The oldest cells need not have been built by the ancestors of the people who now repair them.

Those who remain are sometimes called cell-keepers, though they keep more than cells and do not all accept the name. They have no settlement, no central authority, and no single account of why they are there. Some came to study the wells. Some came for stone, plants, or solitude. Some stayed because a return journey became successively easier to postpone.

Their society consists largely of courtesies practiced at a distance. To sleep in a cell implies certain duties: clear the drip groove, leave the water vessel clean, replace cord if cord was used, dry what can be dried, and close the place properly. If food is taken, something useful should eventually be left, though not necessarily by the person who took it and not necessarily in the same cell. A keeper may know another’s knotting, tool marks, and characteristic way of bedding turf for twenty years before meeting the person responsible.

A village would require paths, fields, and the reasonable expectation of gathering. The Roof discourages all three. Instead, human life is dispersed into overlapping circuits. A circuit is not a territory. It is simply the set of shelters, groves, and water-collecting places whose failure someone has decided not to permit.

The keepers wear close clothing with nothing loose to snap or drag, and often broad, soft soles through which they can feel knocking and changes underfoot. Against the higher flue-notes they stop their ears with wax and fiber, though nothing keeps the bass out of the ribs. They carry low mist screens that can be folded and weighted quickly. They tend useful plants without making fields: a rosette transplanted beside a drip stone, fern starch protected from browsers, edible grove fungus encouraged on a fallen trunk. Some bring food from below and eke it out; others live entirely on what the Roof provides. No one lives richly.

Their palm-writing is not a crude emergency code. It can convey directions, debts, jokes, affection, and finely qualified disagreement. Knocking into stone is simpler and is used when sight is impossible or attention must be kept elsewhere: *water here; bad ground; wait; do not follow; I am within.*

Inside the cells, where voices can be heard, the keepers speak readily. They are not uniformly solemn or wise. They complain about damp bedding, argue over adzes, tell old stories badly, and sometimes discover that two people who have admired each other’s repairs for years cannot stand each other’s company. The Roof has made them careful. It has not made them agree.

About the hush, they differ in explanation but not in conduct. Some regard it as a coincidence among many passages; some as a single deep movement; some refuse to discuss it. All secure what can be secured and get low. A newcomer may lift his head in wonder when the roar stops. An experienced keeper caught beside him will already be flat, one hand gripping his clothing and the other buried in the root mat. No one tries to use those clear heartbeats to shout a message across the plain.

Afterward, people and animals emerge into a landscape that appears almost unchanged. A keeper unfolds a mist screen and finds it full of grit and petals. A stonefoot resumes feeding in a patch turned suddenly emerald. A white swimmer circles once in a new pool and disappears into a crack. Along the rim, wings tilt into the rising warmth.

Then someone resets a drip groove, replaces a length of cord, and walks on. From a little distance there is nothing to see but knee-high green, stone, and mist. The Roof looks empty again—which is only to say that most of what lives there has succeeded at not being noticed.
