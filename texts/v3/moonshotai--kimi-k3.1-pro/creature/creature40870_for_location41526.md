## What Lives in Vey

A census taken at noon would misunderstand the place completely. It would record a keeper crossing a court, several figures asleep below a dwelling shaft, perhaps a crab colony if the census-taker happened to see one of its shells move. It would miss nearly everything else.

Most of Vey’s life does not occupy open ground or even open water. It occupies boundaries: hot brine against cool condensate, wet stone against air, the lower surface of a pipe, the last hand’s width of a channel before it disappears into salt. The visible animals are only the largest inhabitants of those margins.

The oldest are invisible. In the rock fractures, and wherever seepage spreads thinly enough to meet oxygen, microscopic communities draw their energy from iron, sulfur, and other minerals carried up by the hot water. They have no use for the white sunlight. Some form films so clear that their presence is known only by a slight drag on a fingertip; others make rust-colored threads along a seam or a pearly roughness on submerged ceramic. They are not plants, and they leave no green stain. A channel may look freshly scoured and still be densely inhabited.

Farther down, life becomes slower. Organisms lodged in isolated pockets of black glass and salt may wait years between one useful seepage and the next. The well engines occasionally bring up traces of them: a cloudiness in a sample, an unexpected film inside new pipe. Keepers disagree about how deep the living rock goes. They agree only that the pumps have never reached its bottom.

Other microscopic populations live on what the larger animals leave behind. They gather in the dark gut of a dead eel, beneath a crab’s discarded shell, in the brief organic richness around a wader’s droppings. This is why Vey’s water can remain so clear without being empty. Matter disappears quickly into bodies too small to see, then reappears as food.

The brine fleas are the first animals a visitor is likely to notice, though not the first to understand. Their name is an old approximation. They do not bite, and their relation to anything called a flea elsewhere is doubtful. Each carries a minute, hard shell around a body of almost nothing. The bright points seen in the shallows are flashes from those shells as the animals turn.

They gather most thickly where the chemical films are richest. A hand held motionless among them feels a faint, irregular ticking, not at the skin but just short of it. Their lives are brief and enormously numerous. Some hatch, feed, and release their own eggs between one Turning and the next; others wait as dormant specks in seams that may remain dry-looking for days.

They are sensitive to changes too small for Vey’s older gauges. Before a warm outflow fails, they may draw away from its mouth. Before condensate begins to spread across a pan, they rise into its advancing edge. Keepers who work the same field for years learn to read these movements without quite being able to explain them. A newcomer sees stippled water. An old keeper sees that a buried valve has begun to cool.

The glass eels live in routes rather than pools. Even the adults, some as long as a forearm, are rarely seen whole. Usually there is only a dark gut-thread slipping behind a crust of gypsum, or the quicksilver line of a spine passing through inspection glass. Their transparency makes distance difficult to judge. An eel apparently suspended in a deep cistern may actually be resting against the near wall.

They graze the microscopic films when young and take brine fleas throughout their lives. They also consume smaller eels, especially where a crowded outflow suddenly loses oxygen. Nothing in Vey is made innocent by being transparent.

Their breeding happens mostly out of sight, in warm fissures and neglected conduits. The keepers know its results better than its circumstances. After certain pressure changes, young eels appear at an outflow in such numbers that the water seems to acquire a second current inside the first. They enter bypasses, roof channels, overflow pipes, and the narrow spaces beneath paving. Some are carried into pans from which no eel could return. Others find the old routes downward.

A pipe full of eels is not necessarily considered blocked. Nor is an empty pipe considered clean. The distinction depends on flow, season, and what is waiting elsewhere. Keepers have been known to delay an urgently requested repair while arguing over whether a transparent mass behind an inspection window represents a migration, a spawning refuge, or simply too many eels.

The mirror crabs are less elusive. Their colonies have no queens and no shared shelters; a colony is merely the visible shape of a good place to feed. Dozens may gather along one ledge while the apparently identical ledge beside it remains empty.

Their reflective armor is built slowly from the water itself. Mineral material is laid down over the shell in thin, bright layers. In the hottest outflows, where the animals must shed heat quickly and food is abundant, the shells become chalky silver or nearly white. In cooler channels they take on bronze and smoky brown. Old crabs are often patchy, their shells recording earlier homes and injuries.

A crab at noon is not necessarily resting. It may be holding its broad back at a precise angle to the sun while its underside remains in a film of seepage. Its bristled feeding parts continue to work, drawing in fleas, microbial film, and any eel eggs exposed by the flow. When it moves, it usually does so sideways by a few deliberate joints, then settles again.

Their molts remain after them. At first the discarded shells are perfect, weightless mirrors. Later they cloud, flake, and become indistinguishable from the pale fragments around old condenser foundations. Soft, newly molted crabs hide beneath wet ledges. These are among the few animals in Vey that children are expressly forbidden to handle, not because they are dangerous, but because a child can too easily leave a permanent fingerprint in a shell that has not yet hardened.

The waders make all the other animals seem busy.

They are not birds, despite the name. They have no feathers, and the smooth pale skin over their bodies becomes faintly translucent where it stretches across a joint. Their long legs lift most of the body away from the heat of the rock. The feet are darker than the rest of the animal, broad and dull-surfaced, and can remain on paving that would blister a human hand.

The narrow feeding limbs are usually folded so closely beneath the body that a resting wader appears incomplete, like a piece of apparatus with an essential part removed. When it hunts, those limbs unfold separately. One may enter the water while the other remains braced against a pipe rim. There is no stab, no splash, and usually no visible struggle. An eel is simply no longer in the channel.

Their eyes are protected by clear membranes that can be drawn across without any change in posture. This adds to the impression that a watching wader is not watching. Its stillness is both hunting method and heat discipline. It waits for the small disturbances made by transparent prey, disturbances a person generally cannot see at all.

The young are seldom encountered. Now and then, after a Turning, one may be found standing on a cool shaft ledge below an adult, its legs still too thin to lift it properly. The adult does not appear to guard it. It does not appear to ignore it either.

Adults follow long routes between well-fields and districts. Individual animals become known by a missing foot joint, a cloudy membrane, or a habit of standing on one particular gantry. They are not tame. They do not come for food, and they move away if crowded. But they tolerate keepers as they tolerate pumps: large, intermittent presences whose habits are worth learning.

People are the newest conspicuous inhabitants, and the only ones who regularly mistake the town for something separate from its water.

They are fewer and more various than the white clothing suggests. Some families have kept the same shafts for generations. Others come from the lower country for a term of work and leave before they have learned the difference between one silence and another. There are engine keepers, pipe setters, ceramic workers, water testers, cooks, teachers, caregivers, and people whose principal skill is remembering which apparently abandoned conduit still supplies a distant cistern.

The surface silence does not mean that they are solemn. In the cooled rooms below, they argue about repairs, inheritances, food, and the unreliability of neighbors. They play tiles, mend clothing, compose excessively long complaints, and sing when a shaft gives the voice a pleasant resonance. A meal can be noisy by Vey’s standards. The thick walls simply prevent most of it from reaching the plateau.

Vey grows nothing, so much of the people’s food is brought up from below. To this they add eels, crabs when they are plentiful, mineral salts, and preparations made from carefully tended microbial cultures. The nearest thing to a household garden is a row of covered ceramic jars in a cool room. Their contents are fed and adjusted through changes in brine, condensate, warmth, and air. Some cultures are used for food; others are kept to reseed a cleaned channel or condition new ceramic. Households inherit them, divide them, and defend their particular qualities with surprising fierceness.

Residents stop expecting to be dry. Salt forms at their collars while their cuffs remain damp. They learn to sleep through engine vibration but wake when a familiar seep stops. A newcomer hears silence; an experienced keeper may hear that one small sound is missing from it.

Three Turnings give the day its ordinary grammar. People make appointments for after the second Turning, promise to finish work before the fins darken, or postpone a difficult conversation until water is running from the roofs. Children learn the pressure cycle before they learn the formal names of the wells. They also learn the water law by breaking it. Nearly every child in Vey eventually builds a small dam from ceramic offcuts. Nearly every child is then made to stand beside it and watch how many separate places the backed-up water had been supplying.

The people are not uniformly gentle with the other inhabitants. They eat them, clear them from drinking cisterns, curse them when they complicate repairs, and sometimes destroy a colony because a district has failed to plan around it. Vey’s restraint is not the result of universal tenderness. It comes from older experience: a sealed passage tends to become several more expensive problems elsewhere.

Thus animals appear in maintenance records alongside machinery. A service slate might read: *second gate slow; bronze shells beneath it; eels thick in the west bypass; wader on the outer pier.* All four observations may contribute to the same diagnosis. A wader abandoning a route can matter as much as a pressure reading. Crabs gathering on a previously unused ledge may reveal seepage before the paving darkens.

At a Turning, the whole scattered population briefly becomes easier to see.

The microscopic films spread into newly wetted seams. Brine fleas shift with the advancing condensate. Eels crowd the gate mouths. Crabs angle their shells toward the coming flow, and waders take their places above channels that were empty a minute earlier. Keepers come to doors and inspection points, some because there is work to do, some because cool water on the face is worth interrupting sleep.

For a short while, several districts may have people outdoors at once. Tools are passed, gates are opened, a child is called back from hot paving. There is no celebration and no common prayer. Sometimes nobody speaks at all. Water falls from the fins and tower rims, the animals enter their new feeding grounds, and the people watch for whatever has changed since the last Turning.

Then the heat returns. The keepers withdraw, the waders fold themselves back into stillness, and the smaller animals disappear into margins too narrow for a casual eye.

Vey has no central species and no inhabited center. Its life is a circulation with many temporary bodies: a film on ceramic, a flea in warm brine, an eel behind glass, a crab under a mirrored shell, a person carrying a cup toward the last drip from a roof. None of them possesses the whole route. Each survives by using part of it without entirely closing it to the rest.
