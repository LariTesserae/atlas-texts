## Life in the Intervals

Nothing native to the Crown builds a home.

There are no burrows, nests, hives, or root-bound islands. Even the deepest living thread occupies defects already present in the stone. This is not merely because the inhabitants are small or slow. A shelter on the Crown is only a shape the plate has not yet corrected, and a foundation is a temporary disagreement with the province’s final flatness.

Most native life therefore passes through two conditions: a brief condition in which it feeds, grows, and moves; and a much longer one in which it can be overlooked, buried, or transported without ceasing to be alive. A traveller may stand within a few paces of several species and see only gravel, tarnish, and dust.

A seamgrass felt is not one plant but a layered colony. The visible black-green filaments are its light-gathering surfaces. Beneath them lies a paler tissue that makes no roots in the ordinary sense. Instead, it extends conductive threads into microscopic fractures, establishing contact with deeper stone. Its two sources of energy meet only within that narrow base: sunlight from above, electrical difference from below. A felt is consequently always slightly starved in one direction or the other.

Where a felt is lifted, it does not leave torn soil. It comes away almost entire, leaving a patch of mineral marked by tiny, clean pits. The underside is brittle and intricate, more like a broken circuit than like flesh. Yet the breakage matters less than might be expected. Any sufficiently large fragment containing both light-gathering and conductive layers can, given years or centuries, close its damaged surfaces and begin again.

Its response to an approaching storm is not foresight. The front alters the electrical conditions to which the colony is already exquisitely sensitive. Filaments buckle in sequence, fold inward, and lock around the living layers at the felt’s centre. The whole organism contracts into one or several hard grey nodules. In that state it does not feed, and its remaining chemical activity falls below the useful resolution of most instruments.

The nodules are not seeds, though they often perform a seed’s work. A storm may crush all of them, carry one beyond the old felt’s horizon, or distribute thousands through flowing powder. Most come to rest where no growth is possible. Others remain buried until a later disturbance exposes them. A young felt may thus appear on ground that has no history of vegetation, its parent colony having vanished before any survey recorded it.

No reliable age can be assigned to an old felt. Its visible spread may represent a decade of growth after a storm, while the closed tissue at its centre is far older than the stations nearest it.

Under magnification, the felt is less solitary.

Its interstices contain minute translucent animals usually called contact mites. Dormant, they are indistinguishable from angular powder. Active, they move slowly along the filaments, feeding on exhausted outer tissue and on the charged films that form across damaged surfaces. Their grazing exposes fresh conductive contacts. Felts with abundant mites often grow better than felts without them, although the mites also consume living tissue when other food is scarce. The distinction between parasite and maintenance organism is not sharp.

When seamgrass closes, some mites are sealed within the nodules. Others remain outside and die. A nodule scattered by a storm can therefore carry not merely the possibility of a new felt, but a small portion of its former inhabitants. New seamgrass is sometimes an ecology from its first opening filament.

The charged films near stations support a different pasture. Some of these films are themselves alive: transparent, overlapping colonies known as station enamel. In thin growth they are visible only as a slight softening of reflected light. Older growth becomes grey and minutely blistered. It spreads across natural stone as readily as across station walls, though it is usually richest within the shallow outer ring.

Station enamel does not appear to use sunlight. It grows where small electrical differences repeatedly form and collapse, incorporating mineral from the surface into layers so thin that a skate can graze an area for years without visibly thinning it. Whether the stations deliberately provide the conditions it needs, or merely leak them, is unknown.

The plate-skate is the largest consumer of both pastures.

It looks less like an animal than like a piece of the Crown that has considered moving. Its back consists of broad, overlapping mineral laminae, grey where the local surface is grey, faintly ivory or brown where it has spent long periods feeding elsewhere. The colour is not quick camouflage. Dust and grazed mineral become part of the outer tissue, so that an old skate carries an imperfect record of its own travels.

It has no conspicuous head. The forward end is simply the end that moves first. Sensory tissue lies around the rim and beneath the dorsal plates, responsive to light, electrical change, and vibration transmitted through the ground. A skate touched lightly may show no response for several minutes. Then its limbs fold, its rim settles, and its plates lock so completely that the observer may begin to doubt the earlier movement.

When it walks, it does not crawl so much as redistribute its weight among many small limbs. Each limb ends in a cluster of hard, conductive bristles. The broad back remains nearly level. A journey may occupy an entire night and cover only a few body lengths. The evenly spaced dots left by the bristles are often the best evidence that anything has happened at all.

A skate feeds by settling over seamgrass or station enamel and extending a narrow ventral surface against it. It crops old filaments, mites, films, and loose charged mineral. Healthy felts usually survive this grazing; badly weakened ones do not. Around stations, a skate may make the same minute circuit for years, its track repeatedly interrupted by long periods in which it lies dormant and dust softens the outline of its back.

Nothing hunts it. The principal dangers of its life are starvation, burial, abrasion, and the ground opening beneath it.

Its reproduction is so slow that it was initially mistaken for disease. Along the rear margin of a well-fed adult, small closed lenses may form between the dorsal plates. Eventually these detach. They are not miniature skates, and most never become skates. Each contains a little living tissue within an inert mineral envelope. Limb development generally begins only after severe mechanical and electrical disturbance—conditions supplied, in nature, most reliably by a storm.

Most lenses are destroyed. A few are carried into newly stabilized country and there unfold into animals small enough to escape notice except under a lens. The storm that kills an adult may thus be the event that activates its offspring. Individual skates are not stormproof. Their kind persists because endurance and dispersal have been divided between different stages of life, and because neither stage requires the next calm to resemble the last.

A shield-wide skate beside a station may be immensely old. It may also be a comparatively young animal that has lived through an unusually generous interval. Size, like location, is a poor biography.

The whole ecology is correspondingly quiet. There is no chase across the plain, no pollination, no seasonal greening, no carcass promptly opened by scavengers. Dead tissue remains where it fell until mites or enamel make slow use of it, or until the plate takes it. The Crown itself is the final scavenger: crushing, mixing, transporting, and at last concealing every remainder beneath the restored grey skin.

Human life arrives with more noise, though nearly all of that noise remains enclosed.

No human lineage is native to the Crown, but there are surveyors and station keepers whose working lives have mostly happened there. Their pressure houses are low structures standing on broad feet, without foundations. Masts fold flat. Exterior cables can be released from within. A camp is designed not to withstand the plate’s movement indefinitely, but to stop being a camp quickly.

The keepers’ task is to preserve continuity of measurement where there is no continuity of terrain. They return instruments to coordinates, compare the restored surface with the ancient survey zero, track the empty centres of storm systems, and recover whatever equipment the last front has spared. A station requires no keeping in any domestic sense. What must be kept is the record.

Among long-serving keepers, *home* generally means the pressure house. *Address* means wherever the house currently stands. The distinction ceases to seem precious after the first major storm.

Their official maps remain almost blank. Their private logs are less austere. They record a felt opening near a coordinate that had been bare for fifty years, a skate’s dotted track ending beneath new dust, the reappearance of enamel on one side of a station and not another. A keeper may follow the same large skate through decades of observations, giving it a private name that never enters the survey. When it disappears, the name sometimes remains in the log long after the coordinate has ceased to be useful.

These attachments do not make the animals tame, or the stations hospitable, or the outer rings safe. They only make it harder to pretend that residence consists solely of instruments and return dates.

Every account of Crown life eventually reaches the question of whether the mineral plate should be included among the inhabitants. It has no observed cells, no individuals, and no offspring. Yet it responds to conditions, organizes itself across continental distances, transports material, expends and stores energy in ways still imperfectly understood, and restores one persistent state with extraordinary fidelity.

For survey purposes, it is classified as environment. Ecologically, it is environment with some of the powers of an organism: climate, soil, catastrophe, and grave. Calling it alive explains little. Assuming that it is simply inert explains no more. The small lives on its surface offer no evidence either way. They use the conditions available during the calm and close against the conditions that end it.

At evening after a front, a felt may begin opening at a coordinate that was bare stone the day before. One filament uncurls, then another. Within the old tissue, a few contact mites stir and become still again. Farther across the plain, a shield of grey dust shifts by less than a finger’s width as a skate unfolds one limb beneath it.

There is no call, no rustle, no visible abundance. For a while, however, the restored flatness contains several living things within reach of one another.

None of them possesses a lasting place. On the Crown, life does not hold one. It survives the loss of one.
