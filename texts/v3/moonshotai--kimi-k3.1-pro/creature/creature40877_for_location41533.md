Life in the Accretion City is easiest to find where the city has failed to remain dry.

At a persistent outlet, the film is not uniform. Closest to the water, where the stone is hottest, translucent cells live in scattered chains and brief iridescent patches. Farther out, fine colorless threads extend through the flow, taking energy from dissolved iron and sulfur. Beyond them, where the surface wets and dries irregularly, the life survives as a crust too thin to distinguish from scale. It crumbles when touched. Not all of it is dead. Some patches resume growth after rewetting, spreading again along the old boundaries.

The films do not depend on daylight. Their geography is chemical: a change in mineral content can stop them more abruptly than a wall, and a warm pipe may carry the same colony through several otherwise unrelated districts. Their generations pass while a freight hook descends the width of a hand. Over longer periods, their residues help bind glass dust and mineral salts into a gray rind. It is not soil. Nothing roots in it. But it is among the first material in the city that can properly be called sediment.

The sill-shell lives on these films.

An adult is usually no wider than a hand, though larger ones occur beside reservoirs that have not been drained for many years. Its porcelain-like shell carries growth lines too fine to see except when it is empty and held against the light. Beneath it, the body is flat and dark, with numerous jointed limbs folded so closely that the whole animal seems incapable of movement. On wet stone, however, it can dart with surprising quickness. Then it remains still for hours, drawing a comblike mouthpart across the surface and leaving behind a clean, narrow track.

It follows condensate rather than weather. A sill-shell may spend its active life within the shadow of one coolant main, traveling between a catch tray, a damp joint, and the crevice in which it shelters. When the stone begins to dry, it backs into the crevice and seals the shell’s rim with a mineral secretion. Inside, its body becomes increasingly still. It can remain sealed through intervals in which entire maintenance cycles are completed above it.

Not every empty shell is a corpse. Some split along a regular suture and are left behind as the animal grows. Others contain only a few dry membranes. From a step, there is usually no way to tell the difference.

The young appear only where moisture persists through several seasons. A dependable leak may therefore support a lineage, while the repair of that leak ends it. To a sill-shell, a coolant main is more permanent than climate; its failure is not a change in the weather but the disappearance of the world’s only river.

Other animals live in the lightless reservoirs: pale, threadlike forms found occasionally in filter beds, and soft knots of almost transparent tissue that contract when exposed to air. Some may spend their entire lives within one cistern. Draining the cistern produces both their remains and their complete fossil record.

None of this explains the clean filters.

The other inhabitants are known first by preference. A cable in one district is always given a little more slack than necessary, and the excess is folded in the same peculiar way. Elsewhere, catch trays are set fractionally out of level so that their overflow returns to one particular joint. Certain valve handles are left warm from being held; others have not been touched in years, although the machinery beyond them continues to be adjusted. These habits survive the replacement of the parts on which they are practiced. They are signatures. A visitor may come to recognize several residents without ever seeing one.

Eventually, one may be seen at work.

It does not step out of a doorway so much as resolve out of an arrangement of parts. A low body unfolds beneath a gauge shelf, or from the apparently empty frame of a chair. Several limbs take its weight in turn. Ceramic plates cover its back, their colors differing where repairs have been made: blue-black glass beside old gray tile, a patch of clinker, a thin copper seam. The material at the joints is dark and flexible. When a plate opens, there is a brief smell of wet iron rather than oil.

For want of a better term, the mobile ones may be called maintainers. The word is useful chiefly while they still move.

They are not simply animals wearing armor, nor quite machines that have developed habits. Some of their components have clearly been cast. Others have been deposited in fine layers, like shell or scale. They take current from the city and water from its cisterns. Mineral material for repair is carried internally, sometimes for years, before being added to a plate or joint. No two old maintainers are alike. Each bears the accumulated shape of its work.

The chairs fit their folded bodies, though not in the way a chair fits a human body. A maintainer may brace itself against the back while extending its limbs across a wall of valves. It has no single face to turn toward them. Heat, pressure, vibration, and small changes in electrical resistance are received through different parts of the body. Watching one read an instrument bank resembles watching someone listen, although most of what it attends to never becomes sound.

Speed is possible. A fractured main or sudden pressure loss can produce a flurry of pale limbs and a shower of scale from an old joint. But rapid movement causes damage that is difficult to repair, and there is rarely any advantage in it. Ordinary work proceeds at the tempo of the machinery. A maintainer may wait beside a gate for most of a season in order to make one small correction at the proper point in its closure.

The mobile population is small, and the summit has the smallest share of it. Most maintainers spend their active periods below the exposed streets, in galleries where temperature and pressure change less abruptly. A district may be well inhabited by their standards while every visible lane remains empty.

They are not, however, solitary.

Messages pass through the infrastructure as pressure patterns, interruptions of current, or minute vibrations impressed upon a pipe. Alarms travel quickly and are answered quickly. Other messages travel quickly but wait a long time for reply. A maintainer may finish a journey, compare several cycles of a pump, and only then respond to a question left in a return line months before.

A gathering therefore requires no square. Participants can remain in separate strata, connected by whatever conduits they share. To stand in an insulated hall and hear nothing is not to know that nothing is being said.

They recognize one another partly through these exchanges and partly through the character of their work. One eases pressure in gradual increments; another permits a brief knock before a gate seats. Such differences are more durable than bodies. When components are replaced, the preference remains.

New maintainers appear rarely. An older one may spend years preparing a small body, then much longer leading it through a circuit of tasks. At first the younger one repeats routes and adjustments so exactly that its work cannot be distinguished from its predecessor’s. Later, deviations appear. It chooses a different place to rest, leaves a different amount of slack, or begins paying attention to a sound the older one always ignored. Whether this is manufacture, apprenticeship, or reproduction is not a distinction easily applied from outside. What the new maintainer inherits most completely is a set of unfinished tasks.

With age, a maintainer’s range usually contracts. It remains longer in one cell. A temporary connection to a pump hall becomes permanent. When a limb fails, it may not be replaced; a fixed conduit takes over the limb’s function instead. The body becomes less a thing that travels through the district than a small, especially dense part of it.

Later still, the surrounding systems begin to do most of the moving.

A chair remains before a wall of numbered valves. Behind the wall, something continues to make choices that are not quite automatic. It responds to an unusual pattern of heat by altering a flow that no ordinary regulator controls. It preserves an old preference after every visible component associated with that preference has been renewed. In another cell, nothing responds at all, and the ceramic body is cold. Between these conditions are many cases for which no useful classification exists.

Stillness is not death. Even failure to move is inconclusive. The practical questions are whether a response comes, whether it remains particular, and whether anyone continues the habits when it stops.

Some of the oldest inhabitants may therefore be pump halls, sections of a pressure network, or warm volumes of rock containing several embedded galleries. Others may be no more than unusually elaborate machines. The city offers no reliable marker for the moment at which a resident becomes part of the residence.

Accretion does not simply drive this population upward. When a street is embedded, its useful connections are extended, rerouted, or transferred to a newer level. Deep chambers remain occupied while their doorways disappear. An individual may outlive the stratum in which it was last mobile and continue below several later districts. The youngest, emptiest parts of the city are often those nearest the sky.

Altitude does not seem to be their ambition. It is the residue of continuation: deep melt must be brought up, cooled, and discharged; foundations must remain stable; old conduits cannot always be abandoned without abandoning what still lives around them. They are making room for further maintenance. The room happens to lie above.

They disagree about small things for very long periods. Some seal every incipient channel, preserving the dry faces and keeping water in the plumbing. Others allow certain leaks to continue, observing what condensate does when left to repeat the same path. Sill-shells gather at these sites. Films spread. A pale thread becomes a shallow flute. The groove on a black wall may record a failed repair, a deliberate experiment, or the physical length of an argument.

A census of the city is consequently exact only about its smallest lives. Beyond the films and animals, every count depends on how much plumbing one is willing to include in a body.

On an ordinary day, visible life may amount to a damp sheen beside an outlet and an empty shell rocking slightly on a sill. Farther down, a maintainer completes a reply. Something older adjusts a gate without any separate limb moving. A preference survives another replacement. Warm stone closes a little farther around a conduit that remains in use.

Nothing crowds the lanes. Yet the deeper one goes, the harder it becomes to say whether one is entering a residence, a body, or a city. That distinction is easiest at the surface, and the surface is still very young.
