## The Narrow Multitudes of Anvess

Life in Anvess is abundant, but almost never continuous. It gathers where water can be persuaded to remain, and becomes active in the intervals when the basin briefly permits movement.

On new stone, the first inhabitants are usually invisible. Dawn condensation leaves a film too thin to shine, and within it mineral-feeding films begin to spread. Translucent mites follow; then threadlike larvae, then the small grazing things that feed on both. A slab can be geologically new, devoid of soil, and already contain several generations. Seams are consequently aged by wettings as much as by years. Two fractures cut on the same day may belong to entirely different worlds because rain crossed one and missed the other.

The visible vegetation builds on these precarious beginnings. Bladder moss is both reservoir and refuge. In drought it is a brittle black crust, easily mistaken for a stain left by an old installation. Wet it, and its cells open into clear cushions crowded with eggs, dormant animals, and the fine pale growth that binds them together. Fan-ferns stand above it like pieces of hydraulic machinery. Their pleats conduct dew toward central cups; their unfolding fronds make temporary gutters; their litter slows the escape of moisture into the warm air.

Veinwood is the closest thing Anvess has to a river. Its pale roots pass from fracture to fracture, joining pockets that may never exchange a visible drop. A stem at the dry end of a seam can remain alive because the rest of the network extends into shade, or beneath a slab, or around the buried footing of a condenser line. The visible stems do not reliably indicate the extent of the organism. In Senn, engineers have learned to ask where a veinwood goes before asking whom a proposed conduit might inconvenience.

Within this vegetation, water produces the impression of noiseless movement. Most of it is too small to see until it multiplies. Mirrorbacks graze through the moss with their mouthparts close to the surface and their weight distributed across delicate feet. A person approaching the edge of a bed may see thousands of shells cease glittering before hearing or seeing anything else: the animals have registered the footfall through the substrate and frozen together. When they resume, their reflections travel through the wet growth in small, restless flashes.

The moths live by another schedule. Through the bright part of the dim day they hang beneath fern fronds, their matte wings giving back almost no light. At humid dusk they detach themselves in such numbers that the seam seems to be losing its shadow. They drink from fern cups, mate in the sheltered air above the moss, and carry spores and minute eggs on their bodies. Their flights across open ground are dangerous not because the plain is full of predators, but because it offers no reason to stop and almost nothing with which to recover. Many never reach another seam. Enough do.

Rain eels make the briefest use of opportunity. Dry, they are easily overlooked: tough, dull threads lying in depressions or caught beneath a flake of mineral crust. Warm water restores them with alarming speed. They thicken, uncoil, and begin feeding on the small life released from moss and fern litter. Their urgency is not desperation in any meaningful sense; it is simply the shape of their lives. A pool that will last two hours permits leisure. One that will last eight minutes does not. They feed, mate where their kind requires it, lay in the deepest available film, and begin reducing themselves again before the last exposed water has vanished.

The long-legged listeners occupy the opposite extreme of tempo. They wait.

A listener among bare stones can be difficult to distinguish from a pale mineral rib. Its stillness is not merely concealment. It is listening with its feet and the plates beneath its body, sorting the vibrations of the living seam from the immense, recurring confusion of the engines. During quiet intervals it can detect the small, irregular tremors made by mirrorbacks. During an engine surge, that distinction disappears for its prey. Then the listener moves: not hurriedly, but with an exactness that makes twenty metres seem like a single decision. Afterward it may stand for another hour with one foreleg wet from the fern litter.

Even human installations acquire unofficial ecologies. A condenser vane is meant to surrender its water to closed channels, but an imperfect seal can support a thumbnail of moss. A warm joint beneath a reservoir pipe may hold droplets long enough for mites to gather. Moths shelter in the lee of survey markers. Spores cross the basin on maintenance clothing and pass through gates in the folds of freight cloth. A fault discovered because mirrorbacks have gathered beneath it may be entered in the maintenance record as a leak and in the research record as a colonisation event. Both entries are correct.

The people of Senn have made their own version of this concentrated, connected life.

They are intensely urban without being extensively spread out. Most of them live within nested shells, stacked courts, freight chambers, cistern levels, and planted terraces. Their streets often have ceilings. Their neighbourhoods may continue downward as readily as outward. From the plain, a district containing markets, schools, workshops, and several thousand sleeping rooms can present little more than a ceramic roof and a door.

Senn’s gardeners, water registrars, condenser keepers, gate clerks, and survey archivists are therefore not servants of separate cities. They maintain one habitat whose components happen to be far apart. The gardens are reservoirs that photosynthesise; the cisterns are gardens with the light and roots removed; the gates are infrastructure that makes an empty thirty kilometres socially smaller than an obstructed stairway.

Construction begins with this in mind. Before a new shell is enclosed, root wardens may already be setting fern mats into its outer terraces and marking the paths along which veinwood will be permitted to travel. Masons work around irrigation voids that will not contain water for months. Residents moving into an unfinished court sometimes wake to the smell of wet mineral dust and crushed stems before the interior partitions have acquired their final doors. The pruning is exact, but the result is not sterile. Roots swell, moss escapes its beds, and mirrorbacks discover that municipal terraces offer excellent grazing.

This gives the water offices their particular importance. Their ledgers measure more than stored volume. They account for root reserves, anticipated condensation, freight demand, emergency allocations, and the losses no instrument quite succeeds in explaining. Public readings are attended by people who can interpret a fractional adjustment in a condenser spire with the expertise other societies bring to grain prices or elections. Disputes are frequent, highly technical, and not always gentle. Yet they are conducted in rooms where a cup can be heard ticking as it cools, and even anger tends to acquire a clear, unshouted diction.

Senn is not universally quiet. Its markets clatter, freight levels knock and roll, and covered courts can become dense with conversation. The difference is that these sounds have edges. They belong to chosen interiors. Just beyond an open threshold, the basin may be loud enough to erase speech; one step inward, a leaking tap becomes conspicuous. People pause there to swallow, flex their feet, and let the pressure leave their bodies. Visitors often continue shouting for one or two words after entering. Children learn early that silence does not mean nothing is happening.

Their sense of distance is equally distinctive. A child may visit relatives thirty kilometres away through an ordinary arch every week, yet regard a walk of three kilometres over open slab as a serious expedition. Nearness is partly a matter of gate schedules. An outpost with a direct connection can feel closer than a court requiring several interior transfers. If a gate is taken out of service, the intervening plain does not suddenly become a road; it becomes an absence in the day’s arrangements.

The thousands who live in such outposts may rarely see more of the basin than a framed view from an observation slit. Their visible building can be no larger than a reservoir house, while inside are stacked kitchens, sleeping chambers, meeting rooms, repair shops, and gardens. This is not a population clinging to scattered shelters. It is a regional society occupying selected volumes and leaving the intervals materially alone.

Fieldworkers experience another Anvess. Surveyors, slab researchers, vane keepers, and outer gardeners work by exposure tables as well as clocks. They wear sealed ear protection and thick, replaceable soles; they carry dated maps, written slates, and more water than the dry air makes them think they need. Much of their communication is manual. A concise field hand-language serves at condenser lines and gate thresholds, and many people remain fluent in it indoors. Older surveyors whose hearing has been damaged may prefer it even in silence, especially when an engine surge has left the room feeling louder than it sounds.

New ground interrupts everyone’s assumptions. To a researcher it may be an unrepeatable sample; to a water office, a change in condensation; to a gardener, a possible root path; to a freight clerk, no immediate concern at all. Some residents speak of it as a birth. Others treat it as an engineering event requiring markers, measurements, and restricted access. Both groups buy the revised map. An old map is not necessarily considered false. It is simply a map of an earlier Anvess.

The city’s administrative patience comes from this distinction. Its institutions expect to endure, but they do not expect every fact beneath them to remain unchanged.

Nor do they keep the wild seams entirely outside. Moths gather in the damp air around condenser works. Mirrorbacks are tolerated on mature outer terraces and discouraged, with varying success, from newly planted beds. Cistern keepers inspect for organisms that have mistaken covered water for an unusually durable pool. When a listener appears at the edge of the western fern beds, pruning stops and the outer workers come inside. There is no general campaign against the animals. They are simply another schedule with which the city must occasionally coincide.

A small rain cell shows how differently these lives occupy the same event.

As the dark curtain approaches Senn’s western seam, a registrar notes its bearing. A condenser keeper watches the spires make their fractional adjustments. An outer gardener leaves a half-finished row of fern cups and steps through a silence boundary. Beyond the shells, warm rain begins striking one narrow band of stone while dust remains dry a few metres away.

The moss turns black, then translucent. Fern pleats fill and overflow. Mirrorbacks emerge in numbers that make the seam appear to brighten internally. In a shallow depression, water gathers around what had seemed to be a handful of dead fibres. The fibres soften. Rain eels rise through water no deeper than a hand and begin feeding before the surface has developed its first continuous reflection.

For the city, the event is minor. Covered channels accept a little water; cistern levels rise by an amount too small to alter the day’s planning. For the depression, it is the entire available world. Eggs are laid. Small animals are consumed. A listener extends one leg beneath the outer ferns and waits for the next engine surge to hide the vibrations of its approach.

The cell breaks apart before its runoff can establish any direction. Steam lifts. Exposed stone begins drying almost immediately. The pool contracts around a crowd of brief, furious lives, then separates into isolated lenses beneath litter and mineral flakes. By the time the western seam is again visible as a simple dark line from the plain, the eels have diminished into dormancy and the listener is motionless once more.

Senn’s ledger records almost nothing. The depression retains eggs, fed bodies, disturbed moss, and the beginnings of another generation. Both records are accurate.

This is how Anvess becomes inhabited without becoming full. Life persists in roots that cross from one wet pocket to another, in shells that close around moisture, in gel threads under dust, in spores on a gate clerk’s sleeve, in cisterns and ledgers and institutions capable of revision. It does not wait for the basin to acquire rivers, deep soil, or a finished shape.

Somewhere beyond the slab field, new stone cools beneath the bronze sky. At the next dawn it will hold moisture. Something already present in the air, too small to see, will make use of it.

And beneath roots, shells, sleeping animals, and the dense inward rooms of Senn, the space goes on ringing.
