## The Lives of the Bellows

Most lives in the Bellows have more than one address. A thing may be a fruit at dawn, a walker by noon, and a fixed patch of wax-green growth by the following week. Others remain rooted all their lives but are no less purposeful for it. They turn, constrict, listen, and spend their bodies slowly.

Because growth interrupts sight at every scale, vibration is often more useful than vision. A huskback may be deaf to a shouted warning yet feel a companion’s footfall through fifty paces of root felt. A folded kite receives an approaching fault as a tightening along its wing spars. Many seeds have no organs that correspond to ears at all, but their cases can distinguish a sustained note from a knock, and a knock from the irregular ticking of warm stone.

### The rooted multitudes

The corky thickets near the rim are less like stands of trees than crowds holding one another up. Their black leaves appear separate, but below them stems have fused into irregular conduits. Oil passes through these unions, though not freely or equally. A vigorous stem may draw from three neighbors and give little back; an old one may be narrowed at its sockets until it dries, stiffens, and becomes part of the support beneath the others. When a harvester cuts a stem, the surrounding growth sometimes seals the wound before the blade has been cleaned.

Their leaves are inhabited almost as soon as they unfold. The first arrivals are minute wax-suckers, pale and soft enough to fit inside the leaf’s pleats. Later come the gray grazers, which scrape away old varnish and outer tissue without usually reaching the living core. A heavily occupied leaf is therefore not necessarily a sick one. It may remain productive for a long time beneath its moving crust. When the leaf is finally exhausted, the grazers lock their legs together and walk off in a single gray sheet, leaving the stripped ivory skeleton behind.

The hollow-stem districts have a more exact social life. A bundle of sounding stalks is often one body, though it may have begun as several. It does not merely endure the note produced inside it. It alters that note. Wax thickens around a split lip; a side wall adds a rib; a narrow bridge grows across a throat until the passage whistles instead of booms. Some of these changes take days. Others follow a hard tremor within the hour.

Inside the stalks live the chordlings: dry-bodied, thumb-length creatures whose wings develop in narrow cases fitted to the curvature of the stem. They hatch only when a particular chord has held long enough. For a short while they crowd the open tops, then pour out to feed from ripe seed cases and carry their adhering dust through the district. If the parent stand changes pitch too early, the chordlings remain sealed. Borers eventually find them, and the stand receives no visitation that cycle. Plant and pollinator can fail one another without either having died.

The pale root-plateaus are the largest rooted lives. They have no obvious heart or principal trunk. Their tissues run in braided sheets over the fractures, sending fine members into warm stone and thick, sunlight-storing growth into the air. A section can be cut away, crushed, or carried off by huskbacks without settling the question of whether the plateau has been injured or merely divided. Some appear to be single organisms. Others are populations whose boundaries have become meaningless. All of them carry smaller inhabitants in their hollows: bark plates that unfasten and wander, brood chambers lined with seed-wool, and slow colonies of unthreaders reducing old tissue to powder.

### The winged and crawling weather

The crimson cushions produce the most violent outbreaks of animal life. Between their finger-length growths, winged carriers develop by the million. Before hatching, the cushion crawls with small activity: cases split, legs test the air, wing scales loosen. Then a low call or a change in the stalk-notes passes through the district, and the whole red surface seems to rise.

The carriers are not all alike. Some are narrow and sulfur-colored, with wings that shed a peppery dust. Others are almost black and fly so closely packed that they strike one another and fall back into the growth. The largest have ivory wing cases and feed by tearing open seed valves; the smallest enter valves already opened and take oil without doing further damage. Their swarms follow outbreaks of ripening rather than any season of the year. A district may be full of wings for an afternoon and comparatively empty by the next morning.

Kites wait above them. At rest, a kite is a narrow, folded object hanging beneath a branch, easily mistaken for a split pod. Warm rising air opens it. Its wings spread into broad, angled membranes, and the fine structures between the spars become a living sieve. It does not chase individual carriers. It climbs through the swarm with its mouth and wing-mesh open, straining bodies and dust together. When the updraft fails, it folds wherever it can. Some kites are accidentally grown over while hanging; their dry frames are later found inside branches, still jointed.

Below this weather, the huskbacks perform the heavier work. Young ones can sit in a cupped hand. Old ones are broad as doorways and leave tunnels in which a person could crawl, though doing so would be unwise. Their armored backs overlap in blunt plates, and their low heads are built around opposing sets of cutters. They do not simply bite through growth. They worry it apart, splitting fibers along their grain and crushing the woody valves into dry meal.

That meal, mixed with powdered shell and oil, is left behind in seams. Roots enter these seams almost immediately. In this way a huskback’s destruction becomes a kind of planting, though the huskback itself shows no interest in the result. It is concerned with the next obstruction, the next pocket of rich tissue, and the knocking of other huskbacks through the felt. Their signals are blunt: single impacts for location, rapid alternating knocks before a territorial rush, and a long pressure of the body against the ground when a fault tremor passes.

Threadbrowsers occupy the upper growth. Their bodies are long, narrow, and almost without visible legs; rows of gripping hooks beneath them do the work of limbs. They move by taking tension from one branch and lending it to the next, often causing a whole gallery to sway before the browser itself becomes visible. They crop leaf edges and young side-shoots, opening small wounds whose oil attracts winged carriers. Seed walkers cling to their dorsal bristles and may be carried across gaps that no rooted growth has yet bridged.

Nothing waits for dead matter to soften. Carcasses and collapsed stems are dismantled. The most assiduous dismantlers are the unthreaders, flat animals whose backs resemble bark plates until their many feet appear. They work in crowds, pulling fibers apart and reducing joints, sockets, and seed shells to portable fragments. A trunk may remain standing long after it has died, looking solid from a distance, while within it unthreaders have separated every fiber from every other. When it finally falls, it falls as a directionless spill of dry material.

### The low-voiced lives

The bellowers are usually taken for animals until one is found rooting.

They begin among the oil-heavy fruits at the base of certain hollow-stem stands. Most of these fruits detach, unfold their jointed legs, and carry their seed a short distance before splitting and fastening themselves to a suitable surface. A few do not stop. Their legs remain active, their seed chamber enlarges, and the original fruit shell becomes the first part of a roaming body.

Young bellowers browse on corky growth and seed-wool. As they grow, the stored chamber within them becomes hollow and ribbed. Their first calls are dry knocks. Later comes a rattling exhalation, and finally the bass note that can be felt through the soles long before the animal is visible. Adults move heavily through the stalk districts, their broad backs bearing old growth scars and sometimes entire gardens of small rooted passengers.

Their calls do different work in different bodies. They bring other bellowers together, cause ripe cases to open, send carriers into the air, and make certain root tips turn aside. When bellowers meet, they press their ridged throat plates together and exchange pollen-like bodies carried in the folds there. The same gathering may trigger a hatch that feeds kites three districts away. No single recipient contains the whole meaning of the call.

Eventually a bellower seeks fresh fractures. It stands for long periods with its feet planted apart, receiving the ticking of the stone. Then its legs stiffen. Root members emerge from old joints, the body settles, and the great internal chamber becomes the first hollow throat of a new stand. The armor plates spread and flatten into bark. A bellowing district may therefore be, in part, a population of former bellowers answering others that still roam.

Not every transformation is so complete. The feeding arms of certain ivory flowers sometimes detach as latchhands: flat, six-limbed hunters with hooked palms and a mouth formed from the old nectar cavity. For a season they prowl the cushions and lower branches, taking grazers and young carriers. If a latchhand finds its parent soon enough, it can fit itself back into the vacant socket and resume its former use. Once it has driven a root peg into stone, however, it cannot return. It becomes a new flower, then a new rooted body, and its hunting season survives only as a set of hard hooks beneath the bark.

### The uses of quiet

Because loudness is ordinary, quiet is also a habitat.

In some enclosed thickets grow hushfelts: dense rosettes and folded mats of wax-green tissue, their surfaces pleated so finely that they seem dusty even when newly made. They absorb much of the high wing-drone. Small case-building animals carry seed-wool and shed husk into these growths, lining their chambers until even the stalk-booms arrive dulled.

Their broods depend on delicate signals that would be lost in the open. A parent may tap three times on a case, pause, and tap once more; the young emerge only to that sequence. Kites sometimes roost at the edges of hushfelt colonies, and seed walkers hide in them while their shells harden. Within the largest patches, two people can hear one another speak at arm’s length.

The silence of a hushfelt is never complete. Tiny feeding ticks continue. Fiber shifts. A case-builder scratches inside its wall. Experienced listeners distinguish this from the other kind of quiet, in which small noises stop as suddenly as large ones.

That held silence can precede a subsidence. It can also precede a going.

No reliable anatomy exists for a going. What is seen first is usually behavior: carriers settling by the thousand, grazers clamping themselves to leaves, huskbacks wedging their bodies against tunnel walls. Then the growth bows in a line too broad to follow with the eye. Root arches rise. Stems separate at their sockets instead of breaking. Sometimes a succession of plated backs is visible beneath the felt; sometimes there are only many jointed supports moving with a common delay.

A going may be one immense roaming phase of a root-plateau. It may be several organisms joined temporarily, carrying whole populations of smaller lives with them. The people below the rim use a word for the event rather than the creature because the distinction does not help them get out of the way.

Its passage leaves a clear swath, but not a conventionally crushed one. The ground looks unfastened. Useful stems lie beside powdered ones; living cushions have been carried a little distance and set down at a new angle; exposed stone ticks in the open. Huskbacks and unthreaders enter the wake almost immediately. So do roots. The clear space may last a day, an hour, or only until the surrounding growth leans far enough to close over it.

### The people below the rim

People live most lightly in the Bellows, and most deliberately.

They come below the rim in small working parties to harvest oil, seed-wool, cane, shell, and the hard valves used for bowls and cutting edges. Their tools are portable because permanence is not a realistic property of toolsheds or roads. A good blade is valuable, but a good mallet may be more valuable still. Mallets are handed down with their voices known: which one opens a particular seed case, which one summons huskbacks, and which must never be struck near a stand whose chordlings are close to hatching.

A working party usually includes someone whose task is simply to listen. This person watches the suspended rods, keeps a heel or hand against a suitable stem, and pays attention when the others are absorbed in cutting. The skill is not hearing more than everyone else. Often the listener’s ordinary hearing has already been damaged by years below the rim. It is the ability to distinguish growth from subsidence, a bellower’s approach from a fault’s pressure, and inhabited quiet from held silence.

Speech is reserved for shelters and rare hushfelt refuges. Elsewhere the workers use hand signs, touches on the shoulder, and tapped messages. Some tapping patterns are forbidden in certain districts, not from taboo but because they resemble hatch signals too closely. A message sent carelessly may arrive together with several thousand ripe seeds and all the wings that come to take them.

Their acoustic devices are modest exercises in timing. The stone slab beside a stand of seed cases does not command the plants. It works only when the cases are already near opening. The ring around a whistling trunk does not make the trunk whistle on demand; it protects a particular lip from being grown over too quickly. The tuned rods give warning only to someone who knows which tremors are worth heeding. The most skilled harvesters speak of persuasion, but they do not confuse persuasion with obedience.

Nor do they expect their structures to remain where they left them. A sleeping cell is successful if it stays usable until the work is done. A platform incorporated into a root arch may still be visited, reached now by climbing rather than walking. A doorway discovered halfway up a new trunk is not treated as evidence of malice. It has merely been carried into another arrangement.

Their routes are appointments. One gallery is crossable while the ivory arch remains low enough to pass beneath. Another should be avoided after the crimson cushion has hatched, because the wing-swarm hides the hand signs. A third is safe only between two low calls, when the huskbacks have stopped knocking and the ground has not yet begun to answer. A map without an hour is considered incomplete; a map without a date is considered entertainment.

After dark, the workers seal themselves into seed-wool and woven cane while the Bellows continues without them. Huskbacks knock under the root felt. Kites hang folded in the warm updrafts. Somewhere a young bellower tries its first hollow note and receives no answer. Elsewhere a rooted one adds another fraction of a rib to its throat.

No inventory of the inhabitants stays accurate for long. A list of species is also a list of stages, shelters, meals, instruments, and future bodies. The fruit walker may become a thicket. The thicket may become a huskback’s tunnel. The tunnel may become the seam that holds a new plateau together.

Nothing lives on neutral ground. Every surface is somebody’s body, somebody’s leavings, or somebody’s work. The inhabitants of the Bellows do not merely endure its noise and growth. They are the means by which noise becomes growth, growth becomes shelter, and shelter becomes the next sound.
