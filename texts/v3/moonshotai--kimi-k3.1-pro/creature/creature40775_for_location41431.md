At first, the animals seem too few to account for the noise. A snail at a root pore. A frog on a buttress. Several shrimp hanging apart in brown water. A beetle ticking somewhere overhead. They appear less like a community than a set of solitary specimens, each with a great deal of room around it. But the Bellows is not inhabited at its center. It is inhabited along its edges, in its surfaces, and in the moving passages of its water.

The reeds are the first lives to seem like inhabitants rather than parts of the room. Each of the three clusters has a slightly different voice. In one, the woody clack is dry and abrupt; in another, the gulp seems to stumble into a second, smaller swallow; the third draws air so long through its green mouth that the intake becomes a thin whistle. These differences come from the bore of the stalks, the thickness of a membrane, the way an old root braces a basal bulb. They are individual bodies doing the same work with small variations.

They are also bodies in progress. A striped node is not a permanent fitting but living tissue under repeated strain. It thickens where it flexes most, thins elsewhere, and is eventually replaced by growth close to the cluster’s old footprint. New shoots do not wander toward the vacant middle. The reeds remain three separated stands, not because space has been allotted to them, but because every outward tendency is cropped, crowded, or folded back into the existing circulation. A stalk can age and vanish without the cluster becoming either younger-looking or larger.

Beneath them, the moss is more densely inhabited than anything visible from standing height. Its wet shoots hold water in films fine enough for rotifers and hair-thin worms. Midge larvae, pale as splinters of root, work through the current carrying particles too small for the shrimp. Fungal threads enter clipped tissue almost as soon as it falls. There is rot here, but it never becomes a layer or a smell. It occurs at the scale of a fragment, a cell, a softening edge, and is overtaken immediately by new growth.

The amber snail at the root pore lives within a area a visitor could cover with one hand. Its shell is clear and honey-colored at the growing lip, cloudier in the older whorls. When it feeds, it leans slightly to one side and draws its rasping mouth across the root in close arcs. The film it eats is nearly invisible: bacterial slime, algal cells, the first tender felt of fungal growth. Left alone, that film would narrow the pore and foul the flexible tissue around it. The snail does not know this. It is simply finding its food where the food always gathers.

A heavy concussion passes through the floor, and the snail stops. It does not withdraw fully; only its forward motion ceases, its body drawn a little closer to the root. Then it resumes. The pumping is ordinary weather to it, more constant than rain. The clean mouth of the pore is not its intention but the shape its hunger leaves behind.

Higher up, the ticking beetle is dark, shallow-bodied, and narrow enough to vanish into a seam that looks too small to admit it. Its wing covers lift a fraction and shut. Tick. A bead of water jumps from the hinge. Sometimes there is a long pause; sometimes the movement repeats with such regularity that it seems separate from anything alive. No other beetle answers. Between these fits of ticking, it works around an intake, cutting loose fragments with its jaws and pushing softened tissue aside to reach what lies beneath.

Other beetles make less noise. Spores stick to the fine bristles beneath their bodies and in the grooves of their legs. A beetle feeding at one root pore carries them away on its underside, grooms some off at another, and leaves the rest in damp cracks where they may or may not grow. Its travels are neither regular nor purposeful at the scale of the chamber. Yet over time, very little in the walls remains entirely local.

In the basins, the transparent shrimp inhabit the current rather than the water as a whole. Each holds a position where return flow brings food within reach of its small, ceaselessly moving legs. Their bodies are not perfectly clear. A dark thread of gut shows through one; another has a faint milky cloud around the gills. Occasionally a female carries a narrow cluster of greenish eggs beneath her abdomen. When a pump stroke alters the flow, the shrimp turn one after another, not in unison, each correcting its own small station.

If two drift too close, there is a flick of a tail and a wider separation. They do not school. Even their molts seem solitary: a shrimp withdraws from its old transparent skin, leaving behind a hollow likeness that catches for a moment against the moss. Soon it is bent, fragmented, and carried into the filter. Nothing about it remains available to mark where the animal had been.

The water striders live one dimension higher, on the tense skin of another basin. Each stands within a territory described less by distance than by vibration. The broad, recurring tremors of the reeds pass beneath them without response. A midge striking the surface makes a different signal—small, broken, irregular—and a strider is immediately in motion. It seizes the insect, carries it a short way, and feeds with its body still resting on the dimpled water.

At the boundaries of their stations, striders sometimes skate toward one another and stop, their legs making quick semicircles on the surface. Usually that is enough. One turns back. The basin remains divided among animals that rarely touch and never gather.

The midge columns above the warm roots look continuous only from a distance. Individuals enter, rise, fall out, and are replaced. Most of the hum is made by insects whose adult lives are brief and whose earlier lives were passed out of sight in the wet moss. After mating, females return to the filter to lay their eggs; other adults are taken by frogs, snapped from the water by striders, or caught for a few seconds in condensation before disappearing into the same close cycle. The sound overhead is therefore not the voice of a persistent swarm. It is the sound of constant replacement.

The red frogs give that replacement its most visible interruptions. Their color ranges from wet brick to a clearer rust-red along the throat and inner legs. In the gray-green light, the skin between their toes is thin enough to show its vessels. For long periods a frog does nothing but hold itself against the root, its throat moving slightly, its eyes following motions too small to interest a larger watcher.

Then it shouts.

The call is not musical and not especially prolonged. It is a single hard assertion of presence. Another frog may call soon afterward from the opposite wall, but the interval never settles into an answer. Often a shout comes in the relative opening after a gulp or clack, when it can carry farthest through the close rootwork. A frog that hears a nearby call may shift its perch by a body-length. Sometimes it does nothing at all.

Their hunting stations are equally economical. A frog waits until a midge’s flight brings it within range, makes one short lunge, and returns to stillness. It does not clear the air around itself. It takes what comes, and because there are several frogs and many small insect lives, what comes is usually enough. The bare root around a perched frog is not a little kingdom it has made. It is simply part of the same open surface that everything else, by other appetites, helps preserve.

The gray caterpillars work closest to that surface. They are the color of wet ash, narrow enough to follow the wall seams without touching either side. Most seams hold none. A few hold one. A caterpillar travels with its head moving slightly from side to side, testing young tissue. When a root tip pushes out of the wall or a frond begins to uncurl toward the center, the caterpillar eats it back.

There is no patrol and no boundary it recognizes. It turns where the food becomes scarce, rests where the seam narrows, and eventually pupates in a crease above the wettest growth. The adult is a small, narrow-winged moth that can spend much of its life folded against a roof leaf, indistinguishable from a thickened vein. Eggs are laid singly. The result is not an even grazing of the walls but a scattered, continuing interception of growth. The central emptiness survives because so many small beginnings end at a caterpillar’s mouth.

It would be easy to make these lives sound like occupations: pumpers, filterers, cleaners, croppers, hunters. But nothing here reports to anything else. The snail is not employed by the pore. The beetle does not deliver spores. The frog has no interest in the number of midges the moss can support. Their order comes from appetite, growth, obstruction, predation, and death occurring repeatedly within narrow limits.

Nor is the balance gentle. A midge is eaten before it mates. A shrimp’s molt is consumed almost before the animal has hardened. A frog falls silent, and for a while one part of the wall has no voice. A reed membrane tears and a circuit changes its sound until new tissue closes the fault. Individuals are lost constantly. What does not change is the general shape in which their replacements live.

There is no common generation and no shared season. At any one time, something is hatching in the moss, something is hardening a new skin, something is wearing thin, and something is being taken back into root and water. The Bellows persists without asking its inhabitants to persist with it.

None of them knows the chamber whole. The snail’s world is slope, moisture, and the taste of film. The strider’s is a trembling surface and the difference between a pump stroke and struggling prey. A frog knows the root beneath its belly, nearby movement, and the nearness of another shout. The midge column is not a column to the midges. Even the reeds, which hold so much of the place in motion, know nothing of the room their voices fill.

At the center, where none of them remains for long, a listener can hear all those separate lives at once: the snail’s silence, the shrimp’s small disturbances, wing hum, wing-case tick, the frog’s abrupt shout, and beneath everything the reeds drawing water through their own bodies. A striped node narrows. A bulb fills. The old snail advances by less than the width of a grain of sand.

The Bellows goes on being lived in, which is also how it goes on.
