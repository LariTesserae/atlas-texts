A census of the Low City would begin badly, because it would have to count the walls.

Some of the walls are alive. Some are the abandoned skeletons of animals. Most are both, with living tissue spread over mineral produced by earlier generations and cavities occupied by creatures whose own remains will eventually be added to the structure. At low flow, an arcade can appear deserted. The fish have dropped into the lanes, the shrimp are deep inside their sponges, the crabs have flattened themselves beneath shell lips, and every feeding crown has been withdrawn. Even the eels show nothing more than a sensitive snout at the mouth of a borehole. Then the current changes, and the masonry begins to open.

The most numerous conspicuous builders are the suture worms. Each lives in a tube little wider than its own soft body, but the tubes stand together in such numbers that their accumulated weight helps hold whole terraces in place. From the mouth of its tube, a worm extends a feathered crown into the current. The crown is at once a feeding net and an instrument for sampling the water. A change in sediment load, the approach of a predator, or the waste plume of a nearby animal can cause it to vanish in an instant.

Particles caught by the crown are sorted. Edible material is carried toward the mouth. Grains of suitable size and roughness may be pressed into the rim of the tube and bound with mucus. The worm has no conception of a wall or terrace; it maintains its own aperture. Yet larvae settling nearby are influenced by the same flow, and their tubes tend to succeed where the local water brings food without filling every crevice with silt. Generation after generation, private maintenance produces a shared embankment.

On exposed current-facing surfaces, the apertures crowd together and lean with the prevailing flow. In sheltered chambers they point in many directions, following small eddies. Dead tubes remain useful. Tiny crustaceans nest in them, brittle stars insert their arms, and young eels enter passages abandoned by much smaller animals. Some of the city’s oldest routes began as spaces inside bodies.

Oysters live among the worms in irregular, interlocking beds. Their shells thicken where they must bear weight and grow thin and extravagantly curved where neighboring shells obstruct them. They pump water even when the worms have closed, straining food and binding rejected silt into mucus-heavy pellets. These fall to the bases of the walls, where they become food for deposit feeders or material that other animals can push, pack, and bury. Boring sponges work slowly through older valves until the shells resemble mineral lace. When an oyster dies, its shell may remain cemented in place for a very long time, acquiring tenants long before it loses structural use.

The larger cavities belong to the cavern sponges, though a sponge is less a landlord than a second kind of watercourse. It draws water through countless small pores and expels it through wider openings. Within it live pale shrimp, often several generations together, moving through chambers that seem spacious only because their bodies are small. They pick at lodged particles, seize tiny animals drawn in by the sponge’s pumping, and retreat into side passages when larger tenants force their way in. Their eggs are sheltered in the quieter cavities. The sponge supplies them with food-bearing flow; their presence helps keep some of its inner spaces clear, and their waste enters the dense microbial economy of its tissues.

A shrimp may spend nearly its whole life inside one sponge. Its known world has a pulse, a chemical flavor, and a system of narrow exits. If the sponge sickens and its pumping falters, the colony becomes restless. Some shrimp leave along the outer surface, testing neighboring sponges with their antennae. Others remain until predators enter the failing canals. After death, the sponge’s skeleton can persist as a hollow stack, inhabited first by animals that prefer its open chambers and later by worms and shellfish that reduce those chambers to smaller, darker cells.

The maintained doorways of the lanes often belong to threshold crabs. These are low, flattened animals, able to brace themselves against current with their legs spread across the shell. Each keeps clear a small apron in front of its refuge. It loosens compacted grains, carries larger fragments aside, and grazes the films that would otherwise bind sediment into a crust. Where many such refuges open onto one passage, all this separate housekeeping helps keep the passage open. When a crab dies or moves away, its apron begins to silt over. An unobstructed doorway is therefore often a sign of continued occupancy rather than an enduring feature of the wall.

Snails, chitons, and other grazers follow the slower work of the microbial films. Their rasping is usually too faint to distinguish from the general murmur, but its effects are visible to touch: polished bands on shell, clean margins around apertures, and grazing trails that end abruptly where a current becomes too strong. Sea cucumbers move through the softer deposits, swallowing sediment and leaving behind worked material. Brittle stars occupy the intervals between these larger animals. They coil inside empty shells with one or two arms extended, collecting falling particles or reaching toward the chemical trace of carrion. If the shell breaks, they do not defend it. They simply flow into the next suitable cavity.

The old boreholes are the territories of eels. Several may inhabit a single thick wall without sharing a single chamber. Their winding tunnels interleave in three dimensions, and each animal knows its own system by touch, by the taste of its mucus, and by the direction water moves when it flexes its body. At a junction marked by another eel, it usually turns aside. Disputes are brief because the holes offer so many ways to withdraw.

An eel feeds with most of its body still hidden. It may wait through long intervals of poor water, then take a shrimp, a small fish, or a young crab during the confusion of a feeding pulse. Older eels occupy deeper holes and travel less. Younger ones use narrower passages and are themselves hunted. To a fish in the lane, the wall is shelter; to an eel within the wall, the lane is the exposed edge of its territory.

Octopuses use the city differently. They do not hold one mineral address for a lifetime. A chamber beneath a collapsed arch may shelter one for several feeding excursions, then be abandoned for a hole with a better approach current. Their dens can be recognized by the cleaned shells and crab remains accumulating nearby. These remains do not stay refuse for long. Small animals settle on them, scavengers work through them, and worms cement the stable pieces into the surrounding substrate. When an octopus broods eggs in a sheltered cavity, the den becomes unusually still. She drives water over the egg clusters and removes sediment, maintaining for a time a tiny hydraulic system within the larger one.

Above all this, the open water has its own residents. Small fishes gather over the arcades when food is moving, each holding station by constant slight corrections of fin and body. They feel their neighbors through pressure and keep the school together without needing to see it. A disturbance travels through the group as a tightening and turn. At high density, their combined swimming alters the local flow enough to change which particles reach the feeders below.

They eat small crustaceans, larvae, and other living material carried through the city. Larger fishes pick at whatever sinks more slowly. Between feeding periods, many drop into grooves behind shell berms or rest almost motionless in the lanes. Flatfish lie in the dust between doorways, their gill movements making shallow pits in the sediment. They are easy to overlook until some small animal passes close enough to touch them.

The migration cuts belong to animals for which the arcades are too narrow. Heavy-bodied fishes move along them with sensitive barbels close to the mud. Rays follow the broader routes, and old eels sometimes cross them when shifting between districts. Shellfish larvae may settle along these cuts, but repeated disturbance prevents most dense growth. The cuts remain open not because they are empty of life, but because the life passing through them is too large and too insistent for a crowded crust to endure.

The broad ray is the most complete interruption to the city’s ordinary activity. It reads the faint electrical disturbances of buried muscle and respiration, finding animals whose scent has been dispersed and whose bodies cannot be seen. As it approaches, worms contract, fish press into alleys, and crabs cease their small movements. Even animals beyond its immediate path respond to pressure and to the reactions of their neighbors. The silence travels farther than the ray.

Its departure is followed by a uneven recovery. A fish rises first in one lane. A crab resumes worrying at a grain of shell. Feeding crowns open cautiously along a terrace while those deeper in the arcade remain closed. Scavengers move toward any sediment the ray has disturbed. If it has taken an animal, the chemical evidence spreads through the water, drawing investigation from holes that had seemed empty moments before.

At the seep fields, the basis of life changes. Pale clams and mussels position themselves where mineral-rich water meets oxygenated water from above. Many carry bacteria within their tissues and receive much of their nourishment from them. A clam’s body spans the necessary boundary: one part samples the water, another the chemistry of the sediment. The beds shift gradually as seeps weaken or strengthen. Dead shells mark former zones of flow; new ridges accumulate around them, and animals less directly dependent on the seep settle on the hard surfaces.

Other small worms live within the seep sediment itself, some nourished largely by symbiotic microbes. Crabs and snails graze the bacterial crusts, keeping them patchy rather than allowing them to become continuous felt. Organic remains drifting from the crowded districts add another source of food. Thus the outer seep fields are not separate from the shell city. They supply hard substrate, chemical nourishment, and places where young animals can grow without immediately entering the densest competition.

The sheltered mud nurseries appear emptier than they are. Egg capsules lie beneath overhanging shells. Young crustaceans occupy burrows too narrow for their parents. Juvenile flatfish rest in sediment so shallow that a slight change in current uncovers them. Some broods are released into a particular tidal pulse and carried far beyond the district. Many of the city’s animals therefore spend their earliest lives elsewhere, suspended in water their parents never entered.

Those that return do not remember the Low City. They respond to it. Larvae ready to settle test surfaces and reject most of them. Some respond to the chemical trace of established worm beds, others to old shell, microbial film, or the fine-scale turbulence made by a inhabited wall. A bare saddle produced by collapse may remain unoccupied for many tides and then, under the right flow, acquire a dense crust of young settlers. The city’s continuity depends heavily on these arrivals, few of which can be predicted from the condition of the adults already living there.

Food arrives from both directions. From above comes organic snow, sometimes sparse for long periods and sometimes enriched by events in the distant sunlit water. From below comes the bacterial production of the seeps. Between these supplies, the inhabitants pass material through an elaborate sequence of uses. Worms and shellfish strain particles; sponges capture smaller ones; fishes take the animals attracted to the flow. Mucus, waste, rejected sediment, and fragments of feeding crowns sink into chambers. Deposit feeders work through them. What reaches still deeper mud is grazed, burrowed, and worked again.

Death enters the same sequence quickly. A carcass produces a plume that bends with the local current, and scavengers arrive from downstream refuges. Small crustaceans reach exposed tissue first. Brittle stars extend arms into crevices. Crabs drag pieces away, and eels investigate openings that would ordinarily be too strongly defended. The occupants of a chamber do not clean it for the sake of the chamber. They are hungry. Nevertheless, their feeding prevents many bodies from remaining long enough to foul the confined water.

Even a structural collapse becomes a succession of occupancies. When an old arch gives way, the pressure-wave empties nearby holes and the sediment cloud closes pumps and feeding apparatus. After the water clears, scavengers gather around crushed tissue. Surviving crabs excavate beneath the new rubble. An eel may take a freshly exposed borehole. Sponge fragments continue pumping where they remain intact. Worm larvae later settle on stable upper surfaces, while oysters attach along the edges. No animal repairs the arch. A lower dwelling forms because different lives find different advantages in its remains.

The inhabitants consequently live by very different clocks. Microbial films spread and are grazed back over short intervals. Shrimp generations turn within a sponge whose own tissues may outlast the arcade around it. Worm tubes accrete through repeated settlement. An eel’s long occupation can be only an episode in the life of a shell ridge. A ray’s route may cross in minutes distances that a threshold crab will never travel.

Nor do any two inhabitants know the same Low City. For a suture worm, the world narrows to one aperture and the water immediately beyond it. For a sponge shrimp, it is a branching interior with a few dangerous exits. A crab knows shell roughness, shelter depth, and a small apron of sediment. An eel knows a discontinuous network of holes and junctions. The schooling fishes know open volumes, pressure shadows, and the refuge offered by a berm. The ray knows broad routes punctuated by buried electrical activity. Settling larvae know almost none of this; they encounter the city as a series of tastes and surfaces on which attachment may or may not be possible.

At the turning of a strong tide, these separate lives briefly act upon the same water. Shellfish close, sponges reduce their pumping, burrowing animals reverse the stroke of their ventilation, and fishes lift away from the lanes. Then the flow steadies. Small pumps resume, crowns unfold, and water entering an old arcade passes through a city already full of other animals’ movements. It leaves carrying mucus, waste, eggs, shed particles, and the chemical signatures of occupancy. Another district receives it and responds.

Nothing there possesses the Low City whole. A body lives in a tube, a colony in a sponge, an eel within a wall, a school above an arcade, a ray along a valley-wide route. Their lives overlap without becoming a single organism. But shell outlasts flesh, empty space is quickly tested, and every maintained opening alters the conditions around it. Almost every surface becomes a place where something may settle; almost every cavity acquires a tenant; and almost every body, in time, becomes food, shelter, or foundation.
