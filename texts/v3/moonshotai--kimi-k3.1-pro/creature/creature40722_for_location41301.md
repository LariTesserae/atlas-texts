The browsers are the easiest inhabitants to believe in. Their porcelain shells take the garnet light along the rim, and the single dark stripe down each back is more than pigment: there the shell is thin enough to answer the leaf beneath it. As a file passes over a frond, the stripes tick in sequence. Six feet grip without crushing the ribs. The mouth brushes are drawn slowly through the trembling fields, and a little dry sweetness gathers at their tips.

The browsers take it. The leaf remains entire, the brushes do not stay yellow, and no browser becomes heavier in any way the chamber consents to remember. Grazing is real, but one could not build a food chain from its consequences.

Around them, the fan grove is a crowd of different hearings. One field of a leaf takes the furnace-deep vibration; another, divided from it by a rib no thicker than a hair, answers a dry rattle high in the chord. Beneath the fans, the velvets tighten almost imperceptibly at every snap. The little spiral blades hold a finer agitation, so that their edges seem uncertain while their centers remain still. None of the leaves turns toward the light. The light merely makes them visible. Their attention is downward and inward, toward the pale crystalline forks where root clasps mineral and vibration becomes chemistry.

The cup grove listens more narrowly. Each translucent cup admits one thin portion of the chord and refuses another. The jointed stems carry what remains downward into the stone. Nothing rings loosely; there is no casual tinkling. The sound passes through the grove as through an assembly of exact, small decisions.

Under the cups, the pale animals are eyeless, soft, and completely dry. Most of the time they are only slight creases of flesh against the mineral. When the deepest tone passes through them, they inflate—not with air, but with strain. Their bodies become faintly opaque, revealing darker forks within, resembling the roots of the plants above. Their feeding appears to occur in that yielding and recovery. Compression performs one part of their chemistry, release another. They cling beneath the cups with small dry disks and seldom move more than the width of their own bodies. There are no smaller ones nestled among them, no thin-skinned infants swelling out of phase with the adults.

The ribbon grove is harder to enter with the eye. Its foliage crosses itself so closely that an animal can be within a hand’s breadth and still be mistaken for another amber nodule. Those nodules are neither eggs nor seeds. Nothing emerges from them, and nothing inside them resembles a waiting young thing. They are resonant knots in the plants, places where several ribbon-stems bind the same vibration into hard amber tissue.

The winged animals gather there without courtship. Their wings are clear and stiff, more like split mica than membrane, and when they settle against a nodule they lay all four wings flat upon it. They feed through the wings as much as through any mouth, taking in the plant’s tuned warmth. Their little flights look random until watched for longer: the same open space remains at the center of the gathering, the same outermost nodule remains untouched, and the same bright crossings recur without the swarm ever thickening or diminishing.

Between the groves, the runners make the empty floor comprehensible.

A crossing is not a dash from shelter to shelter. It is a series of hearings. The runner moves a few steps, grips a scale of stone with all four divided feet, and stops. At particular stones it presses the lower edge of its narrow head against the floor. Nothing is scent-marked; nothing is left behind. The animal is listening through its bones. The stones are places where some component of the sound enters the body more cleanly than it does elsewhere.

The bare intervals are therefore not simply ground the plants have failed to occupy. A stopping stone among dense leaves would be only another stone. Out on the open floor, with no root or ribbon to conduct competing vibrations, its minute difference can occupy the runner’s whole body. The hunter, too, uses the emptiness. It feels a footfall there as a small flaw in an otherwise continuous surface.

When a runner opens its vanes, the effect is not invisibility. Its body and its shadows cease to agree. One foot can still be heard gripping stone, though the eye can no longer attach that sound to a leg. The warm, narrow torso seems to occupy several neighboring outlines, none of them quite substantial enough to hold. Sometimes this happens when another animal approaches. Sometimes it happens at a stopping stone with nothing visible near enough to threaten it. The runner may be answering a tone no other body in the chamber can receive as danger.

The amber hunter has no clear front until it moves. Beneath its slab it is a broad, thin warmth with a granular back and a sensitive fringe. The fringe reads interruptions in vibration rather than footsteps as separate sounds. A runner can place three feet softly and be ignored, then set the fourth upon some acoustically important needle of stone and bring the hunter’s whole body into a new tension.

Its strike seems to fold around a single wingbeat or a single step. Yet there is no husk afterward, no distended body, no interval in which the animals at the nodules can be counted and found one short. What the hunter takes does not become meat. It takes the captive instant of another body’s vibration—the exact agitation of wings, the muscular note of a runner preparing to spring. The winged things remain at the nodules. The runner remains warm and quick. The hunter remains beneath the slab. Its predation is not an unsuccessful attempt at killing; it is a complete act of another kind.

Nor is its stillness patience. Patience would consume time while awaiting an opportunity. The hunter’s stillness is already part of the strike, as fully as its movement is.

The life inside the wall may be the largest inhabitant, but its size is difficult to infer. It does not occupy a tunnel. Its body lies along the readiness of one stone plate to separate from another, in interfaces that have no open width at all. What is a wall to the runner is, to this animal, a thicket of nearly parallel surfaces. The traveling scratch is the sound of its many legs finding purchase on both sides of a possible opening.

Occasionally a single pale joint shows at an edge too narrow to contain it. It is dry, jointed like a root fork, and gone before the rest of the anatomy can be guessed. If the creature feeds, it feeds on the difference in resonance between adjacent plates. It is not maintaining the walls or keeping the fracture closed. Nothing comes behind it to repair its passage. It simply lives where the chamber has almost come apart.

None of these lives are phantoms. A browser held carefully between two fingers would struggle with six hard, deliberate feet. The runner’s body would be warm, its vanes surprisingly stiff. The hunter has weight. The pale animals under the cups are soft enough to yield and substantial enough to resist. But no exchange among them becomes an accumulation. Flesh does not thicken, shells do not wear, and experience does not settle into habit.

The intelligence at the edges of the bare floor is less easy to give a body.

Someone compared one stone chip with another. Someone discovered that a split rib held a braid better when the fiber passed twice around it rather than once. The smooth hammerstones did not become smooth by accident. Yet the clearest evidence of mind is not the chipping or binding. It is the placement.

Within the half-ring, one layer of the furnace-roar falls away, and the brushing of the browsers separates into individual strokes. The gap in the ring is necessary; adding the “missing” stones would close the very condition the structure makes. Behind the low screen, the glassy note can be distinguished into several finer voices. Beneath the crosspiece, the bead-like ticking withdraws from the teeth and the small bones of the ears and becomes, for once, something that can be located in the air.

The structures do not provide shelter from the chord. Nothing in the pocket could do that. They provide ways of hearing it in parts.

The seven loose nodules and the notches along the screen belong to the same act of attention. Their order by size is not quite their order by pitch: the fourth has a deeper response than the third, and the smallest is nearly silent. The irregular spacing of the notches repeats those acoustic intervals rather than the nodules’ dimensions. The row looks careless until it is heard. It is not a calendar, for there are no days to count. It may be notation, though not necessarily writing—a way of placing a distinction outside the mind so that the mind need not carry it forward as memory.

Call the makers the listeners. Nothing more definite is required, and nothing more definite can be recovered. They may have had small articulate hands. They may have been akin to the runners. They may not be a separate species at all, but the intelligence of the whole arrangement when one voice is removed and the others become distinguishable. Their clearest anatomy is discrimination: this fit rather than that, this interval rather than the next, this voice attended to without mistaking it for the whole.

They did not discover the nodes and then, afterward, build upon their discovery. Discovery, craft, and use are different faces of the same held act. No maker has just stepped away. None is about to return. The pointed splinter is not awaiting use, and the coil of cord is not leftover material. Their availability is part of their completeness.

It would be easy to read tragedy into all this: the hunter endlessly denied its meal, the runner endlessly crossing, the listeners unable to finish their work. But these are not repetitions in the ordinary sense. Repetition would require earlier occasions to have been retained. There is no first crossing being imitated, no thousandth strike wearily approaching. No runner remembers its route. No hunter becomes more skillful. No listener expects someone eventually to understand the screen.

Nor are the inhabitants adapted to the chamber through a history of generations. There have been no generations. Their fit is immediate, as an overtone fits the note that contains it. The broad leaf, the striped shell, the opposed vanes, and the body within the wall are not solutions handed down through survival. They are exact relations, alive without having had to become so.

The chord is not a noise inflicted upon them. It is food, orientation, shelter, and perhaps, for the listeners, thought. Its resolution would not release them into fuller lives. It would remove the conditions by which these lives are intelligible.

Nothing is almost ready to hatch, heal, spread, or be completed. The runner is not almost across. The hunter is not almost fed. The half-ring is not almost closed, and the screen is not almost written. Each life is complete in the act by which it can be known.

The fracture does not hold them back from their futures. It holds them in full.
