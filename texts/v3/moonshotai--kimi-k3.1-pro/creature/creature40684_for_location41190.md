The Low Chorus has no keepers. Nothing comes out at dusk to tend the meres, and no hidden settlement lies just beyond the reeds. Most of what lives there could be washed from a stone on the tip of a finger. Yet it would be wrong to call the basin sparsely inhabited. Its life is crowded into contacts: water against mineral, water against root, root against peat. A census by visible animals would miss nearly all of it. A better census would count continuities.

Along the seepage, within water films too thin to see directly, live communities of cells in subdued grey, olive, and rust-coloured associations. Some obtain their energy from mineral reactions rather than light; others consume the fine organic wash released by peat. They help lay down pale marl in the clear pools and dark mineral skins on the stone ribs. Among them move translucent grazers, soft many-segmented things, and little encased animals that open only when water covers them completely. Almost none has a flying stage. The air is not their principal medium. Rain is.

A hard shower can carry their spores, cysts, and dormant bodies from a rock film to a root surface, from a root surface into open water, and from there across ground that had no channel an hour before. A population may extend through several reaches without any of its members travelling more than the length of a reed. Their chemistry does not account for the basin’s looseness. They have simply become very good at waiting through it. When a film thins or a reach grows vague, many of them cease almost all activity. When water again has a definite surface along which to spread, they resume.

Waiting takes another form among the plants. The visible shoots are often expendable; the old living tissue is kept below them. Some of the sedge rootstocks are centuries older than anything rising from them. Their rhizomes follow channels that recur most often, while shallow roots make brief, vigorous use of new silt. If the silt remains, the hummock spreads. If the deposit ceases to have been laid down, the shallow growth withers or vanishes, and the deeper plant continues. A hummock can therefore be ancient underneath and little more than an experiment above.

The mosses are less conservative. Their spores lodge in rain films, in the feet of small animals, and later in the folds of much larger hides. They germinate wherever several alternatives have repeatedly left the same old ground available. A successful cushion is seldom a clean succession from one founding spore. It is a crowded, layered inheritance, including lineages brought in with sediment from states that left little else behind. Such cushions hold moisture long after open pools have gone, providing refuge for eggs, cysts, and the finer peat animals.

The low ferns and cup-leaved plants make smaller refuges still. Each leaf cup contains a little diluted community of film life, sometimes a few grazing animals, sometimes an egg attached so lightly that it seems to be part of the water. When wind turns the pale underside and spills the cup, the contents enter the moss below. Fern fronds release their collected water drop by drop, sometimes striking the same stone for hours. The sound is slight, but reliable.

Living thickets are often gathered around just such reliable sounds: seepage ticking through a rock joint, runoff pulsing inside an old root channel, fern water dripping onto marl. Whether the sound helps a patch remain coherent or merely reveals where the ground was already oldest, the association is strong. The plants add to it themselves. Rain strikes their crowded stems; their leaves shed water; their roots give runoff narrow passages in which to click and knock. A thicket is thus a little louder than open clay, and seldom entirely silent.

The animals large enough to see respond to the chorus chiefly through contact. The bass note comes up through root mats, submerged limbs, and bellies resting on peat. While it is strong, they feed and move. When a hollow loses it, they do not usually flee. They stop. Stillness against something old is safer than flight across young mud whose depth may presently become difficult to establish. A swimmer presses itself to a submerged root. A burrowing animal wedges its body against stone. Something heavier simply settles its full weight and waits. When the note catches again, they resume, sometimes in a hollow whose inlet has changed sides but whose old stone is still beneath them.

The colourless swimmers are the animals most often seen, though even they may be absent from a pool for years. Their bodies are almost clear tissue, interrupted by a fine grey line of gut and a dim pulse near one end. They are visible mainly when they roll and their sides briefly take the pewter light. They graze the films on submerged roots and pale marl, growing slowly where the water is cold and food is dilute.

Their reflections lag because the animals themselves are young, recent, and not yet very firmly carried from one agreement to the next. While one swims, the water seems unable to keep a single position for it. When it stops against an old root, the reflection usually arrives: first the root, then a colourless interruption beside it, then the small animal entire. This is not something it appears to notice. It has only learned, if individual experience can be called learning in so short a life, that open water is poor shelter when the chorus weakens.

Most hatch after water has risen between rains. Their eggs lie in damp moss or in the upper root mats, and can remain viable through long intervals without open water. Others arrive already hatched, left behind with the water and fine sediment of a discarded state. Not every swimmer therefore has parents in the valley that remains. Some have no discoverable ancestry there at all. The moss accepts them without distinction.

Most of these animals live out their small lives in pools. A few enter the peat. Those few are the beginning of the basin’s other visible animal, though the intermediate years are almost never seen.

Below the root mats, the surviving young grow slowly on detritus and the rich organic wash around old roots. Their bodies broaden. Mineral and dense black residue accumulate in their skins and internal supports. By the time one emerges again, it is a lowback: heavy, splayed, and close to the ground, with most of its head and limbs below water or reed litter. Its back is not cleanly shelled or furred. It is uneven dark hide, stained and partly inhabited by the same films and mosses that occur on wet stone. In poor light it resembles a hummock until the hummock continues.

There may be only a handful of mature lowbacks in the whole basin. They range widely, feed little for long periods, and can remain buried or nearly motionless through seasons in which their accustomed pools disappear. After hard rains they become more active, cropping new shoots and viable growth left on fresh deposits. Their passage opens black lanes through sedges and carries spores, eggs, and encysted film life from one reach to another. Some of their routes later fill with water; sedges follow the margins. It would be easy to make gardeners of them, and wrong. They are feeding, remembering, and looking for others of their kind. The valley uses their leavings as it uses everything else.

Age changes their relation to the ground. An old lowback has persisted through so many local arrangements that alternatives tend to revise the water depth around it before they revise the animal. This does not make it immortal or immovable. It means only that its body has become difficult to treat as a minor discrepancy. Its wake is another matter. The wake is new, and may remain after the reeds have closed, or lie briefly across water that has already found another surface.

Lowbacks navigate less by sight than by sequences of vibration. A stone rib gives one kind of pressure through the feet; deep peat gives another; an old channel beneath new mud gives a third. An individual may follow a route whose visible channel has been absent for years, or wait beside a rib until water resumes along it. To an observer it would appear to be anticipating the valley. More likely it is remembering something older than the present shoreline.

The single rough note from the far margin is probably theirs, though the calls are too infrequent for every note to be assigned with confidence. It has weight beneath it and a torn, uneven edge. It serves both to locate other lowbacks and to test the reaches through which an answer might travel. The valley returns it generously: from clay slopes, from distant meres, from places that seem nearer than the caller. The animal remains still through these replies. A genuine answer is usually late, and it does not repeat merely because the first call is repeated.

Encounters between adults are correspondingly rare. A courtship may consist of a few calls separated by seasons, followed by a journey along stone ribs and persistent channels while pools come and go on either side. Eggs are left in old root mats rather than open water. Most calls receive no animal answer at all. If a hollow sometimes becomes precise around the sound, that is a consequence, not a duty. The lowbacks do not maintain the chorus.

Nothing living in the basin manages it. The films did not devise its molecular processes; the sedges do not choose which channels persist; the lowbacks are not its guardians. Life there has simply favoured three habits: keep some part of yourself attached to old ground, risk other parts on whatever new ground appears, and be able to wait without spending much.

So the visible encounters remain isolated. A colourless turn beneath black water may be a hatchling, a dormant survivor newly returned, or a viable remnant with no parent in the surviving arrangement of the valley. A dark back beyond the reeds may be the only mature animal for many kilometres. Neither sighting promises another.

When the rain thins, the films resume along stone and root. The sedges put a few shallow roots into whatever sediment has remained. In a pool, a swimmer holds itself against an old rhizome until its reflection joins it. Far away, weight enters the reeds, and a wake lingers after the animal has gone.

The Low Chorus does not need to become one place for life to continue in it. It needs only, here and there, an old root, a stone that keeps its note, and time enough for a living thing to hold still.
