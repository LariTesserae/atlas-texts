Nothing lives in the middle of Mill Hollow. A snail may spend a night on the open moss, a moth may blunder across it, and a bird may use its air as the shortest distance between two walls, but no animal makes its home in the central space. The inhabitants occupy the plant shell. Most live within a few handbreadths of the surface on which they were born, and many never encounter the grove as a whole.

The largest inhabitants are the buttressed trees, though even they are not simply old individuals standing in possession of their ground. Living wood encloses dead wood; younger stems rise close against older roots; a buttress may remain long after the crown that first supported it has vanished. What appears from the center to be one massive trunk can be a congregation of ages. Their crowns move separately in the wind, and their creaks answer one another without synchrony. Beneath the bark, beetle larvae open the damp wood into soft, dark passages. Other insects follow those passages, and birds follow the insects.

Between the great trunks, the hollow-stemmed plants provide narrower worlds. Some stands contain culms at every stage: green and tightly sealed, mature and dry, silvered, split, softening at the base. Wind sounds in the empty ones while larvae inhabit the others. A small pale moth spends almost its entire life inside a single dying stem, consuming the pith and leaving the outer skin standing. When it finally emerges, the adult may live only a few nights. It is one of the moths occasionally seen from the center, crossing the open air with uncertain, conspicuous slowness. Most never make so visible a journey. They emerge into cover, mate within a few stems of their birthplace, and disappear back into the walls.

The decomposers are more numerous still and less often presented to the eye. Fungal threads whiten the interiors of old stems and loosen dead fiber in the root mat. Bacteria flourish in the water films around moss leaves and in the blackened ends of broken culms. Their work is detected by softness, discoloration, and smell rather than by fruiting bodies. A stem that looks capable of bearing weight may collapse into wet threads under slight pressure. A fallen leaf can remain recognizable for weeks while becoming almost entirely available to other lives.

The moss has its own crowded population. Springtails graze among its wetter seams, springing away so abruptly that a disturbed patch seems to lose color. Mites move after them, some feeding on plants or fungal growth, others hunting. Still smaller animals live in the films of water between leaf fibers. Their abundance changes with each rain and each dry interval, but the surface above them remains low and even. From a standing person’s height, none of this disturbs the impression of an unoccupied floor.

Spiders keep to junctions: where a culm meets a leaf sheath, where a root crosses dead wood, where two stems make a narrow, sheltered angle. Some construct webs no wider than a spread hand. Others use no web, waiting with their forelegs touching a stem and taking vibration for information. None spans the central air. The open volume offers too little anchorage and too few prey; nearly everything worth catching travels along a surface.

Snails are the inhabitants most likely to be discovered alone. Their shells are dark, thin-looking, and often filmed with the same green growth they scrape from bark and leaves. After rain they emerge in numbers that the empty grove gives no reason to expect. They graze the shaded walls and the inner margins of the moss. Occasionally one continues outward, and by morning a single snail may be visible far from any trunk, its body casting a disproportionately clear shadow. Its trail shines briefly and then ceases to register. By afternoon, most have withdrawn into leaf bases or beneath old fiber.

The frogs live above the floor. They are small, wet-olive animals with darker flanks and toes suited to smooth stems, and each persistent cup of water may support its own separate little history of eggs, tadpoles, and calling adults. There is no common pond and therefore no single assembly. The harsh chorus heard after rain comes from dozens of scattered reservoirs. One frog begins with a few hard clicks and accelerates into a rattle; another starts before the first has finished. At close range the calls are rough and almost painfully distinct. From the open floor, reflected and mixed with dripping water, they become part of the larger laboring sound. During dry intervals the adults retreat into deep axils and moist broken stems. The loudest amphibians can then be entirely absent from sight and nearly absent from hearing.

Several kinds of small birds work the walls. One creeps over bark and buttresses, bracing itself with its tail and probing the softened wood for larvae. Another moves through the dense outer growth, taking insects from the undersides of leaves and spiders from their retreats. Their nests lie well inside the intergrowth, cups of fine fiber lodged where rain seldom strikes directly. The birds know routes through vegetation that appears continuous from the center.

They also account for the hollow’s most misleading voices. One gives a clear two-note call, the second note carrying much farther than the bird’s size suggests. Another produces a dry, accelerating chatter easily mistaken for something striking repeatedly against a hollow stem. These calls enter the central air while the birds themselves remain concealed. When one finally crosses the opening, it often does so low and fast, without alighting. Its passage looks less like an inhabitant revealing itself than like a brief failure of concealment.

The mammals are fewer and still more fragmentary in appearance. Small, slender hunters move through the root thickets after beetles, snails, and fallen seeds. A slightly heavier animal visits from the surrounding plain, threading the same cover without establishing any visible entrance. Usually there is only a displacement of leaves, a naked ear at a gap, or a dark shoulder withdrawing before the rest of the body can be seen. Their routes lie within the walls or beneath overhanging roots. They have no reason to cut across the moss. Larger predators occasionally pass near the grove, but none owns it. The most usual sign of one is a local silence: a patch of wall in which frogs stop calling and small birds cease to move, while the rest of the hollow continues unchanged.

The central air is thus inhabited only by events. Rain occupies it for an hour. Pollen passes through invisibly. A leaf turns several times before landing. At dusk, a moth may cross from darkness into a little pale sunlight and then into darkness again. Once in a while a bird takes an insect there, the whole pursuit completed in less time than a call from the walls. Even the wingbeats seem large because there is nothing among which to misplace them.

After heavy rain, the population appears to increase without any creature arriving. Snails extend from cover. Frogs call from cups that were silent the day before. Bark darkens, and birds work more actively along softened wood. Culms that were dry enough to hum now tick under dripping water. Animals shift by centimeters rather than meters: under a leaf base, farther inside a stem, higher on a root. By afternoon the same abundance has folded itself back into the surfaces. In dry weather the outer thickets become louder with seed cases and brittle stems while the inner life grows quieter and deeper.

No resident experiences the hollow as the open-floored structure seen by a visitor. For a larva, it may be one culm of softening pith, with the world beyond represented by vibration. For a frog, it is a few water pockets, the wet bark between them, and the danger of crossing exposed growth. A creeping bird may know every buttress and seldom touch the moss. A root-thicket mammal may spend its life beneath an interlaced ceiling without ever seeing the long opening to the sky. Even the trees encounter the place piecemeal: moisture below, wind above, pressure and damage along their surfaces.

Nothing coordinates these lives, and nothing tends the emptiness between them. The frogs do not chorus for the grove, the birds do not call into the center to fill it, and the decomposers are not clearing anything away. Each is occupied with a small, immediate set of conditions. Their crowded worlds touch and overlap around a space almost none of them needs.

Only from the open floor, where nearly nothing lives, do all those separate lives become audible at once.
