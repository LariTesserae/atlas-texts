## What lives in the Black Catchment

The first mistake is to imagine that everything there has become an ear. Hearing is useful, but the marsh is loud, sound can be masked, and the most dangerous animals are often detected by what they remove from it. The Catchment’s successful inhabitants use several senses in succession: pressure through the feet, current along a jaw, heat conducted through a shell, the taste of iron in water, and only then a voice in the rain.

Nor are they uniformly eyeless. Some animals have small, functional eyes inherited from ancestors that lived elsewhere. The organs simply do not help much. Others have heat pits where eyes might be expected, or naked sensory lines along the face and flanks. In many of the smaller creatures, the distinction between feeling, tasting, and hearing is not easily made. A grazer clamped to a sheet of spring felt can detect the approach of a snail by the strain in the bacterial skin beneath it.

The spring grazers are among the Catchment’s quiet gardeners. Their shells are ridged and porous, and the felt grows in the grooves. As they crop one seep and move to another, they carry its chemistry-feeding growth with them. Some springs have probably been colonized less by flowing water than by generations of slow, shell-backed animals. Where they gather, the stone becomes populated in layers: felt below, grazers above it, larvae feeding on their waste, and small electric hunters buried in the silt with only their mouths exposed.

A chain-eel is not one animal, and a chain is not always one species. The eels join through flooded root systems, each retaining its own teeth, hunger, and eventual fate. A disturbance at one end passes down the line as a series of muscular contractions, faster than any single eel could swim with the news. In oxygen-poor water they cluster around the better-flowing stems; during seasonal pump reversals they separate into the warmed channels to spawn. When a root bed is struck hard, the chain may come apart all at once. For a few seconds the whole flooded thicket seems to consist of individual escapes.

Mud-herons hunt these root systems without needing to enter them. Their six folding limbs distribute their weight so widely that they can stand on floating peat or the loose roof of a drowned fern bed. The bill is less a spear than an instrument. Held just above the silt, it detects the faint electrical disturbances of muscle: a larva contracting, an eel turning, a buried animal trying not to do either. A heron may take one step in several minutes. Near compressors, some time that step to the mechanical stroke. Elsewhere they use mossback footfalls or the impact of heavy rain. Their stillness is not empty. It is a form of attention.

The mossbacks themselves are much less delicate. An adult can obstruct a culvert, and a herd crossing soft ground changes the drainage for a season. Their backs carry thick growths of moss, fernlets, and dark microbial turf. This is not camouflage; nothing in the Catchment needs to look like moss. The growth cushions the hide against hard rain, holds colonies of parasite-eating larvae, and provides a wet refuge for small fliers that may seldom touch the ground. Mossbacks rub against particular root beds to renew these back gardens. A herd therefore leaves behind not only dung and broken stems, but patches of inoculated ground from which new fern basins can spread.

Their voices travel below the rain and, through the feet, farther still. A call that seems to come from a nearby animal may be answered by ground pressure from beyond the next ridge. Calves learn the herd’s particular pattern of calls and pauses. They also learn its routes through the machinery: which outflow becomes passable after a load change, which warm pipe attracts biting fliers, which embankment remains firm when the surrounding peat begins to move.

Above and between these larger lives, the air is occupied in layers. The dry-winged clickers spend their short lives avoiding one another’s rhythms. Soft fliers roost in pipe seams and hunt the intervals left by engines. High sonar callers use pylons and escarpments as acoustic landmarks, sending cries into country too large for any answer to be quickly understood. Some return to the same ceramic insulator for years, not because it offers safety, but because the structure gives a recognizable shape to the distances around it.

The soft-footed predators use the whole arrangement differently. They do not need to be absolutely silent. They need only to be quieter than the water concealing them. Many wait beside mossback paths or in the noise shadow of a station, where an approaching animal has already allowed the machinery to cover small mistakes. Their approach is sometimes marked by an expanding omission: one frog stops, then a band of clickers, then a heron steps away from the silt. Experienced animals notice the gap before there is anything in it to fear.

People belong to this ecology too, though not very numerously. There is no shared name for all of them, no capital, and no reliable census. Outsiders sometimes call them listeners, which is a little like calling marsh dwellers swimmers. It identifies a necessary skill and mistakes it for a whole life.

They live in small households, temporary line camps, and widely separated dwellings built on root platforms, in dry ash galleries, or against the outer walls of warm culverts. A house may be occupied for generations and abandoned in an afternoon when the note of nearby water changes. Larger gatherings occur for repairs, exchanges, marriages, and the movement of herds, but they tend to disperse quickly. Too many people exhaust a root bed; too many voices also erase the fine sounds by which a household knows that its surroundings are still in order.

A dwelling does not become visible at its threshold. It becomes specific. One drip strikes ceramic, another a hollow rail; a reed screen gives a soft rattle that no animal makes; the floor changes from woven root to stone before the first step down. In hard rain, these sounds may be all that distinguishes home from another piece of warm, dripping country.

The inhabitants’ sensory knowledge is considerable but not magical. Most of it is stubbornly local. A celebrated listener in one watershed may be nearly helpless in the next, where the rain strikes different leaves, the stations run at another pitch, and the predators use different paths. Children learn routes as sequences of pressure, scent, sound, and effort. Knotted cords and strings of ceramic beads preserve these sequences. A route record may contain a change of footing, a cable hum, a bitter seep, the sound of water in a pipe of a particular diameter, and a warning not to continue if the frogs have stopped.

Such a map is not a picture of country. It is country in the order in which a body can survive it. To be lost is not always to be somewhere unknown. Sometimes it is merely to have encountered the sounds in the wrong order.

Speech varies with the local soundscape. In reed country, people often favor low, slow consonants. Near a compressor, they may speak in bursts, fitting whole exchanges into the mechanical pauses. There are tactile signals for a few urgent matters—stop, water rising, remain still, I am here—but the inhabitants are not silent wraiths. They argue, joke, boast, sing, and talk too much when frightened. Their music ranges from songs that occupy a temporary gap in the marsh’s voices to pieces played through a reed held between two people’s hands or teeth. The second kind can be performed beside a sleeping child without waking it.

Some songs belong to particular stations. Their rhythms depend on a turbine’s load or the interval between pump strokes. When an engine changes, the song must change with it. A few are therefore records of machinery that no longer exists: no one can perform them properly, but everyone agrees that the silence left for the missing part is essential.

Subsistence is equally various. Households tend warm-stone gardens of edible grazers, set eel baskets in root channels, cut fern hearts and starchy rhizomes, and hunt or scavenge along mossback routes. Geothermal seeps and heat exchangers provide places to cook or ferment food. Not every warm pool is wholesome; some outflows accumulate metals that only the local felt and its grazers can tolerate. Much practical knowledge consists of knowing which animal can safely eat what a person cannot, and which part of that animal can then be eaten.

People also maintain machinery. They clear intakes, pack leaking valves, free jammed gates, and divert roots away from warm cable trenches. Usually the reason is immediate: a blocked intake starves a seep, a seized gate floods a basin, a failing outflow destroys an eel run. A keeper may understand the hydraulics of one station intimately while having no useful idea what the wider factory produces. Skill at a valve does not imply knowledge of the main.

This explains some of the Catchment’s maintenance, but not all of it. No household claims responsibility for every newly greased valve, replaced tread, or door seal made from unfamiliar material. Many otherwise unrelated languages have an expression that can be translated, imperfectly, as *the other shift*. Depending on the watershed, the phrase may suggest a traveling crew, people living behind the sealed cliff doors, automatic mechanisms housed deeper in the stations, or simply work done by someone too distant to contradict the story. In one basin it is also the term for the skin left behind by an eel.

There is no agreement, and no necessity for agreement. To one family a turbine is a dangerous neighbor. To another it is the organ that keeps their basin habitable. To a third it is principally a bearing that must not be allowed to seize. None of these relationships requires worship, and none requires ignorance.

Human social worlds are small but not always closed. Certain dry mains carry tapping farther than a voice could travel. Households along a pipe may recognize one another’s rhythms without ever having met. A person can be known for years as a cautious style of questioning, a tendency to repeat the last phrase, or an annoying habit of transmitting during heavy rain. Then the tapping stops. Whether the correspondent has died, moved, or merely lost access to the line is often impossible to discover.

It would be misleading to call such people the masters of the Catchment, or even its caretakers. They are one set of intervals among many: eels fitted to pump reversals, frogs to compressor silences, herons to the electrical privacy of silt, mossbacks to paths that outlive individual herds. Most of the inhabitants are very small. Most of the rest never see a station, insofar as any of them can be said to see anything. Their lives nevertheless cross through the network, taking warmth, shelter, timing, and sometimes danger from it.

If there is an inhabitant at the scale of the whole Catchment, none of these others can distinguish it from the conditions of their lives. Its voice would have to include rain, roots, engines, migrations, and the movement of water through stone. Perhaps it has no voice at all, only load.

At a change in station demand, people often pause. The turbine note lifts; frogs test the opening; a mossback answers from somewhere beyond the immediate basin; water takes a new level against the house piles. For a short while, routes and distances become easier to judge. Each listener takes stock of what is near and what has ceased to answer.

Nothing becomes visible. Nothing needs to. The Catchment is inhabited, and for a moment its inhabitants can hear the fact.
