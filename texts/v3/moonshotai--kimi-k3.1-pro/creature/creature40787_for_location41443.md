No resident needs the Hollow as a whole. The dome supplies light and the basin occasional water, but life happens in much smaller regions: along one wet seam, inside a solution pocket, beneath a scale-leaf, within the few paces of wall a lizard can cross without spending more moisture than the journey is worth. An animal may live out its life without ever entering the open center.

The oldest residents are probably the black lichens. A rosette no wider than a thumbnail may outlast many generations of everything moving below it. Rain that transforms the seam concerns such a lichen only indirectly, as a slight change in the humidity of the wall. It lives on dust, delayed daylight, and long intervals of near-suspension. Its growth is so slow that it seems less like an organism than a stain the stone has not yet finished making.

The scale-leaf plants practice a more visible patience. In drought, each is a gray, brittle rosette with no clear boundary between leaf and mineral flake. Wetting does not revive it dramatically. The center thickens first; the outer scales loosen; over several days the plant becomes translucent and olive where light passes through it. Even fully open, it remains low and spare, as though unwilling to expose any more of itself than necessary.

Their roots provide the closest thing in the Hollow to an inheritance. The fine grooves they cut retain dust and guide later moisture. When a plant dies, some of that script remains. Another root may follow it, deepening the line or branching from it, until a patch of calcite carries the faint record of several lives. None of this is soil, and none of it is deliberate shelter. It is merely that feeding leaves conditions in which feeding may later be slightly easier.

Their seeds are made for postponement. The hard cases protect them through dry intervals and eventually soften enough for water to open them. The slate-backed lizards simply refuse to wait that long.

Around the moss cushions, life becomes smaller and more numerous. The moss holds moisture after the visible seam has dried, and within it persists a film of algae too thin to notice except where the springtails gather. These are almost perfectly transparent. The clearest thing about them is often the dark thread of food inside the body. At their densest they resemble a movement within the dampness itself rather than animals upon it. When they molt, their abandoned skins remain for a while as colorless duplicates, so delicate that the next drop collapses them.

Mites work the edges of the moss and algal film. Some move so slowly that their progress can be judged only against a crystal or a moss stem. Others make sudden, short corrections, as if repeatedly discovering that the surface beneath them is not quite what they expected. Their grazing is continuous but slight. A cushion that looks untouched may support hundreds of tiny mouths without ever showing a bare patch.

The pale beetles are masters of another kind of stillness. Folded into their pockets, legs drawn close, they can appear not merely dead but fossilized. This is not the attention of a hunter. It is withdrawal: a reduction of life until the pocket, the beetle, and the dry interval seem to be waiting together. Humidity reaches them before standing water does. One may move an antenna, then nothing more for hours. Another emerges fully, walks a few body lengths, and stops. Their appearances are staggered, so even an awakening population does not look like an emergence.

The webless hunters are mostly flat, pallid spiders fitted closely to their crevices. Their stillness has a different quality. A beetle is waiting for conditions to change; a spider is waiting for something else to make a mistake. It rests with its legs touching separate grains and ridges, reading small disturbances through the stone. When it strikes, the whole action may occupy less distance than a fingernail. Then it returns to the crevice, and the wall resumes its appearance of being uninhabited.

The moths belong principally to the roof. During the strongest light they are almost indistinguishable from calcite crystals, their wings closed into narrow, angled forms. Movement makes them unexpectedly soft. A moth loosening its wings before flight seems less mineral than anything else in the chamber. Their flights are usually brief, from roof to wall or wall to basin, and they seldom remain long in the open air.

When they come down to drink, they stand on damp stone rather than on the water. The proboscis unrolls to the edge of the lens; the wings remain lifted and still. A whole visit may amount to no more than a bead’s worth of moisture. Then the moth rises, becomes a pale fleck against the dome, and is again difficult to find.

The smaller ash-colored lizards hunt among these crevices with an economy that resembles caution but is closer to arithmetic. A long chase would cost more than most prey repay. They therefore advance, stop, and advance again, taking beetles, spiders, or other small animals only when the distance has already been reduced almost to nothing. Their pauses are not empty. During them, the hunter and the hunted often appear equally motionless, and it is not always possible to tell which has detected the other.

The slatebacks make the most complicated use of this spareness. They are not large animals except by the standards of the Hollow: the biggest may be no longer than a forearm, much of that length tail. Their backs are the color of dark slate, but the pale dust soon softens the outline of one resting on the floor. Each sleeps in whatever natural pocket suits it. A favored cavity may be used repeatedly without becoming a home in any visible sense. There is no bedding, no stored food, nothing carried in.

A slateback’s range is better imagined as a sequence than a territory: several sleeping pockets, a few feeding places, a stretch of seam, perhaps two or three scale-leaf plants beneath which seeds sometimes remain. It travels this sequence without making a path. Where the ranges of two animals overlap, they often avoid one another simply by waiting. Even at water, several may drink around the same small basin with wide spaces between them. Their sociability consists largely of not requiring the others to leave.

The pebble-strike is equally free of ceremony. A lizard finds a seed, turns it until it sits securely against the floor, and braces it with one mobile forefoot. It then brings a pebble down with the other, rising slightly on its hind legs and letting the tail take some of its weight. Sometimes the case opens at once. Sometimes the seed jumps away and must be recovered. Sometimes the pebble strikes bare calcite and produces the clear, solitary click that carries so far through the room.

Individuals differ. Some test and reposition a seed repeatedly; others strike almost at once. Some use several light blows, while others rely on a single heavier one. Not every slateback uses tools with the same frequency, and some seem to prefer hunting to opening seeds. Young animals are conspicuously less accurate, though whether their later competence comes chiefly from practice, from watching adults, or from both is difficult to determine. The behavior has not become a craft. There are no standard stones, no special striking places, and no accumulation of skill visible in objects.

A pebble that fails is dropped. A pebble that works is also dropped. The lizard eats the exposed kernel, or the soft body within a beetle’s broken armor, and leaves the stone where it fell. The same pebble may serve another animal years later without carrying any trace of the earlier use. Thus the Hollow’s cleverest behavior is also one of its easiest to overlook. When it is not happening, there is nothing to show that it ever does.

A rain on the plain does not summon these lives together. It reaches them as a succession of different events.

For the springtails, it begins with humidity. For the beetles, it begins when the seam darkens and their pockets become less dry. The moss deepens to olive while there may still be no visible drop. The scale-leafs remain folded. Only later does water reach the basin, and only then do the moths have a reason to descend. By the time a clear lens has formed, some of the smallest animals have already been active for days.

For a short while, their separate clocks overlap. Springtails molt at the damp margin; mites spread through the freshened film; beetles remain out of their pockets; spiders find more movement reaching their crevices. Ash lizards hunt along the wall. Slatebacks come singly to drink or to take advantage of prey that can no longer afford prolonged stillness. There is more feeding, more drinking, and more killing than usual, yet the chamber remains quiet enough that a drop can dominate it.

The gathering then disperses without a clear ending. The moths stop coming. Beetles vanish one by one. The basin contracts to a dark patch, but the moss is still wet and the scale-leafs may only just be opening. Long after the standing water is gone, the rain continues underground as open leaves, active roots, and animals carrying a little more moisture than they did before.

Even death does not substantially mark the place. Small bodies are reduced within the same films and crevices that supported the living. Their substance returns to algae, moss, roots, and other animals without becoming soil. A cast skin, a displaced pebble, or a briefly darkened patch of dust may be the only evidence, and none of these lasts long in a form that can be read.

The Hollow therefore has no center of population, no ruling species, and no visible community that a visitor could enter or disturb all at once. Its inhabitants are connected without often being together. Light becomes leaf and lichen; leaf becomes seed; seed becomes a few seconds of precision beneath a lizard’s forefoot. Algae become springtails, springtails become prey, and prey becomes another period of motionless waiting in the stone.

To call the chamber empty is to mistake its open volume for the scale of its life. The distance between residents is part of their survival: fewer journeys, fewer mouths at any one place, fewer demands upon a seam that may remain dry for weeks. Nothing has filled the Hollow, and nothing has needed to. Its life is not merely scattered through the emptiness. It is shaped by it.
