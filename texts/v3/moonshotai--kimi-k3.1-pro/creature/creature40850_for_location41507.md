## The Population of the Low Works

No people live in the Low Works. The narrow beds accommodate no sleepers, and the toolshelves belong to animals that have never lifted a tool. Nor is there any separate ecology of vermin: no insects in the pleated folds, nothing nesting in the roofs, nothing feeding on the feeders. What lives there is the slate-colored multitude, repeated from thumb-length to cart-size. The longer it is observed, the less satisfactory it becomes to call the animals either vermin or machinery.

At close range, their industrial appearance is largely a matter of color and stillness. The pleats are not armor. They are long, muscular folds, cool and almost stony along the back, warmer and more yielding at the sides. A contracted animal can make itself nearly cylindrical; an inflated one opens lengthwise into deep gray ridges, each ridge carrying its own line of condensed water. The smooth head contains no expressive feature. Two shallow pits, usually half covered by a fold, may be eyes, but they do not shine and do not follow a passerby.

A small animal lifted from a basin is heavier than expected. It does not writhe or bite. Its blunt legs fold against its underside, its pleats close, and the pale plates around its mouth open and shut with a faint porcelain click. It feels warmly animal in the hand, except along the dorsal ridges, where the cold seems to belong to another body. Its indifference is not helplessness. It turns its head persistently toward iron, testing the air with its mouth. Returned to a pipe, it fastens at once and becomes difficult to distinguish from the pipe’s covering.

Their world appears to arrive through surfaces. A shout produces no response; it is merely more noise in the air. Two fingers pressed firmly to a rail may cause heads to lift along its length. Animals gather around vibrations too slight for a visitor to hear, and orient themselves toward a hammer-stroke felt through the pavement before its sound has crossed the court. Current, resonance, temperature, and mineral deposits seem to have more reality to them than streets or roofs. To live in the Works, as they do, is perhaps to inhabit a geography of live pipes, cold returns, resonant walls, and silent intervals.

Feeding is the unavoidable word for what they do, but it is misleading. Feeding ordinarily ends in satiety, shortage, growth, or waste. Here it ends in nothing visible.

The smallest animals work over the condensate basins and wet seams, rasping at white scale with their mouth-plates. Mineral moisture passes behind the plates; clear drops gather along their backs. Yet the scale never disappears, and the animals never become sluggish with fullness. A cluster may work the same hand’s breadth for hours, leaving it neither cleaner nor rougher than before.

Larger animals fasten themselves to cables, engine frames, and flywheel housings. On the power lines they hang mouth to cable and body to body, forming heavy loops between the insulators. Their plates ring once on attachment. After that, their pleats begin opening in time with something in the line. Around the engines they press close enough to seem in danger, but none is caught. Each loosens itself just before a moving part would pinch it, shifts by the width of a foot, and attaches again. The belts do not slow. The housings are not marked.

The greatest animals are less easily explained. They stand in the courts, breathing with slow, deep folds while smaller bodies cover and uncover their flanks. Water runs from them in brief, clear threads; warm grit collects beneath them and is taken away. It is natural to call them reservoirs, return vessels, or living sums of the smaller animals’ activity. Then one of them spends an entire afternoon in a dry court with nothing attached to it, doing nothing that completes the theory.

They do not maintain the Works, because nothing requires their repair. They do not strip it, because nothing is diminished. Whatever they take is returned before it can be called a loss. Their feeding is less a meal than an attachment without an aftermath.

The different sizes are not visibly ages. The thumb-sized animals are complete, with the same finished mouths and deliberate movements as the largest. They are not proportioned like young creatures, and they do not cluster around any particular adult. The cart-sized animals bear no special scars or other signs of seniority. There are no eggs, no pairings that can confidently be called courtship, no shed skins, no small bodies resembling incomplete versions of large ones. Neither are there carcasses. If generations occur, they leave no interval in which they can be counted. The population has a size, but no visible history.

Its crowding is not family life. Chains, bracelets, layers, and heaps are temporary arrangements, not loyalties. One animal clamps to another’s flank because that flank is available; later it releases and crosses the street. There is no grooming, no defense of feeding places, no competition for the mouth of a large animal. When a gap opens in a chain, another body usually fills it, but the displaced animal does not object. The small ones covering a great court animal are not necessarily its young, and it gives no sign of noticing when they leave.

And yet they are exquisitely responsive to one another. An animal joining a layer soon breathes with the bodies around it. A change in the rhythm of attachment passes along a pipe more quickly than any of them could walk. Their vast, flat hooting has no detectable address: it does not summon stragglers, clear a court, or announce a discovery. No leader begins it and no separate chorus answers. If it carries information, nothing in the Works acts upon that information. Their society, if it is a society, consists largely of being in time with whatever is nearest.

Still, they are not perfectly reducible to mechanism.

Occasionally one leaves a vibrating pipe and fastens instead to a cold railing. Another remains with its smooth head resting against a larger animal’s side, though there is bare iron within easy reach. A gap in a chain may remain open for several breaths because the nearest animal simply does not close it. Then it does. Nothing follows from any of these choices. No preference spreads, no new arrangement persists, and no other animal appears to learn. But for a moment the body has done something unnecessary.

That is where their character lives: in behavior without consequence, too slight to become habit and too individual to be mistaken for a regular process.

They respond to visitors in the same limited way. They open slowly around a descending foot and close behind it, without panic or resentment. They do not beg, follow, or gather to inspect. A lifted animal notices the loss of its surface and nothing else; returned to the ground, it does not run. It walks until it finds something worth attaching to. One can pass among them without being either welcomed or treated as an intruder. Their attention is brief, material, and sufficient.

They are hardest to explain inside the workers’ houses. Away from the strongest vibrations, they still occupy the floors, lie beneath the beds, and fasten themselves to cold iron bedsteads. They make no nests and move no furnishings. Rooms are neither defended nor marked. The houses never quite become dens; they remain furnished rooms through which animals happen to pass and settle.

Nor do the animals form a hidden workforce. They tend no valves, answer no whistles, and gather at the street signs no more than elsewhere. Their labor has no task that could be completed. If they reside in the workers’ houses, it is without domesticity; if they work in the factories, it is without shifts. The process names describe their surroundings better than any name applied to the animals themselves.

At the surveyed boundary, even these habits cease. There are no scouts on the plain and no straggling chain attempting to extend the circuit. The outermost animals occupy the last gutter, curb, or pipe support, and beyond them lie dry stone and silence. This does not look like fear of the outside. It looks more like absence of relevance. Beyond the last return, there is nothing their mouths recognize as a beginning.

It is therefore easy to leave the Low Works with a neat explanation: that the animals are mobile organs of the town, or that the town is their mineral body, or that engines and animals together compose a single organism whose divisions are only temporary. Close observation supports every one of these ideas a little, and none completely. The animals are too regular for an ordinary ecology, but too warm and occasionally wayward to be satisfactory components.

Their lives can almost be summarized: fasten, receive, loosen, cross, breathe, fasten again.

Almost.

The remainder has no consequence. It produces no offspring, no history, no completed work. It is only the individual weight in the hand, the arbitrary preference for one cold railing, the head lifted before anything visible happens, the single breath drawn out of phase with all the others.

The breath changes nothing. It is still a breath.
