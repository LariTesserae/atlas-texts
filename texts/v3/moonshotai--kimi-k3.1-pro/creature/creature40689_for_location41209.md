Nothing in the Condenser Ward keeps a household, but the chamber is not empty. Its machines occupy it almost completely. Its living inhabitants occupy the margins of that occupancy: a seam that stays wet, a lamp that burns a little longer than the others, a pocket of grit where dust and evaporated minerals have accumulated in the right proportions.

The machines have stations rather than territories, cycles rather than habits. The sealed turbines are present mostly as power delivered elsewhere. Pumps persist behind panels and under walkways, their slow rotation communicated through pipes. The presses have their brief, weighty closures; the gantry has its repeated journey between finished blocks and unfinished wall. The fin-brusher and wire-spooler perform work so narrow and regular that they seem less like individual devices than continuing features of the ward.

Calling them inhabitants does not require pretending that they are alive. A relay’s clack is not speech, and the red indicator lights are not watching. There is no hidden society in the cable trays. But the machines have duration, deterioration, replacement, and consequences. A failed pump leaves behind the geometry of its pipework. A new pressure vessel forces a stair to turn aside. A conduit becomes the core of a larger conduit; an anchor remains after the load it held has gone. In this sense, the ward’s mechanical population has generations. Its present arrangement is made partly from the accommodations imposed by earlier ones.

Life in the stricter sense begins where water is allowed to linger.

The lichen is the least conspicuous and perhaps the most patient. It lies so close to the basalt that it can be mistaken for a mineral stain until moisture reaches it. Dry, it is pale, tight, and brittle-looking. After condensation, its grey-green deepens and its edges soften. It follows the paths of dampness rather than spreading evenly across available stone: a narrow seam beneath a condenser, a discontinuous border around a bolt, a crescent where drops repeatedly strike and run. Some patches are marked with white or ochre at their margins, where the same water that sustains them also leaves behind a slow mineral accumulation.

Its dispersal is almost nothing at all. A fragment dries, loosens, and is lifted by a ventilation current or carried a short distance by vent-rain. Nearly every fragment arrives somewhere impossible: hot stone, clean steel, an oily casing, dust with no dependable moisture. The lichen’s presence in a new place is therefore less an expansion than an exceptionally unlikely repetition.

The moss makes richer but more conditional patches. Its cushions gather near certain service lamps, where warmth, illumination, and condensation overlap without becoming lethal. Almost black in a dry phase, it can become a deep, soft green after a release. Its finest leaves hold water without shining loudly; the whole cushion simply seems to gain weight and colour. Slender spore stalks sometimes rise above it, too small to disturb the ward’s prevailing impression of metal and stone.

When vapour passes through the upper galleries, spores may travel farther than any rooted fragment could. Most are lost. A few settle in ceramic grit beneath a cold return line or in the angle between a lamp bracket and the wall. There they wait through hot draws that would destroy anything less capable of suspension. A moss patch may thus appear where there has never been visible greenery before, with no connecting trail across the intervening stone.

The copper-coloured grass is rarer and more exposed. Each tuft has a kind of posture. Its narrow blades incline toward artificial light, some rising nearly straight, others leaning over the rim of their little accumulation of dust. Their roots and finer filaments penetrate no true soil. They occupy basalt powder, powdered ceramic, flakes of dead growth, and the salts left by repeated wetting and drying. The same mixture can support them or, after enough evaporation, bind them into a pale crust.

The grass spreads cautiously, chiefly around the edges of established tufts. Where a lamp is renewed or a pipe begins to sweat more regularly, new blades may emerge beyond the old outline. Where conditions fail, the tuft does not dramatically wither. It becomes quieter in colour, then wiry, then almost indistinguishable from the copper fittings and heat-stained debris around it.

The absence of animals gives all this an unusual stillness. There are no quick visits to the pools, no sudden consumption, no minor thefts of dead leaves, no tracks recording that something passed while the lamps were dim. Nothing grazes the grass back or opens a path through the moss. Nothing deliberately carries life from one ledge to another. Even dead material remains in place for a while: narrow pale blades among living ones, brown lichen fragments at the edge of a seam, dry moss reduced to a rust-coloured impression of the cushion it used to be. Decay, where it occurs, is too small and slow to give the ward a smell of rot. More often, remnants are washed into a sump, buried beneath fresh grit, or incorporated into the next layer of construction dust.

The shallow pools therefore do not become ponds in any familiar sense. They gather no visible community around themselves. Their surfaces are broken only by falling drops, ventilation tremors, and the occasional vibration transmitted through the walkways. The largest pool can reflect half the ward while supporting nothing that a watcher could point to. It is important less as a habitat than as a brief concentration of possibilities.

Every living patch is, in effect, a record of infrastructure. A gasket weeps for long enough to make a dark stripe on the stone; moss establishes itself within the stripe; the gasket is sealed, and the stripe outlasts the moss. A condenser is rerouted, leaving one lichen seam to dry while another begins several metres away. A lamp burns hotter before failing, scorching the grass nearest to it. Its replacement shines at a slightly different angle, and the next growth appears on the opposite side of the bracket. A new wall intercepts a ventilation current, and the spores that once reached a protected cavity arrive there no longer.

There is no steady succession toward a mature ecology. The ward has instead a series of temporary addresses for life. Some overlap; many do not. During the hot draw, whole patches contract into dormancy while others remain wet beneath an actively sweating line. During condensation, moisture becomes available in places that rain never reaches. Venting can awaken one alley and leave the next untouched. The living map changes not with seasons but with load, leakage, replacement, and the small thermal consequences of building another wall.

Seen from the central bridge, the entire settlement can suggest a single metabolism: heat drawn upward, power distributed, water recovered, material taken in and made into structure. But the ward need not be imagined as a creature, much less a mind. Its resemblance to life lies in persistence and circulation. It does not require citizens to complete it, and its unfinished passages are not necessarily waiting for anyone.

During a pressure release, the distinction becomes briefly visible. Warm rain ticks along one row of tanks. Beneath it, lichen softens, moss darkens, and a tuft of copper grass holds several bright drops without bending. Across the chamber, another patch remains dry and dormant. A gantry sets a honey-coloured block against raw basalt; a relay gives its precise clack; the rain narrows to dripping.

No footsteps approach the water. Nothing calls or answers. The machines continue their occupancy, and the few living patches continue theirs—each patch the temporary shape of an agreement between heat, light, and water.
