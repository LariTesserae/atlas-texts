Nothing in the Calving Basin belongs to only one element for the whole of its life. The floor-grazer has a winged season. A sump animal may wait out dryness as a leathery knot in a crack. The ox carries future pavement on its back. Even the people, who most want a path to remain a path, survive by treating every route as provisional.

### The living floor

The film itself travels. Water carries it, but so do the lips of oxen, the wet undersides of scale-lice, and the boots of people. A thin remnant can endure drying in the shadow of a shell flake, then spread again when condensation darkens the stone. This is why a newly fallen block need not be connected to an old pool by any visible stream. Life arrives on feet and in splashes, and often gets there before the block has stopped ticking.

The familiar scale-louse is only the settled stage of the animal. In this form it is all underside and shield: a broad, soft scraping surface beneath a flat mineral-colored hinge. It feeds with its body pressed almost flush to the stone, leaving fine, pale curls across saturated black film. The layered aggregations are not cooperative colonies. They are crowds. Individuals climb over one another to reach fresh grazing, moisture, or a place out of the crushing traffic.

Their escape is similarly individual. A scale-louse springs by snapping its body hinge, without much regard for direction. One jumps, strikes several neighbors, and causes them to jump; the disturbance passes through a sheet faster than any of the animals could run. Most land only a short distance away. Some come down on their backs and rock violently until their legs find purchase. Others begin feeding again almost immediately. The pouring hiss is the sound of their scraping and shell friction; the minute clicks come afterward, when they land.

Those living on molt-oxen are not principally blood-feeders. They graze film, dust paste, and sloughed material from the deep grooves between plates. Around a loosening molt they can become irritating, crowding against softer new tissue, and an ox will rub hard enough against a block to bring down both plate and passengers. Shedding is therefore more than growth. It disposes of excess mineral, old damage, and an entire population of unwelcome residents.

Under crowded, damp conditions, some scale-lice molt into a narrower form with wings folded beneath the hinge. These climb wall cracks, shell ridges, and the backs of oxen. When warm air rises through a roof-opening, they let go. For a little while the animal that had been part of the pavement becomes part of the weather. Most are taken by clapwings or dashed onto dry stone. A few reach another filmed surface, lose or fold their wings, and flatten again at a later molt.

The silence of the settled animals is as informative as their noise. It rarely happens everywhere at once. A patch no larger than a hide may stop while the surrounding floor continues to hiss. The stillness may spread, hold for several breaths, and then vanish under a fresh wave of clicking. Sometimes the cause can be found: a joint-runner passing underneath, a shadow, the first cool breath before condensation rain. Often it cannot. Experienced inhabitants do not assume that an invisible cause is a harmless one.

### The herds

A feeding molt-ox is unexpectedly delicate. Its soft lips close over a patch of stone and draw sideways, the inner ridges taking film and a little loosened mineral without necessarily breaking the surface beneath. When food is scarce it rasps harder, and the sound changes from a wet leathering noise to a dry scrape. A herd that has remained too long in one district begins to consume crust as well as film. Soon afterward, it moves.

Each back plate begins as a comparatively flexible growth and hardens outward. Seep water and swallowed mineral give it color and density. White, honey, iron-yellow, and dull red may occur in successive layers on an old animal, so that a plate can preserve a partial record of where the ox has fed. The record is temporary. Eventually the outer growth loosens, joint-runners gather around its edges, and the ox begins seeking angles against which to rub. When the plate finally comes away, it does so with other plates rattling against it and half a load of shell grit spilling from the groove. The new surface beneath is darker and less resonant until it hardens.

The great herds are not permanent, unified societies. Smaller associations of animals join around grazing and water, separate at broken ground, and lose or gather members along the terraces. Old individuals often approach familiar seep districts, but familiarity is only an advantage, not a map. A dependable seep may become a dry white basin; a court that once divided a herd may no longer have a roof or a floor.

The youngest oxen are soft-backed, comparatively light, and almost continuously vocal. Their peeping is answered by grunts from several adults, not only the mother. They ride where they can: in the deep plate grooves, against the broad base of the neck, or beneath an adult’s body, dropping down to feed or nurse and scrambling up again when the surrounding legs begin to move. As they grow heavier, they spend more time running in the sheltered spaces between adults. Many of the small peeps heard in a herd come from animals that cannot be seen at all.

A threatened herd protects its young chiefly by compression. Bodies close, gaps disappear, and the calves are forced toward the middle. There is no neat defensive circle. The whole mass may remain almost stationary for several breaths and then pour sideways with startling speed. An animal that seems incapable of anything but geological patience can still kill by simply arriving where a person or predator has no room to escape.

Oxen often hesitate on ground that ticks or flexes, planting their forward feet while the herd bunches behind them. This makes them useful pathfinders, but not reliable ones. Pressure from the animals behind can force the leaders onward; a floor that bears one ox may fail beneath fifty. People walking in an ox-wake inherit the herd’s cleared grazing and scattered scale-lice, not a guarantee.

The herds also transport the basis of their own food. Wet film remains in the folds of their lips and on their legs. Their dung feeds joint-runners and is eventually crushed into pavement. Their shed plates make shelter for smaller animals before becoming sediment. A herd does not merely cross the Basin. It carries pieces of one chamber’s ecology into the next.

### What runs between, and what waits beneath

The joint-runners are several kinds of narrow scavenger sharing a similar solution to crowded ground. Their bodies can flex at points that look like legs until they move. The smallest are no longer than a finger; the largest may be as long as a person’s forearm, though seldom thicker than a thumb. They can pass beneath a loose plate, turn inside a crack too narrow for their apparent width, and emerge on the other side already running.

Under the herds they take dung, afterbirth, shed tissue, crushed scale-lice, and fragments dropped by larger feeders. Some specialize in the fresh material around a molt. Others follow clapwing colonies and live on what falls from the roof. Around people they become bold very quickly. Fat, sinew cord, and the stitching of a water bag are all worth investigating. A hide left on the floor may be held down at dawn by more than its stones.

At a carcass, joint-runners usually arrive before anything larger. They enter at soft openings and along damaged plate seams, cutting narrow access ways through hide. Stilt-hunters steal pieces from the edges. If water rises, thread-eels and purses take what washes into the sumps. Scale-lice colonize the drying surfaces, and mineral growth begins binding the remainder. The rapid disappearance of a carcass is therefore not the work of one immensely efficient scavenger, but of several crews whose labors overlap.

The pavement-mouth is the conspicuous exception to the Basin’s general restlessness. It waits.

Its broad back is rough enough to hold shell grit, and in older animals a secretion cements that grit into a genuine crust. Film grows over it. Scale-lice settle on it. Its camouflage is not a perfect imitation of stone; it is the ordinary consequence of remaining still in the Basin. The animal beneath the crust is shallower and more flexible than the apparent slab, with much of its body folded around a wide, seam-like mouth.

It favors herd margins, old runnels, and places where smaller animals regularly pass. A healthy adult ox is too much for it, and a herd can crush it. Calves, joint-runners, landing clapwings, and careless walkers are another matter. When it strikes, part of the “pavement” folds upward and inward, followed by one heavy heave of the body underneath. It cannot pursue for more than a short distance. A miss leaves it exposed and awkward, and it may take hours to settle convincingly again.

Sometimes the only warning is a tiny, localized silence among the scale-lice riding its back. Sometimes there is no warning at all.

### The sump animals and the roof hunters

Thread-eels are pale, blunt-headed animals ranging from finger-thickness to the thickness of an old person’s arm. Their eyes are small; pressure, taste, and contact are more useful in the crowded lower water. They feed on submerged film, drowned scale-lice, joint-runner caches, and any carcass material carried into a sump. Several feeding together can knot so tightly that the water appears to be twisting around a single many-bodied animal.

When a sump begins to empty, the eels usually go before the water does. They force themselves into fissures that seem much too narrow, following moisture downward. The purses remain longer. A purse at rest resembles a small, closed hide bag. Feeding, it opens a broad slit and pulses water through its ribbed interior, straining out organic particles. Startled, it clamps shut and expels water hard enough to skip itself across a pool.

Some purses survive a short disappearance of open water by sealing a little moisture inside themselves and wedging into a damp shell hollow. If the hollow dries completely, they die there and are eventually found by joint-runners. When brine rises through connected cracks, more eels and purses arrive with it or emerge from deeper refuge. A pool can thus appear deserted one day and crowded the next without any animal having crossed the intervening dry ground.

The Basin people distinguish between the churn of feeding, the panic of stranding, and the deeper agitation that precedes a drainage. They harvest animals from emptying sumps when they can, but the exposed bottom is often the most dangerous part: soft sediment over flexing shell, with new brine already whitening elsewhere.

Above them, the clapwings live in overlapping roosts whose lower surfaces are never entirely still. Individuals crawl over one another to reach a launching edge, adjust their wings against condensation, and quarrel silently or with small, breathy cries. The louder clapping comes from close flight and from the stiff edges of their membranes striking together when a colony bunches in moving air.

They are not simply nocturnal. Their feeding follows the winged scale-lice and the pressure currents that carry them. A colony may pour from one skylight district at midday, feed in reflected sunlight over a white terrace, and be hanging in another chamber before dusk. Their droppings fall steadily into the lives below, feeding joint-runners on ledges and washing into pools during local rain.

Stilt-hunters wait where clapwings come down to drink, roost, or crawl through a narrow landing place. Folded at rest, a stilt-hunter looks like a small body supported by an unreasonable quantity of leg. Its broad, soft feet allow it to stand among shell litter without the constant ticking that betrays heavier animals. It can remain crouched for hours, then extend and strike in one motion, hooking a clapwing before the wings have fully opened.

It also takes joint-runners from carcass margins and follows herds for the small animals flushed by their feet. It wants nothing to do with the main mass of oxen. Where clapwings become restless on a landing shelf without any visible change in the air, a stilt-hunter is often the reason.

### The tool-users

The people add very little to the Basin’s total mass of flesh, but no other inhabitant studies its habits so single-mindedly.

They are not one nation, and the low lodge is not their capital. Small companies use the Basin in different ways: some spend most of their lives below, some know only a few districts, and some cross it when travel elsewhere is worse. Their equipment and signs look similar because shell, hide, bone, chert, and stone impose similar solutions. The meaning of an old ochre mark is not necessarily shared. To an experienced traveler, such a mark says first that hands were once here, and only second that those hands considered the place important.

Company size is governed chiefly by water. A large group can defend itself more easily but cannot necessarily drink at a succession of gypsum cups. It may split for several days and reunite at an old landmark, if the landmark and the route to it still exist. The lodge serves as a meeting place, repair shelter, and occasionally a place to wait for someone who may already have taken another way.

No animal below is truly domesticated. People can turn part of a herd with a hide baffle, noise, and careful placement on old ground. Turning animals for a few minutes is not the same as owning them. Healthy, fully plated oxen are difficult and dangerous to kill with hand tools, so much of the people’s ox meat comes from animals injured by collapse, mired at a changing sump, or already failing behind a herd. When they do hunt, they prefer separation and confusion to direct contest. A carcass is a race: hide, sinew, fat, and useful bone must be taken before joint-runners damage them or crust begins binding them to the floor.

Different companies make different use of smaller foods. Some spread hides beneath a heavy fall of winged scale-lice and pound the collected bodies with fat. Some take clapwings at landing cracks. Almost everyone gathers purses and thread-eels when a familiar sump empties safely. None of these is dependable enough to be called a staple. Dependability lies in knowing several kinds of opportunity and recognizing them quickly.

Water is treated more conservatively than food. What oxen can drink may sicken a person. Clear water may be brackish, and an inviting pool may conceal a draining crack or a thin shell margin. The best cups are small enough that their use requires patience: drinking, filling gut bags, waiting for the drip, and deciding whether the next company or the next day will find anything there at all.

A traveler’s equipment is correspondingly light and repairable. Clothing is stitched in hide panels so that a crusted or damaged section can be replaced without discarding the whole. Boots are scraped frequently. A chert core and a few good flakes, a bone awl, needles, cord, a shell scoop, fat, and water containers are worth more than a collection of specialized objects that cannot be mended. Sleeping hides are beaten, rolled, and carried rather than left invitingly on a platform. Anything left in the lodge is assumed to have been investigated by something.

Routes survive mainly as speech. They are not simple lists of directions but sequences of conditions: follow a particular sound until it changes; cross behind oxen rather than across an empty court; if a skylight has vanished, stop instead of searching for its former light; do not use a remembered cup unless the drip is still active. When companies meet, recent route knowledge can be more valuable than chert or fat. Old accounts remain useful too, though often as records of how someone responded when the expected route failed.

Children learn through listening games. They are asked to distinguish an ox shedding a plate from a small roof fall, water moving under stone from scale-lice moving over it, and the wing-noise directly above from its reflection in another gallery. They learn to walk without making shell pavement ring unnecessarily and to recognize the different meanings of dust on the tongue. A child who can name every animal but cannot explain why the scale-lice have stopped is not considered ready for difficult travel.

Within a company, reputations matter more than formal offices. One person may be especially good at reading ox plates, another at anticipating condensation, another at hearing a bad floor. In a dangerous moment, the others defer to the relevant experience. A patient listener can be worth more than an additional hunter.

The lodge itself is repeatedly occupied by its nonhuman residents. Joint-runners get beneath the sleeping stones. Scale-lice gather in old grease and around damp shell grit. Clapwings may roost under the highest ribs. Returning travelers clean what they need, repair the alarm cord, and do not expect to erase every sign of the previous occupants. An empty lodge is not abandoned. It is merely being used differently.

On an occupied night, it functions chiefly as a listening post. Someone may still be stitching a boot beside a small animal-fat lamp; water bags hang where their cords will twitch if chewed; the others sleep in short, irregular stretches. Outside, the noise separates into oxen, shell, wings, water, and stone.

If the nearest scale-lice stop, the listener wakes the others without needing to shout. No one runs immediately. Hands find boots and cord. They wait for a second sign: cool air, a change in the herd, a tick overhead, the small clatter of alarm shells. If condensation rain begins two terraces away and the lice resume, the people still check the roof and move their bedding. They do not call the earlier silence a false alarm. The next identical silence may belong to something else.

By morning the company may be gone, its grit route-line broken and its shelter partly struck. A joint-runner will test the grease left on a stone. Scale-lice will gather beneath the newest wet stain. The people’s footprints will begin to whiten.

What lives in the Basin is not, finally, a collection of creatures adapted to its chambers. It is a collection of creatures adapted to losing them—and to finding life already multiplying in the next one.
