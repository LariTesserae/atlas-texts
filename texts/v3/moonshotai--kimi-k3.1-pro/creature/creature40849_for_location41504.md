# The Tenants of the High Thatch

Most of the High Thatch’s inhabitants live in containers made by something else: a cup-plant, a chamber in a mast, a space between root arches, the abandoned basin at a dew station. Even the animals of the open air are organized around brief opportunities. They feed, mate, and travel quickly, then return to some small damp or durable refuge.

The plain looks vacant because the open plain is often only the space between one refuge and the next.

## The floor and its tenants

The grey-green covering is not a single plant. Its principal builders are slow rosettes called felt-leaves by travelers. Their outer leaves are thick and grey; the inner ones are greener and remain folded close except after rain. Instead of shedding dead leaves, a felt-leaf keeps them as overlapping sheaths around its base. Roots penetrate these sheaths, bind them to neighboring plants, and gradually turn each rosette into part of the common mat.

Between the felt-leaves grow wiry, creeping plants whose roots can span hollows without much stem above them. They are among the chief makers of the roof galleries. A depression that might otherwise have become a pool is crossed, generation by generation, until it has a living ceiling.

Some of the low domes are very old. Not necessarily old as individual plants—the growing points divide, die back, and replace themselves—but old as continuous root systems. Human routes have appeared and disappeared on them. Masts have risen beside them and fallen. There is no trunk to count and no single moment at which such a dome can be said to have begun.

At the surface, the common animals are small and drab. Grey springers feed on damaged leaf tissue and vanish into seams when disturbed. Mites work along the boundary between living and dead fiber. Beetles with polished backs carry fragments below, where pale larvae reduce them further.

The largest colonies belong to the seam ants. They seldom build anything above ground. Their nests occupy natural galleries and abandoned root channels, sometimes extending through the whole interior of a dome. They clear loose material relentlessly, cutting it into portable pieces and carrying it along established passages. The ammoniac sharpness in some chambers comes from their refuse beds and from the dense populations of other insects living around them. Those beds are not wasted either. Fungi thread them, larvae feed in them, and roots enter from above.

A hand-sized carcass left on the mat may be reduced by nightfall to skin, bone, and a damp stain. The pieces do not all go to one place. Some are carried into ant colonies; some are buried by beetles; some are eaten where they lie by animals that later die elsewhere. Nutrients move through the Thatch in stomachs and mouthparts as much as through roots.

Below the surface live the animals that rarely need to see daylight. There are flattened, many-legged hunters that lie motionless along gallery walls until vibration passes through the roots. There are thread snakes slender enough to follow a root channel, some nearly eyeless, others merely dark-adapted and reluctant to emerge. Drumfly young occupy the wetter felt, feeding at rootlets and in decaying pockets. Certain frog-like animals spend the driest intervals folded into chambers scarcely larger than themselves, their skin dull and their breathing almost imperceptible.

When rain comes, many of the creatures that suddenly appear on the plain have not arrived from anywhere. They were already there. The emergence is less an invasion than a change of address.

## Water with walls

A cup-plant is a pond with vertical shores and a weather of its own.

Its outer leaves may be old, scarred, and tightly bound by roots, while the inner basin remains smooth. In a large specimen, the water is dark enough that the bottom cannot be seen from above. A dipperful raised to the light, however, may contain a crowded little suspension: transparent wrigglers with visible guts, armored larvae lying flat until prey passes, eggs strung along a shred of leaf, and minute hunters that seem to consist mostly of appetite and a means of turning.

Different depths have different inhabitants. Some animals keep to the surface film. Others cling to the walls, where the water is slightly warmer in daylight. The bottom collects shed skins, drowned wings, and fine dark waste, but very little remains undisturbed. Burrowing larvae turn it over; frogs forage through it; the shrinking and refilling of the cup repeatedly changes who can hunt whom.

The cup frogs are not one kind of animal. The smallest can sit comfortably on a human thumbnail and may pass their whole lives within a few neighboring plants. Larger kinds shelter beneath the mat and climb to the cups after rain. Their calls differ as much as their sizes: a low booming that seems to come from inside the ground, a wooden knocking, a brief two-note sound easily mistaken for a stem ticking in the sun.

Some lay eggs directly in the water. Others fix them just above the waterline, where the first rise after rain releases the young. A few can endure the shrinking of a cup by retreating into the wet leaf axils around it. Those that cannot must complete their aquatic lives quickly or leave eggs capable of waiting.

The cups are isolated, but not perfectly sealed. Eggs adhere to wet feet and feathers. Tiny animals are carried in mud on a drinking browser’s lip or in the fiber around a frog’s toes. Fliers defecate into tanks. Snakes and birds remove frogs from one plant and lose part of a meal over another. Even a human water jar, abandoned along a route, can become a small and accidental colony.

The serpentine climbers know these reservoirs well. The larger ones ascend cup-plants and young masts by pressing alternate bends of the body into flutes and leaf scars. At a tank they may wait with only the head and a little of the neck above the rim. They take frogs, nestling fliers, and animals coming to drink. Their patterned skins are conspicuous if spread on bare stone; against wet fiber and tannin-stained leaves, they are usually discovered only when they move.

## The inhabited masts

A living mast is less like a tree than a stack of differently conditioned rooms.

The lower chambers are damp and crowded with insects. Higher partitions may be dry, especially where age or injury has opened a narrow vent to the air. Old leaf bases collect around the crown, making dense, fibrous pockets for nests. Storm damage creates entrances that are used long after the original wound has closed or rotted wide.

Drumflies make the masts audible, but the masts give the drumflies their reach. An adult calling alone produces a small, hard note. When many brace themselves at openings and imperfections in a stem, the hollow chambers take up the vibration. Stems of different ages reinforce different parts of the sound. A stand therefore has something like a characteristic chord, changing slowly as chambers decay, partitions fail, and new masts mature.

Experienced travelers sometimes recognize a familiar stand by its voice during an ordinary evening chorus. At full chorus this knowledge becomes useless. There are too many voices, and the hollow floor adds its own unpredictable responses.

The drumflies’ adult lives are short compared with the years spent below ground. They mate, disperse, and dust themselves incidentally with whatever the masts offer. When flowers open during a chorus, pollen catches on legs and bellies. Vibration releases more of it and sends dew down the flutes. The flies are not tending the plants. They are using one another, and the plants have become proficient at using the occasion.

Other residents use the same stems without participating in that mechanism. Small birds nest in abandoned borer holes. Furred softwing grazers roost in dry upper chambers, emerging in flocks when flowering or rain promises food. Predatory insects wait just inside openings. Snakes follow the scent of nests upward. Roots sometimes enter a damaged mast from below, so that an old stem becomes partly plant, partly animal shelter, and partly a conduit back into the mat.

Death does not make a mast vacant. For a time it may be the richest structure in its interval of plain. Its stored water leaks or is drunk through damaged tissue. Beetles and larvae enter the softening partitions. Fungi whiten the dead fiber. Birds enlarge old openings. When it finally falls, the hollow trunk shelters another succession of inhabitants, while humans may take poles, splints, or lengths of sound fiber from it. Nothing remains for long as a clean, useless log.

## The animals of the intervals

The grey striders are the animals by which travelers most often estimate distance, simply because a strider may be the only large moving thing visible.

They are tall without being massive. A narrow body is carried on four long legs, and each foot divides into broad, fleshy toes that spread under weight. On firm ground the feet leave surprisingly slight marks. Over a hollow section, the same footstep can send a boom wandering through the galleries.

A strider commonly presses and turns one forefoot before committing its full weight. Calves imitate the motion long before they are heavy enough to need it. Even so, animals are sometimes injured when old root bridges fail. For a creature so large, the Thatch’s most serious hazards are often structural rather than predatory.

Striders follow no simple annual migration. Their routes bend toward recent rain, flowering stands, and patches of soft new growth. They crop young mast shoots and fresh floor growth; in poorer intervals they strip older outer leaves and other dry forage that few animals can use. Much of their lives is spent walking between feedings.

The birds along their backs, called backpickers on several routes, take parasites from the hide and insects stirred around the legs and head. They also leave readily to hawk over a swarm or nest in a mast. The association is useful but not solemn. A bird may spend the morning on a strider and the afternoon stealing food from another bird fifty body-lengths away.

Strider dung is an event on the mat. Beetles and ants reach it quickly; larvae develop in its moist interior; roots concentrate beneath what remains. A carcass produces a larger disturbance. Scavengers arrive from the air and below the surface, and portions are carried far beyond the place of death. Long afterward, the patch may be recognizable only by a denser growth of felt-leaf and an unusual number of young plants.

After rain, the striders are joined in the open by the flying grazers. These are not all close relations. Some are insects rising directly from the mat. Others are softwings that have followed storm paths and flowering stands, their muzzles becoming plainly yellow or ochre with pollen. They feed in crowded flocks but roost separately, packed into different stems and dry chambers.

Hunting birds and long-jawed gliders gather behind them. The gliders roost on high dead stems or near the rim and enter a swarm with wings partly folded, opening only to brake or turn. Their long jaws snap among insects too small to be worth pursuing individually. When the swarm thins, they remain to take injured fliers and animals struggling on the wet floor.

At the rim, where cloud water is more dependable, life is less rigorously concealed. The hanging plant curtains stay wet longer. Small grazers work their surfaces, birds nest deep in the root nets, and insects accumulate in pockets that never quite dry. Animals move between this crowded margin and the sparse interior, carrying food, eggs, parasites, and nutrients with them.

## People, intermittently

No single people or way of life covers the Thatch.

Many of those who know it best keep their home hearths on sheltered rim slopes or beyond them. Others belong to families that have used particular interior routes for generations, though even they may spend much of the year elsewhere. Lowlanders often give all of them one name. Their own names, languages, and claims to routes are more various.

The absence of villages on the plain is not evidence that no one has wished to settle there. A fixed camp makes fixed demands. Its water must be dependable, its sleeping places must remain dry, and repeated traffic must not break the root arches under it. A camp that answers those requirements in one decade may fail in the next when a mast dies, a catchment is damaged, or rain begins following another path.

Experienced parties travel with broad, stiff soles that spread their weight and with staffs used as much for listening as for support. The sound returned by a tap matters, but so does the feel through the feet and the behavior of nearby leaves. A place that was safe last season may not be safe now. Sleeping platforms and peg ladders are tested before use; living roots can strengthen an old structure, weaken it, or do both in different places.

Routes are remembered as sequences of practical facts: where the floor becomes dangerously resonant, which basin is shaded longest, which cup is unreliable, which stand can be recognized in mist, where a fallen mast provides sound material. A knee-high cairn may be less a marker than a correction to memory.

During the loudest choruses, conversation stops. Some parties use a small vocabulary of gestures; others rely almost entirely on touch and the cord between them. A hand at the elbow, pressure on a shoulder, or a change in the cord’s tension can convey what a shout cannot. When the sound falls away, travelers sometimes continue signing for several minutes before remembering that speech has become possible again.

Custom around water varies. Along certain routes, anyone may drink from a maintained dew station but is expected to clear its catchment groove and leave the basin shaded. Elsewhere, particular families tend particular stations and strangers ask before using them. Green masts are generally left alone, not because they are invulnerable or sacred in any universal sense, but because cutting one wastes water, destroys nests, and may bring insects or rot into a stand on which many later travelers depend.

These agreements are not always kept. There are fouled basins, stripped dead stems, stolen cord, and stations abandoned after quarrels or deaths. The light human footprint is not proof of unfailing restraint. It is also the result of failure: heavy, wasteful, inflexible ways of using the summit tend not to last.

People have nevertheless added a few habitats of their own. Cup frogs lay in neglected clay basins. Mosquito-like larvae rise and sink in old jars. Birds nest beneath the steep roofs of dew stations. Ants use catchment grooves as sheltered paths, and serpentine climbers learn that a sleeping platform may contain eggs or roosting animals. A cord bridge becomes a perch; a cairn shelters a small colony beneath its lowest stones.

An abandoned station may therefore be more crowded than an occupied one. Roots pass through its lashings, felt-leaf establishes on the roof, and water continues to gather according to a human design that no human presently maintains.

The High Thatch has no single characteristic inhabitant. The strider is its most visible body, the drumfly its most unavoidable voice, and the mast its great piece of architecture, but none explains the country alone. Its life lies in the repeated movement between containers: up a stem, down a root channel, from cup to cup, from carcass to colony, from one brief opening in the weather to the next.

Then the shower passes, the wings fold away, and the plain resumes its immense vacancy. Nearly everything has gone back indoors, and most of the doors are leaves.
