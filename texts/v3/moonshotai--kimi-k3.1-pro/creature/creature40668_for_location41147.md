The Underbloom has very few inhabitants, but one of them is almost the whole place.

Nearly all the vegetation belongs to a single physiological individual. The apparent forest is a body with the manners of a forest. Its columns are not related trunks, its root pavement is not a congregation of competitors, and the crowns differing in leaf and stature are not successive generations. They are organs of one continuous organism, diversified according to light, mineral, and water. The central basin lifts and illuminates; the lower basin stores and circulates; the mineral galleries draw sustenance from raw stone. None is the heart. None is the oldest part.

Deep within the mat, the scarlet and ivory vessels maintain separate circulations. Their fluids move without a central pulse, driven by small pressure differences distributed through the whole body. The clicks inside the stems are the nearest thing it has to a heartbeat. Its warmth is likewise dispersed. There is no hot core and no cool extremity, only an immense organism keeping every part available to every other part.

This does not make it a god, or even demonstrably a mind. It does not answer speech, favor a visitor, or respond differently to reverence and carelessness. If it knows anything, its knowledge is embodied in adjustment: pressure met by pressure, loss replaced without surplus, damage closed without scar. Its unity does not require intention.

Even the buds need not be promises. “Bud” is a visitor’s word, based on the expectation that a folded green thing must eventually become something else. No seed, pollen, or opened flower has ever established that expectation here. Their folded condition may simply be their sufficient form, as a clenched hand is not necessarily an incomplete open one.

Against the enormity of that common body, the separate animals seem like punctuation.

The transparent creature in a water lens might be called a **lens-flex**, since movement is usually the first evidence of it. At rest, it lies along the woven bottom as little more than a clear thickness in the water. When it bends, a milk-white thread becomes visible along its axis, and the whole animal briefly takes the shape of a written stroke. It has no obvious eyes, hunts nothing, and never rises to the surface to breathe. It takes nourishment where the living floor releases a trace of sweet exudate. Its flexing may help it absorb that exudate, or may merely be what it does instead of swimming.

No smaller specimens, eggs, or discarded skins have been found. It is not recognizably a juvenile, and there is no reason to assume that another stage follows. Each lens-flex appears complete at the size and transparency it has.

The pale arthropods beneath the leaves are similarly self-contained. A **leaf-press** is flatter than a coin and so closely colored to the leaf underside that its legs seem at first to be small faults in the veins. It settles where two vascular lines meet. It does not chew the tissue or lay eggs in it. At long intervals, one limb changes position, preserving the exact contact between its body and the leaf as internal pressure shifts. When touched, it does not flee. It becomes, almost imperceptibly, flatter.

Neither animal appears necessary to the common body. Nothing pollinates on their behalf, nothing visibly preys on them, and no missing multitude can be inferred from their rarity. They are not the surviving remnants of a rich fauna. They are separate lives for which the Underbloom happens to provide enough room.

Then there are the residents.

They are people, or human enough that the distinction has no bearing on courtesy. There may be only a handful. The growth conceals them without concealing anything deliberately, and the chambers swallow their voices close to their mouths. One can pass within a short distance of a seated resident and notice only that a gray shape has slightly changed its posture.

They are not ghosts. They cast shadows, tire, sweat in the heat, and can be bored or irritated. Their faces and bodies differ, including in apparent age, but there are no children among them and no social position corresponding to elder. They sleep without establishing a common hour. They drink, though thirst seldom becomes urgent. Nothing is hunted, cooked, stored, or cultivated.

Their memories have not been erased; they have lost their indexes. A resident may remember rain, the balance of a metal tool, or a particular face with perfect clarity, yet be unable to place any of these things reliably before or after another. This is not confusion. They know where they are, what they prefer, and how to do what they are doing. They simply cannot use the Underbloom to corroborate a sequence. Water levels do not mark one, clothing does not wear into evidence, and even a much-used route remains physically identical to an untried one.

They keep their names. Names are useful because they require no chronology.

Nor are they uniformly serene. One may be curious about every visitor while another dislikes being spoken to. One knows the turns of the mineral galleries with uncanny exactness; another can always tell which lens contains a lens-flex but cannot reliably find the roofless enclosure. These are aptitudes, not duties. No resident tends the common body, guards the water, or speaks for the others.

When two residents meet, they may walk together through several chambers and then part without arranging another meeting. This is not coldness. The walk was complete. Affection, interest, and disagreement are all possible among them, but none naturally develops into a household, a faction, or a plan. Their closest approach to a shared culture is a set of small courtesies: making room at a narrow threshold, speaking softly near water, offering a drink in cupped hands without asking whether it is needed.

Their relationship to the made-looking things is less certain. Recognition and authorship have come apart. The three steps may fit one resident’s stride exactly, while that resident can say only that they knew the height of the lowest step before seeing it. Another calls the roofless enclosure “the room” but cannot explain whether this is because they built it, found it, or simply understood it immediately. None trims the roots away from the clean outlines. The common body observes those boundaries without assistance.

The objects need not be evidence of abandonment or disaster. Each may have become sufficient before it became complete. The steps reached the water; a longer stair would have reached no second level. The enclosure established an inside; a roof would have excluded no weather. The sill made crossing it feel different from walking around it. The basin embodied containment, though there was nothing it could preserve better than the root mat already did. In a place without accumulating necessity, construction has little reason to become architecture.

Thus the residents are neither the last survivors of a settlement nor the founders of one. They are not caretakers, chosen inhabitants, or a hidden ruling few. The common body does not need them. The small animals do not depend on them. Even the stone forms may not belong to them in any straightforward sense. They simply persist here without being organized into a history.

The common body adjusts its fluids. A lens-flex bends and vanishes again into clarity. A leaf-press moves one limb. Somewhere, a resident answers to their name or offers warm water in both hands. None of these acts is an omen, and none requires a sequel.

That is what makes life in the Underbloom so easy to overlook and so difficult to classify. It is not dormant, impoverished, or unfinished in its own estimation. It is full of gestures, and almost empty of consequences.
