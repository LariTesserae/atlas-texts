No one lives there in the sense of keeping the room. The threshold has no attendant, and no maker returns to press a second thumb into the clay. Most of the inhabitants have already been mistaken for the room’s furnishings.

The greater part of the life is vegetal, though not comfortably plantlike. Its clearest boundaries are chemical rather than physical. On the freshly exposed silica, transparent films release a faint mineral sweetness. Within the scalloped breaks in the mudstone, darker films work at sulfur and iron, cell by cell, century by century. Root hairs draw on both. The ribbons, fleshy leaves, and small seam-cushions are the visible abundance made possible by that almost invisible labor.

Energy passes from broken mineral to film, from film to frond, from dead frond back to film. Very little is discarded. Matter merely becomes progressively harder to assign to one body.

The larger growths may be several individuals. They may also be one individual with several recurrent solutions to the problem of shape. A black-green ribbon can be followed into a cushion of liverwort-like tissue and followed out again as a bundle of root hairs, without any certain point of transition. Some of the fleshy leaves share not only their veins but their injuries: a shallow crease appears in one, and generations later the same crease is discovered in another leaf on the opposite side of the pocket, already old. A root hairs’ breadth of warmth causes a milky growing tip to turn. Elsewhere, long afterward, a ribbon thickens as though answering it.

Nothing hatches. Nothing rises from the sediment as a seedling. A new tip is simply noticed among older tissues, pale and substantial, sometimes already connected at both ends. The spore veils do not disperse. They hang in the viscous intervals until their parent growth passes through them, or until they are found surrounding growth that has not yet quite arrived. Reproduction, if that is what occurs, cannot readily be distinguished from a body continuing to consider itself.

Death is similarly inconclusive. An old leaf does not fall. It becomes thinner, clearer, and less insistent upon its outline. Its skin remains capable of carrying the current and supporting films. Other leaves root in it. The grazer feeds from it. After enough time, it may be ancestor, food, substrate, or merely the oldest portion of a leaf that is still growing elsewhere. The pocket offers no practical reason to decide.

At first, the little grazer seems more definite.

It is small enough to be lost against a single mineral grain, a transparent body with no hard shell and no pigment except the dark green comma in its gut. Its many legs are finer than the root hairs among which it moves. The foremost ones test surfaces before the body reaches them; the others seem less to push than to persuade the surrounding growth that there is space for another step. It leaves no furrow, no wake, no widened passage. Where it has been, the vegetation closes without visible motion.

Its transparency is not camouflage. Nothing hunts it. It has no eyes because there is nothing for eyes to receive, and no quick muscles because nothing in its world needs to be overtaken or escaped. Even its feeding is almost without incident. It presses its mouth to a film and remains there while the film becomes thinner. After a meal on the oldest leaf-skins, the comma in its gut pales to gray-green. After feeding near the threshold, it becomes nearly black. Its recent whereabouts could be read from that one internal stain, if there were any reliable way to establish which moment was recent.

For the grazer, the pocket is neither dark nor especially small. Its geography is made of flavors and resistances: iron near the walls, stale sweetness on the ghost-leaves, the faint richness of clay film, the cold open water that is never truly open. Some of these places neighbor one another although their bodies do not. It can spend what would be, for a larger animal, an entire journey without crossing any visible distance.

No eggs, young, cast skins, or dead bodies have ever been found. This proves little. Nothing here is conveniently past. The animal may be the latest member of an immense lineage, each generation so long-lived that only one is usually required. It may be several generations occupying adjacent moments. Or it may be one appetite, prolonged beyond any natural term by the pocket’s reluctance to let an event finish cleanly.

When the second pale shape follows it, the difference is not always that of a simple echo. The follower sometimes repeats each step exactly. At other times it turns before reaching the place where the leading animal turned. It may carry a faint green stain of its own, or it may be empty clear through. The two never engage in anything recognizable as courtship, conflict, or recognition. If their outlines coincide, there is no collision. Afterward, one grazer is visible.

A census is therefore less useful than a statement of activity: *grazing occurs.*

The pearl-white moss is easier to distinguish, though not necessarily easier to explain. It is the only growth that keeps a fairly consistent edge. Each pale point is minute and almost opaque, and the whole colony rarely extends beyond the clay’s squared lip. It grows most densely in the shallow impression beneath that lip, not because it recognizes a handprint, but because the film it uses gathers differently in the hollow. A touch whose meaning has vanished remains effective as a small difference in surface.

The moss has no knowledge of thumbs, makers, doors, or intentions. Nevertheless, it preserves the impression more faithfully than burial would. The grazer removes film from the surrounding clay but seldom clears the hollow completely. Larger fronds approach and bend aside, or are found to have left the place open. Without cooperation, and without purpose, the living things maintain the artifact as a niche.

When the threshold is absent, the moss is usually absent with it. It does not hang forlornly in the water or colonize a neighboring root. Yet the grazer sometimes follows the same short, squared route through the rectangular heaviness that remains. It pauses where the lip would be. The comma in its gut darkens. When the clay is present again, the white moss is often already occupying the print, with no visible frontier of colonization to show how it got there.

This is the closest the pocket comes to keeping house.

At long intervals, the current brings the inhabitants something from beyond their immediate chemistry. A faint recollection of a bank before inundation passes through the root hairs. A frond alters its growth toward a warmth that will never arrive. The grazer stops on the clay, all its legs settled, which is its nearest approach to attention. It cannot remember stems crushed in open air or rain striking a surface. No single organism in the pocket remembers those things. But for a while the recollection passes through film, tissue, gut, and water, and is not entirely lost.

Nothing in their behavior requires the seam. None of them migrates toward it, releases spores through it, or waits beside it for a change in season. The current already disregards walls when it needs to. The vegetal bodies have their stone, their old tissues, and one another. The grazer has films that accumulate more quickly than it can exhaust them. The moss has clay, when clay is there.

So the final sealing would not make the pocket a tomb. It would scarcely alter the terms on which life continues. The inhabitants’ world would not become smaller; it would merely lose its last reachable elsewhere.

Life there is not a vigil. It is a set of small practices: film feeding frond, frond thinning into food, moss occupying a hollow, an animal taking the next step. Performed patiently enough, these practices have made even uncertainty habitable.

And if a second pallor gathers just behind the grazer, that too is habitable. The plants become a little less crowded around both animals, or around the single animal they may be, and the room continues without ever learning how many lives it contains.
