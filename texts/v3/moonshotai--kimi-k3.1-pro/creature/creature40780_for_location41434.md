## What Lives in the Undermere

The first inhabitants are easily mistaken for weathering.

A rust-orange seam at the lip of a pool, a grey slick on submerged limestone, a milk-coloured cloud leaning away from a seep: these are the Undermere’s pastures. They are not all the same life, and not all are food to the same mouths. Some films flourish where sulphur stains the clay yellow; others gather around cold, iron-rich upwellings. A slateback may rasp one stone down to clean grey while leaving the crust on the next untouched. When the flow or chemistry changes, a film can loosen in translucent sheets, and what looked like a permanent stain becomes food drifting through the water.

Most of the basin’s animals live at two tempos: long stillness, then sudden concentration.

The pale swimmers in a small pool may hang for hours with almost no movement, letting the seep bring particles to them. Disturb the water and they scatter in bright, brief stitches, then settle again. Blind eels knot themselves around seep mouths not only to feed but to anchor themselves. Their heads point into the current; their bodies keep one another from being drawn into the conduits below. Pull one loose and the knot seems to dissolve, each eel sliding separately into a crevice. A little later, the knot has formed again.

Veilwing larvae are less patient. After rain, when films spread quickly over newly submerged stone, they graze in dense, shifting bands. A stone behind them is left visibly cleaner, though not for long. Their buried eggs can wait through the contraction of a pool, and their hatching is timed more closely to water and chemistry than to any season. The adults rise in columns above the pans, wings repeatedly dulled by mist and cleared again by shaking. They do not need to live long. Mating, laying, and becoming food for something else occupy nearly their whole aerial existence.

The larger gliders come to these emergences without hurry. They roost flat against the lee sides of domes or beneath low ledges, where folded wings and mottled bodies can be mistaken for pieces of wet hide. In flight they are broad and quiet, making small corrections rather than steep dives. Often the clearest sign of one is not the animal itself but its reflection passing over a pool. Where several gliders turn above otherwise empty clay, there is usually a hatch below them, or has just been one.

The roar has made subtle airborne calls nearly useless, but it has not made the basin’s animals insensitive. Many of them hear through contact. Limestone carries a sharp strike a long way; wet clay dulls it but reveals weight and hesitation; water spreads pressure around obstacles. A body resting on the ground can receive information that an ear in the open air cannot. Stillness is therefore not necessarily inattention. A motionless animal may be listening with its feet, belly, jaw, or the whole length of its spine.

The slatebacks are the most visible expression of this tactile life.

Their segmented backs flex as they cross uneven stone, so that even an old, heavy animal can keep its body low and its broad feet planted. On soft clay the feet spread, leaving dish-like tracks; on limestone the same feet give purchase without much noise. The crescent mouth works close to the surface. A grazing slateback does not simply wander with its head down. It tests, scrapes, turns slightly, and scrapes again, following the richest part of a film too thin for a person to see.

The youngest remain close to reliable shallows, where their tracks overlap in confusing little clusters. Larger animals range farther. Herds join and divide as seeps enlarge or fail, and the several hundred animals packed around one upwelling may not constitute the same gathering a few days later. Within a herd, contact is maintained through the substrate. A hard, abrupt blow can bring grazing to a stop; slower repeated strikes help keep animals moving together when fog hides all but the nearest backs. The young answer with lighter impacts that an experienced slateback can apparently distinguish from the incidental tremors of water and stone.

They are not wholly defenceless against clayjaws, but their caution is imperfect. A herd may cross a dangerous pan in a close, steady body, the smallest animals surrounded. At other times a hundred may pass without incident, only for one animal to stumble into a hidden soft place. Its irregular struggling is the signal the buried hunter has been waiting for.

A clayjaw’s exposed sensory seam gives little indication of the body beneath it. Nor does the size of the visible closure reliably indicate the hunter’s length. What is known best is its patience. It can ignore rain, flowing water, the passage of healthy herds, and the ordinary ticking of scavengers, then respond to one particular sequence of pressure through mud. The strike is not a chase. It is a brief revision of the surface: clay shuts, water rings outward, and the tracks leading into the place have no continuation.

Afterward, the pan becomes crowded.

Shell-legged waders arrive first, their narrow, plated limbs leaving stitch-marks around the disturbance. Their bodies are carried low, and their weight is spread among enough feet that they can investigate soft margins without immediately miring themselves. They pick at exposed tissue and at the swimmers drawn to the clouded water. Small eels enter from below. Gliders remain on nearby stone until the larger slatebacks have moved away, then descend one at a time. Even people may examine an old kill from a safe distance, though few will contest a fresh one. Within a short while the congregation disperses, leaving scutes, fragments, disturbed clay, and a richness in the film that was not there before.

Pressure changes sometimes bring up stranger inhabitants. A siphon may discharge soft, colourless swimmers, transparent-shelled creatures, or eels that belong ordinarily to the flooded chambers. Some are dead on arrival; others writhe briefly in water whose chemistry does not suit them. Waders learn to gather at outlets where this happens often. People pay attention for another reason: an unusual fall of deep animals can indicate that water is moving through routes it does not commonly use.

When the surface pools contract, much of this life does not die so much as narrow. Films retreat toward permanent springs. Swimmers gather in the last connected water. Eggs remain in silt. Eels withdraw into conduits. A seep that appears nearly abandoned may be only the visible neck of an occupied system extending far below it.

Human beings fit into the Undermere as another sparse, route-following population, not as its masters.

They are not one uniform people across the whole basin. Beside a dependable spring, a household may occupy the same sheltered ground for generations. Elsewhere, families move between seasonal huts, following herds, eel runs, or the changing usefulness of small pans. Some dwellings hold several related adults and their children; others shelter a pair of old people, a solitary traveller, or a temporary gathering of hunters. A hut standing alone in fog may nevertheless belong to a wide web of obligations stretching days in every direction.

That social world travels along routes. Chert, salt, sinew, shell blades, news of deaths and births, and warnings about altered throats pass from hand to hand. So do people: a young adult goes to live with another household, a child is sent to learn a different set of paths, an injured traveller remains until able to walk again. At certain reliable seeps, several households occasionally converge. For a few days there are arguments, exchanges, courtships, repairs, and more voices than a traveller might otherwise encounter in weeks. Then the gathering breaks apart, and the stone domes appear uninhabited again.

Subsistence is correspondingly opportunistic. Low stone weirs guide eels into places where they can be taken. Slatebacks provide meat, fat, hide, sinew, and bone, but no herd is tame, and a large kill is often shared because keeping it in the damp is harder than distributing it. Salt is gathered only from certain pans; clear water is not assumed to be drinkable merely because it is clear. People learn the taste and aftermath of particular seeps, sometimes by hard experience. Waders steal scraps. Eels enter poorly closed containers. Stored fat becomes more valuable than almost any ornament.

Outside, much human communication is manual. A palm dragged across stone can indicate a slateback; two hands closing abruptly can warn of a clayjaw. Touch is used when sight fails or when a message must not be misunderstood. Patterned strikes carry between parties who can no longer see one another. Spoken language remains important, but much of it belongs indoors, behind turned entrances and heavy hides.

This does not make the people silent by temperament. Inside a well-baffled hut, they can be talkative, argumentative, and loud. Children who have moved with exaggerated care across a pan may shout over one another beside the lamp. Songs and stories sometimes preserve the cadence of routes whose outdoor version is made with stone and foot rather than voice. A traveller may remember a safe crossing not as a picture but as a sequence: rounded cairn, fractured cairn, black clay under the left foot, the dome that gives a hollow answer when struck.

The hand-shaped route markers are practical objects before they are monuments. Their meaning can be read through a hide mitten or in darkness. Some have been repaired so often that stones of different ages lie together; others have almost vanished, leaving only a gravel seam. A good traveller knows when a remembered marker should have appeared and treats its absence as information. The most dangerous journeys are often made by people whose memory is nearly, but not quite, correct.

Children learn the cairns before they are permitted to range far. They also learn that bad ground should not be crowded. If someone begins to mire, the impulse to rush forward can endanger everyone and may produce precisely the struggling vibration a clayjaw detects. Cords, patience, and one person giving directions are more useful than heroics. These rules are taught seriously, though not always obeyed.

The oldest warning remains the abrupt silence of a throat. People disagree about many other signs. Some trust the behaviour of eels; others watch herds, mist, or water rising in footprints. Some households speak of the throats as living things, while others describe them in strictly practical terms. On the silence itself there is little argument. One leaves. No reputation for courage or experience makes waiting beside the mouth a sensible alternative.

Nor is their knowledge infallible. Half-buried huts and abandoned routes record misjudgements as faithfully as surviving settlements record good ones. Seeps change. Flood stains are sometimes misunderstood. A safe clayjaw crossing can become unsafe without any obvious alteration of the surface. The inhabitants endure not because they have perfected the basin, but because enough of them notice, remember, and pass on its revisions.

At the dim end of day, human life contracts to small sheltered spaces. Wet hides are eased away from the entrance. Clay ear protection is removed. A fat lamp is fed sparingly, and someone repairs cord while another recounts, perhaps for the twentieth time, how a particular throat behaved before a flood. Outside, a herd settles into shallow water until its backs resemble a field of low stones. Waders finish working over an old disturbance. A late veilwing hatch rises where there was only bare clay a short time before.

The Undermere’s abundance is not spread evenly. It comes in knots and pulses: film on one seep stone, eels around one opening, hundreds of slatebacks beyond one imperceptible rise, wings above a pool that will soon shrink, human voices behind one hide curtain. Between these concentrations lie long stretches that seem empty, and sometimes genuinely are.

Nothing roots there. Life holds on instead: film to stone, eggs in silt, eels to one another, herds through signals in the ground, people through low walls, carried memories, and a hand on the wrist in fog. The roar continues over all of it. Beneath that roar, the basin is neither quiet nor uninhabited.
