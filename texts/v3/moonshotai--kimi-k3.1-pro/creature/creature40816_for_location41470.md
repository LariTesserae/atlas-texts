Life on the plateau is governed less by season than by permission.

A little condensation permits a microbial film to resume its interrupted chemistry. A brief thaw permits an egg to hatch. A death permits hunters to gather and, perhaps, to breed. Between such permissions, an inhabitant may be frozen, dried, or simply so economical that living is difficult to distinguish from resting. At any one moment, most of the plateau’s population is not visibly doing anything. This is not the same as being absent.

The stone itself is inhabited at several depths. Closest to the surface are the films that millbacks scrape: patchy associations of iron- and sulfur-using microbes, together with organisms that live on what those first microbes leak, excrete, or leave behind when they die. Farther down, in pores rarely reached by a mouth, other populations persist through much longer dry intervals. The pale quartz veins are not food, but their margins matter. Water gathers differently where vein meets bedrock; grains loosen at another rate; films acquire a different composition. A millback appearing to graze along a pale line in the stone may actually be harvesting the dark, nearly invisible margins on either side.

The fitted sockets beneath boulders are small countries of their own. Dust collects there. Condensation lingers. Some of the films are never exposed to daylight, and some of the animals that feed on them may pass their entire active lives within the space between a boulder and the hollow worn to receive it.

In the brine hollows, where liquid can persist beneath clear ice, life becomes slightly more crowded. Transparent grazers move through concentrations of microbial cells, and still smaller transparent hunters move through the grazers. Their world may be no wider than a hand, yet it contains feeding, avoidance, reproduction, and dormancy. When the brine finally freezes, movement does not so much cease as become postponed. The clear ice above them can make the whole community appear embedded in stone.

The visible growth is old even when it is very small. A reddish cushion no larger than a fingernail may represent centuries, though its tissues have been actively growing for only a small fraction of that time. It does not add a little each year. It grows when water and temperature allow, loses a little to frost or wind, and waits again. The black-green lichens survive in grain boundaries partly because those positions shelter them and partly because they are awkward to scrape. Nothing on the plateau is deliberately spared. Enough is merely missed.

The dormants are not one kind of creature. The name covers many minute animals whose lives include the ability to stop. Some are eggs with a larva folded inside. Some are larvae that have lost nearly all their free water. Some are adults capable of suspending themselves just before laying. Beneath one damp slab, thousands may be packed into a handspan, but a single wetting never wakes them all. Their thresholds differ. This protects them from the false abundance of a fog that wets the stone for an hour and then freezes hard.

Those that do wake complete what they can. One feeding, one molt, one act of laying may be all that the interval permits. Development is not merely slowed; it is broken into separately possible pieces. An animal might require several widely separated wettings to reach the stage that another creature completes in an afternoon. For a few hours, however, the underside of a slab can contain more movement than an expanse of open rock visible for days. Transparent bodies cloud slightly as their guts fill. Minute hunters alter direction. Eggs are deposited in mineral seams. Then the water withdraws or freezes, and the congregation becomes indistinguishable from dust again.

Wind is their means of migration and also their principal waste. It lifts eggs and larvae with the mineral grains and scatters them across distances far greater than any active journey they could make. Nearly all arrive where nothing useful happens. Enough arrive beneath a slab, in a brine seam, or beside a future deposit of millback paste.

Against this intermittent multitude, the millbacks seem continuous.

They cannot stop as completely as a dormant can. Their deepest cultures require a degree of continuity, and their bodies are refuges for organisms that would not long persist unprotected on the open rock. But they have made continuity extraordinarily cheap. Their size is not evidence of rich pasture. It is one of the ways thin pasture becomes usable: mass buffers cold and scarcity, the layered hide prevents that buffer from leaking away, and the chambers within allow intake to remain useful long after the place from which it was gathered has been left behind.

An adult carries its own small climates. At the outer tips of the gray filaments, conditions are nearly those of the air: dry, cold, dusty, and sometimes strongly charged. Nearer the skin, the filaments hold still air and a little moisture. Deeper within the body are the grinding chambers and the warmer, wetter cultures. Some of the microbes in those cultures occur elsewhere only in millback paste or in the bodies of other millbacks. The animal is therefore not simply a consumer of the plateau’s ecology. It is also one of the places where that ecology lives.

The lips are among its most discriminating organs. They test before they scrape. A stain resembling a usable film may be rejected; a darkening too slight to see may be followed carefully along a seam. The narrow mouth takes material in small ribbons, often including the condensation that loosened it. Feeding does not leave a visibly cropped patch. At most, wet light may reveal a difference in reflectivity, or a practiced eye may notice that one band of gravel has lost its faint organic sheen.

The grinding stones are not interchangeable. Hard fragments endure; softer ones are gradually reduced and replaced. A millback selects new grit with surprising care, though the criteria are entirely its own. Once swallowed, a stone may remain in service while several seasons of weather pass outside. The chambers do not advance material merely because time has elapsed. Intake can be held, wetted, acidified, cultured, ground again, or moved into conditions favoring a different association of microbes. Some of the animal’s eventual nourishment comes not directly from what it scraped, but from what grew and changed while being carried.

Consequently, a millback can be full and still be failing. Age, injury, or the collapse of an important internal culture may make its intake increasingly useless. Such an animal continues to feed for a long while. Its slowness conceals the difference between economy and decline until a hunter begins to take an interest.

A millback’s world comes to it largely through chemistry, texture, and contact. Its eyes serve well enough in the pewter light, but food is found by the mouth and moisture by the changes in air and stone. The broad pads conform to gravel and register small differences in compactness. Close by, another animal’s shift of weight may be felt before it is clearly seen. At greater distances, grazing traces, paste, scent on wet grains, and long familiarity with a feeding ground matter more.

Millbacks do not call, but they are not without social information. Their vocabulary is largely spacing and residue: a strip already grazed, a fresh deposit, the orientation of another body, the decision to remain or turn aside. When adults meet, they may appear to do nothing. They stand within sight of one another, feed, or sleep through the same fog. Yet mothers and young recognize each other, adults avoid recently worked ground more consistently than chance would explain, and rare contacts allow microbes and hide passengers to pass through the population.

Young millbacks are scarce enough that their presence does little to alter the visible texture of a herd. At first one remains close enough to its mother for their outlines to overlap. Later it feeds a few body lengths away, often on ground her feeding has prepared. It mouths what she mouths and acquires the beginnings of its internal cultures from her muzzle, her immediate surroundings, and the fresh paste near her. Its first hide passengers cross in the same intimate traffic.

A young animal is often easiest to recognize because it moves unnecessarily. It turns its head more often, tries unsuitable grit, and takes steps that an older animal would have saved. With growth, its filaments lengthen and its outline becomes less definite. It does not so much learn to resemble a boulder as gradually cease performing the small movements by which it had been distinguished from one.

The herd itself has no gathering and no leader. Its movement is a bias repeated across innumerable feeding decisions. If condensation becomes more frequent along one side of a swell, animals linger there. Grazing changes the films; paste changes the hollows; later animals find the altered ground worth visiting. Some individuals travel while others remain feeding for weeks. There is no day on which the herd departs. Decades later, its center is somewhere else.

Fresh millback paste is both an ending and a beginning. It is dense with ground mineral and gut cultures, and it changes the way a hollow wets, dries, and freezes. The first organisms to flourish in it are not always those it carried; windborne cells and dormant stages may join them. In time, the deposit becomes less recognizable as waste and more recognizable as pasture. A later millback scraping there may be feeding on ground prepared by an animal long dead. This, more than proximity, gives the herd continuity.

The hides carry another kind of continuity. Their passengers are zoned as strictly as life in the rock. Some cleaners remain among the cold outer filaments, feeding on trapped organic dust. Others live nearer the skin, where shed material and moisture support richer growth. Some consume parasites or damaged tissue; others merely consume what would otherwise accumulate. Not all are beneficial, and even a usually beneficial population can become burdensome on a failing host. “Cleaner” describes an ordinary effect, not an agreement.

Certain passengers can reach another host only when two millbacks touch. Others leave dormant stages in paste or release them into the wind. A cleaner lineage may therefore persist far longer than any one animal, passing from parent to young and occasionally sideways through the herd. When a host dies, its hide becomes briefly abundant and then uninhabitable. Some passengers join the general dismantling of the carcass. Some enter dormancy. Most simply disappear into smaller mouths.

The pale hunters live on the exceptions to millback endurance.

They are capable of more movement than almost anything else of their size on the plateau, but they seldom spend it. Their narrow bodies can remain motionless beside dark stone for long intervals, their pallor resembling exposed quartz when glimpsed through fog. On open rock they are conspicuous once seen; the difficulty is that there is usually so much empty distance around them that the eye does not expect to find anything there.

They follow the herds by what the herds leave: paste, scent, grazing traces, and the chemical alterations of sick animals. They can afford to investigate more widely than a millback because their food, when it comes, is concentrated. They cannot afford pointless contest.

Testing a millback consists mostly of approach and withdrawal. A hunter comes near enough to learn whether the animal turns its head, shifts its weight, changes its breathing, or places itself more firmly between the hunter and its young. A healthy adult needs to do very little. Its mass, hide, and continued responsiveness make further attention unprofitable. The hunter leaves, sometimes returning after an interval during which the millback has appeared to do nothing at all.

When the hunters finally close in, there may be no clear moment at which waiting becomes killing. An animal too weak to rise is still alive; one too weak to keep them from the softest tissue is dying. Often several hunters arrive without having traveled together. They do not cooperate so much as converge. Feeding order is maintained by posture, distance, and occasional brief withdrawals. There are no cries over a carcass and no chorus afterward.

A death briefly synchronizes the plateau’s different clocks. The body is warm, wet, and concentrated: a small province of abundance. Hunters take the most accessible soft tissue. Moisture entering the adjacent stone wakes some of the dormants. Microbial growth accelerates. The hide community increases for a while, then loses the structure on which it depended. Soft tissue disappears first, then hide, while increasingly small organisms work into what remains. Bone persists until its organic material has been removed and weather can reduce the mineral residue. Worn teeth and a few former grinding stones may outlast the recognizable outline.

Afterward, the dark stain and slight enrichment of the hollow are not merely traces of death. They are a change in future feeding conditions. In time, something will grow there thickly enough to be scraped.

Rarely, water and electricity make the whole arrangement visible at once.

A long dry stillness charges the millback filaments. Then cloud settles against the ground, or freezing drizzle darkens the stone. In the slab shelters, some dormants begin to wake. Films resume their chemistry. Millbacks lower their heads to the newly loosened crust. At the same time, charge leaks from the herd in minute blue-white branches, and cleaners withdraw toward the roots of the filaments.

The discharges do not coordinate the animals. No signal passes from back to back, and no response travels through the herd. Yet their accidental succession reveals what daylight conceals. One nearby outline flickers; far beyond it, another appears; between them lies darkness that seemed empty but is only unlit. From a height, the eye can at last perceive population rather than scattered individuals: not a multitude pressed together, but a vast spacing of lives, each maintaining its own warmth, cultures, passengers, and intake.

By morning the light has gone. The films are frozen again or have withdrawn into pores. The young have moved a little farther from their mothers. Hunters have resumed their testing at a distance. A meal has advanced from one chamber to another, and a deposit left generations ago has become slightly more worth grazing.

The plateau is therefore sparsely active more truly than it is sparsely inhabited. Much of its life is stored: in eggs beneath slabs, in frozen films, in passengers waiting at the roots of hair, in a meal that will not finish digesting for years, in pasture that has not yet grown. The largest animals carry the smallest, and the smallest make the largest possible. Death becomes intake; intake becomes transport; transport becomes the conditions for later life.

The plateau is not waiting for life to begin. Life there has made waiting one of its forms.
