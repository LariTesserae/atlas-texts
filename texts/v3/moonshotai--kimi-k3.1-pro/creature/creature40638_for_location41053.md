The hollow has no keeper. It has inhabitants, and everything in it is used, but no one there holds the office that visitors expect.

The people are the easiest inhabitants to overlook. There are never many of them—perhaps a dozen, perhaps fewer when some are sleeping in the roof chambers—and they do not conceal themselves. They merely wear the colors already present: clay gray, dull ivory, the olive-brown of old vegetation. A figure seated at the back of a recess can be mistaken for a folded bundle until breath clouds above it. A person standing beside a pale wall may be noticed first as a hand.

They are of ordinary stature. They are not spirits, and they are not physically adapted to the cold in any way an outsider would consider sufficient. Their lips crack. Their fingers become clumsy. Old injuries trouble them on the narrow stairs. They wrap themselves in layers of plant-fiber cloth, sleep close together in the driest niches, and give the innermost sleeping places to those most troubled by the cold. Their stillness is not a trance. It is economical. When they need to move quickly, they do; it is simply that very little needs chasing, and almost nothing needs to be reached before something else happens.

Not all of them are old. Children grow at the usual rate, become too long for favored sleeping places, and learn routes through the passages that adults can no longer comfortably use. There are impatient adults, incurious ones, people who are kind only when it costs them nothing, and people whose kindness consists largely of remembering someone else’s stiff knee. There is an old disagreement about a badly mended basket. No one involved can consistently keep a straight face when it is discussed.

Inside the hollow, their voices fail like everyone else’s. They speak instead with a complete language of hands, expression, and touch. Some of it can be read across the court; much of it is conducted close to the body, with small movements at the wrist and palm. A lifted cup may signify water, thirst, the cistern, or the fact that someone else has finished using it, according to where and how it is lifted. Private conversations are often carried on by touch alone. The language has preserved words for things absent from the hollow: sunset, river ice, barking dogs, bells. These occur in old narratives and in jokes. Silence has not made the people solemn. They interrupt, tease, sulk, and tell unnecessarily long stories.

Nor has it given them a common hour for waking. With no morning in the light, people sleep when tired and eat when hungry. Someone is generally awake, but not on watch. “Late” means only that another person has waited.

The waxy rosettes on the roofs bear minute green seeds at the bases of their leaves. These are gathered a few at a time and ground with water into a bitter paste. The pale inner portions of some leaves are also eaten, sparingly. The white flowers above the doorways are more often left for the moths. Granary niches hold seeds, dry fiber, and little packets of dark matter, but no niche holds more than a few handfuls of anything. The people do not claim to live on air or to be beyond hunger. They eat, complain of the same food, and occasionally hoard a particularly good handful of seed. Yet the arithmetic an outsider makes from their stores never comes out correctly. The predicted shortage does not arrive.

Most of their work is indistinguishable from use. Walking keeps a strip of passage clear. Washing a cup also cleans the little depression in which it sits. A basket carried from one chamber to another brings away a few fragments that would otherwise have collected in a corner. When dark matter is scooped from a settling basin, it goes into a roof pocket where roots can reach it. A root beginning to press against a pipe is not cut back as an act of civic preservation; someone whose sleeping place has become damp moves it aside with a bone awl and a patient hand.

No one polishes the thresholds. No one trims the moss into its exact boundaries. The people are not continually repairing an ideal town in their heads. They are cold, hungry, affectionate, bored, and occasionally in need of a dry place to sit. The town’s condition is the residue of these ordinary requirements.

The gray-furred animals are neither pets nor domestic stock. Their forefeet have a naked inner pad, and they can turn a seed over with surprising delicacy before carrying it away. They nest communally in the old pens and in granary recesses too narrow for people to use comfortably. They steal from open baskets. People remove them from closed ones. An old animal may permit a particular child to pick it up, while biting everyone else; this is an arrangement between individuals and confers no status on its offspring. The worn routes between the niches belong as much to them as to the people, though the two kinds of traffic use them differently.

The smaller animals are not servants either. Moths leave the eaves to visit the doorway flowers, their furred bodies dusted pale with pollen. Beetles and springtails gather where a fallen frond has begun to soften. Snails graze across fern and liverwort, following paths whose repetition is visible only in the shape of the vegetation. In the cistern, the fish and white crustaceans are watched with attention but not managed as a stock of food. A resident may sit beside the black water for a long time and then decide not to draw from it. An outsider seldom learns what was seen.

Nothing has been assigned a function. Function is what an observer calls the places where appetites overlap.

Children provide the clearest evidence that the hollow is not literally stopped. They learn signs incorrectly and invent rude ones. They find uses for ledges that adults have regarded only as ledges. Their games involve balance, concealment, and the difficult business of making another person laugh without losing one’s own composure. A child may become attached to one of the cistern fish and recognize it by a damaged fin. Years later, as an adult, the same person may still glance for it before anyone else.

People also age, become ill, and die. The equilibrium of moss and masonry does not make grief proportionate or brief. When someone is gone, the others do not behave as though a missing component has been replaced. A name-sign may remain almost unused. A habitual seat may be avoided and then, much later, occupied by someone who never knew why it had been empty. The hollow’s restoration of its surfaces should not be mistaken for indifference to its losses.

Nor are the people confined. The entrance is not guarded, and no one is forbidden to climb the steps. Some residents have gone onto the plain and not returned. Occasionally a visitor remains long enough to become simply another person within, learning first where not to step and only later how to ask for water. For those born inside, the outer world is difficult less because of its weather than because everything there seems to happen twice: once in movement and again in sound. A footstep on gravel can make a newly emerged resident look around for the second event.

The people do not offer a satisfying explanation of the hollow. They know which passages are oldest in their own experience, which walls lean, and how to tell when a pipe is beginning to fail. They do not know why light reaches the back of a covered drain. They disagree about whether their ancestors built any part of the town. The standing stone in the court serves as a backrest, a place beside which a basket may be left, and a boundary in children’s games. If it was once an altar or a monument, that possibility has not exempted it from use.

Seen briefly, the whole community can appear perfect: no litter, no noise, no unnecessary act. Seen longer, it becomes more ordinary and more strange. There is hunger, irritation, tenderness, and cold. There are private preferences and small injustices. What is absent is not difficulty, but the need for difficulty to produce expansion.

A resident may enter the court, take water from a thumb-cup, and help an older person through the narrow door. A gray animal slips around their heels. A snail turns on a fern. Below them, in the cistern, a fish changes the direction of its hanging by a few degrees. When the court is empty again, nothing appears to have happened. The cup is back in its worn place; the path is clear; the moss has risen behind a foot.

But a seed has been eaten, a child has learned a route, and someone has been spared a painful step. The hollow has not returned to an earlier moment. It has continued without requiring continuation to leave a larger shape behind. That, as nearly as an outsider can tell, is what its inhabitants have learned to do.
