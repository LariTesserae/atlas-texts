Nothing has made a home of the Unfinished Hollow in the ordinary sense. A home usually accumulates evidence against its surroundings: a dragged-in stem, a worn threshold, a cache, a little warmth that outlasts the body. Here the surroundings take evidence back. The only lasting resident is the living shell, and even it does not live *in* the chamber so much as around the fact that there is a chamber.

Most of the plant’s body is elsewhere, if elsewhere remains a useful word. Feeding roots occupy wet, hairline fractures beyond the visible swelling. They need no soil: water arrives through the breaks, their sap works on fresh mineral surfaces, and the clear patches of sunlight provide the energy for everything else. That energy is spent lavishly on maintenance. Tissue is pressurized, withdrawn, rebuilt, and reassigned. The hollow’s warmth is largely the warmth of this work.

Its thrift is as important as its growth. A damaged leaf is not discarded; it is unmade. Useful substances retreat into the stem first, then the colorless remainder softens and follows. Root hairs, thorns, trapped animals, and split valves all eventually enter the same circulation. Consequently there is no layer of remains in which another ecology could establish itself. No mould gets a season’s start. No mites inherit a feather or a husk. The plant does not merely clean the chamber. It prevents the chamber from acquiring a past made of objects.

Whether this thrift belongs to one individual is difficult to say. At some seams, neighboring tissues join so completely that pressure in a seed-valve can be answered by a distant change in the floor. Elsewhere, a new ivory tip is walled off by adjacent growth and slowly digested, with every appearance of incompatibility. The plant is unified where sap, water, and defensive responses are being shared, and plural where they are not. Its individuality is less a fixed fact than a network of agreements currently in force.

The open center may have begun as caution. A plant that bridges space blindly could waste years approaching a wall that is no farther away, or emerge behind itself and constrict its own later growth. So its tendrils hesitate at the margin. They thicken at their bases, turn along invisible boundaries, and leave the clear air clear.

But caution does not explain the new pockets. The plant does not simply avoid difficult space; it spends living tissue to make more of it. Nor does it treat sound merely as a by-product of growth. The pauses after an untimely boom suggest something closer to proprioception. In an ordinary plant, the position of a stem is given by the stem. Here position must apparently be established again and again. The shell grows, resounds, and alters its next growth accordingly. Sound tells it where a surface is going to be. Its apparent hesitations are not thoughts, exactly, but they serve some of the purposes of hesitation.

The translucent moth belongs to none of this. It must complete its life elsewhere, for nothing in the hollow supports eggs, young, or a second generation. It arrives as a brief imperfection in the light, drinks at the sweet pores, and usually leaves. No pollination has been observed. Most visits represent a small loss of sugar and nothing more.

A resting moth can crawl over a responsive leaf without springing it. Touch is not the danger. The danger begins in flight, when the particular dry flicker of its wings passes through the chamber. Then a leaf may clap around it, or around the place its wingbeat has just made uncertain. If the moth escapes, there is no pursuit. If it does not, there is no remainder. Months later, another moth may perform the same visit with no inherited knowledge of the last.

The slender valve-opening animal is a more substantial interruption, but no more settled. Its grasping forefeet let it brace itself against roots and work at a seam without relying on its teeth alone. Its use of a fallen thorn is genuine cleverness: the point supplies leverage its own body cannot. Yet the act never becomes a practice with a location or an inheritance. The thorn is not improved, the opened valve is not defended, and no favored corner becomes a feeding place. The animal eats, leaves, and allows both tool and husk to return slowly to the plant.

It is therefore intelligence without history. Whether one animal rediscovers the method on successive visits, or several animals arrive at it independently, the hollow preserves none of the evidence by which the question might be answered. Nor is there any reason to assume a partnership. The visitor may disperse something useful in its body, or it may simply steal seed the plant can afford to lose. The hollow has not made the relationship legible.

Between these rare visits, the visible food web is almost a closed loop. Sunlight, water, and mineral enter the plant; damaged plant returns to the plant; occasionally sugar passes into a moth or seed into a larger animal. There is no spreading web of hunters, grazers, scavengers, and rot across the floor. The floor itself participates directly in nearly every transaction.

Still, the plant alone is not quite the whole answer. The most elaborately maintained thing in the hollow is not a tissue. It is the absence at the center.

That absence has a cost. Every new reach of it requires more living boundary, more surfaces to waterproof, retrieve, illuminate, and keep from growing inward. It also has a development. It narrows under pressure, becomes difficult to cross during loud growth, gathers light without holding an object in it, and opens in sharp-edged portions where no cavity existed before. This does not require a second, invisible creature. An absence can have a shape, a cost, and a course of growth without being a spirit.

Its use remains undecided because its behaviors do not yet agree. As a light-gathering surface, it resembles part of a leaf. Under pressure, it rehearses the closeness of a seed. The sipping pore suggests breathing, though the chamber does not breathe regularly enough to make that a sufficient explanation. The great branching shadow offers another possibility: that the plant is occasionally following the plan of a body much larger than its present one. But a template is not necessarily a prophecy. No larger body has appeared.

Perhaps the hollow is an organ whose function is simply to remain available. Perhaps it is a structure caught among several possible uses because the relations that would settle them are still young. Or perhaps the plant is avoiding a danger no visitor can perceive, and every apparently purposeful refinement is only another way of keeping the middle from closing disastrously around it.

The census, then, is both small and incomplete: one photosynthetic body, or several temporarily wearing a single skin; a transparent moth that visits without descendants; a rare animal whose cleverness leaves no artifact; and no enduring community between them. Around these, the plant continues its other work.

At dusk, a red patch may climb a surface that was underfoot an hour before. A drop is heard while it is still falling. Somewhere in the shell a deep wooden boom gathers, and the newest tendril turns aside rather than entering the center. Another handspan of clear air opens.

Nothing comes out to claim it. No seed is revealed, no lure extended, no chamber furnished. For the present, nothing is exactly what is being grown.
