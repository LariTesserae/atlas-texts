No one lives in the hollow in the way the old steps suggest. If people once descended to the basin, they left no successor to sweep it clear or lift the roots from its stones. The present inhabitants do not maintain anything. They inherit accidents: a crack that holds moisture, a leaf that cups a drop, a fitted block whose slight tilt has made a permanent dampness along one edge.

Most of them could be held in a hand. Many spend part of their lives doing an excellent imitation of death.

The bone-colored snails are the slowest to reveal themselves. In the longest dry weeks they withdraw beneath fern ribs and seal their shells with thin, translucent membranes. Dozens may be fixed to one frond, indistinguishable from old leaf galls until condensation begins. Then the membranes soften. A feeler appears, then the broad, pale underside of a foot. They do not all wake together. A colony may take several mornings to become entirely mobile.

They graze more than they eat. Each snail shaves the living film from a fern surface or a damp root, taking fungi, algae, and the smallest decomposing matter while leaving the tougher tissue behind. Their paths are narrow and strangely clean. Within a day the films have begun to close over them again. Old shells accumulate in root cups and under the basin rim, where they become shelters for still smaller animals. Even after death, a snail remains useful real estate.

The beetles are less patient. Their crowded lines follow the growing margins of the fungal felts, where the food is softest. They jostle, reverse, climb over one another, and occasionally fall with a faint tick onto warm stone. A fallen beetle is usually back among the roots before dark, unless a gecko finds it first. Their larvae live out of sight inside dead root sheaths and compacted leaf matter, reducing material that would otherwise seal the damp colonies beneath layers of their own refuse.

The crickets divide the root curtains among themselves without any visible boundary. Each small group favors a particular tangle, and an individual may spend weeks within a few handspans of stem and stone. Their silence is not an absence of communication. A hind leg drawn lightly across a root sends a tremor through the whole curtain; another cricket answers from several feet away, though nothing between them appears to move. Some patterns bring antennae together. Others cause a hasty retreat into the joints of the rock. At dusk the curtains carry a faint, continuous traffic of these messages, as if the roots themselves were nervous.

The moths belong to several different schedules. The smallest rise first, barely visible except when they cross the pewter light beneath the roof slit. Larger ones leave later, loosening from the warm roots in clusters. Their rest is so close that wings overlap like scales; their departure makes the root surface appear to peel away. Some seek moisture along the fern colonies. Others mate and die within a few nights, having done all their feeding as larvae. Their bodies fall into the growth or are intercepted in flight. Almost nothing reaches the floor whole.

The geckos know these schedules. Each has its favored ambushes and its own narrow retreats into heated stone. They spend the brightest hours flattened in crevices, emerging while the chamber still appears empty. A gecko may remain head-down beside the same fern rib for an entire evening, moving only its eyes. When it finally strikes, the motion is so brief that the more noticeable event is often the fern springing back.

Above them, the slender snakes use the roots as roads. They rarely descend to the dust. Their lives are horizontal and vertical rather than terrestrial: along a column, across a fused root bridge, into the dimness where the roof flutes narrow. They can afford long fasts. Their stillness is not simply concealment but a way of spending almost nothing while waiting for the chamber to provide another meal.

The bats are only partly inhabitants. They arrive from elsewhere, hunt through the upper air, and carry much of what they catch back out into the night. Yet enough remains behind—droppings, a fallen wing, bodies lost among the roots—to make them important. They are one of the hollow’s few reliable connections to the abundance of the surface. Through them, insects caught beyond the fissure become food for creatures that will never leave the stone.

Beneath all this, the fallen material is in constant, quiet motion. Centipedes work the seams between leaves. Soft-bodied hunters follow moisture rather than light. Tiny things gather in the carcass of a moth, then in the carcass of one of their own predators. The dark alcoves contain layers of wings, emptied shells, shed skins, and beetle fragments, but very little of this remains recognizable for long. Even the dead have occupations until they are powder.

Rare rain temporarily abolishes the usual territories. Snails cross places that would ordinarily desiccate them. Beetles appear on bare islands of stone. Crickets leave their root curtains, and hunting animals abandon ambush for the moving margins of the runnels. Around the basin, the waterline becomes crowded with small bodies drinking, laying, pursuing, or simply allowing themselves to be carried a short distance. Colonies that have spent years exchanging little more than spores and drifting fungi suddenly exchange animals.

Then the floor dries. The crossings close. Some travelers remain stranded in colonies they could never have reached unaided; others die on the bare stone and are dismantled where they lie. The hollow’s population changes by these brief, almost invisible migrations.

The oldest inhabitants are the roots themselves. Their polished entrance strands belong to trees standing in the heat above, but the deepest divisions have become creatures of the chamber. They thicken around reliable condensation, abandon routes that dry, and press so slowly against the masonry that their movement can be measured only by what they have already displaced. Fungal threads occupy the boundaries between root, fern, and stone. A drop caught high in the growth may pass through leaf tissue, root tissue, an animal’s body, and the air before any part of it reaches a basin.

No single resident knows the whole arrangement. The chamber has no central nest, no dominant colony, no animal whose absence would cause everything else to stop. Its continuity lies instead in many small acts of retention: a snail sealing itself, a root cup holding through the afternoon, a fungal felt remaining damp after the open wall has dried, a gecko carrying water and nutrients from one colony into the body of a snake in another.

The old builders added one enduring pause to this circulation. Their basin catches what would otherwise be lost among powder and stone. Roots have broken its symmetry, but not its usefulness. Around its imperfect rim, life is denser than anywhere else in the hollow.

Whoever made it may have intended water to remain there for people. It remains now for snails, moths, fungi, roots, and the hunters that follow them. Human workmanship has survived chiefly as a slight delay in the falling of water.

If anything lives in the entire chamber, rather than merely in one of its green islands, it is that shared delay: water being caught, carried, spent, and caught again. The inhabitants have no voice with which to fill the hollow. They fill it by refusing, in thousands of small and separate ways, to let anything useful pass through too quickly.
