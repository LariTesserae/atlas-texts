The animals are the easiest inhabitants to account for, precisely because they barely belong.

In the wet seams, colorless mites graze films too thin to see. They follow the edges of trapped water, retreating when a seam tightens or freezes, and their cast skins disappear almost as soon as they are shed. Deeper down, where decomposition warms a few root layers, soft-bodied creatures lie dormant for periods that may be longer than their active lives. When warmth reaches them, they feed, move a short distance, and become torpid again. Some may never travel beyond the span of a single root cord.

None of these animals is necessary. The basin would continue without them. They do not pollinate it, aerate it, or meaningfully hasten its decay. They are passengers in a system that has closed nearly every route by which an animal might become indispensable. Even the dark bird that occasionally crosses the open air contributes nothing but its shadow.

Everything essential is vegetal, if *vegetal* remains a useful word for life that can be leaf, vessel, pump, scaffold, and food in succession.

The first necessary life is the felt.

It is not simply a carpet of separate low plants. Within a single handspan there may be toughened surface leaves, pale repair threads, fluid-filled cords, and old fibers still active far below the reach of daylight. The broad leaves lying flush against the ground feed from their undersides as well as their upper surfaces. Pressure brings them into contact with whatever the mat has received: a collapsed frond, a dead mite, moisture squeezed from a higher layer. They take what can be taken and pass the remainder downward.

A footprint is therefore not merely crushed vegetation. It is a local change in supply and pressure. Fluids move away from it; mouths turn toward it; filaments begin to line the compressed tissue. If the foot is lifted, the hollow does not necessarily spring back. More often it is slowly corrected until there is no longer any reason for it to exist.

The felt does not restore the shapes that were injured. It restores continuity.

Above exposed slopes, the condenser growths live by a different rhythm. Their cold, waxed surfaces gather what little water the air offers. A bead forms, joins another, enters a groove, and vanishes. The leaf may then remain apparently inactive for hours. There is no visible drama in this, yet entire hillsides are supported by such almost negligible arrivals.

At the bases of the taller growths, the translucent sacs receive and release the gathered fluid. They are not hearts. No one sac sets a rhythm for the others, and stopping one would stop almost nothing. Their importance lies in sequence: a slight contraction here, a swelling farther along, a temporary difference in pressure taken up by a buried cord. The movement is so distributed that it can seem accidental until the same pulse is felt, much later, in a place with no visible connection to the first.

The hollow columns are what happen when that circulation exceeds the needs of the low growth.

A column is not an old individual in the way a tree is old. It carries no long record of seasons. It rises where pressure and material are available, holds fluid and tissue above the crowded lower tiers, and opens its crown according to changes within its stem. For a time it is both reservoir and structure. Then its usefulness alters.

Its death usually begins before anything about it appears dying. Sap is withdrawn. The base becomes a route for other growth. The ribs lose their internal tension by degrees. When the crown finally pales and the column sinks, most of what made it valuable has already gone elsewhere. The collapse is only the last stage of disassembly.

Nothing tall is allowed to become merely tall.

Below the columns, the decomposer felts do not wait politely for death. They are connected to the living system and receive tissue while neighboring growth is still using it. Their work produces the small amount of warmth that keeps the deepest conduits active through freezing nights. Thus the basin’s heat is not stored in a place so much as passed through a process: growth becomes fiber, fiber becomes decomposition, decomposition keeps fluid moving, and moving fluid permits another growth.

The spore domes complicate the boundary between reproduction and disposal. Much of what they release settles back onto the same mat. Some spores lodge in wounds and begin a new arrangement of tissue. Many are simply taken up again as material. The basin does not appear to distinguish seed from surplus until one or the other has found a use.

Only the covered pools seem exempt from this urgency.

Around the oldest hollows, the usual responses change. Filaments approach the margins and turn aside. Roots thicken into rims and supporting roofs but do not close the floors. Growth may cover the water almost completely while leaving the water itself untouched. Columns rise densely nearby and fail to rise within.

Perhaps the pools are reservoirs, pressure buffers, or necessary cold spaces in the buried circulation. Perhaps they are only very old habits: places where growth has turned aside so consistently that the turning aside has become part of the system’s structure. Function and memory are difficult to separate here. A pool may remain open for the same reason a healed wound remains closed—because that is what the surrounding tissue continues to do.

The great waves of growth likewise need no command crossing the valley.

A tract releases its leaves and fluid becomes available below it. Neighboring tissue responds, drawing on the supply and rising. Its growth changes the pressure in the cords beyond it, while the depleted tract settles into another stage. One local consequence becomes the next patch’s conditions. From the rim, the result looks like intention moving over a hillside. Underfoot, it is a succession of thresholds being reached.

Yet the basin is not perfectly harmonious. Not every cord joins every other. Cuttings often attach and resume in a new form, but some unions fail: vessels discolor, tissues seal, and the two sides withdraw. Along certain buried boundaries, one tract may thicken while another dries and is absorbed. Where plumbing is shared, the relationship looks like cooperation. Where it is not, the same transfer of material looks like consumption.

This is why the question of population remains unresolved. The basin may contain several immense compatible bodies, fusing here and starving one another there. It may contain one organism internally varied enough to feed upon itself. Or individuality may simply be the wrong scale at which to understand it. A frond can be separate for the length of an afternoon and an organ by evening.

Nothing suggests a central self. There is no mother column, no deepest root from which all the others descend, no hidden chamber directing the waves. Remove any visible growth and the process continues around it. Even the oldest living tissue may be less important than the routes it maintains: drainage followed, hollows preserved, pressure carried along contours established by ice long before the present covering existed.

Its memory consists of what tends to happen again.

Bare places become lined, padded, and covered. Old depressions remain depressed. Height rises and is reclaimed. Water is drawn out of sight. Damage becomes indistinguishable from supply.

A traveler passing through is not exempt from these habits, but neither is the traveler especially recognized. Warmth, moisture, and pressure each produce their small local responses. A resting hand may be sampled by the leaves beneath it. A torn place in the mat closes behind a knee or boot. Nothing reaches out in pursuit; nothing needs to. If the traveler leaves, the brief disturbance is repaired. If the traveler dies there, the body becomes an unusually rich event in one small part of the felt.

There is no malice in this. There is also no exception.

So the basin has inhabitants in the ordinary sense: mites in their seams, sleepers in the warm layers, spores in their brief cold clouds, columns standing for a time above the felt. But these are not quite the answer at the scale of the valley. None of them, alone, is the thing that remains.

The closest thing to a single resident is the passage among them—water into cord, cord into column, column into felt, felt into warmth, warmth into continued circulation. It has no face, no center, and no final form. At dusk, one sac contracts; far away, a crown opens; beneath a black pool, an old route keeps its hollow shape. Each event is minor and incomplete.

Together, without ever assembling in one place, they constitute the life of the basin.

Every particular growth is temporary. The occupancy is not.
