The oldest inhabitant is the felt, though it resists being counted as one.

There are bodies within it. Experienced cutters distinguish the tight, bluish growth below the cisterns from the longer-fibered stock of the outer courts and the dark, woody felt that rises around old foundations. These differences are not entirely produced by harvesting. One court closes a cut strip mainly from the edges; another sends up dormant buds through the cut surface. One remains springy after several dry weeks, while another becomes hard and pale almost at once. Yet their stems cross and root over one another, and below them their older growth compacts into the same continuous body. No boundary drawn between them would remain accurate for long. The local word is useful precisely because it leaves the count open.

The felt lives thriftily. There is little soft tissue in it for anything else to eat, and much of its moisture is held out of sight: in the narrow spaces between leaf and stem, in wet seams below the surface, and in irregular pockets within the old mat. A pipe leak may produce lush growth several paces away from the pipe. A patch apparently killed by heat or cutting may remain capable of growth at a lower node. After rain, the brief flowers set seed without needing an insect to visit them. Most increase, however, is slower and less eventful—a stem extending, a leaf overlapping another leaf, a new attachment made where an older stem has begun to compact.

It does not grow toward neglect. It simply dies less often there.

A shoot beside a working hinge is nicked, dried, or displaced before it can harden. Beside a motionless hinge, it survives. The same is true along a path, beneath an axle, or around a drain. Over a season, repeated survival takes on the appearance of intention. The people who live with the felt generally understand this, and it saves them from thinking of it as an enemy. They cut it back without punishing it. They do not apologize to a court before harvesting it, either.

Human beings are, in practical terms, its largest animal and its most effective seed carrier. They crop it, press its older stems into sheets, put those sheets above their heads and around their machines, and carry its seeds back into the settlement on bales, trolley wheels, boot seams, and cuffs. Without ever making a breeding plan, they have favored felt that tolerates repeated cutting, produces useful fiber, and closes harvested ground before dust and runoff can open a wound in the mat. Other kinds persist at wall bases, in deep hollows, or in places where nobody has found a sufficient reason to cut.

The human population is easier to count. Thirty-odd people live there in an ordinary season, with a few more when major repairs are needed. The six houses are not six tidy families. They contain couples, single workers, shared rooms, children, and older people who have kept a small place after leaving shift work. Some residents were born in the basin. Others arrived expecting to stay for a contract or two and remained long enough for their handwriting to enter the maintenance books and their preferred tools to acquire a customary hook.

Almost everyone does more than their job title suggests. A mill hand may also keep a cistern record; an engine driver may cut with the harvest crew; the person who cooks at the canteen may be the one best able to hear a failing trolley bearing. Beyond these occupations, there is the less formal work of reading the felt.

The readers are not a separate rank. Usually they are people who have lived there long enough to have repaired some of their own mistakes. They know the change in a blade’s sound when it reaches old woody growth, and the delayed rise of a court after a boot has pressed it. They can distinguish a hummock made by one wet season from the rounded evidence of a buried wall. More importantly, they know when not to intervene. Growth against a disused doorway may have to be cut in stages if the frame is not to twist. A green seam may mark a drain better left visible. Another may be allowed to disappear because no one wishes to maintain every former arrangement forever.

Everyone else has a round.

A round can be very small: opening two shutters, running a handcart over an unused spur, lifting a drain cap, walking the long way back from the mill. Children are given hinges and short paths, and often turn their duties into races. Older residents take routes that keep them near the canteen or the houses. The tasks are not ceremonies, and performing them once with great thoroughness accomplishes little. The settlement is maintained by recurrence. A minute every few days is worth more than an afternoon of clearing at the end of a season.

Not every still thing is preserved. Sometimes a route is deliberately removed from the rounds. A shed door is allowed to narrow; a seat becomes a mound; the outline of an obsolete arrangement softens until only a reader can identify it. This is considered a decision, not a failure, provided somebody has noticed what is happening.

New residents often find the discipline more tiring than the mill work. There is no final state in which a path, once cleared, remains cleared. Others dislike the visibility of the place. The wide courts permit solitude but very little anonymity. People recognize one another at a distance by gait and coat, and nearer by the particular pause they make before opening a door. A person can spend almost an entire day without conversation and still have been quietly accounted for.

That habit extends to care. When someone is ill, a neighbor often takes their round before asking what help is wanted. The sick person hears their usual shutter open or their section of drain being flushed. It is a practical way of saying that their absence has been noticed and that their return has been prepared for. When someone leaves for good, their official duties are reassigned at once. More private traces—a route to a favored sitting place, a shortcut worn between houses—may persist for a while and then green over. These are not quite memorials. They are simply uses that have ended.

The people are quieter outdoors than in their rooms, but they are not solemn. Sound carries across the courts, and much of it is information. Indoors, they argue, play cards, mend canvas, complain about the quality of a pressing, and discuss the private habits of machines with considerable freedom. Outsiders who expect a community of devout gardeners are usually disappointed. Residents swear at the felt when it jams a door and take pride in a good clean harvest. Their disagreements are ordinary ones: whether a court has been cut too early, whether the mill or the condensary has first claim on power after a cold night, whether an unused path is being prudently maintained or merely preserved out of habit.

Their respect for the place is mostly expressed through sharp tools, accurate records, and the willingness to do small things again.

Animals form no third community. They are closer to a series of exceptions.

The felt offers poor grazing, almost no loose ground for burrowing, and few rewards in its brief flowers. Open water is scarce and usually covered. Even the life below the surface is sparse: minute worms and mites in the wetter seams, fungi and bacteria working unevenly through the old stems, nothing like the crowded soil beneath a forest. The mat is not a hidden wilderness. It is a slow, economical body with long intervals of very little happening.

The sump snails are the only animals with anything resembling a permanent address. They are small, dark-shelled, and usually below the waterline, grazing the film on the stone rather than attempting to eat the felt. Their survival is helped by an old cleaning practice: the sump is not scoured all at once, but brushed in sections on successive days so that it can remain in use. A few snails always seem to remain in the section not yet cleaned. Occasionally one travels farther, appearing on a bucket handle or leaving a bright track on a condensary window. Then it becomes visible enough to acquire the status of news.

The grey moths have no established population. Now and then one arrives with freight, or emerges from packing, or is blown over the rim and finds the warmth of a lamp housing. Its presence may be noted in the margin of a work book, between an entry for lamp oil and a delayed delivery. This is not quite scientific recording and not quite sentiment. It is simply unusual for a new animal to be visible in the Feltworks, and people like to remember which day it happened.

At present, the most closely watched new resident is neither human nor animal. It is the young felt below the loading platform.

Its seed came out of the bales, which means it is descended largely from growth already selected by cutting, handling, and harvest. It stands a little more upright in its first season than the older court stock, and its leaves are set slightly farther apart. A sample yielded pale, promising fiber, though not enough to show whether the sheets will be tough. The mill workers would like more of it. The water keepers want to see how deeply it roots and how quickly it dries after rain. The readers have advised doing very little for another year.

So it has been measured, sampled once, and otherwise left alone. It may eventually spread through the courts. It may prove thirsty, brittle, or difficult to cut. It may disappear into the older body without leaving any trait that anyone can reliably identify. For now it is simply the platform growth, and that is name enough.

Nothing in the Feltworks requires its inhabitants to love one another. The felt can outlive a roof; a roof can be rebuilt from felt cut in a court that outlived the previous roof. People arrive, work, leave, or grow old. Snails vanish for a season and reappear. Routes are kept, surrendered, or made by accident beneath the feet of children. Yet each life has become part of the conditions under which another continues.

Late in the evening, someone on the last round opens a door that has not been needed all day. The hinge gives its familiar small resistance and then moves freely. At the sump, a snail’s track is already dulling. Beneath the loading platform, the new felt holds a drop of water at the join of two leaves.

None of these things knows the arrangement. It has simply been repeated long enough to acquire the force of an agreement.
