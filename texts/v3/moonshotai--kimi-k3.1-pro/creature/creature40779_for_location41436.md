Nothing lives in the Meristem Basin in the sense of occupying it. There are no animals concealed by the arcades, no gardeners inside the walls, no minute citizens for whom the capillary clefts are roads. There is not enough separation between body and shelter for occupancy to begin. The place is alive all the way through, and its life is not housed by the city so much as spent in making it.

Even calling that life one enormous plant is accurate only for as long as the appropriate connections last. A graft can make separate growths continuous in every way that matters to them: water crosses, sap mingles, a change in tension in one column becomes a change in another. When the connection closes, the same tissues are several bodies again. Severance multiplies without reproduction. Grafting unites without birth. Any census of individuals is therefore a description of temporary plumbing.

At the finest scale, the basin’s nearest approach to a population is its growing tips.

They are small in comparison with what they produce. A pale root point advances through the felt, divides around an obstruction, branches toward moisture, and leaves behind a few more strands of pavement. A stem tip rises, thickens, and leaves a vascular column beneath it. The living front does not travel through the completed city; it turns itself into city as it goes. A tower is the accumulated route of growths that continued upward. A wall is where other growths spread sideways and became fixed. The conspicuous activity is at the edge, but almost all the visible mass is consequence.

No tip is born into a permanent civic role. There is no inherited kind that must become pavement and another that must become a reservoir. The same living capacity can produce root felt, leaf-scale, culm, waterproof lining, bridge, or canopy. What emerges depends on local conditions: where light reaches, where water is moving, which minerals can be obtained, what strain the surrounding tissue imposes, and what adjacent growth has already claimed. These responses can look like decisions once their results are visible. Before that, they are sensitivities without a plan.

Mature tissue is not the dead remainder of this activity. Its range of possible futures has narrowed, but it continues to conduct, store, photosynthesize, thicken, contract, and graft. The dry outer face of a wall may be almost papery while its joints remain wet and metabolically active. Root pavement flexes and redistributes pressure. Reservoir linings alter their permeability. Old connections close while new ones swell around a junction. Much of the basin’s life is this quiet maintenance of forms that no longer resemble growth at all.

Lignification changes the terms of that life. It does not simply make tissue harder; it makes a particular answer difficult to revise. A stem that could have contributed to an arch, a drain, or a tower becomes committed to one geometry, and surrounding growth must accommodate it. Resorption does not merely destroy that geometry. It withdraws the commitment, returning substance and space to conditions in which another answer can be produced.

Because visible forms and living bodies do not coincide, endings are often misleading. A severed avenue does not necessarily die. Its two portions may continue independently, each with intact storage and growing tips. A collapsing tower is not necessarily a death either. Its height, tension, and organization disappear, but much of its living substance may continue in the pavement that receives it. Conversely, a wall can remain standing while the important event has already occurred elsewhere: its water has been withdrawn, its connections closed, and its continued shape made temporarily irrelevant.

Nothing in the basin needs to see the skyline. Leaf tissue responds to the light reaching its own surface; roots respond to local moisture and mineral gradients; columns respond to loading and pressure. No district has a view of the whole. The life of the basin occupies a geography of connections rather than vistas. Two root tips pressed close together in the felt may share nothing but space. A tower and a reservoir separated by several wards may belong, for practical purposes, to the same body as long as a living column joins them. When that column closes, they become remote without either having moved.

At the active meristems, the population becomes stranger than a collection of tissues and connections.

There, growth is briefly plural. Forms that cannot remain together nevertheless produce shadows, sounds, and movements of water. An uncompleted arch affects the light beneath it. A route with no surviving vessels still passes droplets along its expected course. These are not concealed organisms, spirits of demolished buildings, or visitors from some other basin. They have no separate continuing bodies and no independent history. They are growth before growth has become accountable to one sequence.

Yet while they persist, they cannot simply be dismissed as unreal. They are audible. They can be wet. Other tissue jumps when their geometry disappears. If they are to be counted among the basin’s lives, they are its briefest: forms that affect the place without ever becoming fixed inhabitants of it. Lignification leaves one of them standing, or conducting, or casting an ordinary shadow. The others do not fall and rot. They cease to be available. Their aftermath may be only a concussion, an abrupt adjustment in nearby streets, and a sound still travelling toward the rim after its source has become impossible to identify.

Resorption opens the question again, though it does not preserve a catalogue of the vanished alternatives. The basin has no archive of cities that almost were. Its memory is more material and less complete: a fixed wall constrains later growth; an old graft determines where sap can go; a reservoir retains the consequences of one rain into the next dry interval. Forgetting is not blankness but the removal of those constraints.

The closest thing to offspring is a ruptured fragment.

It may begin as part of a wall, a piece of reservoir lining, or a length of tower, with mature tissue at one end and a pale growing zone at another. It is not a seed containing a miniature skyline. It does not carry a privileged shape waiting to unfold. Runoff or the strain of expansion moves it; if it comes to rest where water and minerals can be obtained, it roots and differentiates according to its new conditions. What had been reservoir tissue may give rise to a tower. What had been part of a tower may spread into pavement. Its inheritance is not an urban plan but the capacity to produce urban tissue.

This is why the green precincts beyond the main growth are not quite scouts and not quite colonies. No centre has dispatched them, and they need not remain related to what produced them. Some fail almost immediately. Others persist long enough to establish a living connection back into the basin. Then their remoteness can vanish before the visible distance has changed. A fragment that was an independent body becomes a district; a district becomes part of an avenue; the route by which it arrived may matter more than the place where it originally lay.

Flowering and pollen continue beside this, but without animals to visit or carry them, they do not interrupt the basin’s essential solitude. The great multiplication is not a season of blossoms. It is the repeated demonstration that separation can be a beginning and that almost any sufficiently living piece may become a whole new extent of city.

None of this requires a mind behind the growth. There need be no oldest root, central reservoir, or governing meristem in which the complete form resides. Coordination is real, but it is local, transmitted, resisted, and revised. A district can respond coherently without knowing that other districts exist. The basin can change as a whole during rain without any part having received an image of the whole.

If the language of persons must be used, a self is the present extent of a living connection. Memory is the constraint left by fixed tissue. Forgetting is resorption. Reproduction is often division. None of these belongs to a permanent individual.

So the basin has no native people and no fauna to list. Its population consists of tissues maintaining the forms already produced, growing fronts turning themselves into further forms, fragments capable of beginning elsewhere, and, at the meristems, brief incompatible growths that will never acquire a continuing body. These are not separate kinds of organism. They are different conditions of the same life.

The skyline is only the part of that life currently fixed enough to persist. The rest is being withdrawn, redistributed, or formed again at a pale edge. The city is not where the basin’s life resides. It is the shape that life has temporarily agreed to be.
