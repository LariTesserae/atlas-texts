The Kettle Shelf has no presiding animal. Nothing nests above the pump house or patrols the lip. A visitor looking for a resident might see only vegetation and conclude that the garden has mistaken itself for life enough.

The actual census begins in the mineral crust.

Nearest the steam, where the rock is too hot for ferns, anything living is microbial and mostly invisible. The rust-red algae form delicate, wet filaments rather than a continuous slime; a finger drawn through them leaves clear stone, which colours again within weeks. Farther into the heat, even that colour disappears. Life there is betrayed only by slickness, by faint iron-stained fans around a seam, by the way one kind of deposit gathers where another does not.

The white rind is not alive, but life helps make it. Fine filaments catch mineral dust and silica; water closes over them; another film establishes itself on the new surface. In this way, older growth is slowly sealed into the margins of leaks. Broken crust sometimes shows green, orange, and white in layers no thicker than paper. The settling pool has the same patient stratigraphy. Its dark green algae live at the cool rim, die toward the inflow, and are gradually incorporated into the silica they help to roughen.

The visible garden is richer in repetition than in species. Keepers have never agreed on formal names. In the service books, when plants are mentioned at all, they are usually **blackstem**, **cup-leaf**, and **saddle moss**.

Blackstem is the polished-stemmed fern. Its fronds are modest, but its rhizomes are persistent almost beyond the usefulness of the word. They travel through fractures too narrow to admit a fingertip, emerge beneath a pipe saddle, cross a handspan of warm scale, and disappear again. A plant apparently growing alone beside a gauge may be connected, under stone and steel, to the cushion beside the pump house.

Its young croziers appear without regard to season. Inside the warm boundary there are no true winters, only alterations in plumbing. The fern’s fertile fronds are shorter and slightly darker than the rest, their spore cases held close to the mat. Water does most of the work of reproduction; wind is unreliable, and beyond the warm pocket it is usually fatal. Blackstem advances along leaks and abandoned channels. When a leak is repaired, the fronds may remain green for months on water stored in the roots, then brown from the outer edges inward. Years later, the dead stems still stand, black and polished, looking less dead than they are.

Cup-leaf makes the broad, waxy rosettes beneath the ferns. Each leaf turns slightly upward at its base, and the overlapping angles hold water long after the exposed rock has dried. The plant rarely flowers. When it does, the flowers are small, greenish, and easily overlooked; they do not require the moth or any other uncertain visitor. Most increase is by runners. A rosette puts out a pale cord, finds an accumulation of dust in the angle of a root, and begins again.

Its wax is useful in two opposed climates. Within the garden it sheds the constant condensation, preventing the leaf centres from rotting. At the margin it offers some protection against the dry wind. Not enough, usually. A runner that crosses the last warm pipe may produce one tight, glossy rosette in the shelter of a bolt. The leaves remain small and thick, and frost eventually whitens their edges.

Saddle moss is the first occupant of many bare places. It establishes in the mineral dust behind pipe supports, in the seams of old root mats, even in the shallow pits of corroded iron where water sits without quite running. It can survive a dry repair interval as brown, brittle felt. When water returns, the outer tips green first, so that for several days a patch looks as though it has been lightly painted.

Together these plants make the mat, and the mat makes nearly everything else possible.

Where an overflow channel has cut through it, the mat has distinct depths. The top is green and springy. Beneath that lies a brown thickness of dead leaf bases, old moss, and root fibre. Farther down, where water stands and air enters slowly, the material is almost black. Fine fungal threads run through all of it, especially the older interior. They break down what the plants cannot reuse and return some of it in forms the roots can take.

There are no earthworms. The soil is too young, too discontinuous, and in places too warm. It is mixed by roots, water, and the occasional disturbance of maintenance rather than by anything burrowing through it.

The clear snails live almost entirely within this manufacture.

They are the translucent snails beneath the overflow moss, flattened a little by the habit of keeping close to wet surfaces. Their shells are extremely thin. Through them one can see the slate-coloured body and, after feeding, a dull green thread of gut. They scrape algae and microbial film rather than living leaves. Where several have passed, dark moss may be marked by paler, cleaner arcs, visible only when the light strikes low.

Their eggs look like additional drops of condensation: a few clear beads fastened beneath a leaf or against the underside of moss. A snail may lay only a small clutch, and many of the young disappear into channels before their shells have acquired any visible spiral.

Most individuals travel very little. For one beneath the second return pipe, the habitable world may be two square metres of moss with a warm end, a cool end, and a stone margin beyond which the film of water fails. The mountain, the void, and the cable do not enter into it.

The great pump’s cycle does. When vibration passes through the mat and the shelf lets go its accumulated drops, the snails pull close to their surfaces. After the brief private rain, they extend again. For a few minutes the overflow seems unusually populated, though nothing new has arrived; the residents have merely become easier to see.

Occasionally a surge carries one away. This is the snail’s only rapid means of dispersal, and it is more often fatal than useful. Most are swept into hot water, onto bare scale, or down a fracture. A very few lodge in another patch of moss. Some of the separated snail populations beneath otherwise identical pipes may have begun in this way, with one accident and enough algae.

The smoky moth is the shelf’s other established animal, though “established” would seem too confident to anyone judging by sightings. Keepers usually call it the **fin moth**, and often write *the moth*, as though a single patient individual had outlasted successive maintenance crews.

At rest on condenser metal, with its wings closed, it resembles a flake of scorched paint. It does not gather at the lamps so much as at warmth. After dark it settles where the fin bank is mild beneath its feet, opens its wings slightly, and remains there until its body has taken enough heat from the metal. If disturbed, its flight is short and heavy. It usually lands on another warm surface rather than crossing the garden.

Almost all of its life is passed out of sight. Its caterpillars live in the older, cooler layers of the mat, feeding on dead cup-leaf tissue, decaying moss, and fungal growth. They do not ordinarily attack healthy foliage. Their development is slow and irregular; a drying repair can delay it, and an especially warm, stable year can bring several hidden age classes through together. Pupation occurs in comparatively dry refuges: behind a saddle, inside a curled dead leaf against warm stone, sometimes in the fibrous dust beneath an unused machine bed.

The adult does not feed and lasts only a few nights. Its eggs return to the damp margins of the mat. Some years no keeper sees a moth at all. That does not necessarily mean the shelf has lost it.

Beyond the snails and the moth, animal life becomes a matter of patient searching. A hand lens may reveal a few mites in the fungal layers or pallid springtails where a dead frond has lodged against grating. Wind delivers other insects from time to time. They remain arrivals rather than inhabitants. There are no established swarms over the pool, no reliable spiders beneath the pipes, and no regular chain of predators. Scarcity itself performs much of that office.

The lichens outside the warm boundary belong to an older order of residence. They may be the only visible life on the shelf that predates Condenser Nine. Their rosettes and dark crusts occupy stone that has never held a root mat and may never hold one. They do not benefit from the garden’s abundance. A little too much heat dries them; a shifted overflow can scour them; mineral-rich water can bury them.

They also make the station’s timescale look brief. Blackstem measures opportunity by leaks. The fin moth measures it by warm refuges and successful hidden generations. A lichen on the cold outer rock measures it by weathering and the slow enlargement of a crust. The pump house is an event within its vicinity.

Birds and people pass through without becoming residents. A bird resting on the freight cable may leave a seed, a dropping, or nothing. People leave considerably more, but not always more durably. Their boots carry moss fragments and spores from one end of the shelf to the other. Their repairs create small droughts and floods. Clearing the condenser bank removes in an afternoon a curtain that took years to accumulate; abandoning a minor leak can prepare a new one. Inside the warm pocket, a fitter with a wrench can alter more habitat than several ordinary seasons.

Yet the garden also depends on the intervals when no one comes. Left unwatched, roots find the fractures, snails establish their separate little populations, and the fin moth completes whatever portion of its life requires stillness.

Nothing about the community is independent. It has soil because roots and machines have made it. It has weather because steam and waste heat sustain it. Its inhabitants have thermal addresses rather than territories: this distance from a vent, that side of a return pipe, the cool rim of a pool, the one dry hollow behind a saddle. A change of a few paces can be equivalent to a change of climate.

If the station were merely shut down for a day, very little of this would alter. The snails would miss their brief rain; warm rock would tick; the moth might seek another fin. If the machinery were abandoned altogether, the garden would probably contract by degrees toward the natural steam seams. No keeper has seen that happen, and the order of disappearance remains uncertain. The lichens would not need to hurry.

For now, the shelf remains occupied by small lives with no knowledge of the towns receiving its water. To the clear snail, it is a wet patch with boundaries. To the fin moth, an archipelago of warm metal. To blackstem, a system of stone and temporary rivers. To the cold lichens, a brief green disturbance nearby.

What lives on the Kettle Shelf lives by narrow tolerances, patient repetition, and the continued good fortune of a leak.
