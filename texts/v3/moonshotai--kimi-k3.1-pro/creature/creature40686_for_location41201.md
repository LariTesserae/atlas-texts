# What Lives in Vey

Vey has no meaningful census. Citizens are counted by pressure ward; scutes by estimated tonnage; sleet by the time it takes an intake to foul; chords by the reservoirs whose plans they interrupt. The microbial film is mapped most carefully where it has unexpectedly disappeared. A census concerned only with bodies would miss the essential fact of the basin: much of its life is also surface, load, flow, or obstruction.

## The citizens

The citizens occupy the warmest and most completely mapped parts of that arrangement. Their households gather around heat conduits, with kitchens, nurseries, workshops, and sleeping chambers pressurized behind the same inhabited dams that restrain the reservoirs. Privacy usually means acoustic and pressure separation rather than distance. An outer room may be a suit lock, a cold store, or simply a buffer against the voice of the water.

They are not blind, and darkness is not sacred to them. A clinic may be brightly lit while opening onto an unlit street. Field imagers produce detailed visual records, and there are citizens who make pictures, study them, and prefer them to any echo map. Public darkness persists because illumination would solve few problems while interfering with instruments, animals, and the careful acoustic legibility of the city.

That legibility is learned early. A child in Vey may know the sound of a pressure door, the double return of a particular stairwell, and the difference between a healthy pump and a cavitating one before learning to read a municipal plan. Suit transponders and mapped echoes do most of the work; native ability is literacy, not a sixth sense. A well-run district is not quiet. It is a place where important things can still be distinguished.

Rest and work follow ward schedules, loom maintenance, lock operations, and reservoir exchanges rather than dawn. Some districts keep nearly continuous activity, handing obligations from one shift to the next. Others become still for long intervals while a loom note is checked or a chord’s lower harmonics make fine measurement impossible.

The population includes loom listeners who can spend a career attending to the same structural note; load surveyors who estimate the weight of animals on bridges and roofs; intake tuners; lock keepers; brine chemists; and carcass wardens, whose maps are among the most frequently revised in the city. There are also people with no special affection for any of this. To a drainkeeper, a scute may simply be vermin with a maintenance budget. A chord can be fondly named in one ward and cursed in another for the same call. A family whose street has been closed for forty years is rarely comforted by being told that the migration is ecologically valuable.

Nor are the citizens naturally more patient than people elsewhere. They argue, litigate, abandon projects, and demand immediate remedies from institutions designed to outlast them. Their patience is mostly archival. Someone must preserve why an arch stops short of its footing, which attempts have already failed to move it, and what its unfinished load will do if the loom field changes. An obstruction with two centuries of records is still an obstruction, but at least it is not an unfamiliar one.

Property reflects the same constraints. A deed may confer exclusive use of a wall without granting the right to deaden its echo, seal its seepage, or prevent animals from crossing the water behind it. Some reservoir charters recognize prior chord passage, not because the chords have been shown to understand law, but because they cannot be lifted, convincingly threatened, or hurried.

## The film

The gray film is the only widespread life that can flourish without reference to the city. Around mineral vents it forms a patchwork rather than a uniform pasture: sulfur-using growth nearest some seeps, hydrogen-using communities along fractures, and lean, nearly starved crusts where the brine has carried away more than it supplies. In warm, nutrient-rich places it can become thick enough to peel. Elsewhere it survives as a discoloration in porous stone.

Elsewhere in Vey, the film follows infrastructure. It colonizes new ceramic, warm exchanger seams, leaking feedstock lines, and the damp margins of locks. Its distribution can therefore reveal conditions that instruments have missed. A film advancing across a supposedly dry wall is evidence of seepage. A sudden retreat may indicate a chemical change, a temperature drop, or the arrival of something that eats it.

Citizens maintain selected strains in controlled culture beds, but the open film is not treated as clean food or harmless fuzz. It can carry metals, parasites, and chemical signatures from whatever it has been growing on. It is simultaneously pasture, contamination, diagnostic instrument, and the first claimant on almost every new surface.

## The sleet

“Sleet” names a condition of the air as much as a group of animals. Several lineages contribute to it, though most citizens encounter only their shared consequences: vanes in the faceplate seals, mineral spines in the suit joints, and a soft, persistent impact against every exposed surface.

The common drifters do not truly fly. Their vanes let them prolong a fall, turn within an updraft, or spread feeding surfaces across moving vapor. Warm currents carry them upward; cooling and their own accumulating mineral weight bring them down again. Some appear to complete their lives aloft. Others leave minute, salt-resistant resting stages along cistern margins and in the wet angles of shafts. This is one reason an intake can be thoroughly cleared and then, years later, become populated again without any obvious breach.

At low densities, individuals feed and drift with little effect on one another. Crowding changes them into a local weather system. Their accumulated body heat slightly alters the vapor around them; their packed vanes trap more organic material; parasites multiply among them. A dense fall may end in a sudden collapse, with dead and living animals matting together against screens and walls.

The mats do not simply disappear. Small scutes graze them. Ribbon hunters enter their wet interiors. Films colonize the spines after the softer tissues are gone. The remaining mineral grit migrates through drains and settling pools, a faint, glassy sediment made mostly of former weather.

Intakes sing because a packed mat is heavier and more dangerous than its individual animals suggest. Suit seals flex and shed because the same process would otherwise occur around a person’s joints. Citizens clear their faceplates without much thought, the way someone elsewhere might brush water from a sleeve.

## The scutes

A scute’s plate is not a shell it can leave. It is load-bearing skin: firm through the center, flexible at the margins, and continually thickened or repaired with material derived from its food and surroundings. Beneath it lies a broad gripping surface and a band of scraping mouths. Chemical receptors along the edge find film and mineral scale; vibration tells the animal whether the surface beneath it is hollow, moving, or already crowded.

Small scutes commonly settle on larger ones. Those, in turn, may carry younger animals, old sleet mats, and films of their own. A bridge apparently burdened by a single layer can therefore be supporting several generations of overlapping inhabitants. When a touched street shifts by centimeters, the movement often propagates through many bodies before any one of them has traveled its own width.

Their migrations are largely transfers of occupancy. Individuals follow warmth, food, and available grip. Animals behind them occupy the vacated surfaces, while those at the leading edge press into new territory. Over years, the population appears to flow. No stampede occurs, and there may be no day on which a resident can truthfully say that the migration has arrived. There is only a gradual discovery that the street can no longer be used.

Engineers manage scutes by attraction more often than by force. A vulnerable bridge may be protected by making a nearby buttress warmer or supplying a sacrificial grazing surface with richer film. A drain may be kept open by the same animals that, a few decades later, become its principal blockage. The oldest scutes incorporate ceramic grit, salts, and other basin materials into their plates, so their bodies become irregular records of where they have fed. Interpreting those records is difficult: a large animal may have spent fifty years almost motionless and then crossed three districts in the following twenty.

Nothing in Vey is fully domesticated in the old sense. There are only arrangements that remain attractive.

## The chords

The chords give the reservoirs their longest memories.

An adult’s ribbed body contains a series of unequal volumes, filtering tissues, and resonant spaces. Water and sound do not necessarily follow the same route through it, but feeding, movement, pressure regulation, and calling are difficult to separate. A chord can appear nearly stationary while passing enormous quantities of brine through itself. Its movement is often inferred from cleared tracks in sediment, altered flow, or the slow relocation of its voice.

Young chords are small enough to use service channels and produce shorter, less stable calls. As they grow, reservoir architecture becomes increasingly important to them. The same animal sounds different in a narrow cistern and a broad one; dwellings, locks, and dam faces return portions of its call and alter what reaches other listeners. Identification therefore depends on more than pitch. Keepers look for persistent irregularities, damaged harmonics, feeding behavior, and the animal’s responses to particular flow changes. Even then, old records are disputed.

No maximum lifespan has been established. Some individuals have remained in reservoir ledgers longer than the districts that wrote the earliest entries. Others vanish into deep or inaccessible water and are presumed dead until an unfamiliar call reveals that they have merely been elsewhere. A living chord may also go silent for years. Silence alone does not make its reservoir vacant.

Passage between reservoirs is a civic undertaking measured in careers. Channels must be enlarged, loads recalculated, scutes redirected, and adjacent dwellings retuned before the animal commits its body to a lock. It may then decline to proceed. The inconvenience can outlive everyone who initiated the transfer.

Whether chords possess anything like civic awareness remains unresolved. They respond to other chords, to pressure, and to architectural resonance. Sometimes a call changes after repeated exposure to a particular structure, as though the animal has incorporated the return into its own behavior. This is evidence of responsiveness, not proof of intention. Their legal standing is simpler than their biology: they must be accommodated because the alternative is usually impossible.

When a large chord dies, the event occupies its reservoir for decades. First comes the alteration of flow and chemistry, then the clogging and scavenging, then the slower colonization of the body itself. Ribbons enter the internal passages. Films spread across exposed tissues. Borers and small grazers establish themselves around mineralized ribs. The hollow remains can shelter communities that did not exist in the open cistern. Removing the carcass may cause more disruption than zoning around it, so a death can become a new district with a temporary name and no reliable expiry date.

## The lives between

The smaller fauna make every boundary porous.

Ceramic borers are not simply stone-eaters. Many excavate to reach salts, trapped brine, or films in microscopic defects, leaving galleries that admit more water and more life. In dead construction they help break material down. In a newly deposited wall they can be a structural emergency. Loom tenders learn to distinguish their fine, irregular activity from the discord of an actual deposition fault, though not always quickly.

Active loom volumes acquire transient ecologies of their own. Films follow warm deposition fronts. Borers arrive before a wall has fully settled. Sleet spines collect in vertical threads of moisture. A supposedly sealed construction volume may first announce its leak through what has begun living inside it.

Beneath the scutes, ribbon-thin hunters navigate by pressure and vibration. Some prey on parasites and juvenile grazers; others scavenge whatever becomes exposed when the layered population shifts. Eyeless animals colonize pump intakes, attaching themselves where the current delivers food but does not quite tear them loose. Sleet carries its own parasites, many of which become noticeable only when a host population is dense enough to foul machinery.

Beyond the city’s heat boundary, communities thin rapidly. Film persists in protected brine seams, drifters pass through on vapor, and a few cold-tolerant animals survive in fault angles and pumping keeps. The central basin is crowded because the city concentrates warmth, circulation, and surfaces. Its ecology is richest exactly where engineering has made the most promises.

The arrangement is not tidy symbiosis. Animals weaken walls, crush structures, block water, complicate construction, and occasionally provide the first warning that all of these things are about to happen. The citizens use the reef as a second sensor network; the reef uses the city as a set of thermal and chemical gradients. Neither side has agreed to a common purpose.

A chord does not know that its lock has been ready for sixty years. A scute does not recognize a closed street. The sleet makes no distinction between an intake and a cliff. Each follows heat, pressure, chemistry, and food. The citizens follow those things too—and then keep minutes of the meeting, so that whoever comes after them will know which temporary arrangements have held before.
