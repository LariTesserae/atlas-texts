Press close to the spire and the apparent machinery resolves into appetite, growth, and touch. Nothing in the Kettle is born knowing the shape of the whole. Most lives are organized around a sensation no larger than a fracture, a neighbor’s contraction, or a difference in charge across a shell.

### At the veins

A staple feeds beneath a bowed, black shell. One end of the shell grips each side of a fissure; beneath the arch, the animal spreads a sheet of pearl-gray flesh over fresh sulfide. The sheet is folded and delicate, and withdraws at the touch of a grazing tooth. Chemical reactions where flesh meets mineral sustain the animal. Ions pass through its body and outward into the shells touching it on either side.

Young staples are almost transparent, with a single dark line of gut visible inside. Older ones thicken their shells with magnetite until they become opaque and uneven. A long row is therefore less regular than it appears from a distance: transparent recruits between ancient black shells, empty arches still bridging a fracture, and occasional gaps where a dead animal has been crushed and carried away. Even an empty shell may conduct for a while. A circuit can look intact after much of its life has gone out of it.

An individual yoke-eel is a slender, muscular animal with a brass-colored gut and a small crown of feeding cilia. Its free-swimming youth ends when its mouth seals against the tail of another eel. The joint does not quite close its gut; it can still strain nourishment from particles drawn past it. But nearly all its adult musculature serves the contraction passing through the chain.

A chain has no front and no rear. No segment leads it. Each animal responds to the pressure and electrical state of its neighbors, sometimes correcting a contraction, sometimes merely transmitting it. Injury travels through the loop as hesitation. When a segment dies, the neighboring bodies draw the gap closed; later, a juvenile may settle there and grow into the vacant interval. Until then, the enclosed wheel turns unevenly, and the association’s ticking develops a missing beat.

The wheel itself is a colony of flat, wedge-shaped zooids joined edge to edge. Its center is open water. There is no axle, and nothing holds it except cilia, pressure, and the flow through the loop. The membranes between its rays are dark with captured iron on the intake face and much paler behind. New mineral is laid down at the bases of the rays as shell, nodule, and the clear material of egg cases.

Wheels are not uniform throughout their lives. A ray may be torn away by a shifting fracture and replaced by several smaller zooids. An old colony can be lopsided, with one broad ancient sector and a crowded fan of young tissue opposite it. Occasionally a few outer zooids separate as a tiny rosette. Most of these fragments drift into other tiers and are eaten. A few become caught in young loops, add rays, and begin the slow rotation again.

New associations arise in no fixed order. Staples may colonize a fracture decades before eels close a loop around them. A wheel fragment may turn briefly in a current that soon fails. Clinkers may consume the young staples faster than they can replace themselves. The repetition visible across the spire is partly the repetition of successful survival: badly spaced associations stall, lose their inhabitants, and disappear.

### The pavement and the crevices

Between and over the circuits live the clinkers, the Kettle’s commonest armored grazers. Their low shells are made of overlapping plates, each plate shaped by the neighbors against which it grew. Where food is abundant, their shells touch so closely that the rock appears paved. Where a vein has failed, the pavement becomes sparse without becoming empty; isolated animals can remain closed and nearly motionless for a long time.

A clinker feeds on the temporary tissues spread over the stone: staple feeding sheets, discarded wheel membrane, injured eel flesh, and the soft bodies of newly settled larvae. Its mineral teeth frequently strike the rock beneath, producing the dense rasp heard through the water. It does not cultivate the staples. Enough clinkers can strip a vein bare and weaken the current on which their own food supply depends.

Their shells also record pressure from the crowd. Growth stops where a neighbor presses close and resumes when that neighbor dies. A dead clinker may remain in place for years, an empty plate among living ones, until a scavenger breaks it out.

Fin-slips occupy the intervals. They are clear, exceedingly flat animals ribbed with many small fins, some no wider than the gap between two shell plates. Larger ones hover beside wheels or within the angles of a yoke loop, moving by waves so slight that they seem to be trembling rather than swimming.

They eat joint parasites, loose eggs, damaged tissue, and larvae too young to keep their appointed tier. Some spend nearly their whole lives within one association. They are not attendants, though their feeding can remove infestations from an eel joint; given the opportunity, they eat staple brood and wheel zooids as readily.

Freshly opened stone is the only truly bare terrain. After the mountain’s groan, new black faces and quartz seams may remain exposed for years. Then transparent staple larvae begin to catch in the fractures, followed by clinkers, eel juveniles, and all the small animals that live off their losses.

### The suspended tiers

Away from the stone, the inhabitants sort themselves less by swimming ability than by electrical weight.

The tiers are not simple horizontal floors. They follow surfaces of equal charge, bowing around the spire and pinching together near its blades. Some are broader than a wheel; others are only a few body-depths thick. Water on either side may appear identical, yet the animals in one tier can seldom persist in the next.

The smallest are collectively called hook-motes, though they include many kinds of animal. Some are little more than a beating mouth, a gut, and a set of hooked limbs. Others wear transparent armor or carry their eggs in a clear sac along the back. Nearly all contain a few mineral specks, around the gut or embedded in the shell. Those specks turn with the local field, while cilia and limbs make continual small corrections.

At close range, similarly charged bodies tend to glance apart. This, more than agility, allows the tiers to become cloudy with life without collapsing into continual collision.

Their food comes ultimately from the stone, but often after passing through several bodies: staple spawn, fragments of wheel membrane, fecal threads, crushed tissue, abandoned egg jelly, and smaller hook-motes. The open water has no independent pasture. It lives on what the vein associations release, lose, and fail to retain.

Comb-bells move through these suspensions. The smallest are pinhead clear; the largest cast a soft-edged shadow broad enough to darken several clinkers at once. Their ciliary combs appear to gleam rather than move. A slow contraction draws a volume of the tier into the bell, where hooked prey may remain visible for days before the gray knot of digestion disappears.

Their transparency does not make them difficult to find. Each has a mineral stomach, a faint shadow, and a distinctive pressure pulse. Other animals respond to these signs long before the bell itself becomes visible.

Eggs collect separately. Most are not laid in the long, transparent strings seen in the water; they are spawned loosely and sorted afterward by charge. Embryos of different kinds accumulate at different distances from the stone, dark punctuation inside cases of clear gelatin. Development may take years. A change in voltage can slow it further, so that a cluster drifts through several seasons with almost no visible alteration.

For many species, the wandering larva is the quickest and most vulnerable stage of life. It must find a fracture, a loop, or a tier before its small store of tissue is exhausted. The crowded water is full of these travelers, and fuller still of mouths able to use them.

### The slow giants and the brief hunters

The largest veilbacks may be twenty paces long, though their folded, translucent bodies often make them difficult to distinguish from a temporary thinness in the tiers. Hundreds of small fins line their edges. A long ventral seam opens into a pleated filter, and water rich in eggs, motes, and fine tissue passes slowly through it.

A mature veilback can take a season to travel its own length. It does not pursue food. It adjusts, almost imperceptibly, until a profitable margin of a tier passes into the filter.

Its approach is often announced on the rock. The animal’s shadow moves first; clinkers close beneath it in a long sequence, and the wave of shell-ticks travels ahead of its body. Fin-slips fold themselves into crevices. Comb-bells contract and drift aside.

Behind a veilback remains a lane of depleted water. For years it can be read by the scarcity of mineral-speckled bodies and by the scavengers lingering along its margins. Small animals also shelter in the grooves of its fins, feeding on particles that escape the filter. They are passengers only in the sense that the veilback has not yet eaten them.

The Kettle’s swiftest hunters are the needlebacks. They are rigid, narrow animals, usually dark enough to resemble splinters detached from the spire. Much of the body is given to electrical tissue; the gut is surprisingly small.

A needleback may hold station beside a bell tier for years. It does not chase. When pressure, charge, and prey coincide, it discharges and closes its mouth in the same brief event. The blue stitch is over almost immediately. Sometimes the only remaining evidence is a faint rain of transparent bell fragments and a needleback drifting with its gut visible inside it.

Not every electrical stitch is a hunt. Some originate in the stressed stone. But the animal-made ones are often followed by a momentary hole in the continuous ticking, as nearby shells close and small mouths stop beating.

### Refuse, carcasses, and the rim

Molts and bodies do not simply fall out of the Kettle’s ecology. There is no soft bottom to receive them.

Moltcatchers wait both on the circuits and in open water. They have flat central shells and long, thorned limbs that can be held open like a loose sieve. At rest, some resemble empty molts so closely that they are recognized only when their limbs close. They crush what they catch, consume the organic lining and remaining tissue, and eject fine mineral fragments. Much of the pale shell-flour in the crevices has passed through such animals.

A large carcass becomes a habitat before it becomes refuse. A dead veilback may drift for decades, its gelatinous sheets colonized by fin-slips, its filter opened by moltcatchers, its remaining flesh visited by animals from tiers that would never have approached it alive. A dead wheel may continue turning for a short time in the current that once sustained it, even as its zooids are removed one by one.

Decay is mostly consumption. In the cold, clear water, flesh can remain recognizable long after death. A body may complete much of a circuit around the stone before only mouthparts, shell, and a little flour remain.

The last substrate is the boundary itself. Rim-scales live against it: thin, transparent animals with smooth bodies and a narrow fringe of cilia. They use the inner curve much as clinkers use rock, crawling slowly over a surface that yields beneath them. They feed on disabled motes, torn egg jelly, and other bodies pressed into the boundary by circulation.

Nothing lives on the dry side. A rim-scale exposed by a severe dimple withdraws its fringe and remains motionless until water closes over it again. Occasionally one is carried into a droplet pulled loose from the lens. It slows with the droplet, hesitates in the air, and returns.

### The use of silence

For the inhabitants, a reversal is not only a loss of sound. It is a loss of geography.

Staples cannot move, and clinkers usually close. Yoke-eel contractions become ragged as different segments respond at different times. Wheels coast, their membranes hanging at unfamiliar angles. Fin-slips wedge themselves beneath rays or between shell plates. Hook-motes stop holding their narrow tiers and begin, very gradually, to cross feeding grounds that were previously separate. Egg strings lose their straightness and sag into translucent loops.

Some reproduction waits for this interruption. Gametes that would ordinarily be carried apart mingle while the field is weak. Certain bells release their brood only after the hum has faltered; wheel fragments and young eels can drift across boundaries that would later turn them back. There is no sudden bloom. Development remains slow, and most of the new life simply joins the transparent crowd.

Not every association restarts. An old loop may resume with several contractions missing, then fall silent for good. Its wheel tilts, its fin-slips disperse, and scavengers arrive long before the last current has faded. Elsewhere, reversed flow brings rich brine to staples that had been declining.

As the tiers reform, their inhabitants exchange depths according to charge, age, and the mineral burdens in their shells. Some animals spend the rest of their lives in a different part of the Kettle from the one in which they were born. The noise returns unevenly: first isolated valves, then rasping, then the bass contraction of the outer loops, until separate sounds can no longer be distinguished.

The Kettle has no central awareness of these events. Its memory is anatomical: an asymmetrical wheel, an interrupted staple row, a pavement grown around old shells, a population of animals all hatched during the same silence.

A staple knows the fracture beneath its flesh. A yoke-eel knows the contraction arriving through its neighbor. A hook-mote knows a difference in charge thinner than its own body. A veilback knows whether the water entering its filter is rich or poor. None knows the silver lens seen from the valleys.

The Kettle has no keeper. It has conductive shell touching conductive shell, contractions passed onward, colonies turning in brine, broods held for years in clear jelly, hunters waiting beside bell tiers, and mouths small enough to make a meal of a molt. Each is occupied with its own survival. The hanging ocean is what all those occupations look like from far away.
