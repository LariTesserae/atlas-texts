No animal is hiding in the Thirst Engine, and no keeper has taken the maintenance booth. The life of the chamber is not a rumor in the black pool or a shape just beyond the lamplight. It is the vegetation itself, easily mistaken for passive growth because its important movements occur through plumbing, dormancy, and differential survival rather than through anything an animal would recognize as behavior.

The engine is best understood as climate, not company. It has a pulse but no appetite, a cycle but no response to the grove. The plants are not its crew. Neither are they necessarily one organism. They are a small, tangled flora whose forms have been determined less by light than by access to stillness, vapor, heat, and electrical faults.

At the base of most thickets is a mat-forming plant that might be called *hushroot*. Its visible portion is largely historical: dead root sheaths, compacted dust, and the remains of brown leaf sheets pressed into a springy felt. The living strands run within and below this material, dark green when broken but brown-black where exposed. Hushroot establishes itself only where dust can remain undisturbed long enough to be bound together. Once established, it deepens its own refuge, holding more dust and protecting the finer roots beneath it.

Its apparent preference for acoustic hollows is not hearing or choice. Root tips that enter the loud ribs are abraded, desiccated, or shaken loose before they can branch; tips in quieter ground survive. Over many generations, this produces edges so precise that they seem deliberately trimmed. At a loud boundary, the mat ends in rounded, scoured lobes. The bare stone beyond may contain spores or dormant fragments, but nothing there can establish quickly enough to remain.

Rooted through these mats are the broad-leaved *blackfans*, the chamber’s principal water holders. Each fan is condenser, cistern, and slow spillway. The upper leaf surface is armored in wax and dust; the cooler underside is divided by copper-colored veins into shallow collection channels. Vapor that escapes a seal and touches those channels may leave only a few beads, but the beads are conducted into hollow petioles rather than being allowed to evaporate freely. With age, minerals deposited by repeated droplets harden along the channels. An old leaf therefore becomes partly a gutter built from its own residue.

Water is stored in the petioles, root crowns, and waxy chambers among the roots. These reservoirs are not a common supply for the whole grove. A plant releases water when dryness in its surrounding root mat trips its valves; neighboring roots may benefit, but only because they happen to share the wetted ground. One fan can be releasing stored water while another, a short distance away, remains tightly sealed.

Their shapes record the rain columns. Beneath a reliable column, blackfans develop tiered crowns, with older leaves forming successive catchments and younger ones living on the drip. Between columns, the same kind of plant may remain low and thick-stemmed, with fewer leaves and disproportionately large reservoirs. A visitor could easily mistake these forms for separate species. The overlapping foliage is not a canopy competing for sunlight; it is a stack of condensing surfaces and drip lines. Height concerns plumbing, not illumination. Even the noon blade at the stairwell is treated less as nourishment than as another possible source of desiccation.

The translucent-jointed trunks and looped canes—the *pithcanes*—occupy a narrower set of opportunities. Their finest roots enter old insulation and settle around corroded contacts. Dark nodules form there, embedded in scale and warmed by the passage of leaking charge. The canes draw on both that current and the chemical gradients in the surrounding corrosion. The clear joints along their stems interrupt excessive conduction and allow the tissues to expand without splitting. Within them, the honey-colored pith acts as a thermal reservoir, smoothing the changes between pressure cycles.

Their bitter green sap is not a warning to herbivores; there has never been anything here worth warning. It is simply the odor of their metal-rich chemistry when exposed to heat and air. Wounds close beneath a dark, varnish-like skin rather than remaining wet for long.

A stand of pithcanes leaning toward a failing coil is not reaching for it in any purposeful sense. Shoots on the favored side receive just enough additional energy to grow faster, while shoots too close are scarred or killed. Over decades, growth and removal together produce the appearance of a slow, unanimous inclination.

Highest in the chamber, the narrow-leaved veils suspended from cable runs perform most of the visible reproductive work. The cables are only scaffolding. Their value lies in placing dry capsules within rising currents of warm air. Some veils root in dust caught in cable loops; others send hair-fine root lines down toward older mats.

Nothing here flowers to attract an eye or fruits to be carried on a body. The reproductive structures are made to be shaken. Their dry capsules have ribs and lips of slightly different dimensions, so each resonates most strongly within a narrow range of harmonics. As a capsule ages and dries, that range changes. Opening therefore depends on both the condition of the capsule and the present voice of the engine. Some open during an ordinary pressure cycle. Others require an overtone produced only by the hard internal knock, or the brief shriek of a particular valve. Many remain closed for years.

A change in the engine’s pitch can consequently leave a curtain healthy but reproductively silent, while another patch, previously unable to release anything, suddenly sheds spores whenever the cycle turns. Empty capsules may remain attached long after the stem bearing them has died.

Most spores accomplish nothing. They are carried onto hot metal, blown against wet leaves, or deposited in loud zones where they cannot remain. A successful spore settles in quiet dust and later receives enough condensed water to germinate. Its first structure is not a leaf but a small root knot, binding dust around itself. Only after that knot has survived several dry intervals does it invest in exposed growth. Fallen stems rooting at their joints are a more dependable means of local increase; spores matter chiefly for crossing the bare ribs and reaching newly formed refuges. Spores carried toward the stair find progressively less heat, charge, vapor, and shelter. The grove’s boundary is therefore physiological rather than merely architectural.

The plants interpenetrate without forming a chamber-wide individual. Some thickets are old clones; others contain several lineages with roots crossing but never joining. Hushroot traps substrate, blackfans occasionally wet it, and pithcanes create small zones of persistent warmth. These effects can resemble cooperation, but none requires coordination. Competition is similarly unfamiliar: not for sunlight, but for quiet ground, access to condensation, and positions close enough to an electrical fault to benefit without being burned.

Death returns material only slowly. A fan may remain apparently intact long after its reservoirs have stopped filling. Eventually its leaf collapses into a thin brown sheet. In a quiet pocket, that sheet joins the dust; in a louder one, it is abraded away. Hot condensate and the chemistry of living roots release some of what the sheets contain, but there is no rapid conversion into rich soil. The dust is a mixture of basalt, corrosion products, mineral scale, dormant propagules, and the grove’s ground-down ancestors. A visible census of green tissue would miss much of the life merely waiting in it.

Even the pool is not an oasis apart from this economy. Its water is costly to use, laden with dissolved material and filmed with copper-colored dust. A few scale-sheathed roots work at its margins, and the nearest blackfans tend to be stunted rather than luxuriant. Nothing floats or swims beneath the surface. The open water persists because uptake is slow and the failed condenser continues to replace it. It is less an aquatic habitat than a sump being processed at the pace of roots.

The visible grove is thus an archipelago. Each acoustic hollow has its own population, water history, and reproductive prospects. When wear changes the engine’s note and a quiet zone shifts, there is no collective migration. At the advancing edge, sporelings and rooting stems appear where dust has begun to settle. Behind them, established mats persist on stored water. Farther back, relict canes and fans may remain alive long after conditions have ceased to favor replacement. Size is a poor guide to age: a small fan may be an ancient survivor, while a large thicket may owe its bulk to a comparatively brief period of reliable dripping.

The grove does affect the machine locally. Roots can partly seal a worn seam until pressure clears the obstruction; mats alter where condensation gathers; a cane tapping a bad contact may eventually char and change the leakage it once used. These effects are too limited and irregular to constitute maintenance. Neither cooperation nor awareness is needed to explain the arrangement.

If the engine’s faults were corrected and its leaks sealed, the grove’s subsidies would diminish. If the engine stopped altogether, the vapor, thermal gradients, and reproductive harmonics would disappear. In either case, stored water and dormant tissue would delay the decline, not prevent it. The plants persist because the machine remains active but imperfect.

For them, a faulty seal is rain, a corroded contact is a feeding ground, and a loud standing wave is a desert. What lives in the Thirst Engine is, in effect, its inefficiency—given roots, patience, and a means of keeping water.
