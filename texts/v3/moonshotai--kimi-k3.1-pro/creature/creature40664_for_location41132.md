## The Interflows

The terraces are not uninhabited. Their inhabitants simply have no reason to cross open ground, and almost never possess a body that could do so.

They live along the route taken by the rain: through open joints, along the buried ash beds, and out again into the damp seams. Call them **interflows**. The name describes a path rather than a shape, which is appropriate. An interflow has no fixed outline apart from the connected wetness through which its body extends.

At the mouth of an inhabited seep, part of it may sometimes be seen. Beneath the moss there will be a dull charcoal-green skin, or a slippery translucence on the underside of an overhang. It looks very like an ordinary algal film. It does not recoil when touched, quicken when shaded, or reveal itself by movement. Over several hours it may thicken slightly or withdraw a fraction deeper into the rock. The rest of the organism lies behind it: colorless filaments following pores in the ash, fine living cords occupying spaces that water itself can scarcely enter.

An interflow is a partnership of tissues. Green cells near the seep mouths gather light; the pale body below distributes what they make and takes in minerals and dissolved organic matter. It does not browse the ferns or consume fallen leaves. By the time leaf matter reaches it, rain, fungi, and bacteria have already done most of the work. Much of its nourishment arrives as leachate, too dilute to discolor the water.

Not every wet seam contains one. Some are small enough to live behind a single garden wall. Others extend beneath most of a district, with dry basalt on every side. The whole connected tissue may be one individual, though *individual* can be misleading. A fracture may divide an interflow, and at first the two portions will behave identically. If they remain separate, their responses gradually diverge. Eventually, should water bring them into contact again, they will treat one another as neighbors.

An interflow has no experience of the view from the rim. Its world has depth and pressure but no panorama. It knows rain that has fallen on bare basalt by its rapid arrival and clean mineral composition; rain delayed by a grove comes later, carrying leaf acids and fine organic matter. A new fracture may announce itself as a loss of resistance deep in the ash before any water appears at the surface. A hot afternoon is perceived only as a slight drying at exposed margins and an increased demand from the green tissue there.

Its responses are distributed. Filaments swell here, contract there, deposit a little mineral at an outlet, or loosen sediment so that the next pulse of water will carry it away. Nothing resembling a thought needs to travel through the whole body before a local correction can be made. Yet larger changes propagate slowly, and the colony’s character lies in those larger patterns: which route is favored after heavy rain, how quickly a covered tank is replenished, how much water is allowed to pass into a garden before an outlet is narrowed.

Two interflows can recognize one another without merging. Each maintains a distinctive composition in its surface tissue and a characteristic pattern of response. A brief constriction in one seep can send a pressure change toward another; a release of dissolved material may follow more slowly. Neither the drops nor their intervals constitute a simple language. A human listener ticking them off beside a cistern would hear only the small spills and overflows accompanying a much larger exchange.

This helps explain the spacing of the town. The open shelves between districts are not wasted ground. They keep neighboring bodies hydrologically distinct. Two interflows may discharge into the same marsh and remain separate there, just as they may share the cistern without sharing an identity. Their neighbors are not necessarily those closest to them on the surface, but those whose water they can affect.

Growth and hydraulic adjustment can accomplish much, but they cannot lift a roof slab or true a threshold. For that, an interflow occasionally makes **wrights**.

A wright is not a young interflow, nor a member of a second species. It is a temporary organ, grown when work must be done beyond the reach of water. The first sign of a building season is usually a pale felting around the edge of a washing court or covered tank. Over weeks this tissue gathers fine ash and plant fiber into a compact core, then cords itself into limbs.

A mature wright is seldom taller than a human knee. It resembles a wet root ball that has learned to brace itself. It has no head, no front, and no permanent number of limbs. Some of its cords can tighten around a stone for leverage while others fan into delicate tips, reading moisture and vibration along a fracture. Several wrights can twine around the same slab and share its weight. Their strength comes from anchoring and joining rather than from stature.

They work under cloud, in rain, or before the exposed stone has warmed. During bright afternoons they return to the tanks and narrow roofed rooms. A wright separated too long from water simply slackens; it does not leave a skeleton. Its cords loosen, its green tissue dulls, and eventually there is only a little fibrous matter and a handful of dark silt.

A building season may involve only a handful of wrights and a modest amount of work: six roof stones reseated, a stair tread replaced, a channel lip raised by the thickness of a finger. The town accumulated through many such seasons, separated by long periods in which no wright existed anywhere on the middle step. There is no fixed generation and no regular anniversary of building. Wrights appear when several wet years have provided enough reserve and when the parent body has work that cannot be accomplished by seepage alone.

When the work is finished, they return to the washing courts and unmake themselves. Their tissue is carried back into the drains and gardens; the ash in their cores settles harmlessly into the basins. Unshaped stones used for hammering or wedging are left among other stones. Useful timber is already incorporated into a door or roof support. There are no tools to store because there are no separate tradespeople to own them, and no burials because nothing remains that requires burial.

The town’s rooms are therefore not houses in the human sense. They are sheltered work chambers, places where wrights can fit stone, remain humid, or wait out a dry interval. Their small size suits both the available roofing and the bodies that use them. A door leaf is a valve against drying wind, not a barrier against intruders. The narrow slots behind the walls are more important entrances: through them, the permanent body maintains contact with the temporary ones.

The courts provide open working ground. The gardens supply organic matter and hold shade. The covered tanks are places of restoration. Stairs serve the wrights; the longer, gentler water routes serve the interflow itself. A path is for the body that exists briefly. A channel is for the body that endures.

The broad communal cistern is both a reserve and the nearest thing the settlement has to a meeting place. During a congregation, no figures gather around it. Each contributing interflow sends in a carefully limited flow and extends a little living tissue near its inlet. The shallowness of the cistern prevents these small contributions from being lost in depth. Exchange may continue for days or weeks. At its end, mixed water is released toward the marsh, and each colony withdraws behind its own route.

What has been arranged remains visible only as slight alterations. One outlet is permitted to clog. Another is cleared. A garden receives more overflow, while a district whose ash has become unstable is relieved of part of its supply. A refusal can be expressed just as quietly, by sealing an inlet or mineralizing a shared passage. No council chamber is required because the resulting agreement is not a text. It is a working condition.

The interflows preserve no dated history of the town. An old decision is simply one that no longer needs to be remade. The fit of a stone, the depth of a settling basin, and the gradient of a conduit are maintained by use and occasional repair. Tool marks remain because smoothing them would change nothing that matters. There is no carved ornament, but there can be an almost excessive exactness in places no wright needed to make exact: a turn eased beyond the minimum, a threshold fitted so closely that it holds damp without clay, a cistern rim whose overflow always begins at the same point.

If they have anything corresponding to pride, it lies in return. A pool rises after rain and comes back to its dark mark. A spring quickens and then resumes its modest rate. A storm enters the town, is divided among tanks, gardens, and drains, and leaves without stripping the ash. This constancy is not passivity. It is the visible result of innumerable small corrections.

A visitor could live among the buildings for a season and attribute all of this to fortunate construction. The evidence of inhabitants would look like a series of minor coincidences: sediment remaining loose in one basin while hardening in another, a fissure clearing before its outlet could flood, a channel keeping its pale liner clean though no path leads conveniently to it.

An interflow would not necessarily recognize the visitor as a single being. It might know only warm shade on one tank, an occasional pressure through a stair, and leaf matter repeatedly introduced into a drain. A person wishing to be noticed would do better to make one small, harmless, repeatable change—to clear the same settling basin after each rain, for example. The first response would probably be correction. Only after many repetitions might correction become accommodation. Even then, the visitor would be known less as a face or voice than as a recurrent coolness and a slight delay in one branch of water.

From the upper rim, none of this distinguishes the town from the rock. There are no wrights in the courts now, and there may be none for another century. But the settlement has not been abandoned. It is between building seasons, not between inhabitants.

The interflows remain beneath the ash and behind the walls, extending through the useful wetness and withdrawing from the exposed stone. The town is the dry, durable portion of their lives—the part that can hold a shape while the rest of the body changes. And the silence between drops is not entirely empty. Some of it is simply the time required for a disturbance in one part of the plateau to reach another, and for an answer to begin making its way back.
