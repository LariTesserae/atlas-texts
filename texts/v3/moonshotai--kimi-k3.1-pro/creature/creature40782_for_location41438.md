Nothing lives in the Ninth Heat Ward in a way that would trouble a census. There are no households, no nests, no colonies with a centre that can be found and counted. Life appears at the edges of things: a dulling of reflected light at the waterline, a hand’s width of grey on ceramic, a transparent movement that might at first be another falling drop. Yet the ward is not sterile. It supports an ecology almost exactly scaled to its emptiness.

Most of that ecology has no visible body. Bacterial films occupy the basin rims, the seep joints, and the replaceable mineral beds, living on trace organic matter carried down in the water and on the few usable substances in the stone. At their thickest they look less like growth than a slight reluctance in the rock to dry. Between wettings they contract into pores and hairline fractures. Condensate from a discharge contains little nourishment in itself, but it loosens older film, moves dissolved material, and delivers both to places where something can use them. The ward’s warm rain is therefore not quite weather and not quite food, though for some of its inhabitants it amounts to a combination of the two.

The colourless root-fans are only partially inhabitants. Each is the far end of an organism standing elsewhere, beneath a pavement tree-pit or in some narrow surface garden. Sunlight reaches the parent leaves; sugars descend through hundreds of metres of root; the final pale branches spread into a darkness where they can never repay the journey. They resemble cracks until water moves them. Around their finest tips the bacterial films grow a little richer, fed by small leakages from the living tissue. When a parent tree is cut down or a borehole dries, its fan may persist for months, still branching with exquisite slowness, before the subsidy finally fails. Then it becomes one of the ward’s rare meals. Almost nothing is wasted because almost nothing arrives.

Ashgrass is the only life that looks placed rather than merely permitted. It is also the only life whose individual holdings are numbered. The municipal records treat each patch as a small, very slow piece of equipment, and the patches themselves seem willing to accept the arrangement. They do not spread across the warm floors or soften the distant walls. They remain at the exchangers’ cool skirts, where heat, cold ceramic, and condensing water occur within a distance small enough for the organism to use.

A patch grows by adding scales at its margin and losing them within. The youngest tissue is nearly colourless; older scales take on the flat grey of weathered ash, and those burdened with removed metals may darken further. Nothing about it hurries. A patch may be decades old without becoming appreciably wider. Fragments carried away by runnels almost always settle beyond the thermal margin and die. The intervening metres of bare stone are not an unfinished invasion. They are the condition of the plant’s survival.

The transparent isopods live mainly around the two shallow basins, though their actual territories extend backward into wet joints and under mineral shelves where a hand lamp cannot follow. Many are smaller than rice grains. Light passes through their bodies so readily that a moving shadow, or the dark thread of a recently filled gut, may be easier to see than the animal itself.

They spend long periods doing very little. Then a discharge shakes film loose, sends brief runnels across the rims, and scatters usable particles through the shallows. The isopods emerge separately, without anything that could honestly be called a swarm. A visitor who has watched bare stone for an hour may suddenly find a dozen at work around one basin: scraping, turning, pausing, each movement so slight that the whole gathering seems on the verge of being an optical error. Minutes later the rim is bare again.

Their small economy is exacting. They graze film, rasp at dead ashgrass scales, recover what they can from shed root tissue, and consume their own molts and dead when they find them. There is too little carrion for anything to specialize in carrion. A body left above the water is not the beginning of a foul-smelling decay but a brief concentration of minerals and carbon, visited until almost nothing recognizable remains. Whole generations can pass without a human observer seeing a young animal or a brood.

The blind loaches of the third basin are the ward’s only vertebrates and its largest permanent flesh. Even they seldom look large. The longest are no more than a finger, pale as the root-fans and usually less visible. They do not school, rise to lamps, or hang decoratively in open water. Most of their lives are spent in crevices, with only the movement of the gills and an occasional adjustment of a fin distinguishing them from a strip of pale mineral.

Their world is composed of vibrations too small to survive in a human’s attention. The shift of an isopod beneath a shelf, the click of a shell against stone, the faint disturbance of water at a crevice mouth: these are events. The ward’s roar is merely the medium through which events must be detected. During a discharge the basin becomes so crowded with noise that hunting is largely pointless. The loaches wait. Afterward, as the impacts separate again and the water’s confusion subsides, they may leave their crevices to feed. A hunt often consists of one short movement and a return to stillness. There is no pursuit worth the energy it would cost.

Their stillness is not wisdom or mystery. It is simply the cheapest way to be a fish where meals are rare.

No reliable census of them exists. Every survey method counts its own disturbance. Lamps, probes, traps, and changes in pressure cause some animals to withdraw and others to move; an empty trap may indicate absence, satiety, caution, or merely an unfortunate hour. Young loaches are seen too seldom for their breeding sites to be known with confidence. The official estimates are therefore accompanied by notes so extensive that the notes have become the more trustworthy document. The fish themselves remain unaffected by the disagreement.

People enter this ecology only as weather enters it: briefly, warmly, and with consequences they may not notice. Maintainers bring salt on their skin, fibres from their clothing, microbes on their boots, and vibrations that travel much farther than their voices. They feed nothing intentionally. They introduce nothing to make the ward richer. Even a well-meant alteration—a little shelter for the ashgrass, a basket to protect the basin rim, a ledge on which instruments might conveniently be left—would be both an ecological intervention and a change to the cavity’s response. The paths keep visitors safe, but they also keep the inhabitants from being loved too heavily.

If the ward itself sometimes seems like the largest living thing present, that is an error encouraged by scale. It has convection for breath, condensation for sweat, and discharges for a pulse. It responds to distant loads, holds warmth, sheds water, and changes after every passage of sound. But it does not feed, reproduce, or repair itself with anything other than physics and human maintenance. Its apparent moods are the city returning through stone.

The genuinely living things are nearly all supported from elsewhere. Tree crowns subsidize the roots. Surface seeps subsidize the films. The city’s heat and machinery sustain the narrow conditions in which ashgrass persists. A discharge may determine when the isopods feed, and the settling of the water may determine whether a loach catches one. Even the ward’s small apex predator lives, at several removes, within the consequences of trains braking and pressure doors closing far above.

Just after a discharge, the ward comes closest to offering a visible census. Water beads on the numbered patches of ashgrass. The root-fans shine in their seep lines. Transparent grazers work briefly along the basin rims. In the deep basin, where instruments disagree about the bottom, a pale fish may leave its crevice and then seem to dissolve into the general shimmer. Above all this, the exchangers’ chord settles and the warm air resumes its climb.

Then the isopods vanish. The loach becomes still. The lamp finds wet stone, numbered ceramic, and nothing obviously alive.

The official population remains zero, and the ward remains empty in every way that matters to its operation. Its inhabitants take almost no room, add almost no mass, and leave the open air open. They do not fill the Empty Choir, or accompany it, or give it a voice.

They are simply the few small things that can live between its notes.
