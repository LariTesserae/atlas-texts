The only unambiguous life in the Underprint is the moss, and even that is easy to mistake for a defect in the sealant.

It has no presence at the scale of the chamber. It does not stir when the relays close or retreat from the vibration in the floor. A patch can be examined from a hand’s breadth away without revealing whether it is growing, dormant, or mostly dead. Its gray-green is close to the color of contaminated dust. Only when condensation arrives does it separate itself from the manufactured surfaces: each minute leaf takes its own bead of water, and what looked like a smear becomes densely, precisely reflective.

The patches are not a community. Nothing connects them except the plumbing that occasionally moves water between them, and the fact that they are remnants of the same failed expedient. Each occupies a narrow coincidence of conditions. There must be a lamp close enough to reach it, a seam that sometimes releases moisture, and enough old gel or mineral residue to hold it against the composite. A short distance beyond the patch, one of those conditions fails. The moss cannot expand merely because there is empty wall beside it. Empty wall is not habitat.

Under the amber lamp, a patch may have a darker, older interior where growth is packed into exhausted gel, with its living edge confined to a few millimeters near the seam. Beneath the greenish light, another may remain little more than several flattened islands, visible mainly when wet. A patch dependent on the third lamp receives illumination in doses separated by long darkness. Its persistence does not necessarily mean that it is flourishing. Some of the moss may be replacing its losses; some may simply be declining slowly enough to resemble permanence.

Its cold tolerance has not made it vigorous. It has made it difficult to finish killing.

For most of the cycle, the patches wait in dry cold. Their surfaces dull. The gel around them contracts or hardens. Frost may whiten the metal beside them while the moss itself retains only the dampness held deep in a joint. There is no soil to buffer these changes, no surrounding vegetation to shelter them, and no season in which conditions reliably improve. Their entire climate is a consequence of pumps and valves elsewhere.

When warm fluid enters the ceiling loop, the change reaches them indirectly. Metal ticks. Frost releases its grip. Water gathers overhead. The first drop to strike a patch may run off without wetting it completely; the moss holds moisture in separated points rather than under a continuous film. Then the gel softens slightly, the leaves take up water, and the gray in the patch becomes fractionally more green.

For a little while, it also approaches its original purpose. A swollen edge may narrow a seam by almost nothing. A thread of gel may interrupt the path of a film of water. A drop may pause at the patch before continuing downward. The moss cannot seal the Underprint or repair any meaningful failure in it. But it has not entirely ceased to be sealant. Its function survives as a gesture too small for the machinery to measure.

The same water that sustains it can remove it. A fragment loosened by dripping may be carried into a basin, washed against a ridge, or taken through the sump. Most such journeys must end in filtration or in some unlit, waterless joint. Very occasionally, a fragment catches where light and moisture coincide. It may remain there through enough cycles to anchor itself in dust and mineral wash. In this way the moss spreads without anything tending it. Drainage performs the dispersal; filtration performs most of the selection.

Nothing browses the patches down. Nothing nests in them or shelters beneath their leaves. There is no food web to return their dead tissue to use. What dies dries where it is, washes away, or becomes part of the material accumulating beneath it. Silt can dull a patch until it resembles the floor again. A new bead of composite can cover one edge while leaving another exposed. A translucent vein may preserve a gray-green fleck below its surface for a time, although preservation and survival are not the same thing.

The deposition carriage therefore inhabits the chamber more actively than the moss does, without being alive at all. It has a fixed place on its rail, periods of stillness, and a repeated way of addressing the ground. It would be easy to call it a gardener: it follows the weather, works around what the water has changed, and sometimes leaves a moss patch undisturbed. But it does not distinguish care from erasure. Both are produced by the same pass.

There is no reason to think its understanding of the floor includes life. A patch swollen with condensation may matter only as a minute alteration in surface. A silted patch may be indistinguishable from any other irregularity. The carriage may lay material beside it, over it, or in a line determined by some feature several layers older. If a hollow remains damp and later shelters a fragment, that is not protection. If a living patch is buried, that is not hostility. The machine sustains and destroys the moss through exactly the same indifference.

Yet the moss can enter the chamber’s memory.

A patch may interrupt the deposition of a bead by less than a hair. The next pass repeats part of that interruption. Water subsequently follows the altered edge, laying down a different trace of silt or wearing a slightly different channel. The carriage records that change as well. Long after the original growth has died or been washed away, the floor may continue to reproduce a small deviation that began with living tissue.

This would not quite be a fossil. It need not preserve the shape of the moss itself. It would be something stranger: a habit the landscape acquired from life and continued after life had gone.

Nor is the number of surviving patches a promise of balance. Five or six may represent a tiny, self-renewing population. They may equally represent the present stage of a long failure, with burial and filtration proceeding a little faster than replacement. Nothing in the Underprint requires the moss to remain. Its persistence is not an equilibrium maintained by the chamber, but the temporary outcome of systems pursuing unrelated tasks.

Life here survives through a chain of small omissions: a lamp that continues to shine, a seam that still sweats, a fragment missed by filtration, a nozzle passing slightly to one side. The chain need not hold.

When the next brief rain comes, though, that uncertainty is not visible. Water gathers on the surviving leaves, one silver point at a time. A patch darkens toward green beneath a service lamp. Above it, the carriage waits with its arms folded, ready to survey the ground again. It may record the moss, or its outline, or only the place where the moss used to be.

It will not know the difference.
