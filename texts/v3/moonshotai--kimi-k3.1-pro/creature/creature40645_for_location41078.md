Nothing old lives beneath the White Court. The roar has no throat, and there is no pale, secret animal cruising behind the pier. The cistern has not had time to acquire a resident in that sense. Its inhabitants are smaller and more provisional: cells held in a film, filaments anchored at a joint, a plant experimenting with roots. Even the visible life is less a population than a first attempt at remaining.

The clear water itself is almost uninhabited. Single cells arrive with the roof water, on dust washed from the catchments, or from the surfaces of the clay pipes. For a while they drift through the warm upper layer or sink slowly toward the cooler floor. A few may use the sunlight while it lasts. Most accomplish nothing before the next siphon carries them toward the outlet. Open water offers no lasting address. To drift is eventually to leave; survival begins with attachment.

For that reason, the perforated basket is more important than its size suggests. The hand-rolled clay pellets inside it are full of small pores. Within those pores the water is comparatively still, even when the current outside combs the rush leaves sideways. Bacteria can settle there without immediately being stripped away. Around the roots they find traces of organic matter—shed cells, slight exudations, the first leakage from living tissue—and a little nourishment passes from the plant into films too thin to discolor the clay. The basket is not fertile in any ordinary sense, but it is the richest small region in the chamber.

The rush is the only inhabitant with a body large enough to have a posture. It is also an immigrant. Its oldest tissues predate the cistern and contain food made under another sky. That stored allowance supports it while it discovers whether this bright, warm submersion is a place where it can live or merely a place where it will take a long time to die.

Its roots advance cautiously through the pellets. Some remain within the basket, branching among the protected pores. Others find the perforations and emerge into a harsher world. A root tip outside the clay has no shelter from the draw of the siphon; it must thicken, flatten itself against the basket, or risk being torn. Above it, the narrow leaves bend together when the outlet takes, then recover at different rates. The plant thus makes the hydraulic weather visible, though it does not respond to the roar itself. By the time sound fills the chamber, the rush has already felt the change.

The three patches of algae live by shorter intervals. One receives the full force of the midday column; the others depend more on reflected light. During bright stillness, their cells divide and, now and then, minute beads of oxygen collect within the film. They are so small that they silver the green rather than rising immediately. Then the inlet surges or the siphon draws, and the outermost growth is loosened. Some of it remains nearby; some vanishes through the drain. The patches stay hand-sized not because nothing is happening, but because growth and removal are still nearly balanced.

The transparent filaments near the inlet lead a more precarious version of the same life. Their anchored ends survive where the falling stream supplies movement without quite delivering its full impact. Their free lengths are repeatedly straightened, scattered, and straightened again. Pieces break away. Most are exported, but a holdfast remains, and from that small persistence the filament begins again.

Weather above the court therefore reaches these inhabitants with unusual directness. After rain, the more frequent flushing reduces them to their safest refuges: pores in the pellets, joints in the plaster, the protected bases of filaments. During a dry spell, the longer intervals allow them to venture outward. Films extend imperceptibly. Another root crosses a clay surface. The water remains clear because there is still so little to cloud it.

There is not yet a food web, only a few slight transfers: light into tissue, tissue into leakage, leakage into microbial growth, loose growth into the drain. Nothing grazes the algae. Nothing stirs the floor in search of food. Even decay has scarcely begun, because there has been so little to die.

One day the rush will lose a leaf. If the current takes it immediately, the event will mean almost nothing. If it lodges behind the basket, however, it may become the chamber’s first substantial store of organic matter. A few mineral grains could settle around it; a thicker film could form in the shelter of both. That small pocket, rather than the brilliant open water, would be where a more complicated ecology might begin.

For now, the builder’s fingerprint is still simply a human mark, not yet a habitat. Nothing in the cistern knows the court above or the hands that made it. Life beneath the paving is attempting something much simpler than haunting or stewardship: to attach, to catch a little light, and still to be there after the next roar.
