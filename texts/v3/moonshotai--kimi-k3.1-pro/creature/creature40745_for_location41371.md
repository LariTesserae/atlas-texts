The High Loom has fewer inhabitants than its noise suggests, and more than any census can conveniently contain.

Most of the visible ones are people. They live in small districts gathered around cisterns, mills, and governor houses, often within sound of one another but not necessarily within an easy walk. A group of twenty roofs may contain six households, a press crew’s dormitory, and several buildings visited only when a gauge changes. Whole arcades stand empty without being abandoned. Their drains still matter.

Nothing about the people’s appearance is especially strange. They wear close-woven fiber coats, short enough not to catch in machinery, waxed at the shoulders and patched with resin where wet bark has rubbed them thin. Press workers carry ear plugs on cords; line crews wear soft-soled boots for the living ground and iron cleats for the condenser towers. Their hands are stained green at the creases and black around the nails. Resin rash, crushed fingers, and damaged hearing are more common than anything an outsider would call uncanny.

Outdoors they can appear furtive, pausing at corners, comparing markers, looking at a chimney and then deliberately walking away from it. Indoors they are considerably less mysterious. Covered markets are loud with bargaining. Mill cooks complain about the quality of the seed meal. Children shout in courts and are told to stop by people working on the other side of a wall. Arguments about missing tools can outlast arguments about a street that has ceased to lead anywhere useful.

A good house is not necessarily large, dry, or old. It is a house whose rooms are usually adjacent to one another.

The oldest dwellings require continual pruning. Their floors are living foundations, their doorways thickened growth, their roofs a weave that has to be opened around chimneys and closed again against driven rain. Furniture stands on iron feet. Stored food is kept in sealed bins above the floor, not because of vermin—there are almost none—but because a patient root can enter a bin more cleanly than its lid. Housekeeping includes cutting pale threads out of table legs and checking that an interior wall has not begun to thicken around a warm pipe.

Beside the outer door hangs an approach slate. It is often more important than the key. One might read:

*CISTERN ARCH. LEFT AT THE THIRD INSULATOR. IF THE UPPER YARD IS WET, USE THE STAIR. TRY THE RESIN DOOR LAST.*

Children learn their home sequence early. They also learn that a visible landmark is information, not instruction. Being sent to fetch someone does not mean being allowed to walk directly toward them.

Neighborliness is therefore only partly a matter of proximity. A neighbor is someone whose gutter you would clear and who would, at least in principle, clear yours. Two households in adjoining courts may have almost nothing to do with each other, while a family across the plateau shares a cistern obligation, a freight account, and three generations of exchanged maintenance. Introductions often include these relationships before they include occupations.

No one farms open fields. There would be little point. A cleared strip begins closing at its margins while the tools are still being put away. Gardens occupy warm, narrow beds against mill walls and in sheltered cistern courts. They produce a grey-husked oilseed, a thick young shoot whose pith can be baked, bitter leaves for tea, and a few carefully preserved varieties that would disappear into the general pelt within a season if left unattended. Gardening consists less of planting than of preventing everything else from joining in.

Meals are plain, and water is measured, though a well-run district need not be short of either. The precious things are dependable supply and the labor that maintains it. Control of ground conveys little power; the ground will grow, shift underfoot, and decline to remain an intelligible property boundary. Control of a cistern allocation, press time, freight priority, or a reliable approach is another matter. Wealth often takes the form of redundancy: a second water pipe, a duplicate foundation, two possible roads home. Poverty can be having only one door and being obliged to trust it.

Nearly everyone’s work involves some form of upkeep. Mat-cutters take surface growth without opening more of the deep fiber than they can shore and close again. Fuel crews cut dry bricks from trenches that begin budding behind them. Press crews feed the endless clippings into rollers. Condenser mechanics work in wet cold beside towers ticking with collected warmth. Road keepers clear drains, reset flexible pipe joints, repaint markers, and replace power fittings, continually reasserting that a causeway is a road rather than a slightly flatter portion of the mat.

Every district also has someone whose duties include trying doors.

This is not a ceremonial office. An unused connection may remain serviceable for decades, or it may quietly acquire another yard. The tester records weather, governor load, the condition of the threshold, and the order in which the approaches were made. A good tester is suspicious of both panic and coincidence. District archives contain duplicate approach books kept in separate buildings, on the theory that a record of the way home is more useful if it does not all reside in the same place.

Governor keepers possess considerable practical authority, but they are not priests. Some believe the regulated beat genuinely helps relations hold; others think it merely provides a common reference against which slippage can be detected. The argument has been going on for generations and has not prevented either party from using the governor halls for meetings. Councils convene there because power is dependable, minutes tend to remain in order, and there is usually hot water. Cynicism does not make a steady flywheel less useful.

Visitors sometimes mistake all this maintenance for serenity. It is not. People become attached to particular rooms, particular views, and particular routes. A road keeper who has spent twenty years preserving a bridge’s connection to its two visible ends does not necessarily experience its failure as an interesting natural process. There are bitter disputes over abandoned approaches and over who has the right to declare a household unreachable. People keep spare keys for doors that no longer agree with them. They want full cisterns, guarded presses, dry bedding, and children who come home by an intelligible sequence.

The greater part of the population, however, is vegetable.

It would be too simple to call the root-mat one organism. Its depths contain roots that compete, graft, smother, support, and outlast one another. Some of the low silver-green growth spreads almost exclusively through rhizomes; other plants maintain small, fiercely separate crowns in the midst of the general weave. Whole populations rise and vanish over centuries without the surface changing its expression. The mat is less a single body than an old, crowded agreement among lives that cannot be conveniently separated.

The column trees are the oldest visible inhabitants, though their ages and even their individual boundaries are uncertain. One may carry dead, hollow wood on the side facing a court and vigorous young growth above a roof in another district. Its oldest roots need not be the roots presently supplying its crown. People use such trees as condensers, shelters, and references, but seldom as simple landmarks. A tree is more reliably identified by the pattern of its ribs, the sound of wind in its crown, and the obligations attached to its runoff than by where it happens to be seen.

Runoff from an old tree can support several households. Rights to it are inherited, traded, and fiercely disputed. A crown that drips into two otherwise unconnected courts may oblige their districts to share a gutter inspector. This does not make the tree sacred. People steal water from sacred things too, but there is no particular evidence that they consider these trees divine. They are simply senior infrastructure, alive enough to have interests of their own and old enough that no living person remembers all of them.

The open basins are part of the same population. The pelt there puts little effort into height, and young column shoots are rare. Browsers crop some growth, but not enough to explain the openness. Root tips turn aside around old groups; certain mosses thicken where taller stems would otherwise take hold. Whether this amounts to self-limitation, competition, or an accommodation too old to reconstruct is disputed. The people build within the openness without imagining that they have created it.

No one has taught the plants a sign or received an answer. Yet when spatial relations loosen, the plants often appear already prepared. Their roots occupy alternatives without requiring those alternatives to be reconciled. A severed route does not necessarily sever supply. A crown can continue to thrive while its trunk serves two arrangements of ground. Human engineering imitates this with loops, multiplied supports, and repeated approaches, generally without understanding whether the resemblance is more than practical.

A few plants have been drawn directly into industrial work. Current-sensitive vines are normally cut away from transmission routes, but some line keepers preserve a single trained strand in a ceramic trough. Its tip will turn toward a leaking feeder before an instrument registers the fault. Elsewhere the same vine is destroyed without sentiment, since an appreciated indicator and a bridged power line can be separated by only a few days of growth. Pale thread-stems are likewise useful in dead fiber and disastrous in a structural beam. The people do not divide growth into good and bad species. They divide it according to what it is doing, and where.

The conspicuous animals are few enough to be remembered individually.

A grey browser may spend an entire morning at the edge of a basin, taking a mouthful of tender growth and then standing so long that its stillness becomes more noticeable than its movement. It is lighter than its height suggests. Its narrow feet press almost nothing into the mat, and its grey flanks take the color of wet bark or pale stem according to the light. It feeds around young column growth and along certain bands of the pelt, leaving clean, resin-darkened cuts.

No one has made a working animal of it. It does not prosper in a court, and there are too few browsers to support a settled hunting economy. Mostly it is left alone. Road crews record sightings in the same books as unusual rain and unexplained vibration. Its call—a wooden, descending knock—can stop conversation in a market. Not because everyone believes it an omen, but because an animal voice carries through a country where almost all other sounds have been made by weather, plants, or machines.

The pale moths are less impressive and more closely watched. Their larvae live in dry, warm dead fiber, often around old flues and dynamo housings. The adults appear singly, resting where heat leaks into the cool air. They do not swarm around lamps or fill the evening with wings. An experienced vent keeper may note one and know, without looking at a gauge, that a seam has opened. A moth settled on a cold vent receives more attention. Usually the pipe is warm somewhere else.

There are no flocks on the mat, no murmur of poultry from the courts, no rats in the grain bins, no evening insect chorus. Consequently, domestic sounds are unusually exposed: a spoon against a cup, a cough through a resin window, someone drawing a bolt two rooms away. A person can be startled by another person simply moving in the vegetation.

Whether the engines count as inhabitants is a question people answer according to temperament. No sensible mechanic claims that a flywheel has wishes. Nevertheless, each hall has habits. One governor sheds load in certain rain; another runs most steadily when slightly out of tune. Workers recognize these tendencies long before they can explain them. Empty buildings also retain functions. Their pipes brace other pipes, their roots hold roadbeds, their lamps mark approaches. An unoccupied structure can be load-bearing in more than an architectural sense.

Even removed infrastructure sometimes continues to participate. When condensation appears along the course of a vanished pipe, no one expects to find iron inside the silver dotted line. If enough water gathers, however, a crew may put vessels beneath it and credit the result to the cistern account. Practical use does not require agreement about what has survived.

The most difficult residents to count are the people who live in the city only on certain approaches.

Now and then a market receives a crew from a district absent from the current approach books. Their clothing is familiar in material but not in cut. They carry wage tokens for mills no local crew can presently reach, and their governor calls may use different numbers for the same rhythms. Sometimes their boots are wet from rain falling in a court that, for everyone else, is dry. They buy food, arrange lodging, complain about freight charges, and leave by a route that cannot always be repeated.

They are not apparitions. They become tired, lose tools, remember relatives, and dislike being interrogated while eating. Nor is there good reason to consider them another kind of human being. The conservative explanation is that they live very far away and have arrived by an unusually efficient sequence. Often that explanation is sufficient. More awkward cases occur: a worker reports to an address currently used for cable storage, or a family insists that its house stands behind a door everyone has known for years as the entrance to an empty yard.

Most districts feed such travelers before asking them to prove their addresses. This is partly decency and partly insurance. Anyone may eventually belong to the household without a current approach. Road authorities then face less hospitable questions: whether a gutter serving an intermittent court must be maintained, whose labor pays for it, and whether its inhabitants owe freight dues when their market cannot be found. A census, in practice, becomes a list of present obligations rather than a total of souls.

Many people go their whole lives without meeting an unlisted household. They hear the stories, lose an occasional route, and otherwise get on with their work. Some districts keep no special category for such visits at all. Others preserve spare bedding and old letters for relatives whose approaches have not opened in decades. These rooms are not quite shrines. Pipe washers and winter clothing are stored in them too.

Toward evening, after a rain cell has crossed one district, the ordinary population becomes briefly visible. Press crews come in with wet shoulders. Someone prunes a root from beneath a stove; someone else copies an approach book by lamplight. Wet coats steam near a flue. A child asleep under a fiber blanket does not stir when the governor misses, though a grandparent does. Outside, an empty arcade lights itself. A pale moth clings to a dynamo vent. At the basin’s edge, a grey browser stands with water shining on its legs.

The mat tightens as the air cools. Condensation ticks into cisterns. Old column trees creak above roofs that may not all remain equally near one another by morning.

What lives in the High Loom, then, is a sparse human city, an ancient and internally divided population of plants, a handful of conspicuous animals, engines with deeply known habits, and households whose addresses cannot always be honored. None of them alone explains the place. Together they maintain a loose, practical occupancy: rooted, wired, employed, related, and not all reachable on the same day.
