Nothing has had time to become native to the Upper Bleedwell.

Everything alive there arrived after the paint dried: in incoming water, in dust drawn through the hatch, on the damp cuff of a commissioning glove. Most of it passed through or died without leaving a visible trace. The chamber offers no soil, no sheltered litter, no accumulated organic matter in which a foothold might deepen. What remains is not quite an ecosystem. It is a few small exceptions to drainage, filtration, and cleaning.

The visible inhabitants are the three algal films beneath the lower lamp.

The largest would fit beneath two fingers. It is nearly black at its center and greener at the edges, where the colony is thinnest and receives the most light. The second follows a slight irregularity in the ledge, a narrow crescent no wider than a pencil line. The third is easier to feel than see: a faint slippery roughness under a wet fingertip, with a few dark points where the film has thickened. No one has examined them closely enough to know whether they are one species or several. Their differences are principally differences of circumstance.

They live without a day. Cloud brightens and darkens beyond the wired glass, but that light does not reach them in any useful quantity. Their sun is the caged amber lamp. Their water arrives according to condensate, leakage, and the changing level in the sump. A long wet operating cycle spreads them slightly farther across the ledge. A harder flush tears at an edge. When the sump pump draws the water down, they are left exposed but still damp, held in their own thin mucilage and in the film of water clinging to the concrete.

Growth is almost imperceptible. The cold slows it; the weak light limits it; the water supplies minerals but little else. The algae persist partly because there is nothing present to graze them and partly because the ledge is just awkward enough to clean. They are not flourishing. They are merely losing more slowly than they grow.

The fourth patch, behind the drain, has a more precarious existence. After a series of gentle cycles it appears as a green shading in the roughness of the surface. It may spread for a while, following the meniscus of water that lingers there. Then a stronger flushing cycle scours the recess, or cleaning solution reaches it, and the color disappears. Its return does not necessarily require a new arrival from outside. A few cells may survive deeper in the surface texture, beyond the reach of the worst flow, and repeat the attempt.

The pale filaments in the seam are another attempt, less successful still. Now and then a spore germinates in the damp roughness and puts out a few colorless threads. They never gather into a cushion of moss. They make no leaves worth the name, no soft green shelf, nothing that could hold a droplet away from the metal. They remain a minute discoloration in the seam, easily mistaken for a fault in the paint.

If the sump water were sampled, it would contain more life than the ledge shows: bacteria, dormant cells, spores that will never germinate, organisms carried in from elsewhere in the station. Most are travelers. A few can persist in the cold, nutrient-poor water, dividing rarely and consuming traces of organic matter too slight to produce a visible stain. They contribute a little slipperiness at a meniscus, perhaps a faint cloudiness in a sample bottle. They do not make the chamber fertile. A sample would reveal more inhabitants, but not more habitat.

For all this life, the decisive violence is not the noise. The roar that makes the room uninhabitable to a person means little to a film of cells. Their disasters are hydraulic and chemical: a sudden current, a change in the wetting pattern, a pulse of cleanser, a drain cycle that leaves a previously sheltered surface exposed. Even vibration matters mostly when it alters the path of a drop. A shift of a few millimeters in that path can determine whether a patch remains wet or slowly disappears.

Occasionally a maintenance technician enters. For those few minutes, nearly all the living biomass in the chamber stands on the grating in one body.

The technician is not an inhabitant. They bring in heat on their clothing, moisture in their breath, dust in the folds of a glove, and perhaps a few new cells on a tool handle. Their presence briefly changes the small climate around the hatch. Their worklight passes over pipes, fasteners, labels, and the sump, sometimes crossing the green ledge without pausing. If they notice the algae at all, it is only to establish that it is not blocking a drain or concealing corrosion. At most it becomes a minor notation: trace growth, no operational significance.

They follow the painted instructions, complete the inspection, and leave. The hatch closes. Their warmth dissipates quickly. Nothing in the chamber depends on their return.

This is the whole population: three small holdings of green, one recurrent possibility behind the drain, a few filaments that never become moss, and an invisible traffic of cells in the water. None of it is secure. A different cleaning agent, a failed lamp, or a slight alteration in drainage could remove every visible trace without affecting the station’s performance in any measurable way.

Until then, however, the Upper Bleedwell is not sterile. Beneath the lower lamp, in water too thin to hold a reflection, cells continue to divide. The station moves water. The films hold still. For now, that is enough.
