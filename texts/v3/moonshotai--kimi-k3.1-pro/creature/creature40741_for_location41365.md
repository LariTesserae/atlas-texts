Coldseep appears uninhabited until the fog comes in.

Then the high growth begins to take water from the air. Beads gather along leaf edges, join, and slide into the upper courts. A translucent membrane slackens over what looked like a window. The basin beneath the seep rises by a fraction. Nothing has entered the borough, but much of it has started to move.

The most established residents crowd around the highest basin. Cupped plants sit directly in the seep’s cold diffusion, their oldest leaves blackened and half submerged, their newest growth folded tightly at the center. Where water lingers against their rims, they add tissue; where it drains away, they harden and darken. In this manner the basin deepens without any part of it having been made to hold water. Some of the cups are no larger than a clenched hand. Others have grown together into broad, uneven cisterns divided by living ribs.

Above them stand the cloud-combing plants. Their spires are gatherings of narrow, flexible shoots rather than trunks, each sheathed in leathery leaves. Fog catches on the smallest projections and runs downward through the leaf axils. Mites live in those wet recesses, almost motionless when the cold is deep, creeping over the films there during a thaw. Between the cloud-combers grow the hollow-stemmed plants. These take the basin’s overflow and carry it through their own tissues or along channels sheltered beneath their leaves. After heavy condensation they swell; in hard cold they contract, narrowing the water’s passage. A runnel can thus be open at its surface and nearly closed a hand’s breadth below.

The apparent columns and arches are equally crowded. A single column may contain the stems of several plants and several generations, the living sheaths wrapped around older cores. Moss and pale filaments bind the surfaces, but binding is not always union. One plant may be drawing water from a fracture in the cliff while its apparent twin is rooted entirely in the compressed vegetation below. Where two masses meet overhead, they press against one another and continue growing. Later, one may shade out the other. The arch remains, although the lives that first formed it have changed.

This is true throughout the older districts. Coldseep’s antiquity is often a succession of occupants rather than the age of any one body. Living roots descend along dead ones. New leaves roof courts whose original canopy has already become part of the floor. A wall may be green on its wet face, wine-dark within, and threaded through with pallid life at its back.

Color records these conditions. The oldest exposed leaves are nearly black, their surfaces thickened against cold and desiccation. In sheltered courts, where the light is weaker but the air less cutting, the growth takes on deep wine tones. Amber appears only at the tips, where tissue is still thin enough for light to pass through it. Whole courts lean subtly toward the same cold side of the sky. The membranes spanning some openings are alive as well, delicate enough to loosen in damp air and draw close at night. Nothing looks through them. Behind most of them there is simply more growth.

The silver-green turf of the lanes is a multitude of minute plants packed so closely that their separate forms disappear. Their wiry stems and water-filled cushions give under pressure, then recover. Along the reliable overflow routes, taller plants cannot readily establish themselves, and so the turf remains low. But a lane stays open only for as long as water and wear favor it. If a channel shifts, shoots advance from the margins, first narrowing the passage and then meeting across it. Years later, the roofed remnant of that lane may still carry a thread of moisture through darkness.

Beneath the visible borough lives its least visible population. Pallid thread-plants branch through the lightless interior, leafless and colorless, occupying the spaces among roots and old remains. Finer filaments enter fallen leaves and broken stems almost as soon as they come to rest. Cold and waterlogging make the work slow. A leaf may remain recognizable long after it has ceased to be part of the canopy, its outline preserved in the black depth while its substance is gradually altered around it.

That depth is not inert. It holds water, air in narrow pockets, dormant propagules, living roots, and the slowly changing bodies of former surfaces. Some softened material is taken up again by new growth. Some is compacted until it can support another layer. The thread-plants are especially abundant where a court has been roofed over and its green inhabitants have begun to fail. They are often the first life in what looks, from within, like an abandoned chamber.

Animal life becomes visible mainly at the basins, and even there only with attention. The translucent crustaceans are small enough that the water seems empty until one of them moves. A dark line within the body betrays its course as it grazes the film along a submerged leaf or basin rim. Its feeding leaves a faint, irregular clearing that soon closes again. When ice forms, the animals retreat into the deepest liquid pockets and beneath overhanging growth. Much of their population persists out of sight, as eggs or scarcely moving young in the organic silt.

The “warmer” basins where they gather are warmer only by Coldseep’s measure: sheltered from the wind, fed steadily by seep water, perhaps a degree less bitter than the rest. Nothing in them swims quickly. There are no clouds of larvae turning the water cloudy, no mass emergence in the brief thaws. A midge may appear above an overflow channel and vanish again. Another may cling to a wet leaf with its wings held close. Their scarcity leaves no hum in the air and no season of flowers dependent upon them.

Most of the borough’s reproduction needs no animal intermediary. Fog brings the conditions for spore release, and a pale dust travels a short distance before settling onto wet tissue. Much of it disappears into the existing films. Here and there, long afterward, a pinpoint of new growth appears. Fragments are more consequential. A piece detached from a basin rim may lodge beside a lower runnel; a propagule carried through a hollow stem may establish itself where two roots have narrowed the flow. If it survives, it begins to alter the very route that delivered it. A dependable channel becomes a thicket. A bridge thickens on one side. A court receives less water and starts, almost imperceptibly, to change its population.

At the lower rim, there is as yet little of this accumulated intricacy. The residents of the fresh frost-slip are small, close to the rock, and exposed to everything. Fast mats spread along the wettest seams, their outer surfaces dull and hard, their inner tissues saturated. In the drier fractures stand tight clusters of shoots, none tall enough to catch much cloud. They live on mineral dust, seepage, and whatever moisture the mats can hold.

Their hold is not secure. A freeze can lift a whole patch; another collapse can remove it entirely. Yet even a short occupation changes the surface. Roots enter fine fractures. Dead outer layers cushion the living growth beneath. Water lingers where it once ran away. If the mat survives a few seasons, other fragments begin to catch in it. If it does not, the raw rock waits again.

Beyond the exposed boundary, the borough has no outlying population to speak of. The last shoots are low and wind-clipped, their leaves often rimed while those a short distance within remain wet and pliable. Spores and fragments may be carried farther, but almost none establish themselves. The abrupt edge is not a line defended by the growth. It is simply where one life after another ceases to be possible.

A hard night reduces nearly every resident to its own manner of waiting. The plants stiffen without splitting, their concentrated sap and flexible stems enduring the rime. Membranes close. Water moves beneath clear skins of ice, when it moves at all. The crustaceans become difficult to find. The mites remain in their axils. Deep in the compressed mat, insulated from the sharpest cold, the thread-plants alter so little that the night scarcely registers.

The thaw does not produce a general awakening so much as a series of local permissions. Ice lifts where water presses beneath it, then settles with a tick. A drop falls. Film softens along one basin rim. A crustacean resumes its grazing. Somewhere a stem bundle creaks as its frozen coat shifts. Fog may bring a little more activity; returning cold may end it an hour later. Even at its liveliest, Coldseep has no common bustle, only many small lives briefly able to continue.

Over longer intervals, the same lives displace one another. Canopy plants take light from the courts below. Roots divert seepage into new fractures. Turf enters a lane whose water has failed. A once-open chamber darkens, its green growth thins, and pallid threads spread through the remains. Nothing maintains the borough in a finished state, and nothing mourns the loss of an earlier arrangement. The survival of a form often matters less than the survival of the conditions that let another form follow.

Very rarely, a solitary dark bird shelters beneath an outer arch. It is suddenly, disproportionately animal: warm-blooded, quick-eyed, able to cross in seconds distances that the other residents require generations to traverse. Its call carries through the packed growth and makes the dripping and ice-ticking seem even smaller afterward. If it presses the turf, the depressions close behind it. If it leaves a feather, pale filaments will eventually find that too. The bird has not discovered a city. It has found a brief shelter in a multitude whose scale is not its own.

To count Coldseep’s residents only by the things that move would be to miss nearly all of them. The count would have to include the cups around the seep, the separate lives within an arch, the turf underfoot, the dormant bodies in the basin silt, and the colorless threads ramifying through walls that appear solid. It is not a single organism, nor an entirely harmonious one. Growth shades growth; roots steal water; burial ends some lives and prepares others.

From above, the borough looks deserted because its inhabitants do not cross its streets or gather in its courts. They are the streets and courts. They are the reservoirs, the membranes, the bridges, and the low green crust bracing itself at the rim: a crowded cold multitude, almost wholly mistaken for architecture.
