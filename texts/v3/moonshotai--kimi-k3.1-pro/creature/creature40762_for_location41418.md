## What Lives in Sere

Sere is inhabited by a few hundred people, by plant lineages and fungal mats that may be older than the rooms around them, and by so few animals that their appearances are remembered like weather. The people keep most of the records, but they do not imagine that this makes them the town’s principal life. They are simply its most mobile form of maintenance.

Visitors often mistake the people of Sere for a naturally patient race. They are not. Patience is imposed on them by hot metal, narrow stairs, sleeping neighbours, slow machinery, and the consequences of spilling anything. A hurried person bruises an elbow, tears a frond, misses a change in an engine’s note, or steps where a drop has made glazed clay slick. Eventually they learn to move with a deliberate economy that looks like serenity. Their arguments, however, can be as swift and bitter as arguments anywhere. They are merely conducted in lower voices.

Their common appearance is acquired rather than inherited: loose woven clothing, thick-soled sandals, hair bound out of the way, cuffs often damp from brushing against leaves or pipes. Fitters carry small crescent burns on their forearms; gardeners’ hands are stained around the nails; people who work near the condensers may go home with wet shoulders and no memory of having touched water. In the engine courts they move in short shifts and drink often. In the planted lanes they linger, because the air there feels almost luxuriously cool.

Nearly every adult can perform a little of the town’s essential work. A cook may also be a scale-reader; a wireworker may keep a balcony garden; a clerk may know how to repack a valve and pollinate food-vine flowers. Specialization exists, but no one is considered fully useful if they cannot distinguish harmless condensation from a new leak, shut off a local line, or recognize the smell of a fungal bed going sour. Children learn these things alongside letters. Many can sleep through the flywheel vibration yet wake when a familiar drip changes its interval.

Addresses in Sere are often directions rather than names: *Above the old kiln, behind the third fern bridge, at the east return*. Public life follows the same routes. There is no place where the whole town gathers, so news travels with those entitled to enter other people’s rooms—water readers, gardeners, repair crews, cooks carrying meals between shifts. A message may cross Sere quickly without ever being announced. Formal decisions take longer. Every affected pipe, root bed, floor, and sleeping household must be considered, and everyone affected seems to possess one additional complication that the plan failed to mention.

Privacy is real, but it is not made primarily of locks. It is made of agreed times, recognizable knocks, and confidence that a visitor will touch only what needs touching. A maintenance worker may have the right to open a panel behind someone’s bed but no right to comment on what is in the bed’s storage niche. A balcony may belong to a household in every domestic sense while remaining part of a public cooling path. The greatest domestic luxury is not space, which no one has in abundance, but an uninterrupted night: no hot-work shift, no inspection knock, no need to wake and move a covered jar because a condensation line has migrated.

This intimacy does not make Sere equal or especially gentle. Old households defend old water rights. People with rooms near reliable condensers enjoy greenery, cooler air, and the status of keeping a productive garden; they also endure public access and persistent damp. Those nearer the dry engine courts may have fewer inspections but harsher sleep and poorer food-growing space. Gardeners can be ruthless about roots. Engineers can order a beloved balcony stripped because a pipe has become inaccessible. A decision made for the whole town may still feel, to the person whose wall is opened, like an injury.

Grudges therefore last. Two households may avoid speaking for years while continuing to maintain the same cold main. This, too, is part of Sere’s patience: not necessarily forgiveness, but continued maintenance.

Water is held less as a possession than as a succession of uses. A household drinks it, cooks with it, washes with it, and returns what it can. To waste condensation is an offence; to prevent a wall from yielding moisture merely because the damp is inconvenient can be a worse one. District ledgers record not only quantities but conditions: a new shine, a dry interval, scale, a root entering a seam, a roof that answered after surface rain. Children grow up with an unusually precise vocabulary for wetness. They have fewer ordinary words for rain.

Food is a less closed cycle than water. The gardens provide vine beans, leaf crops, and other small yields; food fungi are cultivated apart from the structural root mats; grain, salt, oil, and whatever cannot be produced below come down by the lift. Meals are consequently plain more often than abundant. Hand-pollination is real work, done with fine brushes and considerable concentration. Children are trusted with it early, though their first flowers are usually not the most valuable ones. When a pallid moth does the work unasked, gardeners treat the resulting seed as worth marking.

The noon line organizes time without quite becoming a religion. Sere does not stop when sunlight enters. That would waste heat and interrupt work for people who may be nowhere near a visible tile. But clocks can be checked against it, apprentices learn its sequence, and appointments are made for “when the light leaves the kiln tile” or “just after the white stair.” A person cooking beside one of the open wells may feel direct sun cross a hand or shoulder and continue stirring. On some days almost nobody watches. The prohibition on obstructing the line survives because everyone builds as though somebody will.

The rare fall into the eastern lane produces a less restrained response. Its first effect is work: roof-keepers call to one another, gutters are checked, covered vessels are put in place, and the recorders try to establish which surfaces answered first. Only afterward do children come out to get wet. Adults often join them while pretending to inspect something. The water may last only a few minutes, but people will refer for years to a long fall, a muddy fall, or the fall that arrived during a lift repair. Moth appearances and lane-falls supply Sere with some of its most convenient dates.

The gardens are neither wilderness nor ornament. They are populations of tolerated, inherited, and carefully edited lives. A fern may be encouraged until it shades a pipe, then cut back the same afternoon. A vine can outlive three households and still be removed without ceremony if its roots threaten access or open a fracture. Sentiment appears in the cutting taken beforehand. Gardeners exchange fungal mats as well as plants, and an established mat has a reputation: vigorous, salt-tolerant, slow to recover, prone to sourness after heavy feeding. To divide one is to divide a small, living piece of local history.

Nothing falls and rots unattended for long. Gardeners return dead growth to the beds, where the fungal layer does work that surface animals would otherwise perform. A healthy garden is therefore not one in which everything is allowed to grow. It is one in which growth, access, condensation, and decay remain negotiable.

The translucent snails complicate this management. They graze within certain fern beds and refuse, for reasons no one has established, to occupy others with apparently identical moisture, light, and fungal cover. Transfers have usually failed. Gardeners now tend to leave their distribution alone and record it instead. A small mark on a bed’s slate indicates that snails are present; experienced keepers notice when the number visible declines. The animals are not named, exactly, but neither are they quite anonymous. One particularly old snail may be recognized by the clouded edge of its shell and sought beneath leaves after a bed is cleaned.

A pallid moth causes still greater disturbance. No caterpillar has been found, no route of entry has been proved, and no appearance has yet produced an established generation. News of one travels stair by stair, often faster than an official notice. Gardeners want to know which flowers it visits; children want simply to see it; engineers ask again where it could have come from. People who have never shown interest in pollination can describe, decades later, whether a moth’s wings were open or closed when they first saw it. A child who reports one may be questioned for years about details that seemed unimportant at the time.

The draw inspires less excitement because it is always available and never dramatic. Builders accommodate it without agreeing on its cause. Engineers argue from temperature, mineral structure, and the behaviour of water; gardeners consult old diagonal deposits and the direction in which suspended roots have slowly curved. Children amuse themselves by putting beads on abandoned tile and waiting to see which way they will disobey the slope. In ordinary speech, a plan that advances by an unlikely sideways route may be said to “follow the draw.” This is not necessarily a compliment.

Most residents do not believe that Sere is conscious. They are too familiar with blocked pipes, scale, unpleasant neighbours, and unsuccessful repairs to find the idea immediately persuasive. Yet their practical language often treats the town as something with conditions rather than a collection of objects. A wall is *holding*, a bed is *settled*, a pipe is *unhappy*, the upper galleries are *dry in the wrong way*. Whether these are metaphors or diagnoses depends on the speaker.

People do leave. Some want sunlight that lasts more than an hour, a room with no pipe through it, or the freedom to be careless with a door. Others discover that life above ground makes them strangely anxious. Rain falling on an empty road can look to them like failed plumbing. They cover cups without thinking, listen for water behind walls, and wake at uncontrolled dripping. Those who return are not always happier; they are simply once again able to read their surroundings.

On an ordinary night, a water reader may enter a home by appointment, step around a sleeping child, and open the panel behind a kitchen shelf. The cold pipe is wet where it should be. One root has thickened since the last inspection, and she writes down its width. On the balcony, a snail has crossed the length of a black leaf. Somewhere below, a hot-work shift changes; somewhere above, a cloth is lifted from a covered jar so that the next condensate can be added. The reader leaves the wall damp and the household asleep.

The engines cannot decide which root to spare. The gardens cannot remember why a gutter was pitched against its visible slope. The snails and the rare moth have their own concerns, almost entirely unknown. The people of Sere are not quite the town’s masters, and not exactly its soul. They are the part of it that notices.
