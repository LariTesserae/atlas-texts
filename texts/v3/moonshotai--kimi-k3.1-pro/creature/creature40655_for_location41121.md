No one lives in the Ninth Cold Basin continuously. The pump-house watch rooms contain dry clothing, ration heaters, and emergency bunks, but no one mistakes them for homes. Crews descend for shifts, inspections, and repairs; construction gangs remain longer near the eastern face. Eventually all of them go back up. A census taken from the central gantry would record little besides these transient people, a few moving machine lamps, and no visible animals at all.

It would miss almost everything.

The basin’s inhabitants occupy its edges and omissions: the cones of service lamps, the undersides of loose tiles, stagnant branches of pipe, warm seams behind motor housings, and corners that cleaning brushes do not quite reach. Most of the open floor is not so much hostile as repeatedly emptied—scoured, dried, frozen, flooded, or built over. Life persists where those processes fail to coincide perfectly.

The gray-green moss is officially a remediation and assay organism. The crews usually call it **lamp moss**. Close up, it does not look soft. It forms low, granular crusts, gray-green while actively growing and almost white after a long Draw. Metallic dust caught among its filaments gives older patches the staining and weight of soot. Some still have assembly layout marks visible beneath them.

It grows slowly, but it waits well. During the Draw it becomes brittle enough to powder under a glove. When the Weep begins, water darkens it from the edges inward, and for a short time the patches look almost plush. Then the Flush strips grains and fragments away.

That damage is how it travels.

A living patch cannot cross hundreds of metres of cold, unlit deck. Fragments can. They lodge in crawler bristles, wheel grooves, boot treads, and rafts of ice. Nearly all end up on catch screens or in lightless water. A very few reach another lamp. Months or years later, a new stain appears on insulation below a valve light, far from any registered bed. The distribution of the moss therefore says as much about traffic and cleaning routes as it does about where moss can grow.

Its engineered discolorations remain useful. Crews read unusual yellows, rust colors, and chalky margins as evidence of salts in the condensate. Certain patches are left undisturbed not out of affection but because they have become instruments. A crawler may be instructed to avoid one; three bays away, the same organism is vacuumed up as fouling.

The basin’s closest approach to old growth is sometimes no larger than a hand. It may lie behind a valve support where successive crawler models have all made the same slightly imperfect turn. The growth is layered, brittle, and only a few years old. In the Ninth Cold Basin, that can be enough to make it ancient.

Below the lamps, the waterline films form a different kind of habitat. The olive-black threads follow seepage; the velvety margins accumulate where water stands. They are not uniform growth, but shifting mixtures of microorganisms, trapped dust, and organic residue. Near light, some of the film photosynthesizes. In dark pipework it persists on trace material carried by exhaust condensate. Around motor casings, browner films bloom in oily water that remains liquid only while the machinery is warm. When the motor stops, the habitat freezes with it.

During a hard Draw, exposed film can resemble flaking paint. The Weep softens it again. Growth extends by distances too small to see from a walkway: a thread reaches along a gasket, a margin thickens beneath a panel, a previously clean waterline develops a dull velvet edge. This is the basin’s pasture.

The transparent crustaceans graze there. Maintenance crews call them **glassseed**, although they neither resemble seeds nor remain where they are planted. Their bodies are nearly absent from sight: sometimes a gray thread of gut, sometimes the minute repetition of legs. In dark water they vanish entirely. Above a submerged lamp, their shadows appear disproportionately solid, sliding over the plate like debris moved by a current that is not there.

They spend much of their time in stagnant pipe branches and other sheltered water. Open sumps are marginal ground, visited or colonized when flow gives them access. A loose tile may shelter more animals than a kilometre of exposed pan. Lift it during the wrong phase and there appears to be nothing beneath it except black water; return after the Weep and several small shadows detach themselves from the film.

The Flush is both their principal catastrophe and their only reliable means of long-distance dispersal. Some are carried to the catch screens with shredded moss, salt flakes, and sediment. Others lodge in expansion joints, behind ice collars, or in channels not shown on the plans. Days later they may appear in a pan that had previously been sterile. Their arrival is sometimes the first evidence that two pressure zones have acquired a connection.

Consequently, glassseed are reported in maintenance logs less often as wildlife than as hydrology.

The scrubber-crawlers are the basin’s megafauna. Seen across a pan, one is only a work lamp moving more slowly than the mist. Close up, it is brushes, spray, suction, and a patient mechanical vibration, laying down a clean lane whose edges are as distinct as a cut bank.

The crawlers crop the films, crush the moss, and remove the dust in which new growth might establish itself. They also carry life on their wet equipment. Their ecological influence lies not only in what they consume but in the small inaccuracies of their cleaning. A worn brush leaves a crescent. A misaligned wheel rides over an expansion joint. A revised turning radius exposes one corner and neglects another. What an ecologist would call refuges, maintenance calls missed corners.

When a route changes, some of the basin’s oldest films are stripped away in a single pass. Elsewhere, new margins begin to accumulate. Months later, moss appears around a lamp that the revised route now visits. Ecological succession can thus begin with a maintenance order, without anyone involved intending to alter a habitat.

Individual machines acquire reputations. Crews know the unit with the dry, skipping brush note, the one that repeatedly hesitates at a particular grade mark, the one whose suction housing leaves a darker margin than the others. None of this requires the crawlers to be alive. They have ranges, periods of activity, individual histories, and effects on other inhabitants. That is enough to make them part of the ecology.

People, by contrast, are the largest and warmest animals ever to enter the basin, but among its least established. They arrive in pairs or small groups, bringing brief islands of speech, exhaled moisture, food smells, and heat. Their boot-cleaning procedures are good but not absolute. They, too, transport fragments between lamps.

Experienced crews notice life chiefly as evidence. Film at a supposedly dry joint means standing condensate. A new color in lamp moss may indicate a leak. Glassseed in an isolated sump provoke questions about plumbing. An unexpected patch of old growth may reveal that a crawler has been missing the same corner since its route was last revised. This usefulness does not make the growth welcome; it merely means that not all of it is treated as dirt.

For everything living there, the operating phases provide a calendar more important than any cycle above ground.

The Draw is a contraction. Exposed films whiten or freeze. Lamp moss hardens. Open grazing grounds shrink, and animals concentrate in remaining water and sheltered branches. The Weep is the growing time: films soften and spread, moss darkens, and droplets dislodge material from high surfaces. Then the Flush converts the whole basin into a dispersal mechanism. It kills, strands, transports, and occasionally connects. The catch screens receive the closest thing the basin has to a harvest—ceramic dust, salt, oil, moss fragments, black threads of film, and tiny transparent bodies.

Afterward, the low places refill unevenly. Some habitats return almost where they were. Others have been moved several hundred metres or cease to exist.

At the construction face, this process can be watched from its beginning. New plate is not biologically empty for long, but the first change is rarely anything as visible as moss. It may be a dark thread inside a gasket where quench water has stood, or a waterline that dries more slowly than the surrounding plate. Film follows in sheltered corners. Lamp moss appears only where illumination and dust remain stable long enough. Glassseed often arrive suddenly, with no visible intermediate stage, as though switched on by the water.

No final community is waiting to establish itself. Lamps fail, crawlers are rerouted, temporary pools disappear, and new floors are added beyond the old edge. The basin itself is not alive and does not provide for these inhabitants. They persist because it is illuminated in places, wet in others, imperfectly cleaned, imperfectly mapped, and never quite finished.

The Ninth Cold Basin has no wilderness, and nothing in it is native. But it does have inhabitants. Their ecology is thin enough to overlook and persistent enough to alter maintenance practice. It exists neither wholly outside the machine nor according to its design, but in the narrow, changing space between the two—in the machine’s tolerances, and in what those tolerances allow to live.
