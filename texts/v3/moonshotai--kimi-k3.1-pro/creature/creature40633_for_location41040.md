Nothing in the Low Reservoir is native. That does not mean everything is equally foreign.

Some animals still pass through much as they arrived: larvae in intake water, eggs adhering to stock transports, small things carried alive in the wet corners of equipment. Others have become so closely associated with particular structures that their habits would make little sense outside them. There are crustaceans that gather beneath certain lighting fields and not others, crabs that treat expansion joints as both refuge and road, and molluscs whose growth records the changing depth of a factory’s warm discharge. They do not inhabit the Reservoir in general. They inhabit a flange, a thermal boundary, a lee, a recurring vibration.

Apart from its microbes, the Reservoir is too young to have produced a wholly new fauna. Most of its larger animals would be recognisable in the outside sea. What is new is their sorting and behaviour: which species persist, which fail, where they gather, and what they have learned to expect. The station names reflect this. *Lamp krill*, *silvers*, *joint crabs*, *gate dogs*—these are descriptions of occupancy more than taxonomy. “Lamp krill” may mean several kinds of copepod and larval crustacean. A name often says more about where an animal is found than about what it is.

The pioneers were films.

Bacteria and diatoms settled wherever light, nutrients and a little shelter coincided. On open floor their growth remains almost invisible. In a lit expansion joint, however, a diver may find a narrow olive line running straight to the horizon, with slightly richer growth at every bubble hole and chipped edge. Weld seams hold delicate brown patches. The lit faces of roof supports carry thin green grazings, interrupted by the clean trails of snails and amphipods.

These films are easy to overlook and difficult to replace. A current storm strips them; a cleaning crew removes them; a shift in flow starves them. Where they persist, they support the first small residents. Pinhead snails work across painted steel. Amphipods shelter under the lips of joints, emerging to graze and retreating at shadows. Some painted depth marks remain unusually clean because grazing animals prefer the faint roughness of the lettering.

Above the floor, most life is drifting.

Under productive lighting fields, the water can appear faintly dusty. Close up, the dust separates into translucent bodies, dark eyes, trailing antennae, egg sacs. Copepods hang in layers whose depth changes with the working light and the movement of water around the lamp arrays. When the roof dims, some layers loosen; others rise or collect more tightly around suspended equipment. The swarms are not evenly distributed. One lighting district may hold almost sterile water while another, fed by a distant nutrient outflow and a fortunately angled current, becomes a pasture.

The schooling fish find these places.

A large school is often visible first as an alteration in the light. The distant haze darkens. A roof field seems to lose intensity. Then the front of the school arrives: individual fish holding their spacing with remarkable precision, silver sides flashing dully rather than glittering. Pressure movements pass through the body of the school before any visible turn reaches its rear. Around a support column it divides, closes, and continues, leaving no sign that it was ever there.

Some of these fish follow long routes between pump basins. Others recirculate within gyres, feeding and maturing without crossing any charted border. Hatchery-released fish are among them, but so are the descendants of intake arrivals and species nobody remembers introducing. Their abundance is misleading when seen once and easy to doubt when sought deliberately. A survey vessel can cross for days and record an empty sea while passing within a few kilometres of millions of lives.

Spawning gathers around warmth. Eggs and newly hatched larvae collect along the boundaries of discharge plumes, where particles also accumulate. The faint optical shiver of such a boundary may contain a drifting population dense enough to cloud a sampler, while the clearer water above and below holds almost nothing. A load transfer can lift the plume, disperse the eggs, or carry the whole aggregation into a colder return flow. Many spawning events fail. Enough succeed to establish associations between particular fish populations and particular industrial basins.

Some spawning fish add their own sound to the Reservoir. Hydrophone crews initially logged their swim-bladder drumming as a loose valve: a patient, irregular knocking beneath the mechanical note, recurring only where warm water gathered. Its origin became clear during shutdowns, when the machinery fell silent and the knocking continued. Silence, which made the human listeners uneasy, sometimes made the animals audible for the first time.

Squid hunt along the edges of the schools. They use the shadows of trusses, lamp supports and factory hulls, often taking fish that have become separated at a turn. In open water they are brief shapes, easily lost against the pewter background. Around stations they are more deliberate. Their egg strands appear in sheltered cable trays, beneath inspection platforms and along the protected sides of ladders—pale, soft concentrations of life in places otherwise designed to be wiped clean.

Behind them come larger hunters, though never as many as station stories imply. The Reservoir’s size has not made its animals large. Vast water is not vast food. Most of the regular predators are lean, cold-water fish and dogfish, impressive at close range but insignificant once they turn back into open water. Often the only evidence of one is a school contracting around an absence.

The so-called gate dogs have learned the vibrations associated with freight locks and large valves. They do not understand the operation. They know a sequence: distant concussion, displaced water, stunned or disoriented prey, sometimes a pulse of organic material from screens and galleries. Some wait downstream of particular gates for days. When the gate opens elsewhere on the route, they leave without visible disappointment and become difficult to find again.

On the floor, animals have had to solve the absence of mud.

Most bottom-dwelling species cannot use bare concrete. Those that persist are crevice users, clingers and opportunists. Joint crabs settle sideways into expansion gaps, holding themselves against the flow with hooked legs. At slack water they emerge to feed on drifting particles, moulted carapaces and whatever has fallen from richer communities above. During a current storm they disappear so completely that a previously populated floor looks freshly poured.

Eels and eelpouts occupy drainage trenches, cable runs and the outer edges of ballast galleries. The “sump eels” of the deepest provinces include several unrelated animals, some of them not eels at all. Pressure sorts them more strictly than distance does. Species living along the lit roof supports may never reach the deep sumps alive; animals brought up from those sumps often cannot survive the journey. A deep province can therefore contain a small, sparse community almost entirely separate from the water above it.

Down there, feeding is an event rather than a routine. A dead fish, a fall of organic material from an intake, or a carcass caught and then released by a grating may support scavengers for months. Amphipods arrive first, then slower hunters. When the food is gone, the site returns to cold water and bare structure. These animals are not remnants of an abyssal past. They are recent arrivals making use of conditions that happen to resemble one.

Freight locks support richer and less patient gatherings. Crabs assemble not directly beneath the gates but along the lines where eddies deposit food. Eels work the outer trenches. Fish wait just beyond the strongest turbulence. Scraping an intake screen elsewhere can, hours later, produce a brief feast here. Then the lock changes operation and the assembly disperses.

The fixed animals make the operating history of the Reservoir visible.

Mussels, barnacles, tubeworms and other filter feeders colonise selected faces of columns and factory hulls, usually downstream of nutrient-rich flows. Their distribution is sharply asymmetrical: a dense crescent on one side of a support, bare paint on the other. On some columns, horizontal bands of growth mark the former depth of a warm plume. An older band may stand abandoned above a newer one after a factory changes output. Painted warnings still show through most of this growth, but no longer equally well everywhere.

Each colony makes the structure slightly less smooth. It interrupts the flow, traps particles and creates shelters for smaller animals. Algae establish on old shells. Worms occupy the spaces between them. What began as fouling becomes a narrow habitat, and then a source of larvae for other structures. Maintenance is therefore also an ecological event. A scraped column releases food into the water, attracts scavengers, and resets decades—or at this early stage, months or years—of local accumulation.

The managed kelp lines are similarly concentrated. They do not form forests. They are individual ropes and frames holding cropped fronds, with amphipods in their holdfasts and small fish using them as temporary cover. Escaped kelp occasionally establishes on a suitable lit surface, but most such growth remains a tuft or green smear until grazing, cleaning or a storm removes it. The lines matter less as vegetation than as addresses: fixed places around which other lives can gather.

A current storm reveals how thoroughly these lives are arranged around machinery.

After the pressure boom, the lamp krill draw into ribbons. Schools compress and then turn into the strengthening flow. Squid move into the turbulent lees of buttresses, where prey becomes disoriented. Crabs wedge themselves deeper into joints; eels withdraw into conduits; filter feeders close. Loose eggs, larvae and weak swimmers begin journeys that no adult could have chosen.

When the storm ends, the water may carry animals into provinces where they have never been recorded. Most arrivals die or fail to reproduce. A few find an outfall, a lit seam, a sheltered grating. In this way the Reservoir’s communities do not spread continuously. They appear at distant points, skip enormous spaces, and sometimes vanish again.

The great gyres produce the opposite effect. Within them, drifting larvae may hatch, feed, mature and spawn without ever crossing the gyre’s edge. The same species in different basins can consequently develop different average sizes, spawning seasons and associations with stations, without yet being different species in any formal sense. Charts call these circulation basins. The animals are making them biological provinces.

People live in the Reservoir in much the same gathered fashion: densely in small places, hardly at all across most of it.

Freight crews know migrations and seasonal appearances but rarely individual animals. Station crews may know a notched dogfish, a favoured squid ambush or the exact joint where crabs gather after cleaning, while having almost no direct knowledge of the next basin. Control rooms encounter life as sonar masses, blocked screens, fouling estimates and changes in current forecasts. Divers know the shallowest millimetres of it. No one’s version is complete.

Old station hands sleep through valve concussions and wake at unexpected silence. New arrivals do the reverse. Off duty, people watch the water through ordinary windows, often seeing nothing for entire shifts. At night, however, those windows become small lighting fields. Crustaceans gather unevenly beyond the glass. Fish follow. Occasionally a squid stations itself farther out, using the window light to silhouette prey. On one side, someone stands with a cup going cold; on the other, an animal waits with remarkable stillness. Neither has tamed the other, but both have incorporated the light into their routines.

It would be wrong to call the result harmony. Intakes kill. Screens concentrate living things and then crush or remove them. Maintenance strips colonies. Spawning aggregations fail when a plume moves. Introduced animals starve in enormous quantities, and some productive districts are productive mainly because others supply them with losses. The Reservoir is not becoming an untouched sea. It is becoming a managed system with increasingly unmanaged consequences.

Life has also begun to make material of its own. Faecal pellets, shell fragments, egg cases, moulted carapaces and scraps of kelp collect in sheltered trenches, mixed with paint flakes, metal dust and fibres from human equipment. These are the Reservoir’s first sediments. They are thin and easily stirred, but bacteria and small worms already occupy them. In a few low-flow corners, a sounding weight no longer strikes cleanly through to concrete.

Most of the Low Reservoir remains vacant. Any particular patch of open water is still likely to contain nothing visible. Then a school arrives, a lamp field fills with krill, or warm discharge triggers spawning, and for a few hours that patch is more crowded than a coast. Afterwards, the animals leave behind eggs in a cable tray, a new band of shell on steel, a little organic dust in a trench.

None of this yet softens the whole container. It only gives certain places a history.

The water was the youngest part of the construction. Life was younger still. It is the first part that has begun, without further commission, to make more of itself.
