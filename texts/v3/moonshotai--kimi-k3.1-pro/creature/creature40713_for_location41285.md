Nothing living there knows the hollow as a whole. No inhabitant can withdraw far enough to see an arch, and few have eyes that would help. Their world is made of current, contact, chemical traces, and the difference between a surface that can be gripped and one that cannot. A builder’s world is a cup and the water immediately beyond its rim. A crustacean’s is a chain of footholds. The hunter’s is the sheltered edge where open water begins. A larva may know only the central current. They occupy the same small space without sharing a geography.

The builders spend much of their lives appearing dead. Withdrawn, each is a translucent knot folded into the bottom of its chamber, with a darker grain of undigested food visible inside. When the water grows rich, the knot unfolds. Pale branches spread, subdivide, and begin to beat. They do not merely strain the current; they sort it, rejecting some particles, drawing others inward, sometimes closing around a probing appendage until it is taken away. At the first hard touch, crown and body vanish into the cup. Neighboring builders often close a moment later, not because they have been warned, but because living threads run between some of their chambers and carry the disturbance from one body to the next.

A colony can therefore be densely alive while showing almost nothing to the water. During lean pulses, hundreds of cups remain shut. Their inhabitants survive on what little passes through the connecting tissue, or simply diminish, becoming thinner linings within chambers that still look substantial from outside. Growth resumes unevenly. Builders at a sheltered margin bud rapidly; those deeper inside may never feed fully again. Some are slowly partitioned off by their own descendants.

Other chambers hold eggs rather than feeding animals. The larvae that emerge are not small builders. They are swimmers, brief and nearly invisible, and most will never make a cup. Some are eaten in the central water. Some are carried through the throat. Some settle on surfaces where the current will tear them loose or where later growth will bury them. The few that endure undergo a more drastic change than anything the city’s architecture suggests: the swimming body is taken apart, a new mouth and crown are formed, and the animal fastens itself permanently to a place it had only touched for an instant. Its first secretion is clear and almost too slight to catch the bacterial light.

The free-moving crustaceans are several kinds of inhabitant sharing one general transparency. The smallest browse within empty chambers, their legs working over films and particles too fine to see. Larger, flatter animals travel the bridges and ceilings on hooked feet, pausing where edible dust collects in the joints. Some pick delicately around the builders’ crowns; others scavenge whatever the crowns reject. Their darkness is internal: a full gut, a compact liver, a mass of developing eggs. A brooding animal can appear almost solid when it passes before a glowing seam.

Their routes have little to do with up and down. A ceiling is useful if it breaks the current; a vertical wall is convenient if it offers enough irregularities for the feet. When a strong pulse enters, they clamp themselves to the surface and let fragments stream past. When it weakens, they resume at different points along their routes. Nothing keeps a territory for long, but certain sheltered angles are used again and again because eggs lodged there remain when almost everything else is moved.

Molting makes them briefly helpless. The soft animal withdraws into a pore or beneath an empty shell, while its discarded covering drifts a short distance and snags. Almost immediately, smaller animals begin investigating it. One may hide inside the hollow legs; another may scrape the first bacterial growth from its inner surface. Long after it has ceased to be a body, it remains useful as a set of rooms.

A death causes a more concentrated gathering. There is no summons. The first scavenger happens to encounter the leaking chemistry, and its feeding releases more. A second kind arrives to work at the openings; a third takes fragments from the water around them. They do not cooperate and frequently steal from one another. For several pulses, the dead animal may be the richest site in its district. Then only the hard parts remain, already being colonized.

The ribbon worm hunts less conspicuously. It belongs to the undersides of things, where it can extend itself along a seam until its length seems unrelated to its thickness. At rest it is almost an imperfection in the bridge. In calm water its forward end explores angles too narrow for the crustaceans, finding egg cases, recently molted animals, and the injured inhabitants of chambers that otherwise appear empty. A strong current reduces it again to a line pressed flat against the substrate.

The rust-red hunter is broader and, in its own slow way, more disruptive. Its color has no audience. At rest, with its transparent margin spread against pale shell, it resembles an iron stain or a shadow retained in the material. It moves by continuous small changes of that margin, testing the water and the bodies on it. The crustaceans respond before any visible pursuit begins. One presses down and stops the movement of its legs. Its neighbor, feeling the alteration in contact and flow, does the same. Stillness spreads by subtraction until an avenue that had been full of small motions resembles its own dead foundation.

The hunter does not need to outrun the whole avenue. It needs one body within reach. After feeding, it is thicker and darker at the center. It withdraws beneath a bridge or into an abandoned run of chambers and may scarcely move through several changes of the water. It is not a ruler of the hollow. Starved, injured, or caught by the wrong current, it becomes another source of food. Its redness would survive it only briefly.

The central water has a looser population. The pinhead jellies pulse without gaining much control over where they go. Their almost invisible threads catch smaller drifters; after a meal, a single cloudy point appears inside the glass. Some larvae gather in the little eddies around them or pick at the mucus they trail, then scatter when the current tightens or a jelly changes its stroke. Against bridges, where strong water pins the jellies helplessly, crustaceans and browsing larvae take pieces from them. A drifter can become part of a wall’s economy without ever leaving the water voluntarily.

Not all the larvae belong to animals that can live in the hollow as adults. Some arrive from the open sea already committed to forms for which there is no room or food. Their brief lights wander through the plaza and go out. Occasionally, when one is swallowed by a transparent animal, the light persists for a moment inside the eater, so that predator and prey appear to share a body. Other larvae are the hollow’s own exports. They leave carrying nothing of its architecture except whatever microscopic films happen to be on them.

Those films are inhabitants, not scenery. The hair-fine growth on fresh mineral seams draws a meager living from chemical differences in rock and brine. Olive patches spread over settled organic matter and are cropped back almost continuously. In dead cups and roofed chambers, other films work through tissue, eggs that never opened, discarded gut linings, and the soft residue inside old shells. Their cold light accompanies the density of that work. Nothing gathers there because of the glow; things gather because the dead are still being converted into food.

Even a scrap of seemingly clean shell carries cells on its surface. A crustacean crossing from one district to another seeds the route behind it. Microscopic hunters follow the films, and smaller hunters follow the particles released by their feeding. Much of the hollow’s predation takes place below the scale of anything with a body plan.

A census would depend entirely on when it was taken. During a cold pulse, the place would seem nearly abandoned: builders withdrawn, crustaceans wedged into shelter, the red hunter thin and dull, whole lineages present only as eggs, resistant cells, or motionless bodies whose survival could not be determined by looking. A rich inward tide would make the same hollow appear suddenly overpopulated, though many of the feeding animals had merely unfolded, and many of the swimmers would be gone by the next tide.

Nor do the inhabitants experience destruction as a single event. A falling flake is, to the builders beneath it, the end of a body; to the films, an abundance; to surviving crustaceans, a changed route with unfamiliar shelters; to a larva arriving later, simply black stone. Nothing repairs the crushed quarter. Occupation resumes where occupation is possible.

On the underside of one fresh flake, beyond the last faintly glowing chamber, such a larva has now stopped swimming. Its fringe is being drawn back into its body. A clear rim hardens against the rock.

A crustacean crosses the flake, investigates the rim, and finds nothing yet worth taking. For the moment, the new animal has no neighbor of its own kind. If it lives, another cup may eventually touch the first. If it dies, the rim will still offer purchase, shelter, and a surface for film. In the hollow, the difference between founding a city and merely furnishing one can take several generations to become visible.
