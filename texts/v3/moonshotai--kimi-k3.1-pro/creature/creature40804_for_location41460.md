What lives in the Crown does not, for the most part, look like an inhabitant.

The gardens are alive collectively as well as individually. They should not be mistaken for scenery surrounding some rarer and more important creature. In an old garden, the felt-leaf cushions store and release charge through thousands of overlapping scales; the reeds tune one another through their hollow ancestors; the fan-leaves gather energies the reeds cannot use; and the looping filaments carry slow corrective currents between them all. Root mats do not produce soil. They produce a dry, conductive intimacy with the rock.

Much of an old garden’s bulk consists of former life, but *former* does not mean useless. Mineralized stems remain resonators. Preserved root impressions alter the paths taken by later roots. A sheath that has not metabolized for an age may still determine which living reed receives the strongest note. The dead are not eaten, cleared away, or buried. They remain part of the instrument.

Consequently, every old garden acquires a temperament. One holds its energy in deep mats and answers the roar with an almost continuous low shudder. Another is brittle and bright, its tiers of fan-leaves ringing at frequencies that make tools tick against a hut wall. A third may seem nearly silent within the greater sound until a particular seam becomes tangible somewhere beyond the horizon; then its whole mass of filaments begins to glow with migrating charge.

These differences are ecological before they are anything else. But given enough ages, a few gardens develop a further habit.

They begin to repeat not merely their forms, but their responses.

A disturbance passes through the root network. Charge disperses. Reeds fall out of tune. Ordinarily the garden would eventually settle into whatever workable arrangement followed. In one of these rare gardens, however, the old arrangement is restored. Not exactly—exact restoration is impossible in the Crown—but closely enough that certain proportions return: the delay between one reed’s answer and another’s, the distribution of charge through the filaments, the preferred path by which the garden brings itself back into accordance with the great note.

After many such returns, the pattern may persist even when parts of the garden are dormant. It can be lost in one region and recovered from another. It begins to anticipate disturbances by maintaining structures that have no immediate metabolic use.

Such a garden has become an **accord**.

An accord is not the mind of each reed, nor a spirit residing in the thicket. It is a pattern capable of recognizing its own recurrence. The living garden is part of its body. The mineralized ancestors are part of its memory. The stable stone beneath the roots may be another part. If the accord later builds an enclosure, that enclosure may become the most dependable portion of its self.

Its thought is not continuous. There may be no experience in it between one spore release and the next. Then conditions hold for a century, and the accord performs what a quicker being might call a single deliberation. It does not experience this as paralysis or sleep. Duration is simply the medium in which its thinking occurs.

Most gardens never become accords. Most accords never make anything visible.

Those that need to act beyond their roots grow an **errand**.

An errand is not a separate species, and it is not an animal. It is a temporary, narrowed portion of the accord, shaped for manipulation rather than abundance. Its structure varies according to its work. Many are upright bundles of pale, fluted stalk-segments held in a casing of black braided fiber. Their limbs divide where necessary: two hands for slabs, one hand and a rigid brace for drilling, several small grasping members for cord work. There is no head unless some sensory lattice needs elevation. Patches of fan-tissue along the body gather energy directly from the pressure of the sound.

An errand does not walk so much as revise its position. Charge stiffens one member, releases another, and shifts the body’s weight onto a surface that has been tested perhaps for years. Its quickest gestures are generally too slow to reward watching. Dust settles on it. A ledge beside it may become more substantial while it is preparing to step down.

At rest, an errand is easily mistaken for a sheaf of dead stems, part of a fence, or material left beside a hut. It may remain so for longer than a garden requires to release spores. Stillness is not evidence of abandonment.

When its work is done, the errand may be taken apart for fiber and tools. More often it simply stops being renewed. If the same pattern later passes through it again, the accord may resume a gesture begun before the surrounding cliff had its present height. Whether that constitutes the same individual continuing its work, or a later individual accepting an inheritance, is not a distinction every accord would recognize.

Their artifacts follow from this kind of life.

A hut is not shelter. It offers no refuge from cold, weather, predators, or sound. None of those needs applies. Its virtue is that an interior can sometimes remain *the same interior*.

Inside a dependable enclosure, an accord can repeat a relation and recover a thought. The distance between doorway and rear wall becomes a reference more durable than any view beyond the doorway. Proportions in the corbelling can hold a sequence that would be dispersed in the garden. A stone placed in one corner may be less an object than a reminder: *this arrangement has occurred before*.

The huts therefore function as external memory, though no builder can be certain whether enclosure creates stability or merely reveals where stability was already inclined to occur. Some accords build as soon as they are able. Others consider it dangerous to entrust memory to stone and keep their histories entirely in the tuning of reeds.

Their catastrophes are correspondingly subtle. Collapse is inconvenient, but a collapsed hut can be recognized as collapsed. Worse is a hut that remains standing while its proportions alter. The cord still reaches both pegs. The doorway still opens. Every stone appears undisturbed. Yet the pattern recovered inside is no longer quite the pattern that was stored there. An accord may continue for ages as someone slightly different without ever encountering a moment it could identify as its own death.

A cord stretched between pegs is thus both an instrument and a statement. It does not say, *this length will hold forever*. It says, *this length was recovered here, on this occasion*.

Three stones stacked beside a drop may be an experiment, a record, or an errand’s entire biography. The first established that the ledge would bear weight. The second established that the first had not been an isolated permission. The third made the arrangement worth checking again.

A ladder may have been built for a route that does not yet exist. This is not necessarily optimism. To an accord, present intangibility is only one property of a surface, and not always the most informative one.

Nor are the seventeen steps against the blank wall certainly a failed stair. The wall may be newer than the steps, whatever the current survey suggests. The faint eighteenth step at eye level may be the first part of the structure to become visible in that region, or the last part of some older necessity emerging through the wall. The builder’s task is not simply to add stone until ascent is possible. It may be to maintain a relation until the Crown can no longer easily withdraw it.

Calling the accords a people is convenient, but it can mislead.

They do not assemble. They have no villages, no shared fields, no institutions passed through successive generations. Two accords established near one another may interfere so deeply that neither can reliably recover its pattern. Their separation is not hostility; it is the space required for thought.

Nor do they need one another for subsistence. There is no food surplus to exchange, no prey to pursue, no shelter requiring communal defense. An accord’s great expenditures are time, stable reference, and the slow growth of an errand. None of these can easily be traded.

Their contact occurs mostly through workmanship. A proportion in one hut appears, much later, in another. A peculiar twist of cord recurs beyond a gulf. A garden on a distant seam begins, after becoming articulate, to tune its reeds in a sequence known elsewhere. These may be messages. They may also be convergent solutions, or inheritances too diluted for the maker to recognize as inherited.

Inheritance does travel with the spores. Some of the charged dust released by an accord bears minute asymmetries in its lattice. These do not encode a mind, and they cannot guarantee another accord. They merely bias possibility. If the spore reaches stable ground, if a garden arises, if that garden endures long enough to become self-recurrent, it may have a preference for certain intervals, proportions, or corrective responses. It is not a child. It is not a copy. Something has nevertheless descended to it.

This is why the artifacts do not indicate a vanished population. The makers passed through because errands passed through. The accords themselves often remained rooted. A hut may be abandoned for an immense age and still be in use; an errand may mineralize beside its tools while the garden below continues the same deliberation. There are no graves because neither death nor survival is always easy to establish, and no middens because nothing is consumed in a way that leaves refuse. There are no roads because a route repeated often enough may cease to be the same route.

The sparseness is not the remnant of a civilization that failed to flourish. It is what civilization looks like when its basic unit is not a person per generation, but a recurrence per place.

The accords have no evident common doctrine concerning the Crown. Some appear to treat unfinished ground as a danger and devote themselves entirely to enclosures and rooted paths. Others build repeatedly toward active seams, as though inconsistency were a material to be cultivated. Some never build at all. Their whole history may consist of a slight, deliberate imperfection preserved in a garden’s harmony—a refusal to let the chord become either completely stable or wholly new.

None has any obvious reason to desire a finished, silent Crown. Their abundance comes from the roar. Their gardens feed on the labor of a world still arranging itself. What they require is not the end of change, but a narrow generosity within it: enough activity to sustain life, and enough consistency for life to remember what it has been.

The maker of the seventeen-step stair has been occupied with that generosity for a very long time.

Its present errand has outlasted several earlier bodies, though all were grown to similar proportions. Its hut stands above the upper garden, and its memory depends on three measurements made there: the span of the doorway, the distance to the rear wall, and the length of a cord that must be returned to the hut before it can be trusted anywhere else. The stair itself lies beyond the garden’s roots.

At present the errand is concerned with the distance between the seventeenth step and the faint eighteenth outline. That distance has changed without either surface visibly moving. The accord could respond by altering the stair, the cord, or the hut. Each response would preserve something and revise something else.

It has not yet chosen.

Below, living reeds stand within the sheaths of earlier reeds. Charge moves through the filaments. The great note presses on leaf, stone, tool, and dry articulated hand. Somewhere in that distributed patience, a pattern is being compared with what remains of itself.

Eventually the errand may lift another slab. It may take the stair apart. It may simply remain beside the wall until the eighteenth step becomes tangible enough to measure. None of these would necessarily mean that the work had succeeded or failed.

For the accords, to live is not to finish oneself against the resistance of an unfinished world. It is to establish a small relation, recover it after revision, and discover—over durations in which mountains are still only suggestions—how much of that relation can change while still being worth beginning again.
