The human population of Sumpwick is small enough that an absence is felt immediately. A cart driver laid up for a week, an experienced hand away from the drying shed, or a pump keeper taking leave all require adjustments beyond their own households. Most residents have some connection to the works, though not always a single, neatly defined trade. The person keeping stores and records may also know how to free a clogged screen; the carpenter who replaces bridge planks can usually identify a slipping belt by sound. Reed-cutting, pulping, drying, pump tending, carting, cooking, mending, and caring for children overlap at the edges.

Some families have been there long enough for their names to recur in old work records. Others come for a season or a few years and then move on. Sumpwick inspires attachment, but not universally. For some residents it is home; for others it is dependable wages; for a few it is simply too wet, too noisy, and too small. There is no shared performance of loving the place.

Residents are often less troubled by damp than visitors are. Water standing beside a step, moisture on a pipe, or a sleeve darkened by wet leaves does not necessarily signify a problem. What draws attention is a departure from the accustomed condition: a new tide mark, seepage on the wrong side of a wall, water carrying an unfamiliar smell, or vibration in a handrail that was still the day before. People can walk past black, saturated ground without comment and then stop together because one pump has added a faint, irregular note.

Their practical knowledge is similarly particular. A beater hand judges pulp by touch and by the way it drains. Someone working the screens can often tell where material is accumulating from the sound and movement of the water. Experienced residents know which walkway dries first, which gutter must be cleared before a hard rain, and which patch of apparently solid green is actually an unused channel. This knowledge can look instinctive after enough years, but it is taught, corrected, and supplemented by logs, chalk marks, and the careful passing on of exceptions.

A dramatic repair may be remembered and retold, but the deeper reputation belongs to people whose sections simply keep working. Being reliable in Sumpwick usually means noticing something early enough that the response remains modest.

Domestic life follows the same distinctions. Boots are left on slatted trays or outside raised doorways. Tools are wiped and oiled without anyone regarding this as a special ritual. Paper, spare clothing, and anything else injured by moisture are kept high, wrapped, or near a reliably dry room. A cottage may feel humid and still be meticulously maintained. Dry bedding, clear drains, firm steps, and a roof that sheds water properly matter more than unstained brick or an immaculate floor.

In warm weather, people sit on the upper cottage steps after work, mending clothes, playing cards, shelling food for the next meal, or simply talking across the narrow channels. Voices carry easily between buildings, and the works has left nearly everyone with a slightly excessive conversational volume. Privacy depends less on distance than on tact: one may hear a great deal without later admitting to having heard it.

There are ordinary friendships, courtships, old grievances, and disputes over borrowed tools. There are also residents who keep largely to themselves. Sumpwick is too small for complete anonymity, but not so harmonious that everyone wishes to eat together.

Children learn the settlement first as a set of permissions. Certain bridges and store platforms are safe territory; sluice gear, mill races, and the spaces around belts are not. They learn early that duckweed does not make water into ground, and that a plank which looks merely dark may also be slick. Later, if they enter the works, they acquire the more subtle lessons: how to listen through noise rather than merely endure it, and how to tell a familiar inconvenience from the beginning of a failure.

Older residents are valuable partly because they remember previous interventions. They know which willow root lifted a brick, when a culvert was last opened out, why one cable takes an apparently wasteful route, and how long the crack above the pump-house door has remained exactly as it is. Their knowledge is not always comprehensive, and it is not treated as infallible, but it often saves someone from solving an old problem a second time.

The most established living inhabitants, however, are not human. The willows have settled into the works so thoroughly that their roots must be considered alongside masonry and earth whenever a bank is repaired. Residents may be fond of a particular tree and still cut its shoots away from conductors, roofs, or moving parts. A willow can be welcomed as shade and bank reinforcement in the same afternoon that one of its branches is removed.

The reeds live at a quicker tempo. They are crop, obstruction, shelter, and raw material according to where they stand and when they are due to be cut. Their brief openings and rapid return give the outer settlement its seasonal changes. Duckweed, floating ferns, moss, and gutter ferns occupy smaller cycles still: spreading, being cleared where necessary, and reappearing without anyone regarding their return as a defeat. Beneath them, fallen stems and leaves are continually worked into the soft mat underfoot. Even what has died remains part of the settlement’s structure for a while.

Animals remain exceptions rather than a constant presence. The moorhen working the outer reed margin may be spoken of as “the moorhen,” even when no one is certain that it is the same bird from one season to the next. Its disappearance after cutting is noticed. Snails are found on the lower bridge planks often enough that their silver traces are familiar, but an individual snail on a handrail may still be remarked upon. Insects gather around the mill lamps after dark, briefly giving the deep interiors a visible outer halo. A fish breaking the surface would be repeated from one cottage to another. There is no hidden abundance of animal life waiting to make the settlement seem wilder than it is.

The closest thing to a continuous communal presence is the pair of pumps. They need not be loved, personified, or even consciously noticed to become intimate. Their vibration passes through bridge rails; their combined note lies beneath meals, conversations, and sleep. People who have lived beside them for years can remain asleep through their steady running and wake when that running changes. Some who leave Sumpwick miss the sound without expecting to. Others are delighted by their first quiet night elsewhere and never miss it at all.

At night, responsibility for the works does not disappear merely because most of the cottages have gone dark. A lamp remains visible at a deep mill doorway. Water continues at the sluice. Someone pauses on a bridge, not in alarm, but to confirm that the sound underfoot is the expected one. Beyond the immediate roofs and channels, the reed beds are mostly indistinct, and the willows show only where they interrupt a little reflected light.

What lives in Sumpwick is therefore not one close-knit folk with a single character, nor a collection of people and plants arranged in perfect balance. It is an overlap of long residence and temporary employment, human attachment and human boredom, slow trees and quick duckweed, scarce animals and incessant machinery. Its residents share something narrower and more durable than a common mystique: repeated exposure to the same water, growth, noise, and small indications of change. They live among things that persist if tended and things that persist if left alone, and much of their skill lies in knowing which is which.
