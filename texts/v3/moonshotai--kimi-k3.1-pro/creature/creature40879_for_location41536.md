There are people in the basin, though a traveler may walk for days with no better evidence of them than a bowl set upside down to drain or a coil of cord moved from one side of a shelter to the other. They are thinly scattered, and many prefer the groves to the open courts. Even those who welcome company may simply be elsewhere when a shelter is approached. Nothing about the tools guarantees that their users are near.

When encountered, the people are disconcertingly ordinary. They are not clay figures, spirits of workmanship, or the original makers lingering at their tasks. They sweat. Their hair sticks to their necks. They eat fruit, catch fish, sleep in the shade, scratch insect bites, and sometimes wake with stiff shoulders. Some are broad and slow-moving, some slight, some bent by bodies that have always been bent. Their faces differ more than the paving blocks do. Nothing obviously artificial shows in them, and most dislike being examined for seams, fingerprints, or other signs of manufacture. Their aliveness is not a puzzle they feel obliged to help a stranger solve.

The hooked, scarred thumbprint belongs to none of the hands a traveler is likely to meet.

They are not maintaining the basin. It needs no maintenance, and they know this. They have no rounds of inspection, no inherited duties, no hidden labor by which the sharp copings and constant water levels are preserved. A person working at a channel is usually working because the activity absorbs them: the resistance of clay beneath a scraper, the gradual straightening of a line, the possibility that dampness will gather where there was only a groove.

Most of what they make remains merely made. Cord is useful or badly twisted. A bowl stands steady or rocks. A reed mat is comfortable to sit on. Only occasionally does an action pass beyond usefulness. A channel-maker repeats a shallow cut with such patience that water begins to follow the tool. A knotter draws four loops tight and feels, through the fiber, that the tension has gone farther than her hands can account for. When this happens, the people are interested, sometimes pleased, sometimes annoyed, but rarely awed. They do not regard it as communion. It is more like discovering that a familiar joint has taken weight properly.

Skill carries no moral authority. A kind person may be incapable of cutting a true channel; an irritable, vain person may tie knots that hold an entire island. The gifted are not rulers, and the ungifted are not considered unfinished. One shelter-dweller is sought out for company although everything she weaves curls at the edges. Another is avoided despite hands so precise that reeds seem to cut themselves for him. People have reputations, but no chronicles to justify them. Someone is simply known to be generous, difficult, amusing, or unsafe with borrowed tools.

Their memories are likewise real without becoming histories. A resident knows the route through a grove, recognizes a friend’s step on clay, and may remember that a particular traveler talks too much while fishing. These things are known in the way the weight of an adze is known. They do not stand behind the present as datable events. On meeting a visitor again, a person may say, “You borrow things badly,” rather than, “You have come back.”

This does not make them incapable of sequence. Within a task, before and after are perfectly clear: fiber must be softened before it can be twisted; a cord passes under before it passes over; one does not drink from a bowl while still holding it upside down. They can intend, pause, make a mistake, and correct their grip. What they lack is biography. An experience does not become a period of their lives, and a preference does not need an origin.

Nor are they predictable. They can be bored, startled, suspicious, affectionate, or suddenly tired of a conversation. The same person may welcome company at one pool and leave another pool to avoid it. No one is serene merely because nothing is urgent. The heat gives some of them headaches. The sweetness of the white flowers nauseates others. A person can be bored by perfection without possessing any power to improve it.

Sometimes two or three people share a shelter or range of gardens. Sometimes one walks away because the others have become irritating. These associations do not grow into villages. There are no households busy with increase, no stored surplus around which authority could gather. A favorite seat does not become property merely because someone always sits there, though others may leave it empty out of consideration. Hospitality is common but not uniform: water, fruit, shade, and the use of a tool may be offered freely, while questions are refused.

They rest without making a calendar of their rest. No one counts sleeps. If two people mean to meet, they go to the same place and see whether the other is there. Waiting is possible; so are impatience and departure. Nothing makes any of these choices cosmically significant.

The animals are known in much the same way—not as stock, species, or symbols, but as particular company. The people do not own them or breed them. Fishing and gathering are skills, not economies. Nothing is husbanded toward abundance.

A tortoise on one flight of steps will tolerate a certain channel-maker sitting beside it and withdraw from everyone else. The dark, otter-like animal sometimes enters shelters to take loose ends of cord, not apparently for a nest but because it likes swimming with something held in its mouth. It can be playful and still bite when cornered. One of the soft-hoofed browsers permits a person to approach while it drinks, but not while it is eating. Marsh birds learn nothing so simple as human harmlessness: some fishing places are abandoned at the first footstep, while at others a bird will merely move into the reeds and watch.

The residents distinguish these dispositions without supposing that every animal has a hidden purpose. A fish shoal is not a message. A frog is not a knot that has learned to jump. They find such suggestions funny, or tiresome, depending on who makes them.

They are less agreed about the continuing works. Nobody sensible treats the climbing water stair as a person, and the great knot under the reed island is not worshipped. Yet these things have behavior that must be accommodated. A true channel can gather moisture around an idle hand while remaining dry beneath a careless copy of the same gesture. A knot can take up a pull with a responsiveness that seems almost attentive. Some residents call such works alive; others reserve that word for things that feed and can be frightened. The argument has never needed settling. Neither the water nor the knot participates.

No one speaks for the basin as a whole. There are no priests of the white light, no keepers of a first fingerprint, no guardians expecting instructions. Asked who made the distant courts, one person may speculate about many hands and another about a single act repeated properly. A third may say only that the court is difficult to cross in the heat. The scattered tools are not relics unless someone happens to be using one as a tool. The people do not expect the makers to return, and the suggestion that they themselves are waiting for such a return can make them laugh or take offense.

Visitors often interest them most as sources of impractical information. They may ask whether water elsewhere really makes a continuous noise when it falls, whether shade can be cold, or whether darkness has a texture. Not everyone wants to hear the answers. One person listens greedily to descriptions of rain; another finds the idea of a sun crossing the sky oppressive and changes the subject. They do not necessarily envy elsewhere. Curiosity is not homesickness.

At the long water stair, a woman sometimes sits with her feet in the lowest pool, working a groove into the white clay with a stone scraper. She will give a traveler water if there is enough to cup, but she does not like being watched while she works. Once, if the question interests her, she may ask whether cold water truly hurts the teeth.

If told that it does, she laughs.

“I would dislike that,” she says, and bends again to the cut.

Her thumb is unscarred. After a while the groove darkens. A little dampness gathers at its lower end, hesitates there, and begins—without noise, and without making the pool below any less full—to climb.
