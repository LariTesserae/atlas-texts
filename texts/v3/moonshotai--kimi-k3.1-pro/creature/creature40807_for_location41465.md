Nothing in the Grading Pocket keeps it. There is no caretaker in the motor housing, no central nest, and no animal whose experience encompasses the whole chamber. What lives there is a layered, wholly dry fauna fitted so closely to the grader that the machine has become terrain, climate, and season. Its inhabitants divide the pocket according to particle size, vibration, warmth, and access to current. For most of them, the white lamps are incidental. The important distinctions are between a tapping shell and a falling grain, a live terminal and an exhausted one, a stroke of the screens and the brief stillness before it.

Buttonbacks constitute most of the visible life, but they are not a single coordinated crowd. Their apparent collective behaviour comes from hunger, crowding, and thousands of similar responses to the same conditions. They do not consume electricity itself. They crop the energy-rich mineral film that leaking current leaves around the terminals, shaving it away with paired mouthparts and packing the richest ground until their shells overlap. A thriving feeding place is therefore also a place of continual obstruction. Animals climb across feeding animals, are scraped against the wall, lose their footing, or find themselves feeding on the backs of others rather than on the mineral beneath.

They are persistent without being especially prudent. Some continue working a terminal after the film has become too thin to repay the effort. Others wedge themselves into cooling slots or seams from which their next moult will be too large to escape. Wanderers cross open ground where there is neither food nor cover. When a newly renewed patch is discovered, there is no summons and no scout system; the first animal simply begins feeding, others encounter it or follow the disturbance, and within seconds the discovery has become a crush.

The seams between ledges hold the less visible parts of their lives. Animals retreat there to shed, to mate, or to remain still while a new case hardens. Newly shed buttonbacks are paler and less mineral-looking than the old crowded feeders, their flexible tissues close to the surface and their movements uncertain. Even a buttonback is habitat for something smaller. Nearly invisible joint-mites shelter beneath the free edges of its case, taking residue from the flexible tissue and passing from host to host wherever shells touch. A feeding animal crossing a ledge carries a smaller fauna with it.

At intervals, some buttonbacks undergo a different moult and emerge winged. The winged animals are not a separate people arriving in the pocket; they are the brief, reproductive form of the crowd already on the walls. Their flight has no destination beyond the chamber. They rise into the dry air, mate, collide with one another and with the housing, and are battered by the pressure changes around the slot. Many never return to a ledge. Some are crushed against the screens; others fall spent among the coarse ridges. After mating, the translucent wings loosen and are shed whether or not their former owner survives.

A major flight changes more than the appearance of the air. It redistributes bodies and food through the whole pocket. Winged animals that spent their feeding lives on one terminal may fall above another. Wirebacks receive prey without having to surface for it. Scavengers gather beneath the densest flights. Then the wings themselves come down, dry and almost weightless, and settle over everything. The air, usually only a space crossed by dust and shadows, has temporarily been a feeding ground, a mating ground, and a means of dispersal.

The wirebacks inhabit a different version of the pocket. For them, its crowded surfaces are primarily a field of vibrations. An adult buried in a coarse ridge can remain almost indistinguishable from the line of a buried artifact while feeding taps pass through the ground. Its stillness is not watchfulness in any human sense, and its interpretation is not always correct. A dislodged shell or an animal struggling in dust can provoke the same abrupt rush as a dense group of prey. When it commits, however, there is no prolonged pursuit: the upper shell breaks the surface, the narrow legs drive it forward, and the blunt jaws close once.

A successful strike leaves more than a dead buttonback. The recoil of hundreds of nearby animals alters the feeding pattern for several seconds. The sharp smell of crushed tissue draws smaller animals. The emptied case becomes cover, then scavenging ground, then powder. Wirebacks often station themselves near the edge of a returning feeding front, where prey is plentiful and the tapping gives them continual information. During the grader’s heavy stroke, that information is swallowed by the larger violence of the machine. The predators stop wherever they are, some exposed, some half buried, until fine distinctions return to the ground.

Their young live lower in the pocket’s size order. Slender and pale, they hunt among the fine material of the basin and around fallen wings, taking scavengers and other minute animals rather than attempting the armoured crowds on the ledges. As they grow, they move into progressively coarser cover. Not all reach the ridges. A young wireback is both a predator and an acceptable meal for a larger one, particularly when a feeding aggregation has shifted and the buried adults have been left behind.

Below these conspicuous events lives the pocket’s most numerous and least visible fauna. Wing-fretters gather on shed wings, dead cases, and the remains of wireback kills. They are flattening and pulverizing animals rather than agents of decay. Nothing rots here in the wet-world sense. Bodies are dismantled while remaining dry. Soft residues and grey oils are consumed; adhering tissue is scraped away; hard material is cut, worried, and broken again. Much of the shell and wing substance is not food at all. It is simply worked until it becomes small enough to move.

Smaller animals live between the resulting flakes. Some glean body residues from grain surfaces. Others prey on the gleaners. Their crevices are the spaces between particles, and a fresh fall of ceiling dust can bury their entire landscape. The heavy pressure pulse lifts some of them with the dust and deposits them elsewhere, giving animals without wings an irregular, involuntary form of travel. A veil descending from the roof is therefore not entirely mineral. It contains survivors, casualties, eggs, fragments, and hunters too small to distinguish from the material through which they move.

The grader completes this redistribution without participating in it as a living thing. Material too coarse to remain suspended becomes ridges in which wirebacks can hide. Finer flakes spread into the basin and make a different habitat. The smallest fraction escapes through the breather slot. This is not a perfectly closed cycle. Energy continues to arrive through the cable, new mineral dust enters the system, and matter is continually lost as powder. The pocket persists not because everything is recovered, but because production, consumption, breakage, and loss have remained in a workable imbalance.

Changes in current produce the nearest thing the pocket has to slow seasons. A feeding aggregation thins; animals accumulate elsewhere; abandoned ground darkens with new film and waits for wandering buttonbacks to find it. The rest of the fauna responds with delays. Wirebacks may remain buried beside ground that no longer feeds anything, eventually taking smaller prey or one another. Scavengers briefly inherit the remains of the old crowd. Around the motor’s narrow band of warmth, buttonback lives turn over somewhat faster, though the heat also kills animals that crowd too deeply into the housing. In the hollow gauge and other constricted spaces, smaller forms persist beyond the reach of the largest predators. Every apparent migration is less a journey than a gradual change in where lives are being spent.

It would be easy to mistake the whole arrangement for a deliberate symbiosis. It is not. The animals do not maintain the grader. Their bodies obstruct it, their feet help wear its surroundings smooth, and their remains add to the material it must sort. The machine, in turn, shelters none of them intentionally and crushes whatever occupies the wrong place at the wrong part of its stroke. Nor has the pocket become a single organism. It has something like a pulse and something like a metabolism, but no intention, no self-repair, and no memory beyond worn surfaces, current pathways, and the present distribution of animals.

When the heavy stroke comes round again, all these separate lives briefly reveal their dependence on the same event. Winged animals lift ahead of the falling dust. Wirebacks halt. Feeding buttonbacks grip, recoil, or vanish into seams, leaving the ledges strangely exposed. Then the dust settles and the apparent emptiness is filled from its edges. Feeding taps resume, first scattered and then continuous; predators gather along the sounding front; fretters turn toward the newest fallen wings.

Individuals vanish quickly. A ledge may look unchanged after every animal upon it has been replaced. What endures is the close fitting of brief lives to an old machine: not obedience to its rhythm, but thousands of variations taking place inside it. The grader supplies the beat. Everything living in the pocket supplies the deviations.
