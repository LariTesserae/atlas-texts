Nothing that lives there is unfinished.

That is the first correction the basin demands. Its frogs are not crude ancestors of other frogs. Its pale swimmers are not experimenting toward some later shape, and its low trees are not a forest that has failed to begin. Every creature is intricately complete. A mouth fits the thing it opens for; a foot fits the root mat; a parasite fits the precise fold of skin in which it spends its life. The inhabitants have the exactness of long adaptation, though there is no past behind them in which such adaptation could have accumulated. They are complete without having become.

Consider one wet root, though the basin offers no reason to choose it over any other. Amber frogs sit along its upper curve, so close that the expansion of one throat presses gently against a neighbor. They are not basking; there is no sun in which to bask. They are feeding, listening, maintaining the precise wetness of their skins. Translucent mites clean the creases around their eyes. When a frog snatches a winged insect, a charcoal frog below takes the legs that fall. A snail rasps at a scrap of shed skin. Within the root itself, white larvae work at an injury in the bark, and something smaller lives within the larvae. Even the stillness of these animals is several kinds of work.

Their crowding is acoustic as much as bodily. The frogs divide the available intervals of sound. A hollow knock fits into the space after a long trill; a bubbling note runs beneath the wader’s rounded call; a smaller frog uses the narrow silence between two insect wings. No individual knows the arrangement. Remove one voice and there is a gap, briefly. Then another call shifts, or another frog arrives. The chorus is not composed. It is the shape left when every silence usable by a frog has been used.

Beneath the root, the black fish hang in layers, a sediment that has refused to settle. The smallest take what falls from the surface. Others hold themselves among the submerged stems, waiting for larvae to detach from the floating vegetation. The paler swimmers below them are not the same fish grown older and larger. They are another appetite at another depth: long-bodied, small-eyed, their flanks lined with sensory pits. They turn slowly because quickness would waste the information coming to them through the water. A distant footstep, a crustacean crossing stone, the brief struggle of something caught above—all arrive along their sides as distinct pressures.

The comb-billed waders bring those pressures with them. Their bills open into thin, dark laminae for sifting mud, and their long toes close independently around roots, allowing them to grip without tearing through the mat. Fish attend every step. Some dart beneath the foot before it has fully lifted; others wait at the bird’s knees for insects washed free of its legs. The association is exact and without ceremony. The fish know the pressure of a feeding wader, not its face. A bird does not reward them, and they do not follow it out of loyalty. Each step renews the arrangement, and each step exhausts it.

The largest animals of the thickets are the matbacks: low, hairless browsers with broad shoulders and feet that spread into soft lobes beneath their weight. Their folded hides hold water after they leave a pool. Algae green the creases along their backs; minute snails graze there, while thin red worms occupy the warm skin behind their legs. Winged insects rise around them in a loose halo. Frogs sometimes sit between their toes, taking whatever the next footfall misses.

A matback is therefore not simply an animal moving through the basin. It is a mobile portion of the basin’s population.

It crops ferns with a muscular lower lip and pushes through shrubs by leaning rather than forcing. Behind it, fronds lift, stems meet again, and saturated roots settle into their former interlacings. It leaves no trail. Its dung is attended by larvae, worms, and fungal threads almost before it settles. Even a repeatedly visited feeding place does not become easier to reach or richer to feed in. The animal’s many passages never add up to a path.

There are hunters, but none that rule the place. The largest is known chiefly by subtraction: a shallow hush moving through the frogs, fish leaving the shelter of a wader’s legs, a matback stopping with its lip full of greenery. Then, perhaps, a flat wet shoulder appears among the reeds. Its color is not quite leaf-green or stone-black, but the pewter dimness beneath both. Its body compresses between stems without seeming to bend them far enough. It listens for irregularities in a sound that has no score.

When it feeds, death becomes an occasion for mouths.

Almost nothing of the body is returned directly to the ground. Scavengers carry tissue away in themselves. Crustaceans and snails rasp at bone and shell. Fungal threads lace through what remains, and the sweetness of decay draws further crowds. A carcass enriches a gathering, not a patch of earth. Soon there is no skull to mark the event, no stained root, no rank growth where the body lay. The hunter itself will eventually be disassembled with the same thoroughness.

The trees participate in this refusal of residue. Their wood bears no seasonal rings, because there are no seasons to write them. A tree may fall and become, for a while, the busiest object in its pool: a bridge for insects, a surface for moss, a shelter for eggs, a long meal for larvae and fungi. But it does not become a nurse log in the beginning of a newer forest. It becomes substance elsewhere. An individual tree can grow old; old age never becomes old growth.

Birth is equally devoid of arrival. A leaf cup may contain frog eggs, insect larvae, a transparent worm, and a snail small enough to live on the film around them all. Their presence does not foretell a multitude. By the time the eggs hatch, other mouths and other deaths have made the necessary room. Young animals enter fullness rather than vacancy. To be born here is not to appear at the start of something. It is to join something already at maximum intensity.

None of this makes the inhabitants mechanical. A wader can learn caution after nearly being seized. A matback remembers the give of a rotten root and refuses it on a later passage. Calves know their mothers and cry when separated. Young animals play in the shallows, striking at one another and at moving debris with an energy that serves no visible necessity. There are preferences, fears, attachments, satieties, and private miseries. An animal’s experience is real while the animal is having it.

But experience does not readily become inheritance. No nesting pool is cherished because generations used it. No route is easier because ancestors took it. Calls are not ornamented by lineages, and skills do not accumulate into traditions. There are parents and offspring, but no genealogy that matters to the land. There may be old animals, clever animals, even thoughtful animals. Nothing is ancestral.

The pale swimmers in the deeper pools are the easiest to mistake for exceptions. Sometimes two hang side by side beneath the roots, their sensory filaments nearly touching, and remain so while layers of smaller life turn around them. It is tempting to make archivists of them, or patient intelligences waiting for the basin to awaken. Their slowness, however, need not be patience. Patience implies an expected event. If they remember, their memories are private and leave no sediment. If they know one another, that knowledge changes nothing beyond the interval in which they are together.

Only a visitor needs these events to become a story: the fallen tree, the vanished hunter, the calf that survived, the voice that ceased. Within the basin they remain texture. Nothing is diminished by that, and nothing is redeemed by it.

The basin has no history, but it is full of experience—warm eggs held in leaf water, a foot testing a root, hunger satisfied and renewed, small bodies sheltering briefly against large ones, attention flaring and fading. None of it adds up.

All of it is lived.
