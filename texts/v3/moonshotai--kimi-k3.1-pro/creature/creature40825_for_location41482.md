## What Lives in Serein

Serein looks deserted because its population does not distribute itself as a town’s population is expected to. It gathers around events, disappears into surfaces, and spends much of its time in forms too flat, too small, too submerged, or too widely rooted to be recognized as a resident.

A census taken from the moat would miss nearly everything.

### The people who keep the addresses

The permanent human population is small—perhaps a few hundred—and even those people do not necessarily live where they sleep. They belong to addresses before they belong to buildings.

A household’s continuity lies in its obligations. One family keeps a cistern drinkable and its rim accessible. Another maintains a pumping threshold, a market stall, or a stretch of path that must remain passable to people carrying the sick. If growth makes their present rooms unsuitable, they move. If their threshold is regrown elsewhere in the district, they follow it. From their point of view, they have not necessarily changed residence. The address is still being kept.

This makes Serein’s people at once highly mobile and extremely reluctant to abandon responsibilities. They may regard the loss of a house as an administrative inconvenience while remaining sentimentally attached to a bowl, a measuring cord, or some small, useless object carried through twenty removals. Furniture is rarely worth such devotion. It can be grown again, and usually has to be.

They go barefoot when thorns and active unmaking permit it. This is practical rather than ceremonial. A bare foot detects the faint ticking of loom eels, the first sponginess of a path losing coherence, and the difference between ordinary wet mat and mat under tension. Their clothing tends to be loose, light, and perpetually a little damp. Their conversations are full of route corrections: not *where* something is, but how it was approached that morning.

They are not especially dreamy or mystical. Serein requires clerks, cooks, carriers, negotiators, and people willing to clean frog eggs from a cistern screen. Its civic meetings can be tedious and occasionally vicious. No one is permitted to discuss a proposed roof as though it were merely a roof. Somewhere else, enough usable matter must be released; a storehouse may have to stop existing, or a lane may have to be surrendered to white down. The underlying question is always: **What are we prepared to stop keeping?**

The graft custodians are simply the most specialized members of this society. They learn the immune temper of different root beds, the stages of moth settlement, the sounds made by healthy and failing watercourses, and the behavior of the white knots under their filaments of dew. They commonly work in pairs. One tends the graft while the other watches the district, because someone intent on a cistern may not notice that the street behind them has begun to soften.

An apprentice’s first important competence is not making anything grow. It is leaving promptly when the insects and frogs fall silent together.

Children learn the same public signs, along with the more ordinary knowledge of which roofs flex safely, which frogs are all call and no size, and how long edible wall-thread can be carried before it becomes something else’s meal. Forty people in the market constitute a crowd. For most of the day, however, Serein’s human presence is a figure testing a seam, two people arguing beside a cistern, or someone sitting apparently idle while listening to water move beneath the commons.

### The animals that keep the works

The useful animals are not domesticated. They cannot be stationed, commanded, or owned. Serein has merely become a place whose recurring conditions reward them for doing what they already do.

The **loom eels** are the least visible and most consequential. Their silver-black bodies are usually encountered as a momentary turning beneath amber water, one flank flashing while the other remains dark. In the buried channels they are almost continuous motion, compressing themselves through root interstices and worrying open every passage that flow has made worthwhile.

A faint ticking underfoot generally means they are at work. Its absence is ambiguous: there may be no water, no eels, or a sealed channel with something trapped behind it. Custodians pay close attention to which ambiguity is likely.

The eels are not sacred. Serein has simply learned that care around an eel run is easier than reopening a district’s drainage without one. Occasionally a diversion sends too many into the same open pool. Then the water appears to boil with black and silver bodies for several seconds. The eels disappear again, leaving no sign of where so many animals had been.

**Flat cistern frogs** practice the opposite strategy. They remain still enough to be overlooked for hours, their green eyelids resembling small floating leaves. When water levels change, they flatten further into root seams or cistern margins. Their voices are much larger than their bodies. Hollow walls, water chambers, and the spaces beneath paths act as resonators, so a frog may seem to be calling from inside a public hall.

A single call is difficult to locate. Thirty answering calls can make an entire district appear vocal. Cistern keepers sometimes claim to recognize individuals by slight variations in pitch or by the delay before an answer; this may be true, or it may be one of the small consolations of repetitive work.

The **white moths** are treated as precise animals rather than omens. They detect tissue designated for unmaking before an ordinary observer can see any change. One moth means little. A gathering along a roof seam is noticed. A roof furred with waiting wings causes people to check whether a civic notice has failed to reach them.

Their eggs are already in place when the tissue begins to soften. The larvae enter the pale, edible threads almost as soon as those threads become available. For a short time, a condemned wall may contain more animal life than it ever did as a wall.

The **long-legged marsh fowl** follow this abundance. They are deliberate, reserved birds when little is happening, capable of standing alone on a commons until their stillness becomes more conspicuous than movement. Around an active unmaking they become numerous and bold, stepping through the wet debris of softened architecture and extracting larvae with quick, exact strokes.

They appear to understand not only where food will be but when a surface is no longer worth standing on. Visitors sometimes watch the birds instead of the custodians. This is often sensible, though not infallible; a marsh fowl’s idea of an acceptable risk is not necessarily a person’s.

None of these animals is evenly distributed. Each is usually absent until it is briefly overwhelming. Then the eels vanish into the channels, the frogs subside into isolated booming, the moth larvae pass into birds and fungal down, and the fowl disperse across the green intervals.

### The brief populations

Below the scale of birds and moths, Serein becomes much more crowded.

Thin water films carry transparent swimmers that appear after rain and vanish when the films retreat beneath the leaves. Minute grazers inhabit corky wall packing. Dry-winged insects maintain colonies in the silver-green interiors of older buildings, contributing to the high metallic sizzle that visitors often mistake for a single continuous insect. Other small lives specialize in root scars, leaf junctions, frog cavities, or the brief chemical stages between sound tissue and edible thread.

For some of them, a condemned storeroom is not a temporary disturbance. It is an entire world, lasting from egg to adulthood. Its demolition is also the completion of their history.

The decomposers arrive in successions rather than as one white, fungal agent. Some loosen fibres; some sweeten sap; some produce the brief ammoniac sharpness associated with dismantled buildings; some spread the down that can temporarily occupy a whole lane. A few stages are gathered by people for food. Most are left to the larvae, fowl, and smaller gleaners already assembling.

Nothing remains rubbish because it is so quickly somebody’s nursery, outbreak, shelter, or meal. Even absence is usually crowded at this scale.

### The rooted majority

The largest inhabitants are the plants and fungal networks among which all these shorter lives occur.

They are not one being, and their continuity is difficult to measure. A root system may extend beneath several addresses without sharing one immune response. A leaf-bearing lineage may survive repeated unmakings while none of its visible tissue lasts long. A fungal network can be widespread during one transfer and fragmented into unrelated remnants after the next. Some living continuities in Serein may be extremely old, but none can be pointed to as a venerable individual body.

The human residents learn them as tendencies. One root bed accepts structural grafts readily but seals watercourses whenever flow diminishes. Another produces excellent roof tissue and attacks tissue introduced from across the street. Certain mats bear weight predictably for years, then develop defensive growth against their own corridors. These behaviors have chemical and hydraulic causes; they do not require personalities. Nevertheless, no experienced custodian plans as though the plants were passive material.

The white grafting knots do not rule this rooted commonwealth. They allow people to introduce one more temporary relationship into it. Sometimes that relationship holds long enough to become a bridge, a cistern, or an address. Sometimes the local inhabitants reject it before the custodians have finished their work.

Consequently, Serein is governed at the scale on which its other residents can be accommodated: usually one wall, one watercourse, one root bed at a time.

The commons are essential to this accommodation. They are not vacant land awaiting a use. Their low leaves, temporary pools, insect emergences, and hunting birds depend on the fact that nothing there is continually being asked to become a stair, a vault, or somebody’s roof. They provide room for animals to gather, for water to change its mind, and for growth to fail without taking an inhabited district with it.

The open spaces are among the few conditions in Serein that nearly every kind of resident can use.

### Occupancy

When a district falls silent for a major graft, the human residents gather their portable possessions and leave. Often the fowl have already withdrawn and the frogs have stopped answering. For a while, the visible population approaches zero.

It does not remain there. Moths find the first designated tissue. Fungi enter it. Larvae and smaller grazers follow. Eels arrive with new seepage. Frogs occupy the new hollows and discover what their voices sound like there. People return to check the address, correct the route, and resume arguing about what has been gained and what released.

By evening, the market may have folded and a single marsh fowl may again be standing in an apparently empty commons. The eels tick beneath it. A frog calls from somewhere that sounds like a wall. A condemned storehouse is passing through a hundred small bodies, while the rooted majority continues several disagreements too slow for any visitor to follow.

Serein is not sparsely inhabited. It is inhabited at different speeds, and most of its population is between visible events.
