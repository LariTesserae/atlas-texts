The people are only the part of the Root-Ward that makes maps.

The root itself is the largest inhabitant. It is present in every meal, every narrowing doorway, and every plan for the future, but absent from every agreement. The warders study it with an attention that can resemble worship until someone proposes cutting too deeply; then it becomes plain that they are studying weather. It can be used, endured, and misunderstood. It cannot be persuaded to care.

Most of the other inhabitants live by more immediate appetites. The first pasture is a film too thin to see, spread over wet tissue and the surfaces of cords. Springtails graze it, mites work through it, and transparent worms strain whatever the roof-drips bring down. Beetles take sap and tender growth; snails take the softer accumulations along valves and seams. Hunters follow all of them. What the hunters leave, the root hairs find.

The warders read this multitude as carefully as they read the walls. Springtails gathering above a curb mean moisture has shifted there. Mites deserting a twist of cord may mean the cord has begun to sour from within. A file of beetles turning back before it reaches a growth ring is worth investigating: something has changed in the sap, or something is waiting around the bend. No animal is simply useful or simply a pest. A beetle on old callus is a cleaner; the same beetle on the tender ring must be removed. A ceiling hunter above a beetle run is tolerated. One establishing itself over a sleeping recess is not.

The gutter-eels are the only inhabitants the warders consistently describe with fear, though even then their stories tend toward argument. Few people have seen a large eel whole. They know individuals by a scratched gate, a missing length of cord, or a particular disturbance passing beneath a bridge. An eel can close a cross-passage without appearing in it: someone feels the water go still, someone else touches the warning cord, and work ceases until the gutter resumes its small sounds. The oldest eels are credited with remembering sluice schedules. Whether they truly do is less important than the fact that the schedules are now varied.

The many-legged hunters of the ceiling are treated with less ceremony. They are dislodged from cisterns and food recesses, left alone elsewhere, and never handled without cause. Their absence is more worrying than their presence. A roof suddenly empty of predators usually means the grazers have gone too.

Against this multitude, the nineteen households are conspicuously deliberate.

At a gray inspection, the warders can be seen to be neither luminous nor ghostly. They are a closely wrapped people with cold-reddened knuckles, dark eyes watering in the light, and hair braided or clipped so that it cannot trail through sap. Their clothing is stitched close to the body; loose hems would brush walls, catch on rootlets, and carry water where water is not wanted. Their feet are padded and rebound before the old wrapping wears through. Their hands are often scarred across the fingertips from work that had to be done by touch.

Their sight is ordinary. It is no use in the year’s deeper darkness. They know the Ward through the grain beneath a thumb, the tension of a guide cord, the smell of a neighboring house’s food, and the particular note a cistern makes when its level changes. They recognize one another by gait and by the small noises of their work. Home is not merely the place where they sleep. It is the place they can cross without reaching for a cord.

Nothing about them is especially ancient. The oldest residents remember the first split and the first season of building. Every rule is recent enough that somebody can recall the accident, argument, or piece of good luck behind it. Households have had time to develop pride and grudges, but not dynasties. Some are formed mainly by blood; others are work parties that have acquired children, elders, apprentices, and people who left another house after a quarrel.

Their names are practical and therefore often wrong. Low Lintel may have a high lintel after a shift. Backflow House may stand beside water that has not reversed for two thaws. Three Drips now has five, and its inhabitants refuse to change the name. A household’s name is one of the few things the root cannot easily alter.

There is no chief of the Ward. There is, however, an order of gates, and everyone learns it. There are also people whose judgment is sought about particular things: a pore-keeper before a drinking seam is cleaned, a bridge crew before cords are moved, the houses downstream before a new cut is made into a vein. Surveyors are respected without being entirely trusted. Each household keeps its own knotted map and tends to regard the others as incomplete, outdated, or optimistic.

A map is therefore both geography and argument. Some knots record distance; some record the width of a threshold; some mean that a passage existed during the last survey but should not be relied upon now. After a pressure pulse, households compare cords in the central oval. The disputes can be lengthy and extremely quiet.

The oval itself is not sacred. It is uncommitted. Cords are spread there, households sleep there temporarily when their own rooms begin to close, and children learn the gate order on its naked floor. No one mistakes its emptiness for wasted space. It is the Ward’s shared admission that the present arrangement of lanes may not survive the next growing cycle.

Children learn three things before they learn a trade: how to stop moving when the water stops, how to tie and untie in darkness, and how to read a wall without injuring it. Wall-reading is partly temperature, partly smell, and partly the slight give beneath a fingernail. Pale sharpness means a new split; callus has a different resilience; a wall that appears firm but transmits a fine, continuous tremor may already be divided by a rootlet on the other side.

Later, a child may become particularly skilled at clearing pores, culling beetles, folding cistern tissue, making cord, or tending the midden-lock. Specialization never excuses anyone from pulse work. When the far seal tightens and the walls draw inward, status matters less than whether a person is standing in the right place. Bridges are lifted, gates exchanged, cords retied, and vulnerable household goods passed hand to hand. Afterward comes the slower labor of discovering what the pulse has changed.

Their food is cold, usually soft, and seldom abundant enough to be careless with. There is sap taken where the root already weeps, scrapings from tissue that must be cut back anyway, pastes made from the smaller grazers, and brief plenty when the winged things hatch. Eel is rare enough to be feast food and troublesome enough that no sensible household plans around obtaining it. Storage is a continual contest with condensation and root hairs. Containers are inspected nearly as often as their contents. Bone, shell, and well-kept cord commonly outlast the rooms in which they are used.

Quiet is a discipline, not a temperament. Children have to be taught it and sometimes fail spectacularly. People argue about gate duty, hoard good sinew, make wagers on which doorway will narrow next, and tell increasingly implausible eel stories. Affection is often expressed through maintenance: a wrapping repaired before its owner notices the hole, a house cord replaced without being asked, a share of food set aside for someone working at the far seal.

Their sense of beauty is largely tactile and temporary. A braid may have a pattern meant to be read by thumb. A threshold can be shaped so that it guides the foot without catching it. Some households adjust a drip-cup until the cistern makes a sound they like. Fixed ornament is foolishness unless one is willing to watch the root grow through it. The most admired work is often work whose quality can be recognized in complete darkness.

When the outer plug is opened, sight becomes briefly important again. The gray inspections are practical audits: new growth is examined, maps are checked against the actual lanes, and unreported narrowings become difficult to deny. But they are also occasions for looking at one another. Faces seem unexpectedly expressive after a year of voices and touch. Some warders wait eagerly for the light; others find it painful and are relieved when the plug closes. Children born inside have been known to speak of the outside as if it were merely an occasional condition of the plug, which amuses some founders and troubles others.

No one can speak for the root. There are people who know particular parts of it extremely well, and people who claim more than that. Some warders love the shelter it gives; some hate living inside an appetite; most hold both attitudes without supposing the root is affected by either. A worker may murmur to it while making a delicate cut, not from expectation of an answer but because speech helps keep the hand slow.

The nearest thing to a shared moral rule is simple: leave a way around. Do not make a repair that traps the neighboring house. Do not rely on one passage, one food source, or one person’s memory. Prefer a knot that can be undone and a cut that can heal. The rule is frequently broken. It is also applied to conduct, so that a person who corners another in an argument may be accused of building across the lane.

The dead go to the midden-lock with the other matter the Ward cannot keep. This is not thought to make their lives less their own. Their tools return to the households; their knowledge is carried on where anyone found it worth learning. Memorial customs are still new and vary. One house speaks its names while the gutter is running. Another keeps a cord separate from the survey maps. Others attach a dead person’s name to a technique—a bridge knot, a way of cleaning a valve, a fold that keeps a cistern from sweating into the sleeping room. Being absorbed by the root is not confused with being remembered.

At the next pressure pulse, the springtails often climb before anyone speaks. Beetles unhook themselves. The gutter lifts and hesitates; somewhere beneath it, an eel turns into a side pore. Nineteen households reach for cords already checked and checked again. For a short while the Ward is intensely active, each creature responding to its own small portion of the same change.

Then the water settles. A drop strikes the cistern. A gate closes with its familiar suction. Someone whispers that a threshold has narrowed, and someone else insists it was always narrow. The map cord passes from hand to hand while, overhead, a new rootlet enters the dark.
