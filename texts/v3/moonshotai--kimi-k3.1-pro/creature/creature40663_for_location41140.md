No settled people remain in the High Catchment, and nothing has inherited the work of its builders. There are no keepers passing from cistern to cistern, no households concealed behind the blank walls, and no animals numerous enough to make the streets feel occupied. Yet the plateau is not merely empty space between rains. It has a sparse, highly particular population of its own.

Its residents occupy conditions rather than territories. Their map is not one of districts and lanes, but of surfaces that dry within an hour, joints that remain damp through summer, basins that fill gently, and pools that last just long enough for a life to be completed. Two cisterns separated by a single wall may be entirely different countries. A dry stairway can be a stronger barrier than a cliff.

To live there permanently, a thing must be good at one of three things: clinging, closing, or hurrying.

The most widespread life is also the least visible. Where water runs often but not violently, a thin olive-brown film forms on submerged stone and along the lower edges of overflows. It is made up of microscopic algae and other organisms living together in a layer sometimes no thicker than a sheen. Hard spouts and flood-scoured channels are bare; quieter corners carry enough film to cloud a fingertip. It draws nourishment from light, dissolved minerals, windblown dust, and the small amounts of organic matter washed from roofs and courts. Much of it can survive drying. During a rainless interval it becomes almost indistinguishable from mineral stain, then resumes activity when water returns. Nearly all the small animal life of the Catchment ultimately depends on these films.

The lichens live at a still slower pace. Their scattered patches are not simply decorations on exposed walls: each marks a particular balance of wetting and drying. Some occupy faces that receive fine rain but little direct runoff. Others cling beneath slight overhangs where condensation gathers. When wetted, they darken and become briefly active; when the wind returns, they stiffen and wait again. An old patch may have spent far more of its existence dormant than awake. Their edges gather minute quantities of dust, and their deaths leave equally minute amounts of organic residue. This is rarely enough to make soil, but it can give a grass seed one additional season in which to establish.

The low shrubs are the largest permanent residents. They occur chiefly where a wall breaks the wind and a deep joint supplies seepage without keeping the roots submerged. A shrub no higher than a knee may be very old, its stem thickened by many brief growing seasons and its crown repeatedly reduced by frost or drought. Its hard leaves lose little water, and it does not necessarily flower every year. Seedlings are commoner than young shrubs: most germinate in a favorable spring and vanish when the joint dries. The survivors tend to occupy their refuges for a long time. Their roots do not so much break the masonry apart as follow openings already made by weather, helping to bind loose rubble while leaving sound walls largely untouched.

The silted courts support a quicker kind of life. Their dust contains the seeds of small annual plants, but the seeds do not all respond to the same rain. Some require cold before they can germinate; some need repeated wetting; some remain dormant through several promising seasons. This staggered response protects them from a single shower followed by drought. When conditions are right, a court can produce scattered rosettes and fine stems within days. The flowers are small, usually pale, and often fertilize themselves rather than depending on scarce pollinators. Seed is set quickly. By the time the surrounding paving has dried, the plants may already be brittle stems, with the next generation lodged in cracks or buried under a fresh skin of silt. One court can green while an adjoining one, apparently identical, remains bare.

Grasses follow the same uncertain fortunes in the paving joints. Rushes are less itinerant. They persist only where the water supply is dependable enough to carry their rhizomes through the dry season, and even there their margins advance and retreat. A flood may tear out one stand, carry a living fragment into a lower basin, and leave another stand buried in mineral sediment. None of this leads toward a thicker plant cover. Soil is washed away or blown out almost as readily as it forms.

The visible animals are specialists in these narrow opportunities. The snail found beneath a channel lip is likely to belong to that channel alone. It grazes the film while the stone is wet, then seals itself to the underside of the lip with a thin dried membrane. When rain returns, it resumes feeding almost where it stopped. Its shell records irregular periods of growth. Eggs are laid deeper in the joint, beyond the reach of ordinary overflow. A whole population may occupy a few paces of wall while the next channel, drier by only a small margin, remains without snails. Smaller still are wingless joint-dwelling arthropods that feed on films, spores, and fragments of dead plants. They appear as scattered moving specks in damp dust, never as swarms.

The dry sediment of the meres holds another population that cannot be seen at all: the eggs and cysts of tiny crustaceans and other short-lived pool animals. Not every filling wakes them. Some hatch only after prolonged inundation, and some remain dormant even then. When a pool persists, however, the water near the bottom becomes faintly animated. Translucent bodies move through the shallows, grazing suspended particles and the films on submerged stone. The animals mature rapidly and leave behind another generation capable of surviving the pool’s disappearance. This is why a mere that looks sterile from its mineral shore can, after several suitable weeks, repay the visit of a wading bird.

Among the largest animals that can fairly be called residents is the black cistern newt. It is no longer than a hand, silent, slow in cold water, and so dark that its movement is often noticed before its body. Not every cistern suits it. It needs a submerged refuge, a seep that outlasts ordinary dry spells, and an inlet gentle enough not to wash away its eggs or young. The adults can withdraw deep into fissures; the young remain aquatic, sometimes through more than one cold season. Breeding is irregular, and a newt may occupy one covered reservoir for years without appearing in any of the neighboring pools.

During long spells of mild rain, the adults occasionally travel. They move along wet joints and shaded seepage rather than across exposed paving, taking advantage of the brief period when the city’s separate damp places become connected. Such journeys are rare enough that colonization is largely a matter of chance. This explains the otherwise puzzling distribution: one cistern contains a persistent lineage while dozens of equally deep, equally cold cisterns remain empty. There are no choruses from the pools at night. Even where the newts are present, the usual evidence is only a disturbance beneath an overflow or a shape withdrawing from the edge of the light.

The birds belong less completely to the place. Waders arrive from elsewhere when the meres offer food, work the narrow belts of silt, and depart before the margins close in. Their importance is greater than their numbers suggest. Seeds and dormant eggs can travel on their feet and feathers, linking basins that runoff does not connect; their droppings add small, localized enrichments of nutrients. The raptor at the escarpment is still less a resident. The rim provides rising air, not a living. It passes over the Catchment and hunts beyond it.

There are consequently none of the busy commensal animals that often make ruins seem tenanted, and no substantial scavenging community. A dead creature is an isolated event, not a dependable food supply. Most small bodies dry, are washed into sediment, or return slowly to the films and joint-dwellers.

Nothing in this assemblage maintains the city, but everything is shaped by its grades. A gentle overflow feeds a film; a harder one strips it. Calcite seals one snail’s refuge and creates a ledge beneath which another can settle. A collapsed wall catches dust for a shrub, while the next flood removes the dust and leaves the roots exposed. Life stabilizes small things without ever gaining enough ground to transform the whole.

A census taken at dry noon would therefore be misleading. It might record lichens, shrubs, a snail beneath a lip, and little else. After several days of rain, the same ground could contain germinating annuals, hatching pool animals, active grazers, and a newt moving between refuges. Weeks later, nearly all of that would again be invisible.

Much of the High Catchment’s population survives as seed, egg, spore, dry film, or a closed body in a seam. Its inhabitants live less in the city’s rooms than in its intervals: an hour of activity on a wet wall, a month in a silted court, a cold persistence in one deep cistern. The birds can leave. Almost everything else has learned to wait.
