What lives there is difficult to count, because the upland does not consistently honor the boundary between an individual and an organ.

There are no animals concealed by the vegetation, no buried kindgom awaiting discovery, no small quick lives masked by the roar. The things that drift through the water and air are spores, plant hairs, and torn fragments of living tissue. Yet “plants” is too easy a word for the inhabitants. Roof-scale, vault-column, pale receiver, stone-root: these are useful names, provided they are not mistaken for permanent kinds. A life may pass from one condition to another. A tissue that appears to be a separate organism may be an outlying organ of growth several chambers away.

The black-green scales are simply the first lives an eye would meet. Each is roof, cup, reservoir, and light-trap. Their darkness is an appetite: very little useful illumination is allowed to escape them. While a scale bends beneath accumulated water, its inner membranes maintain arrangements far more delicate than its visible motion suggests. Energy passes through ordered living lattices with an exactness that seems impossible in something so wet, cold, and continuously disturbed.

The life of a scale therefore moves between two extremes of scale. Within it, energy is conserved through almost impossibly subtle states. Around it, tonnes of water are admitted, held, and released. When a basin reaches capacity, living ribs alter their tension. The scale tilts or opens along a seam, and its contents fall into the waiting structure below. The concussion is not the sound of injury. It is part of the leaf’s ordinary existence.

Nor does a scale necessarily belong for all its life to the growth that produced it. A torn margin may be enclosed by a neighboring column. Its vessels may be joined to new capillaries. A leaf raised in one vault can end its green existence as part of the roof of another, still photosynthesizing, still storing rain, but nourished through connections that did not exist when it unfolded.

The vault-columns beneath are equally reluctant to remain individual. They have no heartwood in which age could accumulate, no patient record of rings. Their capillary bundles are continually added to, sealed, reopened, and repurposed. A strand carrying water upward may later become storage; a storage strand may collapse and be reclaimed; a new bundle may grow through the space left behind. Where columns meet, their tissues sometimes fuse, so that a load is carried not by a trunk but by an agreement among many trunks.

To ask the age of such a column is to ask which part of it one means. Its youngest capillary may have formed during the last storm. Some deeper connection may have persisted through repeated enclosure and renewal. Nothing is preserved merely because it is old. Nothing is discarded merely because it has stopped serving its original purpose.

Below the green growth, life loses color.

Translucent fronds and pale sheets occupy the regions where light has ceased to be useful. Some line reservoirs. Some hang from the living ceilings of root chambers. Others extend through the saturated felt in networks too fine to follow without tearing the very connections being examined. They receive material from above—energy-rich tissue, dissolved constituents, fragments already being prepared for reuse—and return water, sorted matter, and new growth.

They are not an understory in the ordinary sense, and they are not a kingdom of rot. The upland has no undertakers. Material is often being reclaimed before it could reasonably be called remains. An injured surface is opened by its neighbors; its contents enter other vessels; its structural elements are separated and taken elsewhere. The green world above and the pale world below may, in many places, be the same body encountered at different distances from light.

At the mineral frontier, the inhabitants become harder still to distinguish from their surroundings.

A stone-root does not spend long as a tip at any one point. It advances by repeated acts of examination and transformation. Its membranes sample the water films against the rock. Constituents are admitted, rejected, concentrated, or carried away. Behind the growing front, silicate order is established and stress is redistributed. Living conduits may remain within the new structure for a time, surrounded by mineral that they helped to organize.

That mineral is neither shell nor skeleton. It is not waste, and it is not a foundation prepared for some later inhabitant. It is the portion of an act of growth that no longer needs to remain cellular.

Some roots branch toward fractures and occupy them while the fractures are still only lines of stress. Others withdraw, leaving ordered planes and altered pathways for water. Still others are cut by uplift and continue from both ends, their former connection replaced by new mineral. Eventually many are lifted by the very mass accumulating around and beneath them. A root can thus spend its existence descending toward stone and end higher above the basins than it began.

The closest approaches to separate, adventurous lives are the spores and fragments.

A spore no larger than a fog particle cannot swim or choose its destination. It rises, circles, settles, or is carried down through the vegetation according to movements of air and water. But its smallness conceals an unusual freedom of outcome. Lodged in dim light, it may produce green scale tissue. Established against a reservoir wall, it may become pale receiving tissue. Held against raw mineral, it may put forth a root before producing anything that resembles a leaf.

A living fragment can do much the same. Carried passively through the upland’s drainage, it may lodge in an injury and become repair, or settle at a margin and become reproduction. It may remain distinct; it may fuse with the first compatible tissue it touches. There is no seed bank waiting in the ground, because there is no ground in which to keep one. The future is maintained wet, active, and capable of changing form.

This is why classification would be so difficult even if the growth could be made to stand still. Some apparent species may be organs. Some apparent organs may be descended from organisms that were once independent. A boundary visible in one generation may vanish in the next, while a new boundary opens where a fragment has been isolated by storm or fracture.

Even death leaves little that could be used to reconstruct a biography. There are no shells, no bones, no accumulated litter, no ancient trunks standing after their living surfaces have gone. A tissue ceases to maintain itself; neighboring growth opens it; its substance enters other lives. What persists is not usually a body but an alteration: a different route taken by water, a thickened place in a vault, an ordered mineral plane, a tendency for the next growth to close a gap in one direction rather than another.

And yet the inhabitants are not merely dissolving into one another.

At certain scales, their separateness becomes almost irrelevant. As storm charge gathers, fine hairs lift across immense tracts. Stomata alter their state in coordinated fronts. Reservoirs change the thresholds at which they release. Roots modify what they draw from the water. No chemical messenger could have visited every participating surface. Something coherent is maintained among them, not as a command sent from a center, but as a shared condition of readiness.

Then vapor rises from one province, cloud forms above another, and rain returns days later along a route determined partly by growth that occurred before the storm began. The event cannot be neatly assigned to either physiology or weather. It is leaf behavior, root behavior, atmospheric circulation, and development, unfolding as one extended response.

The upland’s memory has the same distributed character. It is not an archive of storms. A healed injury conducts pressure differently. A new stone plane carries uplift differently. A thickened region stores and releases vapor differently. Each disturbance leaves the living body more particular, and therefore changes what the next disturbance can do.

If there is one great inhabitant, it is that continuity of response. It has no hidden chamber, no central anatomy, no voice beneath the hydraulic roaring. Calling it a mind would attribute intentions it never displays. Calling the mat a population of separate plants would miss the fact that a single event can belong simultaneously to canopy, root, mineral growth, and weather. The continuity is not a creature concealed in the upland. It is what keeps the countless visible lives from becoming merely countless.

At the rim, the whole manner of existence can briefly be seen at nearly individual scale.

A spore adheres to a wet mineral face. It extends a colorless filament and a narrow green film. It does not wait for soil. It delays a little water, draws one dissolved constituent inward, and leaves another in a more orderly configuration. A second filament follows the first. Above them, a minute scale holds back its first drop.

For a short time, this is a pioneer: tiny, bounded, and apparently alone. Then a root cable from higher growth reaches it. Surfaces meet. Vessels join. The pioneer’s boundary becomes difficult to find, and its life continues as part of the advancing edge of something whose farther provinces lie beyond cloud and weather.

Nothing there is merely residing on finished land. Nothing is waiting for rivers to make the country habitable. Its inhabitants are green where light reaches them, pale where it does not, fibrous where water must be carried, and mineral where flexibility is no longer required.

Nothing merely lives on the upland. The upland is what its life looks like from far away.
