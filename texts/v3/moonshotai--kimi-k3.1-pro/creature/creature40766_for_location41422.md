## Life in the Vey Crown

There are no people in the Vey Crown in any ordinary sense, and nothing makes a home there as a burrow, nest, or shelter. Even the largest animals keep close to the stone. Yet the Crown is inhabited almost to excess. Its population simply occupies dimensions a standing observer is poorly equipped to notice: downward, laterally, and in time.

Most of its bodies are sheets, threads, flattened sacs, or growths that can fold back into the ground. Even the exceptions tend to become one of these when conditions worsen.

### The living floor

The vegetation is not background. It is habitat, food, reservoir, and communication medium, and in many places it is difficult to say where one organism ends and another begins.

The wine-black fans are the most conspicuous leaves, though few rise higher than a hand’s breadth. Each fan is a succession of tough, overlapping surfaces, new ones forming beneath the old. Their axils hold water when water can be had. In drought they close so tightly that a whole colony may feel like mineral crust; after a charge pulse, they loosen and become briefly supple. The younger tissue within is sweetish, saline, and rich enough to be worth eating.

The filament leaves do different work. Some stand only a little above the fans, but each divides again and again until its finest branches can comb moisture from air too thin and cold to wet stone. Others lie flat against the rock, following temperature and potential through a fracture. Their minute openings serve as valves for both brine and current. A patch may contain thousands of these leaves and still be, in some meaningful sense, one organism.

The red-threaded cushions are older and slower. Their surface growth is modest, but beneath it they form dense nodules where living root, salt, and wet mineral meet. These nodules store charge and water in different proportions according to the habits of the basin. The oldest are irregularly layered, not by seasons but by storms: a dark deposition after one great discharge, a pale mineral rind after years of drought, a sudden thickening where a root network survived a slide. Cut open, one would resemble a biography written without dates.

Between pulses, much of this life is scarcely alive by any quick measure. Sap stops moving. Leaves become brittle. Some root tips withdraw into sheaths of concentrated salts. But dormancy is not simple suspension. The network continues to leak, balance, and occasionally reroute minute amounts of charge. A damaged path may be abandoned over decades while another thickens. A mat can thus remember an old injury in its shape without possessing anything that needs to be called a recollection.

Nor is a basin necessarily one being. Roots fuse where fusion is advantageous and remain separate where it is not. A warning may cross several colonies and stop at a fourth. Charge can be shared while sap is withheld. Some patches appear to cooperate only because the same storm has forced the same response upon all of them. The Crown’s organization is real, but its boundaries are negotiable.

### The small multitudes

The first animals to return after moisture are often the clear grains.

Most are no larger than coarse sand. Dry, they resemble transparent splinters or empty mineral shells. Given brine and a little current, they uncurl, take up water, and resume lives that may have been interrupted for years. Their bodies are almost entirely visible from outside: a colorless outer sac, a thread of gut, a few dark granules concentrated near the mouth. Some graze the thin living films that become active on charged roots. Others fasten themselves between two surfaces of different potential and draw a little energy directly from the passage they facilitate.

They are also water carriers. A clear grain can swallow one of the Crown’s round ice beads whole and spend days melting it internally. When the animal is eaten, the water passes on without ever having joined a pool or stream.

The root ribbons are larger, though not always easier to find. Some are no thicker than a hair and longer than a finger; others extend half a meter through the root mat while remaining flat enough to slip between layers of dead leaf. They feed on tender inner growth, on spores, and on one another. A few follow the tiny chemical alterations left by a matback’s passage. Others hunt clear grains by waiting beside the pores through which water enters the roots.

Their transparency is not camouflage against sight—sight is of limited use in the Crown—but against disturbance. A soft, conductive, nearly undifferentiated body introduces very little noise into the living circuit. More active tissues make an animal easier to detect. A full gut, a rapid pulse, an injury: each can make it briefly conspicuous to something listening below.

At this scale, the food web is crowded and fast during activity. Films spread, grains strip them, ribbons consume the grains, smaller hunters consume the ribbons. A single wet lens may contain a whole sequence of feeding and pursuit while the black stone above it appears unchanged. When the lens fails, the inhabitants dry in place, become electrically dull, and vanish from one another’s senses.

### The matbacks

The great grazers are called matbacks because, at rest, there is little else to call them.

A large one may be six meters long and three across, yet stand less than half a meter above the ground. Its body has no raised head and no meaningful silhouette at a distance. Broad, short feet arranged around its margin move it by slow changes of weight rather than obvious strides. Its back bears a dense growth of branched filaments. These are part of the animal, though spores, root fragments, and grains of dark mineral lodge among them until the distinction between coat and covering becomes imperfect.

A matback resting for several seasons can acquire a thin garden of its own.

It prefers to feed just after a current pulse, when the fans have opened and their inner tissues are newly rich. Before committing itself, it touches its lips to bare stone. The stone gives it a reference against which to judge the charge in the mat. If conditions suit it, the frost on its sensory whiskers lifts outward; if not, the animal remains where it is, sometimes for days, with its mouth almost touching a meal it will not take.

Its feeding is not gentle in intention, but it is conservative in effect. It crops fans and filament growth, strips active films, and worries open the edges of cushions without usually exposing their deepest nodules. A starving matback may dig farther. Ordinarily it leaves the structures on which its future food depends.

Where it rests, the ground becomes busier. Broken leaves release sap. Spores pressed into its coat are deposited elsewhere. Salt-heavy waste enters the roots. Clear grains gather around the animal’s feet, and ribbons follow them. The matback is therefore not simply a consumer moving across a finished pasture. It is one of the means by which the pasture mixes itself.

These animals do not form herds. A basin may have only one mature individual, following a route so slow that it cannot be seen as a route except over many years. Its young remain deep in the root layers and are seldom recognized as young at all. A mature matback may outlive several generations of the surface growth on which it feeds.

When a hunter is near, the matback withdraws its exposed filaments and disconnects itself as completely as it can. Its coat settles. The small animals around its feet hesitate or encyst. It becomes, for a while, an unusually thick piece of ground with a slow heart hidden inside it.

### The sails

The membrane creatures are the only inhabitants that regularly experience the Crown as a province rather than as a single basin.

A sail’s central body is small: a dense knot of gut, nerve, and reproductive tissue. The membrane extending from it may be many times wider than the animal is heavy. Fine charged filaments stiffen its margins, and by altering their distribution of charge the sail can coast along electrical gradients in air too thin to support ordinary wings.

It does not flap. It trims itself.

Spores stick to the membrane, as do clear grains and other small animals drawn upward during discharge. At intervals the sail contracts part of its surface, bringing what it has gathered toward the central mouth. Feeding, steering, and staying aloft are therefore not wholly separate actions. A heavy meal can force it down.

Sails appear singly not from any preference for solitude but because two membranes close together disturb the gradients each is trying to use. What looks from below like a procession of isolated black edges is often a loose stream of individuals separated by considerable distances.

When activity ends, a sail folds. At rest it may resemble a scrap of burned leaf caught beneath a stone, though nothing in the Crown has burned. It can remain that way through long droughts. Many never unfold again. Others are carried across saddles they could never have crossed as creeping young, taking with them spores, parasites, and the genetic possibilities of distant basins. They are the Crown’s irregular couriers, without knowing that anything has been entrusted to them.

### The pale hunters

The hunters beneath the mat are pale because they live where pigment would do them little good. Most are small enough to lie under a single fan. The oldest may spread for meters while remaining only a few centimeters thick.

They do not hide primarily by being still or by matching the ground. They hide by being electrically uninteresting.

Root current passes around and through their sensory surfaces. To the hunter, the ordinary network has a texture: the regular activity of valves, the slow breathing of storage nodules, the minor disturbances of water and growth. A living animal adds something else. A heartbeat is not steady enough; a nerve fires irregularly; a body tenses where root and stone meet. The hunter reads these imperfections.

It cannot easily find an animal that remains disconnected. It waits for the animal to need food, water, or information from the mat. It waits for a connection to be completed.

The strike is usually a single closure of the body. Sometimes the hunter folds upward around its prey; sometimes it draws the prey down between root layers. From above, there may be only a brief settling of leaves. Feeding takes much longer. While the hunter digests, clear grains gather at the edges of the meal and root filaments begin entering whatever soft tissue remains exposed.

Pale hunters can endure extraordinary intervals without feeding. Their bodies become thinner, their sensory activity narrower, their presence in the circuit less pronounced. They are not equally dangerous at all times. A recently fed hunter may be comparatively easy to detect; an old, starving one can be little more than a pallid absence beneath the roots.

Matbacks are generally too large for them, though young, injured, or weakened grazers are vulnerable. Most hunters live on the traffic of smaller lives. Their influence nevertheless exceeds their numbers. A hillside can carry warnings far beyond the hunter’s actual reach, causing grazers to disconnect and small animals to remain dormant. For hours, a rich patch of the Crown may eat almost nothing because one flat body has changed position.

### How the inhabitants pass through one another

Death in the Crown is often a change of ownership before it is a disappearance.

Stored charge is taken first, where it can still be reached. Brine follows. Clear grains enter soft tissue; ribbons widen the passages; root filaments arrive along them. The mat does not wait for a carcass to become carrion in any tidy sense. It begins reclaiming usable material while the former animal is still recognizable.

A dead sail may leave almost nothing but a dark, wrinkled membrane and a brief richness in the patch where it fell. A matback takes much longer. Its body becomes a low feeding ground, then a region of unusually vigorous growth, then a broad mineral stain threaded with roots. Eventually its outline is legible mainly in the arrangement of nodules and the direction of new leaves. Even the animal’s last shape has been put to use.

During a great storm pulse, all these lives briefly overlap. Nodules release brine. Clear grains wake and begin grazing. Fans open beneath the mouths of matbacks. Ribbons move toward the new growth. Spores rise, pause, and stream sideways; sails unfold to meet them. Pale hunters shift beneath the root layers, reading a world suddenly crowded with hearts and nerves.

Then the charge ebbs. The sails fold or fail to land well. The grazers settle. Water returns to bodies and roots. The hunters continue feeding a little longer, and below them the small processes of reclamation outlast the visible activity.

No inhabitant knows the whole Crown. A root network knows its fractures and injuries. A matback knows the potentials of its long grazing route. A sail knows a succession of gradients. A hunter knows the difference between an empty connection and an occupied one. The oldest mats possess memory in their growth, but memory alone does not make them a single mind.

Nothing tends the country as a whole. Everything tends some small part of it, usually by eating, resting, reproducing, or dying there.

The Crown runs not because its creatures serve it, but because their private and uncertain lives are the means by which charge, water, minerals, and tissue continue to move.
