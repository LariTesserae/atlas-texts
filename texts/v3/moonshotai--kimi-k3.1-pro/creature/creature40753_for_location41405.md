Nothing on the plateau lives by going anywhere. There are no routes between feeding ground and shelter, no daily returns, no territories marked by passage. The escarpment steps harbor no cliff-dwellers; the shallow basins contain no buried eggs, dormant spores, or patient colonies waiting for better conditions. Outside the groves, the rock is not sparsely inhabited. It is uninhabited.

Even within a grove, there is no concealed world of small animals. Nothing threads through the mats, no crystalline analogue of an insect occupies the fans, and nothing comes out after a footstep to investigate the disturbance. To ask what lives there, one must look at the vegetation—and then resist relying too heavily on what vegetation means elsewhere.

Life begins abruptly at a grove’s margin. There is no fringe of hardy pioneers, no litter thinning outward across the stone. A conductive zone ends, and with it the possibility of tissue. On one side of that boundary stand dense crowns and layered growth; a short distance beyond it lies bare silicate, with not so much as a struggling scale in a fracture. The edge looks clipped, although nothing has cut it. It is simply where a living circuit can no longer be maintained.

The bronze-green mats are usually the first growth beneathfoot. Their softness is entirely visual. They are not moss, felt, or damp cushions, but dense arrangements of minute, overlapping surfaces supported by flexible carbon mesh. Pressed lightly, they would feel warm and dry, with a faint granular resilience. Their apparent pile comes from the orientation of countless photoelectric films. Each contributes its small potential to pathways below.

There is no decaying layer under them. Lift one edge and there would be no dark humus, no pale threadwork feeding on dead tissue, no moist congregation of microscopic lives. Beneath the mat is more dry structure, then interface, then stone. Nothing accumulates because nothing is thrown away.

The finer upright scales appear to specialize in exchange with the atmosphere. Gas is held briefly against their mineral films, altered there, and incorporated into solid tissue with energy supplied from elsewhere in the grove. The broader translucent fans catch light across greater area. Their ribs are not veins carrying fluid; they are structural and conductive pathways. Along a single fan, one zone may appear almost colorless, another violet where the films repeat at a different spacing, another smoky yellow near its attachment. These colors are not displays. Nothing needs to be attracted, warned, courted, or deceived. No surface has been shaped by the attention of another organism.

Above them, the smooth crown-stalks divide into their open, layered canopies. They have no bark because there is no living layer beneath bark to protect, no wood because they do not thicken by annual addition, and no sap because nothing fluid moves through them. A stalk is at once support, conductor, and reaction volume. Its glassy fibers carry stress; its carbon pathways carry charge; its mineral junctions supply the precise conditions under which new structure can replace structure being reclaimed.

The canopy’s openness is not the temporary result of competition. No crown is overtopping another, no shaded fan is preparing to die, and no gap represents an opportunity for an opportunist. There are no vines trying to reach the light and no seedlings waiting for a fall. The arrangement is simply the stable geometry of light capture, atmospheric exchange, and reclamation at that site.

Even the apparent ages of the tissues are misleading. Nearly black-green outer layers are not necessarily approaching death, and pale surfaces are not necessarily newborns. Both are maintained conditions. A half-unfurled frond is especially deceptive: “unfurled” imports the history of a leaf from some other world. Here, the frond is not pausing partway through an act. It has no later, fully open form toward which it is tending. Material continues to pass through it while its suggestive, unfinished shape remains exactly what it is.

An observer trying to count the inhabitants would soon encounter another difficulty. Physical resemblance does not reliably distinguish individuals, and physical attachment does not always establish unity. Two neighboring fans may belong to the same continuous electrochemical system as a mat some distance away. Two apparently identical crowns, touching at their outer films, may remain entirely separate. The significant boundaries are not always visible boundaries. They lie in the continuity of charge flow, material exchange, and regulated reclamation.

A grove might therefore be one life with many exposed organs. It might be several lives occupying the same favorable seam, adjacent without being communal. Some of the tall crowns could be less like trees than like repeated openings through which a thin, widespread body meets the light.

Much of that body lies against or within the rock. The seam-webs are not roots in the searching sense. They do not taste their way toward distant richness, branch around obstacles, or probe the plain beyond the grove. Where suitable mineral continuity exists, they form broad interfaces with it. Where it fails, they stop. Their finest filaments are so continuous with the surrounding crystal that there is no clear line between an organism resting on stone and stone temporarily organized into an organism’s activity.

Not every conductive seam is alive. Some are mineral growths with no tissue attached, carrying no part of a grove’s balance. A dark line crossing kilometres of empty tableland need not connect anything to anything. The plateau contains both living interfaces and lifeless conductors, and from a distance they can look equally deliberate.

Within a living seam-web, nothing is stored for a future season. There are no tubers, resin bodies, starch caches, nurseries, or stockpiles of refined material. Energy is used in the continuing maintenance of form; feedstock passes directly into structure or back into exchange. Reclamation is equally inconspicuous. Tissue does not fall, brown, and become someone else’s food. It is opened locally into gas and mineral constituents, with no interval in which a carcass, leaf litter layer, or rotten stump can exist.

Consequently, the grove has physiology without much of an ecology in the familiar sense. There is almost no predation, competition, scavenging, pollination, or succession to describe. One form does not live off another’s surplus because there is no surplus. Their relationships are adjacency, electrical continuity where it exists, and participation in the same narrow chemical conditions. It is an abundant-looking community with almost no incidents between its members.

Its continuity is not visibly organized around multiplication, either. No grove sends colonists across the plains. No reproductive body is offered to the thin air, and nothing arrives to carry one away. Pattern is reasserted locally as material is replaced; whether that should be called repair, reproduction, or simply metabolism is largely a matter of terminology. Two widely separated groves may closely resemble one another without being settlements from a common parent. Their similarity can arise from the same chemistry solving the same local problem, not from a history of travel.

Nor is there a central inhabitant concealed within all this regularity: no heartwood chamber, no principal nodule, no caretaker coordinating the crowns. The largest stalk is not necessarily the oldest or the most important. There is no throne at the grove’s center and no reason for it to have a center at all. The faint tonal pressure near the greatest concentrations of living interface is not a voice, summons, or response. It does not change when approached. Nothing in the canopy turns toward a shadow.

This makes the life of the plateau peculiarly difficult to dramatize. It has forms, boundaries, and persistence, but almost no visible biography. No individual can be followed from birth through growth to decline. No generation can be watched replacing another. No year’s history can be read in a trunk, and no catastrophic season lies preserved in the ground. Matter moves; charge moves; the visible arrangement remains.

Yet the groves are not impoverished versions of forests. Their lack of animals, flowers, fruits, litter, migration, and weather-driven change is not a series of omissions awaiting correction. Their dry lattices are complete materials. Their closed reactions are complete economies. Their fixed, half-suggested shapes are complete shapes. Nothing in them requires rain, a pollinator, a decomposer, or a future dispersal across the plateau.

What lives there, then, is a small number of persistent living geometries: mats taking light at the surface, scales binding gas, fans and crowns extending into the still air, and broad interfaces below returning tissue to feedstock and feedstock to tissue. The plateau is inhabited without being occupied. Its life has abundance of form but almost no abundance of event.

A visitor may feel that everything is waiting. Nothing in the grove is. The waiting has been brought in from elsewhere.
