No gardener lives there. The handwheel, the ceramic fuse, and the worn threshold still suggest hands, but they no longer describe a dwelling. What lives there is the vegetation—and then, once the roots have been followed far enough, the distinction between vegetation and engine begins to fail.

At first there seem to be several kinds of life. The broad leaves appear separate from the black fern-work, and both from the wire-like moss packing their joints. But there are no root crowns from which individuals can be counted, no clear boundaries between one growth and the next. Conductive strands divide and rejoin beneath the pavement. A vein serving a translucent frond may continue, through several changes of substance, into the root mat beneath a bottle-green leaf. The chamber may contain a fixed consortium of organisms, or a single organism with several botanical organs. Counting its plants soon becomes only a way of counting shapes.

The broad leaves take the low portion of the chord. Their green is not the green of light-gathering; nothing in them turns toward the entrance or the lamps. Each leaf is a dry, flexible diaphragm, stiff enough to hold its final curve, soft enough for the mill-note to work through its microscopic frame. To the touch it would feel cool and springy, with neither the wet resistance of flesh nor the brittleness of dead tissue. The pale sulphur veins carry away the charge produced in its lattice. Leaf and collector and conductor are not successive parts. They are the same thing considered in different ways.

The fern-work is finer and more exacting. Its nested divisions answer the high metallic note, combing it through multitudes of small, rigid frameworks. Where the broad leaves gather, the ferns correct. Their excessive intricacy prevents any slight deviation from becoming a tremor, a beat, a swelling resonance. They do not make the chamber louder. They make variation impossible.

The moss is neither bedding nor ornament. Some of its filaments conduct; others isolate. It carries charge across joints that must remain joined and keeps apart surfaces that must never touch. Without it, the crowded garden might become a single undifferentiated contact. With it, density becomes precision. Even where the growth seems most soft and accidental, every crossing has been resolved.

Below them, the roots are not searching. None is on its way toward water, deeper soil, or a more favorable seam. Their ends are terminal in the strict sense. They lie in the stone’s fractures like stitches, taking up stress without widening the cracks. Where they enter salt and native copper, mineral and botanical lattice become difficult to distinguish. Where they pass into the engine, the difficulty disappears altogether: root and busbar are simply two appearances of one continuous path.

The engine therefore does not live alongside the garden, nor does the garden merely inhabit the engine. The iron cylinders are part of its hard anatomy. The crystal stacks maintain the strain on which the rest depends; the roots return that strain as tuning; the leaves and ferns hold the returned conditions exactly. Calling this symbiosis would suggest partners that could still be imagined separately. Here, separation would not reveal two diminished lives. It would reveal components on one side and dry lattice on the other.

Yet none of this is dormancy.

At the finest scales, work continues. Vibration is received, charge passes, tension is maintained, and the resonant stacks are held in their narrow accordance. At the scale of leaves, roots, needles, and shadows, all that work resolves into perfect sameness. The garden is not doing nothing. It is doing exactly enough, everywhere, that nothing further happens.

Its processes have no outward product and no developmental direction. Nothing is stored against a later need. Nothing is converted into offspring, armor, excess growth, or reserve. There is no sap rising toward a bud because there is no bud, no shed tissue because there is no replacement, no richness in the floor because nothing has ever fallen to enrich it. The garden has metabolism without accumulation and duration without biography.

Consequently, none of its leaves reads as young or old. There is no venerable central trunk, no recent colonist at the margins, no fragile last generation. Age cannot be inferred from size, and size is not an achievement. Each form is simply the dimensions required of it. Nothing is food, nothing is waste, and nothing is provisional. The chamber is not necessarily indestructible, but it contains no internal route by which its present arrangement must become another.

Nor does it display behavior. Nothing leans toward a visitor, withdraws from a hand, or alters its note in acknowledgment. The gauges are not watching. The lamps are not signals. There is no need to give the system curiosity, memory of its makers, or an awareness of the plains below; consciousness would be an additional tenant, and the closed ecology does not require one. It has regulation without attention, sensitivity without gesture.

A visitor therefore is not greeted, rejected, or even tolerated. Those are social possibilities. The visitor remains outside the circuit, able to feel its note in wrist and teeth but unable to become an event within it. A leaf’s pliancy is not welcome. Its dryness is not defense. They are merely what that form of life feels like when encountered from outside.

The continuous chord is not a voice, either. It carries no appeal, warning, or name. It does not travel far enough to summon anything, and there is nothing anywhere in the garden equipped to answer a summons. It is the sound of the whole arrangement maintaining the conditions under which it can remain that arrangement.

If the place must be said to have one inhabitant, it is that entire accordance: dry leaf and black fern, conducting root and iron cylinder, crystal strain and returning charge. Not a spirit housed in machinery, and not machinery imitating a plant. A finished physiology, botanical in its delicacy and industrial in its exactness.

It does not hold on. Holding on would imply effort, danger, and an end deferred. It continues without any of those. The loud, unchanging note is not the sound of survival. It is the sound of continuation and completion having become the same act.
