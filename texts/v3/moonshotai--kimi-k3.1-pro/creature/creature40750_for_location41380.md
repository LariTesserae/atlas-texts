Life on the escarpment is easiest to understand during a wetting, and hardest to divide into individuals.

The first mouths to open are not in the groves but beneath them. They belong to the root-mat animals: flattened, ciliated things packed between fungal threads and root hairs until the whole mat seems less like soil than a crowd holding one another in place. In the dry intervals, they fold into black seams. Condensation softens them. Their mouths take in water, dissolved minerals, spores, and fragments of leaf film, while their contractions push fluid through the mat. The fungi use them as living valves; the plants feed them sugars and, in return, draw on the chemical wealth the crowd releases. A handspan of this ground can be pasture, water organ, and electrical switch at once.

Inside the wetter stems live other animals, threadlike and almost transparent. They move against the flow of sap in slow peristaltic loops. Their passage helps lift water into the upper fronds, and a stem containing too few of them may collapse its bladders before the wetting is over. Too many, and they begin grazing on the stem itself. Most groves maintain a shifting majority in which neither plant nor animal quite has control. It is often unclear whether a stem is habitat or a shared body.

The commonest large animals are the manyfoots, though common does not mean simple. Seen from above, a herd resembles vegetation being combed. Close up, each animal is a low, flexible procession of its own: dozens of broad, soft feet, a front lip fringed with cropping plates, and a back of overlapping folds that quickly acquires a coating of mineral dust. The feet support the animal without tearing the root mats, but they also taste the ground. Charge, moisture, and the faint chemistry of fungal activity all register through them. A manyfoot can feel a grove preparing to discharge before the nearest leaves begin to open.

Their young are born with all their feet and none of their timing. At first they stumble against the cropping wave, nip at closed mouths, and brace themselves when the rest of the herd is already turning. Older animals crowd around them, not gently but persistently, until flank pressure and repeated footfalls teach them the herd’s rhythm. Membership seems to depend less on scent or appearance than on this timing. A stranger that can enter the cropping wave may be accepted within a few hundred steps. A duplicate that emerges from a disturbance slightly out of phase can cause confusion along an entire column.

Herds therefore have identities that are not quite contained in their membership. A herd may divide around an impassable cleft, wander separately through several basins, and eventually produce two complete versions of the same migration. The route, the cropping interval, and the habit of turning left before rain can all survive even when no individual that enacted them a decade earlier remains alive. When additional manyfoots arrive from some spatial disagreement, hungry and entirely real, the herd does not necessarily become a different herd. It simply has more bodies with which to repeat itself.

Much of the escarpment’s thin soil has passed through them. Their droppings are dark pellets of plant fiber, mineral grains, and still-active fungus; root mats spread from them within hours. Their backs are vehicles as well as armor. Spores lodge in the dust, transparent pool films adhere to the folds, and small flat armor-walkers spend their lives cleaning out the deeper creases. Some armor-walkers eat trapped spores. Others eat parasites, or the eggs of parasites, or one another when the herd has gone too long without rain. A few can live only in dust derived from a particular kind of basalt. Their range is consequently not a territory but a migration: they occur wherever a certain herd has recently been.

Spore-moths occupy the narrower intervals. During dryness they sit folded into the joints of fronds, looking like bits of charred leaf. Their wing markings are almost invisible because they are not primarily visual. Each pattern holds and releases charge differently. Another moth feels it through the wet stem beneath its feet; so, sometimes, does the plant. Courtship may consist of two moths altering the charge in the same frond until a seed valve opens between them. The moth that caused the opening eats some of the spores, carries others away, and leaves still others glued to the rim. The arrangement is not tidy. Some moths trigger valves too early and abandon the grove before the pump wind rises. Some groves have altered their thresholds until only one particular pattern will move them.

The moths themselves do not cross the great basins. Their flights are short, local corrections. The spores they waste or dislodge travel immeasurably farther than they do.

The inhabitants of temporary water treat dryness as an interval rather than a boundary. The translucent swimmers contract into films so thin that they can be mistaken for silica stains. The flat bottom-dwellers—the pool-flaws—do something similar, though their dried bodies often retain the exact outline of the stone grain beneath them. When water returns, they do not all revive in the same way. A film may open into one swimmer, several swimmers, or a broad sheet of feeding tissue that later separates. Pool-flaws lift their margins first, test the chemistry with thousands of fine cilia, and only then develop mouths.

At the grove’s electrical pulse, many of them divide. Others merely change shape, as though division had been considered and postponed.

These animals can be transported without ever appearing to travel. A film on a manyfoot’s back, a speck in a glider’s wing crease, or a mineral grain carried through a fault may contain enough of a pool’s population to begin it again elsewhere. Occasionally the revived animals repeat behavior suited to their former water. They make feeding circuits around stones that are not present, gather against an invisible current, or avoid a depth that existed in another valley. Most soon accommodate the new pool. A few do not, and their unsuccessful motions become part of the next dry film.

The wide gliders are the long-distance memory of many such small things. At rest they wrap their membranes close and cling to warm basalt, resembling seams of barklike mineral growth. In flight, their bodies seem almost incidental: a narrow weight suspended beneath a broad surface whose leading edge is crowded with pressure-sensitive pits. They read thermals and pump strokes through that edge. Landmarks are useful when available, but the breathing of the basins is more reliable.

They feed high in the spore streams, opening a sieve along the throat, and take moths when the opportunity arises. What adheres to them is often more important than what they eat. Pool films, armor-walkers, fungal knots, and the dormant stages of stem animals all survive in their wing folds. A glider leaving one province may carry the beginnings of several communities into another.

Their routes can outlast their roosts. When a cliff vanishes, the gliders sometimes continue circling because the rising air remains for a while after the stone has gone. They test the fading column in silence, making the same banking turns. If the cliff returns, they resume their places with no visible interval in the habit. If it does not, the flock eventually descends along another pump wind. Generations later, a different flock may enact the same circuit, having inherited it from gliders that never saw the original cliff.

The vent fields support slower lives. Not all the pale root fans there are plants. Some are sedentary colonies whose branching tips are mouths, chemical sensors, and respiratory surfaces together. They open toward the intake stroke and fold away from the hotter outward breath. Their inner tissues contain dense chemical gardens; the visible fan lives on the surplus. Small changes of color pass through a colony before it opens, and from fan to fan along a mineral throat. On the slowest pumps, many generations may live and die between one full opening and the next. The colony’s continuity lies less in any body than in its agreement with the stroke.

Wherever there are rhythms, there are also animals that eat the mistakes.

The most accomplished predators are not especially fast over open ground. They are fast at recognizing an error. Their long, narrow bodies often resemble stripped black stems until they move, and then the movement is difficult to count: a head advances, another possible head appears beside it, the middle arrives late, and a faint afterimage remains where the animal has already ceased to be. Sensory pits along their jaws detect slight repetitions in muscle and nerve. A doubled footfall, a grazing film contracting twice, a moth answering its own signal—these draw them more reliably than blood.

They hunt at the edges of herds, sometimes for many wettings, without approaching closely. A hunter may rest one forefoot against a charged root and introduce a tiny interruption. The cropping wave stutters. Most of the herd corrects itself; one animal does not. That is usually enough.

When a narrow hunter strikes, its afterimages converge. Ordinarily only one body remains to feed. Occasionally the herd is short by two animals and the predator still looks thin. Whether the second loss occurred in another version, or whether some part of the hunter fed elsewhere and has not yet returned, is not a distinction the remaining grazers can afford to investigate.

Their reproduction is similarly indefinite. A small hunter may follow a larger one for several wettings before it begins leaving footprints of its own. At first it eats only what the larger animal leaves. Later it makes a kill. From then on, whatever its origin, it must be fed like any other creature.

These predators are not guardians of coherence. They merely prosper where coherence fails, and their feeding changes which rhythms have the opportunity to continue.

Nothing here has need of tools in order to remember. A root web that has been heavily grazed can alter its discharge threshold and delay the next opening until the herd has passed. Manyfoots modify their routes when a repeated turn begins producing dangerous delays. Gliders abandon pump winds that no longer carry spores. Such adjustments enter the shared cycles and may remain after the animals that first made them are gone.

One widespread habit consists of five electrical pulses, the fourth so weak that many roots barely register it. In one grove it opens spore valves. Farther down the fault, pool animals divide at the same sequence. A herd crossing the basin answers it by turning left, even when the rain that once made the turn necessary no longer comes. Gliders use the accompanying change in warm air as a departure signal. Remove any one participant and the others continue. Replace the whole valley, and the sequence may still appear in whatever roots, feet, wings, and pool films arrive next.

It would be too much to call every such pattern a mind. It would be too little to call it weather. It remembers, anticipates, and can be altered by those who enact it.

Not all old habits are beneficial. A grove may continue sending the signal that once summoned armor-walkers long after those animals have vanished. A root web may preserve a predator’s interruption until an entire herd flinches at every fifth pulse. A pool can retain the feeding rhythm of a richer age and exhaust its inhabitants in water that no longer contains enough food. Dense foliage may consequently be full of beautifully coordinated, unanswered communications.

The oldest inhabitants of the escarpment are therefore not groves, herds, or hunters. They are sequences that bodies keep agreeing to perform. Their age lies in how often they have survived replacement, and their evolution consists partly in becoming portable: capable of being enacted by another herd, in another kind of root mat, around a pool of slightly different shape. A form exquisitely fitted to one cliff may disappear with that cliff. A habit that can tolerate approximation has more ways to return.

During the rare comprehensive click, all these agreements briefly acquire clear edges. Root mats contract as single mats. Herds lower their heads without a lag running through their columns. Pool-flaws open together. Even the narrow hunters stand, for a few moments, almost awkwardly in single bodies.

Then a young manyfoot misses the next step. A moth answers a valve that has not opened. Somewhere a transparent film begins unfolding in rain that has not yet arrived.

Life takes up these discrepancies without comment. It has always used them as material. Nothing there endures by holding still; it endures by leaving enough of itself in other bodies that, when the country changes its mind, something already knows how to feed, turn, open, or begin again.
