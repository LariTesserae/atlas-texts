## A Census of Noll

Noll has no citizens in the ordinary sense, but it is far from uninhabited. A census taken from beside one of its lamps would be almost blank: one moth, perhaps the silver interruption of a snail, perhaps a shrew’s foot vanishing from a cable. A census taken with a hand flat against the ground would be impossible. The pavement would be ticking against the palm.

Most of what lives there is small, buried, brief, or not yet continuous enough to count comfortably as an individual. Residence is less a matter of address than of return. A creature that passes through Noll once is an incident. One that passes every night may become part of the street.

The largest inhabitant is the living municipality itself. This is not quite the same as saying that Noll has a mind. Its roads, drains, columns, and root-skin share sap, moisture, warmth, and electrical leakage, but their responses remain local. A warm junction box encourages insulating growth. A wounded stalk seals itself. Repeated pressure alters the grain of a road. If the whole body remembers anything, its memory is indistinguishable from habit.

It does not protect its animals, and they do not serve it in any disciplined way. It incorporates their consequences. A shrew’s nest may cause a wall to thicken around the nest until the junction box has acquired a second chamber. Larvae may hollow a structural stem; the stem walls off the damage, leaving a tube through which water later runs. Nothing in this requires agreement. Noll is not wisely managed by its ecology. It is simply adept at making use of what its ecology has done.

Beneath a single pace of root pavement, the white larvae lie in green cells no wider than their own bodies. They consume sloughed tissue and the sweet fluid that collects where a root has sealed a pore. Their mouths make no sound in the open air, but through the ground their feeding is a faint, continuous abrasion. Copper mites gather around them, at rust wounds, and wherever the Works has shed metal into the sap. Some carry iron in their bodies until their abdomens become dull red. Others strip copper from contacts and cause maintenance lamps to fail. They are cleaners and pests without changing species.

Springtails graze the films that would otherwise close the finer root pores. Thread-thin beetles pursue them through spaces that have no names and scarcely any dimensions. The beetles also take young larvae, and are taken in turn when they enter the wetter drains. There are courtships down there, and territorial struggles, and whole generations that never encounter open air. To them, a footstep is a compression of the world. The Works is weather: a deep, regular change in pressure against which eggs can be laid and hunting can be timed.

Not everything they do is useful. An infestation may leave a lintel crooked. Mites may interrupt current to a whole ward. Beetles may consume so many springtails that a patch of ground seals, dries, and is abandoned by the larger roots. But abandonment in Noll is rarely final. The dry patch may later be preferred by pupating moths. A road may eventually follow the boundary of the old damage. A leaning lintel may become the place where salamanders shelter before rain.

The blind snails are the most deliberate of the visible residents. Their shells are low and almost colorless, often stained green on the side that rests against the road. They graze without reference to lamps, street names, or survey stakes. Moisture interests them; so does the thin black-green film, and the particular tenderness of growth along a recent cut.

A snail may cross and recross the same seam for much of its life. Its silver trace heals, unless other snails begin to graze its edges. Then the line can become curiously durable. Roots avoid it or rise slightly beside it. A survey mark adopts it. Generations later, animals that have never touched the original snail’s shell move within limits established by its appetite.

Sometimes the arrangement turns against them. A snail may graze both sides of a street before the street exists, then find itself unable to cross the horn-hard curb its own repeated passage helped encourage. It will remain there, feeding along the boundary, indifferent to having become a resident of one side.

The felt-winged moths live by another kind of fidelity. Their caterpillars develop among trimmed growth and discarded fiber near the Works, where warmth is dependable and shrews are a constant danger. The adults emerge with wings that neither shine nor flutter cleanly in lamplight. Even in flight they look slightly unfinished, like pieces of dark fabric not yet cut to a pattern.

An architectural flower can occupy nearly the whole of a moth’s adult life. It may crawl for hours across one petal, mate in a fold deeper than its body, drink sap near the flower’s base, and lay its eggs in a seam that will close before the eggs hatch. Pollen collects along its underside. When it finally travels to another flower, it carries instructions between structures without being an instruction itself.

Its loyalty matters more than its comprehension. A moth returning nightly to the same flower helps that flower remain a flower. After an arc discharge, it may find only the pale beginning of a stair where the flower had been. Often it continues visiting the stair. It settles on the same riser, crawls to the same height, and waits. If other moths join it, the stair may persist for months.

This is not exactly an error. Noll does not reliably distinguish between maintaining something and repeatedly mistaking it for something else.

The shrews are warmer, quicker, and more obviously individual. A torn ear or crooked tail can remain recognizable while the street beneath it changes names. They nest in junction boxes, lining them with black fiber and fragments of dried leaf blister. Their young learn the vibration of the cables before their eyes open. A live cable has one kind of hum; a cable feeding an empty slab has another. The shrews run both, but they descend from the second more often, as though disappointed by it.

They hunt moth pupae, beetle larvae, and anything injured by the abrupt little weather of Noll. Some pupae are carried back to the nest and eaten. Others are forgotten among the nesting fiber and hatch there, so that a junction box occasionally opens and releases both a startled shrew and several moths.

Below the cables, the flat pale predators wait in the drains. In dry periods they press themselves against the vascular linings and become difficult to distinguish from a wrinkle in the tissue. Water restores their thickness. Sap gives their skin a faint internal green. They take frogs, beetles, and young animals that fall from above, but their preferred stillness is beneath a shrew route. A healthy shrew may cross the same drain grate hundreds of times. Then, one night, the route continues without it.

Nothing marks the loss. Another shrew uses the cable. The street does not notice any difference between the two journeys.

The transparent frogs belong to events rather than places. Their eggs wait in root pores, in the seams of gutter leaves, and in dry mineral dust that seems incapable of supporting anything. A fingerprint-deep puddle can contain hatchlings, hunting tadpoles, mating adults, and the next generation’s eggs in rapid succession. At first the young are almost entirely eyes and digestive thread. Later the heart can be seen working beneath the skin. When food is scarce, they eat one another. Not every puddle completes the rehearsal.

As the water contracts, the surviving frogs spawn. Some shrink with it, leaving transparent skins around the egg mass. Others are taken by salamanders or drain predators. The remaining eggs can endure until another puddle discovers the same place.

The salamanders often arrive before there is anywhere for them to stand comfortably. They gather around dry depressions, beneath empty gutters, or beside a road whose pores contain no moisture. Their skins are sometimes already wet. This proves nothing in Noll.

They feed heavily once the frogs appear, but they also carry eggs accidentally in the folds of their legs and along their wet flanks. A salamander leaving one puddle may therefore supply the next. It is not tending the frogs. It is hunting them, and by hunting them repeatedly makes their continuation more likely.

Nor are the salamanders quite omens. An omen merely refers to something that will happen. Their gathering seems to be one of the local arrangements by which a future puddle becomes easier to occur.

The browser belongs to the empty wards.

Usually the first sign is that a lamp disappears. Then the eclipse separates into several narrow darknesses, each bending at a different height. The animal’s body follows only afterward: tall, narrow, and covered in short black filaments that return almost no light. Its many joints do not make it swift. They allow it to lower its weight into Noll one decision at a time.

For its size, its mouth is remarkably precise. It takes a bud from an unbuilt wall without tearing the surrounding tissue. It can strip a row of prospective window blisters and leave the cable threaded through them untouched. Its breath smells of crushed stems and the mineral bitterness of new sap. Mites gather in the seams of its hide; occasionally a moth rests between its shoulders, though the browser gives no sign of noticing either.

Where it feeds, the walls branch. Where it walks repeatedly, the ground becomes more dependable. New construction often angles toward its path, not as an avenue exactly, but as though the municipality were considering the possibility of accommodating it.

There is no reliable count of browsers. Bite heights differ. Some paths are broad enough for one body, others for several. Yet no two are seen together in the present arrangement. An arc flash may reveal a whole group moving between close-packed roofs; when darkness returns, only one set of cropped buds remains. It is possible that the browser is a species with a provisional population. It is equally possible to think of it as Noll thinks of a street: a route that, after sufficient repetition, has acquired a body.

It is not a guardian. It would crop a numbered door as readily as an unnamed wall. Its appetites prevent some futures and encourage others.

For the smaller animals, the Assembly Works is the safest nursery in Noll, though not because it lacks predators. Its warmth, trimmed fiber, condensation, and copper waste all attract life. Its most important product may be sequence.

Near the flywheel, eggs generally wait for water. Young remain young until they have grown. A shrew can learn that the knock in the sap pipe follows a particular stroke of the engine. Moth pupae develop in an order their bodies can anticipate. Farther from the Works, these certainties loosen. Close to it, repetition is an ecological resource as real as food.

The third pump remains an exception. Moths settle on the conduits of the water pump. Snails gather where condensation forms. Shrews nest near the warmth of the sap line. Around the unconnected pump there is usually a small bare interval. Nothing maintains it, and nothing crosses it often enough to make it a path. Nevertheless, its housing stays smooth.

There may also be residents less substantial than any of these. After the arc lamp has shown the crowded version of Noll, animals sometimes retain behaviors appropriate to it. A moth tries repeatedly to enter a window with no room behind it. A shrew carries nesting fiber along a cable and stops where a junction box used to be, or will be. Salamanders gather beneath eaves that were visible only in the flash. The browser crops cleanly along the line of a wall packed among thousands of other walls.

These need not be memories. They may be habits borrowed briefly from another arrangement.

If that crowded Noll contains people, they are not present in the way a shrew is present. They leave no warmth, no droppings, no gnawed fiber. But if people ever do arrive, they will not find an empty stage waiting for occupancy. They will inherit routes grazed into permanence, walls kept alive by moth visitation, rooms warmed first by nests, and water anticipated by animals standing in the dry.

Their ordinary behavior would be enormously powerful. A person using the same door each night would give it a stronger claim on its room. A daily walk to work could fix a street more firmly than any survey. Even an unpleasant habit—a leak ignored, a shortcut taken in fear, a window opened whenever rain began—would become part of the municipal body.

Until then, Noll’s population is recorded indirectly: silver on the roads, warm fiber in the junction boxes, dry eggs waiting in root pores, and buds bitten cleanly at a height no other animal can reach. The arc lamp may alter the total. It does not alter the basic arrangement. Hidden multitudes feed and hunt beneath the pavement; warm young sleep against a cable; the large dark appetite crosses an vacant ward.

None of these lives knows the name Noll. None needs to. Names, like roofs, can be added once the underlying habit is strong enough.
