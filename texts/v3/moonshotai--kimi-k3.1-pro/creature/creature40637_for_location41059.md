Nothing lives there quickly.

That is not the same as saying the cistern is empty. A visitor looking for eyes, mouths, or bodies that approach and retreat will find it vacant. But at the entrance to a root shoe, or in the narrow clearance between a leaf and a pipe, Header Nine is populous. Its inhabitants simply do very little that a human visitor would recognize as an event.

The visible population is botanical, though it is not easily divided into specimens. The service numbers identify beds, not necessarily individuals. A dark-stemmed climber may be rooted in several ceramic shoes and spread through half a trellis, while a dozen genetically identical feathered plants can share one filtration cassette without sharing a body. Some of the apparent thickets are single organisms extended through the machinery. Others are dense assemblies of separate plants whose roots never meet. Counting green shapes would give a misleading census.

There are no volunteers among them. No seed has arrived on the wind and found a promising crack. No frond occupies its place because it defeated another frond. Even the crowding is a set of permissions, revised at the pace of growth. The garden can become more intricate, but it cannot become wild in the usual sense. It has no empty ground awaiting an occupant and no marginal species awaiting a disturbance.

In the filtration beds, the feathered plants present enormous surfaces to water that barely moves. Their fronds do not tremble to improve the exchange; most of the time they do not move at all. Their significant activity occurs at root tips and across membranes. Dissolved material enters tissue. Oxygen and metabolites leave it. A change that would be too small to detect in a glass of the water becomes, over years, the plant’s whole occupation.

When a root encounters a bolt, the bolt can become the most important object in its world for a season. The root thickens on one side, enters a casting seam, and gradually accommodates the thread. It does not wrench or grip. By the time it passes, it has partly taken the bolt’s shape. Much of the garden remembers in this way: not by retaining an image, but by becoming slightly different where something resisted it.

The ribbon plants appear to have flowed through the pipe racks, but nothing about them has ever flowed. A growing tip extended, rested, thickened, and extended again. Their coils are records of clearances that existed years earlier. If a pipe were removed during maintenance, the leaves around it would not spring back. They would keep the absent pipe’s shape until later growth slowly revised them.

A swimmer can bend these plants aside and open a temporary passage, but the passage is not a trail. Nothing else will use it. It closes through the elasticity of stems and the accretion of new tissue, and even then not quite completely. Years afterward, a few leaves may still point obliquely toward the route taken by someone who remained in the chamber for less than an hour.

The saucer leaves make a second architecture near the surface. Seen from below, they are a broken green ceiling, each waxy edge overlapping its neighbors without quite sealing the gaps. Their oxygen enters the water directly; there are no celebratory strings of bubbles. Beneath the exchanger banks, where the water is fractionally warmer, their petioles lengthen by almost imperceptible degrees. In the shade of a settling tank, other leaves remain smaller and thicker. These are not different landscapes so much as different chemical sentences being spoken to the same kind of plant.

Below the visible census, the root ceramics have their own minute inhabitants: fixed microbial films deep within the pores. They do not cloud the open water or form a fleece on the tile. A film may occupy a few connected cavities for its entire existence, receiving what the roots release and altering what the roots receive. There is no drifting planktonic world between the leaves, no glitter of hunting specks in a lamp beam. Even at the smallest scale, residence here means attachment.

Pollination reveals most clearly what kind of garden this is. The ivory flowers have no audience to attract. Their color is not an advertisement within Header Nine, and there is no nectar economy gathered around them. A blossom need not exhaust itself during one warm afternoon. It can remain receptive while the brass carriage approaches at a rate that would make an ordinary flowering season meaningless.

The carriage does not behave like an animal. It does not seek, choose, feed, or show preference. It lowers its brushes because the rail and its mechanism bring it to the place where a brush should be lowered. Nor does the flower respond with anything resembling eagerness. Yet together they complete an act that neither could complete alone. The plant has outsourced one motion of reproduction to a machine, without making the machine alive.

Seed, consequently, is not a plan for dispersal. There is nowhere to disperse to. Most of the garden continues by extending bodies already present: another root into a ceramic pore, another stem along a brace, another leaf into a clearance that has remained narrow for decades. A seed is a way of beginning again without going elsewhere.

Death does not produce wilderness either. An old leaf does not fall among others to make a layer of mould. It reaches its separation point, enters a screened hopper, and leaves the visible garden. Its substance returns much later through the nutrient lines, possibly to a root belonging to the same extended plant. There will never be a soil in which a different Header Nine might arise. The dead are not allowed to become terrain.

Nothing among the residents investigates a moving shadow. Nothing becomes still in order to hide, because hiding is not required. A human swimmer is neither predator nor pollinator, and scarcely even a disturbance in the ecological sense. They are a brief deformation of the habitat: displaced leaves, a wake that takes longer than expected to vanish, a little warmth and chemistry soon diluted into a body of water much larger than the visitor. They leave before any growing tip has had time to make a decision about them.

If the inhabitants can be said to have a world, it is composed of gradients rather than vistas. Slightly more warmth beside a radiator fin. Light reduced by one additional leaf. A difference across a root membrane. Humidity gathering around a flower. Cloud beyond the open sides matters only as a change in illumination. The two-kilometre drop does not enter the garden’s affairs at all. The height belongs to the tower and to the people who built it.

After long enough in the chamber, it becomes tempting to call the whole cistern one organism. Too many functions cross the boundary between tissue and machinery for the metaphor to seem entirely foolish. Leaves maintain the water in which roots work. Roots alter the water carried to other leaves. Hoppers remove what would otherwise accumulate. Digesters return it. The carriage completes the flowers’ slow touch. Below the floor, the level engines preserve the condition under which any of this can happen.

But the room has no center, no private sensation, and no will that survives its arrangements. The engines do not care for the plants. The plants do not know that the water has been given walls. Their purposes have been fitted together from outside. What they share is not consciousness, but dependency and tempo.

At any particular hour, almost nothing can be seen to happen. Somewhere a root adds a fraction of a cell to its length. Oxygen crosses a leaf surface without disturbing the water. A microbial film renews itself inside a ceramic pore. Pollen adheres to a brush or does not. A bent ribbon leaf remains bent, preserving the memory of a pipe, a swimmer, or simply its own former position.

That is what lives in Header Nine: attached lives, extended through machinery, with no quick center and no use for the abyss. The chamber is still only if stillness means the absence of animals. In the time of its inhabitants, it is thoroughly occupied.
