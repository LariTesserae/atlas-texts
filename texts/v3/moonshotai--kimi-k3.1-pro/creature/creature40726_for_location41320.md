## The inhabitants of the Thimble Deep

No creature is native to the whole sea. Nine kilometres of pressure divide it more effectively than the walls divide its width. The snails of an upper olive garden would not survive in the lowest provinces, and a basin crab carried upward would soon sicken. Their shells are not armour against this. Like nearly everything in the Deep, they have no compressible cavities; their survival depends on tissues adjusted to a particular pressure range.

The needle-eel, certain larvae, and the tougher spores are the great travellers. Most other lives remain within a few chambers.

The result is an archipelago stood on end. Adjacent gardens exchange young; distant ones may share little beyond an ancestor and the sound of the same valves. There are local snails distinguished by the milkiness of their shells, local crabs with different brooding times, local filter feeders whose reflexes are tuned to slightly different vibrations. A single chamber can look nearly empty. The Deep’s richness is in the inventory, not the view.

An inhabitant’s address is more precise than an elevation. It might be the side of a bracket that catches sediment during the long downward glide, the lee of an inspection housing during the ninety-second ascent, or a seam close enough to a valve that the wall trembles an instant before the water does.

### The living substrate

The foundational life is the felt.

It is not one plant but a succession of consortia: descendants of filtration cultures, algae, fine fungal threads, and organisms with no remaining equivalents outside the bore. Where it is young, it resembles a discoloration of the ceramic. Where it is old, it can be peeled in layered sheets, each layer enclosing enamel dust, carbon, shell fragments, and the abandoned tubes of minute animals.

At a perished gasket seat, the felt behaves in a way that resembles repair without being repair. A slight leak delivers particles and dissolved material; filaments grow more thickly in the jet; the growth narrows the leak. Snails later graze channels through it, keeping the seal from becoming a plug. A gasket may thus remain alive and porous for centuries, passing water slowly enough to feed its inhabitants but not quickly enough to scour them away.

On broader ledges, the felt becomes the first stratum of a garden. Ribbon algae fasten themselves to it with small, tough holdfasts. Their tissues contain no floats. Each ribbon makes continual, almost imperceptible movements, offering a curve to the current and then recovering it. During the long glide they stream close to the wall. When the water turns upward, they lift and comb it.

The larger curtain fronds are never entirely still either. A travelling looseness passes along their lobes, preventing them from hanging as dead weight. In the upper gardens they are bottle-green, olive, or clear enough for the inspection light to shine through their gold edges. In the deep gardens, fine deposits of metal oxide stiffen particular folds. These bronze veins do not support the plant so much as give it a preferred attitude: after every reversal, the frond returns to the same orientation in the current.

Flowering follows heat. A warm front passes, and for a short time the fronds produce dense ranks of sporangia along their veins—cream on the upper plants, rusty orange or pale pink on the garnet ones. A dark garden can appear dusted with copper. The next strong ascent opens them. Spores enter the column, most to perish in an unsuitable pressure province, a few to catch in felt and begin the slow work of becoming attached.

At the very top, the flexible diaphragm itself is nearly bare; its movement wipes away anything thicker than a film. Around its fixed rim, however, grows a low, quick province of gold felt and short ribbons. The double knock shakes fine material loose from this rim. Some of it is eaten immediately. Some begins the long fall.

### The ledge animals

A glass-shelled snail carries its garden’s colour inside it. Its shell is transparent except for fine cloudy growth bands; the body within may be olive, amber, rusty red, or almost black. It grazes the film without usually destroying the felt beneath. The path left behind is not a wound but a temporary clearing, already being recolonised by the time the snail has moved on.

The paths are widely separated because a fresh trail contains little worth eating. A snail encountering one—its own or another’s—usually turns aside. In this way, without contests or visible territories, a garden becomes partitioned into slow grazing routes. A snail may spend weeks on a ledge that a threadfish crosses in seconds.

Before certain valve closures, it stops. Its foot spreads, its shell settles against the ceramic, and a rim of mucus hardens enough to hold it through the pressure confusion. When it resumes, the only evidence of the interruption may be a small break in the grazing line.

The old gasket seats contain another kind of abundance. Closed, the seam-fans look like dark stitching or hairline cracks. Each is a sedentary animal in a tube bound together from carbon and shell dust. During the pump’s ascent, their crowns open: hundreds of minute, feathered fans, too small to lend the wall any obvious movement, but enough to make a dry-looking seam briefly velvety. They strain enamel dust and fragments of frond, and their tubes give larvae purchase where the original ceramic would have offered none.

The flat crabs are the visible proprietors of such places.

They are not colourless deep-sea ghosts. Upper crabs are often mottled green-brown or oxide red; others carry garnet markings that match the gardens beneath which they shelter. Basin crabs may become chalky with dust, but their eyes remain dark and alert. Their bodies are flattened less for squeezing through narrow spaces than for disappearing beneath a ledge while the current passes over them. Folded, a large adult can look like nothing more than a thickened lip of sediment.

A crab tests a new refuge with the tips of its legs and occasional sharp taps. The clicks are not calls. A firm seat returns one kind of vibration; loose felt, hollow sediment, or a fractured edge returns another. Often these small tests are heard long before the crab is seen.

Adults space themselves according to shelter. Where a chamber has one good ledge, it commonly has one adult crab, and the animal’s grazing, scavenging, and sediment-turning can be traced around nearly every part of it. It crops old fronds, crushes durable shell fragments, and intercepts falling tissue before the current can carry it away. The especially rich garden around its refuge is partly an unplanned consequence of its habits.

For much of the year, juvenile crabs are almost invisible. They live in the felt as translucent, slow-moving specks. A brooding female carries a later generation beneath her body and releases it during an ascent. Only then does the ledge reveal how much life it has been concealing.

### The open column

A resting threadfish is usually visible only as a silver punctuation mark.

It has no swim bladder. Its head hangs almost motionless while the transparent remainder of its body makes small corrections against the current. At close range, the tail can be found by the way it interrupts the light; at a distance, there is only the head and, occasionally, the brief line of a turn.

Threadfish are solitary and widely spaced, each occupying its own pressure band. During ordinary water, one may wait among bare walls for prey that is not visible at all. During spawning, several converge from neighbouring reaches. They feed so quickly that the larval cloud seems to be collapsing inward. A fish that has eaten heavily becomes easier to see for a while: its transparent tail carries a faint cloudy thread of green, gold, and milky tissue until digestion clears it.

The pale needle-eel lives on a larger scale. Its ordinary patrol may encompass several hundred metres of bore, including bare descents, gardens, narrow gasket seats, and the mouths of bolt recesses. It is one of the few animals tolerant enough of pressure change to make such a route routine.

It hunts by disturbances before it hunts by sight. Prey leaves a small asymmetry in the water, and the narrow bore preserves and returns that disturbance in ways an open sea would not. The eel follows these remnants with its head slightly inclined toward the wall, using its eyes only when the chase has become short. At rest, it presses part of its long body into the lee of an inspection housing or along the shallow remains of a screw thread.

An old eel’s route is learned. It skips brackets where gardens have failed, returns to ledges where crabs brood, and lingers below seams that release larvae after warm fronts. Before a major valve closure, it becomes motionless. In the confused pressure after reversal, it hunts again.

No other visible animal knows so much of the column. Even it knows only a province.

Most of the time, open water appears empty because it really is sparsely occupied. The minute swimmers are not a permanent planktonic multitude. They are eggs, larvae, newly released juveniles, and brief reproductive stages. Plankton in the Deep is less a population than an event.

### The lowest lives

With depth, the gardens become more pressure-bound, but not less coloured. Garnet curtains hang beneath black-purple lobes. Bronze veins flash when a frond turns. Glass snails move through them with bodies so dark that their shells seem empty until they reach the light directly.

The food reaching these provinces has often already had several lives. A piece of frond may be shredded by a crab, caught by seam-fans, broken again by larvae, and carried onward as progressively finer material. A deep animal may therefore consume, in greatly altered form, tissue that flowered kilometres above it and days earlier.

The basin receives the last of this relay.

Its crabs work through the white dust without haste. They swallow the bacterial films and organic traces coating the grains, turn over shell fragments, and leave most of the mineral material only slightly rearranged. There are no rich carcasses here and no accumulation of intact remains. The basin is not a graveyard so much as the final, very thorough stage of digestion.

Around the turbine head, the anemone-like animals fasten themselves in interrupted rings. Their crowns are amber, cream, or pale rust. When the turbine’s precursor tremor arrives, they contract into low buttons. The long groan follows; the head shifts by its minute fraction; a little dust rises. Then the crowns open again and strain the disturbed water.

The oldest colonies occupy the stationary rim, where each shift cleans some patches and feeds others. Their rings record that difference. Nothing there is still, but almost nothing is hurried.

### The common clock

There is no night in the Deep. Rest is not a darkness but a posture: a snail clamped to ceramic, a crab folded under enamel, a seam-fan withdrawn, an eel motionless beside a gasket, a frond making only the smallest movement needed to remain supported.

The eleven-minute reversal governs feeding and attachment. The less regular warm fronts govern growth, migration, and reproduction. In their wake, gardens flower, grazers move toward new felt, and predators follow. Young are released into the upward water. For a few minutes, larvae cloud the light, crab broods spread like transparent grit, and threadfish gather out of apparent nowhere. Then the cloud is eaten, stranded, or carried away. The visible emptiness returns.

This stability is not gentle in every detail. Felt can choke a narrow seat before grazers open it. A crab can strip its own refuge. An eel can clear a reach of young fish. A spawning cloud can fail entirely. But the hard profile changes very little while these local arrangements rise and collapse. The ledges are finished; their tenancy is not.

The machine and its inhabitants remain indispensable to one another without any inhabitant recognising the machine as such. The felt knows the leak only as food arriving in a jet. The crab knows the valve as a vibration requiring a firmer grip. The turbine anemones know the groan as the approach of suspended dust. The needle-eel knows its province as a sequence of refuges, pressure changes, and productive sounds.

When another valve begins to close, the wall carries the news first. Seam-fans shut. Snails settle their shells. The needle-eel stops its patrol. Far below, the turbine crowns withdraw. Then comes the clean blow, the reversal, and the brief upward streaming of fronds, sediment, spores, and young.

Ninety seconds later, the long glide resumes. The gardens settle. The fish become distant glints. The basin crabs return to the white dust.

Nothing there has ever seen the cap. Everything there is shaped by the knock.
