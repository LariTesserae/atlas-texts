Everything in the Night Basin lives by a local difference: dry sand lying over damp sand, warm stone over cool stone, bark that still holds the day’s heat and bark already softened by rot. These differences are addresses. An animal need not travel far to enter another country.

The trees are the oldest visible residents. Their crowns interlock, but their trunks are not merely supports for the canopy. Each is a column of separate habitations. On one, a gecko lies so flat against the grain that its body seems only another roughness in the bark. Beneath the same bark, beetle larvae chew galleries the gecko will never see. Ants inspect the galleries where they open to the air. Higher up, a liana provides a road for animals able to cross from crown to crown without touching the ground. Even a dead tree remains densely occupied. Fungi open its wood from within; beetles and their larvae loosen it further; seedlings establish themselves in pockets of softened trunk. Long before it falls, it has become porous with other lives.

Underfoot, much of the population is engaged in moving the basin from one place to another. Leafcutter ants bring pieces of the canopy underground. The leaves are not their food, but the foundation of their fungus gardens, which must be fed, cleaned, and kept within narrow limits of moisture. A returning column may appear to be stripping vegetation without purpose, but each fragment is part of a transfer from sunlight into darkness. Termites perform a slower and more buried work, taking wood, soil, and their own bodies into structures whose interiors are warmer, cooler, wetter, or drier than the air outside as conditions require. Within them, still smaller organisms help make wood digestible.

Neither kind of colony is secure. A garden fails. A nest wall softens after rain. Raiders enter a passage, or a portion of the colony is abandoned. The survivors do not restore an ideal design; they build around what remains. Old nests subside into inhabited soil.

Other residents work at the scale of a single footprint or a fragment of carcass. Mites gather in damaged leaf tissue. Springtails move through the damp interfaces between grains of soil. Beetles bury dung and open dead bodies, while fly larvae, fungi, and bacteria reduce what would otherwise remain intact. A fallen log is not passed directly from tree to earth. It passes through appetites, mouths, guts, infections, and periods of waiting. Some material lies untouched for a long time because the organism able to use it has not yet arrived, or because the wood is still too dry.

Above this work live the hunters of small distances. A spider waits beneath a leaf without a web, its forelegs resting lightly on the midrib. When something moves along the leaf, it turns toward the vibration, then decides against it. A hunting insect stands motionless inside the curl of a dead leaf, its stillness broken only by the cleaning of one antenna. Under a stone, a centipede occupies the cool side of the day’s remaining heat. The gecko on the trunk takes an insect that comes within reach and then resumes its former stillness. Most hunts end in nothing observable: a withdrawal, a missed approach, a vibration that ceases before the hunter moves.

The air is inhabited as thoroughly as the wood. Bats use it at different heights and according to different maps. Some pass low over the pools, taking insects whose presence a listener on the bank would barely suspect. Others travel among flowers and fruit, carrying pollen on their fur or seeds away from the parent tree. Their routes join parts of the basin that no rooted organism can join. Moths move on slower paths, visiting flowers, feeding, laying eggs, or simply blundering through foliage until something larger notices them. Most of the signals by which the bats avoid trunks and find prey pass above ordinary hearing. What feels like empty darkness between the trees is, to them, a detailed and continuously changing space.

The seep gathers another population, though the animals there have not assembled for a common purpose. Each has arrived for its own narrow reason. The turtle breathes with only its snout exposed, the rest of its body held in water that offers both concealment and food. Beetles dimple the surface and carry air beneath their bodies when they dive. Larvae hang below until a disturbance sends them flexing into deeper water. Along the living root mat, where sedges and soft-stemmed plants bind the mud together, frogs sit close enough to touch but oriented toward different dangers. Their stillness is not trust. Each remains ready to jump according to its own perception.

The small mammal comes to drink by a route that keeps something solid close to its body. Its whiskers test the roots before its feet commit to them. It takes water quickly, freezes, and withdraws. The impression of its feet remains after its scent has begun to fade. Ants cross the damp marks, investigating a disturbance that no longer contains the animal. Much later, a snake may pass over the same roots, reading warmth and scent where another creature would find only darkness. It need not encounter the mammal for the two lives to affect one another. The possibility of the snake is already part of the mammal’s behaviour.

The basin also contains animals large enough to alter vegetation simply by lying down. One sleeps in a thicket where repeated use has pressed the herbs into a bed. When it turns, stems knock softly together. Its body supports smaller residents: biting insects, ticks, organisms within its gut, things waiting for its dung or for the disturbance of its passage. Its journeys to water widen routes later used by animals that would never travel beside it. When it feeds, broken stems and bruised leaves become opportunities for creatures unable to tear the living tissue themselves. It is not the master of the basin. It is one of its larger mobile events.

No large body is entirely a single inhabitant, and no small body is insignificant merely because it cannot be seen. A beetle may carry mites; a mammal may transport seeds in mud drying on its feet; a frog’s damp skin may briefly shelter organisms whose whole journey is the distance from one root to another. Lives overlap without becoming equivalent. Shelter for one is exposure for another. The pool that saves an animal from drying also brings it within reach of things that hunt at water.

Some of the population is present almost entirely as waiting. Eggs adhere to the undersides of leaves. Pupae lie inside wood or soil. Spores remain in places that are, for the moment, too dry. Seeds rest in clay and sand, not all viable, not all destined to be found by the right season. There are animals whose entire residence in the basin consists of a developmental stage hidden inside something else. Rain may release some of these waiting lives, rot others, and expose still others to predators. A puddle that lasts only a few nights can matter greatly to one species and not at all to the next.

It would be easy to mistake this density for cooperation, but there is no pact among the residents. The fungus opening a log is not working for the seedling that may later benefit. The frog does not regulate insects for the sake of the pool. Leafcutters can strip a sapling; a hunter’s meal is another resident’s ending; a tree’s shade preserves moisture while its roots deprive smaller plants of water. Parasites weaken their hosts, colonies fail, and nests full of eggs are discovered. Yet neither is the basin simply a contest. Most lives are occupied with narrower things: feeding, moulting, avoiding detection, keeping skin damp, tending young, finding a surface on which the next step will not give way.

From those separate concerns arises the basin’s continuity. Not harmony, and not a single purpose, but innumerable purposes pressed so closely together that even a falling leaf changes where several of them can live.

By dawn, much of the population will have changed position rather than departed. Bats will be inside hollow wood or deep fissures. The gecko will be behind bark. Frogs will press closer to the wet roots, and the small mammal will be in a nest whose entrance reveals nothing of the route leading to it. Ants and termites will continue. Sleeping birds and day-active insects will begin to use portions of the same branches, though rarely in quite the same ways.

First light will not reveal the whole population. It will reveal shelters, feeding scars, intersecting paths, and the fresh absence of certain leaves. Behind bark and below mud, the night’s residents will still be digesting, moulting, tending eggs, repairing nests, or waiting for the next dark. The basin will look quieter. It will not be less lived in.
