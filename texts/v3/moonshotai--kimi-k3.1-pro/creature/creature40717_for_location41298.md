# The Lives Kept There

Nothing on the plateau is only an individual. A stoneback is an animal, then a catchment, then a nursery. A builder colony is a population, then a wall, then a small climate. Even the transparent grazers carry spores in the grooves of their legs and mites beneath their mouths. To ask what lives there is to keep changing the distance from which one looks.

No creature behaves as though the plateau belongs to it. The largest animals do not rule the smaller ones, and the builders do not clear ground for their work. Life persists by fitting into whatever life has already made.

## The living ground

The dark crust is not one organism but a layered community packed into a mineral skin. Its uppermost cells endure the light and photosynthesize when conditions permit. Beneath them lie paler threads, dormant bodies, and minute consumers that can remain dry long enough to seem indistinguishable from dust. The whole crust is seldom active at once. Rain wakes one part of it; condensation wakes another; the rest remains in reserve.

This divided waking is characteristic of the plateau. A single rain never receives everything. Some eggs hatch and some do not. Some rosette seeds germinate while genetically identical seeds a hand’s width away remain closed. Part of a film brightens while the adjoining film stays dark. A failed wet season may therefore be disastrous for the animals already active without extinguishing the community that produced them.

The grazers keep the film from becoming a continuous, lightless rind. Their mouths remove its oldest surface, and their waste returns concentrated nourishment to the sheltered places where new growth begins. Where they are too numerous, the stone is briefly stripped pale. Where they are absent, the crust thickens, cracks, and sheds. Most of the plateau lies somewhere between these conditions, maintained by cropping so slight that it resembles weathering.

The visible plants belong to this same economy. A ring of hair-thin grass may be one old plant surviving only at its margin, its dead center recording where moisture once lingered. Coin rosettes often begin in the organic silt of abandoned shell basins. The fleshy ground-plants keep their seeds in shallow pits along their edges; when rain lifts those edges, the seeds stick to passing feet and are carried without any animal having intended to carry them.

## The animals that become places

A stoneback’s life is a gradual withdrawal from travel, not from activity.

The young are soft enough to bend around grains. They shelter beneath pebbles during the brightest hours and emerge when the stone has cooled, grazing with a wide, delicate margin that is later almost entirely concealed by shell. They are vulnerable then. Their hunters are flattened larvae, parasitic mites, and other small creatures able to follow the chemical trace of a moist body across dry dust.

Mineral accumulates on them unevenly. The first deposits are little more than a dulling of the back; later come overlapping patches, colored by the ground over which the animal has lived. A stoneback that spent its youth crossing an ash-gray swell may carry that pallor beneath the darker minerals acquired elsewhere. Its shell becomes a record of routes it will never travel again.

Eventually, movement costs more water than it finds. The animal settles where shade, grazing, and condensation can be brought into a workable balance. Its back broadens. Fine grooves develop, not as ornament but as catchment: dew gathers along their cooler edges and is guided toward the underside. Before dawn, the narrow seam opens. The breathing fringe emerges, and around the animal’s margin a still thinner grazing surface takes the enriched film growing in its own shade.

An old stoneback is therefore busy while appearing inert. It adjusts its orientation by minute degrees. It closes against parasites and opens to humidity. Its waste feeds the ground from which it feeds. If the surrounding film declines, it may shift position, but so slowly that the change is easier to detect in the vegetation than in the animal.

The eldest become catchments for other lives. Their eggs are not necessarily the eggs in their own basins. It is mostly the smaller, still-mobile stonebacks that breed and deposit eggs in damp silt; an ancient individual may shelter generations to which it is only distantly related. Veilwing larvae develop in the same warm shallows. Glassfeet gather at the margins. Rosettes root where the basin is beginning to fill.

Much of that silt is former life: ground shell, cast skins, collapsed wings, mineralized films, and the compacted waste of innumerable small inhabitants. An old stoneback does not merely hold water. It holds a crowded past in which new things can root.

Death changes the tenancy more readily than the form. The soft body is consumed gradually through the seam, but the mineral back remains. Without the living animal to regulate its openings, more creatures enter. A dead stoneback can be more visibly inhabited than a living one. Later, when the basin silts and dries, young stonebacks settle in the shelter of its rim.

## The smaller multitudes

The transparent, thread-legged grazers are not one kind of animal repeated everywhere. Different sorts occupy the upper film, the shell seams, the basin silt, and the undersides of stones. Some are so flat that their legs appear to belong to the surface over which they pass. Others have bodies thickened almost entirely by stored water.

In drought, many fold into cracks and dry until they resemble their own discarded skins. Their chemistry trails remain longer than their activity. When moisture returns, these trails become routes again, read by grazers, parasites, and hunters alike.

Some routes outlive every animal that made them. Generations renew the same faint chemical line, polishing it with their feet and altering the film along its course. No individual travels the whole path. The path is a behavior with a longer life than its performers.

Parasitism here is often a form of residence rather than a brief attack. Mites live in the joints of larvae and in the sheltered folds of stoneback breathing tissue. Some draw fluid from their hosts; others consume growths that would otherwise obstruct a seam. They carry spores, dormant eggs, and still smaller organisms in their mouthparts. When a young stoneback leaves its birthplace, an entire minute community emigrates with it.

There are hunters, but nothing large patrols the open ground. Nothing could afford to feed such an animal. A pallid, ribbon-flat creature may spend its life within one shell seam, clipping a few filaments from the breathing fringe whenever it opens. It seldom takes enough to kill its host; the host’s longevity is its pasture. Other hunters wait beside grazers’ trails and leave only empty transparent skins behind. The largest acts of violence commonly occur within the width of a dewdrop.

## The inhabited air

A veilwing is mostly surface. Its body is a small knot from which the wings extend in sheets so thin that their veins seem to be cracks in the light. Fine combing structures gather spores and organic threads from the air; at intervals, these are drawn toward the mouth. The animal does not need to descend merely because the ground below is rich. The thermal itself is its feeding place.

Their vast turnings have no leader. Each animal responds to pressure, wing contact, and the slight disturbances made by its neighbors. A change passes through the suspended multitude, and for an instant thousands of wings present the same angle to the sun. Then they edge on again and vanish.

Before dawn, some settle against cool shells and stones, taking condensation through the edges of their wings. Others remain aloft until the thermals fail. During wet periods they descend to drink and lay eggs in warm shallows or saturated silt. Their larvae are flatter and heavier than the adults, living among the crowded margins of stoneback basins and builder chambers. As with so much on the plateau, only part of a brood hatches at the next wetting.

A few broader, rarer wings drift among them and take exhausted or newly emerged veilwings rather than filtering dust. Even the air has its predation, but it produces no chase and almost no sound. Dead adults eventually fall as transparent flakes. On the ground, their wings are cropped, incorporated into chamber walls, or reduced to the organic thread from which new film grows.

## The colonies that grow rooms

An individual builder is easily overlooked: soft-bodied, slow, and usually hidden within a pore of the structure it helps maintain. It spends part of its life foraging, part tending young, and part adding material to the colony’s edge. These roles are not rigidly separate. The same animal may perform all of them as it ages.

Building occurs mostly when the material is damp enough to remain workable. The animal lays a silk crescent against an existing surface, incorporates shed body material, and coats the addition with mineral-rich secretion. Thousands of such acts produce a lamina. Countless laminae produce a rib.

No builder carries the plan of a dome. Each responds to moisture, damage, the taste of old silk, and the work immediately beside it. Yet the colony retains a kind of memory. A chamber that repeatedly dries is extended less often. A rib that condenses water reliably is thickened. When part of a wall collapses, the repair does not always restore the former shape; it follows the new pattern of dampness. The structure becomes a record of corrections made by animals that no longer exist.

The ribs cool and collect condensation. The wall’s organic matrix swells and shrinks with humidity, altering its pores. Inside, the builders crop a dim photosynthetic film and carry its spores from chamber to chamber. Their nurseries are never perfectly sterile shelters: mites live in the wall, larvae occupy the wettest pockets, and small grazers follow the builders’ routes.

Even an active colony is therefore shared. When it dwindles, sharing becomes succession. Veilwing young use the reservoirs. Rosettes establish in roof debris. Young stonebacks occupy the entrances. The builders’ most enduring achievement is not a sealed dwelling but a place where humidity remains possible after its makers have gone.

## The brief country of rain

Rain does not simply enrich the plateau. It briefly permits lives that are otherwise impossible.

Mobile stonebacks converge along routes that may have been dry for years. Eggs hatch in basin silt, but never all of them. Grazers emerge before the ground looks fully wet. Builders reach the outer crescents and add material faster than at any other time. Hunters wake with their prey. For a short period, eating, breeding, hatching, and dying all accelerate together.

Then the water is taken into shells, tissues, seeds, chamber walls, and the upper layers of crust. Surfaces close. The veilwings rise. What remains openly visible is not the abundance itself but its stored consequences: new crescents on a dome, eggs left unhatched, a little more silt in a basin, a generation of soft-backed young concealed among pebbles.

A drop of water may pass from a condensation groove into the film, from the film into a grazer, from the grazer into a hunter, and back to the ground as waste without ever standing in an open pan. Bodies are the plateau’s most dependable reservoirs.

At noon, the old stonebacks are stones again, the chambers are mineral eccentricities, and the air appears empty. But nothing has become lifeless. The inhabitants have merely returned to the scale at which moisture can be kept.

To live briefly here is to travel between shelters. To live long is to become one.
