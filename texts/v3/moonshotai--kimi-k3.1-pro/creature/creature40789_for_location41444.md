The loudest place on the moor is inhabited almost entirely by things that have no use for sound.

Nothing large makes its home in the hollow. There is no concealed multitude beyond the cleft, and no animal lies in the dark waiting for the weather to turn. The place could not feed such a life. Its residents occupy a much narrower world: the thickness of a water film, the sheltered side of a mineral grain, the few millimetres between bare limestone and the full force of the current.

The basis of that world is a growth so thin it is usually mistaken for wetness. Along the seep, and in a dim band beyond it, the rock carries a dark biofilm of algae, fungi, and bacteria. Where a little daylight reaches, the algae manage a slow photosynthesis. Farther back, the film lives partly on what the moor surrenders to the inward current: pollen, spores, vegetable dust, the almost weightless remnants of insects. Most of this material is carried through or combed into the sheltered angle. A very little remains on the stone. That little is the hollow’s pasture.

Its most characteristic animal is a small seam-snail. Fully grown, it is no broader than a fingernail, with a shell flattened enough to let it withdraw into narrow bedding planes. The shell is cloudy grey at the opening and chalk-white toward the apex; the living animal within is darker, almost translucent where its foot meets the rock. It is not often seen. A visitor may search the wet wall for an hour and find only old grazing marks, each a faint interruption in the biofilm.

The snail’s life is governed by moisture rather than noise. When its patch of limestone loses its water film, or when the current strikes it too directly, it withdraws and seals the shell opening with a papery barrier of mucus and mineral dust. It can remain so for a long time. When rain renews the seep, or mist persists long enough to wet the stone, it pushes the seal aside and resumes feeding. Its excursions are short. Over a year, one may traverse no more than a few lengths of wall. Over a lifetime, it may never leave the seam in which it hatched.

The old shells in the protected fracture are therefore misleading. They do not record a vanished colony or a former abundance. They are simply what remains when a sparse population has occupied the same shelter for a very long time and almost nothing has come to carry the dead away.

The green lining contains other populations, most of them invisible without taking the moss apart. Between its saturated leaves live rotifers, tardigrades, minute worms, and pale springtails. Some endure the colder, drier intervals by becoming almost inert; others retreat downward into the older, blacker layers of the cushion. When water returns, they resume. After heavy rain, the temporary pool on the floor may briefly contain a crowded microscopic world washed from the moss and silt, though to the eye it is only rainwater reflecting the slot of sky.

The moss itself is old in a way its freshness conceals. The green surface is young, but beneath it lie years of compacted growth. Some cushions have thickened around projections of stone rather than spread beyond them, making the same small mound larger by increments. The ferns, too, are less numerous than their fronds suggest. Several may rise from one old rootstock following a fracture, dying back and returning without ever successfully colonizing the adjacent bare rock. Spores fall on the grit often enough. Almost none find the combination of water and shelter that would let them become another fern.

In the black silt and the sheltered grit behind the roof fall, blind springtails graze on fungal threads and organic dust. They are the commonest animals in the hollow, though a handful of floor may appear to contain none. They avoid the exposed surface except when it is thoroughly wet. Their lives are brief compared with the moss, but their numbers can never rise far: there is only so much film to eat, and the current regularly removes what little additional food arrives.

The dark beetle beneath the trough stone belongs to the same sparse economy. It is wingless, slow for a beetle, and strongly flattened. Flight would be of little use to it in the hollow; its territory is the connected shelter of cracks, stones, and deep silt. It hunts springtails and other small animals by contact and scent, sometimes remaining under one stone for days. The trough was not made for it, but the stone’s patient hollowing and the water that collects there have made its shelter slightly more dependable. The beetle is one of the few lives in the place that human hands have indirectly improved.

The wren and the fox belong to the moor rather than the hollow. The wren works the brighter rim, where insects are more numerous and escape into cover is easy. It does not need to enter the roaring room. The fox investigates the opening because foxes investigate openings; the prints end where its interest ends. Neither leaves enough behind to become part of the place.

Nor did the people who cut the steps. Their crescent of stone, their trough, and their pole sockets describe waiting or brief shelter, not residence. Yet their works have acquired small ecological afterlives. The crescent interrupts the current by a hand’s breadth. Moss has taken that margin. The fallen timber holds moisture against the rock and is threaded through with fungus. The trough prolongs one wet edge after the seep elsewhere has diminished. Whoever the visitors were, and whatever they came to do, this is their continuing occupation of the hollow: three modest alterations, each now used by something they probably never noticed.

When the roar fails, there is no general emergence. Nothing hidden comes forth to claim the silence. The snail may continue grazing without any alteration at all. The moss responds, if at all, to the changing dampness of the air. The springtails remain in their silt.

The beetle is more likely to change its behavior. With the current slack, scents persist instead of being stripped immediately from the stone. Small movements are no longer carried away in the same relentless flow. It may leave the trough and range a little farther along the wall. For it, the important event is not that sound has returned to the world, but that the air has briefly stopped erasing local differences.

A person hears the drop in the trough, the click of water in a crack, the fern stem against limestone. The inhabitants experience something less audible and more useful: still air, lingering scent, a temporary stability at the surface.

Then the current resumes. To the visitor, the hollow becomes overwhelming and apparently empty again. In fact, nothing has withdrawn. The snail is still within its seam. The springtails are still beneath the grit. The old green cushions continue their almost imperceptible thickening, and the beetle waits under stone.

The hollow is not secretly full. It is inhabited in exact proportion to its gifts: a little reliable water, a little shelter, a little food. The roar fills the room. Life never has.
