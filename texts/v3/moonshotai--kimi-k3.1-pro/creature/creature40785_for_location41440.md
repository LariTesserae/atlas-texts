Most of what lives in the Serein Uplands is neither quite dormant nor quite active. It lives by intervals. A rain beetle may spend years as a hard egg in the dust and an afternoon drinking, mating, and laying eggs of its own. A cushion plant may resemble lichen for several seasons, then put out a single shoot after a shower that wets no more than a few square metres. Even the larger animals organise their lives around things absent or not yet arrived: water beyond the next ridge, shade that will reach a ledge at noon, a carcass whose location is still only a disturbance in the flight of birds.

Consequently, the population of a basin is difficult to census. It can increase a thousandfold between dawn and dusk without anything crossing the rim.

The first rain beetles to emerge are not necessarily the first to have hatched. Some wait beneath the crust until the surface has begun to dry, when the larger animals are already trampling the wettest ground. Their dark backs are excellent heat collectors in the cool minutes after serein; later, when the stone becomes dangerous to touch, they retreat into cracks or burrow beneath their own emptied shells. They do not commit an entire generation to one shower. Some eggs hatch after a brief wetting, others only after water has stood in mud, and others remain unchanged through several rains. A false shower, or one that evaporates overhead, can therefore cause great activity without extinguishing the life still held in reserve.

Smaller hunters inhabit the same brief abundance. Thread-legged larvae move through the microbial films on wet gravel. Flat, pallid things no larger than dust grains gather beneath beetle wings. The rust-red filaments of the rain-flush are grazed almost as soon as they unfold, while the translucent scales close at a shadow, a footfall, or sometimes only the passage of hot breath. By the second day, much of this life has already become eggs, spores, husks, or food. What remains visible is a bronze stain and an unusual number of tracks.

Wireleaf and the seep-crowned trees represent the opposite strategy. They are not brief. A wireleaf plant small enough to fit in a hand may be older than the wayhouse, its visible stems repeatedly replaced while the root persists deep in a fissure. The solitary trees grow with similar slowness. Their muscular trunks are less often twisted by wind than by old breaks, uneven growth, and the gradual movement of roots around buried stone. A large tree occupies a hidden fracture system far more extensive than its crown.

Its shade is inhabited as carefully as its foliage. At noon a slateback may stand beside the trunk and move a few steps every hour, keeping within the narrow shadow. Small winged animals spend the bright part of the day beneath the bark. On still nights the leaves cool enough to gather a little condensation, much of which falls back into the tree’s own root cleft. The tree thus makes a minute climate around itself. It does not create a seep, but it can make an unreliable one last longer.

The slatebacks bind these separated lives together.

They can go without drinking for considerable periods, but not indefinitely, and they are not independent of water in the manner sometimes claimed by travellers from below. They take moisture from rain-flushes, browse, mud, and the damp interiors of clefts. Their broad muzzles can press into places where a person would see only stained stone. They know such locations individually and, in some sense, collectively. A calf follows its mother at first, but over several years it also follows older animals that are not related to it, learning which covered basins deserve investigation and which routes merely look promising.

A herd has no consistent leader. Its oldest members possess different portions of the circuit, and the animal that others follow toward one seep may be ignored at the next crossing. Their wide spacing is partly a response to sparse food and partly a way of preserving information. Animals at the edge of a herd often see or smell things that those near its centre would miss. When alarm passes through them, it rarely begins with a sound. One slateback raises its head, closes its ears, and stamps. Others feel the impact through their feet before they have identified its cause. The herd appears to change its mind in several places at once.

Calves recognise the particular cadence of their mothers’ steps. Adults use slower, heavier signals to gather before crossing exposed ground. At a distance, the result looks less like communication than a series of unrelated decisions.

Their interest in the rain devices is ancient and uncomplicated. A device is smooth, immovable, and excellent for rubbing loose skin from a flank. Some are used by generation after generation for this purpose alone. Slatebacks do not gather whenever one wakes; often they are kilometres away. Yet their trails converge on the devices because trails converge on everything durable in the uplands: a slab that gives shade, a sill that can be crossed without loose gravel, a pin beside which the ground has already been worn smooth.

Slatebacks also redistribute the consequences of rain. Seeds and spores adhere to their legs, pass through their guts, or become lodged in the loose folds of their skin. A flush cropped in one basin may contribute to a faint line of growth beside a resting hollow several days’ travel away. They cannot be herded efficiently. A person attempting to drive ten animals discovers that the rest of the herd is beyond sight and has no intention of following. Slatebacks can be accompanied. They cannot conveniently be possessed.

The kites make another kind of circuit above them. Only their wings are truly translucent; their bodies are narrow and dark, and at height they often appear as brief flaws in the light rather than as birds. Their gathering before a device wakes is not prophecy. They respond to slight changes high in the air, and then to one another. A single kite turning over bare stone means little. Thirty arriving from separate thermals usually mean that something is already happening above the visible rain.

Usually is an important word. Kites sometimes gather, wheel for an hour, and disperse beneath a sky that remains empty.

When insects rise, the kites feed without crying or colliding, each bird dropping through a different portion of the glittering column. The arrival of a shearwing changes the geometry at once. The larger hunter does not need to stoop to empty part of the sky. Kites open a corridor around its probable path, continuing to feed on either side. Many passes end without a kill. The shearwing, like everything else here, must be able to afford failure.

The pale slab-backed ground hunter has made failure into a way of conserving energy. It may occupy one ambush for days, then abandon it shortly before a herd arrives. It rarely waits directly beside water. More often it lies one terrace back, along a route used by animals that have already drunk and lowered their guard. Its stillness is not perfect camouflage; at close range it is plainly an animal. But at close range it has usually already decided whether to move.

Its hunts leave little evidence beyond a short disturbance of gravel, a drag mark, or a patch of dust that remains warm after the body has cooled. Scavengers find the site by watching other scavengers as much as by scent. Small ground-runners arrive first, then gliding animals that have seen their movement from several ridges away. Beetles complete the reduction. A kill becomes a succession of temporary residences.

The nest below the wayhouse cistern belongs to one of the uplands’ smallest persistent residents. Cistern-nesters are compact, warm-blooded animals with furred feet and powerful hind legs. They can obtain most of their moisture from seeds, larvae, and the occasional beetle, and they plug their entrances against the worst of the daytime heat. The animal beneath the wayhouse has incorporated slateback hair, dry filaments, and fragments of old nest material into its own shelter. When people sleep above, it generally remains quiet until they do. Its alarm signal is a rapid stamping felt more easily through the floor than heard in the air.

People are among the least settled inhabitants.

There are upland-born families, guides, gatherers, and solitary travellers who spend most of their lives beyond the escarpment, but no permanent villages and no single hearth around which their society gathers. Lower-country accounts call them rain-readers. They seldom use the term themselves. It suggests both more control and more certainty than they possess.

Their camps are small and reversible. A fitted windbreak may be used for one night and then left empty for a year. Their most important technologies are covers: lids for vessels, stone caps for basins, cloth for shading skin, and plugs for keeping blowing sand out of narrow clefts. They do not dig aggressively for water. Widening a damp seam may yield a few mouthfuls while destroying the cool, protected surface that made moisture collect there.

Their maps are largely sequences of judgments. From the seven steps, take the eastern crossing if the slatebacks have already passed; do not cross if the kites are gathering beyond it, because the visible rain may be farther away than it appears. At the three-quarter ring, examine the mud but do not assume the covered basin is full. If the wayhouse doorway is dark at sunset, look first for the pale hunter on the southern approach.

Some device names preserve old errors: Wrong Kites, Late Water, Three Days Rubbing. A cairn beside a device is understood to mean that a visitor once felt the need to mark it. It is not taken as evidence of water.

The rain-readers have no ceremony for petitioning the devices. They may admire them, fear them slightly, or speculate about them at night, but nothing in their practical behaviour suggests negotiation. If they possess a ritual obligation, it is simpler: water that has been found covered should be left covered. A person who replaces a stone lid carefully is not thinking only of the next human traveller. Slatebacks, cistern-nesters, and smaller animals also benefit from a little less evaporation.

At dusk, when several groups happen to share a shelter, their most valuable exchange is not news of kin or trade but a recitation of conditions. Which basin held water. Which flush was already cropped. Where a shearwing had been seen. Whether the beetles at one seep had emerged in numbers or only in a brief, unsuccessful scattering. Such information is never treated as a forecast, only as material from which a forecast might be made.

A child raised in the uplands learns early that a route is not a promise. It also learns that visible rain may be farther away than a day’s walking, that an empty device can remain empty for a lifetime, and that a herd moving with apparent purpose may simply be following an animal that has remembered wrongly. This does not produce passivity. It produces a particular kind of attentiveness.

For the devices have become part of the ecology without becoming understood. Slatebacks polish their bases. Kites use the altered air above them. Plants occupy the brief wetness they create, and people name them according to the successes and failures of animals. They have the ecological importance of ancient trees, though none corrodes and none shows whether it will ever wake again.

On a single evening, the inhabitants of neighbouring basins may be living in entirely different phases of rain. In one, beetles are already dying on dry stone. In another, translucent scales have just begun to open. Slatebacks are walking toward a flush they may reach too late; the pale hunter is leaving a ledge that has ceased to be useful; and beneath the wayhouse floor, the cistern-nester is pushing sand from its door. Far off, kites gather under cloudless sky.

Nothing has solved the problem of living there. The beetles hedge against uncertain rain, the trees against depth and time, the herds against distance, the hunters against hunger, and the people against their own confidence. Between them, they have made uncertainty into a kind of shelter.
