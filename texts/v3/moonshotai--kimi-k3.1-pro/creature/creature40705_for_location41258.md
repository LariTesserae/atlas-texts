## The inhabitants of the Pale Crown

No people live in the Pale Crown. Nothing there builds a dwelling or keeps a boundary. Shelter is grown, found in the hollow of a dead stalk, or inherited as a pore in the root felt. The gardens are its settled populations. The veilbacks are its only habitual travelers across the whole continent. Between them lives a great deal that is too small, too slow, or too dormant to announce itself.

A garden is older than any plant visible in it. Plume stalks rise and unweave; cupfans replace their vanes; whole generations of silver-backed felt spread and thin. The root net persists by continual revision. It is not necessarily one organism. In some places living roots join and exchange mineral-rich oils; elsewhere they remain distinct, competing grain by grain and pore by pore. The apparent unity of a garden before a dust front comes from shared sensitivities to pressure, light, and electrical charge, not from a central will.

Deep within the felt, the air is not trapped, exactly, but made to linger. Its countless narrow passages hold useful gases a little longer than the open basin does. Changes in temperature draw thin air down through the leaves and force it out again. Microscopic films inhabit the oil-coated crevices between root and grain, etching reactive minerals from the dust and slowly working through dead fibers. Nothing there rots wetly. Recycling is nearly as gradual as erosion, and the oldest felt contains material that has passed through several kinds of life without ever becoming loose soil.

A new garden begins by preventing a very small amount of dust from moving.

A spore that survives its landing puts out filaments before it produces anything resembling a leaf. For years, its entire visible achievement may be a cluster of dark grains that does not shift when the surrounding pavement is combed clean. If that anchorage survives enough seasons, a low scale of felt appears. More dust gathers within it. Only then can the cupfans establish themselves, and the tall plumes may not follow for centuries. They are not inevitable. Some hollows remain gardens of felt and fan for their whole existence, complete without ever producing a stalk.

The visible plants divide the work of survival. The cupfans take light, but they also comb dust from the moving air. Their narrow vanes slow it just enough that reactive grains settle near their bases. Older vanes become mineral-laden, stiffen, and are shed into the roots, where the films and gleaners take them apart. The plume crowns are more sensitive to the sky. Their bundled surfaces respond separately to charge and illumination, so a crown opens unevenly and seems to deliberate. Mature spores leave the stalk with a sealed droplet of oil and a few cells of the garden’s root films. Each carries, in miniature, not merely an embryo but the beginning of a method for living.

Most animals in a garden could be mistaken for its debris.

The mineral-shelled gleaners are low, many-legged ovals whose overlapping plates take their color from local dust. In one basin they are porcelain-pale; in another, almost black. They graze along dead fibers and fine roots, consuming chemical films, discarded leaf skins, and the amber coatings shed from old wounds. Their movement helps keep the smallest passages in the felt from sealing. A handful of root architecture may contain hundreds of them, though on a still day not one needs to be visible.

The spore eaters live deeper and do less, for longer. Their soft, lens-shaped bodies can flatten until they are scarcely distinguishable from the fibers around them. They may remain inactive through several releases if conditions are wrong. When they finally emerge, they follow the trace of fresh spore oil through the felt. Not every spore they consume is destroyed. Some lose only their resistant outer layers and pass onward as stripped kernels, more ready to germinate than they were before. Thus the brief feast after a release also becomes part of the garden’s redistribution of itself.

The flattened hunters attend that feast without joining its hurry. They lie between fibers with their edges spread, resembling flakes of shale or pieces of an old, colorless leaf. Movement does not alert them, and warmth interests them little. They wait for the particular chemical disturbance made by a body pushing through oil-coated roots. Their response is one swift contraction, sometimes after years of stillness. Then the felt closes over the place where it happened.

Nothing large flies. For animals, the air is weather rather than habitat. Communication travels mainly through residues, touch, and minute vibrations in the roots. There are no territories announced across a basin and no gatherings that could be called herds. Even death is quiet. A small animal’s body stiffens into a waxy husk; gleaners reduce it over seasons, and its mineral plates remain in the felt long after its softer chemistry has been taken back.

The veilbacks belong to another scale entirely.

An adult’s articulated body may be longer than a plume stalk is tall, though it moves so low that often only the crest shows above the fans. Its weight is spread through many short, deliberate limbs. In a garden it seems to pass without steps, each segment following the last through a path of least resistance. At rest on dark pavement, dust quickly dulls its outline until it resembles a seam of stone.

The crest is neither ornament nor weapon. Turned toward the early light, it gathers enough warmth to keep the animal’s internal oils mobile; the muted red veins carry that warmth inward. Along its edge are organs sensitive to electrical charge. A veilback may fold the crest while the horizon is still clear, long before the first dust reaches it. During the worst weather it presses its whole length against the ground and lets the storm pass over a narrow, dark ridge.

Within a garden, it browses with restraint imposed by its own body and by the plants’ changing chemistry. The outer vanes are richest and most accessible. After they are cropped, the injured fan alters its oils, becoming progressively less rewarding. The veilback moves on before it reaches the crown or tears the felt. Its passage opens narrow lanes through the thicket, admitting light and dust to places that may have been shaded for decades. A single visit can therefore influence the shape of a garden long after the tracks have vanished.

Between gardens, the animal becomes almost as dormant as the life in the dust. It can fast for extraordinary periods, waking to travel when temperature, charge, and wind are favorable. It finds distant vegetation by following chemical traces too dilute to nourish anything: garden gases and frayed spore material carried far into the bare intervals. If weather closes around it, it folds its crest and waits. Dust may cover all but a few segments.

A veilback leaving a garden carries a minute sample of it away. Spores lodge in the articulations of its body. Root films persist on its feet. Dormant gleaners sometimes shelter beneath its plates. Most of these passengers die during the crossing, but enough survive to make widely separated gardens less than absolutely separate. For some of the Crown’s smallest inhabitants, the body of a veilback is the only bridge they will ever have between basins.

The animals are not solitary by preference so much as by arithmetic. When two meet, they may remain together for a season, often on bare ground, exchanging chemical information through touch and moving with their crests folded close. Then their tracks separate. Their young are few and widely spaced. At first they are small enough to live beneath the felt, feeding on spores and minute animals; the crest remains a soft, translucent fold along the spine. By the time a young veilback begins to browse, it has already acquired something of its natal garden’s mineral character.

Isolation gives every garden particulars. In one, the fan vanes grow in nearly perfect spirals; in another, they are straight and irregularly forked. Some plumes release at the first fall of electrical charge after a storm, while others wait through several calms. There are gardens without flattened hunters, gardens whose spore eaters almost never reach the surface, and gardens that have not been visited by a veilback in so long that their outer fans form a dense, living rim. These are not stages toward some richer norm. They are local histories still in progress.

The bare intervals are not secretly lush. Much of the dust is sterile, and most dormant life scattered across it will never wake. Dormancy is common, but genuine absence is common too. Both matter. A spore can cross a hundred empty hollows without finding a place to begin; a veilback can spend much of its life where nothing visible responds to its passage.

Life in the Crown has therefore put distance inside its own cycles: the fast between gardens, the hunter’s motionless year, the centuries in which a root net does little more than keep one small hollow of dust from blowing away. A plume may spend a lifetime preparing for an afternoon of release. A veilback may cross a whole bright basin between meals. None of them is incomplete while it waits.

Then, somewhere, a front passes. The plumes open, spores darken the air, and the felt stirs with hundreds of small bodies. For a few hours the garden is almost busy. Afterward the fibers settle, the hunters become still, and the silence returns—not as inactivity, but as the ordinary pace of nearly everything alive there.
