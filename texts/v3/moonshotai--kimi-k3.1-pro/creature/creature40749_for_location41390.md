## Residents of the High Moss

The High Moss is sparsely inhabited even where it is densely alive. Almost nothing makes a territory of the whole basin. A resident occupies a condition: water that remains unfrozen slightly longer than the water around it, a root mat deep enough to survive a dry week, a particular chemical gradient in a warm runnel, the lee of a steel shed. A creature may be abundant in one square metre and absent from the next, though both look identical from standing height.

The green cover is both population and habitat. A firm hummock may contain several mosses growing through one another, their living tips only a few centimetres above layers of dead and compressed stems. The dead material is not merely waste. It holds water, shelters eggs, slows frost, and gives fungi and bacteria somewhere to work. Decay is so gradual in the cold, waterlogged depths that the dead often become ground before they become food. Sedges and cotton grass add their own architecture, raising root platforms, trapping fine ash, and turning what would have been a draining pool into a saturated terrace.

Almost nothing grazes this growth hard enough to shape it. There are no teeth keeping the moss short, no herds opening paths through the sedges. The boundaries of the plant communities are drawn mostly by water, frost, mineral chemistry, and the plants’ own slow alterations of those conditions.

The rosette plants are the closest thing the basin has to inhabitants with conspicuous individual biographies. One may occupy the same slight rise for longer than a nearby pipe run is expected to remain in service. For most of its life it does almost nothing that can be watched: a leaf added, another lost, the rosette widening by an amount only repeated measurement would reveal. Field crews sometimes call the flowering plants **candles**, because of the thick stalks that suddenly stand above the mat.

That flowering is not necessarily a triumph followed by a multitude of seedlings. Pollinators are few and irregular. A plant may spend decades accumulating the means to flower and then do so in a cold, windy week when almost nothing is flying. Seed set varies greatly. Even an unsuccessful stalk remains useful, however. Mites shelter in its drying bracts, small spiders attach lines within its hollow stem, and water collects where the stalk begins to rot. When it finally falls, the surrounding mat takes years to close over it.

At the scale of a water film, the moss has a considerable animal population. Rotifers, tardigrades, nematodes, mites, and springtails live among the wet leaves and decaying stems. Many spend much of the year inactive—dried, frozen, or simply waiting in water too cold for rapid growth. Rain does not merely refresh them; it begins an interval of active life that may last only hours. A bright drop carried on a moss leaf can contain feeding, reproduction, and predation, none of it visible to someone walking past.

Among the largest animals that can fairly be called year-round residents are the **black pool beetles**, though even these are shorter than a fingernail. They are flattened, slow to expose themselves, and usually found beneath overhanging roots rather than crossing the open middle of a pool. Their larvae may require more than one short summer to develop. They scavenge drowned insects and take smaller aquatic animals when they can.

Their distribution has no obvious tidy pattern. Two pools of the same size and temperature may lie within sight of one another, one containing beetles and the other apparently empty. Some meres also support tiny seed shrimps and other crustaceans, whose dormant eggs can remain in damp organic matter when a pool shrinks or changes its outlet. A pool may therefore seem lifeless for a long interval and then, after sustained rain, contain a brief crowd of minute activity. Its inhabitants have not necessarily arrived from anywhere visible.

The warmer-season insect hatches are equally local. Midges rise where the air is sheltered, often remaining so close to the mat that a person standing upright looks down through apparently empty air. Their adult lives are brief. The small spiders of the basin do not usually risk broad webs in the open wind; they hunt beneath cotton-grass heads, among dead rosette leaves, or within the little hollows where stems cross. Most of the animal economy is detrital. It runs on dead plant tissue, drowned insects, fine organic matter in pool water, and the occasional concentrated opportunity rather than on continuous abundance.

The geothermal outlets support a separate ecology. The clearest, hottest water is not the richest. Life becomes more evident along the cooling margins, where thin olive, grey, or rust-coloured films establish themselves within the mineral deposits. Some of the microorganisms depend ultimately on sunlight and organic material washed in from the mat. Others use dissolved gases and minerals, drawing energy from chemical reactions that require no green surface at all. Their continuity lies as much in the wet fractures below ground as in the visible runnel.

Each outlet is chemically particular. A film flourishing beside one may never appear beside another a few hundred metres away. Nor does warmth reliably create an oasis. Water that is comfortably warm at one point may be scalding, excessively mineralized, or starved of suitable nutrients a short distance upstream. When an outlet shifts, a community can be cooked, chilled, dried, or buried under fresh silica. The basin’s warmest places are not necessarily its most crowded.

The industrial works add further narrow habitats without amounting to a second living world. Condensation dripping from cold cladding sustains a brighter stripe of moss beneath one pipe and not beneath its neighbour. A separator shed provides enough shelter for midges to gather in its lee. Moss begins on a bracket where paint has failed and water remains. In cooled condensate traps and other sufficiently temperate parts of the system, microbial films establish themselves until maintenance removes them. To the crews, such growth is fouling or corrosion risk. Ecologically, it is simply opportunistic occupation of another temporary seam.

No native animal has had time to evolve specifically for the industrial roar. The machinery has instead made the basin’s existing quietness advantageous. Life there was already conducted largely at close range, through scent, contact, substrate vibration, and brief visual encounters. Song and long-distance calls were never the basis of a busy animal community. Now even the sounds that do occur are frequently erased.

The dark bird seen crossing the basin is most often a raven from the broken country around the rim. It is a visitor with a much larger range, not a creature sustained by the High Moss alone. It may land on a gauge post, investigate something newly painted, or pick through an insect hatch in the shelter of a shed. Then it drops behind a pressure ridge and seems to vanish. Its call, if it gives one, usually goes unheard. No larger animal makes a dependable daily round of the meres.

Human beings, too, are visitors even when they spend much of their working lives returning. Well-test crews, fitters, instrument technicians, and hydrologists sleep beside the turbine hall or below the rim and come onto the plateau for particular tasks. There are no households out among the wellheads. The nearest thing to a permanent human population is a continuity of practical knowledge passed between people who keep coming back.

That knowledge is bodily as much as documentary. Experienced workers arrange their movements before entering the loudest ground, because speech there will be useless. They do not rely on hearing a vehicle or a shouted warning. In luminous fog they travel between known points rather than toward shapes that appear familiar. They learn to treat greenness as an unresolved question: old cushion, floating roots, flooded hollow, or warmed crust? The official drawings give equipment numbers and pipe routes. The ground acquires unofficial names—the Dead Candle, Brown Steps, Three Saddles—some of which outlast the workers who coined them and others of which disappear with a single crew.

Not everyone who acquires this knowledge becomes fond of the place. The noise follows people into sleep; wet clothing chafes; the same light that makes the moss radiant burns exposed skin. A worker can know the field intimately without ever feeling welcome in it. Others become attached to precisely those things that make casual visits unpleasant: the scale, the absence of unnecessary movement, the privilege of noticing an event almost no one else will see. A technician who happens to pass a rosette during its only flowering may be the sole human witness.

People also alter the lives of the other residents. Wells change pressures and paths of flow. A track interrupts a seep. Cleaning removes a film; a new discharge creates one somewhere else; a warm outlet diminishes and its margin cools. These effects do not appear on the drawings as inhabited consequences, but they are consequences all the same. The crews are not outside the ecology, merely working at a different speed and with narrower intentions.

If the machines were eventually silenced, the first change noticed from the rim would be acoustic. For the inhabitants, the more important changes might come later and underground. Some warm films would vanish. Certain seepages would return or fail to return. Moss would continue over brackets, the beetles would remain patchily distributed, and the raven would still have reason to cross. The rosettes would flower or fail according to schedules unrelated to abandonment.

No single creature summarizes the High Moss. The raven belongs to the rim and visits; the worker belongs to a rotation and returns; the beetle belongs to some pools but not others; the seed shrimp belongs to water that may cease to stand; the microbial film belongs to a temperature and a flow of dissolved chemistry; the moss belongs to ground it is still making.

On a bright afternoon, a valve receives fresh paint, a drying flower stalk loses another fibre, and somewhere beneath the roar a beetle moves along the underside of a root ledge. In a drop of rainwater, a rotifer unfolds and begins to feed. None of these events carries as far as the steam. Most of the basin’s life never needed to.
