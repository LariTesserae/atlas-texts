Most of what lives in the Silt Sea is not, at any given moment, visibly alive. It is a cell halted between plates of clay, a filament waiting for seep water to return, an egg using its provisions so slowly that development scarcely seems to be occurring. Even the active life often resembles the basin itself: a discoloration in sediment, a slight toughness in silt, an organic thread passing through the gut of something transparent.

Nothing here lives by light. Every food chain begins with something that arrived or something that leaked.

The suspended matter supports the most widespread communities. A drifting floc may carry bacteria along its mineral surfaces, a different population within its organic core, and still others consuming the waste of the first two. Its chemistry changes as it sinks, breaks apart, or joins another aggregate. For a while it is an island. Then it becomes part of a larger floc, or settles into the plain, and the island ceases to exist without any of its inhabitants necessarily having died.

Other microbial cells lie dispersed through the upper sediment. Many are almost inactive. They persist through long intervals of scarcity, then resume metabolism when a carcass enriches the clay, a current exposes an older organic layer, or seep water shifts a few metres sideways. A handful of silt may contain more living individuals than an entire stretch of plain contains animals, though nearly all of them are doing very little.

The seeps support the nearest thing to settled communities. There are no chimneys or gardens around them. Often there is only a broad stain where water from below meets the water of the basin, bringing reduced minerals into reach of microbes that can use them. Sulfur-colored patches form around the stronger flows. Elsewhere, colorless filaments make a delicate felt over the clay, holding themselves within a chemical interval too narrow for an animal to detect as a place.

These communities live on differences rather than abundance. They need both the seep water and the water above it; too much burial cuts them off, while too strong a current carries them away. Their edges therefore move continually. A patch may be brightest where the mat is being torn, because exposed filaments encounter fresh water there. Another may thrive beneath a skin of sediment so thin that the surface still appears bare.

Small grazers work across the films, taking individual cells and leaving the mineral skeleton of the mat almost unchanged. There are also paper-shelled bivalves partly buried near some seeps. Their shells are thin, their soft bodies thinner still. Many harbor microbes in their gills, giving those microbes access to both seep chemistry and basin water and receiving, in return, part of what the microbes produce. A bivalve can thus remain in one place for a very long time without requiring much food to arrive. Its shell may be old even where the sediment around it looks newly laid.

The brine hollows are less generous. Their strongest brines contain only specialists, and even those seem more often to endure than to flourish. Most animals cannot remain within them. The richer life gathers near the boundaries, where mixing supplies nutrients and dilutes the salt just enough. There, discontinuous veils of microbes form and fail without ever becoming true mats. They are too dense to be mere plankton and too unstable to be a floor.

The animals most likely to be encountered away from the sediment are small transparent crustaceans. Some are flattened and quick in short bursts; others are narrow, almost threadlike, with antennae many times the length of their bodies. Their transparency is not primarily camouflage. There is little light in which to hide and almost nothing watching from a distance. It is simply the appearance of bodies built without surplus pigment or armor. When one has fed well, its gut may be the only colored part of it: a gray, rust-brown, or nearly black thread moving inside clear tissue.

None possesses a gas-filled buoyancy organ. They float by being nearly water themselves, by oils stored after feeding, and by making small corrections to their posture. They seldom attempt to swim across the basin. Instead, they move between layers of water. A few strokes can take them into a current with a different direction, or into denser water where sinking becomes slow enough to resemble rest. Their geography is therefore largely vertical. To them, a salinity gradient can be more important than any valley on the floor.

They feed by combing aggregates, shredding organic threads, and taking microbes from surfaces too small for a larger animal to use. Much of what they ingest is mineral and passes through unchanged. Their molted skins drift until colonized, soften, and become food in turn.

Reproduction is correspondingly restrained. Rather than releasing clouds of poorly provisioned young, many of the crustaceans carry a few eggs for long periods. The young emerge relatively large and capable, already able to feed on fine matter. A substantial meal may determine whether a brood develops at all. There is no spring to summon it, only the chemical fact of enough food.

Still rarer are gelatinous drifters that feed on the crustaceans. Some resemble irregular sheets; others trail soft lobes or adhesive filaments. They do not pursue prey through open water. They extend themselves where smaller animals are already concentrated and wait for collisions. When food disappears, they contract, drift, and survive with very little movement. A large one may contain nothing more substantial than a few recent meals and the water held within its tissues.

The rare blue light belongs to this drifting world. It is usually associated with attack or rough handling rather than courtship: a brief discharge from a small animal seized, struck, or tumbled too violently through a shear layer. For an instant it reveals transparent limbs, several grains of silt, and perhaps the enclosing lobe of a predator. Then it is gone. No answering lights appear. Whatever advantage the flash gives must work without an audience, and often without anything present capable of seeing it.

Within the floor, the worms follow a different economy. Some continuously swallow clay and extract the sparse organic coating from its grains. Their passages close behind them; they leave no durable burrows. Others remain nearly motionless until dissolved organic matter reaches them. These are the animals that can make a stretch of apparently empty sediment suddenly seem populated.

They are pale, soft, and variously threadlike or flattened. A few extend only their mouths above the sediment. Others emerge completely when food is abundant, risking the current because remaining buried would mean missing an event that may not recur within their lifetime. Many can delay reproduction during scarcity. After a rich arrival, their bodies change rapidly by local standards: eggs mature, feeding intensifies, and young are produced while the food supply still lasts.

A substantial carcass brings all these otherwise separate habits together.

The first response is microbial and begins before the body has fully settled. Decomposition alters the water around it; fine organic matter spreads downstream and enters the sediment. Worms move toward the enriched layer. Transparent crustaceans accumulate where fragments are suspended. Grazers that normally take individual cells from seep films feed directly on the softer tissues, while other microbes establish sulfur-stained patches in the darkening clay nearby.

For a time, the carcass supports more activity than many square kilometres of plain. It is not merely eaten. It is translated—into microbial cells, scavenger tissue, eggs, drifting larvae, and fine particles carried away by the current. Some of the worms reproduce for the first time in years. Crustaceans that encounter the remains may leave with enough stored material to complete a brood elsewhere. Even animals that never find the carcass can descend from it.

The soft hunter sometimes arrives late. It feeds less on the original body than on the scavengers gathered around it.

This hunter is the basin’s largest regularly active inhabitant, though it is neither swift nor conspicuously powerful. It has a low, boneless body and a circlet of muscular arms that can spread into a broad star or gather into a narrow crawling point. At rest it presses itself close to the sediment until its outline nearly disappears. Its upper surface is pale and granular; its lower surface is acutely sensitive to texture, vibration, and dissolved chemistry. It can distinguish a shell seam from a grain of sediment and detect the faint, repeated water movement of a buried bivalve.

Any vision it retains is of little use beyond arm’s reach. It hunts chiefly by touch and taste.

Usually it moves slowly enough that settling silt keeps pace with it. It may remain still for long stretches, then cross a patch of plain in a series of economical crawls and brief pulses. It has no enduring den. A resting hollow is only the impression of its body, and the next sediment current smooths it away.

Its occasional use of objects arises from the practical difficulty of feeding. A bivalve may clamp shut while its shell slides in the current. A piece of carrion may turn continually as the hunter tries to tear it. The animal needs resistance, and the basin offers almost none.

Sometimes it finds that resistance in a stone.

It may pin one valve beneath the stone while working at the other, or use a shell fragment to hold food against the flow. The object is not shaped. It is often simply the nearest thing with sufficient mass, though an individual may carry one for a short distance when nothing suitable lies nearby. Once feeding ends, the hunter releases it. There is no cache, no favored anvil, no accumulation of shells marking a feeding place.

Nor does every encounter with the same problem produce the same solution. Some meals involve no object at all. The behavior has the clear form of an improvisation, and an individual may become more adept with practice, but a solution can be reinvented without becoming a tradition. The young begin life without instruction, and meetings between adults are too infrequent to support anything resembling a shared craft.

Even the hunter’s body leaves little behind. When it dies, it becomes another soft carcass, another temporary province. Its most deliberate act may have been to place one stone upon another object for the duration of a meal.

When the basin’s weather arrives, none of these inhabitants treats it as weather. There is only a change in pressure, an acceleration, a thickening of the water with sediment. Microbial films lift in ribbons. Crustaceans fold their appendages and are carried into layers they did not choose. Worms are buried more deeply or drawn out with the floor’s upper skin. Brine-edge communities shear apart. The soft hunter flattens itself and grips what it can; if it was feeding, stone and shell are abandoned immediately.

Afterward, some communities have been dispersed rather than destroyed. Filaments lodged in new sediment may encounter suitable chemistry. Dormant cells may be uncovered. Animals may find themselves far from where they began, with no broad landmark by which to return and no particular need to do so. Recolonization does not require a memory of place. It requires only that some living fragment arrive where persistence is possible.

The inhabitants of the Silt Sea thus form no continuous occupation of it. They are brief intensifications: a chemical stain at a seep, transparent bodies along a density boundary, worms gathering beneath a fall of organic matter, a crowd assembling around death, a boneless animal pausing with a stone in its arms. Between these events, life remains present but diluted almost to absence.

When the water clears enough for the plain to be plain again, a few filaments may already be extending at the edge of a seep. Somewhere a crustacean is carrying a brood through water that tastes faintly of a carcass it will never find. Worms are moving beneath sediment that appears untouched. And somewhere, perhaps, the soft hunter sets a stone on a shell, feeds, and lets both fall.

The next current takes away the evidence that anything with intentions was ever there.
