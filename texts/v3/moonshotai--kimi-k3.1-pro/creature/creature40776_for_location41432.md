## What Lives in the High Choir

The High Choir’s inhabitants are best understood by where they wait.

The visible animals are only those whose waiting has been interrupted. At noon a traveler may cross an entire basin and see nothing larger than a flier, yet be walking over layered galleries, crowded water bladders, dormant eggs, and herds close enough beneath the pelt to touch. The plateau itself may be the oldest and largest resident, though no one knows whether to count it once or many times. The animals are less ambiguous. They feed on it, wound it, carry its seeds, open its water vessels, and sometimes close them again.

Beneath the leaf-scales live the glassbacks, as some camps call them: clear, many-legged grazers ranging from grains of rice to the size of a small fingernail. A green or amber line of food shows through each body. They crop the thin films and tender cuticle beneath the pelt without usually breaking its weathered surface. When a herd turns, the light turns with it. This is why a wave can cross the leaves against the wind, and why a patch of apparently still ground sometimes seems to be flowing slowly downhill.

Button insects sit above them on warm stems, clamped so tightly that they resemble seeds until disturbed. Some feed for days without moving. When a shadow or footstep alarms them, they release their hold and drop into the fiber, where they lie with their legs folded and become almost impossible to distinguish from vegetable debris. Their hunters are correspondingly flat. The leaf-soles press themselves against roots and stems with only their sensory bristles raised. They can remain motionless through an entire passage of grazers, then move once, quickly, and be flat again before the herd has flowed around them.

The galleries below are more crowded still. Their inhabitants are arranged by depth, moisture, and tolerance for heat. Tough-skinned larvae occupy the dry outer cork; softer, pale animals live deeper, feeding on loosened fiber, sap, dead insects, or one another. Some enlarge the narrow passages. Others pack them with frass and shed skins until they become warm, humid chambers. A burrow that seems abandoned may contain several generations waiting at different depths. Rain brings the upper layers into furious activity. Drought drives the survivors downward.

A footstep in such country is not merely a footstep. It compresses nests, breaks water films, collapses passages, and alarms animals several paces away. Usually the traveler sees only a shiver.

Every cistern is a small country with its own population. In a cup-sized hollow there may be threadlike larvae around the margin, translucent swimmers in the center, and one hunting nymph clinging motionless below the waterline. Larger crown cisterns contain layers of life: surface skaters, divers that carry bright bubbles against their bodies, brood chambers in the walls, and scavengers working through the black sediment at the bottom. Some animals survive the dry season as flakes so thin and brittle that they appear to be old leaf skin. Restored to water, they thicken, unfold, and resume feeding.

Other species cannot endure drying at all. Their eggs arrive on the legs of fliers, in the damp fur of browsers, or accidentally in a traveler’s gourd. Thus two cisterns only a few days apart may hold almost different worlds. One is clear and nearly empty except for a single patient predator. Another seethes so densely that the water seems to have a skin of its own.

The commonest cistern guards are flat amber animals that fit themselves to the rim. From above they resemble woody lids or healed scars. They are not guarding water for the tree, or for anything but themselves; they simply profit from everything that comes to drink. Experienced water-gatherers touch a large cistern first with a frayed fiber wand. If the wand returns cut to ribbons, they wait, use another opening, or go elsewhere.

The hot hollows inside trunks support stranger communities. There the water may be yellow or nearly black, and clear water is not necessarily safer. Blind white swimmers move through it slowly. Wingless larvae crowd the wet walls. Some surfaces are coated with colonies that contract when touched, making an entire hollow appear to flinch. Animals fall in, die, and feed the rest. A few predators are equally at home in these fermentation baths and in clean rain cisterns, which is one reason a camp never assumes that water familiar in one season will be wholesome in the next.

Above and through all this live the choristers.

They are not one kind of insect, or even one close family of kinds. Their nymphs occupy different depths in the galleries and emerge according to different combinations of heat, sap condition, moisture, and vibration. Some wait only a few seasons. Others may remain below for much of a person’s lifetime. There is no single great year in which everything emerges together. Instead, overlapping broods accumulate until a crown, a root valley, or a whole district crosses some threshold.

A singer fastened to a trunk contributes more than the sound made by its own body. The fluted wood selects, deepens, and carries certain notes. Galleries return others. A crowded crown does not merely contain more insects; it becomes a different instrument. This is why the loss of one brood can alter a crown’s voice for years, and why a familiar note may split or vanish after damage without the crown appearing sick from outside.

Not all the adults sing. Some answer with a single dry knock. Some are almost silent and contribute only wings, mating flights, eggs, and eventually bodies. Their aboveground lives are often brief. They gather in the hot branches, coat one another with pollen, lay their eggs in tender growth or narrow fissures, and die in such numbers that their wings collect in seams like translucent chaff.

The fliers arrive before the emergence has reached its height. Broad-mouthed rainmouths fly with their throat membranes extended, straining bodies from the air and becoming visibly heavier as they feed. Smaller animals cling beneath platforms and branches, snatching whatever tumbles out of the swarm. Narrow-winged hunters come after these, striking through the densest flights. Their passage opens the brief corridors of silence in which a person may suddenly hear water dripping, cloth tearing, or another person shouting a hand’s breadth away.

Then the hunters withdraw, and the separate pitches close over the gap.

The largest browsers seem at first to belong to another, slower landscape. The camps call them gantries when they are being practical and old walkers when they are telling stories. They stand on jointed legs tall enough that a person can pass beneath the belly of a full-grown animal without ducking. Their bodies are narrow but deep, their heads small for the amount of vegetation they crop. Loose folds of skin hang along the neck and flanks. Their outer coat is a mixture of hair, trapped fiber, pollen, dust, and whatever small plants have managed to root in it.

An old gantry is not quite a single animal ecologically. Damp folds carry cistern eggs and minute swimmers. Its coat shelters button insects, larval broods, and flat predators that prey on the parasites rather than on the browser itself. Small fliers clean around its eyes and wounds. Seeds germinate in the accumulated matter along its back, sometimes living there long enough to put out several leaves before being rubbed off against a branch.

Gantries connect places that would otherwise remain isolated. They carry life from crown to crown, deposit it in dung or shed hair, and leave deep footprints that briefly hold water after rain. They also carry disease. A camp watches their condition as carefully as their direction of travel. A group that leaves a crown while its voice still seems strong is often remembered later, if the crown subsequently weakens.

The heavy burrowers work below them. These are barrel-bodied animals with shovel-like forefeet and faces armored against splinters. They tear into galleries for larvae and soft fermenting tissue, indifferent to whether a passage was part of an old watercourse. Their digging drains some hollows and fills others. It admits air, exposes living fiber, and gives the plateau’s healing growth new work.

Opposing them are the patient sealers: colonies of small animals that pack wounds with chewed fiber, shed skins, and saliva, as well as larger creatures that repeatedly drag debris into openings. Between excavation, sealing, and the growth of the root itself, no water passage remains dependable forever. This is one reason water maps age faster than distances do.

The most feared hunter is usually identified before it is seen.

The seam-walker is long, low, and flexible enough to use old galleries and root fissures. Its broad feet make little impression on the pelt. Dust and fragments of leaf adhere to its mantle, so that at rest it can resemble a collapsed root or an old healed cut. It spends much of its time out of sight, but after a chorus it often hunts near water, when too many other animals are doing the same thing.

It does not possess some power to extinguish song. Rather, enough animals respond to its approach—button insects dropping, grazers reversing course, choristers falling quiet—that silence travels ahead of it. A sick crown tends to lose its voice unevenly. A traveling silence, accompanied by stillness in the pelt and gantries lifting their heads, is something else. At such times experienced travelers leave the water alone, even if their gourds are empty.

The people of the platform camps live by the same hidden abundance, though not always comfortably.

They do not constitute one nation or one neatly uniform culture. They name camps, lineages, water-routes, and sometimes the districts between particular crowns; there is no universally accepted name for all of them. A camp may be one household, several related ones, or a temporary association formed around reliable water. Dry years divide camps. Rain, a large emergence, or a disputed cistern may bring them together.

Their buildings are lessons in controlled looseness. Platforms are lashed rather than nailed, shelters woven so that they can deform without tearing, and condensation frames adjusted until they hum without developing the hard, narrow buzz that means a cord is about to fail. Skilled builders can pluck a length of cordage and hear whether insects have entered the twist. Nothing is allowed to remain merely because it was strong when new. The living support thickens, a root ridge shifts, and yesterday’s sound joint becomes tomorrow’s crushing one.

Daily work consists largely of small inspections: clearing a leaf gutter without stripping its living margin, testing a cistern, replacing fiber before it is eaten through, checking whether burrowers have opened a bladder beneath the pelt, and seeing which animals have begun to gather near a guarded gourd. At midday people rest where they can. Outside the crowns, they wear layered soles of fiber and seed-valve material; the apparent softness of the plant skin is deceptive.

Their diet changes abruptly with the Choir’s activity. Between emergences they gather seeds, succulent growth, gallery larvae, and whatever animals can be taken without spending more water than the food is worth. A strong chorus brings a surfeit: oily seed, yellow pollen caught on trays, winged adults dried in the sun, and fliers hunted as they gorge. Much must be processed quickly. Fire is used cautiously, in small covered hearths set on trays of rim stone. An escaped spark can vanish into dry galleries and continue out of sight, so fermentation, drying, and slow cooking are often safer than an open flame.

A full chorus is harvest, weather, and hazard at once. People stop arguing aloud because argument is impossible. They use hand signs, touches, and pulls on agreed lines. Soft plugs protect their ears; fine mesh keeps wings from eyes and mouths. Water frames must be emptied before their catch attracts too many animals, and gutters cleared before bodies and pollen choke them. At the height of an emergence, even a familiar platform can become difficult to find through the moving air.

Afterward, when the wave has passed and the basins briefly shine, scattered camps sometimes meet. They trade stone flakes, cord, dried food, seed-valve blades, and news of changing water. Marriages and disputes are carried from one such meeting to another. So are warnings about silent crowns and unreliable routes.

Routes are learned as sequences of water and voice, but no experienced traveler trusts either alone. A route with only one usable cistern is not considered a real route. A tone remembered from childhood may have deepened with drought or acquired a second note after a trunk was wounded. Directions are therefore revised whenever someone returns, and a traveler who repeats an old tonal song without checking its water signs is regarded as confidently dangerous rather than knowledgeable.

The camps are not uniformly gentle stewards. They rob cisterns, overharvest accessible broods, cut living tissue when it suits them, and occasionally foul water through carelessness. Some speak of the plateau as one body; others consider that an obvious mistake made by people who have never watched neighboring growths poison one another. Their light impact comes less from universal restraint than from mobility, small numbers, and the speed with which the substrate absorbs what they leave behind.

Even so, everyone understands that abundance is conditional. A platform may be used for generations and then abandoned in a single dry season. Living tendrils follow its cords, steps heal into hollows, and the camp becomes a swelling that only an old traveler recognizes.

When a chorus ends, the visible congregation gathers for a little while: gantries at the lower seepages, burrowers nosing into softened passages, rainmouths hanging heavy in the branches, people lowering gourds while watching the rims. Somewhere nearby a seam-walker may be waiting for the same reason.

Then the water sinks away. The carcasses are dragged into fiber or covered by new growth. The browsers move on. Under the leaf-scales, the glassbacks resume their grazing, and the distant crowns become separate objects in the heat again.

Very little has come from elsewhere. It has emerged from fur, dry walls, galleries, seed, and living wood. For a few hours it was impossible to overlook; then the High Choir folded it back out of sight.
