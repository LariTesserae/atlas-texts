Nothing in the Bright Seep lives on its light.

That is the first peculiarity of its inhabitants. The radiance, for all its abundance, is not food. No film turns toward it; no animal follows it. Life here could continue in perfect darkness so long as the seep still arrived with the right chemistry. Light matters only because it warms water, dries surfaces, and helps the roof build itself. The inhabitants respond to those consequences, not to the brilliance.

Their habitat is consequently much smaller than the chamber. Most of the bare glass belongs to no ecology at all. Nothing crosses the broad floor planes, nothing shelters beneath the silica blades, and the condensing ceiling is beyond the reach of everything alive. Life occupies the rear fissure, part of the pool margin, and a few capillary films that come and go. It lives less in places than in differences: between hot and merely warm, wet and dry, fresh mineral and water already altered by contact with it.

The microbial films arrive through the same pathways as the water. Most cells are carried onto surfaces where they cannot persist. Some are heated beyond recovery; some dry almost at once; some lodge in water too poor in the particular chemicals they require. The few that remain do not spread evenly. They establish small, discontinuous populations in the feeder throat and along the rear wall, often separated by apparently identical glass on which nothing grows.

Nearest the fissure, the active film may be nearly colorless. Its cells metabolize hydrogen and reduced sulfur, fixing a little carbon into new cell material. This is the beginning of all the food made in the chamber. Farther along the pool margin, where the water is cooler and has already passed over other growth, different organisms predominate. Some live directly on the chemical seep. Others use what the first cells leak, or what remains when they die. The whole food web can occupy a patch of wall smaller than a hand, with most of its inhabitants consuming substances produced within a few cell-lengths of them.

The amber waterline is not a reliable portrait of that life. Its color comes largely from iron and silica gathered around cell surfaces and empty sheaths, not from any pigment made for living in the light. The most intensely active growth may be the clear, wet edge that disappears except at a grazing angle. The darkest amber may contain more remains than cells. A single stain can therefore be a population, a accumulation of corpses, and a mineral deposit at once.

When the seep recedes, the films dry unevenly. Their outer layers become powder; cells in the deeper, damper portions may persist in a glassy dormancy. Some resume activity when water returns. Others never do. Growth after rewetting is consequently patchy, beginning in old fissures and favored hollows rather than advancing as a clean front. There is no turf to spread and no mature mat to inherit the wall. There is only repeated establishment, followed by partial loss.

Success brings its own danger. Cell sheaths and the organic films around them provide surfaces on which silica can collect. A thriving patch may begin to seal the very pore that feeds it. Some cells are entombed while neighboring ones are still dividing. The resulting opal preserves their shapes without preserving their lives. Much of what looks like growth at the waterline is thus life being converted, almost immediately, into part of the chamber.

The crustaceans belong to a larger and darker world beyond the cavity. The Seep is far too young to have produced their form. They bring with them the habits of groundwater: eyelessness, elaborate touch, patience, and bodies that lose almost nothing by being transparent. Each is a small arrangement of joints and fluids, with antennae so fine that seepage can move them. At rest, an animal may be indistinguishable from a slight distortion in the pool. Light catches at its limb sockets and along the rim of its shell, then passes through the rest. Only after feeding does it acquire much visible substance: a faint amber thread of gut within the clear body.

The fissure is their refuge. The pool is an exposed feeding ground at the limit of what they can tolerate.

Before emerging, one may remain at the opening with only its antennae extended into the flow. A change in temperature or dissolved chemistry is enough to keep it there. When it finally comes out, it does not explore. It follows the wet margin, testing each new surface with its limbs and remaining close enough to retreat if a hotter pulse arrives. Dry glass is not merely uncomfortable but impassable; beyond the water film, its body would quickly lose the moisture on which every internal process depends. The open chamber, however luminous, is less available to it than the narrowest wet crack.

Its feeding is almost too slight to call disturbance. Fine mouthparts scrape at the microbial film, gathering living cells, dormant cells, organic debris, and mineral particles together. What is digestible is taken from that mixture. The animal does not clear a path down to bright glass; it only thins the film. Afterward, the grazed strip may be visible because it no longer returns the light in quite the same way. Water and renewed growth soften the interruption. Within the film, released organic matter becomes briefly available to smaller consumers, so that even the animal’s passing is incorporated into the local chemistry.

Between these excursions, it does very little. Food is sparse, heat makes activity costly, and long stillness is part of its economy. Deep in the throat, only the small movements of respiratory limbs may reveal that it has not become another transparent object. Growth is correspondingly irregular. When a molt finally comes, the soft animal remains sheltered until its new covering can protect it. The discarded shell, being lighter and no longer directed by limbs, may be carried into the pool. It can settle there with every leg and antenna still articulated, a more conspicuous and durable presence than the animal that made it.

The cavity need never contain a whole population. Brooding and the survival of the youngest animals can occur farther within the groundwater fissures. Only occasionally might a female carrying one or very few eggs approach the cooler throat. If every crustacean vanished from the pool margin, that would not necessarily mean the loss of the deeper population, nor would a later return require a new kind of animal. It would require only a passable crack and another individual willing—or merely impelled—to follow food and water to their outermost habitable point.

At midday, when the chamber appears most full of event, much of its life is withdrawn. The outer films tighten and dry; dormant cells wait; the crustaceans remain behind the wall. Cooler intervals and renewed condensation may extend the wet habitat again, but there is no dependable daily migration. The last heat pulse matters more than the hour. A single shift in the seep can make the pool margin available or lethal.

As deposition continues, the largest habitats will disappear first. Excursions into the open pool will become shorter and rarer, then cease. The grazed interruptions will vanish from the waterline. Microbes will persist longer in narrowing films and microscopic wet pockets, some active, some dormant, many already enclosed in silica. Eventually the chamber may still fill with white light while containing nothing alive. If the molt on the pool floor is preserved, it may remain after the last visit, looking more like an inhabitant than any of the living animals ever did.

The closing of the bright seam will not be what ends the ecology. Darkness alone would have changed little. The ending will come with the loss of wet surfaces and chemical contrast, when water no longer crosses fresh mineral and no film can renew itself.

For now, the entire living system still fits within that narrow overlap: rock yielding chemicals, water carrying them, microbes spending them, and a transparent animal taking a little of the resulting growth. A drop falls. A dried patch darkens and becomes slick again. Just inside the fissure, antennae move toward the altered flow. If the animal emerges, the light will pass almost entirely through it; only the faint thread of its gut will show that, for a short while, something in the Bright Seep has been fed.
