There are no people in the High Vacancy. There are, however, things that feed, things that divide, and things that can be ended by a change in pressure. Most are alive intermittently. A census taken between tides would find little but dust in faults and a few dark grains in an open court; a census patient enough to watch those grains would have to revise the meaning of a body.

The wall lattices are the commonest lives, and the least committed to remaining alive. A patch’s visible outline is less its body than its present activity. Between pressure tides, the same colony may persist as a slight excess of dark grains lodged along a crystal defect, electrically unremarkable. When conditions return, organization spreads outward from that trace, one exact angle at a time. If no trace remains, a similar lattice may still arise independently on the same wall. Some patches are descendants. Others are only recurrences.

Their feeding consists of keeping two faces of the stone from becoming electrically alike. The colony is most active at its edges, where new grains are drawn into the pattern; its interior may already be relaxing. A wall that loses its difference does not slowly starve the lattice. The angles merely become less exact. The stepped boundary wanders, subdivides without separating, and then the patch is dust again. Nothing is left that could be called a corpse.

The crack-animals spend most of their lives not quite occurring in three dimensions.

A fracture is not simply their shelter. It is the template by which one of them keeps its tissue ordered. At rest, the animal and the crack have nearly the same shape. When it feeds, it draws one edge free, then another, unfolding into a shallow ribbon whose angles change as it passes over the stone. It has no permanent head. Whichever end is advancing becomes more responsive to charge; if the animal reverses, that office passes to the other end.

Nor does it have a mouth. The lattice disappears beneath it without appearing to enter anywhere. A little of the charge is drawn through the silicate tissue, a few grains adhere and are incorporated, and the remainder is left as unorganized dust. Well-fed animals darken slightly. Starving ones become harder to distinguish from the fractures containing them.

On a populated stair, several hundred may lie motionless along separate seams. Before a thin front arrives, they turn across the grain of the stone, not all at once but in a sequence that passes down the steps. For a few moments the stair appears to develop an oblique fault. Then the animals begin to feed, and the fault seems to reconsider itself independently on every tread.

They reproduce by becoming inconvenient to their own cracks. A well-fed animal lengthens until its tissue can no longer maintain a single stable line. A branch forms at an angle, follows some minute defect across the stone, and acquires a separate polarity. The branch is at first only a change of direction in the parent. Later it is another animal. There is no brood, no tending, and no visible interval of infancy. When one dies, it usually remains in place as a dark seam until abrasion takes the seam as well.

Other lives never touch the stone.

Within the level dust banners are patterns of spacing that persist while their material passes through them. These are sometimes called veils, although they have no surface. Each consists of grains maintaining one another at distances that the surrounding stream does not impose. A veil becomes visible when black mineral grains rise from a shaft and enter a pale banner: the dark grains divide around an apparently empty shape, travel through it at slightly different speeds, and close again beyond it.

A veil takes a little charge from the dust passing through its intervals. In still air it must spend more of its own organization merely keeping those intervals open. Most last only days, though the grains composing one may be replaced many times before it fails. At the cliff edges, where banners extend outward without falling, veils sometimes travel to the far end of the stream and then return against its motion. They are among the very few things in the highlands that visibly reverse direction without changing the conditions around them.

When a veil settles on stone, it may collapse into an angular patch. Some wall lattices therefore begin as degraded veils. Whether this is reproduction, deterioration, or the same life under different conditions is not clear.

The additional shadow-city provides them with a brief habitat. During the short part of the cycle when its lanes lie across the saltfield, veils enter the shadow-doors as narrow interruptions in the dust. A shadow-wall cannot support a lattice, but its deflection of charged grains is enough to preserve a veil’s spacing. Processions of them pass through streets that contain no solid surfaces. When the district fades, dust sometimes continues for hours to divide around its rooflines and narrow at its absent doors. No separate creature has ever been seen standing within it. The shadow-city may be less a place than a temporary set of conditions under which organization can survive without mineral.

The largest ordinary inhabitants require the great courts.

Three grains of dark dust, widely separated around a tilted slab, may constitute one animal. This is not obvious from watching them, because for long periods they do nothing. But if one grain is displaced by a dust stream, the others shift during subsequent tides until the old proportions are nearly restored. If all three are scattered, new grains may eventually take up equivalent stations. The pattern can survive the replacement of its matter.

There is no tissue between its parts. Its body is the relation it maintains across empty space.

Dust passing through that relation emerges subtly changed. It carries less charge and is less likely to establish lattices on the shaded walls beyond. This appears to be how the dispersed animal feeds: almost nothing is taken from any one grain, but the court provides an immense passage of almost nothing. Before pressure fronts, the pattern contracts. During long still periods, it may extend until one of its grains lies beyond the court, on a stair or in an elevated channel. The animal has not exactly moved. More of the landscape has become relevant to it.

After a regional settling, two patterns are occasionally found where there had been one. For several cycles they may share a single grain, each maintaining different distances to it. Then one relation weakens, and the grain becomes the property of the other, or merely dust. This is the closest thing they have to reproduction. Death is equally quiet: after a revision of the district, the same grains may remain in the court but no longer answer one another.

Some dispersed animals appear to be very old. Their courts have undergone the erosion of length, and their bodies have become minute in traversal while remaining enormous in perspective. One may be crossed in nine steps, though following its outer relation around the court takes an hour. Others persist in courts whose far corners now touch. Their lives have not become smaller in any simple sense; only the journeys by which they might be measured have changed.

The unmade sounds in enclosed rooms sometimes accompany alterations in their spacing. A room returns one footstep, or one breath drawn with unnecessary care, and at the next tide a distant grain has changed its station. This may be coincidence. The High Vacancy produces enough acoustic errors to make any pattern of coincidences available to a patient observer.

It is tempting to extend the same interpretation to certain routes. Most shortened avenues are only stone and worn distance. A few behave as though being followed has become part of their persistence. Dust returns to them before a front reaches the pavement. Crack-animals gather near openings whose destinations are about to change. A traveler’s last footsteps remain behind, approaching faintly from an empty passage, as if the route has not quite finished accommodating the passage that has already ended.

Such an avenue might be a dispersed animal stretched until its body consists mostly of connection. It might equally be a habitat produced by many lesser lives, or simply a landform whose behavior resembles theirs. At that scale, the distinction between an inhabitant and the place it inhabits becomes unreliable.

Even newly discovered rooms do not settle the question. A blank wall develops a seam, the seam becomes a door, and behind it are floors already worn and corners already softened by age. Sometimes the rooms are sterile. Sometimes a lattice is relaxing on an inner wall, or a fissure contains several crack-animals whose tissues are thin with age. No migration into the rooms has been observed. Their presence does not prove that the rooms existed before discovery. It only proves that, by the time there is an interior in which to look, something may already have been living there for a very long while.

The crossing at night may be the farthest extension of this kind of life, or the thing that consumes it.

In the cycle before one occurs, veils often turn back from the open gulfs. Crack-animals crowd the deepest portions of their fractures, and lattices spread unchecked across stairs that would ordinarily be grazed bare. Then the lower lights begin to vanish in narrow ascending interruptions. No silhouette passes in front of them. Nothing rises between the mesas. The dust banners do not bend around a body.

Afterward, the open wards remain empty of crack-animals for several cycles. Some colonies gradually reappear in their old stairs, suggesting that they had withdrawn or become dormant. Others never return. There are no fragments, no discarded tissue, and no concentration of remains anywhere along the apparent route.

If the crossing is a dispersed animal, each interruption may be one station at which its pattern briefly affects visibility. The rest of its body would be the relation between those stations, extending through spaces no observer can survey. It would not need to climb. Its stations would simply become perceptible in ascending order. If it is instead something like a living route, then the sequence may be the passage of a connection upward from the depths, briefly occupied but never arrived at. It is called a crossing because the lights go out in order. Nothing else about it is certainly known to move.

None of these lives makes the city its own. They build no nests, store no food, carry nothing, and leave no remains that a later room could contain. Their aggregations do not become settlements. Even their abundance would be easy to overlook: a hundred crack-animals can be a stair’s fine fracturing; a veil can be a slight irregularity in a dust banner; an animal spanning a court can be three grains and a great deal of nothing.

That nothing is essential to them. The lattices need broad, unbroken faces of stone. The dispersed animals need courts in which relations can be maintained without interference. The veils need open gulfs and long streams of isolated grains. A crowded place, richly furnished and full of solid bodies, would offer them less habitat than the High Vacancy does.

At the dimmest part of the cycle, the lattices continue their small geometric feeding. The crack-animals become faults. A few veils hold their spacing in the rising outer dust. In the great courts, dark grains preserve distances that no eye could distinguish from emptiness. If the lights below begin to go out in ascending order, nothing visible looks up.

They do not people the High Vacancy. They give its emptiness a few habits, a few appetites, and, on certain nights, a direction.
