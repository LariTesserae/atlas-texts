The Slow Choir’s inhabitants occupy almost none of its visible space. They live at boundaries: where rainwater mixes with vent water, where dissolved iron meets oxygen, where a wet fracture narrows into a sealed one. Even there, life is discontinuous. Much of the stone is sterile, and many clear pools contain scarcely more than the minerals dissolved in them. If every living thing were removed, the basin would look much the same, and its sounding would not change until the slow work of deposition and clogging began to diverge.

Most of the life is in the waterline films, but the films are not continuous mats, nor is each color a single kind of organism. They are layered communities embedded in the material they help produce. Gray patches often develop where sulfur-bearing water meets rain; bronze ones are commonly rich in iron compounds and empty mineral sheaths. The violet sheen may come less from pigment than from extremely fine layers of silica and organic matter interfering with the weak light. Much of what can be seen is already dead or inert. The living cells occupy particular depths within the film, sometimes only a small fraction of its thickness.

This is why they do not behave like slime. A finger passed through one may part the sheen or cloud the water, yet come away clean. There is little loose organic material to remove. The community remains attached to stone, or fragments into pieces too small and sparse to discolor anything.

Some of these organisms use chemical energy to build new organic matter from dissolved carbon. Others live on their products and remains. None depends on a daily rhythm of light. A period of growth may begin when a buried seam starts delivering sulfur, when weeks of rain dilute a pool, or when a falling water level brings two chemical zones together. Elsewhere, at the same time, nothing happens. There are no visible blooms. Activity is more likely to be announced by a slight change in the water than by any change in the film’s appearance.

When a waterline abandons a film, some of its cells form resistant bodies and remain within the dark crust. Others simply die. Rewetting does not necessarily restore the old community. A patch that looks continuous with a centuries-old stain may have been colonized many times, each occupation using the mineral residue of the last. Fresh black glass can remain uninhabited for an age; pure salt and newly shattered surfaces are often especially empty.

There is also life below the visible pools, in wet microfractures and within the clogged interiors of columns. This should not be imagined as a second, abundant world under the basin. It is mostly a scattering of cells in disconnected spaces, with long sterile distances between them. Some may divide only when a rare change in flow supplies the right chemistry. Others persist without measurable activity. The exposed basin is young, but the lineages inhabiting it need not be. Many may have entered from older groundwater systems, finding in each new spring or fracture another temporary surface.

Where films persist undisturbed, they can acquire a sparse population of still smaller consumers: transparent specks that creep between mineral grains, coiled bodies visible only in a drop under magnification, and dormant granules that respond to wetting long after they appear to be grit. They crop the living cells without removing much of the mineral layer. Many films have no such grazers at all. A pool can be chemically inhabited and visually empty for its entire existence.

The finger-length swimmer is the largest organism one can reasonably expect to see, but finding one does not mean that its pool is unusually fertile. Its presence may say more about an old connection through the groundwater than about the richness of the water.

It is less like a small fish than like a body folded around almost nothing. There are no obvious eyes or fins. The clear gelatin can become thin enough that the stone beneath it remains visible through several folds. The dark internal line is its only substantial feature: a digestive tract together with denser tissue and concentrated mineral-bearing cells. Its transparency need not be camouflage. With almost nothing hunting it, it may simply be what a very economical body looks like.

Movement occurs through slow changes in the folds. A contraction passes from one part of the body to another, turning it or drawing it a short distance through the water. Often the motion is so slight that a current appears responsible. When feeding, it settles against submerged stone and presses part of its margin to a film, taking up the living material without visibly scraping the surface. A palm-sized area may provide food for a long time. Afterward the swimmer can remain suspended or lightly attached for days, its body losing definition until only the dark line betrays it.

It can shrink during scarcity, reabsorbing some of its clear structure, and thicken again when conditions improve. This makes its age difficult to judge. An individual need not be young because it is small, nor old because it is large. Its presence in a pool may be less like permanent residence than like a single event lasting decades.

Nothing about the distribution of swimmers requires territory or social behavior. A pool containing one individual is not necessarily being defended against others. More often, there may simply be no others capable of reaching it. How reproduction and dispersal occur remains uncertain. The buried seams offer routes between some pools, but not all, and a viable body small enough to pass through them would be difficult to distinguish from sediment. When a swimmer dies, there is no substantial carcass. The gelatin loses its folds, the dark line disperses, and the films receive a brief addition of organic matter.

No swimmer contributes audibly to the basin. It may contract after a nearby concussion, as almost any soft body would, but the ordinary drone does not organize its behavior. It neither calls nor answers. The delayed pitches, the successive ticking of columns, and the distant repetitions of an impact belong to stone and water.

Nor is there good reason to populate the lightless vents with large animals. The occasional broad ripple moving against an outflow remains unassigned, but gas release, a settling plate, or a pressure change below the pool can produce similar disturbances. Something else may live below. The basin’s emptiness is not evidence against it. Neither, however, is the darkness evidence for it.

The constructions have no present tenants. Films cross their thresholds without interruption. A stair descending into a pool may become a grazing surface; a roofless cell may trap a little sediment and therefore support more life than the surrounding glass, or it may remain entirely bare. There are no nests, food stores, accumulated remains, or repeated paths. Nothing living there now distinguishes a straight mark from a fracture, or a doorway from any other gap. Whatever placed the stones is absent from the basin’s present ecology.

Life’s relationship to the Choir is therefore material and deferred. The films can provide surfaces on which silica gathers, trap fine particles, or alter the chemistry of water enough to encourage deposition. Other communities weaken a seam or keep a narrow passage open by consuming what would otherwise obstruct it. Each effect is minute. Accumulated over centuries, such effects may help determine whether a cavity narrows, whether a seep changes course, or whether a column’s note becomes slightly lower and less clear.

The loudest stands are not necessarily the fullest of life. A clean, open, nearly sterile tube may sustain a pure note for a very long time. Some clogged and quiet columns contain more living film along their internal waterlines. Life does not prefer silence; it prefers usable chemistry, stable moisture, and surfaces that are not too frequently stripped away.

A future mute basin would therefore not automatically be a more lifeless one. Some of its silences would remain as sterile as fresh rubble. Others would contain thin bronze seams, dormant cells, and water moving almost imperceptibly through paste.

The life of the Slow Choir is not a secret explanation of the place. The basin need not be a single organism, and its common note is not a voice. Its inhabitants neither sustain the performance nor gather to hear it. They persist in small chemical opportunities, leaving behind minute quantities of mineral and organic matter. Only much later does their work enter the soundscape at all: when one cavity has become too narrow, one passage no longer carries water, and one small note fails to return.
